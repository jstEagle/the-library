## 7. Smoke Where Nothing Should Burn

The tree line gave up on us the way old men give up on arguments—gradually, then all at once. One hour the birches stood bent and stubborn around us, their leaves turned early to copper; the next there was only stone and lichen and a wind that came off the ice fields with nothing left to slow it down.

Orsen Nystrom paused where the last tree stood—a birch no taller than her hip, twisted into a shape like a question mark—and rested her hand on its bark as if taking leave of an acquaintance.

"Past this point," she said, "the mountain stops pretending to be kind."

Dagny Holt caught up, breathing hard, her survey pack riding crooked on her shoulders. Three days on the trail had burned the office out of her. The pressed collar was gone. Her hands were cracked at the knuckles, her lips split from wind, and she'd stopped asking how much farther somewhere around noon yesterday, which Orsen considered a milestone in the woman's education.

"You say that every morning," Dagny said.

"It's true every morning."

They climbed. That was the whole of the work now—climbing, in the grammar of the high country, where walking became climbing without announcement, the way dusk becomes dark. The river ran beside them still, narrower now, louder, channeled between walls of granite polished smooth by ten thousand years of insistence. Orsen kept one eye on it always. It was the only map she trusted anymore. Official charts said this water should not exist past the glacier's toe; official charts said a great many things.

She thought about maps the way other people thought about old lovers. A map was a promise someone made about the world, and the world broke promises constantly, quietly, in places no one was standing to witness. Somewhere below them, in some government drawer, sat a chart with a blue line ending neatly at the ice—the cartographer's polite fiction, *nothing here, nothing further, move along.* And beneath her boots ran a river that had never agreed to end.

The smoke reached them before they understood what it was.

Orsen stopped mid-stride, one boot on a shelf of rock, and lifted her face into the wind the way a deer lifts its head. There. Faint, threaded through the mineral smell of stone-melt and cold, something warm. Something organic. Woodsmoke—not peat, not diesel, not the chemical stink of a signal flare. Birch smoke, maybe, or pine. Smoke with a hearth in it.

"Dagny."

"I smell it." Dagny came up beside her, and Orsen watched the officer's face do something complicated: hope first, because smoke meant people meant answers about six missing government employees; then fear, arriving a half-second behind hope the way thunder follows light. "That's impossible. We're above—" She gestured vaguely at everything. "There's nothing up here. No structures on any survey. No fuel sources. Nothing burns at this altitude."

"Nothing official," Orsen said.

They climbed toward it.

* * *

The terraces appeared out of the mountainside the way a face appears in a portrait—assembled gradually from details that had been there all along, waiting for the eye to organize them.

First Orsen noticed the wall: a low line of fitted stones running across the slope, so weathered and lichen-crusted she took it for geology until she saw the second wall parallel to it, twenty meters upslope, and understood she was looking at agriculture. Terraces cut into the flank of the mountain itself, each one narrow as a coffin lid, each one holding soil that had no business existing at this elevation—black, rich soil carried up here basket by basket by someone who refused to be told what couldn't grow.

And things grew. Hardy greens she half-recognized—something like kale gone feral, something like barley but smaller, denser, bred for short seasons and thin air. Rows of pale roots she couldn't name. On the lowest terrace, a cluster of squat stone huts roofed with turf, so perfectly camouflaged against the hillside that from fifty meters downslope they would have vanished entirely. From the air, from any distance, from any map: invisible.

Smoke rose from the chimney of the largest hut, thin and blue and utterly calm, as if chimneys smoked here every day of the year, as if the world knew about this place and simply agreed to keep quiet.

Orsen stood very still and felt the vertigo come on—not the physical kind, not the dizziness of height, though there was plenty of height—but the deeper vertigo of a worldview cracking along a fault line she hadn't known was there. She had spent twelve years learning every fold of these highlands. She could name the scree fields by texture, the snow bridges by sound when you stepped on them. She had believed herself the last person alive who knew this country intimately. And the whole time, an entire community had been living above her knowledge, farming the mountain's spine, burning wood that appeared from nowhere, growing barley where the growing season was a rumor.

"How long," Dagny whispered. Not a question really. A prayer said backwards. "How long has this been here?"

"Longer than my blacklist," Orsen said. "Look at the walls. Look at the lichen on the north faces. That's decades of growth. Maybe generations." She crouched, touched the terrace soil, crumbled it between her fingers. Tended soil. Turned soil. Soil someone loved. "This isn't a camp. This isn't survival. This is a *decision* somebody made and kept making for thirty years."

"The missing team—"

"Could be anywhere. Could be nowhere." Orsen rose. "But they didn't vanish into emptiness, Dagny. They vanished into *this*."

Ahead, where the terraces climbed toward a saddle of grey rock, stood a cairn.

It was larger than the trail markers Orsen built herself—waist-high, carefully constructed, each stone chosen for fit rather than merely stacked. And it was marked. Carved into the flattest face of the capstone were symbols: a spiral like water turning on itself, crossed by three vertical lines, and beneath them a row of small marks she might have taken for tally strokes except that they were too deliberate, too even, arranged with the confidence of an alphabet.

Her mouth went dry.

She had seen those symbols four days ago, inked onto the final page of a dead man's journal, in the last lines Sela Halvard had written before whatever came after writing. The journal's last page had carried a sketch—hasty, rain-blurred—of exactly this: spiral, three lines, tally marks. Orsen had assumed it was a cipher, a code the team leader had invented, or coordinates disguised as ornament.

It was neither. It was a signpost. Sela Halvard had drawn directions home.

"Sela drew this," Orsen said aloud, and heard her own voice come out strange, flattened by awe. "Not a code. An address."

Dagny had her notebook out already, sketching the symbols with the mechanical precision of her training, but her hand wasn't steady. "If the team came through here—if they *stayed* here—then they're deserters. They walked off duty and joined—" She gestured at the terraces, the huts, the smoke, and Orsen watched the word *this* fail her. "What do we even call this?"

"People," Orsen said. "Call them people."

She started toward the cairn. Dagny followed. The wind dropped as they passed into the lee of the slope, and in that sudden hush Orsen became aware of a silence with a texture to it—the specific silence of a place that is inhabited but choosing not to show it. She had heard it once before, years ago, tracking a wounded bear through late-season willow: the sense of eyes, of held breath, of a landscape pretending very hard.

Nobody emerged from the huts. No door opened. No voice called a challenge. But the smoke kept rising, and under the smell of it, faintly, Orsen caught wood being split somewhere out of sight, and the small domestic sounds of water carried, of grain poured. Life, continuing just past the edge of vision, refusing to be witnessed.

"They know we're here," she murmured.

"Then why don't they—"

"Because they've had practice." Orsen touched the cairn's carved face. The grooves were worn soft at the edges, handled often, traced by fingers across years. "You don't get this good at hiding by accident. You get this good because someone looked for you once, hard, and you swore it would never happen again."

* * *

They made camp that night in the hollow below the lowest terrace, close enough to see the smoke, far enough to make clear they weren't presuming. Orsen built a small fire, cooked barley and dried fish, and said little. Dagny talked enough for both of them—running scenarios, drafting approaches, filling silence the way nervous people fill wells—but around midnight even she went quiet, and they lay in their bags watching the stars wheel over the saddle, and the settlement smoked and murmured beyond them like a sleeping animal aware of the strangers at the edge of its den.

Orsen slept badly and woke before dawn to the gray hour, that hour she trusted more than any compass. She crawled out of her bag to check the sky and froze.

Footprints.

New ones, pressed into the frost that had formed overnight on the flat stones near their camp. They came from the direction of the terraces, circled their fire pit at a respectful distance, paused—there was a deeper impression, a weight held, someone standing and looking down at two sleeping women for a long moment—and then continued past, uphill, toward the saddle, and faded into the rocky ground where frost couldn't hold a print.

They had not been there when she made the fire last evening. She was certain. Twelve years of reading ground had earned her that certainty.

"Dagny." Her voice came out low and even, guide's voice, the voice she used when panic was a luxury. "Wake up. Don't sit up fast."

Dagny surfaced from her bag, hair wild, eyes going instantly sharp in a way Orsen was beginning to respect. Orsen pointed. Dagny stared at the prints for a long moment, and Orsen watched the officer arrive at the same arithmetic she had: *someone stood over us while we slept, and chose to let us keep sleeping.*

"That's a size nine boot," Dagny said finally, hoarse. "Government issue pattern. Like ours. Like—" She stopped.

"Like the gear at the abandoned camp," Orsen finished. "Boots lined up neat as church pews. Six pairs." She crouched over the print, not touching it. In the pre-dawn gray, the detail resolved: worn at the outer heel, the tread half-filled with the black rich soil of the terraces. Someone from the settlement had walked down in government boots—or someone from the government had walked up and stayed long enough to start farming.

Sela Halvard's team had not vanished. They had arrived.

The sun crested the eastern ridge and struck the terraces, and the whole hidden hillside lit up gold-green, impossibly alive, impossibly secret. As the light reached the cairn, Orsen saw something she'd missed in yesterday's glare: fresh. A new mark carved beside the old symbols, the stone dust still pale in the grooves, cut within days—within hours, maybe. A fourth vertical line added to the three beside the spiral.

A message. A count. An invitation, or a warning, addressed to whoever was meant to follow the river up.

Dagny read it and went white. "That's a tally. That's—" She counted twice, lips moving. "Four."

"Four what?" Orsen asked, though a cold certainty was already assembling itself in her chest, patient as water.

Dagny looked up from the stone, and her voice was barely there. "Four people who made it this far. The team was six."

Above them, at the edge of the saddle, a figure stood silhouetted against the brightening sky—still as the cairn, wrapped in grey wool, watching them with the unhurried attention of someone who had decided, at last, to stop hiding.

Orsen rose slowly to her feet, heart hammering a rhythm she hadn't felt since the worst day of her life, and the figure raised one hand—not a wave, not quite a greeting—and beckoned.
