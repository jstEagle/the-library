## 8. The People Who Erased Themselves

They came out of the fog the way weather does—without malice, without warning, and without any possibility of argument.

Orsen had been watching the terraces for an hour when the first of them stepped onto the trail behind her. She heard him before she saw him: a footfall placed with the deliberate softness of someone who had spent years learning not to be heard. By the time she turned, there were four figures arranged around them in a loose crescent, spaced like points on a compass rose, and every one of them held a long-handled tool that could be a farming implement or could be something else entirely. The tools were clean. That was the detail her mind snagged on and refused to release—the way the iron heads gleamed, oiled and sharpened, maintained by people who understood exactly what they were for.

"Don't," Orsen said quietly, without looking at Dagny. She felt the survey officer's weight shift beside her, felt the coiled readiness in her, the government-trained instinct to reach for authority the way other people reached for weapons.

"I'm a licensed—" Dagny began.

"You're nothing here." The speaker was a woman, fifty or so, with a face weathered to the color of old birch bark and eyes the flat gray of the sky above the tree line. Her voice carried no hostility. That was worse. Hostility you could answer. "No license reaches this far up. No name does either. You'll come with us now, and you'll leave your packs, and if you run we will find you before the light fails, because this valley is ours the way your breath is yours."

Orsen's hands hung open at her sides. Twelve years of mountains had taught her the arithmetic of force, and the sum here was simple: four against two, home ground against foreign, and behind these four, somewhere in the smoke, more. She looked at Dagny and gave the smallest shake of her head—the same gesture she'd use on a client reaching for a handhold that wasn't there.

"We'll walk," Orsen said.

* * *

They took her boots.

That was the thing that frightened her most, though she couldn't have explained it to anyone who hadn't lived as long as she had inside her own skin. They didn't bind her wrists. They didn't threaten. They simply asked—politely, almost tenderly—for her boots and Dagny's, and set them on a shelf inside a turf-roofed hut at the valley's mouth, side by side, laces tucked in with a housekeeper's care. Then they gave each of them a pair of felt-lined slippers, soft-soled, silent.

"No one escapes barefoot into snow," the birch-barked woman said, reading the question off Orsen's face. "And no one walks out of Vaskr in slippers without deciding to. It saves us all a great deal of unpleasantness."

The hut smelled of peat smoke and dried fish and something green—watercress, maybe, grown in channels cut through the floor stones. Warmth rose from below, from the ground itself, and Orsen felt the hair on her arms lift as she understood what she was standing on. The vent. The heat that fed the terraces, melted the winter snow from their fields, kept this impossible garden alive above the tree line. Everything here was built on the same buried fire Sela Halvard's journal had described—and the people who lived on it moved through their days with the untroubled ease of those who had never once considered that the ground might betray them.

*They know,* Orsen thought. *Or they've chosen not to.*

They walked her and Dagny up the valley as the light went long and gold. And because there was nothing else to do but look, Orsen looked.

Vaskr was beautiful. That was the obscenity of it. Terraced gardens stepped down the south-facing slope in green ribbons, beans and barley and something leafy she didn't recognize, irrigated by channels that steamed faintly in the cooling air. A stream ran through the center of the settlement—clear water, cold water, separate from the thermal channels—and along its banks stood perhaps forty buildings, timber and turf and stone, each one different, none of them new, all of them tended with the patient devotion of people who expected to grow old where they stood. Smoke rose from chimneys straight up into the windless evening. Somewhere a child was laughing, high and bright, and the sound went through Orsen like a blade because children did not laugh in places that knew they were dying.

There were no wires. No antennas. No road. No sign, anywhere, of the century that had happened without this place.

"They've been here a long time," Dagny murmured. Her voice had gone strange—stripped of its official cadence, down to something younger. "My God. Look at the joinery on that roof. That's pre-war technique. Nobody's built like that in—"

"Forty years?" Orsen said. "Fifty? You'd know better than me. Your government keeps the records."

Dagny shot her a look, but there was no venom in it. Something had shifted between them since the river, since the crossing where Orsen had hauled her out of the whitewater by the collar of her coat and held her against the current until her lungs remembered their business. Trust was too strong a word. But there was a shared weight now, and they carried it together without discussing it, the way climbers carry a rope.

The residents watched them pass. That was the other obscenity: how ordinary they were. A man splitting wood paused, mallet raised, and studied them with mild curiosity, the way one studies unfamiliar birds. An old woman pulling weeds in a terrace bed didn't look up at all. Two young men carrying a plank across the stream nodded politely. No fear in any of their faces. No surprise, even. As if strangers arrived often enough that courtesy had worn a groove.

But when Orsen met their eyes—one after another, involuntarily, the habit of a guide who reads faces the way she reads cloud—she found the thing underneath the calm. It was in everyone. The wood-splitter had it. The weed-puller had it. The young men with the plank had it, flickering behind their nod.

Recognition. Not of her—of themselves. The look of people who had once been watched, who had once been hunted or pursued or merely counted, and who had crossed some line from which there was no return, and who saw in every stranger's face the possibility that the line had failed.

*These are people who erased themselves,* Orsen thought, and the thought landed in her chest with physical weight, because she knew the shape of that desire from the inside. She had spent twelve years trying to do a smaller version of what these people had done completely. She had fled her name into the high country; they had fled theirs into a valley that maps refused to admit existed. She had wanted to be unwitnessed. They had achieved it, generation-deep, and built gardens on top of the grave of everything they used to be.

She should have felt kinship. What she felt was terror—not for herself, but for them. Because she had read Sela's journal. She knew what slept under their gardens. And she knew, with the cold certainty of a woman who had watched avalanches start, that the ground beneath this peace was counting down.

* * *

The elder's house stood at the head of the settlement, largest of the buildings, its turf roof thick with late-season flowers. They were made to wait outside while a boy ran in with word of them, and Orsen used the waiting to count exits—a habit, a tic, the professional reflex she couldn't switch off. Stream gorge to the north. Scree chute to the west, bad going, visible from everywhere. The terraces offered cover but no route out. Whoever had chosen this site had chosen it the way she would have: with an eye to every approach and a cold assessment of how few ways there were to leave.

"She's thinking about escape routes," Dagny whispered.

"I'm always thinking about escape routes."

"Even here?"

"*Especially* here."

The door opened. "Come in," said the birch-barked woman, whose name, they learned, was Signe, and who had apparently decided that whatever she was, she was also their keeper now.

Inside, the elder's house was one large room, low-beamed, lit by oil lamps and the glow of a hearth that drew its heat, Orsen noted, from a stone channel connected to the ground itself. Maps covered one wall—but not government maps. Hand-drawn ones, inked on linen, showing the valley and the ranges around it in loving topographic detail, contour lines stacked like fingerprints. There was no map of anything beyond the watershed. The world outside Vaskr ended at the edges of the linen, and the linen was hemmed with embroidered flowers.

A woman sat by the hearth, mending a net. She was older than Signe—seventy, maybe more—with white hair braided in a crown and hands that moved with the economy of someone who had done this work ten thousand times. She did not rise. She did not stop mending. She looked at them the way a geologist looks at cores pulled from deep rock: patiently, thoroughly, reading strata.

"Sit," she said.

They sat. Dagny started to speak—Orsen could feel the briefing forming, the official sentences assembling themselves—and the old woman lifted one finger without looking up, and Dagny's voice died in her throat.

For a long moment there was only the sound of the net-mending, the creak of cord, the small tick of the hearthstones heating.

"Twelve days ago," the old woman said at last, still working, "six people came down the glacier. Five of them stayed. One went back up and has not returned." The shuttle paused. "You are not the six. So you will tell me who you are, and why you came, and I will decide what happens next. And before you decide what to tell me"—now she looked up, and her eyes were pale gray, nearly colorless, the eyes of very old northern women, eyes like winter water—"understand that lying in this room has never once worked. We are all liars here. We got good at it before we came. It is the one trade we brought with us."

Orsen opened her mouth to answer and found, to her surprise, that she didn't know what to say. Every version of the truth contained a betrayal of someone. If she spoke of Sela's journal, she betrayed the dead team's secrecy. If she concealed the vent, she betrayed the valley towns downstream. If she named Dagny's mission, she handed these people a reason to make them disappear the way the six had disappeared—or rather, the way the six had *chosen* to disappear, which was somehow worse, because it meant the mechanism existed here, the social machinery of erasure, polished by use.

So she told the oldest truth she had, the one that required no decision.

"My name is Orsen Nystrom," she said. "I'm a mountain guide."

The shuttle stopped.

It stopped mid-stroke, cord taut between the old woman's fingers, and the silence that followed was of a different quality than the silence before—denser, charged, the silence of a room where something has just been detonated under the floor. Signe, by the door, went very still. The old woman set the net down in her lap, slowly, the way one sets down something fragile, and studied Orsen with an attention that made the skin of her scalp tighten.

"Nystrom," the old woman repeated.

"Yes."

"Of which Nystroms?"

"There aren't any others," Orsen said. "That's rather the point of me."

Something moved through the old woman's face—not recognition exactly, but the shadow of it, the way a fish's shadow moves under ice. She turned to Signe and said something in a language Orsen didn't know, three syllables, quick, and Signe answered with two, and whatever passed between them passed over Orsen's head like a bird.

Then the old woman rose. It took her a moment; her knees were old friends with her chair. She crossed the room to the wall of linen maps, and from a wooden box beneath them she took a folded paper, yellowed, soft at the creases, and carried it back, and held it out.

"Do you know this?"

Orsen took it. It was a newspaper clipping, decades old, brittle as autumn leaf. A photograph: a younger woman with dark hair and a hard jaw, standing at a trailhead with a coil of rope over her shoulder. Below it, a headline in a language Orsen half-read, and beneath the headline, smaller print, and beneath that—

Her own surname. In parentheses. Cited as a source. A witness. A name attached to an article about the founding exodus, about the people who vanished from the coastal towns and the mining districts and the debt registries, about the ones who walked into the interior and were never entered into any ledger again.

"This woman interviewed my grandmother," the old woman said. "Before the last crossing. Before Vaskr. She wrote down our names—our real names, the ones we burned—and she promised to keep them until it was safe, and then she died, and the paper kept them instead, in an archive, in a city, filed under a journalist's name." Her colorless eyes held Orsen's. "Filed under *Nystrom*. The only person outside this valley who ever knew who we were. The family that held our names for forty years." She sat back down, heavily, and her hands found the net again but did not work it. "And now a Nystrom walks into Vaskr wearing survey gear, following the tracks of six government people, and tells me she is a guide."

The hearth ticked. Steam hissed faintly in the irrigation channel under the floor, the buried fire breathing in its sleep.

"I didn't know about any of this," Orsen said, and was surprised to find her voice rough. "I swear it. My family kept records. Documents. I inherited boxes I never opened. I never—" She stopped. Swallowed. "I came up here chasing a missing team and a river that isn't on your maps. That's all. That's the whole truth."

"That is not the whole truth," the old woman said gently. "But it may be the truest part of it, which is more than most people manage in this room." She looked at Dagny then, for the first time directly. "And you. You carry a radio. I can see the shape of it under your coat. You carry orders. I can see those too—they sit on people differently than fear does. You will tell me your part soon enough. But first—"

She turned back to Orsen, and now the shadow under the ice resolved, and Orsen saw what it was: grief, old and well-kept, and beneath the grief, hope, which is the cruelest thing an old heart can carry.

"The six who came down the glacier," the old woman said. "Their leader is a young woman. Dark-haired. Careful. She carries a journal and writes in it the way other people pray." Her hands tightened on the netting cord until her knuckles went white. "You have read her pages. I can see it in your face—you flinched when I said *journal*, the way people flinch at a wound they've already dressed. So you will tell me, Orsen Nystrom, daughter of the name-keepers. Where is Sela? Is she alive? And what did she write about the fire under our fields?"

Outside, far downstream, so faint it might have been imagination, the ground gave a single long shudder—and every person in the room stopped moving, and every eye went to the floor, and Orsen understood that whatever answer she gave next, the mountain was already giving its own.
