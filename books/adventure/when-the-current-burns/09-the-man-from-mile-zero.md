## 9. The Man From Mile Zero

They kept us in a house with no locks.

That was the first thing I understood about Vaskr: the doors had no bolts, the windows no shutters that latched, and yet no one left—or perhaps no one wanted to. The room they gave Dagny and me smelled of birch smoke and dried angelica, and through its one small window I could see the terraces climbing the valley wall in green steps, each one held by walls of fitted stone older than any government that had ever claimed this land. Water ran everywhere. It came down the mountainside in wooden flumes, crossed the paths in stone channels, pooled in barrels beside every door. A settlement built by people who had learned to listen to water because water was the only thing that never lied about where it came from.

Dagny sat on the edge of her cot with her boots still on, turning her radio over in her hands like a stone she couldn't decide whether to skip or keep. She hadn't tried it since we were brought down from the ridge. Maybe she understood what I understood—that a signal from inside this valley would be a knife laid against the throat of everyone who lived here.

"They're not prisoners," she said quietly. "I keep waiting for the barbed wire. There isn't any."

"No," I said. "There isn't."

"And you knew." She looked up at me. Not accusing—worse than that. Searching. "When you saw the terraces. When you saw the cairn with those marks on it. Some part of you already knew there were people here."

I didn't answer, because the honest answer was yes and the yes would have opened doors I'd spent twelve years nailing shut. Instead I went to the window and watched an old woman carry a basket of cut hay along a path that switchbacked above the settlement, moving with the unhurried certainty of someone who had walked the same ground ten thousand times and never once been told she couldn't.

The door opened without a knock. Maren stood there—the elder, silver-braided, eyes the color of wet slate—with two others behind her whose faces I was already learning: a broad-shouldered man who moved like a carpenter, and a young woman with ink-stained fingers who wouldn't look at either of us.

"You'll eat with us," Maren said. It wasn't an invitation. "And after you eat, there is someone who has been waiting since yesterday to see you, Orsen Nystrom. He heard your name before we ever brought you down off the ridge. He's been sitting outside the longhouse all night like a dog at a door."

"Who?" Dagny asked.

Maren's gaze slid to me, and something in it—pity, maybe, or the particular caution of a woman who had spent decades deciding which truths could bear daylight—made my stomach drop before she said a word.

"Eirik Halvard," she said. "He is Sela's father. And he says he knows you."

* * *

The longhouse was low and long, built of timber blackened by years of hearth-smoke, with a roof of turf so thick that grass grew on it and two goats were grazing along its spine when we crossed the yard. Inside, long tables ran the length of the room under hanging lamps of hammered tin, and maybe forty people ate together—children first, then elders, then everyone else—in a silence that wasn't grim but practiced, the silence of people who had learned that plenty spoken aloud invites envy, and envy invites maps, and maps invite men with clipboards and warrants.

They fed us barley soup with nettle greens and dark bread that tasted of rye and wood ash. Dagny ate like a survey officer eats—efficiently, watching everything—and I ate like a woman who hadn't realized how hollow she'd been running until food hit the bottom of her.

It was during the meal that I felt it: the weight of being watched, not with hostility but with recognition. A woman near the far end kept glancing at me and then away. A boy of maybe ten stared openly until his mother touched his arm. Word had moved through the settlement faster than the flume-water. *The guide from outside. The one Eirik Halvard has been asking for.*

Afterward, Maren led us out the back of the longhouse into a narrow yard where firewood was stacked under an overhanging shelf of rock, and the mist came off the glacier above in slow white ribbons. And standing among the woodpiles, as if he had grown there out of the stone itself, was a man I would have known anywhere—even after twelve years, even gray at the temples, even thinner, even wearing the same kind of clothes these people wore, wool and hide, the uniform of the erased.

Eirik Halvard.

The mist moved between us. For a moment neither of us breathed.

Twelve years is long enough to build a whole life out of hating someone. I had done it carefully, the way you build anything that has to last—I'd framed it with anger, insulated it with pride, shingled it with every mile I'd walked alone. I knew the shape of that hatred better than I knew my own face. What I had never accounted for was how small it would make him look, standing there in the actual flesh, actual mist, actual failing light. Hatred keeps its subject life-sized. Reality had shrunk him to an old man in a doorway of stacked birch, with his hands hanging open at his sides and his eyes already wet.

"Orsen," he said.

My voice, when it finally came, came out flat as lake ice. "You're dead," I said. "You've been dead twelve years. I buried you myself, somewhere around mile four hundred of the Kestrel traverse. It was a good funeral. Short."

"I know what I did."

"Do you?" I took a step toward him and Dagny's hand twitched toward my elbow and I shook it off without looking. "Because I've had twelve years to imagine what you'd say if I ever found you, and every version started with *I know what I did*, and none of them ended with anything worth hearing. So let's save the time. Tell me the part where you signed the report. Tell me the part where you told the inquiry board that I ordered the crossing. My words, Eirik. You put my words in your mouth and used them to hang me, and then you vanished so thoroughly that even the people who hated me stopped believing I was telling the truth about you existing."

"I vanished," he said, "because staying meant watching them do to you what they'd already decided to do. I testified the way I testified because Halvard Expeditions had three other guides on payroll and a contract with the ministry, and if I'd backed you, they'd have burned all of us. I told myself that. For about a year I actually believed it." His voice cracked down the middle like river ice in spring. "Then I stopped believing it, and by then you were gone, and going back to fix it meant admitting I'd done it at all. So I did the coward's arithmetic. I added up what confession would cost me and subtracted what your name was worth, and I came out here, where nobody counts."

"That's not—" Dagny started, and I raised a hand and she stopped. This wasn't hers. This was old debt, old country.

"The client's name was Tomas Vey," I said. "Thirty-one years old. He wanted to summit the Kestrel needle in October because his father had failed to in 1979 and he thought finishing the route would close some circle. I told him October was wrong. I told him the ice bridge below the col wouldn't hold past the first week. He insisted, and I made the call to go anyway, because I was thirty-four and hungry and Halvard Expeditions paid bonuses for completed objectives, and you were the one who signed off on the itinerary." I was shaking now, and I let him see it. "We crossed at dawn. The bridge held for him. It held for you. It held for me. It dropped under Tomas because bridges don't care about order, they care about load and time, and we'd spent too much of both. And when the ministry came looking for someone to blame, you stood up in a heated room in front of six officials and said, *Miss Nystrom made the final decision to cross.*"

"Yes."

"*Say it again.*"

"Yes," he whispered. "She made the final decision to cross. And it was true, Orsen. That's the poison of it. Every word I said was true. I just said it in the shape of a lie."

That landed harder than a denial would have. I stood in the cold yard with the mist beading on my face and understood, with a sick lurch, that he was right—that was exactly the shape of it. The truth, weaponized. My decision, stripped of the itinerary that forced it, the bonus structure that incentivized it, the partner whose signature shared it. They hadn't needed false testimony. They'd needed testimony shaped like a blade, and Eirik had always been good with tools.

"Why are you here?" I asked finally. "Not why Vaskr. Why me. Maren said you've been waiting since yesterday. You heard my name and you sat outside all night. Twelve years, Eirik. What changed?"

And then his face did something I wasn't prepared for. The guilt fell away—not lifted, *fell*, like snow sliding off a roof—and underneath it was something rawer and more terrible. Grief with no craft in it at all. Grief that hadn't learned to dress itself.

"Sela," he said. "My daughter. Sela Halvard. She led the survey team that came up the valley in September. Six people. They sent their last transmission from below the glacier and then nothing. The ministry wrote them off as lost—but I know my daughter, and I know what she found, because she wrote to me in spring, in cipher, the way we used to write when she was small and we played at being spies." His hands came up, trembling, and pressed together as if holding something invisible between them. "She found the vent. Under the source. She found that the mountain is cooking its own glacier from beneath, and that the dam above it is rotten through, and that when it goes, everything downstream goes with it. Brannvik. Hessel. Four thousand people. And when she reported it, her own superiors told her to bury it, because the mining leases downstream are worth more than the towns are."

The yard tilted. Or I did. Somewhere behind me Dagny made a small sound, the sound of an officer hearing her orders described back to her from the wrong side.

"She refused," Eirik went on, and now the tears came freely and he let them, an old man crying in front of strangers because he had nothing left to protect. "Sela refused. She was always—her mother was like that, she was like that, you don't ask her to carry a lie, she'll set it down where everyone can see it. Six of them chose to stay rather than come home and be silent. They walked up to the source and they stayed. And I have spent every day since telling myself they're alive up there somewhere, that they found a way, that my daughter is—" His voice broke entirely. He steadied it the way you steady a pack strap, with sheer mechanical will. "You're the only person alive who can read that country the way she reads it. You're the only one who ever could. I watched you navigate the Kestrel whiteout with a paper map and a bad attitude and get five clients down alive. If anyone can find where they went—if anyone can find *her*—it's you."

The mist curled between us again. Behind him the woodpile gleamed wet, and above the settlement the terraces climbed into cloud, and somewhere up there, higher than the terraces, higher than the cairn with its strange marks, a river ran out of a glacier that was rotting from underneath.

"You want me to find her," I said slowly, "so she can finish blowing the whistle her own government smothered."

"I want my daughter back," he said. "Whatever she's become. Whatever she knows. I want to stand in front of her and be her father instead of the thing I've been for twelve years, which is a man hiding in a valley because he couldn't face what he broke." He wiped his face with the heel of his hand, roughly, like a boy. "But yes. If finding her means the truth comes down this mountain like weather—then God forgive me, yes. Let it come."

* * *

Dagny caught up with me at the edge of the yard, where the path began climbing toward the upper terraces and the air smelled of cold stone and juniper. She didn't touch my arm this time. She just walked beside me, matching my stride, letting the silence do its work, and I grudgingly admitted—for the second time since the river crossing—that the woman had instincts.

"He's telling the truth," she said at last.

"I know."

"The vent, the dam, the orders to bury it. It matches fragments I've seen. Redacted memos on the search file. A hydrology annex that was pulled from the report two days after the team went missing. I told myself it was routine classification." She exhaled hard, breath smoking in the dusk. "I told myself a lot of things, Nystrom. It turns out I'm also good at the coward's arithmetic."

"Don't flatter yourself," I said. "You haven't testified against anyone yet. Give it time."

She almost smiled. Then she stopped walking, and I stopped, and below us the lights of Vaskr were coming on one by one in the deepening blue—small warm squares in a valley that official maps insisted was empty. Forty households. A hundred lives, maybe more, built on the deliberate erasure of everything the world used to call them.

"If Sela's team is alive up there," Dagny said quietly, "my orders say I arrest them. All of them. Defection from a government survey is a prosecutable offense, and Holt doesn't get to decide otherwise, and neither do I." She was staring at the lights as she said it, and her jaw was tight. "So before I climb another meter toward that glacier, I need to know which side of me is giving the orders tomorrow. Because right now they're both talking."

"Welcome to the mountain," I said. "Everyone who comes up here ends up arguing with themselves. It's the altitude."

But I wasn't really listening to my own joke, because I was doing my own arithmetic, and it kept coming out wrong no matter how I arranged the numbers. Upstream: a missing team, a dying glacier, a daughter who might be alive. Downstream: four thousand people who didn't know they were living at the bottom of a clock. And here in the middle, standing in the mist with a traitor's grief still wet on the air between us: me, the woman who had once chosen erasure and learned it fit like a coat, being asked now to un-erase everything—Sela, the truth, the valley, and if it came to it, Eirik Halvard himself.

The past had walked out of the mist wearing a familiar face, and it had asked me for a favor, and the terrible thing—the thing I turned over and over as we climbed toward the lamplit houses—was that some starved part of me had wanted, for one bright shameful second, to say no. To let the mountain keep its secret and its missing daughter and its rotting dam, and keep mine too.

Then I thought of Tomas Vey, thirty-one forever, somewhere under the Kestrel ice. Of how silence had been the second-worst thing I'd ever carried.

Above the last terrace, where the path gave out into scree and the glacier's breath came down cold and mineral, I stopped and looked up at the black shoulder of the peak and the white line of the river falling from it, and I said, mostly to myself:

"All right, Sela Halvard. Hold on. I'm coming up to read what you wrote."

Behind us, down in the settlement, a door closed softly, and the valley kept its thousand small sounds, and somewhere high overhead—so high we couldn't see it, so high it might have been imagination—a crack of shifting ice rolled down the dark like a clearing throat.
