## 10. What Sela Found

The pages were damp at the corners, softened by years of mountain air, and Eirik Halvard held them the way a man holds a bird with a broken wing—too carefully, as if pressure alone might finish what the cold had started.

They had been found that morning in a dry-stone cache above Vaskr's eastern terraces, wrapped in oilcloth and sealed with wax, exactly where Sela had told her father she would leave things *if it ever came to leaving*. Maren had carried them down herself, both hands, like an offering. Now Eirik sat alone in the guest hut they'd given him and Orsen, a single tallow lamp guttering between his knees, and he read his daughter's handwriting for the first time in fourteen months.

She wrote small. She always had. Even as a girl filling school exercise books in Brannvik, Sela had compressed her letters as though paper were rationed, as though the world's space were finite and she refused to take more than her share. The habit had survived two survey academies, three expeditions, and whatever had happened to her on this river.

*Day 41,* the first recovered page began.

*The tributary doesn't end at the glacier. I've written to the Directorate twice about this and received form replies citing cartographic error. There is no error. The maps are wrong on purpose. I can prove it now: the water comes up warm.*

Eirik's thumb trembled against the page. He steadied it against his knee.

*We followed the current past the moraine today. Halvard—you'll laugh at me for writing your name into a field journal, but I want you with me on this route even if only in ink—the water is warm. Not tepid. Warm as bathwater, and rising half a degree by my thermometer each day we've been here. There is a smell too, sulfur and old iron, strongest at dawn when the steam hangs in the birches like breath on glass. Rask thinks it's a hot spring. I think it's a throat.*

He laughed then—a single broken sound, quickly smothered—and pressed his knuckles to his mouth. *I want you with me on this route.* She had written him into the mountains because she could not bring him there. Because he had not come. Because twelve years ago he had stood in a Directorate hearing room and said the words that ended Orsen Nystrom's career, and after that his daughter had looked at him differently, and he had told himself the distance was normal, fathers and grown daughters, all of it normal, all of it fine.

*Day 44. Found the source proper. It isn't a spring. It's a vent—a fumarole field under the ice lens, feeding a pool the size of a threshing floor. The glacial dam above it is riddled with melt channels. I've never seen anything like it outside textbooks on volcanic terrain, and there is no volcanic terrain here. Or there wasn't. Something below has woken up. The bedrock hums if you press your palm flat to it. Rask felt it too and went pale and wouldn't speak for an hour.*

*Day 47. Sent my full report downstream with the supply runner. Instrument readings, thermal gradients, dam integrity assessment, projected flood path. If the lens fails, everything from the gorge to Brannvik goes under. Four thousand people, minimum. I used the Directorate's own flood models. I made it undeniable.*

*Day 52. Reply received. Not from my section chief. From Claims Division.*

Eirik stopped reading. He set the pages face-down on his thigh and looked at the lamp flame until his eyes burned, because looking at anything else was worse. Claims Division. He knew the name. Every man who had ever signed a government contract knew the name—the division that turned ore estimates into permits and permits into money and inconvenient science into paperwork that vanished. His own last contract before retirement had carried their stamp in the corner, small and red, like a wax seal on a letter he'd never been meant to open.

He picked the pages up again.

*They have mining claims staked along the lower gorge. Copper and something they're calling trace platinum. The claims are worthless if the valley floods; they are worth millions if nothing is said. My report has been "reclassified pending review." I am instructed to log the vent as a minor geothermal anomaly, non-hazardous, and to continue the survey as scheduled. In writing, they thanked me for my diligence.*

*In writing. They put it in writing, Father. As if they weren't afraid of us at all. As if four thousand people downstream were a rounding error.*

*Day 53. I called a team meeting. I won't detail what was said. Some things belong to the six of us. But I will write down what we decided, because someone should know it was a decision, made awake, in daylight, with our eyes open.*

*We will not falsify the record. We will not sign the reclassification. We will not walk back down that river carrying silence in our packs like ballast. And we cannot go home either—if we file through any official channel, the report dies in the same drawer mine already has, and we become six witnesses to be managed. So we are going to do the only thing left that is neither lie nor surrender. We are going to stay. Above the tree line, out of every map, where no one can order us to forget what we measured. We will keep our instruments running. We will keep records. And someday, somehow, the truth will find its way downhill without us having to hand it to men who will bury it.*

*I don't know how to tell you this part gently, so I won't try. I learned what you did twelve years ago. All of it. What you said in that hearing room about Orsen Nystrom, and what actually happened on the Kestrel traverse, and how close those two things sit together. I'm not writing this to wound you. I'm writing it because I spent three nights deciding whether staying silent here makes me like the men in Claims Division, and I realized the answer depends on whether I ever learn to stop doing what you did—which is choosing the version of the truth that costs you least.*

*So: I choose the version that costs most. I choose exile over complicity. And I choose to believe you might still do the same, if anyone ever gave you the chance.*

*If it ever comes to leaving—for any reason—my records are cached at the east cairn. You always said I inherited your stubbornness. Prove it. Come find them. Come find me.*

*Sela*

The lamp had burned low. Eirik did not remember how long he had sat there. His legs were stiff, his eyes wet without his permission, and the hut around him was so quiet he could hear the settlement breathing—someone splitting wood far off, the river muttering beyond the palisade, a child laughing once and falling silent.

*Come find me.*

He had come. Twelve years too late for one daughter of the mountains and fourteen months too late for another, but he had come, walking into the mist behind Orsen Nystrom of all people, the woman whose ruin he had built with his own testimony. And now the pages lay in his hands telling him that his daughter had known everything—had weighed his sin on her survey scales and found it heavy—and had chosen, at the end, to leave him a door anyway.

He read the last page again. Then, because grief needed somewhere to go, he folded the oilcloth with the exactness she would have used, and went to find Orsen.

* * *

Orsen was on the terrace wall above the eastern fields, watching the light fail over the glacier, when Eirik's shadow fell across the stones. She'd heard him coming long before—his gait had changed since the gorge, favoring the left knee, a hitch she could have mapped blind—but she let him arrive in his own time. That was trail courtesy. You didn't rush a man climbing toward something hard.

He held out the oilcloth bundle without preamble. "Sela's records. They were cached at the east cairn." His voice was scraped raw. "You should read them. Both of us should."

Orsen took the bundle and unwrapped it there in the blue dusk, tilting the pages toward the last of the light. She read fast—years of route notes and client itineraries had taught her to strip text for essentials—and what she stripped out of Sela Halvard's careful small hand made the cold climb up her arms and settle in her chest.

A vent beneath the source. A glacial dam honeycombed with melt channels. Thermal readings climbing daily. A flood model pointing straight down the gorge at Brannvik and every farmstead between. And then the reply from Claims Division, logged and stamped, ordering the hazard reclassified into oblivion so that copper and trace platinum could sleep undisturbed in the dark.

"They knew," Orsen said. It came out flat, which surprised her; she had expected anger and found something colder underneath it. "Downstream knew. They had her measurements in a drawer and they chose the claims."

"She chose us," Eirik said. "Her team. Six people who looked at a promotion and a pension on one side and four thousand strangers on the other, and walked away from everything they had rather than carry the lie." He sat down heavily on the wall beside her, the pages trembling in his grip. "I've spent the day trying to understand it. Trying to imagine standing in that meeting. And the terrible thing, Orsen—the thing I can't get out of my chest—is that I understand the other choice perfectly. I've made it. I know exactly how a person talks themselves into the smaller truth. I did it in a hearing room with a cup of coffee going cold in front of me."

Orsen said nothing. Twelve years ago she had rehearsed a hundred responses to this confession—fury, contempt, the cold professional dismantling of his testimony—and now that it was being handed to her freely, under a dying sky, none of the rehearsals fit. What she felt instead was vertigo, the kind you got leaning over a cornice: the ground of her own grievances suddenly dropping away to show how much farther down it went.

Because she recognized the shape of it. That was the worst part. She had spent twelve years telling herself she was nothing like the men in the Directorate, nothing like Eirik—and yet she had built a life out of erasure too. Off-grid, off-record, off every map she could manage. She had called it freedom. Sitting here, reading a dead woman's insistence that silence was never neutral, she wondered how much of her solitude had been principle and how much had been the same arithmetic Eirik had done in that hearing room: choosing the version of the truth that cost her least.

"Your daughter was braver than both of us," Orsen said finally.

"Yes."

"And smarter than the entire Claims Division."

"That too." He almost smiled; it broke apart halfway. "She kept measuring. Did you see? Right up to the last page—thermal gradients, channel widths, flow rates. They exiled themselves and she *kept taking readings*, because someday someone honest would need the data." He turned to look at her, and in the dusk his eyes were wet and steady at once. "Someone honest. That's you, Orsen. That's why I begged you onto this river. Not just because you know the terrain. Because Sela wrote *someday someone* and I've been carrying those words like a compass bearing ever since."

Orsen looked down at the pages in her lap. The last lines of the final entry were smudged, as if written in haste or weather, but legible enough:

*Thermal gradient +0.4°C overnight. Channel C widened another meter. I've flagged the east moraine as unstable. Whatever happens next, happen fast.*

Happen fast. Orsen felt the words land in her body the way a bridge feels weight—not heard so much as loaded. She had crossed enough rotten snow bridges to know the sensation: the moment the surface stops being ground and becomes a countdown.

"How recent are these?" she asked. "The last entries."

"The cache was sealed fourteen months ago. But Maren says the vent's been louder this season. Steam on days it never steamed before. The terraces above the source shed a rockslide in the spring that nobody could explain." Eirik's jaw worked. "Fourteen months of a warming throat under a rotting dam, and nobody downstream counting."

Orsen rose. Below them, Vaskr was lighting its evening fires—one, two, seven small flames in a bowl of mountains that appeared on no chart, sheltering people who had traded their names for safety, guarding a secret that was now, by Sela's arithmetic, a weapon aimed at the valley. Somewhere in that settlement, Maren was deciding—or refusing to decide—what those pages meant for everyone here.

"We take these to the council," Orsen said. "Tonight. Not tomorrow."

Eirik looked up at her. "Maren will resist. You've seen how they live. This valley is the whole world to them."

"I know." Orsen folded the oilcloth over the pages with a care that surprised her, the way you fold a flag or bandage a wound. "But I've read enough dead climbers' journals to know how they all end the same way. With a warning somebody decided could wait until morning."

She tucked the bundle inside her jacket, against her ribs, where a heartbeat could keep it company, and started down the terrace steps toward the firelight—toward the council of ghosts, and the argument that would split this hidden world open like a seam.

Behind her, the glacier ticked and settled in the dark, the way ice does when it is thinking about letting go.
