## 11. Council of Ghosts

The longhouse smelled of spruce smoke and wet wool, and Maren Vaskr stood at the head of the trestle table and counted her people the way she had counted them for forty years: not by name but by the sound of their breathing.

Thirty-one souls in the room. Two more outside on watch, though watch was a word that had gone soft with disuse—no one had come up the river in decades who wasn't meant to. The lamps guttered in the draft from the door Orsen Nystrom had left ajar behind her, and Maren did not tell anyone to close it. Some doors, once opened, were better left open. It gave the illusion you could still walk through them.

She had known this night would come. She had known it the way you know a storm is coming—not from any single sign but from the pressure behind your teeth, the way the dogs go quiet, the way the river below the terraces runs too clear, too cold, as if it is holding its breath. For three days now the ground had been murmuring under Vaskr. Not shaking. Murmuring. A low complaint in the bones of the valley, felt in the soles of the feet at dawn, in the water barrel at noon where the surface shivered without wind.

Sela Halvard's team had heard it first, months ago, and had done what outsiders always did with such knowledge—they had written it down.

Maren looked at the journal lying on the table between the bread board and the lamp. Its cover was dark with old water stains, its spine cracked like a knuckle. She had not touched it since they brought it down from the cache above the falls. She would not touch it tonight either. It was not hers to hold.

"Sit," she said, and her people sat.

* * *

They had built the longhouse the year Maren turned nineteen, felling the spruce themselves, hauling it on sledges over the frozen river because no road led here and none ever would. Her father had laid the first sill beam and said, *This is the last thing we build for other people to see. Everything after this, we build for ourselves.* He had meant it as a promise. Tonight it sounded like an indictment.

The benches filled. Old Tomas with his ruined hands folded in his lap. The Lindqvist sisters, who had come upriver as debt-fleeing girls thirty years back and now had gray in their braids. Young Ivo, born here, who had never held a document with his true name on it because he had never needed one. Petra Sorel, who kept the seed cellars and whose face was already set in the expression of a woman preparing to lose something. And at the far end, apart from the others, Eirik Halvard—gray-bearded, hollow-eyed, sitting like a man who had been given back a chair he forfeited twelve years ago and could not decide whether to sit in it or kneel beside it.

Maren let the silence do its work. Silence was a tool in Vaskr; they sharpened it the way lowlanders sharpened steel. In a settlement of people who had erased themselves, silence was the only inheritance passed intact from parent to child.

"You all know why we're here," she said at last. "Some of you have known longer than others. I'll say it plainly, because plainly is all we have left." She nodded toward the journal. "The vent beneath the source is failing. The ice dam above it is cracking. When it goes—and our guest says it will go within days—the meltwater comes down this valley and into the Brannvik basin. Four thousand people live in the basin. They don't know. Their government knows and has chosen not to tell them, because telling them costs more than burying them."

Petra made a small sound, quickly swallowed.

"We have three choices," Maren went on. "We can stay silent. We can warn the towns ourselves and vanish deeper—which there is nowhere deeper to vanish to. Or we can come down out of these mountains with our faces showing and our names in our mouths, and help evacuate the basin, and end Vaskr forever."

She said *end Vaskr* and watched the word land in each of them differently. In Tomas it landed like a stone into deep water—a ripple, then stillness. In the Lindqvist sisters it landed like a slap. In young Ivo it landed like nothing at all, because he had never been taught what Vaskr cost to build; he had only been taught how to keep it.

"Before anyone speaks," Maren said, "I want us to remember why we are here. All of us came up this river running from something. Debt. War. Testimony. Shame. Names that other people had used against us until the name was the wound itself. We built a place where a person could put down their name like a heavy pack and never pick it up again. Forty years, no census has counted us. No tax roll. No court record. No grave marker with a surname on it—we burn our dead and scatter them in the current, so even the earth forgets us. That was the covenant. That was the whole covenant." She spread her hands. "And now the mountain is asking us what the covenant is worth."

* * *

Tomas spoke first, because Tomas always spoke first—he said it kept the meeting honest to start with the oldest fear in the room.

"I ran from the conscription officers when I was seventeen," he said, his ruined hands turning over each other. "I've been dead on paper for fifty-one years. My mother died thinking I drowned in the Lysfjord crossing. If we go down that mountain and show our faces, I'm alive again. And if I'm alive again, then everything I did to survive being dead—it was for nothing." He shook his head slowly. "I didn't survive fifty-one years of invisibility to die visible."

"No one's asking you to die," said Ivo.

"Everyone's asking me to be seen. It's the same thing, boy, at my age."

The Lindqvist sisters spoke together, as they always did, finishing each other's grievances like a single sentence split between two mouths. They had owed money in Brannvik itself—owed it to a fish merchant with a ledger and a memory like a trapline. "You want us to walk into the town square," said the elder sister, "past his shop."

"With our faces bare," said the younger.

Maren listened and did not interrupt. This was the discipline of the council: every ghost got to name its haunting before anyone voted on exorcism.

But it was Petra Sorel who cut to the bone, as Petra always did, because Petra thought in seasons and cellars and could not help applying that arithmetic to everything.

"How many in the basin?" she asked.

"Four thousand. Perhaps forty-five hundred with the spring fishing camps."

"And how many are we?"

"Thirty-three."

Petra nodded slowly, and Maren saw her doing the division, the terrible clean arithmetic of it. "Then the question isn't whether we owe them anything," Petra said. "The question is whether thirty-three lives spent invisible are worth more than four thousand lives spent visible. Because that's the trade. That's what's on the table. Our whole way of life—" she gestured at the beams, the smoke, the lamplight, the forty years of it, "—against theirs. And I hate that it's even a question. But someone has to say it out loud: it *is* a question. There are people in this room whose entire existence depends on not existing. You're asking them to un-exist. Politely. For strangers."

"They're not strangers," Eirik Halvard said.

Every head turned. He had not spoken since they'd seated him, and his voice came out rusted, like a hinge on a door long shut.

"The people in the basin aren't strangers," he said again, standing. "Brannvik is a mill town and a fishing town and half its children have never seen a map of the highlands, fine. But Sela grew up in a town like it. Before—" his voice caught, and he mastered it the way a man masters a horse that wants to bolt, "—before she chose this place. Before she chose *you*. She wrote me that the reason she stayed, the reason her whole team stayed, was that this valley proved a person could live without being owned. And now the mountain under your valley is going to kill four thousand people downstream, and you're going to sit in your beautiful longhouse and debate whether your anonymity is worth more than their children." His hand flattened on the table, next to the journal, not touching it. "My daughter gave up her name, her career, her country, to keep this place secret. Do you think she'd want it kept at that price? She's down there somewhere—or she was. Every day we spend debating is a day the ice dam spends cracking."

"Eirik." Maren kept her voice level. "Sit down."

"I'll stand. I've earned standing. I spent twelve years sitting in comfortable rooms while a better person than me carried the blame for my cowardice." He looked around the table, and Maren saw that he was not speaking to the council anymore; he was speaking to the room inside himself. "I know what it is to protect a lie because the truth costs too much. I testified falsely against Orsen Nystrom because the truth would have ended me, and I told myself the lie was survivable—for her, for everyone—as long as nobody looked too closely. Twelve years. You know what looking closely costs? It costs exactly this night. It costs one night, eventually, where you can't not look. Your covenant is a beautiful thing. I lived outside covenants my whole life and I'd have traded anything for one. But a covenant that requires four thousand drownings isn't a covenant. It's just a bigger lie, with better scenery."

The silence after that was total. Even the fire seemed to lean in.

* * *

Maren let it stand for a long moment. Then she walked to the window—there was only one window, north-facing, shuttered—and opened the shutter, because she wanted them to see what they were debating about.

Beyond the glass, the valley lay under a sky scoured clean by wind, stars hard as nailheads. The terraces fell away in dark steps toward the river, and beyond the river the gorge walls rose, and beyond the gorge, invisible, sixty miles downstream, the basin with its mills and its schoolhouse and its four thousand sleeping lives. And above it all, past the headwall, the glacier that had no official name either—because maps stopped at the tree line here, the way consciences stop at convenience.

"Do you know what happens if we go down?" she said, not turning around. "Not the noble version. The real version. I've thought about nothing else for three days, so hear it. We descend. Thirty-three people who officially do not exist walk into Brannvik during a storm and start shouting about a flood. Who are we? Madmen. Squatters. Agents of some foreign power—take your pick; frightened towns believe anything. Half of them won't listen. The officials certainly won't listen, because listening means admitting their own survey teams knew and said nothing, which means scandal, which means they will bury us before they bury the valley. So then what? We give our names. Real names, old names, names with debts and warrants and desertions attached. The Lindqvists get arrested in the square. Tomas gets shipped to a tribunal. Every person in this room gets processed, catalogued, prosecuted or pardoned according to rules written by the same government that buried the flood warning to protect mining claims." She turned from the window. "That's the best case. The worst case is we sacrifice everything and they drown anyway, because we were thirty-three voices shouting into a storm, and storms eat voices."

"And if we stay silent?" Ivo asked quietly.

"Then the dam fails, the basin floods, and four thousand people die believing the mountain simply betrayed them. And we live." Her mouth tasted like ash saying it. "We live up here with the terraces and the goats and the clean river, and every spring thereafter, when the snowmelt comes down, we will know what else came down that current one year. We will know it every day for the rest of our lives. That is the price of silence. It doesn't feel like anything at first. That's the trick of it. It feels like safety. And then one morning you're an old woman watering your garden and you can't remember why your hands shake, except that you can, you always can."

"Then what are you telling us, Maren?" Petra demanded. "You've argued both sides. Which side are *you* on?"

Maren came back to the head of the table. She looked at each face in turn—Tomas and his fifty-one dead years, the sisters and their trapline creditor, Ivo who had never owned a name, Petra with her seed cellars full of next spring, Eirik standing like a man at a graveside of his own digging.

"I'm telling you what my father told me when we laid the first beam," she said. "*Everything after this, we build for ourselves.* And I believed him. I've kept this place like a flame cupped in two hands for forty years. But a flame cupped in two hands gives no light to anyone else, and there comes a night when you have to open your hands and see what you actually wanted the fire *for*." She drew a breath that hurt on the way in. "Orsen Nystrom asked me yesterday why we erased ourselves. I told her: to be free. She looked at me the way you look at a child who has described a cage and called it a bird. And she was right. We are not free up here. We are hidden. Those are different countries, and I have been confusing them for four decades."

"So you'd give it up," Tomas said. "Just like that."

"Not just like that. With both hands breaking open. Yes." She looked at him steadily. "Yes, Tomas. Because I have read Sela Halvard's journal, and I have read the last page of it twice, and I will tell you what she wrote, because it belongs to all of us now. She wrote: *If staying hidden means letting them die, then the hiding was never a home. It was just a hole we fit into.*"

Nobody moved. Outside, far up the valley, so faint it might have been imagination, the mountain murmured again—a long subterranean syllable, the glacier shifting its weight, the vent exhaling heat into the dark under the ice. The lamps trembled. The water in the bucket by the door shivered in rings.

Eirik sat down slowly, like a man whose legs had finally been permitted to fail.

"Vote," Maren said. "All who would go down the mountain with faces bare and names spoken, stand."

One heartbeat. Two.

Tomas rose first, groaning, his ruined hands braced on the bench, and that broke something loose in the room—the elder Lindqvist sister pulled her sister up by the arm, and Ivo was already on his feet, and Petra stood with her jaw tight and her eyes wet, and one by one, like a field of grain bending to the same wind, the council of ghosts stood up and became, for the first time in forty years, a crowd of the living.

Only then did the door open, letting in a blade of cold air and lamplight-spill, and Orsen Nystrom stood in it with snow melting off her shoulders and an expression Maren had learned to dread on the faces of messengers.

"You need to hear this," Orsen said. "Now. The dam's cracked through. We have two days. Maybe less."

Behind her, Dagny Holt stepped into the light, and Maren saw the radio in the officer's hands, and saw by her face that whatever it had transmitted, it had not been enough.
