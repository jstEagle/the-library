## 12. The Tremor

The mountain spoke at dawn.

Not a sound so much as a shift—a long, subterranean groan that came up through the bedrock and into the bones of anyone standing on it. Orsen felt it before she heard it, a tremor in her boot soles while she was still half-asleep on the ledge above Vaskr, wrapped in wool that smelled of woodsmoke and someone else's life. Then the world tilted, gently, like a boat riding a swell, and somewhere far above them something enormous let go.

She was on her feet before her mind caught up. Twelve years of guiding had built reflexes deeper than thought: assess, anchor, count your people. Dagny was already awake, sitting up in her bedroll with her eyes wide and her hand pressed flat against the stone as if she could read it by touch. Below them, the terraces of Vaskr were stirring—lamplights blooming in windows, figures spilling out of doorways into the blue pre-dawn cold, faces turned upward toward the glacier.

"Was that—" Dagny started.

"That was the mountain deciding something," Orsen said. "Get your boots."

They climbed. Of course they climbed; that was the whole sickness of people like her, people who heard catastrophe and walked toward it. The trail out of Vaskr's hidden bowl switchbacked up through scree fields still silvered with frost, past cairns marked with those strange spiral symbols Sela Halvard had drawn in the last pages of her journal. Eirik fell in behind them without being asked, moving with a limping urgency that made Orsen's chest ache in a way she refused to examine yet. He had not slept either. None of them had, not really, since the council had broken apart two nights ago with no decision reached—only Maren's flat warning hanging in the air like smoke: *the moment we are seen, we are gone.*

The quake had changed the route. A section of the upper trail had slumped into a gully, and they picked their way across fresh rubble that still smelled of crushed lichen and deep ice. And then they rounded the shoulder of the ridge, and Orsen stopped walking, because there was nothing left to say to her legs.

The glacial dam—the great tongue of ice that had held back the meltwater lake for centuries, maybe millennia, the silent wall at the head of the unnamed river—was cracked.

Not a fissure. Not a seam. A *wound*. It ran diagonally across the entire face of the glacier, wide enough in places that she could see blue light glowing through it from within, the way light comes through a closed eyelid. Meltwater was already seeping from its base in a dozen new springs, threading silver down the rock, feeding the river below with a hunger it hadn't had yesterday.

"The vent," Eirik said hoarsely. He pointed—not at the crack but beneath it, at the ground itself, where a faint shimmer rose off the snow. Steam. Thin as breath on glass, but unmistakable. "Sela wrote about this. The heat's been building for years. The ice thins from underneath, and then—"

"And then the mountain shrugs," Orsen finished quietly. "And everything it's holding back goes downhill."

She did the arithmetic the way she did everything else: automatically, ruthlessly, without mercy for herself. She could see the basin behind the dam—or rather, she could see where it must be, hidden behind the ice wall, a lake of cold meltwater the size of a small sea, waiting. The crack would widen. Water would find it the way water finds everything. First a trickle, then a tunnel, then the whole rotten architecture of the glacier would slump inward and a hundred million tons of water would come down the gorge, through the high valley, into the lowlands where Brannvik slept behind its levees with its eleven hundred souls and its Sunday markets and its children who had never once thought about the source of their river.

"Days," she said aloud. Her voice sounded strange in the thin air, stripped of everything but fact. "Maybe four. Maybe six if we're lucky and the cold holds. Not weeks. I'd stake my name on it, and my name is finally worth something again."

Dagny was writing in her field book, fast, her breath pluming. When she looked up, her face had the particular gray set that Orsen had learned to read as *officer doing math she doesn't like.* "Brannvik can't be evacuated in four days on a rumor. They'll want verification. Instrument readings. An official report."

"Then we get them verification." Orsen crouched at the edge of the meltwater stream and put her bare hand in it—just for a second, just long enough. Warmer than it should have been. Noticeably warmer. "There's your reading. That water came out from under the dam twenty minutes ago and it's warm. Write it down. Date it. Sign it. You're a survey officer; your signature means more than mine does."

"My signature," Dagny said, and then stopped. Something moved behind her eyes—guilt, or fear, or both wearing each other's coats—and Orsen saw it happen, saw the sentence die before it was born.

"What?" Orsen said. "What aren't you telling me?"

Dagny closed the field book. Opened it again. Closed it. Eirik stood a few paces off, pretending to study the glacier, which was a kindness, and also a lie, because he wasn't moving at all.

"There's a second page to my orders," Dagny said finally. "I told you the mission was to find the team. That was true. But the full text—" She exhaled through her teeth. "The full text authorizes me to detain any personnel found to have deserted their posts. To secure them for transport and return them for court-martial proceedings. Six names. Sela Halvard first among them." She looked at Eirik's back, then away, as if looking at him directly while saying it might burn something. "It's dated three days after they vanished. Before anyone knew what happened to them. Someone in the ministry decided the outcome in advance and sent me to make it official."

The wind came off the glacier and cut between them, carrying the smell of stone and ancient ice and, faintly, impossibly, sulfur.

"So you weren't sent to rescue them," Orsen said.

"I was sent to bury them." Dagny's voice didn't waver, but her knuckles were white around the field book. "Rescue was the cover story. If they were alive, I arrest them. If they were dead, I confirm it and close the file. Either way, the mining claims downstream stay clean, the report stays quiet, and nobody upstream ever has to hear about whatever Sela found." She laughed, one dry note with no humor in it anywhere. "I've been carrying arrest warrants for ghosts. For people who walked barefoot into the snow rather than sign their names to a lie."

Eirik turned around. His face was terrible and calm, the calm of a man who had already lost everything once and found out it hadn't killed him. "My daughter chose exile over complicity," he said. "And your government wrote the paperwork assuming she'd need to be punished for it. Do you understand now, Officer Holt? Do you understand what she understood? There is no version of duty that serves them. There is only the version that serves the truth, and it will cost you everything you've measured yourself against."

"I know," Dagnen said—Dagny, Orsen corrected herself, Dagny—quietly. "I've known since the campsite. Since the boots lined up neat as churchgoers. I've been walking toward this conversation for two weeks and I still don't know which side of it I'm standing on."

"You're standing on the ice," Orsen said. "Same as the rest of us. And the ice is cracking."

* * *

They descended to Vaskr as the sun cleared the eastern ridge and lit the whole basin gold, which felt like an obscenity. Light like that belonged over weddings, over harvests, not over a wound in a glacier counting down its own collapse.

Maren met them at the gate of the lower terrace. She was seventy if she was a day, straight-backed as a pine, with a face weathered into something older than age—the face of a woman who had erased herself so thoroughly that even her expressions seemed to belong to no one. Her eyes went from Orsen's face to the ridge line above, reading the news there before a word was spoken.

"How long?" she asked.

"Days," Orsen said. "Four. Six at the outside."

Maren nodded slowly, the way people nod at a diagnosis they've been dreading for years. Behind her, the settlement was waking properly now—smoke rising from chimneys, the smell of bread, a child laughing somewhere near the goat pens, oblivious, ordinary, alive. Forty-one souls. Orsen had counted. Counting was what she did instead of sleeping.

"You'll want us to leave," Maren said.

"I want you to live," Orsen said. "Those aren't the same thing, but they're closer than they used to be."

"They're not close at all." Maren's voice stayed level, but her hands, folded at her waist, tightened on each other. "You know what we are here. You know what we gave up. Every person behind this fence came here because the world took something from them and demanded a signature in exchange. We don't exist. That is our wealth, our walls, our whole country—and you're asking us to spend it in an afternoon."

"I'm asking you to spend it before the water spends it for you," Orsen said. "Maren. I've lived the way you live. Off the maps, off the records, off the grid of other people's knowing. I built a whole life out of being unfindable, and I'll tell you what I learned: it keeps you safe from everything except the truth. And the truth is coming down that gorge whether you're anonymous when it arrives or not."

For a long moment the elder said nothing. Somewhere below, the child laughed again, and Maren flinched—actually flinched, a small motion around the eyes, as though the laugh had struck her like a thrown stone.

"Eirik," she said at last, without turning. "You've been quiet."

He stepped forward. "My daughter is down there, Maren. In your valley. Whatever she found, whatever she ran from, she's one of yours now, and the flood doesn't care about fences or false names. You taught me that yourself, the night you took me in. You said: *the mountain doesn't check papers.*"

"The mountain doesn't," Maren agreed. "But the government does. And the day forty-one of us walk down into Brannvik, every debt, every warrant, every old name comes due at once." She looked at Dagny then, directly, and Orsen watched the two women measure each other—elder and officer, secrecy and state. "And you. What do you do with us, when we come down? Your orders say detain."

Dagny held her gaze. "My orders," she said, "are paper. The water is real. I'll stand between you and the paper as long as I'm able, and when I'm not able anymore, I'll stand next to you anyway."

Something shifted in Maren's face—not softening exactly, but a recalculation, the way a guide recalculates a route when the bridge is out. "You speak like a woman burning her own file," she said.

"I've seen what's in mine," Dagny said. "It burns easy."

* * *

Orsen spent the afternoon doing what she did best and hated most: measuring.

She climbed alone to the spur above the dam with a notebook and a surveyor's eye borrowed from twelve years of reading terrain for rich men's egos, and she mapped the failure before it happened. The crack's width, paced and estimated. The seepage rate, timed against a falling trickle of meltwater off her boot toe. The gorge profile downstream—she knew it from memory, from the descent she'd planned with Eirik two nights ago, every narrows and pool and choke point between the source and Brannvik. She drew it all in pencil, fast, her fingers stiff with cold, and the numbers came together the way numbers do when they've already decided what they want to say.

Six days if the weather stayed cold. Four if a warm front came through—and there was a warm front coming; she could feel it in the wind's wrongness, smell it in the air off the south. Three, maybe, if the vent kept heating the lake from below, eating the dam's foundations the way rot eats a pier.

Three days to convince a valley town to believe a blacklisted guide, a disgraced officer, and forty-one people who officially did not exist.

She sat on the spur until the light went long and red, and she let herself feel it—all of it, the whole weight at once, which she almost never allowed. Because every path forward cost someone everything. Warn the towns, and Vaskr died as surely as if the water took it—died into ledgers and hearings and men with clipboards. Stay silent, and eleven hundred people downstream drowned in their beds with the river's true name still unwritten on any map. Arrest the defectors, and Dagny kept her career and lost whatever was growing between her and the truth. Refuse, and Dagny lost everything and gained only the right to say she'd been right.

Even forgiveness cost. Even love. Especially those.

Orsen had spent twelve years believing that if she just kept moving, kept counting miles, she could outrun the ledger—that distance was a currency she could pay her debts in. Sitting on the spur with the cracked glacier glowing blue in the dusk, she understood at last that the ledger had caught up. The mountain had brought it all the way up here, to the roof of the world, and set it down in front of her, open, with the pen beside it.

Every choice costs someone everything. Fine. Then choose, and pay, and stop pretending there was a route that didn't cross the river.

She clicked her headlamp on and descended.

* * *

Vaskr's longhouse was full when she came in—fuller than the council two nights ago, because this time no one pretended it wasn't a war council. Lamplight on forty-one faces. Farmers and ex-surveyors and a former schoolteacher and a man who had once, improbably, been a customs inspector in a country none of them named anymore. People who had crossed oceans and burned passports to be invisible, now gathered to discuss becoming visible forever.

Maren stood at the head of the table. Eirik sat at her right hand. Dagny stood apart by the door, field book under her arm, neither inside the community nor outside it—suspended, like Orsen, between worlds.

"We have three days," Orsen said, when the room settled. "Possibly four. Here's what the water will do, and here's what it will reach." She unrolled her pencil map on the table and weighted the corners with stones, and she talked—about the gorge, the narrows, the timing, the surge height at Brannvik, the levees that would hold for ten minutes and fail in the eleventh. She talked until her voice went raw, and the room listened the way mountain people listen, which is completely and without hope of being lied to.

When she finished, the silence had a texture to it, dense as wool.

"It's Brannvik, then," said a woman near the back—an old voice, steady. "Eleven hundred strangers, or forty-one of us. That's the arithmetic."

"It's not arithmetic," Eirik said. "It never was. Sela didn't run because of arithmetic. She ran because someone asked her to treat human beings as a line item, and she wouldn't. If we stay silent to save ourselves, we become the ministry. We become the thing we all walked away from." He looked around the table, and his voice broke, just slightly, on the last words. "My daughter is down there. In the water's path. I am asking you to save my daughter, and I am ashamed of how small that request sounds next to eleven hundred."

"No one here thinks it small," Maren said quietly.

"Then decide," Orsen said. "Tonight. Because tomorrow morning, rain or not, I'm going down the gorge, and I would rather walk into Brannvik with forty-one witnesses behind me than alone. But I'll go alone if I have to. I've done most things alone. It's the one skill the mountains ever taught me that I wish I could forget."

Maren looked at her for a long time. Around the room, hands began to rise—not all at once, not unanimously, but one by one, like candles being lit, until Orsen stopped counting because the number had stopped mattering.

"Tomorrow at first light," Maren said at last, and her voice carried the weight of a woman signing her own erasure in reverse. "We break camp. We take the low trail. And God forgive us all—we go down and give them our names."

Dagny pushed off from the doorframe and opened her field book. "Then I'd better start writing down everyone's real ones," she said. "Somebody has to keep the record. That part, at least, is still my job."

Outside, the wind had turned. Orsen stood in the doorway and felt it on her face—warm, wet, southern, wrong—and above the dark shape of the ridge, the glacier waited with its blue-lit wound, and the clock in the mountain ticked on, patient as water, certain as gravity.

Three days. Maybe less.

She went in to help pack.
