## 13. Terms of Silence

The radio weighed less than a dead bird.

Dagny Holt had carried it for eleven days now, strapped to the outside of her pack where the cold could find it, and every night she told herself the same lie: *tomorrow*. Tomorrow she would key the handset and report position. Tomorrow she would give the Regional Office what it wanted—a clean summary, a tidy narrative, six missing surveyors presumed defected, pursuit ongoing, no complications worth mentioning.

Tomorrow kept not coming.

She sat in the lee of a granite shelf above Vaskr's terraces, where the settlement's woodsmoke rose in a thin gray thread that no official map would ever explain, and turned the handset over in her gloved hands like something she'd stolen from a church. Which, in a way, she had. The radio belonged to the Survey Directorate. Its frequencies belonged to the Directorate. Its silence—eleven days of it—belonged to her.

Below, through the blue dusk of the high valley, lights were coming on in Vaskr's windows. Small ones. Oil lamps, probably; she'd seen no power lines, no generators, nothing but water channels cut with a patience that made her think of monks. Forty-one people, Maren had said, though Dagny suspected the number was soft at both ends. Forty-one people who had walked out of their own lives the way Sela Halvard's team had walked out of their boots: deliberately, barefoot into snow, leaving everything orderly behind them.

*Defectors.* That was the word in her orders. Typed on Directorate letterhead, stamped twice, signed by a man whose name she'd had to look up because he'd been promoted twice since her file last crossed his desk. *If contact is established with members of the missing survey team, detain and transport under Section 9 protocols. Do not publicize. Do not engage local authorities.*

Section 9. She'd looked that up too, on the second night, by headlamp, with Orsen asleep ten feet away breathing slow and even as tide. It was the section for people who embarrassed the state. Not criminals—the criminal sections came later, with their own paperwork—but embarrassments. People whose existence required management.

And what did that make her, sitting here with a radio she hadn't keyed in eleven days?

A complication worth mentioning.

* * *

She'd drafted the report forty times. She knew because she wrote them in her head the way other people counted sheep, lying awake in whatever shelter the night offered—in the tent, in the ruined bothy below the tree line, in the guest hut Vaskr had given them that smelled of birch smoke and dried fish—and each draft died before it reached its final sentence.

The early drafts were obedient. *Team located. Six individuals in good health, residing voluntarily among an unregistered settlement approximately 40 kilometers upstream of the mapped headwaters. Awaiting instructions.* Clean. Professional. The kind of language that would get a junior officer a commendation and get six people loaded onto a transport under Section 9, and get a valley full of oil lamps burned to the ground or worse, paved over with a road and a mining easement, because that was what happened to places that appeared on maps.

The middle drafts were honest, which was worse. *Superiors ordered Sela Halvard to suppress geothermal instability data to protect downstream mining claims. Team refused. Recommend immediate independent verification of vent activity; recommend evacuation assessment for Brannvik and river towns.* Honest drafts didn't have endings either, because an honest draft ended with her name on it, and her name traced back through three signatures to the same office that had buried Sela's findings, and honest reports from inconvenient places had a way of dying in filing cabinets while their authors drowned in rivers or fell off ridges or simply stopped being mentioned in meetings.

The late drafts weren't reports at all. They were warnings. *Glacial dam above source destabilizing. Meltwater pooling. Estimated failure window: days. Evacuate Brannvik immediately.* No signature required, technically. A partial transmission, stripped of callsign context if she was careful, stripped of anything that pointed home. A warning that would save thousands and end her career the moment anyone traced the frequency—which they would, because she was the only field officer unaccounted for in this sector, and the Directorate tracked its radios the way shepherds tracked sheep.

Eleven days of drafts. And tonight, finally, the reason she'd stopped drafting.

Because tonight she had stood at the edge of Vaskr's upper terrace and watched a woman named Ingrid teach children to read using letters scratched in ash on a slate, and one of the children had been Sela Halvard's youngest teammate, a surveyor named Pell who couldn't have been older than twenty-four, laughing at his own terrible handwriting. And Maren had stood beside Dagny in the cold and said, not unkindly: "You keep looking at your pack, officer. Whatever's in there, it's getting heavier."

Maren was right. It was getting heavier. And tomorrow the meltwater behind the glacial dam would be heavier still.

Dagny thumbed the handset's safety cover open. The click sounded enormous in the dark.

* * *

"You're going to do it."

Orsen Nystrom's voice came out of the shadow behind the granite shelf, low and even, and Dagny's heart slammed once against her ribs before her training caught up and told her what she already knew: that a mountain guide who could read avalanche slopes could certainly read a woman slipping away from camp with a radio.

She didn't turn around. "How long have you been standing there?"

"Long enough." Orsen came around the rock and settled beside her, folding down onto the stone with the economy of movement that still astonished Dagny—twelve years disgraced, twelve years alone, and the woman moved like her body had never once doubted itself. "Long enough to watch you open the case and close it twice."

"I closed it once."

"You thought about closing it twice." Orsen nodded at the handset. "That counts up here. Everything counts double past the tree line."

The valley breathed below them. Somewhere down in Vaskr a door shut, and the sound traveled up the rock walls and arrived softened, almost gentle. Above them the glacier held its pooled lake the way a cupped hand holds water—Dagny had seen it that morning from the ridge, a new lake where no lake should be, gray-green and rising, pressing against the ice dam with the patience of something that had all the time in the world until suddenly it wouldn't.

"I'm going to lose my career," Dagny said. It wasn't what she'd meant to say first. What she'd meant to say first was some version of *this doesn't concern you*, some professional wall, but eleven days in the mountains with Orsen Nystrom had dismantled her walls brick by brick without her noticing, the way water dismantles things, without violence, just persistence. "If I transmit—if anyone traces it—I'm finished. Twenty years. I joined the Survey Service at nineteen. I've never—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "I've never done anything that wasn't in the manual."

"And the manual says let Brannvik drown."

"The manual says report accurately through channels." Dagny heard how it sounded even as she said it, the dead bureaucratic music of it, and something in her chest cracked along a seam she hadn't known was there. "Channels. God. I've been saying *channels* like it's a word that means anything. Channels are how Sela Halvard's warning got buried. Channels are why there's a lake above our heads and a town full of people downstream who think spring is coming like always."

Orsen said nothing. That was the thing about her—she had the discipline to say nothing, to let silence do the work that lesser interrogators did with questions. Dagny had watched her use it on Eirik, on Maren, on the terrified young man named Pell. She'd assumed it was a guide's patience. Now, sitting in the dark with her career balanced on her knees, she understood it was also a kindness. Orsen was letting her hear herself.

"My father was a lockkeeper," Dagny said, to the valley, to the radio, to nobody. "On the canal system, south. Thirty-one years opening and closing gates so the barges could pass. He used to tell me: the water doesn't care about your schedule, girl. Your job isn't to control it. Your job is to be honest about where it wants to go, and then decide whether you're going to stand in front of it." She laughed, short and joyless. "I became a survey officer because I thought maps were a way of being honest about water. Where it goes. What it threatens. I thought I'd spend my life telling the truth about terrain."

"And instead?"

"Instead I spent eleven years learning which truths survive filing." She turned the handset over one more time. The frequency dial caught starlight. "Sela found a vent that's going to kill a valley. She reported it through channels. Her superiors sat on it to protect mining royalties. And the Directorate sent me—not to verify her data, not to assess the hazard—to confirm defection and manage the embarrassment. They sent me to make six honest people disappear properly."

"They sent you," Orsen said, "because you're good."

"They sent me because I'm obedient. There's a difference, and I'm only learning it now, at thirty-eight, in a valley that isn't on any map, next to a woman whose file I was carrying as leverage." Dagny shook her head. "I read your file, you know. Before I came to your cabin. Every page. I told myself it was due diligence. It was ammunition. I was going to trade your name back to you like it was mine to sell."

"It was theirs to sell. You just carried the receipt." Orsen's voice stayed level, but something moved underneath it, deep water under ice. "What changed?"

Dagny looked at her. Really looked—at the wind-chapped face, the gray starting at her temples, the eyes that had watched a client die in whitewater and carried it for twelve years without once setting it down. "Cold water," Dagny said. "You pulled me out of a river and told me something true, and I realized I'd been carrying a file full of lies about you, and I helped carry those lies another nine days because I was afraid." She exhaled, and the breath fogged and drifted and was gone. "I'm done being afraid. But I want you to understand what I'm about to do, because you're the only person here who'll grasp it. If I transmit this warning and it gets traced—and it will get traced—I don't just lose my job. I lose my pension, my standing, my clearance. I become exactly what you were. A name on a list. Unemployable in everything I know how to do."

"That's not what you'll become."

"No?"

"No." Orsen took the handset from her hands—gently, asking rather than taking—and held it between them in the dark. "You'll become someone who decided, once, at cost, to be honest about where the water wants to go. There aren't many of us. But we find each other." A pause. "I found you in a river."

Dagny's throat tightened. She looked away, down at the lamps of Vaskr, because looking at Orsen just then felt like looking into the sun off fresh snow—too much light arriving too fast after too long in shadow.

"If you transmit," Orsen said, "transmit smart. Strip the header. Use the emergency band, short burst, no authentication handshake—they can't trace what doesn't finish talking. Give them the dam, the timeline, the towns. Don't give them Vaskr. Don't give them names. Let the hydrologists in Brannvik raise hell about the water; that part's true whether or not anyone believes it. And leave the rest—" she tapped the handset, "—to us. We go down ourselves. We knock on doors. We stand in the town square and say it out loud, with faces, which is harder to bury than any frequency."

"You'd come down? Into the valley? After twelve years of—" Dagny gestured vaguely at the whole vertical world around them, the high country that had been Orsen's exile and her cathedral both.

"After twelve years of counting miles," Orsen said, "I'm ready to count something else." She held the handset out. "Your call, officer. Literally. I won't touch it. This has to be yours, or it's worthless—it has to cost you something, or downstream they'll never believe it was real."

Dagny took it. Her hands were steady. That surprised her; she'd expected them to shake. But they were steady as lockkeeper's hands, hands that had learned that gates only work when you commit to moving them.

She stripped the header with numb fingers. Set the band. Composed the burst in her head one final time—not the fortieth draft, but the first one, the honest one, reduced to its bones:

*Glacial dam failing above unnamed tributary, Kestrel headwaters. Vent activity accelerating. Failure window: four days, possibly fewer. Evacuate Brannvik and all settlements on the lower river. Repeat: evacuate. Source verified by field observation. End.*

No callsign. No name. Just the truth, naked on an open frequency, going out into the dark like a message in a bottle thrown toward everyone she'd ever served.

She pressed transmit.

The little indicator light glowed green for nine seconds. Nine seconds. She'd rehearsed careers ending in dramatic moments—arrests, tribunals, shouted accusations across conference tables—and instead hers ended in nine seconds of green light on a mountainside, with the smell of birch smoke rising from a village of ghosts and a disgraced guide sitting silently beside her, keeping watch.

When it was done, Dagny set the handset down on the stone between them, and found that she was shaking after all, now, delayed, her whole body humming like the antenna had hummed.

Orsen put her hand over Dagny's. Not gripping. Just resting there, warm through the glove, heavy as a cairn stone.

"There," Orsen said quietly. "Now you're one of us."

"One of who? The erased?"

"The honest." Orsen's mouth curved, barely, in the starlight. "It's a smaller organization. Worse benefits. But the membership looks out for each other." She stood, pulling Dagny gently up with her, and nodded down-valley, toward the black line of the gorge, toward the distant invisible lights of Brannvik sleeping beside a river that didn't yet know what was coming. "We leave for the gorge at first light. You, me, Eirik. Downstream logic, Dagny. If the radio warning doesn't land—and it might not, bureaucracies are deaf until the water's at the door—then somebody has to stand in that town square and refuse to stop talking."

Dagny looked at the radio one last time. Eleven days of silence, ended. Whatever came now—trace, tribunal, ruin—came.

"First light," she said.

And far above them, unseen in the dark, the glacier groaned—a long, grinding exhalation, like something enormous turning over in its sleep—and neither woman needed a seismograph to know the clock had just ticked louder.
