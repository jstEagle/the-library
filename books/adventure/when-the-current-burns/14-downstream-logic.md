## 14. Downstream Logic

The map Orsen drew was a lie by omission, and everyone in Maren's longhouse knew it.

She'd scratched it into the trestle table with a charred stick, the way she'd drawn a hundred route plans for a hundred clients—elevation lines like fingerprints, watercourses in blue clay, the glacial dam above the vent rendered as a ragged crescent. The meltwater pool behind it she'd shaded dark, and the shading had grown every day since the tremor, fed by her own estimates, Dagny's instruments, and the low, unhappy sound the ice made at night now, like a whale grieving somewhere under the world.

"Brannvik is here." She tapped the lowest mark. "Fourteen hundred people, plus the mill towns strung along the banks below it—Holtbrå, Skarven, the fishing camps. If the dam lets go all at once, the surge reaches Brannvik in under three hours. Maybe less. The gorge narrows here—" her stick ground into the table, leaving a black groove, "—which means it doesn't slow down. It accelerates."

"And the radio?" asked one of the Vaskr men, a broad-shouldered fisher named Torvald who had not spoken to Orsen directly since the council.

"Dead. The relay mast on Kestrel Ridge came down in the quake—we saw it from the moraine. Dagny got one partial warning out before that, and it may or may not have been believed. May or may not have been traced." Orsen glanced at Dagny, who stood at the table's end with her arms folded and her face arranged into official neutrality, the expression of a woman who had already decided to spend herself and was waiting for the right moment to say so. "We can't count on it. We go down and knock on doors."

"Through the gorge," Maren said.

It wasn't a question. Maren stood at the head of the table the way she stood everywhere—spine straight as a survey stake, white hair braided tight, hands resting on the wood as though the whole settlement were a vessel she was keeping bailed out. She had not sat down during the entire discussion. Orsen suspected Maren hadn't truly sat down in forty years.

"Through the gorge," Orsen agreed. "The high route around it would take six days. The meltwater pool grows by the hour. We don't have six days. The gorge floor is walkable in summer—scree and old flood debris, one narrow chute where the river pinches against the south wall. It's a half-day's travel if the rock holds."

"And if the rock doesn't hold?"

"Then it isn't a half-day's travel for anyone, ever again."

A silence moved through the longhouse like weather. Smoke from the central hearth curled up through the smoke-hole and bent sideways in a draft, and Orsen watched it go with the particular attention of a woman who had spent twelve years reading the sky for a living and did not like what the sky had been saying all week. The air smelled of spruce resin and wet wool and, beneath it, faint and wrong, the sulfur breath of the vent, carried down on the katabatic wind from the source. The mountain was exhaling. Mountains only exhaled like that when something below them was changing its mind.

"Three of us," Orsen said. "Me, Dagny, and one of your people who knows the gorge floor. Someone who can read the rock the way I read snow."

"I know the gorge."

Eirik Halvard had been standing near the door, and Orsen had been aware of him the way she was always aware of him now—peripherally, painfully, a scar she kept forgetting was there until it pulled. He came forward into the hearthlight. Twelve years had silvered him and stooped him slightly, but his voice still had that quality that had once made clients trust him with their lives: low, certain, built for bad weather.

"You haven't walked it in twenty years," Maren said.

"I walked it when the settlement was founded. I helped cut the lower trail. I know where the walls are hollow." He set his hands on the table, mirroring her, and Orsen saw the old man and the young guide superimposed for a moment—the man who had testified against her, the man who had carried her out of a crevasse field, both of them true, both of them the same. "Sela's team went into that country and never came out. I'm not sending strangers down to finish what she started while I sit here and split firewood."

"Nobody's sending you anywhere," Maren said, and her voice did not rise, which somehow made it worse. "That's the point of this place, Eirik. Nobody is ever sent. Nobody is ever taken. We chose the gorge when we were young and it cost us—"

"It cost us nothing. It cost *me* nothing. It cost *you*—"

"It cost us everything we agreed to give up, and you agreed with me. You stood where you're standing and you agreed." Maren's hands flattened on the table. "You have a bad hip. You have a heart that Torvald's wife has been worrying over for two winters. And you have a granddaughter's ghost in that gorge, and ghosts make men walk where they shouldn't. I have watched this mountain take people for less."

"I'm not going for Sela." Eirik's jaw worked. "I'm going because if those towns drown and I stayed home, I will have done it twice. I will have stood in a hearing room and let a lie stand to protect myself, and I will have stood in a longhouse and let a valley drown to protect my comfort, and there is no version of me that survives the second one." He looked at Orsen. "You of all people know what it costs to carry that. You've been carrying it for twelve years. I watched you carry it."

"Don't," Orsen said quietly. "Don't put my name in your mouth as a reason."

But it had landed, and she felt it land, and everyone in the longhouse felt it land, because the truth of Orsen Nystrom's twelve years was not a secret here. They had taken her in, fed her, given her a dry bunk, and asked nothing—had offered, in fact, the thing she had once wanted more than redemption: erasure. A life with no file, no name in any ledger, no past that could reach her. And she had lain in that bunk for three nights listening to the ice groan and understood, with the clarity of cold water, that erasure was just another kind of drowning. That she had spent twelve years measuring miles because miles were the only unit in which her life still made sense, and that the towns downstream were full of people who had never heard her name and would die anyway, nameless to her, if she chose her own peace over their breath.

"Orsen." Maren turned to her, and her eyes were the gray of glacial ice under strain. "You're the guide. The route is yours. Say he doesn't come."

* * *

Orsen looked at the map.

She looked at the chute, the pinch point, the place where the south wall leaned out over the riverbed like a man leaning over a well. She looked at the new shading around the meltwater pool, and at the numbers in her head that kept getting worse every time she recalculated them—seepage rates, freeboard, the temperature of the water Dagny had sampled at the outflow, warmer every day, the vent underneath it all cooking the dam's foundations like a fist held against the belly of a glacier.

She thought about the last time she had told a man he couldn't come on a route. His name had been Anders Vik, and he'd been the expedition sponsor, and she'd told him the weather window was closing and he'd overridden her, and the client who died had been a nineteen-year-old girl who wanted to see a glacier before she got sick again. Orsen had said nothing at the inquiry. She had said nothing for twelve years. Saying nothing had a weight, and she knew its exact mass now, the way she knew the mass of a rope or a rack of pins.

"He comes," she said.

Maren's head turned slowly. "Orsen—"

"He knows the hollow walls. That's not sentiment, that's geology, and geology is the only thing keeping four thousand people alive this week. And—" Orsen made herself say the rest of it, made herself say it in front of everyone, "—because I spent twelve years letting other people's cowardice decide what happened on my routes, and I'm done doing that. Including mine. He comes, he stays roped to me, and if I say drop and crawl, he drops and crawls. That's the term."

Eirik exhaled, and it sounded like something being set down after a long carry.

"He's my brother," Maren said, to the room, to Orsen, to the smoke-hole and the gray light beyond it. "Do you understand that? Not in blood. In the founding. We cut the first terrace together, we buried the first winter's dead together, we built this place on the promise that no one here would ever again be made to choose between their conscience and their safety, because we had all already chosen, and the choosing had emptied us." Her voice frayed at the edges, just slightly. "And now the mountain is going to make us choose again, and I am watching this place come apart at the seams one person at a time. Torvald's daughter wants to go down and help the towns. Half the young ones do. If Eirik walks out that door, I cannot hold the rest."

"Then don't hold them," Dagny said.

Everyone looked at her. She had spoken maybe a dozen words all evening, and now she unfolded her arms and stepped up to the table, and Orsen watched her do the thing she had watched her do once before, at the abandoned camp, when she had stood in front of six empty tents and refused to look away.

"With respect, Elder. You built a sanctuary. Sanctuaries are for the people inside them. That's the whole architecture of a sanctuary—walls, and who they keep out. I've served inside one my whole career; it was called the Survey Office, and it had excellent walls." She laid her hand flat over the shaded pool on the map. "The mountain is coming through yours. You can spend what's left of your secrecy trying to keep it, and lose the towns and lose it anyway when the flood drags your river onto every map in the district. Or you can spend it on purpose. Walk down. Help carry children. Let them see your faces. It's going to be spent either way—this is the only choice you get to make about it."

The longhouse was very quiet. Somewhere outside, a dog—Vaskr kept three, half-wild things that had appeared out of the bush years ago—lifted its voice at the mountain, one long wavering note, and stopped.

Maren looked at Dagny for a long time. Then at Eirik. Then, last and longest, at Orsen, and something passed between them that Orsen could not have named if she'd tried—recognition, maybe, between two women who had built their lives around the same desperate verb. *Keep.* Keep people out, keep people safe, keep the past buried, keep the water back. All of them keepers. All of them learning, in the same terrible week, what keeping cost.

"Take the west chute, not the south," Maren said at last, and her voice was the voice of a woman signing something. "The south wall is rotten; Eirik's right, it's been hollow since the founding. The west chute is longer by two hours and the footing is worse, but the rock is alive. And take Torvald's daughter, Signe, as far as the lower trailhead—she knows the gorge mouth, and she'll come back and tell us what she saw, and that will do more to hold this place together than any order I could give." She straightened. "And Orsen Nystrom. If he falls—if my brother falls on that route—I am holding you to the oldest law there is. The guide's law. You bring back what you can carry."

"I know the law," Orsen said. "I've been on the wrong side of it."

* * *

They left at first light, or what passed for first light under the storm deck, which was less a dawn than a slow concession of gray.

The settlement turned out to watch them go. Orsen had not expected that. Vaskr's people were watchers by trade and by temperament—she'd felt their eyes on her since the day she and Dagny were brought in under the terraces—but this was different. This was the whole community standing along the upper path in the blowing sleet, silent, and Orsen thought: *this is what it looks like when people watch their own secret walk out of the walls.* Signe went with them to the gorge mouth as ordered, a wiry young woman with her father's shoulders and her grandmother's unblinking gaze, and she did not speak for the first two miles, and neither did anyone else, because there are departures that are too heavy for words and this was one of them.

The gorge took them at midmorning.

It opened in the hillside like a wound that had been healing for ten thousand years and had just been torn open again—the tremor had done its work everywhere. Old flood debris that had sat stable since before the founding was slumped and shifted. Boulders the size of cabins had come down the scree and were sitting at new angles, shedding grit in little continuous whispers. The river itself had changed its voice. It had run deep and even when Orsen had seen it from above; now it ran shallow and angry, spread wide across its bed, and she knew what that meant the way she knew a client's lie—too much water coming from somewhere it shouldn't be. The pool behind the dam was leaking. Not failing. Leaking. The difference was hours, not days.

"West chute," Eirik said, pointing with his chin. His face was gray under the weather, and his breathing was already too careful, and Orsen catalogued it the way she catalogued everything now—hip, heart, age, guilt, all of it ballast. "There. You see how the wall stands off the water? There's a bench behind that fallen slab. We cut the first switchback up it in—" he did the arithmetic and something moved through his face, "—in the spring Sela was born. Maren carried the pins. I carried the drill."

"Forty years," Orsen said.

"Forty-one." He looked at her sideways, and for a moment the mist between them thinned. "You counted."

"I count everything. It's the only thing I'm good at."

"That's not true," Eirik said, "and you know it's not true, and when this is over I'm going to tell you all the ways it's not true, and you're going to hate every one of them." He shouldered his pack and started up the slab. "Move. We're losing the light."

Dagny fell in beside Orsen at the base of the climb. She said nothing for a moment, just checked Orsen's harness buckles with two fingers, an intimacy so small and so practical that it went through Orsen like warmth through frost.

"Your call back there," Dagny said. "Taking him."

"Geology," Orsen said.

"Sure."

"Shut up."

Dagny almost smiled. Then she looked up the gorge—the leaning walls, the angry water, the gray light failing at the top of the world—and her face went still and official again, and Orsen knew she was doing the arithmetic too, the one with people in it instead of hours.

"Orsen," she said quietly. "The water's rising faster than your numbers. I checked the outflow gauge at dawn. If it's seeping that fast from the pool, the dam's not going to hold to your window. It's going to let go early."

Orsen stood with her hand on the first pin—Eirik's pin, forty-one years old, iron rusted to the color of dried blood—and looked down the gorge at the river running shallow and wide and wrong, carrying its warning past them toward the sea, toward towns that had not yet heard it.

"Then we don't sleep," she said, and started up.
