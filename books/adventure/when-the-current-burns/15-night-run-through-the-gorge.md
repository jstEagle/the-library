## 15. Night Run Through the Gorge

### Orsen

The gorge began where the map ended, which was fitting, because everything in Orsen's life lately had started at the edge of something official.

She stood at its lip in the last gray light and looked down into a wound in the earth. The river they had followed for nine days—the river with no name, the river that wasn't on maps—dropped into a slot of black granite no wider than a wagon road, and the water inside it didn't flow so much as *boil*, white and furious, lit from below by the faint sulfur glow of the vent country still miles upstream but already making itself felt in the smell. Rotten eggs and hot stone. The breath of something waking.

"You're sure there's no other way," Eirik said behind her. Not a question. He'd been saying it in different shapes all afternoon, and she understood it wasn't cowardice in his voice anymore. It was arithmetic. A man of sixty with a bad knee and twelve years of guilt in his pack was calculating what the gorge would take from him, and he was doing it calmly, the way she did it, the way mountain people did everything: quietly, in advance, so there would be nothing left to say when the moment came.

"There's the high route," she said. "Four days around the shoulder of the massif, through country that's been shaking since the tremor. We don't have four days. The dam behind that glacier is holding back a lake the size of Brannvik's harbor, and every hour it grows. We have tonight."

Dagny stood a little apart, checking the charges one last time by the light of a shielded lamp—three crates of requisitioned explosive wrapped in oilcloth, lashed to a frame she'd carried herself for two days without complaint, because carrying them meant she was still an officer, and being an officer was the last identity she had left that hadn't cracked. Orsen watched her hands move over the crates the way a woman touches a sleeping child. Careful. Afraid of waking something.

"Ledger says the crossing shelf runs two hundred meters along the north wall," Orsen said. "Old survey path, cut before the war, before they stopped mapping this whole drainage. It's maybe a meter wide in places. Ice on the first third. The meltwater's raised the spray level since I came through as a girl, so the back half will be wet stone."

"Wet stone at night," Dagny said.

"Wet stone at night," Orsen agreed. "So we don't stop. We don't look down. We move like the water's chasing us, because in about thirty hours, it will be."

Eirik laughed—a short, startled sound, as if his body had produced it without permission. "You always talked like that. Even at twenty-six. Like the mountain was a debtor and you knew all its terms."

She didn't turn around. "You don't know how I talked at twenty-six. You knew how you needed me to talk."

The words came out colder than she'd intended, and she felt them land, felt the silence behind her change texture. Above them the first stars were coming out in the narrow strip of sky, hard and white as chips of ice. She shouldered her pack. There was nothing left to do but go down.

### Eirik

Eirik had spent twelve years rehearsing this descent and had gotten every detail wrong.

In his imagination—on the long nights in Vaskr when sleep refused him and the past came up the valley like weather—he had always pictured the moment of reckoning as a conversation. Orsen across a table, or a fire, her arms crossed, demanding the truth, and him giving it, whole and polished, the way you hand over a stone you've been turning in your pocket for years until it's smooth. In the imagining, she listened. In the imagining, the truth was enough.

The reality was a ledge a meter wide above a river that sounded like the end of the world, and the truth was just weight, dead weight, and there was no table to set it down on.

He went third, behind Orsen and Dagny, one hand on the rock, the old knee complaining with every step. The gorge walls rose on either side, wet black granite veined with quartz that caught the sulfur glow and threw it back faintly green, so that the whole defile seemed lit from within, like the belly of something alive. The spray reached them in gusts. It was warm spray—that was the wrongness of it, the thing his body kept flinching from. Mountain water was supposed to be cold. This was river water heated by the vent field, blood-warm, and it made the ice on the first shelf treacherous in a way no cold ice ever was, slick and sweating, refusing to hold a crampon honestly.

"Contact's good here," Orsen called back, her voice flattened by the roar. "Move when I move. Dagny, keep the charges tight to the wall."

They moved. Eirik counted his steps because counting was what he did instead of praying. At forty he passed a rusted iron ring driven into the rock, some dead surveyor's anchor, and felt an absurd gratitude toward a man fifty years gone. At ninety the shelf narrowed and Orsen's silhouette went flat against the wall, arms out, a woman becoming part of the mountain, and he thought: *she was always this. I just never looked.*

That was the true confession, he realized. Not the false testimony, not the cowardice at the inquiry. The true confession was that he had never looked. She had been the best guide he'd ever walked behind, and he had spent the whole of that last expedition half a step behind her, watching his own feet, guarding his own future, and when the storm took Halloran and the inquiry came asking who had made the call to push the high col in rising wind, he had looked at his boots and let them put her name in the hole where his should have gone.

He had told himself for twelve years that he'd been afraid. It was only in the last three days, walking behind her again, watching her read the terrain the way other people read faces, that he understood fear was the polite word. What he'd felt was relief. The inquiry needed someone, and for one shining coward's moment, it wasn't going to be him, and he had said yes with his silence, and his silence had been the loudest thing he'd ever done.

The shelf opened onto a wider section where the wall stepped back and the footing went to wet gravel. Orsen held them there, breathing, checking the route ahead by feel.

"Halfway," she said. "The bad part's next—the slab where the spray lands. We rope up, single file, no slack. If anyone goes, the other two go to the wall and anchor, we don't try to catch—"

The mountain interrupted her.

It wasn't an earthquake. It was smaller than that, a shudder, a shrug of the deep rock adjusting itself around the heat below, but in the gorge it was a voice. Stones came off the wall above them with a sound like tearing cloth, and Eirik saw, in the sulfur light, a block the size of a stove detach from the overhang directly above Orsen's line.

She saw it too. He would know that later, would replay it: she saw it and did the correct thing, which was to press into the wall under the overhang's lip, where the falling rock would skip past. The correct thing. She had always done the correct thing, and the mountain had always punished it.

The block hit the shelf edge, shattered, and the shelf edge went with it.

### Orsen

There was a moment—half a second, maybe less—when Orsen was in the air and the world was very quiet.

The roar of the river was still there but it belonged to some other place, some other life. She was aware of the spray rising past her, warm as breath, of the sulfur light smearing upward, of her own hands open and empty, and she thought, with a clarity that felt almost like humor: *so this is how it ends, and I never did learn to measure anything but miles.*

Then a hand closed on her wrist.

It was not a gentle grip. It was the grip of a man who had decided something final, and the jerk of it nearly took her arm from its socket, and then the wall was against her back and her boots were scrabbling on stone that was suddenly, impossibly, still there—a spur of rock, a rib of the mountain she hadn't mapped, hadn't known, and Eirik's other arm was around her harness and they were both pressed flat while the river screamed below them and the broken shelf rained gravel into the dark.

She got a knee up onto the spur. Got her weight onto it. Turned to pull him up beside her.

He didn't come.

She looked down and understood, in the space of one heartbeat, everything.

His leg was caught—no, not caught, *broken*, the lower leg bent at the angle of a branch snapped in a storm, wedged in a crack where he'd jammed it to anchor them both, and he had held there, held her whole weight and his own on a shattered bone, because letting go had never been one of his options this time. Twelve years ago it had been the only one. Tonight his body had voted differently, and the vote had cost him.

"Eirik—"

"Rope," he said, through his teeth. His face was gray as the granite. "Get the rope on me before I look at it. If I look at it, I'm going to be useless, and I've been useless once already in your life, and I'm not doing it twice."

* * *

### Eirik

The pain arrived like weather, in fronts. The first was white and total and he rode it with his eyes shut, counting, counting. The second was colder: the knowledge, spreading from the leg outward, that the crossing was over for him, that the charges were still on Dagny's back, that Orsen had a schedule written in meltwater and hours and could not afford to carry a broken man up a gorge wall.

He'd had twelve years to imagine how he'd repay her. Every version had ended with him saying the right words. None of them had ended with him being a burden at the bottom of a gorge, and he found, to his surprise, that this was worse than any punishment he'd rehearsed—worse than her hatred, which he'd long since made his peace with. Her hatred would have been a transaction completed. This—her hands working the rope with furious competence, her face a mask of concentration so he wouldn't see what she was feeling—this was a debt accruing interest he couldn't pay.

"Dagny goes on with the charges," he said, when he could speak. "You go with her. I'll—"

"Shut up, Eirik."

"You'll need someone on the far shelf to anchor the—"

"*Shut up.*" Her voice cracked on the second word, and he stopped, and in the green sulfur light he saw that her eyes were wet, and that she was blinking the wet away with the same efficiency she did everything, because there wasn't time, there was never time, and he understood that this was the thing he had never understood about her: she didn't suppress her grief. She *scheduled* it. And no one had ever come along on the schedule.

Dagny appeared above them, lamp shielded, face pale. "The shelf's gone for thirty meters. We can't cross with him and we can't leave him—"

"You can," Orsen said. "You will. Both of you. I'm staying."

"No," Dagny said, and then, quieter, with the flat precision of a woman reading her own death warrant: "No. I have the charges. I go. That was always the plan. You stay with him, get him up the goat track on the south side when the spray drops at dawn, and follow. That's not a request, Orsen. That's the mission."

Orsen stared at her. Eirik watched the two of them in the green light—watched something pass between them that had nothing to do with orders, and thought: *there it is. There's the thing she found that I never gave her.* Not absolution. Not forgiveness. Someone who stayed.

Dagny adjusted the charge frame on her shoulders, looked down at him, and said, "Your daughter would have liked you tonight," and was gone into the dark upstream, lamp shuttered, a moving shadow and then nothing.

### Orsen

They got him up the goat track by dawn, which took six hours and cost her two fingernails and cost him, by his own later accounting, "everything below the knee and most of what was above it."

They sheltered in an overhang while the light came up gray and cold over the gorge, and the spray dropped, and the river's voice changed pitch—lower, fuller, the sound of a river that was being fed from above by a lake that would not stop growing. Orsen splinted the leg with a tent pole and her spare shirt and did not let herself think about infection, about the days of travel left, about anything except the next hour.

Eirik watched her work. When she tied off the last knot, he spoke.

"Halloran's widow wrote me a letter, three years after. Did I ever tell you that?"

Orsen's hands went still.

"She'd found out about the wind readings. The ones I didn't log. She didn't accuse me. She just said, *I know you know what happened, and I know it's heavy, and I hope you've found a place to set it down.*" He laughed, a dry sound with no humor in it. "A place to set it down. So I bought a valley with no name and no roads and I set it down there, and you know what? It didn't weigh any less. I just couldn't see it from the house."

"Eirik—"

"Let me finish. I've had twelve years to finish and I've never started." His voice was steady, and she realized the steadiness was costing him more than the leg was. "I testified what I testified because I was afraid, and then I told myself I was afraid, and then I built my whole life on the word *afraid*, and it's a lie. I wasn't afraid of the inquiry. I was afraid of being *small*. Of being the man who made the bad call. And you— you'd have carried it. You'd have stood in that room and said *it was my decision* and meant it, and they'd have believed you, because you were always worth believing, and I let them, because your ruin was smaller than mine. That's what I did. Not fear. Arithmetic. I did the arithmetic and you were the cheaper loss."

The gorge roared below them. Orsen found that her eyes were burning and did not blink it away this time, because there was no schedule, there was only this.

"I knew," she said.

He went still.

"Twelve years ago, at the inquiry. I knew you'd seen the same readings I had. I knew the wind was rising when we committed to the col. I could have fought it—could have named you, could have made them look at the logs. And I didn't." She looked at her hands, at the torn nails, at the rope burns. "Because I was tired, Eirik. I'd been carrying everything for everyone for years, and when they handed me one thing I could finally put down, I put it down. Your silence bought my rest. I took the deal. That's my confession, and you can have it, because I'm done paying interest on it too."

He was crying. She had never seen him cry, not on the mountain, not at the inquiry, not once in the two expeditions they'd shared, and he cried now without sound, the way men cry who learned young that sound would be used against them.

"So we're not absolving each other," she said. "Understand that. I'm not forgiving you because you caught me. And you're not forgiven because your leg is broken. There's no absolution in a gorge." She reached out and took his hand, the one not braced against the wall, and held it the way you hold a rope: firmly, without tenderness, because tenderness was a luxury and this was survival. "This is just two people who finally put the weight down at the same time. That's all forgiveness is out here. It's not a blessing. It's a *technique*. You do it so you can walk."

He laughed then, wet and broken and real. "You always talked like that."

"Yeah," Orsen said. "And you never listened. So listen now: can you walk?"

He looked at his splinted leg, at the gray dawn, at the south wall of the gorge where the goat track climbed toward the high country and the vent and the lake that would not wait. He set his jaw the way she'd seen him set it a hundred times, twelve years and a lifetime ago.

"I can walk," he said. "How many miles?"

And Orsen, who had measured her whole life in miles, who had counted them the way other people counted years because years had failed her, looked up at the light coming into the gorge and found, for the first time, that the number didn't matter.

"Enough," she said, and shouldered his arm across her neck, and they began to climb.

Behind them, far upstream, the mountain gave a long, low groan—deeper than any tremor yet—and the river rose an inch while they watched, and Orsen thought of Dagny alone in the dark with three crates of thunder, and climbed faster.
