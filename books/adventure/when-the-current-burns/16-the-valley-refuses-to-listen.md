## 16. The Valley Refuses to Listen

They came down out of the high country on the third day, and Brannvik rose to meet them the way lowland towns do—slowly, then all at once. First the smell: peat smoke and wet wool and the mineral tang of the river running wide and brown with spring melt. Then the sound, which Orsen had forgotten a town could make. Dogs. Cart wheels in mud. A bell at the fish market counting out the hour as if hours were something that could be counted, as if they were not the thing she had been racing for nine days now.

Eirik rode behind her on the pack horse, his shattered leg splinted with ski poles and strapping and a length of Maren's best birch, his face gray as old ash from the pain of every switchback. He had not complained once. That was its own kind of eloquence.

Dagny walked beside Orsen's stirrup, and neither of them spoke. There was nothing left to say that hadn't been said in the gorge, in the dark, over Eirik's broken body while the river shouted beneath them. The plan was simple. Simple plans were the only ones that survived contact with mountains, and Orsen could only pray this one would survive contact with a municipal council instead.

The vent above Vaskr was failing. The glacial dam was cracked like a dropped plate. Behind it, meltwater pooled by the millions of tons, and when it let go—and it would let go—it would come down the valley in a wall of water and ice and pulverized rock, and everything between the headwaters and the sea would be scoured to gravel. Brannvik first. Brannvik most of all, because Brannvik sat fat and comfortable on the floodplain where the valley opened wide, because the river had always been generous here, because no one in three generations had thought to ask the river for anything but more.

* * *

The council chambers of Brannvik occupied the second floor of the customs house, a building that smelled of tar and old ledgers, and the council itself consisted of five men and one woman arranged behind a table scarred by two centuries of official indifference. Orsen stood before them soaked to the thigh, her hair stiff with dried sweat, her hands still trembling faintly from the descent—not from fear, she told herself, just from the cold, just from the miles.

She counted the miles even now. Ninety-one since the tremor. She couldn't help it. It was how she kept herself upright.

"State your business," said the chairman, a man named Aldor Vess whose jowls suggested a life spent at tables like this one, whose eyes went past her the moment she began to speak, seeking some clerk or deputy who might be the actual person worth listening to.

"My name is Orsen Nystrom," she said. "I'm a mountain guide."

"You're a blacklisted mountain guide," said the woman at the end of the table, not unkindly. She had a ledger open in front of her, and Orsen understood with a lurch that her name was already in it. Had always been in it. Twelve years of bureaucracy had gotten there ahead of her, the way it got everywhere ahead of everyone. "Nystrom, O. Restricted terrain violation, 12 years standing. You have no certification, no standing, no—" she consulted the ledger as though the word might be written there, "—standing."

"I have ninety-one miles of evidence," Orsen said. "I have a survey officer of the Crown standing beside me. I have the father of Sela Halvard, who led the government team that found what's about to kill you all."

She laid it out then, all of it, as plainly as she knew how—the vent, the dam, the tremor, the pooling water, the numbers she'd run by firelight with Dagny checking her arithmetic twice because neither of them could afford to be wrong. She talked about cubic meters per second. She talked about the gorge and the narrows and the way the floodplain spread at Brannvik like a hand opening. She talked about evacuation windows and elevation lines and the simple, brutal physics of what a wall of water does to a town built on a floodplain.

She watched their faces while she talked, and she saw the thing she had seen a hundred times in clients who didn't want to believe the weather report: the polite tightening around the eyes, the small settling back into chairs, the whole body's quiet announcement that it had already decided and was merely waiting for the unpleasantness to end.

When she finished, the chairman folded his hands.

"The mining consortium holds water rights on that tributary," he said. "Their engineers surveyed the headwaters last summer. No instability was reported."

"Their engineers surveyed the *claims*," Orsen said. "There's a difference."

"A difference you'd have us act on? On the word of a woman whose own government has barred her from these very mountains?" He shook his head with the weary patience of a man declining a second helping. "Do you know what an evacuation order costs this town in a single day? Do you know what panic does to the spring fisheries? If we empty Brannvik on the word of a disgraced guide and a defector, and nothing happens, we will never recover. And if something *does* happen—"

"If something happens," Orsen said quietly, "there won't be a town left to recover."

Silence. Somewhere below, the fish bell rang again, counting out another hour she did not have.

The woman with the ledger leaned forward. "Officer Holt," she said. "You've said nothing. Your presence here is the only reason this hearing has continued as long as it has. Is this woman's account corroborated by your office?"

And Orsen felt Dagny move beside her—a small shift of weight, the kind of motion Orsen had learned to read on rock faces, the tell that precedes commitment, that moment when a climber takes the hold that cannot be taken back.

Dagny stepped forward.

"My orders," Dagny said, "were to locate the missing survey team and arrest them for desertion." Her voice was level, official, the voice she used for reports. "I located them. They were dead or scattered, and the survivors are living in a settlement above the tree line that does not appear on any map. Before they vanished, the team leader, Sela Halvard, documented catastrophic instability in the geothermal vent at the source of the unnamed tributary. Her superiors ordered her silence to protect downstream mining interests. I have read her journal. I have stood at the dam. I have run the numbers myself, and Nystrom's figures are correct."

"Then your office will issue a formal assessment—"

"No," Dagny said. "My office ordered me to bury it. That is why I am telling you here, in front of witnesses, off the record, against direct instruction." She reached into her coat and drew out her commission papers—the heavy cream stock, the wax seal, twelve years of her life folded into a document—and held them up so the whole table could see. "This is my authority to speak on behalf of the Crown's survey. In thirty seconds I am going to tear it in half. After that, I'm just a woman standing next to a guide, and you can weigh our words however you like."

"Dagny," Orsen breathed.

But Dagny wasn't looking at her. She was looking at the chairman, and her hands were steady.

"An evacuation costs you a day," Dagny said. "A flood costs you everything. I have spent eleven years learning to measure risk for the government, and I am telling you, as a private citizen in approximately twenty-five seconds, that the risk is real, it is imminent, and every hour you spend protecting your fisheries is an hour stolen from four thousand people downstream of a dying glacier."

She tore the commission in half. Then, deliberately, in front of the council, in front of the clerks, in front of the tall windows through which the whole gray town could see, she tore it again, and let the pieces fall.

"That's it," she said. "That's my career. Now evacuate your town."

* * *

They put them out into the street, of course. Two guards, apologetic and embarrassed, walking them down the stairs of the customs house into the rain, which had begun sometime during the hearing without anyone noticing. The fish bell rang. Carts rolled through the mud. Brannvik went about its afternoon with the serene, maddening confidence of a place that had never once been asked to imagine its own ending.

"They'll convene again tomorrow," one of the guards offered, not meeting anyone's eyes. "Maybe send a rider upstream to check. There's procedure—"

"There's no time for procedure," Orsen said, and heard how ragged her own voice had gone.

Eirik slumped against the customs house wall, breathing hard, his splinted leg propped on a barrel. His face had gone from gray to a sort of translucent white, and sweat stood on his forehead despite the cold. "Orsen." He caught her sleeve. "The harbor. The fishing fleet. When the herring run comes in, half the valley's men are on the water—but the women, the children, the old ones—they gather at the drying sheds in the evenings. Hundreds of them. All on the floodplain."

"I know."

"Then don't wait for the council." His grip tightened. "Tell them yourself. Tonight. Go where the people are, not where the power is."

Orsen looked down the long muddy street, at the lamplighters beginning their rounds, at the smoke rising straight and domestic from a hundred chimneys, at the drying sheds along the waterfront where lights were already kindling for the evening's work. Four thousand people. She had carried clients across crevasses thinner than her conscience. She had measured her whole life in miles because years had failed her, and now every mile she had ever walked had funneled down to this one street, this one evening, this one chance to make strangers believe a stranger.

Dagny came to stand beside her, rain dripping off her chin, her torn commission still littering the council floor behind them. She looked lighter, somehow. Unburdened and terrified in equal measure.

"Well," Dagny said. "No standing. No authority. No proof anyone will accept." A breath of a laugh, hollow as a drum. "We're just two women in the rain now."

"Yes," Orsen said. And then, because it was true, and because she was done not saying true things: "Thank you. For what you did in there. I know what it cost."

"It cost paper." Dagny's jaw tightened. "What it *buys*—that's what matters. Come on. The sheds are lighting their lamps. If we're going to be ignored, we may as well be ignored by as many people as possible."

They started down toward the harbor together, three figures in the rain—one limping, one limping worse, one carrying nothing at all anymore except the truth—and behind them the river ran brown and full and patient, already swollen with the first secret melt of a glacier that had begun, high above in the dark, to let go.
