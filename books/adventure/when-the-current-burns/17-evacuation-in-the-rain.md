## 17. Evacuation in the Rain

The rain came to Brannvik the way bad news always did—sideways, without apology.

Orsen stood on the steps of the town hall and watched it fall in gray sheets across the square, hammering the tin roofs of the fish sheds, turning the cobbles into a river of their own. Below her, the evacuation moved like a wound reopening: carts overloaded with children and hens, an old man arguing with a constable about a boat he refused to abandon, a woman carrying a lamb under one arm and a photograph album under the other. Somewhere a child was crying in that flat, exhausted way that meant it had been crying for hours.

Two days ago, no one had listened. Yesterday, Dagny Holt had walked into the survey office, laid her commission on the desk like a severed hand, and said the words *I am corroborating the blacklisted guide's claim, and you can court-martial me for it.* Today there were trucks. Today there were constables going door to door. Today there was rain, as if the sky itself had decided to testify on Orsen's behalf.

It wasn't enough. She could feel it in her teeth—the same pressure she'd felt at the vent, the deep subsonic groan of water finding weakness in stone. The dam above the source was failing faster than her calculations allowed. She'd given them three days yesterday morning. She would give them two now, maybe less if the rain kept up.

"Orsen." Dagny came out onto the steps beside her, hood up, radio crackling against her hip. Her face was pale beneath the weather-flush, and not from cold. "Downstream gauge station just called in. The river's risen forty centimeters since dawn. That's meltwater. That's *his* meltwater."

"The gorge will take most of it," Orsen said. "If the diversion channel holds."

"*If*."

"I don't deal in ifs anymore. I deal in elevations." She turned to look at Dagny fully. Rain ran off the brim of her hood between them like a curtain. "How many left in the low quarters?"

"Maybe four hundred. The ferry's been running since midnight but half the old quarter won't board. They say the water's never touched Brannvik in living memory." Dagny's mouth twisted. "Living memory is exactly the problem."

Below them, the argument between the old man and the constable escalated. The old man's voice cracked across the square: *"My father built this house! The river has never—"*

Orsen went down the steps without deciding to. Her boots hit the flooded cobbles and she crossed the square in twelve strides, took the old man gently by the shoulders, and turned him to face upstream—toward the mountains, invisible now behind the rain wall, toward the white throat of the valley where the current burned.

"Do you hear that?" she asked.

He blinked at her, rain streaming down his seamed face. "Hear what?"

"Nothing. That's what you're hearing right now. In about thirty hours, you'll hear it from your bedroom. It'll sound like the mountain clearing its throat. And by the time you understand what it is, the water will be through your front door and up to the eaves, and it will not be river water. It'll be scalding and full of silt and it will not stop." She kept her voice level, almost gentle. "Your father built a fine house. Let it be someone else's inheritance. Yours is standing here tomorrow."

For a long moment he only stared at her. Then his shoulders dropped, and he let the constable take his elbow.

Dagny appeared at her side. "You should give speeches."

"I gave one twelve years ago. To an inquiry board." Orsen watched the old man climb into the truck. "They didn't listen either."

* * *

Maren felt the valley before she saw it—the way you feel a storm coming in your knees, in the ache of old breaks, in the restless movement of birds.

She stood at the head of the descent trail with sixty-three of Vaskr's people behind her, and below, through the shifting veils of rain, the lowlands spread out gray and drowning in light. Woodsmoke and diesel. Truck engines. The thin, far-off sound of a church bell being rung out of rhythm—alarm, not worship.

Sixty-three. That was who had come when she asked. Not all of them. Some had refused—had gone back behind the palisade and barred their doors, choosing the old silence even now, even with the mountain groaning overhead. She didn't blame them. Erasure had been their covenant for fifty years; you didn't break a covenant in an afternoon.

But sixty-three had come. And Maren had looked at them gathered in the square at first light—young Halvard with his bound ribs, the twins who'd never been below the tree line, old Siv with her arthritic hands—and she had said the words she'd spent decades making sure she'd never have to say:

"We are going down. We are going to be seen. And we are going to help."

A murmur had gone through them like wind through barley. Fear, yes. But underneath the fear, something else—something she recognized because she'd carried it herself for fifty years, ever since she'd walked away from a government ministry and a name and a life, and buried all three in the high valley.

Relief.

That was the terrible secret of Vaskr, the one Maren had never spoken aloud in all her years as elder: erasure was a kind of death. They had chosen it to escape being used, being owned, being spent by other people's ambitions. But somewhere in the burying, they had also buried the part of themselves that acted, that mattered, that *counted*. For fifty years they had been safe, and safety had calcified into a smaller and smaller life, until the whole settlement fit inside a palisade and a silence.

Now the mountain was going to take even that from them. So Maren had decided—if they must be seen, they would be seen *doing something worth seeing*.

"Keep moving," she called, and the line started down through the wet pines, sixty-three ghosts descending into the world of names.

Halvard—Eirik, she corrected herself; down here they would need real names again—limped beside her, his shattered leg splinted and strapped to a pack-frame rig the settlement's healers had improvised. He refused the litter. He refused everything except forward motion.

"You shouldn't come past the tree line," Maren said quietly. "Your leg—"

"My daughter is downstream of that water," Eirik said. His voice was hoarse. He hadn't slept; none of them had. "Sela chose exile over complicity. She chose *truth*, Maren, when every instinct I have ever had was to choose comfort. The least I can do is stand where she stood." His jaw worked. "And if the flood takes Brannvik before we get them clear, then I want to be looking at it when it happens. I owe the valley that much honesty. I've spent a lifetime lying to it."

Maren said nothing. There was nothing to say that the rain wouldn't say better.

They came out of the trees at midafternoon, and the lowlands opened before them, and Maren heard the settlement behind her go quiet—all sixty-three of them stopping at once, struck silent by the sight of so much *world*: fences, roads, wires, rooftops, smokestacks, the sprawl of things built and named and owned.

Old Siv began, very softly, to sing. An old walking song, pre-government, pre-border, older than any of the erasures. One by one the others joined, and they came down into Brannvik singing, sixty-three strangers in homespun, and the townspeople turned and stared, and Maren lifted her chin and let them stare.

Let them look. Let them ask. Let the questions come like the rain.

Anonymity had been their armor. But armor, she understood now, was only useful if there was still something alive underneath it to protect.

* * *

Dagny saw them come into the square and thought, absurdly, of a story her grandmother used to tell—about the dead who walk out of the mist to finish unfinished business.

They came down the north road in a loose column, sixty-odd figures in wool and oiled canvas, singing something old and wordless, and the crowd in the square parted around them the way water parts around a stone. A woman led them—silver-haired, straight-backed, wearing authority like it had never once occurred to her to apologize for it.

"That's them," Orsen said quietly, coming to stand at Dagny's shoulder. "Vaskr."

"They came *down*." Dagny stared. "You told me they'd sooner burn the palisade than show a face."

"So did Maren. Twelve years of my life I believed hiding was the only honest thing left." Orsen's mouth curved, dry. "People keep surprising me. It's becoming a problem."

The silver-haired woman crossed the square directly to them, ignoring the constables, ignoring the staring crowds, and stopped before Orsen with the air of someone resuming a conversation interrupted decades ago.

"Nystrom," she said. "You look terrible."

"Maren." Orsen inclined her head. "You look like a woman leading sixty-three people to their own unmasking."

"I am. It's worse than it sounds." Maren's eyes swept the square—the trucks, the frightened families, the constables with their useless clipboards. "Where do you need hands?"

Dagny stepped forward, officer reflex overriding astonishment. "The old quarter. Four hundred souls, maybe less now, and half of them won't board the ferries. We need carriers, rowers, people to go door to door. People the townsfolk will actually listen to."

"Then you'll want Siv." Maren gestured to the tiny old woman at the column's edge, whose singing voice had gone quiet but whose eyes were bright as a bird's. "Siv was born in a river town. She knows how the old ones think." Maren turned back, and something in her face shifted—grief held at arm's length, examined, put away. "And you'll want Eirik at the ferry landing. He'll want to work until his leg gives out. Let him. Grief needs labor or it turns inward."

"And you?" Orsen asked.

Maren looked out at the rising brown water churning past the docks, at the rain falling without end or mercy, and for a moment her composure cracked—not into fear, but into something rawer.

"Fifty years I taught these people that to be unseen was to be free," she said. "Today I teach them the opposite lesson, and I don't know yet if it's wisdom or ruin. So I'll do what elders do. I'll stand in the middle of it and be counted." She pulled her hood back, deliberately, letting the rain hit her bare silver hair, letting the whole square see her face. "Count me."

* * *

By nightfall the rain had not slackened and neither had anyone.

Eirik worked the ferry landing with his leg strapped rigid, hauling children across gunwales, shouting directions in a voice that cracked and steadied and cracked again. Every boat that pushed off toward the high ground, he watched until it vanished into the rain, and Orsen knew—he couldn't hide it, didn't try—that he was watching for a different boat, one that might carry a survey team of six ghosts down out of the white valley.

"She's not coming down that river," Orsen told him, when the landings emptied between runs. "Not Sela. If they're alive, they went higher. Away from the water."

"Away." He laughed, and it was a broken sound. "Yes. That's what we Halvards do."

"No." Orsen gripped his shoulder, felt the tremor in him that had nothing to do with cold. "What you did in the gorge—that wasn't running. And what she did—walking away from a government order rather than bury a truth—that wasn't running either. You're a family of people who walk *toward*." She paused. "Toward is just a direction. It doesn't promise arrival. But it beats the alternative."

Eirik looked at her for a long moment, rain running down both their faces, twelve years dissolving between them like silt in current.

"When this is over," he said, "I'm going to find her. Alive or—" He stopped. Started again. "I'm going to find her."

"Yes," Orsen said. "You are."

* * *

Past midnight, Dagny found Orsen alone at the seawall, watching the river run brown and swollen under the harbor lights, carrying branches, silt, and pieces of somebody's fence down from the high country.

"Gauge station called again," Dagny said. No preamble. She'd stopped using preambles somewhere around the second day. "Up another meter. The diversion channel's holding, but Orsen—it's holding the way a cupped hand holds water. Eventually the fingers tire."

Orsen nodded slowly. Above the harbor, the mountains were total darkness—the storm had erased them entirely—but she could feel them the way Maren felt weather in her bones. The vent. The dam. The white throat of the valley, filling.

"How long?" Dagny asked.

"If the rain holds? Eighteen hours. Maybe twenty." Orsen turned to her. "Which means I leave for the high route within six. The charges, the diversion trigger, all of it. Alone."

"Alone." Dagny repeated it flatly.

"It's a one-person job. Someone has to stay and finish the evacuation, and you're the only one they'll still obey."

"Orsen—"

"Dagny." She said the name carefully, the way you set down something fragile. "Twelve years ago I learned that the worst thing the mountain can take from you isn't your life. It's everyone else's. I've been paying that debt since. This is how it gets paid. Let me pay it."

Dagny was quiet for a long time. The rain filled the silence between them, drumming on the seawall stones, hissing into the brown water.

"You come back," Dagny said finally. "That's not an order. I have no rank left to order anything. It's a request. From someone who—" She stopped, jaw tight, and Orsen watched her decide, visibly, to spend the words anyway. "From someone who has started measuring her life in something other than duty. And who would like very much to keep measuring."

Orsen looked at her—at the rain-soaked officer who had burned her career in a room full of indifferent men, who had caught her out of scalding water once already and intended, apparently, to make a habit of it.

"Eighteen hours," Orsen said. "Then I'll come back and you can start measuring in something new."

Behind them, across the darkened square, the last truck loaded, and old Siv's walking song rose once more over the water—thin, stubborn, older than any border—while upstream, unseen, unheard, the mountain cleared its throat.
