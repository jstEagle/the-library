## 18. The Source Gives Way

The mountain told us before the instruments did.

I was standing at the edge of Brannvik's flooded market square, watching men in yellow slickers stack sandbags against a river that had already won, when the ground shivered under my boots—a long, rolling tremor that made the church bell toll once without anyone touching it. The dogs started howling all along the waterfront. And high above the valley, where the clouds had torn open for the first time in two days, I saw it: a white plume rising off the glacier's shoulder, fine as flour, hanging in the cold air like smoke from a fire no one had lit.

Ice dust. The kind you only see when a mass of ice the size of a cathedral shifts its weight.

"Orsen." Dagny's hand closed on my arm. She'd been arguing with the harbor master for an hour, her voice hoarse, her survey badge held up like a shield that wasn't working. She followed my gaze upward and went very still. "Is that—"

"That's the dam letting go of something." My mouth had gone dry. Twelve years of reading mountains, and this was the sentence I'd been dreading since the moment I felt the first tremor through the soles of my boots three days ago: *it's moving faster than we planned for.* "Not all of it. Not yet. But the meltwater lake behind that ice is pushing on a wall that's already cracked."

"How long?"

I did the arithmetic I'd been doing in my head since dawn—the seepage rate at the gorge, the temperature climbing with the storm front, the way the melt channels had gone from trickles to torrents overnight. Three days ago I would have said a week. Yesterday, four days. Now I looked at that plume and said the number I'd been afraid to say out loud.

"Hours. Maybe six. Maybe less."

Dagny turned back to the harbor master, and whatever she said to him then must have carried the mountain in her voice, because for the first time since we'd come down into Brannvik, someone in officialdom actually moved. The sirens started ten minutes later—not the evacuation siren, which had been wailing on and off for a day to a town that mostly ignored it, but the deep three-tone pulse that meant *get out now, leave everything.*

* * *

We ran the numbers in the survey office, which had become our war room: maps unrolled across every table, Dagny's requisitioned charges still crated in the corner where they'd sat for two days while she fought a bureaucracy that wouldn't release demolition permits to a disgraced guide and a rogue officer. She'd finally gotten them signed that morning—four crates of shaped charges, detonators, spool after spool of det cord—by threatening to put her own name on a public record as the officer who let a valley drown.

Now the crates were all we had, and the plan they'd been signed for was dying by the hour.

"The diversion channel," Eirik said. He stood over the contour map with both hands flat on the paper, as if he could hold the valley down by pressing on it. His leg was splinted and strapped to a chair he refused to sit in; he'd been standing on it anyway for two days, gray-faced, because sitting felt too much like being carried. "It's still the answer. If we blow the channel at the shoulder ridge, the surge splits—two-thirds goes west into the old quarry basin, away from the town."

"And the channel's not cut yet," I said. "That was the whole problem. We needed a week of excavation and we got a day of rain."

"We don't need it cut." I looked up, and everyone in the room looked up, because it was Maren who'd spoken. She stood apart from us, near the window, watching the plume on the glacier the way a woman watches a storm she has spent her whole life predicting. "You have charges. You don't need a channel—you need a weakness. Blow the ridge at the seam where the rock's already fractured, and the mountain will do the digging for you. Water always finds the wound. You just have to make one."

She looked at me then, and I understood what she was saying, and so did everyone else, because there was only one person in the room who knew that seam—who had climbed that shoulder ridge in summer, in winter, in whiteout, who had mapped its fractures in a journal twelve years ago in another life, before the blacklist, before Vaskr, before any of it.

Me.

"There's a route," I said. "Up the east flank of the vent basin, along the spine where the ice never fully forms because of the geothermal heat. It's exposed. It's fast if nothing goes wrong and fatal if anything does. And the charge placement—" I pulled the contour map toward me, found the seam line I'd drawn from memory two nights ago, traced it with my finger. "It has to be exact. Too shallow and we just scar the rock. Too deep and we bring the whole shoulder down on top of the vent, and then God help everyone downstream."

"How long to get there?" Dagny asked. Her voice was level, officer-calm, but I'd learned to read her, and underneath the calm was a current running hard.

"Four hours up, if the weather holds. An hour to place the charges. Then I need to be off that ridge before they fire."

"Then you'll be off the ridge before they fire," Dagny said, "because I'm going with you."

"No."

"It wasn't a request."

"Dagny." I kept my voice low, which took everything I had. "Someone has to fire the charges from below. Someone has to run the wire, hold the detonator, make the call. That's you. That's the job only you can do, because I'll be standing on the mountain when it happens." I met her eyes and made myself say the rest plainly, because she deserved plainness. "One of us comes down. It doesn't have to be both of us going up."

For a long moment she didn't answer. Around us the room went quiet—Eirik leaning on his bad leg, Maren motionless at the window, two of Vaskr's younger residents frozen halfway through loading a crate. Rain hammered the office roof like a crowd demanding entry.

"You told me once," Dagny finally said, "that the worst thing a guide can do is decide for the client what risk they're allowed to take." Her jaw was set, but her voice cracked on the last word anyway. "Don't do it to me."

* * *

We left at 14:40, in rain that came sideways off the fjord and light that was already failing, though it was barely afternoon. The storm front had swallowed the sun somewhere over the Kestrel Range and wasn't giving it back.

Eirik walked us as far as the quarry road, which was as far as his leg would let him, and when I stopped to check his strapping—he'd re-tied it himself, badly, the way proud men do—he caught my wrist.

"I owe you a debt I can't pay," he said. "Twelve years, Orsen. I've counted them the way you used to count miles."

"Don't count them today." I tightened the strap until he winced. "Count hours. Get everyone in this valley above the flood line. That's the payment."

He gripped my forearm, guide's grip, the old grip, the one we'd used on the Kestrel Range in the years when we trusted each other with our lives—and maybe we still did, that was the terrible and wonderful thing, maybe the trust had survived everything we'd done to it. Then he stepped back into the rain and I didn't look at him again, because looking back is how guides die.

Maren had pressed something into my pack at the last minute: a strip of dried fish, a tin of salve, and a small carved stone, smooth as a river pebble, warm from her pocket. "From the source," she'd said. "Carry it up. Let the mountain know one of us came back honest."

The first hour was the gorge, and the gorge had become a monster. What had been knee-deep whitewater when Dagny nearly drowned in it was now a brown roaring thing filling the slot canyon wall to wall, throwing spray twenty feet up the walls, carrying whole trees like matchsticks. Our route climbed out of it immediately—handline anchors I'd placed two days ago, still holding, thank God—but the sound of it stayed with us, a constant animal roar below, reminding us what waited downstream if we failed.

Above the gorge we hit the spine route, and here the mountain showed me why I loved it and why it terrified me. The ridge crest ran narrow as a knife blade between the vent basin on one side and a thousand feet of air on the other, steaming faintly where the geothermal heat bled through, dusted with rime that glittered in the gray like crushed glass. No wind yet—that was the one mercy—but the air had the heavy, pressurized stillness of a held breath.

Dagny climbed well. Better than well—for a woman who'd spent her career behind desks and in helicopters, she moved with a focused, economical intensity, testing each hold, never rushing, never freezing. Twice I watched her stop herself from looking down, deliberately turning her face to the rock, breathing once, twice, then moving. She didn't complain. She didn't chatter. When I asked, at the second rest stop, huddled under an overhang with rain drumming the stone an inch from our noses, whether she wanted to turn around, she looked at me like I'd suggested she betray her country.

Which, I supposed, she already had.

"Tell me about the mile," she said instead.

"What mile?"

"The one you're always measuring. You do it when you think no one's watching. Your eyes go distant and your lips move and you're counting something." She pulled her hood back despite the rain, and water ran down her face, and she didn't seem to care. "What is it? What are you counting?"

I looked out past her, down the ridge we'd climbed, at the valley below where the lights of Brannvik were coming on in the failing day—hundreds of them, taillights streaming north, the evacuation finally real, finally moving. All those people. All those miles between them and the water coming down.

"When I was blacklisted," I said, "I stopped counting years. Years were the thing that failed me—twelve of them, wasted on a grudge. So I counted miles instead. Miles were honest. A mile is the same whether you're happy or not." I flexed my fingers inside my gloves, feeling the ache of the climb settle into the bones. "But miles are a coward's arithmetic, Dagny. They let you pretend you're going somewhere."

"And now?"

"Now I'm counting people." I nodded down at the valley. "Six thousand in Brannvik. Four hundred more in the farmsteads along the lower bend. Everyone in Vaskr who came down to help. You." I met her eyes. "That's the mile that matters. The one with all of them in it."

Something moved across her face—not a smile, exactly. More like a door opening in a house she'd kept locked a long time.

"Then let's go save it," she said.

* * *

The vent basin opened before us at dusk like the throat of something sleeping.

I'd forgotten—or had tried to forget—how wrong this place was. The ground steamed through fissures in long silver ribbons. Sulfur stained the snow yellow and orange in vast spreading blooms. And at the center, where the unnamed river rose boiling out of the earth itself, the water churned in a cauldron thirty yards wide, throwing off a haze that smelled of iron and rotten eggs and ancient heat. Above it all hung the glacial dam—a wall of ice two hundred feet high, blue-black in the dying light, cleft down its face by the crack we'd seen from the valley. Meltwater poured through that crack in a column as thick as a house, and behind the dam, invisible but audible, a deep groaning hum filled the basin like a struck bell that would not stop ringing.

The lake behind it held perhaps forty million tons of water. I didn't say the number aloud. Some numbers are better left as feelings.

"Charge points," Dagny said, unshouldering her crate, all business, because business was how she kept fear in its cage. "Show me."

I led her along the seam—my seam, the fracture line I'd mapped in another life, visible now as a raw scar in the ridge rock where the shoulder met the basin wall. I placed the first charge myself, tamping it into a natural hollow with hands that shook only slightly, and talked her through the rest: depth, angle, stemming, the spacing that would let the blast walk along the seam instead of just punching one hole. She worked fast and precise, wiring the det cord with the same neat economy she brought to paperwork, and within ninety minutes the ridge wore a necklace of explosives stretching four hundred yards along its spine.

The whole time, the bell hummed. And the whole time, the crack in the dam grew. I measured it against my ice axe at the start—at the start it had been the width of my palm. By the time we finished, I could have fit my boot in it sideways.

"Time to go," I said.

We descended the spine in the dark, headlamps cutting cones through the drizzle, moving faster than was safe because safe had stopped being available. At the anchor station in the lee of the shoulder—three hundred vertical feet below the charges, wired by a det cord run I'd rigged on the way up—Dagny knelt at the firing unit, a clunky military detonator with a hand crank, and looked up at me.

"Say when."

I checked my watch. Checked the sky, where the storm had thinned enough to show stars, absurdly, beautifully, as if the universe hadn't noticed what was happening beneath it. Listened to the hum of the dam, which had changed pitch in the last half hour—lower, wetter, the sound of ice becoming slush becoming memory.

Below us, the valley lights stretched north in a river of their own. Somewhere in that river was Eirik on his broken leg, hauling sandbags or herding cars or shouting sense into officials. Somewhere was Maren, leading her nameless people through the rain with their faces uncovered for the first time in decades. Somewhere was Sela's ghost, and the truth she'd died rather than bury.

I thought about miles. I thought about how I'd spent twelve years walking away from everything—names, records, blame, love—and how every one of those miles had been downhill, and how downhill, in the end, is just another word for drowning slowly.

This mile went up. This one cost everything. This one was worth it.

"Dagny." I put my hand over hers on the crank. "On three."

Her fingers laced through mine. "Orsen—if this works—"

"It'll work."

"If this works, I'm taking you somewhere that isn't a mountain. Somewhere with tables and chairs and terrible wine. I want you to know that. I want it on the record."

A laugh tore out of me, wild and sudden, echoing off the rock. "Noted for the record, Officer Holt."

Behind us, above us, the dam gave a sound I will hear for the rest of my life—a crack like the world's spine breaking, followed by a rush of air and a white bloom of ice dust rising against the stars.

The crack had reached the top of the wall. The lake was coming through.

"One," I said. "Two."

The hum became a roar.

"*Three.*"

Dagny cranked. The night turned white.
