## 19. Where the Current Burns

The mountain had stopped pretending to be a mountain. It was breathing now—great shuddering exhalations of steam that rose from the shattered saddle above the source and hung in the cold air like the ghosts of everything that had ever lived in this valley. Orsen climbed through them with the charge bag heavy on her back and her breath coming in white rags, and every step upward felt like walking into the open mouth of something old and patient.

Below her, far below, Brannvik was emptying. She could see it from here—the town reduced to a scatter of lights along the river's dark spine, headlights crawling out along the valley road in a slow procession, like marrow draining from a bone. The evacuation had been going for eleven hours. Dagny's radio warning, the one she'd sent knowing it would end her career if traced, had bought them those hours. Maren's people descending from Vaskr with mules and lanterns and no names to give had bought more. But eleven hours was not enough, not nearly enough, because the glacial dam above the vent was failing faster than any of them had calculated, and the water behind it was already scalding.

Orsen knew this because she could hear it. A sound like a river arguing with itself, deep under the ice—a groaning, shifting pressure that traveled up through her boots and into her teeth. Twelve years of mountains had taught her every voice stone and snow could make. This one she had never heard before, and she hoped never to hear again after tonight.

*One mile,* she told herself, counting the way she always counted, the old habit rising up like a hand on her shoulder. *One mile to the vent shelf. Four hundred vertical feet. Twenty minutes if the footing holds.*

She had spent her whole life measuring distance because distance was honest. Distance didn't lie to you the way years did, the way people did, the way your own name did when you tried to say it aloud and found it didn't fit anymore. Miles were simple: you put one foot past the other and eventually you arrived or you didn't. Tonight the arithmetic was brutal and clean. Set the charges at the vent shelf, blow the diversion channel Eirik had mapped from Sela's notes, and send the flood west into the empty Kestrel gorge instead of south into the valley. If it worked, the evacuees would come back to wet fields and drowned fences and nothing else. If it failed—

She didn't finish the thought. Finishing thoughts like that was how you died on a mountain; fear wanted an ending, and endings were a luxury you gave yourself afterward, if there was an afterward.

The last pitch before the shelf was blue ice veined with new cracks, and Orsen went up it slowly, front-pointing, feeling each crampon bite answer back with a tremor from somewhere deep in the glacier's body. Steam hissed through the fissures around her, smelling of sulfur and hot stone, and where it condensed on the ice it had carved strange fluted channels, like the throat of some enormous instrument. Once, twelve years ago, she would have stopped to admire it. Tonight she only counted.

Three hundred feet. Two hundred.

The vent shelf opened beneath her like a wound. It was wider than she'd expected—a bowl of black rock perhaps forty meters across, ringed by broken ice, and at its center the earth had torn open to show what it had been hiding all along: a churning pool of grey-white water, boiling gently at its edges, steaming like a kettle left too long on a fire. This was the river's true source. Not the glacier. Not the pristine springhead the maps pretended toward. This—the mountain's own fevered blood, rising for who knows how many centuries through rock heated by something far below, feeding the unnamed tributary, feeding the river, feeding the valley towns that had built their lives beside it without ever once asking where the water came from.

Sela had asked. That was the whole of it, Orsen thought as she crossed the shelf, testing each foothold against the heat radiating up through her boot soles. Sela Halvard had climbed up here with her six-person team, had looked down into the mountain's open mouth, and had done the one thing no one in a government office ever does: she'd told the truth about what she saw. And when they ordered her to bury it, she and her people had walked barefoot into the snow instead—or so the abandoned camp had made it seem—and erased themselves the way Orsen had once dreamed of erasing herself, except they'd done it for a reason worth having.

*You're welcome,* Orsen said silently to the dead woman whose journal had started all of this. *I hope you're watching. I hope the current carries it wherever currents carry things.*

The diversion channel mouth was exactly where Eirik's map said it would be: a shallow depression in the western rim of the bowl, choked with old ice, leading down into the Kestrel gorge. Orsen knelt at its edge and unpacked the charges with hands that were steadier than she had any right to expect. Dagny had requisitioned them from a mining depot two valleys over, forging requisition papers with the same calm competence she brought to everything, and had said only, "If anyone asks, I'll say I lost them." As if losing things were the crime and not the saving.

Orsen worked the way she'd worked a thousand times before—drilling the ice anchors, seating the charges in the seam where the western rim met the main shelf, running line, checking, rechecking. The geometry was simple. Blow the rim low, and the first surge of the dam-break would pour west into the gorge, carving the channel deeper as it went, the water doing most of the work itself. Mountains were generous that way. Give them a crack and they'd make a canyon.

The problem was timing. The charges needed to fire when the dam broke—not before, or the surge would simply refill and spread; not after, or the valley would already be drowning. Which meant someone had to be here, at the vent, watching the ice dam across the pool's upper rim, waiting for the moment it let go.

Someone had to stay.

She had known this since the gorge. She had known it in the way you know weather is turning—not from any single sign but from the whole weight of the air. Dagny had argued with her at the tree line, had argued the whole climb, and Orsen had finally kissed her—hard, brief, tasting of woodsmoke and iodine—and said, "You have a radio and a career and a valley full of people who need someone shouting orders. I have a detonator and a talent for being alone. We're both doing the thing we're best at."

Dagny had looked at her for a long moment with an expression Orsen couldn't read then and still couldn't name now, and had said, "Twelve years you've measured your life in miles. Don't die at the end of one."

Then she'd gone down. Orsen had watched her descend into the steam until the mountain swallowed her, and had turned back to the work, and had not let herself think about it, because thinking about it was a door she couldn't afford to open while her hands were full of explosives.

*Two hundred feet of line to the anchor point. Ten minutes to place the last charge. Then wait.*

She placed the last charge as the sky began to go wrong.

It wasn't dawn yet—it wouldn't be dawn for hours—but the eastern horizon had developed a bruised glow, storm light without a storm, and beneath it the whole upper glacier had begun to move. Orsen felt it before she saw it: a vibration in the rock, low and continuous, like a held note. The ice dam across the pool's head—a wall of ancient ice sixty feet high, riddled now with melt tunnels—shuddered visibly. Chunks calved off its face and fell into the scalding water, vanishing in bursts of steam.

Orsen retreated to the anchor point, coiled the firing line, and crouched behind a spur of black rock with the detonator in her hand. And then she did the hardest thing she had ever done on a mountain, harder than any climb, any rescue, any burial:

She waited.

* * *

Time behaved strangely at the top of a burning mountain. Orsen measured it the only way she could—in small, countable things. The pulse of the glow beneath the ice. The interval between tremors, which shortened from minutes to seconds. The steam plume rising off the pool, which thickened until it blotted out the stars entirely, so that she sat in a private grey noon lit from below.

She thought about miles. She thought about how she'd learned to count them—on the trail with her father, before the blacklist, before everything, a girl of nine walking the Kestrel approach with her father saying, *Count them, little wren, count them and the trail can't beat you.* He'd been wrong about a lot of things, her father, but he'd been right about that: counting kept the fear in its cage. For thirty years she had counted. Miles walked, miles guided, miles between herself and the disaster that had ended her old life. Twelve years ago she had stood in a hearing room while Eirik Halvard testified that she'd ignored protocol, that she'd pushed the client past safe margins, that the fall was her fault—and she had said nothing, because saying something meant admitting the truth was complicated, and complicated truths don't fit on a blacklist form. So she'd taken the name they gave her—disgraced guide, unsafe, barred—and she'd walked into the northern highlands and made a life of counting miles away from everything.

And now here she sat, at the source of the river that wasn't on maps, about to save the very valley system that had discarded her, and the strangest part—the part she knew she'd carry out of this night if she survived it—was that she wasn't doing it for redemption. She'd stopped believing in redemption somewhere around mile ten thousand. She was doing it because the people downstream were there, and the water was coming, and she was the one who knew the mountain. That was all. That had always been all. Competence was just love wearing work clothes.

The tremor, when it came, lifted her off the rock.

Not fully—maybe an inch, maybe less—but she felt her body leave the ground and return, and the sound that accompanied it was not a sound so much as a change in what sound was: a subsonic roar she felt in her sternum, in her fillings, in the fluid of her eyes. Across the shelf, the ice dam cracked from base to crown with a report like a rifle the size of the sky.

Orsen pressed the detonator.

Nothing happened.

She checked the line—intact, connections good—and pressed again, and this time the western rim of the bowl answered: three dull thumps, one after another, and a bloom of orange light as the charges tore the old ice loose. The diversion channel mouth sagged, crumbled, opened—a ragged gap in the rim, dropping away westward into the gorge.

But the dam hadn't broken yet. The great wall of ice leaned, groaned, held. Water behind it was rising fast now, visible even in the dark, a black gleam climbing the dam's face. If the dam failed before the channel was ready to take the flow—if the surge hit the half-opened gap and simply piled against it—

"Come on," Orsen whispered. "Come on, come on—"

The dam broke.

There was no other word for it. One moment the wall stood; the next it was gone, dissolved into a hundred thousand fragments that the water itself carried forward, and the flood came off the shelf like the mountain exhaling a century of held breath. Orsen had seen flash floods in desert canyons. She had seen avalanches. She had believed she understood what moving water could do.

She understood better now.

The surge hit the diversion gap and the gap took it—most of it. A grey-brown wall of scalding water poured west into the gorge with a sound like the world tearing, and Orsen felt a wild, fierce joy spike through her terror because it was working, the geometry was working, the mountain was taking the crack she'd given it and making a canyon—

But water doesn't negotiate. The channel filled faster than it could cut, and the overflow came back across the shelf in a spreading sheet, ankle-deep, knee-deep, hot enough that she felt it through her insulated pants, and the shelf itself began to fail beneath her, great slabs of the black rock tilting and sliding as their foundations melted. Orsen scrambled for higher ground on the eastern rim, the detonator useless in her hand now, its work done, and the water chased her, patient and total, the way water always is.

A slab tilted under her left foot. She threw her weight right, caught a fisthold of ice, and the world went sideways—shelf, steam, sky, flood, all of it rotating—and then the water had her.

Scalding closed over her head. Not boiling—ten degrees short of it, maybe less—but hot enough to shock every muscle she owned into contradiction, and the current took her the way the river had taken everything else: completely, without malice, without interest. She tumbled. Rock struck her shoulder, her hip, the back of her skull. Her lungs burned with the breath she was holding, and somewhere inside her a calm, professional voice observed that she had perhaps ninety seconds of consciousness left, and that the flood was carrying her east, downstream, toward the valley, toward everything she'd tried to save, and that she would not survive the ride.

*Ninety seconds.* She'd measured her whole life in miles and it had come down to seconds, and the irony of it might have made her laugh if she'd had any air to laugh with.

*Let go,* said a voice in her head that sounded like her father. *Stop counting. Just let go.*

She was trying. God help her, she was trying—her hands were open, her body had gone limp in the way that saves swimmers, and the water rolled her over and over like a stone in a tumbler, and the last thing she registered before the dark came down was a shape in the flood above her. A dark shape, human, on a line, reaching.

Then hands. Hands closing around her wrist, her collar, hauling, and the water fighting the whole time, and a voice screaming her name through the roar—a voice she knew, a voice that had no business being anywhere on this mountain—

Dagny.

* * *

She woke coughing on black rock, on her side, vomiting grey water, and the first thing she felt was hands on her back—steady, hard, trembling slightly—and the second thing was the voice.

"Breathe. Breathe, damn you, breathe—there. There. Again."

Orsen breathed. The air tasted of sulfur and rain and, faintly, of woodsmoke, though there was no fire up here, and she understood distantly that the woodsmoke was just Dagny, Dagny who smelled of the cookfire at the tree line camp, Dagny who was supposed to be a thousand feet below.

"You—" Orsen managed, and coughed again. "You were supposed—to be—downhill."

"I was downhill." Dagny's face swam into focus above her, soaked, streaked with ash-grey meltwater, one eyebrow split and bleeding freely. She looked furious and terrified and more alive than anyone Orsen had ever seen. "I got to the second switchback and I listened to the mountain start coming apart and I thought, *she's up there alone, and I have spent my entire life following orders, and I am done.*" Her voice cracked on the last word. "So I climbed. I tied into the old survey line and I climbed, and then the shelf went and I saw you go into the water, and I—" She stopped. Her jaw worked. "I've arrested people for less stupid rescues than mine. I'll court-martial myself later."

"The channel—" Orsen rasped.

"It held. Most of it went west. The gorge is eating it." Dagny pulled her own jacket off and wrapped it around Orsen's shoulders, and her hands were shaking now, shaking badly, the adrenaline bill coming due. "The valley's getting runoff, not the main surge. Brannvik's levees will hold. Maren's people are sandbagging anyway—they wouldn't listen to me when I told them to get clear, they just kept working." A wet, disbelieving laugh escaped her. "They unmasked themselves to strangers to save a town that didn't know they existed. And you—you absolute stubborn—"

"Dagny." Orsen caught her wrist. Her own arm barely obeyed. "Why?"

The question was bigger than the word. They both knew it. Why climb into a flood? Why burn a career on a forged requisition and an unauthorized radio call? Why stand in a hearing room and destroy yourself corroborating a blacklisted guide? Why any of it?

Dagny looked at her for a long time. The steam plume was thinning now; stars were coming back through it, one by one, indifferent and beautiful. Somewhere below, the flood roared westward through the gorge, doing its work, keeping its bargain.

"Because duty told me to leave," Dagny said finally. "And for the first time in my life, I didn't want what duty wanted. I wanted—" She stopped again, and this time she didn't restart with something safer. "I wanted you. Alive. That's the whole report. That's everything I know."

Orsen had spent twelve years refusing to be chosen—for anything, by anyone, because being chosen meant being seen, and being seen meant being lost. She had built a whole life out of the space between herself and other people, and she had called that space freedom, and it had taken a burning mountain to show her it was just a smaller kind of prison.

She reached up with a hand that shook and pulled Dagny down until their foreheads touched. Water dripped between them. Neither of them moved.

"I measure in miles," Orsen whispered. "I've measured in miles my whole life. Teach me to measure in something else."

"Years," Dagny said against her mouth. "Start with years."

Above them, the vent sighed and settled, its fury spent westward into the gorge. Below them, the valley lights flickered and held. And Orsen Nystrom, disgraced guide, erased woman, survivor of the current that burned, lay on the black rock with her arms around the woman who had refused to leave, and for the first time in twelve years she did not count anything at all.

Far below, at the tree line, a lantern began climbing toward them through the dark—Maren's people, coming up through the flood's aftermath, bringing rope and blankets and the first witnesses of what had happened at the source. What they would find there—two women alive, a valley saved, a settlement exposed to the world whether it was ready or not—was a story that had only just begun.

The lantern light grew closer.
