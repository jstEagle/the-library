## 20. Afterwater

The water came down the valley like a rumor made flesh.

Orsen stood on the ridge above Brannvik and watched it happen—the thing she had spent three days running downhill to prevent, arriving anyway, but gentler than it might have been. The diversion channel she'd blown open at the source had taken the worst of the surge, splitting the flood into two braided arms, one of which spent itself against the old quarry terraces while the other spread out across the lowland fields in a slow brown sheet that looked, from this height, almost peaceful. Almost deliberate. As if the river had finally been given permission to go where it had always wanted.

The fields went first. Barley, turnip greens, the flax plots east of town—everything green within a kilometer of the old channel drowned under water the color of weak tea. Fences vanished. A hay barn lifted off its foundation and traveled forty meters with a kind of patient dignity before settling into a ditch and coming apart, its boards fanning out across the current like a hand opening.

No one was in it. No one was anywhere they shouldn't have been. That was the arithmetic Orsen kept performing, over and over, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth: *four hundred and eleven souls in Brannvik, two hundred and six in the hamlets upstream, every one of them standing on high ground when the dam let go.* She had counted them herself, or Dagny had, or Maren's people had gone door to door in the rain with lanterns and voices that did not identify themselves by name. The number held. The number held every time she checked it.

Below her, the town stood on its knoll like an island in a temporary sea, rooftops steaming where the rain had stopped and the sun had found gaps in the cloud. Smoke rose from chimneys. Someone had put a kettle on. Orsen felt something loosen in her chest that she didn't have a name for—not relief exactly, relief implied surprise, and she had known for three days this was how it would end: fields lost, lives kept. It was closer to the feeling of setting down a pack after a long carry, the shoulders remembering their own weight.

She turned to say something to Dagny and found Dagny asleep.

The survey officer sat propped against a boulder with her radio still clipped to her belt, her requisitioned charges case empty beside her, her boots caked in gray mud to the knee. Her face in sleep was younger and older at once—all the protocol scrubbed off it, all the duty, leaving just a tired woman who had burned her career on a mountainside because it was the only thing left worth burning. There was a cut above her eyebrow from the gorge, butterfly-stitched with tape. Her breathing was deep and even, the breathing of someone who has stopped listening for orders.

Orsen sat down beside her without waking her, took off her own boots, and poured out maybe half a liter of the river.

* * *

Dagny dreamed of paperwork.

Not the flood, not the vent, not the moment on the ridge when the charges went and the mountain exhaled and the whole sky smelled of sulfur and snowmelt. Paperwork. Forms in triplicate. A tribunal room with a long table and seven faces behind it, and her name spoken aloud in a tone she recognized from funerals. She woke with her heart going hard and the sun already well up and Orsen sitting beside her barefoot, wringing out her socks with the methodical patience of someone doing the hundredth necessary thing of the day.

"You slept," Orsen said. Not a question. An observation, faintly accusing, as if sleep were a resource Dagny had hoarded unfairly.

"So did you. Eventually."

"I don't remember it." Orsen hung the socks on a gorse bush. "That's how I know it happened."

Dagny's radio crackled. Then crackled again, insistently, and kept crackling, and the voice inside it was one Dagny knew the way you know a stone in your boot—Regional Director Halvorsen, third name on the inquiry authorization she'd been dreading since Brannvik, requesting—*requiring*—Survey Officer Holt to report to the provisional administration office in Brannvik at fourteen hundred hours, and bringing with her, quote, "any documentation pertaining to the unauthorized evacuation broadcast," unquote.

Dagny listened to the whole message twice. Orsen watched her face do very little, which told Orsen everything.

"They want your head," Orsen said.

"They want my head, my testimony, my personnel file, and a full accounting of government property expended." Dagny clipped the radio back on her belt. "The charges were requisitioned under a geological-survey permit. I used them to alter a hydrological feature. There is probably a form for that. There is definitely not a form for what I actually did."

"Which was?"

"Tell the truth on an open channel." Dagny said it flatly, but her hand stayed on the radio a beat too long, the way you touch a wound to confirm it still hurts. "Fourteen seconds of transmission. That's all it took. Fourteen seconds and twenty years of service becomes a question instead of a fact."

Orsen pulled her damp boots back on. "You could recant. Say the signal was spoofed. Say I stole your handset. Nobody would be surprised—a blacklisted guide, obviously a criminal element."

"I considered it," Dagny said. "For about four seconds on the ridge, while you were underwater." She stood, brushed off nothing in particular, and looked down at the drowned fields with an expression Orsen couldn't read until she realized it wasn't an expression at all—it was the absence of one, the face of a woman who had finished calculating and found the sum acceptable. "I decided I'd rather answer questions for the rest of my life than not have any worth answering. Come on. If they're going to hang me, you're going to stand next to me and tell them everything."

"I'm not exactly a credible witness."

"No," Dagny agreed. "You're worse than credible. You're *inconvenient*. Credible witnesses can be managed. You're going to make them work for it." And there, briefly, was the ghost of a smile—the first one Orsen had seen on her since the gorge—and it did more to steady Orsen than any amount of reassurance would have.

They walked down into Brannvik together through the smell of wet earth and diesel and woodsmoke, past people hauling furniture to second floors and children wading the new shallows with nets, hunting the fish the flood had delivered to their streets. A woman washing silt off a doorstep looked up at Dagny's uniform and said, without heat, "You're the one who shouted at us over the wireless." Dagny said, "Yes." The woman nodded once and went back to her step, and that was the whole exchange, and somehow it was enough.

* * *

Maren had not slept either, but then Maren hadn't slept properly in thirty years, and one more night barely registered.

She stood at the window of the Brannvik schoolhouse—which had become, in the space of two days, a command post, a refugee canteen, and now a negotiating chamber—and watched Vaskr's people come down the valley road in a line that stretched back farther than she could count.

They had not been ordered to descend. She had asked, the night before the dam failed, standing in the meeting hall with the tremor still rattling the rafters, and she had told them plainly: the towns below will drown unless someone carries the warning door to door, and we are the ones close enough and few enough to move fast, and going means being seen, means names, means the end of everything we built. She had expected argument. She had expected Eirik's faction and the cautious faction and the long silent middle to split along the same old seams.

Instead, Sela's surviving teammate—a young surveyor named Pell whose hands still shook whenever he touched a map—had stood up and said, "We already chose exile once to avoid lying. We can choose exposure once to avoid drowning strangers. It's the same choice. We just get to make it better this time."

And that had been that. Thirty-one adults and nine children, packing food they could not explain owning, taking down trails no official map showed, walking into the valley with their faces bare.

Now they came down the road in the morning light, and Maren watched the townspeople watch them, and waited for the fear.

It didn't come—or it came and passed through, the way weather passes through. Because the Vaskr folk arrived carrying children who weren't theirs, hauling elderly Brannvik farmers on improvised litters, distributing bread and dried fish from packs stamped with no government mark, and it is very difficult to fear a people whose first act among you is to feed you. An old woman named Signe, who had lived in Vaskr since before Maren led it, stopped in the schoolyard, looked around at the mud and the soldiers and the officials' cars, and said loudly, to no one and everyone: "Forty years I haven't said my name out loud. It's Astrid. Astrid Vik. It tastes strange. Like bread after fasting."

People laughed. And then, one by one, others said theirs—hesitant, rusty, some of them stumbling over syllables they hadn't spoken since youth—and the schoolyard filled with names the way a dry creek fills with the first meltwater, and Maren felt something break in her chest that was not grief, though it used all of grief's machinery.

A man in a regional administration coat approached her with a clipboard and the careful expression of someone about to handle something explosive. "Madam—you are the... spokesperson? For these persons?"

"I'm Maren. I keep the records." She paused, and allowed herself the smallest of smiles. "Though I suppose the records are public now."

"There will need to be registration. Documentation. Amnesties negotiated at the regional level, possibly the national—" He flipped a page. "There are precedents, actually. Border communities, draft evaders from the last war, certain religious—there are *frameworks*. It will take months. Possibly years. But no one," and here he glanced up, and his voice dropped as if the clipboard itself might be listening, "no one is talking about prosecution. Not after what they did. Not after—" He gestured vaguely uphill, toward the source, toward the diversion channel, toward the reason his own mother was alive in a church hall in Hammar rather than dead in her farmhouse. "The word going around is *whistleblowers*. Have you heard that word applied to your people yet?"

"I've heard it applied to one of them," Maren said quietly. "Posthumously. It hasn't reached the rest of us yet. Give it time."

The man wrote something down. Maren watched him write it and understood, with the clarity of exhaustion, that history was being made in the least heroic register imaginable—in clipboards, in frameworks, in the slow bureaucratic digestion of the impossible. Vaskr had spent forty years erasing itself, and it would take the state forty minutes of paperwork to begin writing it back in.

She found Eirik in the canteen, sitting with a cup of tea he hadn't drunk, staring at the wall where someone had pinned a child's drawing of the flood—a brown scribble, a stick-figure town on a hill, and above it, in enormous crayon letters, THANK YOU RIVER PEOPLE.

"She'd have hated the name," Eirik said, without turning. "Sela. 'Whistleblower.' Too tidy. She'd have wanted the whole mess recorded—the arguments, the orders she refused, the night we voted. All of it. She never believed in clean stories."

"Then we'll give them the whole mess," Maren said, and sat down beside him, and for a while neither of them drank anything, and that was its own kind of communion.

* * *

The hearing—if you could call it that; it had the shape of a tribunal and the temperature of a feeding frenzy—took place in the Brannvik chapel because it was the largest intact room, and it was packed.

Dagny sat at the front in a uniform she had deliberately not ironed, and answered questions for four hours. Yes, the transmission was hers. Yes, she knew it violated directive eleven. Yes, she had been aware of the consequences. No, she had not been coerced. No, she would not characterize her actions as a lapse. When the chairman asked, wearily, what she *would* characterize them as, she said, "The minimum required by the oath I actually took, as opposed to the one printed in the manual," and the room made a sound that was not quite applause and not quite laughter and was, unmistakably, agreement.

Then they called for Orsen Nystrom, and the room changed.

She felt it as she walked down the aisle—the weight of recognition, murmurs traveling ahead of her like wind through barley. *Nystrom. The Kestrel guide. The one from the Meridian expedition.* Twelve years of being a cautionary tale, and here she was in the flesh, and half the room had opinions about her that predated anything she'd done this week. A man near the front—she recognized him distantly, a mining consortium man, one of the faces behind the claims Sela's team had died refusing to protect—leaned over to his neighbor and said, audibly, "This is the woman whose file says she abandoned her client."

Orsen stopped beside his row. Looked down at him. Let the silence stretch until it hurt.

"My file also says the Kestrel Range has no geothermal features," she said. "Files are written by people who weren't there. Ask me what happened. That's why you called me."

And they did. For five hours, they asked her everything—the storm on the Kestrel, the checkpoint, the journal ending mid-word, the river that wasn't on maps, the campsite with boots lined up neat as church pews, the crossing that nearly took Dagny, the smoke, the cairn, Vaskr, Eirik, Sela's pages, the council, the tremor, the gorge, the charges, the water rising around her waist and then her chest while she crimped the last detonator with numb fingers. She gave it to them straight, no drama, guide's voice, terrain report style, and the plainness of it did what eloquence couldn't: it made the room believe.

When she described burning her blacklist file—Dagny's copy of it, fed page by page into the stove at the Vaskr meeting hall the night before the descent, twelve years of fabricated testimony curling into ash that smelled like every winter she'd lost—the chairman asked her if she understood that the original document would be reconstructed from archives, that expungement would have to be granted formally, that burning it changed nothing legally.

"It changed everything else," Orsen said. "Legally, I've been a liar for twelve years. Practically, I stopped being one the minute the paper caught. You can grant the rest or not. I've already had it."

The mining man tried twice more—one insinuation about her judgment, one about her association with "the defector community"—and both times the room turned on him with a coldness that needed no words. By the final session, nobody was asking whether Orsen Nystrom was credible. They were asking her to look at maps.

* * *

Evening came down on Brannvik gold and bruised, the floodwater in the fields catching the last light so the whole valley floor seemed to burn gently, a sea of hammered copper going dark at the edges.

Orsen found Dagny on the chapel steps, alone, holding an envelope she hadn't opened. Official seal. Regional directorate.

"Suspension pending inquiry?" Orsen guessed.

"Worse." Dagny turned it over in her hands. "It's a summons. National level. They want me in the capital. They want *both* of us, actually—your name's on it too. And there's a line in here about 'the Sela Halvard disclosure matter' that suggests somebody upstairs has decided this isn't a scandal anymore. Somebody upstairs has decided it's an *opportunity*." She looked up at Orsen, and the exhaustion in her face had a strange brightness behind it, like embers under ash. "I've spent my whole career learning what it means when the government suddenly wants to help you. It means they've figured out which side of the story photographs well."

"Does it matter?" Orsen sat down beside her. Below them, in the flooded twilight, lanterns moved across the water in small boats—Vaskr folk and Brannvik folk together, checking fences, pulling livestock feed from submerged sheds, doing the thousand small repairs that a valley does after the river takes its toll. Somewhere a fiddle had started up, badly, cheerfully. "Whatever they want to call it—whistleblowers, heroes, amnesty cases—we know what it was. A bunch of people who refused to lie, and a river that refused to wait. Everything else is them arguing about the wording."

Dagny was quiet for a moment. Then she opened the envelope, read it through once, and did something Orsen had never seen her do: she laughed. Short, disbelieving, real.

"What?"

"They've attached a travel allowance." She waved the paper. "Per diem. Expense schedule. First-class rail if available." She shook her head. "Twelve days ago I was a functionary with a blacklist file and a missing team. Now I'm a first-class rail passenger with a national summons. And you—" she looked at Orsen sideways, and the ember-brightness was fully lit now, warm as the geothermal water that had nearly killed them both, "—you're the most famous guide in the country, and every agency that blacklisted you is going to spend the next year pretending they always thought highly of you."

"I don't want their agencies."

"No. But you might want what comes after them. There's talk—actual talk—of a proper survey of the new channel. Of remapping the whole headwaters. Of putting names on the things the old maps refused to name." Dagny folded the letter away. "Somebody will have to lead those surveys. Somebody who knows the ground. Somebody inconvenient."

Orsen looked out at the water, at the lanterns, at the fiddle player's window glowing yellow above the floodline, and felt the old arithmetic stir in her—the miles, the counting, the habit of measuring life in distance survived—and found, testing it like a foot on uncertain ice, that it no longer held her weight. Something else did. Something measured in seasons, in shared tents, in a woman who had chosen her over protocol on a mountainside and was sitting beside her now smelling of woodsmoke and wet wool.

"When do we leave?" she said.

"Rail's at dawn." Dagny stood, offered her hand, and pulled her up. "Better rest. I have a feeling the capital's going to want the whole story."

"The whole story takes twenty-two chapters," Orsen said.

"Then they'd better give us a big table."

Below them, the floodwater went on quietly covering the fields, patient as history, and the lanterns moved across it like names being written one by one on a map that was, at last, telling the truth.
