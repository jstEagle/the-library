## 22. Measuring in Years

The melt came late that spring, and when it came it came all at once, the way grief does—held back and held back until some small warmth undoes everything.

Orsen stood at the cabin door with a tin cup of coffee going cold in her hands and watched the Kestrel Range weep itself white to green. Snowmelt ran down every gully and seam of the mountains in bright threads, gathering into streams she had known by name for twenty years, all of them hurrying now toward the valley floor, toward Brannvik, toward the broad brown scar where the flood had taken the fields but not the people.

Not the people. She made herself say it that way every morning, out loud, like a prayer or an inventory. *Fields, yes. People, no.* The bridge at Millward was gone. The lower pastures were silted gray. But every soul in the valley had walked out of the rain alive, and on most days that arithmetic was enough to keep her hands steady.

On other days it wasn't, and those days she went up.

She went up because the mountain was the only place her body still understood what it was for. Twelve years of exile had taught her that a person could survive almost anything except uselessness, and the flood had given her back a use: the new river course, the one nobody had charted yet, the one the old maps rendered as a rumor and the government hadn't gotten around to claiming. The current had cut itself a fresh channel through the gorge during the surge, abandoning the vent's old throat for a longer, gentler line east. Water always finds the honest path eventually. It had just needed help getting there.

Dagny's truck came up the switchbacks at seven, engine laboring against the grade, and Orsen finished her coffee before the headlights swept the dooryard. Some things you did not rush. Dagny Holt arriving anywhere was one of them; she liked to sit in the cab a moment after killing the engine, gathering herself, as if the drive from the lowlands required her to put the officer back on piece by piece. Lately she took longer about it. Lately, sometimes, she forgot pieces entirely and climbed out looking like just a tired woman with surveyor's calluses and ink on her fingers.

"You're up early," Dagny said, hauling the map case from the passenger seat.

"The river doesn't wait."

"The river has been doing nothing but wait for ten thousand years."

"It waited for us specifically," Orsen said. "That's different."

Dagny smiled—the crooked, unpracticed smile she was still learning to let happen—and shouldered the case, and they started up.

* * *

They mapped without instruments, mostly. Dagny had brought a transit and a theodolite and a case of government-stamped brass, and they sat in the truck bed at the trailhead while she looked at all of it, and then she left it there. "For the inquiry," she said. "Let them see I had the tools and chose not to use them." What they carried instead was a notebook, a pencil, a length of knotted cord, and two pairs of eyes that had between them spent forty years reading water.

It was slow work and it was good work. They walked the new channel from the gorge mouth upward, pacing distances, noting bends, sketching the gravel bars where the flood had sorted its stones like a miser counting coin. At each significant bend Dagny recorded the bearing and Orsen recorded what the bearing meant—where the bank would fail in another decade, where a town might safely drink, where the current ran quiet enough for children. Two kinds of knowledge braided together on the page. Neither was worth much alone.

"Here." Dagny stopped at a wide shelf of rock above the third meander and spread the notebook on her knee. "If anyone ever builds again downstream, this is the line they'll need. Flood stage, high water mark, everything."

"If anyone builds," Orsen said. "Maren says the valley will take twenty years to trust the water again."

"Maren says a lot of things." Dagny wrote, tongue between her teeth. "She also sent four hundred pounds of seed potatoes down to Brannvik last week under her own name. Her actual name. First time she's signed it in thirty years, according to Eirik."

"How is he?"

"Healing. He complains about the leg the way other men complain about weather—constantly, and with a strange fondness." Dagny blew a strand of hair off her face. "He asked about you."

"Tell him I'm well."

"That's not what he asked."

Orsen looked upstream, where the channel narrowed into white noise. "I know what he asked. Tell him I'm working on it. Forgiveness isn't a crossing you make once. Some rivers you ford every day of your life, and some days the water's higher than others, and that's just—" she gestured at the whole gray-green world, "—that's just hydrology."

Dagny wrote something in the margin that wasn't a bearing. Orsen didn't ask what. There were only so many confidences a spring could hold before it burst its banks.

* * *

They reached the source on the fourth day.

The vent was quiet now—or quiet the way a healed bone is quiet, carrying its fracture forever beneath the skin. The geothermal survey teams had come and gone through the winter, capping, monitoring, leaving sensors strung across the scarred ground like festival lights. The ground steamed gently in the morning cold. Where the scalding surge had scoured everything to mineral white, moss had already begun its patient reconquest, green freckles spreading across the burn the way lichen takes a gravestone.

Sela's team had camped here once. Six tents pitched in perfect order, boots lined up, a journal open to a sentence that never finished. Orsen had stood in that camp a year ago—no, less, eight months, though it felt like a former life owned by a stranger—and felt the particular dread of a guide who has seen every way a mountain kills and none of them look like this. Now she stood on the same ground and felt something else entirely. Not dread. Debt.

"We're here," Dagny said quietly. "You're sure about this?"

"I've been sure since winter."

They built the cairn together, because some work shouldn't be done alone. There was stone everywhere—the flood had left the slope littered with it—and they chose flat ones, fitting them without mortar the old way, each stone finding the two beneath it that would hold it. Orsen worked from memory of her grandfather's hands, though her grandfather had been dead thirty years and she had never once watched him build anything. Some knowledge arrives in the blood without asking permission. The cairn rose waist-high, then shoulder-high, then done: a rough human shape of gathered stone, standing at the headwaters where the current began.

Dagny held out the chisel and the small hammer without being asked. Orsen took them and knelt at the capstone.

She had rehearsed the inscription all winter and discarded every version. *Sela Halvard* was too small. *Whistleblower* was the government's word and the government would never own it now anyway—the inquiry had closed in March, the team posthumously honored, their names read aloud in a hall full of people who had ordered them silenced—but official words were ash and she wanted living ones. In the end she carved what the journal's last unfinished sentence had been reaching toward, the thought Sela Halvard had died—had *left*, had chosen exile rather than complicity—still trying to say:

**SELA HALVARD
WHO FOLLOWED THE WATER TO THE TRUTH
AND STAYED WITH IT**

Below, smaller, because her hand was shaking and honesty required it:

**THE RIVER REMEMBERS**

Dagny read it twice. Then she said, "Her father will want to see this."

"He can't make the climb."

"No. So we'll bring the climb to him." Dagny took out her camera—the plain film camera she'd bought with her own money, the one that answered to no agency—and photographed the cairn from four angles, careful and unhurried, the way you photograph something you intend to keep. "And the hearing transcripts go public next month. Every school in the valley will have a copy. Let them come up if they want. Let them all come up." She lowered the camera. "That's what Maren said, actually. When they voted on amnesty. She said a secret you've paid for isn't a secret anymore, it's a grave, and graves are for visiting."

Orsen touched the capstone's cold edge. Steam rose around them, and somewhere below the snowmelt gathered itself into the first bright thread of the current that would run all the way down to the sea, gathering tributaries the way a life gathers years.

"She measured in miles too, at the end," Orsen said. "In her journal. Distances, elevations, flow rates. Right up until the last page. And then she stopped mid-word, and I used to think that was a tragedy—that the record broke off. Now I think maybe it was the truest thing in it. Maybe she finally hit a number that wouldn't hold what she was trying to say, so she set the pencil down and became it instead."

Dagny said nothing for a while. Wind moved the steam sideways. Then: "What are you measuring in these days?"

Orsen considered the question seriously, because Dagny always asked seriously and deserved serious answers. She thought about the twelve years she had counted in miles—miles guided, miles banned from, miles between herself and everyone who knew her name—and how the numbers had kept her alive the way a cage keeps a bird alive, technically, at terrible cost. She thought about the flood season behind them and the growing season ahead. She thought about a woman climbing out of a truck every morning, putting the officer on piece by piece, forgetting more pieces lately.

"Seasons," she said. "I'm measuring in seasons now. This is the first one. Spring, year one." She looked at Dagny, and found her looking back with an expression Orsen had seen on survivors and on people falling in love, which were often the same expression. "Ask me again in summer."

* * *

They descended over two days, mapping as they went, and on the second evening they camped above the gorge where the new channel ran calm and deep through country that had been scoured bare and was already, impossibly, greening. Orsen cooked trout over a small fire because Dagny had caught them badly and proudly, and afterward they sat with their boots off and their feet toward the flames and the whole valley laid out below them in the blue dusk, Brannvik's windows lighting up one by one like a slow constellation remembering its stars.

"There's a letter," Dagny said, when the fire had burned down to coals. "From the ministry. Came yesterday. They're offering me my old post back. Reinstatement, commendation, the works. Rebuild the survey office, my terms, my staff."

"What did you tell them?"

"Nothing yet. That's why there's a letter and not an answer." She turned a coal with a stick. "There's a condition buried in paragraph nine. The new office would administer the Vaskr corridor. Permits, checkpoints, oversight." She glanced up. "Fences, essentially. Polite ones. Around people who just unmasked themselves to save a valley."

"And?"

"And I told them I'd consider it." A long pause. Firelight moved on her face. "But I've been thinking about what fences cost, and who pays, and I keep arriving at numbers I don't like. So maybe I write back and decline. Maybe I write back and accept and spend five years dismantling paragraph nine from inside. Or maybe—" she laughed, short and self-conscious, "—maybe I buy a theodolite of my own, with my own money, and map things for whoever needs them mapped. No stamps. No seals. Just a woman with a cord and a notebook."

Orsen fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch. "The valley needs a surveyor."

"The valley has a guide."

"The valley," Orsen said, "has both. That was rather the point of the winter."

Dagny looked at her across the coals, and whatever she saw there made her stop pretending to fuss with the fire. "You know what frightens me? I've spent fifteen years believing duty was a direction. North. Orders. You walk the bearing you're given. And then one flood season and one stubborn disgraced guide later, I find out duty was a person all along, and I've been walking away from her this entire time."

"Then stop measuring the distance," Orsen said. "Start measuring the seasons."

Above them the first stars came out over the Kestrel Range, and below them the new river ran on through the dark toward the sea, carrying snowmelt and mineral light and the names of everyone who had ever followed it to the truth. Orsen lay back on her bedroll and did not count the miles to morning. She counted nothing at all. She let the night be as long as it was, and slept.

* * *

Summer came the way it always did to the highlands—suddenly, extravagantly, the tundra igniting purple and gold as if the mountain had been holding its breath all winter and finally exhaled color.

On the solstice they climbed to the source again, and this time they did not come alone.

Eirik came, riding the front of a supply sled Maren had rigged with a seat and a brace for his leg, complaining cheerfully the entire ascent about the gradient, the altitude, the ingratitude of mountains in general. Maren came, walking the whole way without appearing to tire, signing her name in the summit register Dagny had installed—a plank, a pencil, a tin roof—in handwriting that shook only a little. Forty-one residents of Vaskr came, some of whom had not stood above the tree line in decades, and stood around the cairn in the wind with their hats in their hands. Two survey officers came from Brannvik, young, earnest, carrying Sela's published journal in a new printing, and asked shy questions about route conditions as if the woman who had written it might answer.

And a girl came—sixteen, dark-haired, Sela's jaw and Eirik's stubbornness set in a face Orsen didn't recognize until Eirik introduced her: his younger daughter, born the year of the betrayal, raised on a story about a guide named Nystrom that had turned out, like most stories, to be half true and wholly insufficient. She stood before the cairn a long time, reading the carved letters, and then she took out a notebook—not her father's, her own—and began, carefully, to copy the inscription onto the first blank page.

"What will you do with it?" Orsen asked her.

The girl looked up. She had Sela's eyes too, apparently. The river gave back what it took, in its own season, in its own form.

"I'm going to finish the sentence," she said. "The one she didn't get to. I've been reading her journal all spring and I think I know how it ends. It ends with someone coming up here after her. It ends with the water being followed all the way home." She closed the notebook. "I'm going to be a surveyor. For the valley. Not for any government—for the valley."

Downslope, Dagny caught Orsen's eye and raised an eyebrow: *Well. Look at that.* The current, finding its honest path. It always did, given time, given help, given someone willing to stand at the source and refuse to lie about where the water came from.

They stayed until the light went long and amber, and then they went down, all of them together, the sled runners hissing on summer snow, the whole loose procession of the saved and the forgiven spilling down the mountain toward the valley where the fields were being replanted in straight confident rows. At the tree line Orsen paused and looked back once. The cairn stood small and dark against the brightening sky, bearing its name, keeping its watch over the beginning of the water.

One season down, she thought. Year one, spring into summer.

She turned and followed the others down, measuring nothing, needing to measure nothing, the distance home already shorter than any mile she had ever counted—and getting shorter every day, the way it does, when you finally stop walking away.
