## 1. Miles, Not Years

The rope came off the rack smelling of new nylon and factory wax, and Elodie Montrose ran her thumb along its sheath the way other people ran their thumbs along the spines of old books. Nine point eight millimeters. Dry-treated core. The kind of rope you bought when you expected the mountain to try to kill you and wanted to be rude about it when it failed.

"Three of those," she said to Dawa behind the counter. "And whatever carabiners you've got that aren't counterfeit."

"They're all counterfeit," Dawa said. "But mine are counterfeit honestly. I'll tell you which ones to trust."

"That's all I ask."

The shop was called The Iron Yak, which was ambitious for a building that leaned six degrees toward the river, but it was the last real outfitter before the border and the first real one after it, depending on which direction you were walking, and so it did a steady trade in everything from titanium stakes to knockoff down jackets that would keep you warm exactly long enough to die somewhere inconvenient. Dawa had run it for thirty years. He had watched Elodie come through it for twenty of those, and in all that time he had never once asked her a question she didn't want to answer, which made him one of maybe four people alive who could say that.

She laid the gear out on the counter and counted it twice, because counting was a thing she did, because counting was a thing that kept the world in its boxes.

"Supply run to the monastery at Thangpo," Dawa said, reading the manifest upside down out of habit. "That's easy money."

"That's the point."

"Salt, flour, diesel, rope for the prayer flags. Two weeks, in and out." He squinted at her over his glasses. "You used to take harder contracts, Elodie."

"I used to be stupider."

"You used to be hungrier."

"Same thing," she said, and signed the manifest, and that was the whole conversation, because Dawa knew the shape of her the way she knew the shape of a route — where it was safe to put weight and where it wasn't.

Outside, the border town was doing what border towns do, which is everything at once and none of it well. Mules in the street. Diesel smoke. A man selling oranges out of a crate that had clearly been an ammunition box in a former life. Two flags over the customs post, one of them faded to the color of weak tea, both of them arguing with the wind. The Karveth Range stood behind all of it, north and white and enormous, the way a debt stands behind a man who has stopped opening his mail.

Elodie looked at it for exactly as long as it took to check the cloud line — building over the western peaks, high cirrus, nothing organized — and then looked away, because that was the discipline, that was the whole discipline, twenty years of looking away at the right moment.

"Boss." Priya fell into step beside her, a crate of stove fuel on her shoulder like it weighed nothing, which for Priya it basically didn't. "We've got a problem."

"How big?"

"Man-sized. Notebook-sized." She nodded across the street, toward the tea house, where a man in a clean jacket that had never been above three thousand meters was watching them with the particular stillness of a person who has been waiting to be noticed. "He's been asking about you in three shops. Dawa's cousin tipped me."

Elodie didn't change her pace. "Journalist?"

"Or worse. He's got the soft hands of a journalist and the eyes of a process server."

"Wonderful." She set her pack down on the bench outside the tea house, deliberately, the way you'd set down a card you were choosing to play. "Tea, then."

The man crossed the street too fast, which told her he was young, which told her he was eager, which told her he was dangerous in the way that young and eager men are dangerous — not because of what they know, but because of what they want badly enough to stop being careful about.

"Ms. Montrose." He had a card out already. RAHUL SEN, THE COASTAL LEDGER. "I've been hoping to — "

"You've been hoping to get me to say something about the Keshar disaster, and you've been told by three shopkeepers that I don't talk about it, and now you're going to try the fourth way, which is charm." She poured herself tea from the communal pot without asking. "Sit down. You look like you've been standing there since dawn."

He sat. He was, to his credit, briefly disarmed, and then he rallied. "Twenty years ago this autumn, an expedition on the Karveth high route was caught in a serac collapse. Eleven dead. The official report blamed route-finding errors by the lead guide, Anselm Montrose. His daughter — "

"Was seventeen and at base camp and has been blamed by association ever since, yes, I've read the article. You wrote it. Or your editor did, off a wire summary, and you wrote the version with the adjectives."

His ears went pink. "The blacklist that followed ended her guiding career in two countries. She's never given an interview. Never corrected the record. Never — "

"Never given anyone the satisfaction." Elodie turned her cup slowly on its saucer. Around them the tea house went on with its morning: porters arguing about loads, a radio playing something with a lot of drums, steam rising off forty glasses of tea. "Mr. Sen. Do you know what a serac is?"

"A — a block of glacial ice, I think."

"It's a block of ice the size of a building that has been waiting, in some cases for centuries, for the exact right morning to fall. When it falls, it falls at a hundred and twenty kilometers an hour, and it does not consult the official report first. My father was the best route-finder on the range. That's not grief talking; that's every guide who worked the Karveth in that decade. He was also, on that particular morning, in the wrong place, because the route notes he'd been given by the expedition sponsor put him there." She looked up. "Do you know who wrote those route notes?"

"I — the report says — "

"The report says a dead man can't argue with it. That's what reports are for." She drank her tea. It was terrible, over-steeped, full of tannin. She drank it anyway. "Here's what I'll give you, since you walked all this way. The mountain killed those people. The paperwork killed my father's name. If you want a story about paperwork, go to the capital and ask the mountaineering federation why their archive for that year is missing forty pages. If you want a story about mountains, go climb one. Either way, you're done with me."

Sen sat with his notebook open and nothing written in it, which was, Elodie thought, the most honest photograph of journalism she'd ever seen.

"You know she's still out there," he said finally, quieter. "The story. People in the valley towns still talk about it. The villages that — " He stopped himself. "There are things that don't add up."

Elodie set down her cup. Something moved behind her ribs, old and heavy, like a serac shifting on its own private schedule.

"There are always things that don't add up, Mr. Sen. That's what makes them things." She stood. "Tell your editor the Montrose story is closed. It's been closed for twenty years. Some doors are closed because behind them is nothing. Some are closed because behind them is the rest of the building on fire. You don't want to know which kind this is."

She left money on the table for the tea, because she always did, because debts were the one thing she never let stand — not small ones, anyway.

* * *

The crew had taken over the back room of the Iron Yak for the gear check, which meant Dawa's stock was spread across every surface and Priya was conducting the inventory with the serene tyranny of a woman who had run logistics for three armies' worth of expeditions without ever holding a rank.

"Rope's good," Priya said. "Stove number three is acting up again. Dev, you're on stove three."

"Why am I on stove three?"

"Because you're twenty-two and your hands still work and mine have opinions." Priya didn't look up from the manifest. "Fuel's counted. Rations are counted. Elodie, the client manifest for Thangpo is light — the monastery added a request. Candles. Forty kilos of candles."

"Forty kilos of candles."

"They're devout. It's a big monastery."

"Forty kilos of candles is a yak, not a porter."

"They know. They're sending a yak to meet us at the river crossing." Priya finally looked up, and her mouth did the thing it did when she was about to enjoy herself. "Also there's a package. Someone left it for the crew. It's from Tomas Vey."

The room changed temperature. Dev, who was twenty-two and had joined the crew two seasons ago and had never learned which names were landmines, looked up with honest curiosity. "Who's Tomas Vey?"

"Nobody," said Marcus, from the corner, where he was pretending to repair a crampon strap that didn't need repairing.

"He's the guy who — " Dev stopped, because Priya had raised one finger, which in crew language meant *let the boss answer or don't answer at all.*

Elodie zipped her duffel. "Tomas Vey is a guide who works the big commercial routes and spends money the way avalanches spend snow. Twenty years ago he was my father's second. He came down. My father didn't." She said it the way she read a route — flat, accurate, no wasted adjectives. "That's the whole biography."

"There's more to it than — " Priya began.

"There's always more. The more is not load-bearing." She took the package anyway. It was heavy for its size, wrapped in waxed paper, tied with the kind of knot a guide ties without thinking — she recognized the knot, hated that she recognized the knot. Inside: a letter, and beneath the letter, a small brass compass, old, the lid dented, the needle still swinging true.

She knew the compass. She had bought that compass with her father in a shop not unlike this one when she was fourteen. It had gone up the Karveth with him. It had come down without him, in Tomas Vey's pack, and Tomas Vey had never once, in twenty years, had the nerve to send it back — until now.

The letter was short. She read it standing up, because she trusted her legs more than her knees.

*Elodie. I know what this will cost you to open, and I'm sending it anyway, because what I have to say can't be said at a distance and shouldn't be said in front of your crew. I'm coming to the border town. I have a proposition — triple rates, a permit, and a route you'll want to refuse. Refuse it in person. Please. — T.V.*

"Triple rates," Priya read over her shoulder, because of course she was reading over her shoulder, because boundaries were a thing that happened to other crews. Then she went quiet, and Elodie felt her second-in-command doing the arithmetic that Priya did — the debts, the winter, Marcus's knee, Dev's mother's clinic bills, the two lean seasons behind them and the lean season coming.

"We're not taking it," Elodie said.

"I didn't say anything."

"You did arithmetic out loud with your face."

"My face has a right to free speech." Priya set down the manifest. "Boss. Triple rates."

"We're not taking it."

* * *

They ate that night at the kitchen table in the back of the lodging house they'd used for six years, six plates and a shared pot of rice and beans and the particular silence of people who are full and warm and waiting for the boss to ruin the evening.

It was Dev who broke it, because Dev was twenty-two and didn't know which silences were load-bearing either.

"So when's your birthday, anyway? Priya says you have one but she won't say when."

"Dev," said Marcus, warningly.

"What? It's a normal question. Everybody has one."

"Not everybody," Priya said, and there was something in it that wasn't teasing, and Elodie heard it land the way she'd heard it land every year for twenty years, on the one night a year when the crew noticed that their boss went quiet and drank a little more tea than usual and checked the gear three times instead of two.

"It's in autumn," Elodie said.

"Autumn's three months long."

"It's in autumn." She served herself more rice she didn't want. "Birthdays are for people who keep count of years."

"What do you keep count of?"

"Miles." She said it before she could make it sound like a joke, and then, because the table was looking at her, she made it a joke anyway: "Miles don't get older. A mile is a mile at twenty and a mile at forty. Very reliable unit. I recommend it."

"Boss," Marcus said, in the voice he used for the one dangerous question a season, "you know that's not — a person can't — "

"A person can do whatever a person can carry." She stood, collecting plates, because standing was the oldest trick she had and it still worked. "Dawn is at five. Thangpo's a nine-day walk and the candles weigh forty kilos. Sleep."

They went. They always went; she was good at ending evenings, the way she was good at ending arguments and routes and anything else that might, if left open, become a place where feelings could get altitude sickness and die. Priya stayed last, as she always did, drying dishes beside her in a silence that had twenty years of shared camps in it.

"He's coming, isn't he," Priya said. "Vey. That letter wasn't a letter, it was a countdown."

"He's coming."

"And the route he's selling?"

Elodie looked out the window, north, where the Karveth had put on its evening cloud like a man putting on the uniform he'd sworn never to wear again. High cirrus over the western peaks. Nothing organized. Yet.

"The Karveth high route," she said.

Priya's hands went still on the plate she was drying.

"He knows I won't take it. That's why he's coming in person. He wants to watch me say no to triple rates and a permit and the one route in the world that — " She stopped. Started again, flatter. "It's a good offer, Priya. That's what makes it a knife. You don't sharpen a knife unless you mean to use it."

"And if he uses it?"

"Then I'll bleed the way I always bleed. Privately, on schedule, and back to work by morning."

Priya set down the plate. "Elodie. Twenty years. You've built this crew out of people nobody else would hire, and you've kept us fed through two bad seasons and a lawsuit and Marcus's divorce, and you've never once asked us for anything, and I think you think that's the same thing as protecting us." She turned. "But a rope team isn't one person being strong for everybody. It's everybody being tied together. That's the entire technology of it. You taught me that."

"I taught you that so you'd stop cutting corners on anchors."

"I taught *myself* that. You just demonstrated it until it stuck." Priya picked up her lantern. "He'll be here tomorrow or the day after. Think about what you're going to say to him. And boss — think about what we're going to say back."

She left. Elodie stood alone in the kitchen with the last lamp and the brass compass in her hand, the needle swinging, settling, swinging, pointing north the way it always had, the way it had pointed for her father on a morning twenty years ago when the mountain had been waiting with its patience and its ice, and the paperwork had been waiting too, patient as a serac, in an office a thousand miles below the snowline.

Outside, far down the valley road, where the dark was total, a single set of headlights came on and started toward town.

She watched them come for a long time.

Then she put the compass in her pocket, which was, she would think later, the first honest thing she'd done in twenty years, and went to find out what Tomas Vey had come to spend.
