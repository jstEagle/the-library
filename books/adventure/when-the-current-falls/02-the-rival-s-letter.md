## 2. The Rival's Letter

The rain came in off the border at dusk, the way it always did in Kesh-Tarim — sideways, mean, smelling of diesel and wet stone. Elodie was re-coiling rope in the back of Amberdale & Crew's rented storeroom when Priya stuck her head through the curtain and said, "We've got a visitor."

"We're closed."

"He says he isn't a visitor. He says he's a client."

"Then he can be a closed client."

Priya didn't move. She had that look she got — the one where her face went carefully blank, which was how Elodie knew something was wrong. Twenty years of running a crew together taught you to read the silences as well as the words. "Elodie," she said. "It's Tomas Vey."

The coil of rope slipped out of Elodie's hands.

She caught it on the second bounce, knelt, and finished the wrap with fingers that wanted to shake and were not permitted to. She stood. She hung the rope on its peg. She wiped her palms on her trousers, once, like a surgeon preparing.

"Show him in," she said.

* * *

Tomas Vey filled a doorway the way weather fills a valley. He was broader than she remembered, grey at the temples now, wearing a city raincoat that cost more than everything hanging in their storeroom combined. His boots, though — his boots were still mountain boots, scarred and resoled, and that detail annoyed her more than anything else about him. A man who dressed for the boardroom but kept his feet honest.

"Montrose." He said it like a man testing ice.

"Vey." She stayed behind the workbench. It was a petty instinct and she let herself have it. "You're a long way from your sponsors."

"I'm a long way from a lot of things." He set a leather document case on the bench between them. "Twenty years, give or take."

"Don't." The word came out flat and hard, a piton driven into rock. "You don't get to open with that."

He held up both hands, palms out. Surrender, or the performance of it. "Fair. I'll come to the point." He opened the case and slid a folder across the bench. "I need a crossing of the Karveth Range. West to east. Full traverse, high route, before the monsoon."

Elodie looked at the folder and did not touch it.

"You know what you're asking," she said.

"I know exactly what I'm asking."

"The Karveth is closed. Not difficult — *closed*. Sealed after the disaster. No permits issued, no expeditions licensed, no exceptions written into law by two governments who needed someone to blame." She tapped the folder without picking it up. "So either this is paper from a fantasist, or—"

She stopped. Because she'd seen the stamp through the translucent cover page. Deep violet ink, embossed, official. The seal of the Ministry of Interior of Tarim Province — the same ministry whose signature sat at the bottom of the order that had blacklisted her father, posthumously, twenty years ago.

Her mouth went dry.

"Where did you get this," she said quietly.

"A friend in the ministry owed my family a favor. The permit is real, Montrose. Legal, stamped, countersigned. As far as any checkpoint on that range is concerned, the Karveth crossing has been reopened — for one party, one season, under one licensed guide." He met her eyes. "Under you."

The storeroom was very quiet. Rain hammered the tin roof. Somewhere out front, Dev was laughing at something Sefa had said, the bright careless laughter of a nineteen-year-old who had never buried anyone on a mountainside.

"No," Elodie said.

"You haven't heard the terms."

"It doesn't matter what the terms are."

"Triple rate. For every member of your crew. Plus equipment budget, plus insurance riders, plus a completion bonus that would clear every debt Priya's been carrying since the Dzongla season." He leaned forward. "And something else. Something that isn't money."

"There's nothing else I want from you, Vey. There never was."

"That," he said, "is the first lie you've told me tonight."

* * *

She made him wait outside while she took stock of herself, which was a thing she did instead of shouting. She'd learned it young: anger was fuel, and fuel burned, and burned things collapsed. So she stood at the back window watching the rain gutter down the alley and counted her breathing the way her father had taught her at altitude — four in, hold, six out — until her pulse came back down to something a professional could work with.

Her father. That was the center of it, wasn't it. It was always the center of it. The Karveth Range was where Anselm Montrose had died — officially in an avalanche, officially his own fault, officially the reason his daughter carried a name no licensing board in two countries would touch. *Blacklisted by inheritance.* She'd spent two decades building a life that fit in a rucksack precisely so nothing could knock it over again. And now here was Tomas Vey — Tomas *fucking* Vey, who'd raced her father up routes they should have shared, who'd been the last man to speak to him on the radio before the mountain went silent — standing in her storeroom with a permit stamped by the government that killed him twice. Once with snow. Once with paperwork.

She went back out.

"Tomas." She used his first name deliberately, watched it land. "Why?"

"Why the Karveth? Why now? Why me?" He ticked them off. "Because the Karveth is the only way to where I'm going. Because now is before the monsoon closes everything. And because you are the only guide alive who knows that range well enough to cross it fast, and the only guide desperate enough to say yes."

"I'm not desperate."

"No?" He glanced around the storeroom — the patched ropes, the resoled boots lined up like patients, the inventory ledger with its columns of red. "Your crew might disagree."

"My crew's finances are none of your business."

"They became my business when I decided to hire them." He straightened. "Look. I know what you think of me. You think I'm the golden boy who climbed over your father's corpse into a career. You think I sat in some warm tent while they buried Anselm Montrose's reputation along with his body. And you're not entirely wrong, so I won't insult you by arguing." His voice dropped. "But I'm asking you to walk that route for reasons that have nothing to do with my career, and if you'll give me one hour on the trailhead approach — one hour, somewhere past the last checkpoint — I'll tell you what those reasons are. Until then, all I can offer is the money and the permit, and the promise that I am not the same man who walked off that mountain twenty years ago."

"And I'm supposed to believe that because?"

"No," he said. "You're not supposed to believe anything. You're supposed to check my knots yourself, the way your father would have."

That landed harder than the triple rate. Elodie turned away so he wouldn't see what it did to her face.

"The answer is no," she said. "Find another guide."

"There is no other guide. That's rather the point of you."

"Then find another mountain."

"Not this time." He picked up his case. At the curtain he paused, half-turned. "I'm at the Hotel Meridian until Thursday. The permit expires with the season, and the season is shorter than anyone wants to admit. If you change your mind—"

"I won't."

"—you know where I am." And then he was gone, out into the rain, leaving the smell of cold air and old ambition behind him.

* * *

She called a crew meeting that night, which she almost never did — decisions above camp two were hers alone, that was the structure, that was how nobody died arguing. But this wasn't a mountain decision. This was a life decision, and those, apparently, required democracy.

They gathered in the kitchen of the crew house: Priya, Sefa, old Marta who ran logistics, young Dev perched on the counter like a stork, and the twins. Elodie laid it out plainly. The job. The route. The pay. And then the part that mattered: "The Karveth is where my father died. I'm telling you that not because it changes the money, but because I want you to understand why I'm going to say no, and why I need you to let me say no without hating me for it."

Silence. Rain on the roof.

Sefa looked at his hands. Marta looked at the ceiling. Dev looked at Elodie with the naked, unguarded question of the young: *why would anyone refuse a mountain?*

It was Priya who spoke last, the way she always did.

"Vote," she said.

"Priya—"

"Crew decision, crew vote. That's the structure too, Elodie, and you built it." Her second-in-command's voice was gentle and absolutely immovable, bedrock wrapped in kindness. "Everyone in favor of taking the Karveth contract, raise a hand."

Five hands went up. Six, counting Priya's own, raised steady and unapologetic.

Elodie looked around the table at the people she'd spent twenty years keeping safe by keeping small — the debts, the deferred surgeries, the seasons of thin contracts and thinner margins — and felt something crack in her chest that she recognized, distantly, as the sound of control failing.

"You understand what you're voting for," she said. "Not just the climb. The name. We take this job, we're tied to it forever. To the disaster. To *me*."

"We've been tied to you for twenty years," Marta said mildly. "Might as well get paid for it."

Laughter, nervous, quickly gone. All eyes back to Elodie.

She stood there in the yellow kitchen light and understood, with terrible clarity, that she could refuse. That she had the authority, that no one would mutiny, that they would follow her away from the Karveth the way they followed her everywhere — trustingly, completely, and at cost. That was the trap of being the one everyone trusted: your no weighed more than all their yeses together.

And she understood something else, looking at Dev's open face and Priya's steady hand and Sefa's careful silence. That saying no had kept her alive for twenty years. It had also kept her alone, and broke, and frozen at the edge of the one place her grief had never been allowed to go.

"One condition," she heard herself say.

Priya's eyebrows rose.

"We do it my way or not at all. My route, my pace, my veto on any leg of the climb, exercised without discussion, honored without argument. Triple rate goes into the crew fund first, personal shares second. And Vey climbs roped to me." She let that sit. "If he falls in a crevasse, I decide how long we spend getting him out."

Dev grinned. Sefa exhaled. Marta started doing arithmetic on the back of a receipt.

Priya just looked at her for a long moment, and something passed between them that had no words in it — gratitude, apology, warning, all three.

"I'll call the hotel," Priya said, already reaching for her coat. "Before he finds another crew."

"There is no other crew," Elodie said. "He told me so himself."

"Yes, well." Priya shrugged into the coat, and her voice went light in the way it did when she was carrying something heavy. "Men like Tomas Vey always say that right up until the moment they believe it."

She was gone before Elodie could ask what she meant.

* * *

Later — much later, past midnight, the crew house dark except for the lamp over her desk — Elodie took out the tin box she kept at the bottom of her gear locker, beneath the spare crampons nobody used anymore. Inside: a photograph of a man squinting into sun on a col, a radio handset pressed to his ear, laughing at something the mountain had just said. Her father, three weeks before the end.

She set the photograph upright against the wall and looked at it for a long time.

"You'd hate this," she told him. "You'd hate that it's Vey. You'd hate the money most of all — you always said paid grief was the worst kind." She turned the photo slightly, the way you'd turn your head to catch a friend's eye. "But they voted. All of them. And I don't know how to keep protecting people who've decided what they're willing to risk."

The rain had stopped. In the silence she could hear the river behind the crew house, working at its stones, patient as everything up here that intended to win.

"Fine," she said, to the photograph, to the dark, to the mountain waiting two hundred miles north under its new snow. "We're coming. And this time I'm bringing witnesses."

In the morning, Priya was gone before breakfast — a note on the table in her slanted hand: *Went ahead to sort permits and porters. Don't worry. P.*

Don't worry. From Priya, that was practically a scream.

Elodie folded the note, put it in her pocket, and went to pack for the Karveth.
