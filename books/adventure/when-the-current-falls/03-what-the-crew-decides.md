## 3. What the Crew Decides

Priya counted the chairs before she counted the people. Seven folding seats around the trestle table in the back room of the Amberdale outfitter's shop, and seven crew members who had shown up without being asked twice — which told her everything about what they expected the meeting to be about.

Tomas Vey's letter lay in the center of the table like something dead that hadn't finished dying.

Elodie stood at the window with her arms crossed, watching the street. She'd been standing there for ten minutes. Priya knew the posture: it was the one Elodie took when she was deciding how to say no in a way that would end the conversation permanently. Priya had watched her use it on government officials, on insurance men, on a journalist from Kathmandu who'd made the mistake of asking about the Karveth disaster with a recorder running.

"You've all read it," Elodie said, not turning around.

Nobody answered. That was answer enough.

"Then I'll save us the hour." She turned. Her face was doing what it always did — nothing. Twenty years of practice at nothing. "The answer is no. We're not taking the Vey contract. We're not crossing the Karveth. We take the resupply job to the Lhotse-side stations, we take the bridge survey if they'll pay for it, and in October we go quiet for the winter like we always do. Any questions."

It wasn't phrased as a question. It never was.

Ravi shifted in his chair. He was their rope technician, forty-one years old, hands like old rope himself, and he had three daughters in school in Pokhara whose fees were four months behind. "The pay is triple," he said carefully. "Triple, Elodie."

"I know what it is."

"And it's the Karveth," said Dev. He was twenty-three, the youngest, all elbows and appetite, hired two seasons ago because he showed up early and carried more than anyone asked him to. "The actual Karveth crossing. Nobody's done it since—"

"Since my father died on it," Elodie said. Flat. Not cruel, but not soft either. "Yes. I'm aware of the route's history."

Silence did its work around the table. Priya let it sit for exactly as long as it deserved, and then she didn't let it sit anymore.

"Maybe we should actually talk about this," she said. "Instead of announcing it."

Elodie's eyes came to her. There was no anger in them — there never was, that was the terrible thing — just a kind of weary arithmetic, as if Priya were another variable in an equation Elodie had already solved. "You want to vote on whether we break my oath?"

"I want to vote on whether *we* break *your* oath," Priya said. "Because it's our necks too. And our debts. And our names."

* * *

Here is what nobody outside the crew understood about being blacklisted by association, Priya thought later, walking home through the wet border-town evening with her collar up: it got into everything. It got into the way customs officers read your permits twice. It got into the way expedition brokers quoted you a rate and then quietly gave the contract to someone else. It got into the way other guides looked at you at the tea houses — not hostile, worse than hostile, *careful*, the way you're careful near a house where someone died.

Seven of them lived inside that carefulness. Seven salaries, or the absence of them, in a good year barely covering the bad ones.

She went down her own list in the dark of her room, the list she'd been keeping since Tomas Vey walked into the shop with his stamped permit and his triple-rate offer:

Ravi. Four months of school fees, a mother-in-law's medical bills, a roof in Pokhara that leaked every monsoon and needed replacing before the next one. The Karveth money would clear all of it and leave margin.

Sunita. Their medic, precise and uncomplaining, who had been putting off surgery on her knee for two years because the operation cost eleven months of wages and she couldn't afford eleven months off the mountain. The doctor had stopped saying *soon* and started saying *before it locks*. Sunita had voted with her silence tonight, which from Sunita was a shout.

Dev. Nothing so dramatic — just a family in a hill village sending him money instead of receiving it, a younger brother's tuition held together with Dev's wire transfers and hope.

Karma and Tenzin, the brothers, whose outfitting business in Namche had folded after a client died in an avalanche that no inquiry ever blamed them for but every broker remembered anyway. They knew what a restored reputation was worth. They'd lost theirs to weather and paperwork.

And herself. Priya Osei, second-in-command, thirty-four, with a savings account she was almost embarrassed by and a mother in Accra who asked, every single call, when she was going to do something real with her life. What Priya wanted wasn't money. It was the thing the money stood for: to walk into a broker's office and have the meeting start with respect instead of apology.

That was what the Karveth job actually offered. Not triple pay. Triple *standing*. One successful crossing of the most notorious forbidden route in the range, led by the daughter of the man the blacklist was built on, and every door that had closed in twenty years would have to reopen, because doors reopen for success faster than they close for scandal.

Priya lay awake a long time, listening to the rain start, and admitted the truth she'd been circling: she wasn't arguing for the crew. She was arguing for herself. And she was going to do it anyway, because the alternative was another decade of careful looks in tea houses, and she had decided somewhere between the shop and her bed that she was done being careful.

* * *

She came early the next morning, before the others, and found Elodie already in the back room, re-rolling the topographic maps she'd clearly had out all night. The Karveth sheets. Of course.

"You slept," Elodie said. It wasn't a question either. Elodie read people the way she read ridgelines — fast, accurately, without sentiment.

"Barely."

"Then say whatever you came to say while you're still honest. Tired people negotiate. Honest people tell the truth."

Priya sat down across from her. "I think you should let us vote."

"No."

"You said last night the answer was no. You didn't ask us anything. You decided for seven people."

"I decide for seven people every time I plan a route. That's the job."

"The job is deciding *how*," Priya said. "Not *whether we eat*. Elodie — Ravi is four months behind on school fees. Sunita needs a knee replacement before that knee makes the decision for her. Dev is wiring money home he doesn't have. Karma and Tenzin haven't taken a real contract in eighteen months. And me—" she paused, and made herself finish it, "—me, I want my name back. All of us want our names back. And the one person alive who can lead us to them is sitting right here telling us no because of something that happened before half of us met you."

Elodie set down the map she was holding. Very carefully, the way she did things when her hands wanted to do them otherwise.

"You think I don't know their situation?" she said. "I know Ravi's fees are late. I lent him the money for March and told him it was a bonus. I know about Sunita's knee — I've been shortening her load days for a year and letting her think I hadn't noticed. I know Dev sends half his pay to his brother. I know all of it, Priya. I carry it. Every season, I carry all of it, and the reason I can carry it is that I keep us shallow. Small contracts. Safe routes. A life with no depth to it can't drown anyone." She looked at the maps. "That's not cowardice. That's engineering."

"It's also a cage," Priya said. "And you built it around six other people without asking them."

"They'd say yes. That's exactly why I don't ask. People will always trade safety for money when they're hungry. That's not freedom, that's what hunger does."

"Then let them be hungry and free," Priya said. "Let them choose wrong if they're going to choose wrong. You don't get to protect us from ourselves forever. You especially don't get to protect us from the chance *you* were denied."

The words landed. Priya saw them land — saw the flinch Elodie would deny under torture, the small contraction around the eyes, the breath taken in through the nose and released slow.

"The chance I was denied," Elodie repeated quietly. "My father took that chance. On that exact route. With a permit stamped by the same office that stamped Vey's. And he didn't come down, and then they said it was his fault, and they put his name on a list next to mine, and for twenty years every government from here to the coast has agreed that Montroses kill people in mountains." She stood. "You want to vote? Fine. Vote. But understand what you're voting on. Not a payday. A promise I made over a grave."

* * *

They voted that afternoon, in the same back room, with the rain coming down hard enough on the tin roof to make conversation lean forward.

Elodie laid out the terms first, because she was Elodie, and even surrender would arrive in the form of conditions.

"One," she said, standing at the head of the table with her hands flat on the wood. "We vote as individuals. No blocs, no pressure. Anyone who votes no walks away with a full season's severance from my own account, and comes back in spring with no hard feelings and no questions. That's not a threat. That's a guarantee."

"Two. If we go, we go on my route plan, my pace, my turnaround times. Vey gets a client's voice and nothing else. The moment I say turn around, we turn around, and no amount of triple pay buys a single hour past my call. Three. Nobody signs anything Vey puts in front of them until I've read it. Four—" and here she paused, and Priya watched her choose to say the true thing, "—four. If anyone dies on this crossing, it will not be because I was afraid to say no. It will be because all of you, knowing everything I know, chose it anyway. I need everyone to understand the difference. Because I will have to live with it either way, and I'd like company in the understanding."

Nobody spoke. Then Ravi said, "Vote, then," and Sunita nodded once, and Priya raised her hand first.

Seven slips of paper into the dented tin cup they used for chai. Elodie turned her back while they wrote — Priya caught that, the deliberate courtesy of it, the refusal to watch faces — and when she turned back she drew them out one at a time and read them aloud.

"Yes."
"Yes."
"Yes."
"Yes."
"Yes."
"Yes."

Six. Priya felt her own heart hammering and realized she'd been certain, absolutely certain, of only five.

The seventh slip was Dev's. He was watching Elodie with an expression that was trying very hard to be steady and mostly succeeding. "I voted yes," he said, before she could read it. "But I want to say why. Out loud. So it counts properly."

"Say it," Elodie said.

"Everyone thinks I'm voting for the money. My brother's tuition, sure, that's part of it." He swallowed. "But I signed on with this crew because of what happened to your father. I mean — not because of it. Because of what you did after. Everyone says you got blacklisted for a disaster you didn't cause, and instead of getting bitter you just... kept working. Kept teaching. You taught me to read snow last season when I was ready to quit." His ears went red. "If there's a ledger up there that proves your father was innocent, then I want to be on the rope team that brings it down. That's my vote. That's the whole vote."

The rain filled the silence. Elodie looked at the boy for a long moment, and Priya saw something move behind the practiced nothing of her face — something old and frozen shifting its weight, like ice preparing to calve.

"Well," Elodie said finally, and her voice was rougher than she'd meant it to be. "Then we'd better make sure you're worth the rope you hang on." She gathered the slips, squared them, and put them in her pocket. "It's settled. Six to one — and the one was me, in case anyone's wondering. I voted no. Someone on this crew has to remember what we're risking."

"We know what we're risking," Ravi said gently. "That's why we voted yes."

Elodie almost smiled. Almost. It was the closest thing to surrender Priya had seen from her in nine years, and it frightened Priya more than the mountain did.

"Terms stand," Elodie said. "All of them. And one more, added now: we leave in four days, ahead of that weather system building off the coast. The stations are calling it weeks early. If the monsoon breaks before we're off the high route, none of the rest of this matters." She rolled the Karveth maps closed and tucked them under her arm. "Go home. Tell your families. Settle what needs settling. And Dev—"

He looked up.

"—get more sleep than you think you need. The mountain takes the rest."

* * *

That night Priya wrote to her mother in Accra and, for the first time in years, told her the truth about the coming season. *There's a chance,* she wrote. *A real one. By December you might be able to say your daughter's name in a room full of guides and watch people sit up straight.*

She sent it, then sat by the window watching the rain and thinking about Elodie's face when Dev gave his reason. Priya had spent nine years reading that woman, and she knew what she'd seen: not anger, not fear. Grief, moving for the first time in twenty years, like a glacier remembering it used to be a river.

In four days they'd walk toward the Karveth with a stamped permit, a triple-rate contract, and a leader who had voted against her own crew. In the morning, Priya knew, Elodie would start recalculating every leg of the route, cutting margins, shaving days — because Elodie never did anything halfway, including dread.

And somewhere out along the trailhead approach, Priya thought, pulling her blanket up against the damp cold, Tomas Vey was waiting with his uncle's confession and half a story. Whatever he was carrying besides the permit, Elodie was going to want it laid out on a table before they took one step onto her father's route.

She fell asleep planning how to make him show it.
