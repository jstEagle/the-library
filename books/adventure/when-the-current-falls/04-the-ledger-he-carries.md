## 4. The Ledger He Carries

The valley road ended where the old mule track began, and Tomas Vey paid the driver too much because he could not bear to watch him turn around.

That was the thing about money nobody told you when you were young and hungry for it: it bought motion but not company. The truck pulled away trailing diesel smoke, and then it was just the four of them — himself, Elodie twenty meters ahead as if distance were a discipline she practiced, Priya checking strap tension with the calm of a woman who had already decided how this would go, and Dev, the young one, looking up at the Karveth Range with an expression Tomas recognized from his own mirror thirty years ago. That look said: *I don't know yet what this will cost me.*

Good. Neither had Tomas. He'd learned everything twice, once with his wallet and once with his chest.

The trail climbed through terraced barley gone gold at the edges, past a shrine whose paint had been renewed recently enough to matter. Someone still lived down here. Someone still believed in the mountain's better moods. Tomas adjusted his pack and followed the woman who hated him into the country that had killed her father.

He'd rehearsed the conversation for eleven days. In hotel rooms in three cities. On the flight over, mumbling into a paper cup of airline wine while the man beside him pretended to sleep. He had versions: the blunt version, the strategic version, the version where he let her figure it out herself and only confirmed it. All of them collapsed now, on the actual trail, under the actual sky, because Elodie Montrose did not walk like a woman who wanted to be managed. She walked like weather. You didn't negotiate with weather. You told it the truth and hoped.

*Wait until the second day*, Priya had advised back at the outfitter's shop, in that low voice she used when she was managing him without appearing to. *She'll be softer once the route is under her feet.*

But the sky was wrong. Tomas had spent enough years reading reports from these valleys to know what an early monsoon looked like on the horizon — that bruised, organized cloud standing over the coast like a man waiting outside a door. They might not have a second day of easy walking. And more than that: there were men behind them. He'd seen the two riders at the last village who asked questions about rope teams and didn't buy anything. The consortium moved fast when inheritance was involved. It always had.

So: today. On the approach, where the ground was gentle and retreat was still physically possible, if not spiritually likely.

He caught up to her at the first water crossing, where the trail hopped stones over a stream loud enough to cover a confession if it failed and clear enough to shame one if it succeeded. She was crouched, reading the water the way other people read documents. Her hands were steady. Everything about her was steady except the set of her jaw, which had been tight since the border town, since his letter, since 1999 probably.

"Montrose."

"Vey." She didn't look up. "If it's about pace, we're fine. If it's about pay, talk to Priya. If it's about apology, save your breath for altitude."

"It's about my uncle."

That got him her eyes. Briefly. A flick of dark attention, then back to the stream, but he'd take it. With Elodie, attention was currency and you banked what you got.

"Your uncle," she said, "is a name on a permit stamp. I assumed he was dead or fictional."

"He was real." Tomas crouched a few feet downstream, close enough to talk, far enough that she wouldn't feel crowded. "Aldous Vey. Surveyor. Third-generation, same as half the government mapping service out here. He drew half the contour lines on the maps you've been using for twenty years."

"I know the name." Something shifted in her voice — not warmth, but recognition, which was worse. "His surveys were good. My father used to say Aldous Vey was the only honest pen in the department."

Tomas laughed before he could stop himself, a short ugly sound the stream swallowed. "The only honest pen. Yes. That's what everyone said. That's what *he* said, right up until the end, when he finally stopped lying to himself and started telling me the truth instead, in a hospital room in Meridian, with a tube in his arm and nine weeks left on the clock."

Elodie stood. Water dripped off the stones between them. Behind them on the trail, Dev was laughing at something Priya said, and the sound seemed to come from another country.

"Say it," Elodie said. "Whatever it is. Say it here, where I can walk away from you afterward."

*Where I can decide whether to walk away*, she meant. Whether the contract died at the first stream or the summit. He respected that. He'd built his whole career on people who gave themselves exits and never took them.

"Aldous carried something off the mountain," Tomas said. "Twenty years ago. The week after the slide."

The word *slide* landed between them like a dropped rock. He watched her absorb it — the flinch she suppressed, the breath she rationed. Twenty years and the disaster still hit her body before it reached her mind. He knew the feeling. His own version had different furniture but the same architecture.

"A ledger," he went on, because stopping now would be mercy and she hadn't asked for any. "Bound. Handwritten entries going back decades. Land titles. Seizure records. Compensation payments that were never made — or made to men who didn't exist. Villages written off as *uninhabited* in the disaster assessment so their land could be transferred clean. My uncle kept the books for the consortium. Not the public books. The other ones."

The stream roared. Somewhere above them a raven made its flat comment and moved on.

"You're telling me," Elodie said slowly, "that the disaster report — the one that cleared the consortium, the one that said the slope failure was natural, the one that put my father's signature on a route assessment he never—" She stopped. Recalibrated. He saw her do it, the guide taking a bearing. "You're telling me the whole investigation was bookkeeping."

"I'm telling you the disaster was profitable," Tomas said. "And profit leaves records. Aldous kept them. Every seizure, every erased village, every payment routed through shell companies in Meridian. Names. Dates. Signatures. Including—" and here his throat did something he hadn't planned for, so he pushed through it like bad rock, "—including the page where the consortium's directors signed off on the falsified slope assessment. The one with your father's route notes attached. Altered. Aldous altered them himself. He told me that. He made sure I heard it before he died, because he wanted—" 

"What did he want?" Elodie's voice had gone very quiet, which Tomas had learned, over one long winter of shared tents twenty years ago, was far more dangerous than her shouting.

"He wanted it found," Tomas said. "Before the men who made it find it first. Because they're looking, Montrose. They've been looking for twenty years. Aldous hid the ledger somewhere on the Karveth approach — he wouldn't say exactly where, even dying, even to me. Some old cache, some depot. He said the mountain was the only vault he trusted. And then he said—" 

Tomas stopped. This was the part he'd rehearsed least, because it was the part that wasn't strategy at all.

"He said, *tell the Montrose girl.*"

For a long moment neither of them spoke. The water talked enough for both.

"There is no girl," Elodie said finally. "There's a blacklisted guide with a crew to feed and a client who triple-paid for a forbidden route, and now I understand why the price was high. You weren't buying a crossing. You were buying a search party. You were buying *me* — because whatever your uncle hid, he hid it along my father's route, and I'm the only person alive who can read that ground."

"Yes," Tomas said. "All of it."

"And the men following us?"

"Hired. The consortium's lawyers figured out the ledger existed about six months ago, when Aldous started making calls from his deathbed trying to arrange its recovery. He was sloppy. Grief makes people sloppy. They know it's on the mountain. They don't know where. Same as us." He met her eyes and held them, because running from this part would finish him. "Except they're willing to kill for it, and I'm only willing to spend money, and you're apparently willing to spend everything else."

Elodie turned away and looked up-trail, toward the tree line, toward the white shoulders of the range showing above it like bone through skin. When she spoke again her voice was level in the way that ice is level — load-bearing, and thin in places.

"My father," she said, "was blamed for that slide. Did your uncle mention that? The official report named his route assessment as the contributing factor. They took his license posthumously. They took our name. My mother died believing he'd made a mistake that killed eleven people, and I spent twenty years carrying a dead man's error up and down mountains because it was the only inheritance anyone would give me." She turned back. "And it was never an error. It was a signature forged by the man whose nephew just hired me."

"Aldous forged the assessment pages, yes. The route notes were your father's. Real ones. Aldous copied them and changed the hazard line." Tomas swallowed. "He said your father's original notes were the most honest survey work he'd ever seen. He said that was the whole problem. Your father flagged the slope as unstable, and unstable slopes don't get developed, and the consortium needed the valley declared safe."

"So they killed him on paper."

"They killed him on paper," Tomas agreed, "and eleven people in fact, and then wrote it all down in a ledger, and my uncle carried that ledger off the mountain in his survey case and lived forty years on the interest of his silence." His voice cracked on the last word and he let it. Let her see it. Guilt worn openly was the only kind worth anything; he'd learned that watching Aldous die badly, unshriven, clutching a surveyor's brass compass like it could absolve him. "I'm not asking you to forgive him. He doesn't deserve it. I'm not even asking you to forgive me — God knows I signed the permit that got you banned from your own country, and I've spent twenty years telling myself it was ambition. I'm asking you to help me find that ledger and put it in front of the world while we're all still breathing. Because the men behind us will burn it. Burning it is cheaper than the truth. It always has been."

Elodie was silent for a long time. Above the tree line the wind had picked up, dragging loose snow off the ridgelines in long gray banners — the mountain clearing its throat, or warning them. She watched the banners the way she'd watched the stream, reading.

"The villages," she said at last. "In the ledger. How many?"

"Nine," Tomas said. "Maybe ten. Whole communities reclassified as uninhabited. Their land transferred within eighteen months of the slide. There are people alive today, Montrose, living in resettlement camps and city slums, who legally do not exist, because a disaster report said their homes were never there."

Something moved through her face then — not the fury he'd braced for, but something older and sadder, the look of a woman doing arithmetic she'd been avoiding for two decades. He understood suddenly that she had known. Not the ledger, not the specifics — but she had always known the shape of it. You didn't grow up the daughter of a scapegoat without learning to smell the machinery.

"Priya's cousin," Elodie said quietly. "Her family came out of the upper Karveth valleys after the slide. Resettled. She never talks about it." She looked at him. "You knew that when you offered triple pay."

"I knew a crew that was hungry and good," Tomas said. "I'm not clever enough to have engineered the rest. Life did that. Life keeps doing that." He shouldered his pack. "We should move. The light's going and I want the tree line camp before dark."

They walked. Ten minutes of stone-hopping silence, her ahead, him behind, the proper order of things. Then, at the base of the climb where the trail bent around a granite shoulder, she stopped without turning around.

"One condition," Elodie said. "When we find it — if we find it — the ledger goes to the families first. Before courts, before journalists, before your family's redemption. The people in those pages hear it from us, face to face, before anyone profits from it. Including you."

"Including me," Tomas agreed. "Especially me."

"And Vey." Now she turned, and the afternoon light caught her face, and for one unguarded second he saw not the rival or the blacklist or the legend but the twenty-four-year-old he'd once shared a tent with, before everything, when they'd argued about anchor systems and split a chocolate bar four ways. "If your uncle's ledger clears my father's name — if it does what you say it does — then you and I are going to have a much harder conversation than this one. About permits. About twenty years. About why you let them hang it all on a dead man rather than spend your family's name defending him."

"I know," Tomas said.

"Good." She faced uphill again. "Then climb."

They climbed. The trail steepened, the barley gave way to juniper scrub, and the range rose in front of them, white and enormous and keeping its secrets the way it had kept them for twenty years. Behind them, far below, Tomas thought he saw movement on the valley road — dust, or riders, or nothing. He didn't tell her. There would be time for that conversation tomorrow, or there would be no tomorrow for conversations.

At the tree line camp, while Dev fumbled the stove and Priya anchored the tents against the building wind, Elodie sat alone on a boulder facing the mountain, studying the ridgelines through binoculars with an intensity that looked less like navigation than like prayer.

Tomas watched her from across the fire ring and understood, finally, what he had actually purchased with his triple pay. Not a guide. Not a search party.

A daughter, coming home to a crime scene.

Above the col, the clouds over the coast had begun, very quietly, to organize.

* * *

He dreamed of his uncle that night, which he had expected, and in the dream Aldous was young again, standing in a survey tent lit by a pressure lamp, sliding a bound ledger into a canvas case with hands that did not shake yet. In the dream Tomas tried to ask him where, tried to shout the question, but the dream only gave him the answer Aldous had given in the hospital, the last coherent sentence of a long dishonest life:

*"Where the current falls, boy. Where it always fell. Ask the Montrose girl — her father marked it, God forgive me, her father marked everything."*

Tomas woke in the blue hour with frost on his bag and the words stuck in his chest like a piton.

Across the dead fire, Elodie was already awake, already watching the mountain, and when their eyes met he knew — the way you know weather coming — that she'd heard him say her father's name in his sleep.

The wind rose. Somewhere above the tree line, something on the mountain ticked and settled, like a lock deciding whether to open.
