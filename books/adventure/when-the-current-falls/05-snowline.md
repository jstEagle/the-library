## 5. Snowline

The trailhead smelled of juniper smoke and diesel, the way every beginning in Elodie's life had smelled, and she stood at the edge of it for a long moment before letting herself step through.

Behind her, the town of Sarn Khola was still waking — a mule train clattering down its single street, a radio somewhere playing a song about rain. Ahead of her, the Karveth Range filled half the sky, white and indifferent, wearing the same face it had worn the morning it killed her father.

Twenty years. She had promised herself she would never look at that face again.

"Elodie." Priya came up beside her, checking rope coils with the absent efficiency of someone who could do it blind. "Tomas is counting loads again. Third time."

"He'll count them a fourth. Let him." She watched the mountain instead. "It's how he prays."

Priya snorted. The crew was assembling behind them in the grey light: six porters, two guides, Dev among them at the back where the youngest always stood, trying to make himself small beside a load nearly his own height. Nineteen years old, maybe twenty, hands still soft from valley work. He'd volunteered when they posted the contract, and Elodie had taken him because he'd looked at the Karveth peaks the way she once had — like they were a door and not a wall.

She'd told herself that wasn't sentiment. She'd told herself a lot of things this week.

"Radio check," she said, and turned away from the mountain to face her people. "Weather station at Dzomo Pass reports pressure falling since midnight. Coastal stations are already flagging convective buildup over the bay." She let that sit. Faces went carefully blank; porters knew what an early monsoon meant better than any lecture could teach them. "Official forecast says three weeks out. I don't believe the official forecast. We move like it's ten days."

"It's not possible to do the crossing in ten days," said Ravi, the senior porter, not arguing — measuring. That was why she kept him.

"It's possible if nothing goes wrong." She met his eyes. "Something will go wrong. So we build the schedule so that when it does, we're still alive. Double carries on the first two days. No long lunches. Camp by four o'clock even if we haven't made distance, because a dry tent beats a wet mile." She paused. "And nobody argues with the sky. Not me, not Tomas, not anyone. If I call a turn, we turn."

Ravi nodded slowly. "You've done this math before."

"I've done this math my whole life." She shouldered her pack. "Move out."

* * *

The first day was kind to them, which made her suspicious.

They climbed through terraced millet and then pine, following a track that was barely a track, stone steps worn by generations of salt traders who'd stopped coming when the border closed. Tomas walked point without being asked, moving well for a man who'd spent fifteen years behind desks, and Elodie hated how easily her eyes found him in the line — hated more that she checked on him the way she checked on everyone, automatically, as if twenty years of grudge were just another load she couldn't put down.

At midday the forest opened onto the first real view, and Dev stopped walking.

He didn't mean to. He just stopped, load swaying, mouth open at the wall of ice and rock rising out of the green — the lower ramparts of the Karveth, white rivers pouring down gorges, the whole massif holding the clouds up like a roof.

"You'll get used to it," Ravi said kindly.

"No," Dev said. "I don't want to get used to it."

Elodie, passing him, heard that and kept walking. But she filed it away, the way she filed everything.

By late afternoon the weather had begun to make its argument. Clouds were building over the southern ridgelines earlier than they should have, tall and bright-edged, boiling up out of valleys that should have been dry this time of year. She stopped at a stream crossing and watched them for a full minute while the crew drank and complained about the weight of their loads.

"Priya."

"Yeah."

"What does the barometer say?"

Priya dug it out of her jacket. Read it. Read it again. "Down eight points since dawn. That's... that's a lot for day one."

"That's a storm deciding whether to be polite or not." Elodie screwed the cap back on her own watch-barometer and did the arithmetic she'd been doing all day, the one that kept coming out worse. Margin for the high crossing: originally nine days of weather window. Cut to five. Cut again, if the pressure kept falling like this, to something under four. Four days to cross terrain that had taken her father's expedition eleven.

"We'll make the snowline camp tonight," she said aloud, to no one and everyone. "Tomorrow we start earning it."

* * *

Snowline camp sat in a bowl below the glacier's tongue, a shelf of old moraine where meltwater ran silver over grey stone. They reached it at 3:40 in the afternoon, ahead of schedule, and Elodie spent the extra hour doing what she always did at altitude: walls, water, weather. She built the kitchen windbreak higher than it needed to be. She sent two porters to fill every container in camp. She stood at the edge of the bowl watching the clouds come over the pass to the south like an army that had all night and knew it.

Tomas found her there.

"You recalculated," he said. It wasn't a question. He'd watched her do it at lunch, pencil scratching on the route card, lips moving.

"The monsoon's early. Weeks early. The coastal stations picked up the formation yesterday — you'd know that if you read your own weather reports instead of trusting the government's summary."

"I hired you because you don't trust anything."

"You hired me because I'm cheap and desperate and banned from two countries." She didn't look at him. "Don't flatter yourself."

He took the insult the way he took everything now — with that careful, guilty patience that made her want to hit something. "How much margin did we lose?"

"Half. Maybe more." Now she turned, and let him see how much it cost her to say the next part plainly. "Which means the icefall crossing happens at night or not at all. Which means the high col happens in one push. Which means if anyone gets sick, or a load goes into a crevasse, or the weather turns a day early — we don't retreat, Tomas. Retreat routes on this side take longer than the way forward. There is no plan B. There's only plan A done fast."

"And you still took the job."

"You know why I took the job."

"I know why Priya took the job." His voice was quiet. "I'm asking about you."

The wind came off the glacier then, cold and smelling of deep ice, and for a moment neither of them spoke. Below them the crew was setting tents, small and competent against the vastness, and Dev's laugh carried up the moraine — too loud, too young, the laugh of a man who hadn't yet learned what the mountain charged for mistakes.

"Why does anyone climb a forbidden route?" Elodie said finally. "Because the alternative is staying down there, being the person the disaster made. I've been her for twenty years. I'm tired of her."

Tomas nodded slowly, and she saw him decide not to say whatever was behind his teeth. It was the third time this week she'd watched him swallow something. She was starting to keep count.

* * *

Dev struggled on the second day.

It began small — a stumble on scree, a rest stop taken a little too often, the particular heaviness in a young man's legs that wasn't laziness but fear wearing laziness's clothes. By the time they hit the glacier proper, roped in pairs between the seracs, he was breathing in short shallow pulls and his eyes were doing the thing Elodie had seen a hundred times: darting up at the ice walls, measuring their height, imagining them falling.

Fear like that doesn't tire the legs. It tires everything.

She dropped back and clipped in beside him without comment. For twenty minutes she said nothing at all, just matched his pace, until his breathing found a rhythm it could live inside.

"Look at the ice," she said finally.

His eyes went up immediately, to the towering serac above the trail. "That's what I'm—"

"No. Look *at* it. Not at what it could do. At what it *is*." She pointed with her chin. "See the layers? Old snow, new snow, dirt lines between. Every layer is a year. That serac has been standing longer than you've been alive. It's not waiting to fall on you. It's melting, a little, every summer, same as it always has. The mountain isn't hunting us, Dev. It doesn't know we're here."

"But things fall."

"Things fall. Rocks, ice, weather. Yes." She stepped over a crevasse bridge, testing it with one firm stamp before letting him cross. "So we don't ask the mountain to be safe. We ask it to be *readable*. You look at the slope above and ask: what's loose? Where does the sun hit, and when? Where does the water go when it melts? A mountain is just a machine with rules. Fear is what happens when you can't read it yet."

"How do I learn to read it?"

"You're doing it right now. Badly. Everyone starts badly." Something almost like a smile touched her mouth. "When I was your age I fell into a crevasse because I was looking at the summit instead of my feet. My father fished me out and made me write fifty lines: *the mountain owes me nothing.*"

"Did it work?"

"It worked so well I never celebrated a birthday again, so draw your own conclusions." She heard him laugh — startled out of him, real — and felt something in her chest shift a quarter-inch, some plate that had been locked for years grinding slightly loose. She ignored it. "Eyes on the middle distance. Feet where the last man stepped. Breathe like the hill sets the pace, not you. Do those three things and the fear turns into information."

Dev was quiet for a hundred meters. Then: "My mother thinks I'm carrying rice to a trading post. If she knew where I actually was, she'd—"

"She'd be right to worry. So am I. Worrying is the job." Elodie checked the rope between them, automatic. "But you showed up. That's the whole entry exam. Everything else is tuition."

Ahead, the glacier bent around a shoulder of black rock, and beyond it — visible for the first time, enormous, impossibly white — the upper Karveth opened its whole architecture to the sky. The crew stopped as one, the way people stop at churches.

"There it is," Ravi murmured. "The forbidden one."

"Not forbidden," Elodie said. "Just expensive."

* * *

That evening the radio gave them the news she'd been dreading, and it gave it to her in a voice crackling with static and apology.

"—coastal low intensifying faster than models predicted, central pressure down to nine-eighty-four and falling, movement north-northwest at—" The announcer's signal dissolved, reformed. "—authorities advise all high-route parties to descend below five thousand meters within—"

Priya switched it off. Nobody spoke. Around them the evening was heartbreakingly beautiful — alpenglow turning the peaks rose-gold, the air perfectly still, the kind of evening that photographs well and kills often.

"Nine-eighty-four," Priya said at last. "That's not a monsoon anymore. That's a system."

"That's a system that's going to sit on the Karveth and rain for a week," Elodie said. She unrolled the route card on her knee, and her pencil moved through the arithmetic one more time, though she already knew the answer. Original window: gone. New window: three days, maybe four, to reach the high col before the leading edge arrived as snow. After that, the mountain would close like a door.

Three days. Her father's notes said eleven.

"We cut everything," she said. "Rest day's cancelled. The cache at the moraine camp — we skip it, carry through. Icefall crossing tomorrow night, in the dark, when it's frozen hardest." She looked around the circle of faces, firelight and shadow. "I won't lie to you. This is now a race. Some races you lose by arriving second. This one you lose by arriving dead. Anyone who wants to turn back, tonight is the night, and I will say nothing except thank you."

Silence. Then Ravi: "We didn't come this far to carry your gear back down."

Dev, quieter: "I'm reading the mountain now. It says go."

Elodie looked at him a beat too long. "It says go," she agreed. "For now."

She rolled up the route card and stepped out of the firelight to check the sky one more time — professional habit, personal ritual. The stars were out, hard and cold, but along the southern horizon the clouds had organized themselves into a single vast bank, layered and patient, glowing faintly where lightning flickered inside it like something thinking.

Coming north. Coming for the pass. Coming weeks before it had any business existing.

Twenty years ago, men had lied about the weather on this mountain and her father had died believing them. Tonight the sky wasn't lying. Tonight it was telling her exactly what it intended to do, in the only language it had.

She just didn't know yet who else was listening — or that, two valleys south, other men were climbing toward the same col, carrying the same storm inside them.

Elodie went to her tent, set the alarm for midnight, and slept the way she'd taught herself to sleep for twenty years: lightly, briefly, like a woman keeping one ear on a door she expected to open.
