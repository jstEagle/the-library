## 6. The Village That Isn't There

The maps lied. Priya had known that since the third day of the approach, when the contour lines went smooth and empty across a valley that was anything but smooth and empty. Cartographers, she'd learned long ago, were just people who agreed on a story and drew it. Whatever story had been agreed on for this valley, it was a story of nothing.

The valley itself disagreed.

She stood at the edge of camp as the light went long and gold, looking down the slope to where the terraces began. They were overgrown, collapsed in places, but the geometry was unmistakable — human hands had cut those steps into the hillside, dozens of them, following the contour like rings on a tree. Below the terraces, half-swallowed by scrub pine, stood the foundations of houses. Rectangles of fitted stone, patient as graves.

"Dev says there's a shrine," Elodie said, coming up beside her. She had her map case open, the official survey sheet spread against her thigh, and she was looking at it the way you look at a person who has said something unforgivable. "Right here. Where the map has a blank spot and an elevation mark. Nothing else. No name. No trail."

"Maps get updated," Priya said.

"Not like this. Maps get *surveyed*. Someone walked this valley and decided it was nothing." Elodie folded the sheet with a crisp, deliberate violence. "That's not an error. That's a decision."

Below them, the rest of the crew was setting camp on a shelf of ground above the terraces — Tomas hauling the cook tent frame with the tireless competence that Priya still found slightly infuriating, Dev ferrying loads from the cache line, the others staking out the kitchen. Nine of them now, counting the porters, a small bright village of nylon and rope pitched on the shoulder of a valley that had once held a real one.

"We camp here tonight?" Priya asked.

"We camp here tonight." Elodie's eyes were still on the foundations. "The icefall approach is a half-day beyond, and I want fresh legs and a weather window. And—" she paused, and Priya heard the hesitation, rare as a blue moon, "—I want to know what this place was."

*You already know*, Priya thought. She didn't say it. Some things a person had to walk down to themselves.

* * *

The shepherd came up the trail at dusk with two goats and a dog, and stopped dead when he saw the tents.

He was old in the way mountain people got old — not gray so much as weathered, the way driftwood is weathered, all the soft parts gone. The dog regarded the camp with professional indifference. The goats regarded it with appetite. The man stood with his staff planted and looked at the nine of them as if counting, and Priya saw his lips move and knew he was getting a number, and that the number mattered to him.

Elodie greeted him in the valley dialect, the old trade tongue that the lowland schools had tried to bury and the high passes had refused to forget. The shepherd's face changed when he heard it — not softened, exactly. *Confirmed*. As if her accent settled some question he'd been carrying since he first saw them on the trail.

"You are going up," he said. Not a question. Everything above the snowline was *up*, and everyone who came here was going up, and the direction of a person's life could be read from their boots.

"The Karveth crossing," Elodie said. "We take the high route."

The shepherd looked at her for a long moment. Then at the tents. Then, briefly, at Tomas, who had come to stand at Elodie's shoulder with his hands loose at his sides, the way he did when he was trying to look harmless and mostly failing.

"No one crosses in the early rains," the shepherd said.

"The rains are late this year."

"The rains are never late. The rains are only early." He said it without drama, the way you'd say *the sun is only early*, and Priya felt it settle into her chest like a stone dropped into a well. She'd been reading the weather bulletins herself. She'd seen the pressure systems stacking up over the coast, weeks ahead of any sane schedule, and she'd watched Elodie recalculate every leg of the route with a pencil worn to a stub. But hearing it from a man who'd lived seventy years under this sky was different. The sky kept its own books.

"You know this valley," Elodie said. It wasn't a question either.

"I was born in it."

Priya heard the tense. Past. The valley was right there below them, its terraces and its stones, and the man said *was*.

* * *

They fed him tea because you fed anyone tea, and because Elodie asked him to sit with a courtesy that Priya had learned to read as strategy — though tonight, watching her, Priya wasn't sure the strategy and the sincerity were different things anymore. The shepherd's name was Dorje. He took the tin cup in both hands, blew across it, and did not drink. He held it like something that might be taken away.

"There was a village here," Elodie said quietly. "The maps say there wasn't."

"The maps say a lot of things." Dorje looked down the slope at the foundations, and his face did something complicated — grief and contempt sharing the same small space. "Karchung, it was called. Forty households. Barley, potatoes, goats. A gompa with a bell the whole valley could hear. My father was the headman's brother." He drank at last, one swallow, as if paying a toll. "The maps stopped saying Karchung twenty years ago."

Priya sat very still. Twenty years. The same twenty years that lived behind Elodie's eyes, behind the disaster that had a name in the papers and a number of dead and a finding of fault that had ended her father's name and her family's standing in a single season. She glanced at Elodie. Elodie was looking at Dorje with an expression Priya had never seen on her — not the guide's flat attention, not the rival's wariness. Recognition. The look of someone hearing their own language spoken by a stranger.

"The disaster," Elodie said. "The floods and the slides. They hit this valley too."

"The floods hit everywhere. That is what floods do." Dorje set the cup down. "But Karchung did not die of water. Water kills and then it is finished. Water does not come back with trucks." His voice stayed level, and that was the worst part, Priya thought. A man who had rehearsed this. A man who had said it to the wind for twenty years because there was no one else to say it to. "After the floods, when the roads were closed and the valley was cut off, soldiers came up from the south. They said it was for our protection. Relocation. The ground was unstable, they said. The village would have to move."

"Move where?" Dev asked. He'd drifted over from the kitchen fire with the rest of the crew, drawn the way people are drawn to a story that has a hole in the middle of it.

"Nowhere. There was nowhere. That was the point of nowhere." Dorje's mouth twisted. "They gave papers. Compensation, they said. Sign here. Most people signed because what else do you do when men with guns explain the ground to you? Some would not sign. The headman would not sign. My uncle would not sign." He paused. "The village burned in a single night. All forty houses. The gompa. The bell melted in the fire — you could hear it, they said, a sound like a dying animal. The soldiers said the villagers themselves had burned it, out of grief, and that the fire had spread to the forest, and that the whole thing was very sad, and that the ground was unstable and everyone must go down to the lowlands immediately."

"And the people who wouldn't sign?" Priya asked. She wasn't sure she wanted the answer. She asked anyway, because she was Priya, and her whole life was built on the habit of asking anyway.

Dorje looked at her, and his eyes were dry and very old. "Some went down. Some went up. My family went up, over the passes, into the next valley, and we did not exist anymore either. The census lost us. The land registry lost us. And the valley—" he lifted his chin toward the darkening terraces, "—the valley was surveyed the next spring, and the surveyors wrote *uninhabited*, and the maps were printed, and that was the end of Karchung. Forty households. A gompa. A bell." He picked up his cup again. "You cannot miss a place that was never there. That is the cleverness of it."

Nobody spoke. The fire cracked. Down in the dark, the foundations held their rectangles of deeper dark, and Priya thought: forty houses. She thought about the ledger she'd only heard about in fragments — Tomas's confession on the trailhead approach, the dying uncle, the records of land taken in the disaster's aftermath. She had filed it, then, under *Tomas's guilt, Tomas's problem*. A rich man's family sin. Cargo of a strange kind, riding up the mountain with them.

It wasn't cargo. It had never been cargo. It was *this* — a valley with its name scraped off, an old man carrying tea he was afraid to drink, a bell that had melted.

* * *

Later, when Dorje had gone down to his hut above the terraces — he lived alone now, he said, keeping the goats on land that had been his grandfather's and was, on paper, no one's — the crew sat around the fire in the particular silence of people reorganizing their understanding of the world.

It was Dev who broke it, because Dev was twenty-two and hadn't yet learned to let silence do the work. "The ledger," he said. "The one Tomas is looking for. The villages in it — this is one of them. Isn't it?"

Tomas was across the fire, and Priya watched him take the question like a man taking a blow he'd been expecting. "Karchung is in it," he said. "Page nine. I've had the pages memorized since my uncle showed me the first of them." He didn't dress it up. That was something Priya had come to grudgingly respect about the man — he did not flinch from his own family's name. "The seizures were logged. Parcel numbers. Compensation paid — to whom, and how little. Signatures witnessed. My family's firm held the titles afterward. The land under Karchung became three mining concessions and a reservoir easement." He looked into the fire. "My uncle carried the ledger off this mountain twenty years ago. He was the junior surveyor on the team that wrote *uninhabited*."

"You knew," Elodie said. Not accusing. Worse than accusing — confirming.

"I told you what I knew."

"You told me a piece. On the trailhead. You told me your uncle confessed, and that the ledger was up here, and that bad men wanted it. You did not tell me the ledger was a list of burned villages."

"I didn't know about the villages. Not like this." Tomas's voice was steady, but his hands weren't; Priya saw them, turned palms-down on his knees, pressing flat as if holding something down. "I knew it was land records. I knew they were ugly. I thought I was carrying a scandal up a mountain. I didn't know I was carrying—" he gestured, helplessly, at the whole dark valley, the terraces, the absent bell, "—*this*."

"The headman," Elodie said. "Dorje's uncle. The one who wouldn't sign. What happened to the headman's family?"

Tomas was quiet for a moment. "The ledger has a claims schedule," he said. "Restitution claims, drafted by someone inside the firm — my uncle, I think, near the end, when the guilt got too heavy. Heirship lines. Descents. If the claims were ever filed, the land, or its value, comes back." He looked up. "There's a line for the headman of Karchung. It has names under it. The last one is still living, as far as my uncle knew. A granddaughter. Schoolteacher, somewhere in the southern valleys."

The fire popped. Priya looked at Elodie and saw her doing the arithmetic that Priya was doing — not of money, but of everything else. A village erased. A ledger hidden in the high Karveth, on the route where Elodie's father had died, marked — if half of what Tomas believed was true — by the same disaster that had ruined him. The threads weren't tangled. They were *woven*. One cloth, twenty years old, and they were walking along its edge.

"This was never a cargo job," Priya said aloud.

Everyone looked at her. She hadn't meant to say it out loud, but now that it was out, it stood there on its own legs and refused to sit down.

"The pay is cargo pay," she went on. "The route is a route. But that's not what this is. This is a ledger that clears her father—" a tilt of her head at Elodie, "—and returns a valley to the map, and the men following us are the same kind of men who burned it. We're not hauling anything up this mountain. We're carrying something *down*."

"Poetic," said Rens, the oldest of the porters, from the edge of the firelight. But he said it the way mountain men say such things — as approval wearing a disguise.

Elodie had been silent through all of it. Now she stood, and walked to the edge of the shelf, and stood looking down into the valley for a long time. Priya joined her. Below, the last light was dying off the terraces, and somewhere down there, in a stone hut, an old man was keeping goats on land that officially did not exist, on behalf of a village that officially had never been.

"My father's route notes," Elodie said, so quietly that only Priya could hear. "He had a line in them I never understood. I was a kid. I found them after—" she stopped, started again. "He wrote: *the mountain keeps the accounts.* I thought it was just a thing guides say."

"And now?"

"Now I think he knew." She turned, and the firelight caught her face, and Priya saw something there that frightened her a little and heartened her more: purpose, fully armed. "The ledger is up there. On his route. He died on the route where the truth is buried, and everyone said it was his fault, and I spent twenty years believing the mountain took him and staying away from the mountain because of it." She shook her head slowly. "The mountain didn't take him. Men did. The same men who took Karchung. And they're behind us on the trail right now."

"You can't know that."

"I can know enough." Elodie looked back toward the tents, where Dev was showing Dorje's dog a piece of flatbread and Tomas sat apart, as always, carrying his family name like a load that couldn't be repacked. "First light, we move for the icefall. And Priya — double the watch tonight. I want eyes on the back trail from here to the pass."

"You think they're that close?"

"I think a burned village is worth a lot of money to someone," Elodie said, "and men who burn villages don't stop at fires."

Priya went to set the watch rotation, and as she moved through the camp checking knots and stakes, she found herself looking down once more at the valley — at the place the maps called nothing. Forty households. A bell. A granddaughter who taught school and didn't know she was the last line of a claim twenty years deep.

They'd signed on for a crossing. Somewhere between the trailhead and this fire, it had become something else — a delivery, a testimony, a debt being carried uphill so the truth could come down. The mountain kept the accounts.

And behind them, far down the black back trail, Priya could have sworn she saw — though she'd never swear to it — a light that was not a star, moving.

It was gone when she blinked. She posted the watch anyway.
