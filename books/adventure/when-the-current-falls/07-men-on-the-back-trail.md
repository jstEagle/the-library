## 7. Men on the Back Trail

Dev saw them first because he was the slowest, and being the slowest meant he spent more time looking up than anyone else.

That was the arithmetic of his position on the rope. Priya set the pace ahead of him, and Elodie anchored behind, which meant every time Dev stopped to breathe — and he stopped often, shamefully often, his lungs still learning the altitude — his eyes went forward and back along the line out of pure nervous habit. Forward was Priya's steady shoulders and the grey ramp of moraine climbing toward the col. Backward was Elodie, patient as stone, watching him with that expression she had that wasn't quite disappointment and wasn't quite anything at all.

On the fourth of those backward glances, three hundred metres below, something moved against the scree.

Not weather. Not rockfall. Rockfall fell; this traversed. A line of dark figures picking their way along the lower flank, spaced like climbers, moving like climbers, in single file on ground no herder would choose.

Dev stood very still.

He had learned one thing well in his three weeks with this crew: when you see something wrong on a mountain, you don't shout about it. You name it quietly, you point precisely, and you let the person whose job it is to be frightened do the frightening. So he turned, kept his voice level, and said, "Elodie. Below us. Left of the ice tongue. Do you see movement?"

She didn't even lift her head right away. She finished coiling a slack loop, set it down, and then she looked — and Dev watched twenty years of mountain reading happen in about two seconds. Her body changed. Not dramatically. She just stopped being a woman standing on a slope and became something coiled.

"I see them." Her voice was flat and calm, which scared Dev more than shouting would have. "Count."

"Four. Maybe five. The last one's lost in shadow."

"Binoculars. Chest pocket, left side. Don't run for them — walk."

He walked. His hands were not entirely his own as he passed the glasses down the rope, but she took them without seeming to notice the tremor, and that steadied him a little. She braced an elbow on her knee and studied the figures below for a long minute.

"Tomas," she called forward. "Get up here."

Tomas Vey came back down the line with the unhurried gait of a man who had never once hurried in his life, which Dev suspected was itself a kind of performance. He took the binoculars, looked, and went very quiet.

"Well," he said finally. "They found us faster than I expected."

"You want to tell me who they are?" Elodie didn't take her eyes off the slope.

"You already know."

"I want to hear you say it."

Tomas lowered the glasses. "The consortium named in my uncle's ledger. Or men paid by them. Same thing, practically. They've been climbing from the valley since before we hit the snowline — I'd guess they picked up our trail at the last village and have been pacing us ever since."

"And you knew this was possible."

"I knew it was *likely*," Tomas said. "There's a difference."

"There is," Elodie agreed. "One of them means you're a fool. The other means you're a liar. We can discuss which later." She handed the binoculars back to Dev, and her fingers brushed his shoulder — brief, deliberate, the way she touched things she wanted to keep steady. "Good eyes, Dev. Good call on how you reported it. Where did you learn that?"

"Nowhere," Dev admitted. "I just — I figured if I yelled, everyone would look where I was looking instead of where it mattered."

Something almost like a smile crossed her face. It was gone before it landed. "Everyone rope up tight. We're making camp early."

* * *

The argument happened in the cook tent, which was too small for six people and exactly the right size for a fight.

Priya laid it out first, because Priya always laid things out first — that was her function, Dev had come to understand, the way a keel is a boat's function. She drew in the frost on the tent wall with a fingertip: their route up to the col, the pursuers' line below, the two paths converging at the notch everyone had to pass through.

"They gain on us while we sleep," she said. "They're unencumbered — no client, no cargo, lighter packs. By tomorrow night they close the gap. By the day after, we meet them at the col whether we like it or not."

"So we turn back," said Ravi, the oldest porter, arms crossed. He'd been saying little the whole trip, and when he spoke, the whole tent listened. "We descend fast, light, we're in the valley in two days. Nobody dies over a box of papers."

"It's not a box of papers," Tomas said.

"It's whatever gets us killed!" Ravi's voice cracked across the tent. "I have children. You all know I have children. I signed on to carry loads up a mountain, not to be shot at on one."

Nobody answered him immediately, and in the silence Dev became aware of his own heartbeat, loud and stupid in his ears. He thought about the figures on the scree. He thought about the way one of them had paused, mid-slope, and seemed to look up. He thought about the rifle-shaped bundle slung across the lead man's back that might have been an ice axe and probably wasn't.

Elodie had been silent through all of it, sitting with her back against the pack wall, hands loose in her lap. Now she spoke.

"We don't turn back."

Ravi turned on her. "You'd gamble all of us—"

"Listen to the geometry before you listen to your fear." Her voice stayed low, and somehow that made everyone lean in. "If we go down, we go down through them. They hold the lower ground. They chose their line knowing we'd either come up or come back — both routes funnel past that bench above the ice tongue. Going down doesn't take us away from those men. It walks us straight into them, tired, on steep ground, with our backs to the drop." She shook her head once. "Retreat through a hostile force isn't retreat. It's surrender with extra steps."

"Then what?" Priya asked. "We can't outrun them to the col. They'll close the gap."

"No," Elodie said. "But we don't have to outrun them. We have to make ourselves worth less to chase." She looked around the tent, and Dev watched her shift from guide to general so smoothly it was almost frightening. "Everything we're carrying that we don't need above the snowline — group shelter, spare rope, half the food, the stove fuel reserve — we cache tonight. Sealed, marked, buried under cairn. If they take our depot, they get camping gear. If they follow us light, they climb slower than we do, because we've been acclimatizing for three weeks and they've been pushing hard from the valley. Altitude is on our side now. Every metre up, they get weaker and we stay the same."

"And splitting the team?" Priya asked. "You were thinking it. Say it."

Elodie was quiet a moment. When she answered, she looked at Dev, and he realized with a jolt that she'd been including him in her calculations for some time now.

"Two rope teams. One goes high and fast — me, Priya, Tomas, Dev. Light, minimal bivouac, straight for the col and beyond. The second takes the main load and the slower pace — Ravi, Sanjay, Mingma — and swings wide on the eastern flank, well clear of the pursuers' line. They carry nothing that matters. If the followers are after the ledger, the ledger goes high with us. If they're after blood—" her jaw tightened "—they'll have to decide which team to chase, and chasing both means catching neither."

"That leaves the slow team exposed," Ravi said.

"The slow team stays visible, stays together, and stays *boring*," Elodie said. "No route that looks evasive. No movement at night. You're just porters on a legal crossing who've never seen another soul on the mountain. Can you do boring, Ravi?"

Ravi stared at her for a long moment. Then, slowly, the anger drained out of his shoulders and something older settled in its place — the resignation of a man who had done arithmetic of his own. "I can do boring," he said. "But Elodie. If they catch the fast team on bad ground—"

"Then the fast team will have chosen better ground," she said. "That's the whole job. That's always been the whole job."

* * *

They cached supplies by headlamp, working fast and wordless in the blue cold.

Dev had been given a new job, and the newness of it sat in his chest like a coal. *Lookout.* Elodie had said it plainly, handing him the binoculars as if handing him a rank: "You spotted them first. From here until the col, your only task is eyes. Don't carry extra weight. Don't dig cache pits. Watch our back trail and watch the flanks, and tell me what you see before you decide what it means."

It was the first time in his life anyone had made his slowness into a skill.

So while the others buried crates and built the dummy cairn — Elodie insisted on building it *well*, a proper-looking cache marker, because "if they're going to waste an hour digging, let's make it worth their hour" — Dev lay on a rock shelf above camp with the binoculars and learned the discipline of looking.

Looking, it turned out, was not the same as seeing. His gaze wanted to jump — to the figures far below, now camped at the mouth of the ice tongue, tiny sparks of stove-light visible between boulders; to the clouds building over the western peaks, stacked higher than yesterday; to the ravens working the moraine. Elodie had taught him, in three sentences over dinner, to look in lanes: left lane, centre lane, right lane, sky, repeat. "Amateurs stare at what scares them," she'd said. "Professionals sweep."

He swept. On his third circuit of the right lane, he caught it — a flicker of reflected light on the eastern flank, high, near the notch. Gone before he could fix it. He counted to sixty, sweeping, and caught it again. Not a stove. Too high, too isolated, in ground where nobody would camp.

His heart began doing its loud stupid thing again. But this time he did what he'd been taught. He named it quietly, precisely, and let the professional be frightened.

He slid down the scree to Elodie's tent and spoke through the fabric. "Elodie. Eastern flank, near the notch. Two flashes. Regular interval. Could be a signal mirror."

A pause. Then the zipper, and her face in the gap, awake and fully dressed, because of course she hadn't slept either.

"Show me."

Up on the rock shelf she took the glasses and swept the lanes herself, faster than him, harder, and found it on the second pass. She watched for a long time. The wind pulled at her hood.

"Flash code," she said at last. "Three-and-one. Repeating." She lowered the binoculars, and in the starlight Dev saw something he had never seen on her face before: not fear exactly, but its opposite number — a kind of grim clarity, like ice forming over water. "They're coordinating with someone above us. There's a party ahead of us too, or a spotter placed on our exit route. Which means the col may not be open when we get there."

"Then the plan—"

"The plan holds. Faster." She handed the glasses back and put her hand briefly on top of his fist, closing his fingers around the strap. "You did well, Dev. First sighting, clean report, second sighting confirmed. That's two saves today, and the day's not done." She stood, looking east toward the notch, and said, mostly to herself: "My father used to say the mountain tells you everything if you stop flinching long enough to listen."

"Was he a guide too?"

She was quiet for so long Dev thought she wouldn't answer. "He was the best there ever was," she said finally. "And they took his name off the mountain. Tomorrow we start taking it back." She turned and dropped down toward the tents, her voice trailing back up the rocks, crisp and certain in the thin air. "Sleep if you can. Rope teams split at first light. And Dev — keep sweeping."

He kept sweeping. Below, the enemy stoves burned their small orange points in the dark. Above, the notch flashed its patient three-and-one against the stars. And somewhere between them, one small light in the cook tent where Elodie sat awake over her maps, redrawing the route in lines that no legitimate guide would dare.

Dev raised the binoculars one more time, and swept.
