## 8. Icefall Crossing

The pursuers' headlamps appeared at dusk, a string of weak stars crawling up the back trail like beads on a wire.

Dev saw them first. He came into my tent without knocking, which he never did, and pointed down-valley with a hand that wasn't quite steady. "Elodie. Lights. Six of them. Maybe seven."

I crawled out and looked. The col behind us had gone blue with evening shadow, and against that blue the lamps moved in that patient, metronomic way of men who know they don't have to hurry because you can't get far. Below the icefall, the route funneled into a single corridor — a gorge of blue ice flanked by seracs the size of cathedrals. Anyone following us would walk straight up our throat.

"Options," Priya said, crouching beside me. She'd already done the arithmetic; I could see it in her face. "We go on tomorrow at first light, they catch us by midday on the flat glacier. We split, half go fast, half hold them at the narrows—" She stopped herself. "No. Nobody holds anybody there. Not with those men."

"So we go now," I said.

Nobody laughed. That was how I knew they'd already arrived at the same answer and were waiting for me to say it out loud so it became real.

Crossing an icefall at night is the kind of thing guides tell clients is impossible, the way surgeons tell patients certain operations are impossible — meaning not that bodies can't survive it, but that liability can't. The Karveth icefall didn't move the way textbook icefalls move, in slow groaning tides. It moved in silence, in fractures too deep to hear, and then all at once, without negotiation. My father's route notes called it *the stairway that rearranges itself*. He'd crossed it twice in daylight. No one had ever crossed it in the dark.

"We rope in two teams," I said. "Tight spacing. Headlamps on low, red filters if we have them. Nobody speaks on the ice except numbers." I looked around the circle of faces lit orange by the stove. "And nobody stops. Whatever you hear — however bad it sounds — you keep moving until I say the word. The icefall doesn't care about your courage. It only cares about your time."

Tomas stood at the edge of the firelight, arms folded, saying nothing. Twenty years ago he'd have been arguing for the fastest line, loudest voice in the circle. Age had taught him what it had taught me: that the mountain takes votes from no one.

"You've done this before," he said quietly. "Night crossings."

"Once." I let that sit. "In better conditions, on an easier fall, with a partner who—"

I stopped. The circle went very still. Dev looked at his boots. Priya put her hand briefly on my shoulder, a touch light as snowfall.

"With a partner," I finished. "Pack up. Ninety minutes."

* * *

We entered the icefall at 11:40, under a sky scoured clean of moon. The monsoon's leading edge was still two days out over the coast — I'd checked the weather fax three times — but the air already tasted wrong, thick and warm at altitude, like a held breath. Storms announce themselves early if you know how to listen. This one was clearing its throat.

My team first: Priya, Dev, the Orlan brothers, me on point. Tomas's team twenty minutes behind, spaced to keep us from sharing a single catastrophe. Between the teams, forty meters of darkness that I hoped would read as empty ground to anyone watching from below.

The first hour was almost kind. The lower icefall was old, compacted, its crevasses bridged with snow that held our weight with only a soft sigh. I moved by rhythm — probe, step, probe, step — counting swings of the axe the way my father taught me, letting the mountain speak through the shaft. Solid ice rings like a bell. Hollow ice answers like a coffin lid. Every third probe I listened with my whole body, not just my ears.

Dev climbed well. Better than well — he'd stopped fighting the terrain and started reading it, placing his feet where the surface told him to instead of where his fear wanted to. Twice I felt him pause behind me and I heard him tap his probe twice, checking, before committing. I hadn't taught him that. He'd learned it watching me. There is no teaching like being watched by someone whose life depends on your habits.

At the base of the middle section, the mountain changed its mind.

The middle icefall was younger, steeper, a jumble of blocks the size of houses tilted against each other like drunks holding each other up. Somewhere beneath us, meltwater ran in the dark — we could hear it, a sound like a river trying to whisper. Every bridge here was temporary. Every one had been temporary for years and would remain temporary long after we were bones.

"Team two closing," Priya murmured. Behind us, small red points of light bobbed closer.

I found the first ladder bridge at 1:15. Aluminum rungs lashed across a crevasse four meters wide, left by some expedition years ago, the cables furred with rime. I tested it with my full weight, rung by rung, then sent the Orlans across one at a time, then Dev, then Priya, then myself, unclipping and re-clipping the running belay with fingers gone clumsy inside double mitts. The ladder sang under each crossing, a thin metallic note swallowed instantly by the dark.

Three more bridges. Two ladders, one snow arch wide enough to walk. At the second ladder, thirty meters of vertical blue wall above a black slot, I heard the sound every mountaineer hears in nightmares: a deep, wet *crack* somewhere below and to the left, followed by a silence that had weight to it.

"Numbers only," I said, and kept moving.

The trouble came at the last bridge, where the icefall narrowed into a chute between two leaning seracs. The route crossed a final crevasse on a ladder laid nearly flat, sagging in the middle, its far end anchored to a block of ice that even in the dark I could see was rotten — candled, sun-eaten, more crystal than solid. Beyond it, forty meters of steepening slope led to the upper glacier and open ground.

I crossed it. It held. Barely. The middle rungs flexed like a diving board and the whole structure groaned a note I didn't like.

Priya crossed. Held. Dev crossed, moving fast and light the way I'd drilled into him, and when he stepped off the far side his breath came out in a white flag of relief.

Then I heard Tomas's team arrive at the near anchor, and I heard Tomas say, in the tone of a man keeping his voice level for someone else's benefit, "I'll take it last."

"No," I said. It came out before I decided anything. "Send Ravi. Then you."

Ravi crossed. The ladder complained but held. And then Tomas clipped in, and I watched his red lamp start across the dark gap, and everything after that happened in the space of about four seconds, though I have relived it in slow motion a thousand times since.

He was lighter on his feet than he used to be — age and guilt had pared him down — but he crossed like a man thinking about something else, and I understood later that he was. He was looking up at me, at my lamp, maybe looking for something in my face that he had no right to look for. His third step landed on the fourth rung instead of the fifth. The ladder torqued. The rotten anchor block on the far side — the one I'd hated on sight — gave a sound like a knuckle cracking, and the far end of the ladder dropped half a meter with Tomas on it.

He swung into the crevasse wall. The impact knocked his axe loose. I heard it clatter away into the black, falling for longer than any axe should fall.

"Tomas!" Dev screamed, breaking every rule I'd set.

The far anchor tore another centimeter. I could hear the cable fibers going, one by one, tiny bright snaps like someone plucking harp strings in hell. Tomas hung by his waist clip off a ladder that was now hanging mostly from its near end, swinging slowly over a slot that glowed faintly blue-green with depthless ice, and his lamp swept the walls in wild arcs, and he did not scream, which somehow made it worse.

I was already moving.

I want to be precise about this, because for twenty years I told myself a story about hating Tomas Vey, and in that moment the story turned out to be exactly what it had always been — a story. A coat I wore. Underneath it was just the training, the same training my father beat into both of us on different mountains in different decades: *when someone falls, you go.* Not think. Go. The body decides before the heart gets a vote.

I dropped onto the ladder from the far side, clipping my sling into the near anchor as I went, and the whole structure lurched under our combined weight. Below me Tomas swung, arms windmilling, one boot hooked desperately over a rung. The far anchor shrieked.

"Stop moving!" I shouted. "Tomas. Look at me. Stop. Moving."

His lamp found mine. In the red glow his face was stripped of everything — the ambition, the polish, the rival's careful armor. What was left underneath was just a man, sixty meters of night above a grave.

"I've got you," I said, and the strange thing, the thing I still can't fully explain, is that I meant it. Completely. Without reservation. As if the last twenty years were a rumor someone had started about us.

I got my boot on the second rung, leaned out over the void, and took his harness carabiner in my bare right hand — I'd torn the mitt off without noticing, and the cold bit through my skin like acid — and locked my grip, elbow braced against the ladder rail, taking his full weight through a fist and a shoulder and twenty years of muscle memory.

The far anchor let go.

The ladder swung. We swung together, Tomas's weight jerking down my arm, my feet skidding off the rungs, and for one full second we were a pendulum over the blue dark, two men and a grudge and a single length of aluminum, all of it turning on a near anchor that held because it was the only honest piece of ice on the whole bridge.

Then my boots found the wall. Then Priya's hands were on my pack, hauling, Dev hauling, the Orlans hauling, and Tomas came up over the lip of the crevasse like something born, gasping, and we lay on the far side of the icefall in a tangle of rope and limbs and breath, alive.

* * *

Nobody spoke for a while. On a mountain, after, nobody ever does. Speech feels obscene next to the fact of continued existence.

I sat against a block of ice and looked at my right hand. The skin was split across the palm where the carabiner gate had pressed, blood freezing in the cold, and I found that I couldn't make the hand close properly. Shock, probably. Or something else.

Tomas sat three meters away, still breathing in the ragged, gulping rhythm of a man whose body hasn't been told yet that it survived. His lamp was off. In the dark I could feel him looking at me anyway.

"You caught me," he said finally.

"Don't talk."

"You caught me." His voice cracked on the second word. "After everything. After what I—"

"I said don't talk." I wrapped the hand in a strip of bandage, clumsy, using my teeth. "You'll talk when we're off the ice. You'll talk plenty. But not here. Here you're just a body I'm responsible for getting down, and I don't save bodies I'm angry at. It interferes with the grip."

A sound came out of him that might have been a laugh or might have been something else entirely. In the dark I chose not to identify it.

We moved again within ten minutes. The icefall had heard enough of us. Above the last serac the slope eased, and at 3:20 we came up onto the upper glacier, and the stars were suddenly everywhere, vast and indifferent and beautiful, and below us the icefall lay silent, rearranging itself in the dark, erasing our passage rung by rung.

I looked back once. Far down the back trail, the string of lamps had stopped. They'd seen the lights of our crossing, or heard the collapse, or simply lost the nerve to follow a route that had just tried to kill seven people in front of witnesses. Either way, they weren't moving. By morning they'd find another way, or wait for the storm, or turn back. It bought us a day. Maybe less.

Priya fell into step beside me as we trudged toward the shelter of a crevasse wall where we could pitch tents out of the wind. She glanced at my bandaged hand, then at Tomas limping twenty meters back, then at me, and said nothing for a long time, which from Priya was a speech.

"He's going to tell you something," she said finally. "In the tents. Tonight or tomorrow. He's been carrying it since the trailhead, and after tonight—" She shrugged. "Men drop their cargo when they almost die. It's the only time they do."

"I know what he's going to say."

She looked at me. "Do you?"

I watched my breath drift up into the stars. Somewhere under the icefall, in the dark and the cold, my father's route notes rode in my pack, and his handwriting waited in them like a voice, and twenty years of anger sat in my chest like a splinter worked too deep to cut out — except that tonight, on a ladder over a bottomless crack, my hand had reached for Tomas Vey before my heart could object, and now I didn't know anymore which of us the anger belonged to.

"He's going to tell me what happened on this mountain," I said. "All of it. And I'm going to have to decide whether the truth is worth more than the hate."

The wind picked up from the south, warm and wrong, smelling of distant ocean. The monsoon, arriving early, ahead of every forecast, ahead of every margin I'd calculated.

By dawn, we'd need to be moving. By tonight, I suspected, none of us would be the people who had climbed onto this glacier.

The first flakes began to fall as we drove the tent stakes.
