## 9. Camp of Confessions

The storm found us at eleven thousand feet, exactly where Elodie had said it would.

She'd called it three days ago, standing at the head of the column with her face tipped into a wind that smelled wrong — too warm for the altitude, too wet. "Leading edge of the monsoon," she'd said. "It'll pin us at the col below the icefall. We camp there or we die stupid." Nobody argued. Nobody ever argues with Elodie Montrose about weather, not anymore. They argue with her about everything else — pay, pace, the wisdom of hiring a man like me — but never about the sky.

So when the snow came down in ropes and the wind began to scream across the col like something with a grievance, we were already dug in. Four tents lashed behind a windwall of cut snow blocks, anchored to buried pickets, stoves hissing inside. The mountain vanished entirely. Not dimmed — erased. One moment there was a world; the next there was only white noise and nylon and the small yellow universe of lamplight.

I sat in my tent with Priya and Dev, listening to the fabric crack like whips, and understood that I had perhaps one night left to say the thing I had crossed an ocean to say.

* * *

You should know something about confession: it doesn't come out the way you rehearse it.

I had rehearsed mine for two years, since the night my uncle pressed his hand flat against my chest in a hospital room that smelled of antiseptic and lilies, and said, *Tomas, I did a terrible thing on that mountain, and I am going to die with it unless you carry it down for me.* I had rehearsed it in taxis, in airport lounges, in the first week of this climb while Dev snored beside me and the stars turned overhead. In every rehearsal it came out clean. Elegant, even. A lawyer's confession, structured, persuasive, ending with the listener nodding slowly and saying, *Of course, Tomas. Of course.*

What actually happened was that I unzipped into the storm, duck-walked through the wind between tents with my heart slamming, and stood outside Elodie's shelter like a beggar until her voice cut through the nylon: "If you're dying, die quietly. If it's about the stove, Priya fixed it."

"It's about your father."

Silence. Then the zipper, and her face in the gap, lit from behind by lamplight — sharp-boned, wind-scoured, twenty years older than the girl in the photographs I'd seen in my uncle's papers. She looked at me the way you look at weather you've decided to endure.

"Two minutes," she said. "Then I sleep."

* * *

Her tent was barely big enough for two sitting people. We kneeled facing each other over the stove like conspirators, and the storm hammered the walls so hard the lamplight shivered. Up close she had frost-nip healing at the corner of her mouth and eyes that didn't blink enough. I remembered what my uncle had said about her father: *Arun Montrose was the only honest man on that expedition, and honesty is expensive at altitude.*

"I'm going to tell you something," I said, "and then you're going to want me off this mountain, and I'm asking you to hear all of it before you decide."

Elodie said nothing. She fed a pinch of fuel to the stove, which roared briefly and settled. It occurred to me that she was giving me exactly as much silence as I deserved, and that she would give more if I asked.

"My uncle Ravi was the logistics officer on the Karveth survey. Twenty-two years ago. Your father's last expedition." My voice sounded strange to me, flattened by the wind. "He was also on the payroll of the Meridian Consortium. Had been for years. Land acquisition — they paid him to map villages the way you'd map ore bodies. What was there, what could be moved, what could be made to move."

"I know who the consortium is." Her voice was level. Too level. "Everyone in these valleys knows. Get to it."

"The disaster report. The official one. The one that said route-finding error, guide negligence, that named Arun Montrose as responsible for the deaths of six climbers." I made myself hold her gaze. "Ravi wrote the first draft of it. He told me he wrote it in base camp before the bodies were even recovered. The consortium's lawyers shaped it after. Your father's name went into the final version because he refused to sign off on the land surveys — because he'd seen the villages and understood what the maps were for. He was going to report them. So they buried him twice. Once under the ice, once under the report."

The stove hissed. Somewhere outside, the wind found a rhythm and beat against the tent like fists.

Elodie hadn't moved. That frightened me more than shouting would have. She sat with her hands around her knees, absolutely still, the way rock is still, and I understood I was looking at a woman who had built her entire life on top of this exact fault line and had just felt it shift.

"You're telling me," she said finally, "that you've known since your uncle's deathbed. Two years."

"Yes."

"And you spent those two years getting permits, money, a crew, *me* — instead of going to a newspaper."

"A newspaper needs proof. Ravi kept proof. He took it off the mountain himself, the night he descended ahead of the others. Records. Seizure documents. Payment schedules. Names." I swallowed. "He hid it up here. He never told me where — he said the mountain was the only vault no lawyer could open. But he drew me a route. Your father's route. He said—" My throat closed. I pushed through it. "He said the truth sleeps at the cairn, and that the only person alive with the skill to reach it was the daughter of the man they destroyed."

For the first time, Elodie moved. She reached past me and turned the stove down, and the tent got darker, and somehow that was worse.

"So this expedition," she said. "Triple pay. The forbidden crossing. Me breaking an oath I've kept for twenty years — an oath that's the only reason I'm still alive, Tomas, do you understand that? The Karveth killed my father and everyone who believed him. I swore I'd never walk it. And you let me break it without telling me why."

"I tried to tell you—"

"You handed me a permit and a number." Now her voice did change — not louder, just deeper, like a crevasse opening under snow bridge. "You bought my oath. You knew I'd refuse if I knew the truth, so you priced it above my refusal. That's not confession. That's negotiation."

"That's not—" I stopped. Because it was, partly. Because ambition and guilt had been braided together in me so long I couldn't tell which strand was pulling. "Yes. Partly. I wanted the ledger found and I wanted to be the one who found it. I wanted my name on the correction. I wanted—" I laughed, and it came out ugly. "I wanted to fix it. All of it. Him. You. Me. With one document."

"And?"

"And I know how that sounds. I know what it makes me." I leaned forward. The stove light caught the frost on the tent wall and threw it moving across her face, like water, like falling. "But listen to me, Elodie. The men who wrote that report are still alive. Some of them are still powerful. If the ledger comes down this mountain in my pack, it goes to lawyers I choose, on my timeline, and maybe your father gets a paragraph of correction in some archive. But if it comes down in *your* hands — if you take it to the valleys, to the villages that are still standing, to the families — then it isn't a correction. It's restitution. Ravi said the heirs are listed. Real names. Living people. Your father died trying to get that list to them, and I am asking you to finish it, not for me. For him."

She looked at me for a long time. Outside, the storm screamed and screamed, and inside the little dome of nylon and lamplight there was nothing but the sound of a woman deciding whether I was worth the air she was breathing.

"You should have told me at the trailhead," she said at last. Quietly. Almost gently, which was the cruelest register she owned.

"Yes."

"You should have told me before Priya signed the crew on."

"Yes."

"You used my father's ghost as a route map and didn't even show me the—"

She stopped. Her jaw worked once. And I watched something happen in her face that I will remember for the rest of my life: the anger didn't peak. It *sank*. Like weight settling onto old snow. Her eyes went bright and she blinked hard, furious at the brightness, and turned her face toward the tent wall so I wouldn't see, and I saw anyway.

Twenty years. Twenty years of miles instead of mourning, and I had just told her, in a freezing tent at eleven thousand feet, that her father had been murdered on paper by men who were never punished, and that the proof had been waiting up here the whole time she was staying away.

"Elodie," I said. "Say something. Say anything. Tell me to leave in the morning and I'll go. Tell me you'll cut my rope on the traverse and I'll deserve it. Just—"

She stood up. The motion was abrupt, wrong-footed in the low tent, and she caught herself on a pole with one hand, and for a second she swayed there like someone surfacing from deep water.

"I need air," she said.

"There's a storm—"

"I know what there is." She was already reaching for the door. "Keep the stove lit. Wake Priya if I'm not back in twenty minutes."

And she unzipped into the white roar and was gone.

* * *

I sat there in the heat she'd left behind, doing the arithmetic of panic. Twenty minutes. Windchill near forty below. Visibility zero. I counted to sixty, twice, and then I couldn't sit anymore.

I got my boots on and stuck my head out into hell. The storm hit me like a wall of gravel — snow didn't fall up here, it flew horizontally, and the dark was total except when lightning inside the cloud strobed the whole white world flat. I couldn't see ten feet. I shouted her name and the wind ate it.

A hand closed on my collar and hauled me back down. Priya. She'd come out of her tent half-dressed, rope coiled over her shoulder already, reading the situation faster than I could form it.

"Where is she?"

"She walked out. After I told her—"

"Told her what?" And when I got it out — the report, the falsification, all of it — Priya closed her eyes for one long second. When she opened them, she looked at me the way you look at a man who has brought weather indoors. "Twenty years she's carried that report like a stone in her chest. And you chose a storm-bound night at eleven thousand feet to hand her the rest of it."

"I'm going after her."

"No, you're not. You're the reason she's out there. If she sees you right now she'll walk farther." Priya was already clipping in, moving with the economy of someone who had done this drill in nightmares. "Dev! Rope!" The boy appeared out of the white, wide-eyed, and she snapped orders at him — anchor points, the fixed line to the windwall, the signal lamp — and then turned back to me. "She taught us all how to survive this mountain. Let her use it. We hold the perimeter. If she's not back in thirty minutes, we run a sweep on the fixed lines. You stay in that tent, Tomas Vey, and you pray she comes back hating you, because hating you means she's alive."

They went out into the roar, two lamps swallowed instantly, and I crawled back inside and knelt beside the stove I had no right to warmth from, and waited.

* * *

She came back at nineteen minutes.

I heard the zipper and turned, and there she stood in the doorway, furred white with rime, icicles hanging from her hood, her breath smoking in the lamplight. Behind her, faintly, Priya's lamp winked twice — all clear — and went dark.

Elodie stepped inside, re-zipped, and began knocking the ice off herself with methodical blows of her fist, saying nothing. Snowmelt ran off her in threads and hissed on the stove. She sat down across from me again, in the same place, as if the intervening nineteen minutes had been a brief errand.

"You could have died out there," I said.

"Yes."

"Elodie—"

"Don't." She held up one hand. It was shaking. She looked at it with something like curiosity, then folded it closed. "I walked out because if I'd stayed, I was going to either hurt you or cry, and I don't do either anymore. Out there—" She exhaled. "Out there it was just cold. Cold is simple. Cold I know how to carry."

The stove ticked. The storm went on hammering, indifferent, enormous.

"When I was fourteen," she said, "my father sat me down with his route notes and taught me to read contour lines. He said, *the mountain never lies to you, Elodie. Only people do.* And I built a whole life on that. Mountains, not people. Miles, not years. Because people lied about him, and the mountain just... kept him." Her voice stayed flat, but underneath it I could hear the other thing, the deep-water thing, moving. "And now you're telling me the lie has a paper trail. That it's sitting in a tin box up here like it's been waiting for me. That he knew he wasn't coming down and left me—" She stopped. Pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. When she spoke again it was barely above the wind. "Do you know what the worst part is? Not that you lied. That part I understand; men like you always have reasons. The worst part is that he's been *here*. Twenty years, ten miles from routes I've guided around my whole career, and I never came. I never came, because coming meant admitting I believed the report was wrong, and believing that meant everything I did afterward — every mile, every border I got blacklisted across, every birthday I didn't celebrate — meant it was all built on a grave I was too afraid to visit."

"Elodie—"

"I'm not finished." She lowered her hand. Her eyes were dry now, red-rimmed, terribly steady. "Here's what happens tomorrow. Nothing changes. We climb. We reach the cairn, because my father's route notes are in my head now whether I want them or not, and because if that ledger can clear his name, I will carry it down myself if I have to drag you behind me as ballast. But understand this, Tomas." She leaned forward, and the lamplight filled her eyes, and there was nothing soft in them at all. "You don't get forgiveness on this mountain. Maybe you get it in a valley somewhere, years from now, if the truth does what it's supposed to do. Until then you're cargo. Skilled, useful cargo that I saved from an icefall, and that's the only reason you're still on my rope. Do you accept those terms?"

"Yes," I said.

"Then sleep. Storm breaks tomorrow, if my read is right, and we move at first light." She reached over and killed the stove, and the dark came down like a lid. "And Tomas. Thank you for telling me. I mean that. It cost you something, and I watched it cost you, and I won't pretend otherwise. But it cost *me* more, and you'll be paying that difference for a long time."

In the dark I lay in my bag listening to the storm slowly spend itself against the walls, and I thought about my uncle's last words, which had not been about ledgers at all. They had been: *Some debts you pay in money, boy. The worst ones you pay in years.*

Outside, near dawn, the wind changed direction.

I had been on mountains long enough to know what that meant. The leading edge had passed; the true monsoon was still building over the coast, days out, and the sky behind the wind had gone clear and hard and cold — the brief, treacherous window Elodie had promised. At first light she was already up, moving through camp with her old crispness, assigning loads, checking knots, and if her eyes were swollen no one was fool enough to look twice.

It was Priya who found it, an hour into the morning's climb, kicking snow off a cairn that shouldn't have been where it was — too far east, too old, its stones furred with two decades of lichen. She brushed away the drift and went very still.

"Elodie," she called. "You need to see this."

There were letters cut into the top stone, worn shallow, filled with ice. Dev chipped them free with the edge of his axe, letter by letter, while the whole team stood in the blinding morning silence.

**WHERE THE TRUTH SLEEPS.**

And beneath it, smaller, in a different hand — a hand I recognized from the survey documents, from twenty-year-old field reports, from the margins of my uncle's guilt:

*Arun Montrose was here.*
