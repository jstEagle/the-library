## 10. The Father's Route Notes

The depot was where old depots always were — where a guide who expected to come back would never put one, and a guide who didn't would.

Priya understood this only afterward. At the time she was watching Elodie count paces along the moraine ridge with an expression Priya had never seen on her before, something between recognition and dread, like a woman walking toward a door she'd nailed shut twenty years ago and finding the nails already pulled.

"Here," Elodie said.

It was nothing. A slope of grey scree below the ridge, indistinguishable from ten thousand other slopes, except that Elodie stopped at it the way you stop at a grave.

"Tomas said your father cached here," Dev said. "On the approach. He had the coordinates from the old expedition file."

"I know what he had." Elodie's voice was flat. She knelt, began moving stones with her hands, not searching — *remembering*. Her fingers went to a boulder the size of a stove, wedged against a larger one, and she worked it loose with a patience that looked almost gentle. Beneath it: a cavity, dry despite everything, lined with flattened ration tins gone brown with rust.

Inside was a steel ammunition box, painted green once, its latch fused with corrosion and wax. Someone had waxed it deliberately. Someone had wanted it to last.

Nobody spoke. Even the wind seemed to hold off along the ridge, as if the mountain itself were giving them room.

Elodie lifted the box into her lap and sat back on her heels, and for a long moment she didn't open it. She just held it, thumbs resting on the lid, and Priya watched her jaw work through something that had no words attached.

"Boss?" Dev said softly.

"It's his handwriting." Elodie turned the box slightly so they could see the lid. Scratched into the paint with something sharp — a knife point, a screwdriver — in small, precise capitals:

**E.M. — IF YOU EVER COME BACK THIS WAY**

Below that, smaller: **AND BELOW THE CAIRN AT THE COL, THE TRUTH SLEEPS. FORGIVE ME THE WAIT.**

*If.* Not *when*. Twenty years ago, a man had scratched *if* into a steel box because he already knew how the odds read, and he'd cached his route notes anyway, the way other men leave letters.

Priya felt her throat tighten. She'd known Elodie for eleven years, hauled rope with her through two whiteouts and one avalanche season, and in all that time Elodie Montrose had spoken of her father perhaps six times, each time in the tone of someone reading a weather report. *He died on the Karveth crossing. The report blamed his route judgment. I was nineteen.* Facts. Elevations. Nothing that bled.

Now she was holding a box with her name on it, and every year of that discipline was pressing against the lid from the inside.

"You don't have to read it up here," Priya said.

"Yes, I do."

* * *

They made camp early, below the depot slope, in a hollow the moraine sheltered from the worst of the wind. Tomas kept to the far tent; whatever he understood about what this day was, he had the sense to give it distance. Dev built the stove screen with exaggerated care, twice as slow as necessary, giving the moment architecture. That was the thing about Dev, Priya thought — he'd learned to read people faster than he'd learned to read mountains, and he was getting good at both.

Elodie waited until the light went bronze and long across the glacier, then worked the latch free with a multitool and a patience that cost her visibly. The hinge shrieked. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, were notebooks.

Three of them. School exercise books, the cheap kind, their covers soft with handling. And beneath them, a folded survey map annotated in ink, and a bundle of letters tied with cord that Elodie did not touch. Not yet. She set those aside with both hands, carefully, like setting down something sleeping.

She opened the first notebook.

Priya watched her read. It was, she thought later, the most private thing she had ever witnessed — more private than grief, because grief at least has an audience in the griever. This was a woman receiving her father's voice after twenty years of silence, and there was nowhere in the world for her to put it except her own face.

"He writes the way he talked," Elodie said finally. Her voice came out strange, half a stranger's. "Terse. Everything abbreviated. He hated wasting words on paper — said paper gets found by the wrong people."

She read aloud, because Priya suspected she couldn't not:

*"Day 6. Karveth col reachable via east rib if we fix lines above the serac band. Vey senior wants the west flank. West flank is wrong. Told him. Told him twice. Writing it here so there's a record that isn't his."*

She stopped. Turned a page. Another.

*"Day 9. Seizure notices posted in the valley villages. Men with clipboards measuring fields that aren't theirs. R. says the disaster will be 'managed.' Asked him what he meant. He laughed. Write that down too: he laughed."*

*R.* Priya saw Tomas's face when Elodie glanced toward the far tent — one flicker, quickly mastered. Ravi Vey. Tomas's uncle. The man whose confession had put them all on this mountain chasing a ledger.

Elodie kept reading. The light failed; Dev rigged the headlamp without being asked, angling it so it fell on the page and left her face in shadow, which was its own kind of mercy.

*"Day 11. They've moved the survey markers onto grazing land. The headman of the lower village came up to protest. Good man. Remembers every boundary stone by name. If anything happens to these villages, it won't be weather. Whatever the report says, it won't be weather."*

Her voice caught on that one. She covered it by turning pages faster.

Then, near the end of the second notebook, the handwriting changed. Still precise, but pressed harder, the pen biting through to the next sheet:

*"I have copies of everything. Ledgers, transfer documents, the photographs. Enough to hang them. I'll cache the originals above the col, under the cairn the shepherds use, and carry duplicates out. If I don't come down, whoever finds this: the cairn is marked on my map. The truth sleeps there. Wake it."*

Silence in the hollow. The stove ticked. Somewhere above them, ice calved off the serac band with a sound like a distant door closing.

"The report said his route judgment killed the party," Priya said quietly. "That he took the west flank against advice."

"He took the east rib." Elodie's finger rested on the entry from Day 6. "He wrote it down *because* he knew they'd blame the route. He knew exactly how they'd kill him — on paper first, then on the mountain. So he wrote the truth down and hid it higher than they'd ever bother to climb." She closed the notebook with exaggerated care, the way you close a door on a sleeping child. "And then I spent twenty years treating his name like something contagious."

"Elodie—"

"I swore I'd never take a client on that route. Never touch the Karveth. I told myself it was discipline. Respect for the dead." She laughed, and there was nothing funny in it. "It wasn't discipline. It was hiding. He hid the truth at the top of the mountain and I hid myself at the bottom of it, and neither of us ever came down."

Dev looked away, suddenly fascinated by the stove. Priya didn't. She let her friend sit in it, because some things you can't haul someone out of — they have to climb.

* * *

Later, when Dev had crawled into his bag and the wind had returned to its usual low argument with the ridge, Priya found Elodie sitting outside the tents with the third notebook open on her knees and the survey map spread beside her, weighted with stones. The stars were hard and close. On the map, in her father's ink, a cairn symbol above the col bore a single word in those small capitals: **TRUTH**.

"My mother used to say he loved maps more than people," Elodie said, without looking up. "She was wrong. He loved people *with* maps. Same thing, done properly."

Priya sat down beside her. Didn't speak. Eleven years of rope partnership had taught her the value of silence deployed at the right anchor point.

"He knew he wouldn't come down." Elodie's thumb traced the route line, day by day, upward. "Look at the caching. The wax on the tin. The duplicates. He planned his own death like a logistics problem and left the solution buried above the cloud line for whoever was brave enough to go get it." She paused. "Or stupid enough."

"Brave," Priya said. "We'll say brave."

That earned the ghost of a smile. Then it faded, and Elodie looked out at the black mass of the mountain, at the col invisible somewhere in that darkness, and Priya watched something settle in her friend's face — not peace, nothing so finished as peace, but a decision arriving the way decisions do in people like Elodie: quietly, completely, like a load coming onto a rope.

"When we reach the col," Elodie said, "we're going to that cairn. Not for Tomas. Not for the money or the reputation or any of it." She folded the map along her father's creases, precisely, reverently. "For him. He carried the truth up there and it killed him, and everyone said he deserved it. I'm going to carry it down."

"And after?"

"After, we finish it. All of it. Whatever the ledger names, whoever it names." She looked at Priya then, and in the starlight her eyes were wet but her voice wasn't. "I spent twenty years keeping my life shallow so it couldn't break again. Turns out it was already broken. I just hadn't opened the box."

Priya reached over and squeezed her shoulder, once, hard — the mountaineer's embrace, brief and load-bearing. "Then we climb tomorrow."

"We climb tomorrow."

They sat a while longer, two women and a mountain and a dead man's handwriting, while above them the col waited under its snow with its secret sleeping in the stones.

It was Dev who broke the stillness, unzipping the tent and pointing east with a hand that wasn't quite steady. Down-valley, far below the snowline, lights were moving. Not shepherds' fires — too regular, too many, climbing.

"They're behind us," he said. "But boss — there's lights on the *other* side of the col too. Coming up."
