## 11. Whiteout

The snow didn't fall so much as arrive.

Dev had been on watch for forty minutes when the light changed — not the slow amber fade of evening but a flat, sourceless grey, as if someone had pulled a sheet over the whole sky. He looked up from the stove he was tending and the world had already begun to disappear. The ridge above camp went first, then the moraine wall to the east, then the boulders twenty meters out that he'd been using as landmarks all afternoon.

"Elodie," he said, and his voice came out smaller than he intended.

She was at her tent fly, coiling rope with the economy of motion that never stopped astonishing him — no wasted gesture, ever, like she'd been built for altitude instead of born somewhere low and green. She followed his gaze upward, and Dev watched something cross her face that he'd learned to read over the past weeks: not fear exactly, but recognition. The look of a woman greeting an old enemy by name.

"That's not squall weather," Priya said, emerging from the cook tent with a pot in her hands.

"No." Elodie set down the coil. "That's the monsoon."

"It's three weeks early."

"The mountain doesn't check the calendar." She was already moving, unclipping the barometer from its cord under the fly, tapping the dial. Dev had seen her do this every morning and every night since the coast reports came in, and each time her jaw had tightened a little more. Now she stared at it for a long moment and then laughed — one short, hard syllable with no humor in it at all.

"What?" Tomas asked. He'd been quiet all day, hunched near the gear pile, and Dev still wasn't sure how to feel about him — the man who'd dragged them onto this route, who'd lied about the cargo until the lie was too heavy to carry.

"Twenty years ago," Elodie said, "my father told me the Karveth monsoon would come early someday. He said the weather stations were reading the coastal pressure wrong, that everyone trusted the numbers more than the sky." She clipped the barometer back. "He was right. He was just twenty years early himself."

* * *

They spent the next two hours doing nothing but snow work, and it was the most useful thing Dev had ever done. Walls upwind of every tent, packed and cut into blocks because loose shoveling would just fill back in. Stakes doubled. The cache they'd buried below the icefall re-marked with a second wand, because one wand in a whiteout was a rumor, not a marker. Elodie moved through it all calling instructions in a voice pitched to carry without shouting, and Dev noticed she never once said hurry, even though everything about the thickening air said hurry, even though the visibility had closed to the length of a rope team.

"How long can this last?" he asked Priya while they sawed blocks from the drift behind the cook tent.

"A day. Three days. A week." She shrugged inside her shell jacket. "Wet monsoon snow is heavy. It'll bury anything that isn't moving or dug in."

"And if we're not moving?"

Priya glanced at him — that quick, assessing look she gave whenever he asked a question that mattered — and said, "Then we dig in deeper and wait it out. That's the whole job now. Waiting done well."

Waiting done well turned out to mean melting snow in shifts, because fuel was fuel and bodies needed hot liquid even when they didn't need to move. It meant checking each other's faces for frost-nip every hour even though it was barely freezing — wet cold was treacherous that way, stealing heat through fabric faster than dry cold twice as deep. It meant Dev lying in his sleeping bag listening to the snow land on the fly with a sound like static, endless static, the whole world reduced to that whisper.

He thought about his mother's kitchen in the foothill town, four hundred kilometers south, where rain on a tin roof meant tea and radio dramas. Here the same sound meant: stay small, stay smart, stay alive.

Somewhere out there, he remembered suddenly, were the men with guns.

* * *

It was during the third watch of the night that Dev heard them.

The storm made hearing strange. The wind came in pulses, and between pulses there were pockets of silence so complete he could hear his own blood. He'd learned to use those pockets — listening for rockfall, for the crack of seracs off toward the glacier, for anything the mountain might be saying. What he heard at first he took for wind: a rhythmic crunching, arrhythmic actually, stumbling, wrong.

He sat up. Checked his watch. Called the hour softly to Ravi, who was supposed to be sleeping before his shift, and got a grunt.

The sound came again. Closer. And threaded through it — voices. Two voices, maybe, arguing or encouraging each other, he couldn't tell. Human voices, out in a whiteout at nearly five thousand meters, where nothing human should be moving.

Dev crawled to the tent door, unzipped it a hand's width, and the cold wet air hit him like a slap. Visibility: nothing. He could see the nearest snow wall, barely, a grey suggestion. Beyond it, nothing at all.

But the voices kept coming.

"Elodie," he called, low but firm, the way she'd taught him — loud enough to wake, calm enough not to panic. "People outside. Coming in."

There was a pause, then the zip of her tent. By the time he'd gotten his boots on and his headlamp clicked to red — she'd drilled them on red light in camp, white light ate your night vision and invited avalanches off the slopes above — she was beside him, and Priya, and Tomas, and the whole camp was awake in that silent way mountaineers wake, no shouting, just movement.

"How many?" Elodie asked.

"Two, I think. Maybe three. They're following our wands."

"Of course they are," Tomas muttered. "We marked the only sane line through the moraine."

"They're thirty meters out," Dev said. "Maybe less."

Elodie stood very still in the red glow. Dev watched her think. He'd learned her tells by now: when her eyes went distant and slightly left, she was running scenarios, fast, the way she recalculated route legs when the weather reports turned bad.

"They're armed," Priya said quietly. "Or they were."

"They're buried," Elodie said. "Their camp was in the hollow below the shoulder — I flagged it yesterday. Wet snow at that loading angle, on that slope? Their tents are gone. If they were in them when it came down—" She stopped. Started again. "If any of them are walking, they walked out of a burial. Which means they're hypothermic, probably injured, and definitely lost. In this." She tilted her head at the roaring dark.

"So we let the mountain finish it," Ravi said from behind them. There was no cruelty in it, Dev thought. Just arithmetic. Those men had followed them for days with rifles. Every one of them knew what the men were for.

Elodie didn't answer right away. The snow hissed against the fly. Somewhere out in the grey, fainter now, one of the voices called out — a word, shapeless, swallowed.

"No," she said.

"Elodie." Priya's voice was gentle but it had an edge under it. "They came to take the ledger. Maybe to take us off the mountain. You know what happens if we—"

"I know exactly what happens." Elodie turned, and even in red light Dev could see the flint in her face. "What happens is we become the kind of people who can be erased. Like the villages in that ledger. Like my father. Somebody decides you're inconvenient, and the mountain does the paperwork." She was already pulling on her parka. "Get the big tent open. Stove lit. Two bags ready. Dev — with me. Rope up."

"You're going out there?"

"I'm going ten meters past the wand line with a rope on. That's not brave, it's navigation." She looked around at all of them, and her voice dropped into the register she used for orders that weren't negotiable. "Nobody else comes out. If I shout trouble, Priya hauls. That's the whole plan."

Tomas stepped forward. "I should—"

"You should stand where I can see you," Elodie said, "and think about whose uncle helped write the report that put those men on this mountain. Go."

* * *

Later, Dev would try to describe those ninety seconds and fail every time.

He clipped into the rope end and followed Elodie out of camp, and the world simply ended three steps beyond the snow wall. His headlamp threw a cone of swirling white that showed him her boots, her rope, the wand tops flashing past at knee height, and nothing else — no ground texture, no horizon, no sense of up beyond the pull of gravity and the faint lean of the wind against his left cheek. Snow came horizontally. It filled his hood in seconds. He counted his breaths because counting was something, because panic in a whiteout killed faster than cold.

Elodie stopped. Held up a fist. Listened.

And there — close, so close it made Dev's stomach drop — a voice. Cracked, sobbing-adjacent, saying something over and over in a language Dev half-knew from the valley markets. *Here. Here. Here.*

"Light," Elodie said, and Dev swept his beam low across the ground.

Two shapes. One crawling, one dragging the other. They were so crusted in ice they looked like features of the landscape, grey-blue sculptures that moved. The crawler lifted its head into the edge of Dev's beam and Dev saw a face — young, maybe younger than him, beard full of frozen breath, eyes swollen shut with snow-blindness or exhaustion or both.

"Don't shoot," the man croaked, in accented trade-tongue. "Please. We have no— we lost everything. Please."

"We know you don't have weapons," Elodie said, flat. "You couldn't have held onto them. Get up if you can. Follow the rope. Touch it the whole way. If you let go, we can't find you, and you die. Do you understand?"

A nod, or the collapse of a head that might have been a nod.

Getting them into camp was ugly and slow. The crawler could walk, barely, with Dev hauling him by the harness strap. The other one — bigger, older, a man with a grey-shot beard and hands too damaged to grip — had to be half-carried, and it took all three of them plus Priya at the rope anchor to move him the last fifty meters. When they finally got them through the gap in the snow wall, into the big communal tent where the stove glowed orange, the two men collapsed onto the waiting bags and lay there steaming, shedding meltwater, shaking so hard the ground seemed to shake with them.

Dev stood in the doorway, breathing hard, looking at them.

He'd spent days being afraid of these men. He'd lain awake imagining them — faceless, professional, the consortium's hired muscle, men who'd burn a village or bury a guide without losing sleep. And here was what fear had actually been hiding: a kid with frostbitten cheeks crying silently into a sleeping bag, and an older man whose fingers were the color of candle wax, whispering thank you, thank you, thank you in a voice worn down to almost nothing.

"Hypothermia, both. The older one's hands are gone — third degree, maybe worse. He needs a hospital, not a mountain." Elodie worked as she talked, cutting the man's gloves away with scissors, her touch impersonal and exact. "Ravi, hot water, sugar in it, lots. Not too hot to drink — small sips first. Dev, get their boots off before the feet swell. Priya, check the younger one's core temp."

"You're warming the men who hunted us," Ravi said. Not quite a question.

"I'm keeping two human beings alive," Elodie said, not looking up. "What they were doing an hour ago doesn't change what they need now. On this mountain we're all just cold men. Mercy isn't softness, Ravi. It's the strongest thing up here. Fear makes you stupid; mercy keeps your hands steady." She wrapped the ruined hands in dry cloth, careful, competent. "Besides — dead men can't testify."

That last part landed differently. Dev caught Priya's eye across the tent and saw the same thought arrive in her face that had arrived in his: *she wants them alive for a reason.* Everything on this expedition had turned out to be for a reason. The ledger. The cairn. The truth sleeping under stone.

* * *

By dawn — if it was dawn; the light never changed, just stayed that blind grey — the younger one could talk.

His name was Suren. He was nineteen. He'd taken the job, he said, staring into the mug of sweetened water like it held answers, because his family owed money to the same people who owned the consortium's local office, and the debt had teeth. He'd never been above the snowline before this month. He'd never fired the rifle they'd given him. He'd been carrying it, he swore, with the safety on, hoping to God nobody made him point it at anyone.

"There were eight of us," he said. "Camp was in the hollow, like you said. The slide came at—" his voice broke, steadied. "At night. No warning. It sounded like the whole slope exhaled. Four of us were awake, digging. The others..." He shook his head. "Bhaskar and me dug until our hands stopped working. Then we walked. Or crawled. I don't remember which. I don't remember most of it."

"Bhaskar," Elodie said. "The older man."

Suren nodded. "He was the surveyor. Before this. Before they bought him. He used to map these valleys for the government — he knew the range better than anyone. That's why they hired him. To find whatever you found." His eyes came up, red-rimmed, terrified, and fixed on Elodie. "He knows things. About why they sent us. About what's on the other side of the pass. He was talking in his sleep all night — names, dates, a lawyer climbing from the east side. You should ask him. When he wakes up. Please. He saved my life twice tonight and I don't want them to just—" He stopped himself, looked down. "I'm sorry. I don't know what I'm allowed to say."

Dev, crouched by the stove feeding it fuel, felt the temperature of the tent change. Not degrees — something else. Everyone had gone still. Even the storm outside seemed to lean in.

Elodie set down the bandage roll.

"Suren," she said, and her voice was quiet in a way that made Dev's spine straighten. "You're allowed to say anything you want. You're under my roof now, such as it is. Nobody touches you here — not my crew, not your employers, not the mountain while I'm standing. But understand what you just said." She leaned forward. "A lawyer is climbing from the east side. Toward the pass. Toward the cairn."

Suren blinked. "The cairn? Bhaskar mentioned—" He faltered. "I shouldn't—"

"He saved your life twice," Elodie repeated, gently. "Now save his. Tell me what he knows."

Outside, the storm roared on, burying the world. Inside, a nineteen-year-old boy who'd come up the mountain to hunt them opened his mouth — and began, at last, to talk.
