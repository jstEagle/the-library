## 12. The Bargain at High Camp

The two men came into camp at dusk, stumbling out of the white like things the mountain had chewed and spat back.

Dev saw them first. He'd been on watch at the edge of the snow wall we'd cut that morning, and his shout — "Elodie! People! Two people!" — sent half the crew reaching for ice axes before their brains caught up with their hands. I understood the reflex. We'd been running from armed men for three days. The instinct to meet strangers with iron had been earned.

But these two weren't a threat. They could barely stand.

The taller one collapsed ten meters from our tents and had to be dragged the rest of the way by the collar. The shorter one made it on his own feet, barely, and then sat down in the snow at the entrance to the cook tent and didn't move for a long time. Their camp, we learned later, had taken the same wet slab avalanche that nearly took ours, and it had taken everything — tents, stove, rope, food, the works. Two of their six had walked down into the storm to try for our route. Two hadn't come out of the debris. We never found them. The mountain keeps what it takes.

"Get them inside," I said. "Warm them slow. No hot drinks yet, nothing past lukewarm. And check them for —"

"Reperfusion injuries, frostnip versus frostbite, and don't rub anything," Priya finished. She was already moving. Twenty years of running a crew with me had made her fluent in my orders before I gave them.

Tomas stood apart, arms crossed, watching the two men with an expression I couldn't read. He knew them. I could see it in the set of his shoulders. These were his uncle's people, or the consortium's — the men who had been following us up the back trail, the ones we'd cached supplies and split rope teams to evade. And now here they were, half-dead in our cook tent, and the mountain had made the whole question of pursuers and pursued suddenly, stupidly simple.

They were cold men. That was all they were, right then. Cold men on a mountain.

* * *

The taller one was called Rensu. He was fifty-something, weathered like old rope, and once the shivering eased enough for words, he talked the way some men do when they've been close to death — steadily, gratefully, with no filter left. He'd been a surveyor, he said. Government survey, twenty years ago. He'd walked the Karveth valleys before the disaster with a theodolite and a chain crew, measuring land that belonged to villages whose names he could still recite.

"Kolmath. Serevan. The little one above the gorge — Thandi, it was called." He said the names like a man counting his own sins. "I surveyed them. Then I surveyed them again after, for the compensation registry. Except there was nothing to compensate. The villages were already gone from the record. I drew the maps that erased them."

Nobody in the tent said anything. The stove hissed. Dev, wrapped in his sleeping bag in the corner, watched the man with the unblinking attention of the young confronted with a kind of evil they'd only read about.

"I told myself it was just paperwork," Rensu went on. "Lines on a map. The land was worthless anyway, that's what they said. Then the land wasn't worthless, and I understood what I'd drawn." He looked at his hands, at the frostbitten tips gone waxy and pale. "I've been on their payroll ever since. You don't leave that payroll. You know that."

"I know that," Tomas said quietly, from the tent flap.

Rensu looked up at him, and something passed between them — recognition, maybe, two men who had both spent twenty years inside the same machine, one as a gear, one as a ghost. The other survivor, a thick-necked man called Brakk who hadn't spoken since they arrived, shifted at the sound of Tomas's voice and then went still again. He was the muscle of the pair. He'd carried a rifle three days ago. It was buried under two meters of avalanche debris now, along with everything else he owned.

I crouched by the stove and let the silence do its work. Priya had once told me I was good at that — at letting silence lean on people until they gave it something true. I'd told her it wasn't a skill, it was just that I didn't like talking. Both things were true.

"The lawyer," I said finally. "Your employer's man. He's on the mountain."

Rensu didn't pretend not to know what I meant. "Vasht. Yes." He said the name the way you'd name a weather system. "He didn't come up the back trail with us. He's coming from the east side. The tourist route — the one with the fixed lines and the tea houses." A thin, bitter smile. "He'll have porters. He'll have documents. He'll have a court order from a judge in the valley who's been on the consortium's Christmas card list for a decade. He'll be polite, and he'll be armed with paper, and if paper doesn't work, the men he hired to carry it won't be."

"When did he start up?"

"Four days ago. Maybe five. He moves fast when there's money at the top of a mountain." Rensu closed his eyes. "He'll reach the high col before you do. He knows the route. I surveyed it for him."

* * *

I stepped outside after that. The storm had eased to a steady fall of heavy wet snow, the monsoon's leading edge still working itself in, and in the gaps between gusts the sky over the eastern col glowed a dull, sourceless gray. Somewhere beyond that glow, a man with a briefcase full of other people's erasures was climbing toward the same cairn we were.

Tomas came out and stood beside me. He didn't say anything for a while. That was one thing twenty years of rivalry had taught us both: how to share silence without it becoming a weapon.

"He'll want the ledger," Tomas said at last. "And he'll want it badly enough to pay for it. That's his whole method — he's never once used force when a price would work. It's cleaner. It leaves no witnesses with grudges."

"And if a price doesn't work?"

"Then he hires people for whom it does." Tomas pulled his hood back and let the snow land on his gray-streaked hair. "Elodie. I need to say something and I need you to hear it before you decide whatever you're deciding out here."

"Deciding? I'm standing in the snow."

"You're standing in the snow the way a general stands on a hill." He almost smiled. "Go on, then."

I looked east, where the lawyer was. "Rensu knows where the cairn is. Or he knows how to find out — he surveyed the route, he read the old reports, he's spent twenty years inside the machine that buried the truth up there. And we've been searching for that cairn with a two-word clue from my father's notes and a mountain's worth of ground to cover. The storm's eating our margin. Vasht is eating the rest of it." I turned to face Tomas. "So I'm going to make a bargain with a man who spent his career erasing villages, and I'm going to give him what he wants most, which is to come down this mountain alive. And I want you to tell me why that's a mistake, because you've been waiting to since we dragged him into the tent."

Tomas was quiet for a moment. The snow ticked against the tent fabric behind us.

"It's not a mistake," he said. "It's the only play. I just wanted to hear you say it out loud first, so I'd know you'd thought it through and weren't just —" he gestured vaguely at all of me — "being you."

"Being me."

"Merciful at altitude. It's a dangerous habit. It's how crews die, carrying people who slow them down."

"He won't slow us down. That's the point of the bargain. He walks down under his own power or not at all, and he knows it, because he's a surveyor and he can read a route. And Brakk —" I glanced back at the tent, at the shape of the silent man inside — "Brakk goes down with the first rope team we send to the valley, with Dev, with the spare fuel. He's no use to us and no threat without a rifle."

"And Rensu?"

"Rensu climbs with us." I said it flat, and watched Tomas absorb it. "Because the man who drew the erased maps is going to stand at the cairn when we open it, and he's going to see what his lines on the paper did. And then he's going to testify to it, in front of whoever's left standing when this is over, because justice isn't a ledger in a tin box, Tomas. A ledger can be stolen, burned, disputed. A witness who chose to climb back up the mountain he helped bury — that can't be bought back."

Tomas looked at me for a long moment. Snow gathered on his eyelashes and he didn't brush it away.

"You know," he said, "twenty years ago I thought you were the coldest person I'd ever met on a mountain. Elodie Montrose, who measured everything in meters and margins and never once asked how anyone was feeling." He shook his head slowly. "I had it exactly backward, didn't I. You're the warmest person out here. You're just warm the way a stove is warm. Everything you've got, you spend keeping other people alive."

"Get some sleep, Tomas."

"That's not a no."

"It's not a no," I agreed, and went back inside, because the stove needed feeding and the shivering men needed watching, and because if I stayed out in the snow one more minute he was going to say something else and I was going to have to decide what to do about it, and I wasn't ready. There was a cairn above us where my father had left a letter I hadn't read yet. I couldn't carry his words and Tomas's in the same chest. Not tonight.

* * *

I made the offer at first light, while the storm held its breath and the whole crew stood around the cook tent pretending to organize gear and listening to every word.

"Rensu." He looked up. He'd slept four hours and it had taken ten years off his face, or maybe it was the hot barley soup, or maybe it was just having a decision ahead of him instead of a death. "Here's the bargain, and it's the only one on the table."

He waited.

"You know where my father's cairn is. Or you can find it faster than we can, because you surveyed this route and you know what the old reports said and you know what ground the consortium was most afraid of someone walking. You take us there. Today, in this weather window, before it closes and before Vasht closes it for us. In exchange, you walk down alive. Fed, roped, carried if your feet fail — but alive, and down, and out from under the payroll for good."

"And if I say no?"

"Then you stay here with a stove, a tent, and enough fuel for three days, and you take your chances with the storm and the descent, alone. I'll even mark the route for you." I let that sit. "I'm not going to threaten you, Rensu. I'm not going to trade your life for the ledger. That's the whole point. But I'm not going to carry you up a mountain for free, either. Everything on this mountain costs. That's the price of the view."

Dev, at the tent's edge, shifted. I caught Priya's eye and she gave me the smallest nod — *cold, but fair* — and I held on to that.

Rensu looked down at his ruined hands for a long time. When he spoke, his voice had changed. It was steadier. It was the voice of a man who had spent twenty years waiting for someone to offer him a way out and had finally stopped pretending he didn't want one.

"You should know what you're buying," he said. "The cairn. Your father's cairn. I never saw it — I was never up on that shoulder — but I read the incident reports. All of them, the real ones, before they were rewritten. Your father marked a cairn on the northeast shoulder, below the serac line, at the head of the old goat track. The reports called it a navigation marker." He looked up, and his eyes were wet and clear at the same time. "It wasn't a navigation marker. I've known that for twenty years and I've said nothing for twenty years. The consortium spent a season looking for it in '04 and couldn't find it, because your father built it to be found only by someone who climbed the way he climbed — off the line, against the grain, where no legitimate guide would ever go." A pause. "You'll want the goat track. It's not on any map. I can put you on it."

The tent was very quiet. Somewhere above us, muffled by snow, the mountain made a sound like a door closing.

"And Vasht," Rensu added, before anyone could move. "One more thing, and you need it more than the cairn. He's not coming for the ledger alone. He's coming with a claim — a court order naming the ledger stolen consortium property, naming Tomas Vey as its thief, and naming a guide called Elodie Montrose as his accomplice. If he reaches you first, he doesn't have to fight you. He just has to read you the paper and watch you realize that everything you carry down can be taken from you at the trailhead." He pushed himself upright against the tent pole, frostbitten hands and all, like a man standing up in court. "So we climb now. Today. In this window. Because the only thing that beats his paper is the truth in your hands before he gets to you — and the truth is a half-day above us, on a track no map has ever shown."

I was already pulling my boots on.

"Priya — rope teams. Dev, you and Brakk and the spare fuel go down to the cache at first light tomorrow; mark it well, you'll be our line back. Tomas, Rensu's on your rope. I want eyes on him every step." I looked around the tent, at the faces of the people who had followed me up a mountain no one was supposed to climb, into a storm no one was supposed to survive, for a truth none of them had ever been promised a share of. "We move in twenty minutes."

Dev caught me at the tent flap. "Elodie — the goat track. Below the serac line. In fresh snow."

"I know," I said.

"It's —"

"I know what it is." I looked up through the snow haze toward the northeast shoulder, invisible now, somewhere above the gray. My father had built a marker there for someone exactly like me, and a lawyer with a court order was climbing toward it from the other side of the mountain, and the weather window was closing like a hand.

"Twenty minutes," I said again, and stepped out into the white.
