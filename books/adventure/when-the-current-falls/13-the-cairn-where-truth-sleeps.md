## 13. The Cairn Where Truth Sleeps

The surveyor's map was drawn on the back of a ration wrapper, and it was wrong by two hundred meters.

Tomas knew this because Elodie had known it before he'd finished unfolding it. She'd held the scrap of foil up against the gray morning light, tilted her head, and said, "He drew this from memory. Memory always walks downhill." Then she'd folded it away and led them up-slope instead of along the contour line, and forty minutes later they'd found what the surveyor had only remembered: the cairn.

It stood on a shelf of stone below the eastern face of the col, half-swallowed by twenty years of weather. Not a trail marker — Tomas had built enough of those to know the difference. Trail cairns were stacked fast, stones grabbed in passing, tall and careless. This one was low and wide and deliberate, each rock fitted to the next like masonry, like someone had expected to be gone a long time and wanted the work to hold.

Someone had. Someone had known he wasn't coming down.

Tomas stood back while Elodie approached it. He watched her circle it once, twice, the way she circled everything — cornices, crevasse bridges, him — reading the mountain's handwriting before she trusted a word of it. The others hung back at the edge of the shelf: Priya with her arms crossed against the wind, Dev perched on a boulder with his binoculars trained down-valley out of habit now rather than fear, the two survivors huddled together like men who'd been invited to a funeral they weren't sure they were welcome at.

The wind came off the col in long, steady pulses. Above them the sky was the color of old pewter, and everyone had stopped pretending the monsoon wasn't coming. They had maybe a day. Maybe less. Everyone knew it, and no one said it, because saying it would mean choosing between the cairn and the descent, and no one was willing to make that choice out loud except the woman standing in front of the stones.

"Help me," she said. Not to anyone in particular. To all of them.

They worked without speaking. Twenty years of freeze-thaw had welded the cairn into something closer to geology than construction, and it took all four of them — Priya and Dev prying with ice axes, Tomas working the base stones loose with his hands, Elodie directing from above like a surgeon who couldn't touch the patient — nearly an hour to open it. His fingers went numb twice. He kept working. There was a shame in using his hands for this that he couldn't have explained to anyone, and a rightness too, and both of them lived in the same knuckles.

Near the bottom, beneath a capstone the size of a bread loaf, the stones gave way to something that wasn't stone.

A tin box. Army surplus, dented, the paint scabbed off in continents of rust. A rubber gasket had been set into the lid — her father had thought of water, of course he had, he'd been the best route-finder in the range and he'd thought of everything except the fall that killed him — and the whole thing had been wrapped in oilcloth tied with wire.

Elodie went very still.

Tomas had seen her still before. On the icefall, when the ladder bridge had dropped out from under him and she'd anchored his fall without a word, without a wasted motion, stillness as pure function. This was different. This was the stillness of a woman standing in front of a door she'd spent twenty years not opening.

"Elodie," Priya said softly.

"I know."

She knelt. She unwound the wire with her fingers, slow, careful, the way you'd handle something alive. The oilcloth came away in dry yellow sheets. She lifted the lid.

Inside: a bundle of papers bound with cord. A leather ledger, swollen with damp but intact, its spine cracked like a knuckle. An envelope, wax-sealed, her father's initials pressed into the wax. And beneath it all, flat and fanned like a hand of cards, photographs.

Tomas leaned in despite himself. He'd carried his uncle's confession across three countries and half a mountain; he'd earned the right to look, or told himself he had. Black-and-white prints, then color, faded toward pink at the edges. Villages. Terraced fields. A shrine with prayer flags so sharp in the focus you could almost hear them snapping. Stone foundations laid out in patterns that matched nothing on any map Tomas had ever bought, because the maps had been made after, made *because*, made to erase exactly this.

The village that wasn't there. And others. Six, seven — he lost count — each photograph annotated on the back in a small, precise hand. Names. Dates. Headcounts. *Households displaced: 41. Compensation recorded: none.*

"Those are the seizure records," the surveyor said from behind them. His voice cracked on the second word. "That's what we surveyed for. I told you the cairn. I didn't tell you—" He stopped. Swallowed. "I didn't tell you he documented everything."

"He documented *us*," Elodie said. Her voice was level. Too level. "Every family. Every field boundary. He walked this ridge with a camera and a notebook while the consortium's lawyers were filing paperwork that said these villages never existed." She turned a photograph over. Read the back. Turned another. "He knew what the disaster report would say. He knew before they wrote it."

Tomas felt the words land in his chest like falling rock. Because his uncle had helped write that report. Had sat in a warm office in the valley town and corrected figures and initialed pages while this man — this careful, thorough, doomed man — climbed into the cold to keep the true account alive.

"Read the letter," Priya said gently.

"I—"

"Whenever you're ready," Priya said, "but read it here. Not on the descent. Not in some tent with the storm screaming. Here. He left it *here*."

Elodie looked at her for a long moment. Then she broke the wax seal with her thumb.

She read silently first. Tomas watched her lips move, faintly, tracking the words, and watched her face do things he had never seen it do in twenty years of rivalry and two weeks of shared rope: soften, then shatter, then reassemble itself into something that wasn't composure at all but its opposite — a decision to feel everything at once and survive it.

Then she read it aloud.

---

*"To whoever found this —*

*I hope it was my daughter. I hope it was Elodie, and I hope she is older than she should have needed to be, and I am sorry for every year of it.*

"If you are not Elodie: she will come anyway. She gets that from her mother. Give her the box and step back; she'll want her hands free.*

"To Elodie, then.*

"They are going to erase the villages. You've seen the surveys being run; you've heard the men talk in the tea house about compensation that will never arrive. I have copies of everything — the land rolls, the tax records, the photographs, the names. Names matter most. Paper can be burned, but a name has to be forgotten one person at a time, and I intend to make that take forever.*

"I'm taking the high route to the pass tomorrow. I know what the weather service says. I also know the consortium's men watch the low routes, and if they find these documents on me, they don't just burn them — they burn the people in them. So I go up, alone, in bad weather, because the mountain is the only vault they can't buy.*

"If I don't come down, don't blame the mountain. The mountain has been more honest with me than any man in the valley. Blame the men who made honesty dangerous. And then — this is the part that matters, little one — then stop blaming, and start building. Truth isn't a weapon. It's a foundation. It does no good buried under a cairn. It has to be stood on.*

"I measured my life in miles because miles could be walked in a day. Don't make my mistake. Measure yours in years, and fill the years with people, and when you finally stand where I'm standing now, let yourself cry. It's not weakness. It's the current finally moving.*

"With all the love I didn't say enough of,*
*your father."*

---

The wind filled the silence after.

No one moved. Dev had taken off his hat without seeming to notice he'd done it. Priya had her eyes closed. Even the survivors stood with their heads bowed, and Tomas understood suddenly that they weren't mourning — they were witnessing. There was a difference, and the mountain knew it, and so did everyone on the shelf.

Elodie lowered the letter.

She did not fold it. She held it in both hands, the way you hold something that might dissolve, and she turned to face the cairn — the fitted stones, the deliberate work, the vault her father had built out of patience and grief and love — and the sound she made was not a sob. It was smaller than that and larger, a single breath that broke in the middle, and then she was crying.

Openly. Ugly. Both hands full of paper, tears running freely down a face that had spent twenty years learning how to be weatherproof, shoulders shaking, no attempt whatsoever to stop it. She cried the way water moves through rock — finding every channel, filling every hollow, going wherever it had been sealed out of for years.

Tomas stood ten feet away and did not go to her.

Everything in him wanted to. His feet actually shifted, weight rolling forward, and Priya's hand came up — not touching him, just present, a fence made of one arm — and he understood. This was not his to comfort. This was not anyone's to comfort. Some griefs are communal and some are private even in company, and this one belonged to a daughter standing in front of her father's last work, and the kindest thing any of them could do was witness it and stay upright.

So he witnessed. He looked at her bent head and her shaking hands and the letter going soft with salt water at its edges, and he thought about his uncle dying in a rented room with a confession on his tongue and twenty years of complicity in his lungs, and he thought: *this is what guilt looks like when it's finally allowed to move.* His uncle had never gotten this. Had died holding it. Tomas had carried it up the mountain hoping to set it down somewhere, and here, on a shelf of stone below a col, he was watching what setting it down actually looked like.

It looked like this. It looked like ruin. It looked like the beginning of a foundation.

Minutes passed. The wind pulsed. Somewhere above them, ice calved off the serac field with a sound like a door closing in an empty house, and nobody flinched.

Slowly, the crying changed register. Not stopping — settling. Deepening into something steadier, the way a river stops thrashing when it finds its bed. Elodie straightened. She wiped her face with the back of one wrist, smearing tears into the grime already there, and she looked down at the photographs spread on the stone, and when she spoke her voice was wrecked and absolutely clear.

"Forty-one households," she said. "In this one village alone. Forty-one families, and their fields, and their shrine, and their dead." She picked up a photograph — the shrine, the flags — and held it up to the light as if comparing it to the actual horizon. "And a lawyer is climbing the other side of this range right now to call this box stolen property."

"It is property," the surveyor said quietly. "Stolen from them. That's rather the point."

Elodie almost smiled. Almost. "Yes," she said. "It is."

She began repacking the box with the same deliberation she'd used to open it. Ledger. Papers. Photographs, squared and fanned. The letter last — and here she paused, and Tomas saw her look at the wax seal broken on the stone, and he saw her decide something.

"The letter comes with me," she said. "Not in the box. In my jacket. Against my chest." She met Priya's eyes. "If I go into a crevasse, it goes with me. If we make it down, it goes to Meera with the rest. But it doesn't leave my body until one of those things happens."

"Understood," Priya said.

Tomas cleared his throat. It took two tries. "Elodie."

She turned. Her eyes were red-rimmed and dry now, and behind the grief there was something new, something he recognized from mirrors in his own worst hours: direction. Grief with a bearing on it. Grief converted to fuel.

"Your uncle," she said, before he could speak. "The report. The falsified figures. You told me in the tent, and I walked out, and I owe you an answer." She looked down at the cairn, then back at him. "My father spent his last night writing to a stranger because he couldn't trust a single official in the valley. Your uncle was one of the officials he couldn't trust. That's the truth, and I'm not going to sand it down for your comfort." She held up the letter, slightly. "But he also wrote this: *stop blaming, and start building.* So. You're going to help me build. You're going to testify, and sign, and stand in whatever room they put us in, and say your uncle's name out loud and exactly what he did. Can you do that?"

"Yes," Tomas said. It came out steady, which surprised him. "I've been practicing his name in my mouth for six months. Saying what he did will be easier."

"Good." She tucked the letter inside her jacket, zipped it, patted it once like a wound being dressed. Then she looked west, toward the col, where the pewter sky had begun to lower itself onto the ridgeline like a lid.

Because that was the other thing. While they'd dug, the weather had not waited. The cloud deck that had been sitting on the far peaks all morning had crossed the divide while no one was watching, and it was coming down the eastern faces now, thick and wet and fast, eating the upper slopes in long gray bites. The monsoon's leading edge. Hours early, the way everything on this mountain had been hours early since the day they started.

Dev spoke first, from his boulder, binoculars still raised. "There's movement on the back trail."

Everyone turned.

"Below the icefall," he said. "Six, maybe seven. Ropes and packs. Moving fast." He lowered the glasses, and his young face was pale and set. "They're not following our track. They're cutting straight for the col."

Priya swore softly. Elodie zipped her jacket the rest of the way, and when she spoke again, the grief was still in her voice — Tomas could hear it, would always hear it — but it was riding underneath something harder now, something with edges.

"Then we meet them at the top," she said. "Everybody pack. We climb."
