## 14. Interception

Priya saw them first because she was the one who never stopped looking back.

It was a habit she'd learned from Elodie on her second season — *the mountain doesn't chase you, Priya, but everything you brought with you does* — and for six days it had paid off in nothing but wind and scree. On the seventh morning, harnessed to the fixed line above the col, she turned to check Dev's spacing and saw movement two ridgelines down: a column of climbers moving with the unhurried discipline of people who had never once worried about their margin.

Not refugees. Not porters. A procession.

She counted nine. Three were roped like clients, moving stiffly, boots wrong for the grade. Four moved like professionals — even spacing, efficient rest steps, ice axes carried rather than leaned on. The last two stayed close to a large red pack that one of them never put down, not even during halts.

"Elodie." She kept her voice level. "Company."

Elodie was twenty meters ahead, cutting steps across the col's wind-scoured shoulder. She didn't turn around. Priya watched her shoulders change instead — a settling, the way a house settles before something inside it breaks.

"How far?"

"Two hours. Maybe less if they're fresh."

"They're fresh." Elodie drove her axe in and finally looked back, and Priya watched her do the arithmetic she herself had already done: nine against seven, one of theirs a schoolteacher's grandson who'd learned to read crevasses eleven days ago, another a dying man's nephew carrying a tin box wrapped in his spare shirt. "They came up the east side."

"The east side is ours," Tomas said behind them. He'd gone very still. "That's the approach my uncle's company used. There's a hut. They've been climbing from the other side since before the storm wall."

"Then they've been waiting for us to come to them." Elodie unclipped from the line. "Everyone off rope. We're taking the col while we still choose where to stand."

* * *

The col itself was a cruel piece of ground — a saddle of blue ice and shattered rock no wider than a road, dropping away on both sides into faces steep enough to make altitude feel like a courtesy. Wind funneled through it from the west in long, rhythmic gusts that arrived every forty seconds like a door opening and closing somewhere below the world.

Elodie chose their ground with the economy of someone setting a table. Rope team anchored into the rock rib on the north edge, packs cached, axes out but held low. Dev she placed flat behind a boulder with the binoculars and one instruction: *count, don't stare.* Meera's ledger — the tin box, the photographs, her father's letter — went into Priya's pack, against her spine, where she could feel its weight with every breath.

"You should take it and traverse now," Tomas said quietly. "While they're still an hour out. I'll talk to them."

"And say what? That you'll hand over your dead uncle's confession if they promise to be nice?" Priya adjusted the pack straps until the box bit pleasantly between her shoulder blades. "You heard the surveyor. Their lawyer doesn't make promises. He makes invoices."

"I'm the one who hired her illegally. I'm the one they want."

"You're the one they'll *use*," Elodie said, without heat. "There's a difference, and it's measured in what happens to everyone standing near you when they're done. Nobody moves. Nobody hands over anything. We let them spend their breath getting here."

So they waited, and the wind opened and closed its door, and Priya spent the time doing what she did best: watching people.

Dev had stopped shaking. Somewhere between the ledge and the crevasse city he had crossed some internal line, and now he lay behind his boulder with the patience of a much older man, binoculars steady, reporting intervals in a whisper. Ravi checked the anchors twice without being asked. Old Suren sat on his pack eating dates as though awaiting a bus. And Elodie stood at the center of it all with her arms loose and her weight balanced, looking — Priya realized with a small cold shock — *happy*. Not glad. Not relieved. Happy the way a blade is happy, which is to say: finally being used for the thing it was made for.

The column arrived ninety minutes later, faster than she'd guessed.

They came up onto the col in good order and then stopped, all nine of them, in a formation so deliberate it could only have been rehearsed. The four professionals fanned wide. The three clients — and up close there was no mistaking them, soft hands, sunburned in patches, gear worth more than the crew's combined annual pay — clustered behind the man in the middle.

He was smaller than Priya expected. Grey-bearded, wire-framed glasses fogged at the edges, wearing a technical shell that had clearly never been closer to weather than a hotel balcony. He walked forward alone across the ice with the careful, offended steps of a man crossing a floor he believed beneath him, and stopped ten meters from Elodie.

"Mister Anselm Roth," he said, by way of introduction, as though they had requested him. "Counsel for the Karveth Development Consortium. And you are Elodie Montrose, formerly of this range, currently in violation of—" he consulted a tablet he produced from his coat, gloved fingers clumsy on the screen, "—three separate access statutes, one employment prohibition, and an active heritage protection order covering the route you have just, I'm afraid, illegally completed."

"You forgot littering," Elodie said.

A flicker of something crossed Roth's face — not amusement; recalibration. He turned the tablet toward her. "This is a certified inventory of items removed from a protected site. A sealed container, historical documents, photographic materials. Stolen property, Miss Montrose. My clients are prepared to be generous about how it came into your possession. People get lost in these mountains. People find things. There need be no criminal dimension to any of this — for anyone." His eyes moved, briefly, to Tomas. "Particularly not for a man already grieving."

Tomas stepped forward and Priya caught his sleeve. "Your clients burned a village," he said.

"My clients," Roth said mildly, "acquired land through standard instruments following a natural disaster. Whatever a ledger says happened afterward is a matter for courts, and courts require evidence that hasn't been handled by interested parties at altitude for three weeks. Frankly, Mr. Vey, whatever is in that box is worth more as paper than as proof. Hand it over, walk down the east side with full support, and everyone's reputation survives the season." He smiled with genuine warmth. "Including hers. We can be flexible about old blacklists. These things are reviewed."

"There it is," Priya murmured.

"There it is," Elodie agreed, just loud enough.

Roth's smile thinned. "Miss Montrose. I've climbed nothing in my life and I know exactly what this ground is. Look at it." He gestured — a small, precise motion — at the ice, the drop, the wind. "Nine of us. Seven of you. And not one person on this col can take a single aggressive step without killing half the people present. You can't fight here. We can't fight here. So this is not a confrontation. This is a negotiation between reasonable parties who would both very much like to see their families again."

For a moment nobody spoke. The wind gusted through, hard enough to make the ropes hum, and died.

"You're right," Elodie said.

Priya felt the crew go still around her.

"It's a terrible place to fight," Elodie continued, walking slowly along the invisible line between them, boots finding the same step-sockets Roth had used, as if to say *I own this ground too.* "One bad swing, one pulled anchor, and we all go over together — tied together, some of us, literally. You did your homework. So here's my counteroffer, counselor, and I'll only make it once." She stopped. "You turn around. You go back down the way you came. And when you reach the valley, you tell whoever sent you that the ledger is no longer on this mountain. It hasn't been since the storm wall. Copies exist in places you can't reach and hands you can't buy. What's left up here is just us — witnesses. And witnesses don't get stolen property charges. They get testimony."

Something shifted among the four professionals — a fractional redistribution of weight, hands drifting toward jackets. Priya's own hand found her axe. Behind Roth, one of the clients said, in a voice pitched for reassurance and missing by a mile, "Anselm, perhaps—"

"Quiet, Gerald."

"You're bluffing about copies," Roth said.

"I'm describing consequences," Elodie said. "Bluffing is for people who need you to believe something. I don't need anything from you. That's the whole problem with negotiating against someone who's already given the truth away, counselor. You arrived holding the only currency I ever wanted, and I spent it a week ago."

Roth took off his glasses, breathed on them, wiped them on his sleeve with maddening slowness, and put them back on. When he looked up, the warmth was gone entirely, and what remained was worse — a kind of professional disappointment, the look of a man reviewing a contract clause he'd hoped never to invoke.

"Then I'm instructed to inform you," he said, "that my clients retain other assets in the region. Legal ones. Human ones. The heir you're presumably descending toward — the schoolteacher — lives in a town with a road, Miss Montrose. Roads run both ways. Whatever ceremony you're planning can be attended." He paused delicately. "By anyone."

The wind slammed through the col, and this time it seemed to carry his words out over both drop-offs, scattering them into the country below.

* * *

They watched the consortium party descend the east side for an hour, until the column shrank to dots and the dots to memory. Nobody spoke much. Suren finished the dates. Dev reported positions in a voice gone hoarse from whispering.

It was Priya who finally broke the silence, because someone had to, and because she had spent eleven days watching Elodie carry other people's courage and thought it was time somebody carried hers.

"He's right about the road," she said.

"I know."

"And the agents will be there before we are. Word travels faster than climbers — it always has. They'll be in the market square with money and lawyers and probably the local police chief on retainer, and we'll come down off this mountain exhausted, half-starved, carrying a story nobody's heard yet." She crouched beside Elodie, who was studying the western descent with the fixed attention of someone reading a difficult letter. "We can't outrun them down a valley."

"No." Elodie didn't look up. "But we were never going to outrun them anyway. That was always the shape of this. The mountain was the easy part — I told myself that in the crevasse and I didn't believe it, and now I do." She stood, and rolled her shoulders, and when she turned to face the crew her voice had changed — pitched to carry, stripped of everything private. "Here's the gamble. There's a descent from this col that isn't on any map, because no guide has ever survived claiming it. The Long Traverse. My father drew it in his notes — the line he was scouting when they blacklisted him, the route he swore would cut two days off the descent to the valley towns. It goes through ground so bad the consortium won't follow it, because their guides are good and their guides are sane."

"And ours?" Ravi asked.

"Ours are unemployed," Elodie said, and something that was almost a laugh went around the rope team. "It's three days of ground that will try to kill us every hour. But it puts us into the valley above the town, ahead of the road, ahead of their agents — tired, yes, but *there*, with the ledger and the photographs and the surveyor, before Roth's people can buy the room. After that it stops being vertical. After that it's just people, in daylight, in front of witnesses." She looked at each of them in turn, and Priya saw her save Dev for last, and hold there. "I won't order anyone onto that traverse. It's beyond anything I've asked of you. Vote."

Nobody voted. That was the thing Priya would remember afterward — that nobody moved, nobody spoke, they simply began, in silence, packing rope.

Tomas was the last to shoulder his pack. He paused beside Priya, looking east at the empty col, and said quietly: "He wasn't bluffing either. About the road."

"I know," Priya said. "Neither are we."

Below them, the western face fell away into cloud — two days of unclimbed ground, unnamed on every map that mattered — and Elodie Montrose stood at its edge reading it the way other people read home.
