## 15. The Long Traverse

The col at dawn was a knife laid flat between two worlds.

On one side, the consortium's party had made their camp in the lee of a serac — six tents, cook smoke rising straight in the windless cold, the lawyer's orange parka moving among them like a signal flag planted on territory he believed was already his. On the other side, the descent we'd come down to take: a broad snow ramp, gentle by Karveth standards, that would have put us in the valley town in four days.

The lawyer's men had reached it first. Two of them were already on the ramp, driving wands into the snow every fifty meters, marking a route they intended to control the way a toll-keeper controls a bridge.

I lay on my stomach at the col's edge with binoculars pressed to my eyes and counted them again. Nine visible. Probably twelve total. Ropes fixed across our exit like a fence.

"They're not even trying to hide it," Priya said beside me. "It's almost insulting."

"It's efficient." I passed her the glasses. "Why waste men sneaking up a mountain when you can just stand at the bottom and wait? We have to come down eventually. Everyone comes down."

Tomas crouched behind us, and Dev hovered a little farther back, trying to look like he wasn't listening to every word. Below us, our own camp was still half-struck — tents sagging under last night's snowfall, the two survivors from the buried party sitting wrapped in borrowed insulation, drinking tea we could barely afford to spare. The surveyor, Halloran, watched me with the particular patience of a man who had sold me what he knew and was waiting to see if it was worth anything.

"So we go through them," Priya said. It wasn't really a question. She said it the way she said everything now — flat, tired, ready.

"Twelve against seven, on ground where any fight ends with somebody going into a crevasse." I shook my head. "And even if we won, we'd win a body count. That ledger stops being justice the moment it's carried past a corpse."

"Then we wait them out." Tomas's voice was tight. "The monsoon will break their camp before it breaks us. They'll have to retreat—"

"And they'll retreat down the ramp we need, and they'll leave two men behind with a radio, and the moment we show our faces in the valley town there'll be agents waiting at the bus station. You've seen how fast word moves." I looked back at the far side, where the smoke rose straight as a plumb line. "We don't have weeks. Meera — the heir — she's an old woman who teaches school. Every day we spend up here is a day the consortium spends getting to her first. We already know they've sent people ahead."

Silence, except for wind beginning to stir the col, lifting spindrift off the crest in long white banners.

Dev cleared his throat. "There's always the glacier route. Around the east shoulder—"

"Fourteen days," Priya said. "In monsoon conditions. With this team."

"Yeah," Dev said. "Bad idea."

"Not bad," I said slowly. "Wrong direction."

They all turned to look at me, and I realized I'd been staring at the north wall without seeing it for the last minute — or rather, seeing it for the first time in twenty years.

* * *

Every guide who works the Karveth learns the same map. South faces, valley approaches, the routes the permits cover and the rescue helicopters can reach. It's a good map. It keeps people alive.

But when you're nineteen years old and your father is drawing lines in a notebook by lamplight, muttering about how *the official routes are roads, and roads are where they'll look*, you learn a different map too. He never finished it. He died on the standard route, ironically, doing the honest work he thought would protect him. But his notes — the ones in the depot, the ones I'd read until the paper went soft in my hands — described something else. A line he'd scouted from below and never climbed. A traverse.

"The north wall," I said.

Priya made a sound low in her throat. Not quite a laugh.

"You're joking," Tomas said.

"I'm absolutely not joking." I pulled out the route notes, photocopies now — the originals stayed cached, wrapped, sacred — and spread them on a flattened pack. My father's handwriting marched across the page in tight, confident strokes, annotated with elevations and snow-stability guesses and one long paragraph describing a continuous lateral line across the north face of the range at roughly five-eight thousand meters: a band of broken rock and hanging ice connecting three gully systems, traversable, in his estimation, in good conditions over two long days.

"In whose estimation?" Priya asked. "He never climbed it."

"He soloed the first third in 'ninety-one. Scouted to the second gully. Then the season closed and he came home and told my mother he'd found something worth coming back for." My voice did something strange on that sentence and I let it. "No guide has ever repeated the line. No guide has ever *tried*. After the disaster, after him, the whole north side got written off as unprotectable. Too exposed, no escape options, avalanche terrain the entire length. Any legitimate outfit proposing it would lose their insurance, their permit, and probably their license."

"But you don't have a license," Dev said. Then went pale. "Sorry."

"No," I said. "I don't. Two countries saw to that." I tapped the page. "Twenty years ago that would have been the end of the story. Instead it means I'm the only person alive who has spent two decades learning routes nobody sane would touch, because nobody sane would hire me to touch anything else. The blacklist took my name. It gave me a map."

"That's not a reason," Priya said quietly. "That's a wound talking."

"Maybe. Look at it anyway."

* * *

We looked at it. All morning, while the consortium's camp below cooked breakfast and strung more rope and grew comfortable in the certainty that geometry was on their side.

I walked Priya and Tomas and Dev along the base of the wall with binoculars, tracing my father's line against the real thing. The first gully system was there, exactly where the notes placed it — a steep snow chute funneled between rock ribs, fed by a hanging serac field that would need crossing early, hard, before the sun touched it. Then the traverse itself: two kilometers of lateral ground, a mix of rock ledges and ice ramps stitched together, some of it protected, most of it not, all of it above a drop that didn't bear thinking about in terms of meters so much as in terms of time — how long you'd fall, not how far.

"The second gully is the crux," I said. "My father marked it with a question mark. He couldn't see the upper section from his high point. It might be a walk. It might be a wall. We find out when we're on it."

"A question mark," Priya repeated. "At the crux. Of a route with no retreat and no rescue."

"Yes."

"And if the weather closes during the traverse?"

"We shelter where we are. There are crevasse systems under the ice ramps — his notes mark three. Cold, miserable, survivable."

"And if someone falls?"

I didn't answer that one, because there was no answer that wasn't a lie.

We stood in the wind at the base of the wall and I watched my crew do the arithmetic. Dev, twenty-three, surgery money for his mother waiting at the end of this like a light at the end of a very long tunnel. Priya, who had mortgaged her own equipment business to keep the crew solvent through two lean seasons. Tomas, who had inherited his uncle's guilt along with his uncle's ledger and seemed determined to pay both off personally. And the porters — Sanjay and the brothers, who had voted in a tent in chapter one of this mess to follow me onto a forbidden mountain, and had not yet been given a reason to regret it.

"It's a gamble," I said finally. "I want to be precise about what kind. If we go down the ramp, we lose the ledger, we lose Meera, and we probably lose the crew's future, because the consortium does not forgive witnesses. If we wait, we freeze in place and lose slower. If we go around the east shoulder, we lose by exhaustion — fourteen days in monsoon with this much food is not a plan, it's a eulogy with extra steps."

"And the traverse?" Dev asked.

"The traverse is the only option where the mountain decides instead of the lawyers." I met each of their eyes. "It's also the only option that might kill all of us. I won't pretend those facts cancel out. I'm asking you to choose which risk you'd rather carry."

* * *

The vote happened the way our votes always happened — quickly, quietly, without speeches, because everyone understood that speeches were just fear wearing its good coat.

Sanjay spoke for the porters first. "You got us up here alive," he said. "Through the icefall, through the whiteout, with armed men behind us. Whatever you point at next, that's where I'm walking." The brothers nodded.

Dev hesitated longer than anyone. I watched him look up at the wall — really look, the way I'd taught him to, reading the terrain instead of fearing it — and then look down at his hands, which had frozen once on a ledge already this trip and hadn't failed him since. "If we get down the other side," he said, "I want to be the one who carries the ledger to the schoolteacher. Deal?"

"Deal," I said.

Priya was last, which was also her way. She stood facing the wall for a full minute, and when she turned around her expression was the one she wore when she'd finished grieving a decision and was ready to execute it. "Two conditions. One: if the second gully is a wall — a real wall, not a scramble — we turn around, weather or no weather, and we take the east shoulder and eat our boots. Two:" she jabbed a finger at my chest, "you stop calling it your father's route. It's ours now. Dead men don't rope up."

"Those are good terms," I said, and meant it, and something in my chest loosened that I hadn't known was knotted.

Tomas simply said, "I'll follow," and for once the words sounded like penance rather than ambition.

* * *

We spent the afternoon preparing, and preparation has its own grim liturgy.

We cached everything we didn't absolutely need — tent poles, the stove's second burner, half the rope, all ceremony. Sixty-meter ropes cut down to forty. Four ice screws each, two rock pitons, a minimal rack. Food for four days compressed into bags that rattled. The ledger went into a waterproof tube strapped to my own pack, because I had stopped pretending I'd trust it to anyone else, and my father's letter went inside my jacket, against my skin, where it had lived since the cairn.

Halloran watched us strip down with professional interest. "You're actually going to try the north traverse," he said. "The one from the old survey reports. They flagged it as a hazard corridor, not a route."

"They would," I said. "Hazard corridors don't need permits."

He almost smiled. "For what it's worth — the consortium's brief on you describes you as unpredictable. I'd say they undersold it."

"Tell your lawyer friend we're going east," I said. "Loudly. Let him spend two days watching a glacier that doesn't have us on it."

That was the other half of the gamble, the part I'd been turning over since dawn: misdirection. At dusk, Sanjay and one of the brothers would strike a decoy trail toward the east shoulder, visible from below, complete with a discarded food bag and a false cache. Twelve men with binoculars would watch two figures recede into the wrong drainage and congratulate themselves. By the time the lawyer realized the arithmetic didn't work — that the rest of us had vanished off his board entirely — we'd be two thousand horizontal meters across a face no one was watching, because no one believed it could be crossed.

"The pursuers must follow or die waiting," Priya murmured, watching me rig the decoy. "That's the pitch?"

"That's the pitch. Except they can't follow. That's the whole point. This route exists because the blacklist taught me things no legitimate guide would dare — and no legitimate guide is climbing for the consortium either. Their hired guns are strong men with rifles, not climbers. Put them on the north wall and they're cargo." I cinched the knot and looked up at the face, blue-shadowed now in the failing light, my father's question mark hidden somewhere in its upper reaches. "We're not outrunning them. We're going somewhere they can't exist."

* * *

We left at midnight.

Alpine starts are supposed to feel like escapes, but this one felt like a held breath. We moved roped in two teams — Priya with Dev and one brother, me with Tomas and Sanjay — crampons biting the frozen approach gully, headlamps making small trembling circles of the dark. Below and behind us, the consortium's camp glowed with its cook fires, blind and comfortable. Somewhere on the east shoulder, two tiny lights bobbed westward, selling our fiction.

By the time the first grey light came up, we were at the base of the first gully, and the sun found the hanging serac field above it and set the whole thing burning gold and terrible.

"Sun's on it in forty minutes," Priya said. "Then it's shedding all day."

"Then we climb like we mean it."

I went first. Of course I went first; some habits are load-bearing. And somewhere in the third pitch, kicking steps up a chute that had never been kicked, placing tools in ice my father had described in a notebook thirty years ago, I felt the thing I'd been afraid to feel since the col — not fear, exactly, though fear was there, humming like a live wire — but *recognition*. Twenty years of blacklisted winters, of taking contracts no one else wanted, of learning the mountain's ugly back stairs because the front door was locked to me. Every one of those miles had been building to this. The disgrace had been the apprenticeship.

Above me, the traverse stretched away into the unknown, two kilometers of maybe, ending at a question mark.

Below me, my crew followed, breathing hard, trusting me with the only currency that mattered.

I spat on my hands, reset my tools, and kept climbing.
