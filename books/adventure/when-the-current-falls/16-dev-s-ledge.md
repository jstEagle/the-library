## 16. Dev's Ledge

The traverse began where the mountain stopped pretending to be kind.

Dev had heard the older porters talk about the Karveth's south face the way sailors talked about drowned men — in lowered voices, with a superstitious glance at the sky. No guide had ever crossed it. The route existed only in Elodie Montrose's head, and now, apparently, in a series of chalk marks she was leaving on the rock like a woman writing her will.

They'd left the blockade party at dawn, moving fast along the col while the consortium's guides were still boiling water. Priya had asked once, quietly, "Are you sure about this?" and Elodie had answered by walking. That was answer enough.

Now they stood at the base of a shelf of gray limestone that ran horizontally across the face for perhaps four hundred meters, no wider than a tabletop in most places, tilted outward like a bookshelf overloaded with centuries. Below it, the drop went down through cloud. Not into cloud — *through* cloud, because the cloud was still above them somewhere, and the bottom of the fall was a rumor.

"Rope length apart," Elodie said. "Nobody crowds anybody. Feet together, three points of contact, always. If I say freeze, you freeze. If I say move, you move." She looked at each of them in turn, and when her eyes reached Dev, they stayed a half-second longer. "Dev. You're third."

He nodded. His mouth had gone dry enough that he wasn't sure his voice would work anyway.

Tomas went first, then Priya, then Dev, then Elodie anchoring the rear with the surveyor roped behind her — the man from the buried camp, who kept introducing himself as Renshaw as if politeness might save him. The morning was cold and clear, which Dev knew by now was not good news. Clear meant warm sun on the rock. Warm sun on rock meant meltwater prying at the seams. Meltwater in the seams meant—

"Don't think about it," Priya said over her shoulder, reading him the way she always did. "Think about your feet."

His feet. He could do feet.

The first hundred meters were almost easy. The ledge was wide enough for his whole boot, even both boots side by side in places, and there were handholds worn smooth by some ancient process of the stone itself. He found a rhythm: reach, step, breathe. Reach, step, breathe. The rope paid out behind him in small patient sounds, and Elodie's presence at his back felt less like a person and more like gravity that had decided to be kind.

At two hundred meters the ledge narrowed.

It happened gradually, then all at once — the way things did on mountains. One moment his boots fit; the next, only his toes did, and the wall on his right leaned out over him so that he had to bend forward into the emptiness to keep his pack from scraping stone. The wind came up the face in long slow breaths, warm and cold alternating, pushing at him like a hand testing whether he'd fall.

*Three points of contact,* he told himself. *Reach, step, breathe.*

But at two hundred and forty meters, the ledge simply stopped being a ledge.

There was a gap. Maybe two meters of nothing, bridged by a flake of rock the width of his palm, and beyond it the shelf resumed, narrower than before, sloping down toward the void instead of away from it.

"Tomas," Elodie called. "Report."

"Gap's crossable," Tomas's voice floated back, thin with distance. "Flake is solid. I tested it."

"I know you tested it. Dev—" and there she was, close behind him, close enough that he could hear her breathing, level and unhurried, the breathing of someone standing in a kitchen rather than on a shelf above a kilometer of air. "You're up. Flake first, then step across. Don't jump. Jumping is how people die here."

Dev looked at the gap.

Something happened to him then that he would spend the rest of his life trying to describe. It wasn't fear exactly, or not only fear. It was as if the mountain reached into his chest and turned off a switch. His hands stayed on the rock. His eyes stayed on the flake. But everything between those things — the part of him that decided, the part that moved — went quiet and white, like a radio losing its station.

"Dev."

He didn't answer. He couldn't. The wind pushed. Somewhere below, impossibly far below, a stone clattered down a gully, and the sound took so long to come back up that he understood, in a distant academic way, that he was going to die on this ledge, today, and that everyone had known it would be him, the porter, the boy from the low valleys who'd joined the crew because there was nothing else—

A hand closed on his shoulder. Firm. Warm through two layers.

"Look at me."

He couldn't turn his head. Turning his head meant turning his body, and turning his body meant—

"You don't have to turn. Just listen to my voice. Can you hear me?"

"Yes." It came out as a whisper scraped raw.

"Good. My voice is the only thing on this mountain right now. Not the wind. Not the drop. Me." Her voice was steady. Perfectly steady. Later, much later, he would learn what it cost her — would see, when they were all safe and she thought no one was watching, the fine tremor in her fingers as she coiled rope, the way she pressed her palms flat against her thighs until the shaking stopped. But on the ledge there was only the voice, calm as a well. "I want you to tell me what your left hand is touching."

"What?"

"Tell me what it feels like. Right now."

"Limestone. It's... warm. Where the sun hit it."

"Warm limestone. Good. And your right foot?"

"On the edge. My toes. The rock is gritty there, like sand."

"Gritty sand under your toes. Good. You're doing perfectly. Now I'm going to ask you to do one thing, and it's the only thing in the world. Reach your left hand across and touch the flake. Don't step. Don't look down. Just touch it."

His arm moved. He watched it move as though it belonged to someone else, reaching across the gap, and his fingers found the flake — rough, sun-warmed, real.

"It's solid," he said, and heard wonder creep into his own voice.

"It's solid. Now shift your weight onto it, one centimeter at a time. I'll count with you. One."

"One."

"Two."

Somewhere around seven, his weight crossed. Around twelve, his boot came down on the far side, on the narrow downward-sloping shelf, and his body remembered how to be a body. He flattened himself against the wall and breathed, huge ragged breaths, and heard Elodie say, quietly, to no one and everyone:

"That's it. That's my climber."

*My climber.* The words went into him deeper than any rope.

Then the mountain spoke.

It started as a sound he felt before he heard — a deep grinding note in the rock under his chest, like the mountain clearing its throat. Then a crack, sharp as a rifle shot, somewhere above and to the left, and the sunlit face shed its morning warmth all at once.

"ROCKFALL!" Priya screamed from ahead. "ROCKFALL, GET FLAT!"

Dev pressed himself into the wall as stones began to sing past him — small ones first, whining like insects, then larger ones booming off the face, shattering into shrapnel that stung his neck and knuckles. A piece the size of a skull went by close enough that he felt the wind of it. He squeezed his eyes shut and made himself small and thought, absurdly, of his mother's kitchen, of rice steam on a window.

The barrage lasted maybe forty seconds. It felt like forty years.

When it stopped, the silence rang. Dust drifted past him in golden threads.

"Sound off," Elodie said. "Tomas."

"Here."

"Priya."

"Here. Renshaw's fine. Terrified, but fine."

"Dev."

He opened his mouth. Nothing came. He tried again. "Here."

"Move. Now. The face is unstable — that was a warning shot, not the main event. Everyone across, fast but smooth. Dev, you're closest to the loose section. Go."

Go. He could go. He had gone across the gap; he could go along the shelf. He moved, reach-step-breathe, faster now, the rhythm broken by flinches every time the mountain muttered overhead. Fifty meters. Forty. The ledge ahead widened where Priya waited at an anchor point, and hope rose in him like water in a dry streambed—

And then the world tilted.

Not the world — the ledge. The section behind him, the very stone he'd frozen on, groaned once, deeply, the way ice does before it goes, and Dev felt it begin to move under his boots.

"RUN!" Elodie's voice, stripped of all its calm now, pure command.

He ran. There was no technique anymore, no three points of contact, just a boy sprinting sideways along a shelf above the sky with the mountain peeling away behind him like bark off a branch. The ledge cracked. Tilted. Slid.

Hands seized his pack — Priya's, hauling him over the anchor and onto the wide ground beyond — and as he tumbled onto solid rock, he looked back in time to see the entire section let go.

Two hundred meters of shelf, the gap, the flake, the place where he had frozen and the place where he had run — all of it detached from the face with a sound like a door slamming in the earth's own house, and fell, turning slowly, breaking into pieces as it dropped, down and down, until the cloud swallowed it and the sound arrived late, a long rolling thunder of everything he hadn't been standing on anymore.

Silence.

Dust settled on them like snow.

Dev lay on his back on honest, immovable ground, chest heaving, and became aware that Elodie was crouched beside him, checking his ropes with quick expert hands, and that her face — her famous unreadable face — was doing something he'd never seen it do. It was afraid. For him. Openly, unguardedly afraid, and glad, enormously glad, underneath the fear.

"You froze," she said.

"I know. I'm sorry—"

"No." She held up a hand. "Listen to me, Dev. Freezing isn't failure. Freezing is your body telling you the truth about where you are. Every climber freezes. The ones who die are the ones who lie about it." She pulled him upright by his harness, and her grip was strong and certain. "You told me the truth. Warm limestone. Gritty sand. And then, when it mattered, you ran exactly when I said run and not one second before. Do you understand what that is? That's not a porter following orders. That's a climber trusting his guide and his own judgment in the same breath."

He stared at her. Above her shoulder, the empty space where the ledge had been was already filling with fresh cloud, erasing the evidence, as if the mountain were embarrassed by what it had done.

"I'm not a student anymore?" he asked. His voice cracked on it, half question, half prayer.

Elodie smiled — a real smile, rare as water in the Karveth, tired and fierce and true. "You haven't been for a while. I just wanted you to survive finding out."

Priya whooped, actually whooped, and Tomas laughed his dry rusted laugh, and even Renshaw made a sound that might have been relief or might have been a man discovering he still had a soul. They gathered at the anchor point, five climbers and one ex-ledge, alive on the far side of the impossible traverse.

"Camp's two hours on," Elodie said, shouldering her pack. "We descend tomorrow, weather holding. Which means—"

She stopped.

She was looking east, past them all, toward the valley town threaded with smoke in the distance, and her expression had changed again — the softness folding away, replaced by something sharper. She'd seen what Dev saw a heartbeat later: figures on the moraine below the far end of the traverse. Distant. Deliberate. Moving.

"They didn't follow us across," Priya said quietly.

"They didn't have to." Elodie swung her pack down and pulled out her map, spreading it against the rock, her finger tracing the descent lines, the paths, the roads, all the ways down that didn't require courage, only speed. "There are two routes into that valley. We've taken the hard one. They're taking the easy one." Her finger stopped. "Which means we're no longer racing them up a mountain."

She looked up, and Dev saw in her face the same thing he'd felt on the ledge — the moment when fear becomes direction.

"We're racing them to Meera."
