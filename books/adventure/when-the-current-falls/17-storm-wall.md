## 17. Storm Wall

The mountain announced itself before the sky did.

Tomas felt it first as a pressure in his ears, a deep subsonic hum that traveled up through the ice and into the bones of his skull — the sound of a storm wall the size of a country shouldering its way inland. Then came the light change: the flat, sourceless gray that erased shadows and made the crevasse city look like a photograph of itself. Then, finally, the wind.

It hit at four in the afternoon, or what Elodie's watch claimed was four in the afternoon, though time had stopped meaning much in the blue cathedral of the glacier. The team had spent two hours digging into the crevasse field that Dev had named — half-joking, half-praying — *the city*, a labyrinth of deep blue fissures where the icefall's wreckage had piled against the mountain's flank. They'd cut snow-block windwalls across three of the wider mouths, pitched both tents inside the largest chamber, and stacked their cached supplies like sandbags.

Now the monsoon arrived to collect its debt.

The roar was not like wind Tomas knew from the Alps or the Andes. This was a living thing, a freight train with no track, no schedule, no mercy. Snow didn't fall so much as travel horizontally, a white river pouring through the crevasse mouths above them. The tent walls snapped and bellied like sails in a gale, and the guy-lines sang three different notes, high and thin, a chord of pure tension.

"Thirty hours," Elodie said. She crouched by the stove, shielding the flame with her body the way she shielded everything else — instinctively, without seeming to notice she was doing it. "Maybe thirty-six if we're lucky. We burn fuel for melt only. No cooking dry rations — they eat fine cold. One pot of water per six hours, shared."

Priya nodded and began dividing the fuel canisters with the methodical calm that made her the crew's spine. Dev sat against the pack wall, hands wrapped around his knees, listening to the mountain with an expression Tomas recognized because he'd worn it himself twenty years ago on a different mountain: the look of a young man recalculating every assumption he'd ever made about his own courage.

The two survivors from the buried camp huddled in the corner farthest from the stove. The surveyor — Kessler, he'd finally given his name — watched Elodie with the careful attention of a man taking inventory of his rescuer. His companion, a heavyset ex-guide named Brandt, slept in the exhausted way of men who have been dug out of an avalanche and are not yet sure they're grateful.

Tomas checked the anchor lines a third time, then a fourth, then forced himself to stop. Fussing was fear wearing a work jacket.

He settled onto his pack beside the tent wall and listened.

The mountain roared. Somewhere above them, whole seracs were coming down; he could hear the deep concussions rolling through the ice like artillery fired in another valley. Spindrift poured through the crevasse mouth in silver sheets. The temperature inside the tent hovered just above freezing, and the air smelled of stove fuel, wet wool, and six people's breath.

*Thirty hours,* he thought. *Trapped in a hole in the ice with the woman whose father I helped destroy.*

Not helped destroy. That was the coward's phrasing. His uncle had falsified the report; Tomas had spent twenty years building a career on the reputation that falsification protected — the safe routes, the permitted crossings, the celebrated ascents of mountains other people died on. He hadn't pulled the trigger. He'd simply never once asked who was holding it.

Elodie caught him looking. Her eyes were red-rimmed — from wind, he told himself, from spindrift, from anything but crying — and she held his gaze for a long moment before turning back to the stove.

"We need to talk," she said.

Not now, every instinct in him said. Not in a coffin of ice with two strangers listening and your crew pretending not to.

"Now," she said, as if he'd spoken aloud. She turned off the stove to save fuel, and in the sudden quiet the storm filled the tent completely, enormous and indifferent. "Kessler. You told me you'd testify about the consortium's survey teams. I'm holding you to that when we're down. But right now I need you and Brandt to sleep. Priya — take first watch on the anchor lines. Dev, second. Tomas—"

She stood. It took effort; he could see it cost her something in the knees and the lower back, the small taxes the mountain levied on all of them.

"—walk with me."

* * *

They couldn't walk far. That was the absurdity of it. Two rope lengths up the crevasse chamber, wedged behind a snow wall they'd cut that morning, there was a space barely large enough for two people to stand facing each other, lit by the gray glow filtering through the ice overhead. The storm screamed somewhere above them, muffled to a distant ocean.

Tomas ducked into the pocket and turned. Elodie followed, and in the blue light her face looked carved, older than her years, younger than her eyes.

"My father climbed with your uncle," she said. "Before any of it. Before the disaster, before the report, before the money. Did you know that?"

"No." His voice came out rough. "I didn't know that."

"They were friends. There's a photograph in one of his route notebooks — the two of them at base camp, arms around each other's shoulders, laughing at something. My mother kept it on the shelf for years until she couldn't stand to look at it anymore." Elodie's breath fogged between them. "Your uncle wrote the report that said my father ignored weather warnings. That he pushed past safe margins. That the deaths were his fault. And the consortium paid for that report, and the government stamped it, and two countries blacklisted a dead man who couldn't defend himself, and his daughter inherited the blacklist like a surname."

"I know what my uncle did."

"Do you?" Something moved behind her eyes — not anger, or not only anger. "Because I've spent twenty years knowing it too, Tomas, and I still don't think I understand it. What I understand is this: you built your name on ground my father was buried under. Every permit you hold, every client who trusts you, every summit photo — it all stands on a lie that ruined us. And you came to me in that border-town shop with triple pay and a government stamp, and you asked me to climb the route where he died, and you let me believe it was about a ledger."

"It is about the ledger."

"It's about whether you can live with yourself," she said. "And you thought maybe I could carry that for you. The way my father carried your uncle's guilt up the mountain, and the mountain kept it."

The words landed like rockfall. Tomas stood in the blue cold and let them.

"You're right," he said.

She blinked. Whatever she'd braced for, it wasn't that.

"You're right," he said again, because the first time hadn't been enough. "I told myself it was restitution. I told myself my dying uncle deserved to have it found before the men who made it destroyed it. All true. None of it the truth. The truth is I needed you to be there when it was found. Because if you weren't — if I found that ledger alone, or with hired porters, or with anyone but you — then I'd have bought my clean conscience at a discount. Your presence is the price. That's why I came to you. Not your skills. *You.*"

The storm thundered. A section of the crevasse roof groaned overhead, shedding a curtain of crystals that drifted down between them, catching the gray light like slow sparks.

"My uncle called me in March," Tomas went on, quieter now. "From the hospice. He weighed nothing. He held my hand with fingers like paper and he said, *I put a good man under a mountain so rich men could keep their valleys, and I have been dying of it for twenty years, and I am asking you to die a little too — go up there and bring the truth down.* And I said yes because I loved him. And I hated him. Both at once, the same way you—" He stopped. Started again. "The same way you must hate me. Both things. At once."

"Yes," Elodie said. "Both things. At once."

"So where does that leave us?"

She looked at him for a long time. Above them the ice glowed and dimmed with the passing of clouds, and the wind rose and fell like breathing, like grief, like tide.

"It leaves us here," she said finally. "In a hole in a glacier, with a ledger in my pack that names the men who killed my father's name, and a crew below us who trusted me enough to follow me into this, and a storm that doesn't care which of us deserves to live. It leaves us with thirty hours and one job." She extended her hand — bare, cracked with cold, steady. "I don't forgive you. Forgiveness is a thing that happens after, and we're still in the middle. But I'll climb with you. I'll rope up with you. When the current falls and we carry this truth down to Meera and whoever comes next — you'll be beside me, carrying your share of the weight. That's not forgiveness. That's a truce. Take it or leave it."

Tomas took her hand. Her grip was cold and strong and did not soften, and neither did his.

"A truce," he said. "With honest weight."

"With honest weight," she agreed. "And Tomas — when we get down, you testify too. Everything your uncle told you. On the record, with your name attached. That's your share of the weight. Not the climbing. The *saying*."

"I know," he said. "I've known since the trailhead."

"Good." She released his hand and turned toward the crevasse chamber, then paused. "One more thing. In the icefall. When the ladder bridge went."

"You saved my life. I know."

"You don't know." She didn't turn around. "I didn't decide to anchor you. There was no decision. My hands moved before I thought, the way they would for Priya, for Dev, for anyone on my rope. Twenty years I told myself you were the enemy, and my body never believed it. That's what I can't forgive you for yet — that some part of me stopped hating you a long time ago, and I never gave it permission."

She dropped down into the main chamber, leaving Tomas alone in the blue pocket with the storm roaring overhead and something in his chest that had been frozen for twenty years beginning, very slowly, to move.

* * *

The thirty hours passed the way stone wears down — imperceptibly, continuously, absolutely.

They rationed the world into small ceremonies. Six hours, a pot of water. Twelve hours, a handful of cold rice and dried fruit passed hand to hand. Kessler slept and woke and slept; Brandt's cough deepened, and Priya melted extra water for him without being asked, and Elodie pretended not to see, which was its own kind of permission.

Dev took his watches without complaint and grew visibly taller each time he reported the anchors holding. Once, near what might have been midnight, Tomas found him at the crevasse mouth watching the spindrift pour past the opening like a waterfall running upward.

"How do you tell how bad it is?" Dev asked. "The sound. How do you read it?"

"Low and steady means wind, just wind — the mountain talking in its sleep," Tomas said. "Cracks and booms mean things breaking loose above. If the booms come closer together, the storm's winning. Tonight they're close together."

Dev nodded gravely, filing it away, and Tomas understood that he was watching knowledge pass down a chain that ran through Elodie's father and however many guides before him, and that he — Tomas Vey, celebrated, permitted, blacklisted-in-all-but-name-by-his-own-conscience — was for the first time a link in that chain rather than a man standing outside it.

Near hour twenty, Brandt's fever spiked. They had antibiotics in the med kit — two courses, meant for the crew. Elodie counted the crew twice, looked at the storm, and gave Brandt one course without hesitation.

"He testifies too," she said, pressing the pills into Kessler's hand. "Both of them. That's the price of the medicine."

Kessler met her eyes. "You drive a hard bargain, guide."

"I learned from hard people."

By hour twenty-five the booms had spread apart again. By hour twenty-eight the wind's pitch dropped from scream to moan. At hour thirty-one, Elodie unzipped the tent flap and gray daylight fell into the chamber like something holy.

The world outside had been remade. The crevasse city wore fresh drifts sculpted into waves and wings; the sky was rinsed clean and impossibly high; and the upper mountain gleamed white and silent, as if the storm had been a dream they'd all agreed to share.

Priya emerged, scanned the horizon with her binoculars, and lowered them slowly.

"Tomas," she said. "You need to see this."

He took the glasses. Far below, on the far side of the col, tiny against the snow, a line of figures moved — roped, deliberate, climbing. Counting flags of color through the lenses: five, six, seven.

"The lawyer's party," Kessler said quietly, from the tent mouth. "That'll be Rennick's advance team. They move fast when the money's scared."

Elodie took the binoculars, studied the line for a long moment, and handed them back. Her face, in the washed morning light, was calm in the way that ice is calm — beautiful, load-bearing, and very old.

"They're two days behind us," she said. "On a good day, we make the traverse in one." She turned to the crew, and Tomas saw the last of the storm leave her voice. "Break camp. Hot water first — real hot, all of it. Then we move. The truth doesn't wait for good weather, and neither do we."

Dev was already coiling rope. Priya was already striking the tents. And Tomas stood a moment longer at the crevasse mouth, looking down at the climbers crawling toward them through the snow — men paid to bury a ledger, and with it everyone who'd carried it — and felt the truce in his chest settle into something firmer than a word.

Whatever came over the col, they would meet it roped together.

He shouldered his pack and went down into the blue to help break camp.
