## 18. Descent Into Green

The snow ended the way a fever breaks — all at once, without dignity.

One hour Priya was kicking steps through slush that sucked at her boots like wet cement, and the next she rounded a shoulder of granite and the white simply stopped, a clean line drawn across the mountainside as if some enormous hand had ruled it there. Below the line: green. Terraced fields stitched into the valley walls, bright as new money after twenty days of grey and blue and white. Rain falling soft and steady on all of it, the monsoon's leading edge already settled in for the season.

She stood at the snowline with her ice axe still in her hand and felt something in her chest unclench so suddenly it hurt.

"Down," Elodie said behind her, and her voice had a strange texture to it, roughened. "While the light holds."

They descended out of winter in ninety minutes. That was the thing about mountains nobody told you until you'd lived them: the border between impossible and ordinary was always just a line of altitude, and you could walk across it. One step Priya was in a world where every decision was survival, where the ledger rode against Tomas's spine in its waterproof sleeve like a second heartbeat, where Dev's face was still marked with the memory of the ledge. The next step she was walking through wet grass, past a shrine draped in faded prayer flags, past a farmer who looked up from his yaks and nodded at them the way you nod at weather — present, unremarkable, no threat.

Her hands wouldn't stop shaking for another two hours. She let them.

* * *

They made the first village by dusk — a scatter of stone houses under walnut trees, smoke standing straight up out of chimneys because the rain was too gentle to bend it. An innkeeper named Dorje took them in without questions, fed them barley soup and fried dough, and charged them fairly, which after the mountain felt like a form of grace.

Priya sat by the stove with her boots off and her feet wrapped in someone's spare blanket, and watched Elodie do what Elodie always did: inventory. The crew, counted twice. The gear, hung to dry in ranks along the rafters. The window, checked. The road below the village, watched for a full minute before she allowed herself to sit.

"We lost them," Dev said. He was seventeen and had crossed a ledge that would have stopped most grown men, and right now he looked about nine, half-asleep over his bowl. "The ones following us. They were two days back when the storm wall hit."

"They're not lost," Elodie said.

"They're buried. You saw the campsite—"

"I saw a campsite under four meters of wet snow, yes." Elodie turned her cup slowly on the table, not drinking. "And I saw two of them walk into our camp out of that same storm and eat our food. Men like that don't stay buried, Dev. They get dug out, or they get replaced. Either way, they report."

Priya heard it and felt the warmth of the stove dim by a degree. "You think word's already moving."

"I think word moves faster than climbers. It always has." Elodie finally drank. "A runner left this village this morning before we arrived. There'll be a phone in the next town down. There's been a phone in every town down since before any of us were born. The mountain was the hard part because the mountain doesn't care what we're carrying." She set the cup down. "From here on, everyone cares very much."

Tomas was sitting apart, as he had been since the cairn, since the letter, since Elodie had read her father's words aloud with her voice breaking and then wiped her face and kept climbing. He'd carried the ledger ever since — insisted on it, actually, and Elodie had let him, which Priya understood was either trust or a test, and possibly both. Now he said, quietly, "The heir. Meera. How far?"

"Valley town's a day and a half on foot," Priya said. She'd done the research months ago, back when this was still a job with triple pay and no name attached. "Less if we can get transport. There's a jeep road from the market town."

"And the consortium?"

"The consortium has lawyers in three countries and money in more." Priya met his eyes. "If I were them, I'd have had people in that valley since the day your uncle died. Waiting isn't expensive. Waiting is cheap. It's the only thing they do better than erasing villages."

Nobody spoke for a moment. The fire popped. Outside, the rain came down soft and endless on a world that had no idea what was traveling through it.

Elodie stood, checked the window again, and said, "First light. We move fast and we move quiet, and when we reach the town, nobody says the word *ledger* above a whisper. We find Meera before anyone finds us. Agreed?"

Six tired voices said agreed. It didn't sound like much of an army. Priya thought it might have to be enough.

* * *

The jeep road was worse than walking.

That was her assessment somewhere around midday, bouncing in the bed of a borrowed truck with the crew's packs stacked around her and monsoon water running in brown rivers down ruts deep enough to drown a dog. The driver was a cousin of Dorje's cousin, paid in cash, curious about nothing, which was the correct amount of curiosity. Ahead of them the valley opened in long green steps — rice paddies mirroring the grey sky, terraces climbing the hillsides in curves so patient they looked geological. Children waved at the truck. A woman beat a carpet on a balcony and the sound carried clean across the water.

It was beautiful. It was also, Priya realized, watching the road ahead, completely exposed.

On the mountain, danger had a shape. You could see a serac before it fell. You could hear rockfall coming, count the seconds, choose your line. Here the danger was a man in a dry shirt making a phone call in an office she couldn't picture, in a language of contracts and contingencies, arranging things. Here the danger arrived looking reasonable.

At the junction town they stopped for fuel and Elodie came back from the tea shop with a changed face.

"There was a man here yesterday," she said, low, once they were rolling again. "Well-dressed. Not local. Asking after western trekkers coming off the high route. Showing a photograph."

"Of whom?" Tomas asked.

"You." She glanced back at him. "And me, separately. Two photographs."

Dev whistled softly. Ravi, the oldest of them, spat over the tailgate. "So they know the route. They know we survived the storm wall."

"They know someone told them we survived the storm wall," Elodie said. "Which means at least one of those men we fed is talking. Or both. Or neither, and their office simply assumed." She was quiet a moment, watching the terraces slide past. "I don't blame them, if it's the survivors. Cold men do cold arithmetic. But it means the clock's running faster than my feet."

Priya did the math herself and didn't like the answer. A day and a half on foot became a day by truck became — what? How long did it take a consortium's local fixer to drive in from the regional city? Four hours? Five? They might already be there. They might be sitting in the valley town's one decent guesthouse right now, drinking tea, waiting for a schoolteacher named Meera to come home from her classroom so they could begin the slow, polite work of buying her silence.

Or worse. Priya had spent enough years around Elodie to know how fortunes built on erased villages handled loose ends. Money preferred paperwork. But money kept other options warm.

"We should go straight through," Ravi said. "No stops."

"We stop once," Elodie said. "There's a school on the far side of the market town. If Meera teaches anywhere, she teaches there. We arrive at last bell, when the yard is full of parents and children and everyone can see everything." She looked back at Priya, and there was something in her face Priya hadn't seen in twenty years of working with her — not the flat competence, not the controlled loneliness, but purpose with heat under it. "If agents are going to approach her, I want witnesses. Dozens of them. Mothers with sharp eyes and grandfathers with long memories. Let them try to buy silence in front of a schoolyard."

"That's not subtle," Tomas said.

"No," Elodie agreed. "Subtle is their weapon. It's the only one they brought. So we leave it at home."

* * *

They camped that night in a mango grove above the river, and the sound of the water below them — swollen, brown, monsoon-loud — filled the dark like a held breath finally releasing.

Priya couldn't sleep. She got up past midnight and found Elodie awake, sitting against a trunk with her father's route notes open on her knees even though there was no light to read them by. She wasn't reading. She was holding them.

"You should sleep," Priya said, sitting down beside her.

"In a minute."

"Liar."

Elodie almost smiled. Down here in the green, with rain dripping through the mango leaves, she looked younger and older at once — the lines around her eyes deeper, but something behind them unlocked. "Twenty years I stayed off this range because coming back meant deciding what I'd do if I found out the truth. And now I know. My father didn't die because he made a mistake. He died because he saw what they were doing and wrote it down, and they needed him to be a cautionary tale instead of a witness." She folded the notes closed. "I built my whole life so shallow nothing could break it again. And it turns out the breaking was the point. It was always going to be the point."

"You could still turn around," Priya said. "Hand the ledger to some NGO in the capital, wash your hands, take the blacklist review and go."

"To whom? The men who framed him sit on the boards of three NGOs." Elodie shook her head. "No. This goes hand to hand. To the last person it belongs to. In front of people who'll remember." She paused. "That's why the schoolyard. That's why tomorrow matters. Justice that happens in an office gets filed. Justice that happens in front of a whole town gets *kept*."

Priya looked out through the trees at the river running loud and free toward the plains, carrying everything it carried, hiding nothing. "You know what's funny? Twenty days on the ice and I never once thought we'd fail. Down here I'm scared all the time."

"Good," Elodie said. "Scared means you understand the stakes. On the mountain, the worst case was we died. Down here, the worst case is we live, and they win anyway, quietly, forever." She tucked the notes inside her jacket, against her chest, where the ledger used to ride against Tomas's. "Sleep, Priya. Tomorrow we stop being climbers."

"What do we become instead?"

"Witnesses," Elodie said. "If we're fast enough."

* * *

Last bell.

They came into the market town at the golden hour, when the rain had thinned to a shimmer and the light came sideways under the clouds and turned every wet surface to hammered copper. The school sat at the top of the town's single street, behind a low wall, and the yard was exactly what Elodie had promised: chaos and noise, children pouring out in uniforms of blue and white, mothers calling names, an old man selling roasted corn from a cart whose smoke smelled like every good evening Priya had ever known.

And across the street, outside the tea house, leaning against a parked white SUV with government plates that didn't quite match its government plates: two men. Dry shirts. Clean shoes. Watching the school gate.

Priya felt Elodie go still beside her, the way she went still above a cornice — total attention, zero wasted motion.

"One of them is the man from the junction town photograph," Elodie murmured. "The other I don't know. They haven't moved yet. Which means they're waiting for the crowd to thin, or for her to leave alone."

"Then we don't give them either," Priya said.

They walked across the street together, all seven of them, mud to the knees, rope-worn hands, faces burned by twenty days of altitude wind — and Priya watched the two men register them, watch understanding move across the taller one's face like weather, watch him push off the SUV and start forward with a smile already assembling itself.

"Madam Montrose," he called pleasantly, in accented but fluent English. "What a coincidence. Might I have a moment—"

But Elodie was already through the school gate, already crossing the yard, already scanning the faces of the teachers at the door — and Priya saw the moment she found what she was looking for, because Elodie's whole body changed direction like a compass needle finding north.

An elderly woman in a plain sari stood at the top of the school steps, a roll book under her arm, watching the strangers enter her yard with the calm, appraising look of someone who had taught forty years of children and been fooled by none of them.

"Meera Devi?" Elodie called, and her voice carried across the whole yard, clear as a bell over water. Every head turned. Every mother, every grandfather, every child. "My name is Elodie Montrose. I've climbed down from the Karveth Range carrying something that belongs to your family. And these men—" she turned, and pointed, deliberate, at the two men frozen at the gate, "—are here to make sure you never receive it."

The yard went silent except for the rain, and the corn seller's coals ticking, and somewhere a child asking her mother, loudly, *what's happening, what's happening* —

The tall man's smile finally finished assembling itself. It didn't reach his eyes.

"Well," he said, stepping through the gate. "Since we're all being introduced."
