## 19. The Last Heir

The valley town was called Sarnali, and it smelled like rain on hot stone.

Elodie felt the change before she saw it — the way the air thickened below the snowline, carrying woodsmoke and wet earth and something green. After nineteen days above five thousand meters, where every breath had to be earned, the air here was almost embarrassing in its generosity. It poured into her lungs like a gift she'd forgotten how to accept.

They came down off the last ridge at dawn, six of them, mud to the knees, rope-worn, frost-nipped, alive. Priya walked beside her with Dev's arm over her shoulder — his ankle had turned on the scree two days ago and he'd refused to be carried, so they'd all slowed down together, which was its own kind of lesson. Tomas brought up the rear with the surveyor, Keshav, who moved like a man still apologizing for existing.

Below them, terraced fields stepped down the valley walls in green geometry. Rice paddies caught the early light like broken mirrors. And there, strung along the river where it bent around a shoulder of hill, Sarnali: tin roofs and tile roofs, a market square already filling, laundry lines, a schoolhouse with a bell tower.

"Word will have beaten us here," Priya said.

"I know."

"The consortium's people—"

"I know, Priya." Elodie adjusted her pack strap. The ledger rode against her spine inside a waterproof sack, wrapped in her father's route notes, and she had been aware of its weight for eleven days now, since the cairn, since the tin box, since the letter she still hadn't finished reading because some sentences you can only read once and then you have to walk with them. "Let them come. We're done hiding from things that travel faster than we do."

Dev looked up at the schoolhouse bell tower and said, quietly, "That's where she teaches? Meera?"

"That's what Keshav told us." Elodie glanced back at the former surveyor. He flinched under the look, then steadied himself. Twenty years of doing the consortium's paperwork had left him hollowed out, but the storm had burned away whatever was left of his loyalty to them. "You're sure about the name?"

"She was born Meera Devi Chettri," Keshav said. "Her grandfather was Dorje Chettri. Headman of Lumsa village." His voice caught on the name. Everyone's voice caught on the names now; they'd spent enough nights in tents reading the ledger aloud that the erased villages had become people to them — Lumsa, Thakoli, Renchek, and nine others, each one a list of families, fields, grazing rights, deeds. Each one a line item in someone's acquisition file. "After the flood, when the villages were gone, the government resettled survivors wherever there was space. She ended up teaching school here. She's seventy-three."

"You've met her," Elodie said. Not a question.

Keshav looked at the ground. "I processed her claim denial. Nineteen years ago. I was the one who wrote *insufficient documentation* on the form." He swallowed. "There was plenty of documentation. I was told what to write."

Nobody said anything. There was nothing to say that the mountain hadn't already said better.

They descended into the heat and the noise and the astonishing smell of frying dough, and Elodie understood, walking down the last stretch of trail into Sarnali, why her father had loved the low country even though he lived for the ice. Down here, everything grew. Up there, everything endured. A man could spend his whole life learning the difference.

* * *

Meera Devi Chettri's school let out at noon, and the children came pouring down the steps like water finding its level.

Elodie waited by the gate while the yard emptied, feeling absurdly conspicuous in her ruined expedition gear among mothers in bright cotton and men coming back from the fields. Priya stood at her shoulder. Tomas hung back near the wall, arms crossed, wearing the expression of a man attending his own trial.

The last child through the gate was a girl of maybe eight, who stopped, stared at Elodie's frost-scarred face, and said, "Are you lost?"

"No," Elodie said. "I'm looking for your teacher."

"Aunty Meera?" The girl considered this with great seriousness. "She's old but she knows everything."

"That's exactly who I'm looking for."

Meera came out drying her hands on a cloth, small and straight-backed, silver hair pinned tight, spectacles pushed up into her hair. She took in the three of them — the gear, the rope burns, the thousand-meter stare — without a flicker of surprise. Mountain towns knew what climbers looked like. They also knew what trouble looked like, and often the two were the same.

"You're not from the hydro company," she said. It wasn't a question either.

"No, ma'am."

"They've been here twice this month. Polite men in clean boots who want to talk about 'regional development opportunities.'" Her mouth did something dry. "I taught civics for forty years. I know what polite men in clean boots are selling." She looked at Elodie properly then, and something shifted behind the spectacles. "You've come down off Karveth. Off the high route."

"Yes."

"Then you've been where my grandfather's bones are." Not grief in her voice — something older and more settled than grief. "Come inside. Children gossip worse than magpies, and this is not a conversation for the yard."

* * *

The classroom smelled of chalk and ink and decades. Meera made tea on a small burner behind her desk and would not sit until they sat, and then she sat very straight on her chair, hands folded, and listened.

Elodie told it plainly, the way she'd learned to tell hard things: the crossing, the icefall, the cairn, the ledger. She left out nothing and dramatized nothing. When she got to the part about the erased villages, Meera closed her eyes, and her lips moved — Elodie realized she was reciting names, silently, the way you recite a prayer you've said ten thousand times.

When Elodie finished, the room was quiet except for the burner hissing.

"My grandfather kept records too," Meera said at last. "Copies. He sent them down the valley with a cousin before the soldiers came. The cousin died on the road. Or sold them. We never knew." She opened her eyes. "For fifty years I have written letters. To district offices. To courts. To newspapers. I have a box of rejections taller than my students." She smiled, and it was a terrible, magnificent smile. "So you'll forgive me if I don't weep over promises made in a schoolroom. What is it you actually have?"

Elodie unslung her pack.

And that was when Priya, who'd been watching the window, said quietly: "Elodie. Market square. Two men just came out of the tea house. Clean boots."

Of course. Word traveled faster than climbers; Keshav had warned them, and Keshav had been right. The consortium's agents had reached Sarnali before them after all — or had never left. Elodie looked at the window, at the two figures crossing the yard beyond the school fence with the unhurried confidence of men who had bought governments before breakfast, and she felt something settle in her chest that was not fear.

It was arithmetic. Twenty years ago, men like these had written her father out of history with a pen. Today there were witnesses in that market — dozens of them, market day, the whole town within earshot. Clean boots were powerful in offices. In a market square full of people who remembered Lumsa, they were just boots.

"Ma'am," Elodie said, standing, "how would you feel about taking a walk?"

Meera was already rising, pinning her hair, picking up her cloth bag as though she'd been waiting fifty years for precisely this errand. "I take this walk every day," she said. "Today it will simply matter."

* * *

Market day in Sarnali was a river, and rivers do not make appointments.

Vegetable stalls and spice sacks and a man selling bolts of cloth and children darting between legs, and above it all the awnings throwing striped shade against the afternoon glare. Elodie walked into it with Meera on her arm and Priya and Tomas flanking, and she felt the crowd notice them the way water notices a stone — a ripple, a turning of heads, the particular hush that follows people who look like they've come down off a mountain that kills people.

The two clean-booted men found them within four minutes. Of course they did. One was young and sleek, the other older, heavier, with the patient face of a professional. The older one stepped into their path with a smile calibrated to the millimeter.

"Madam Chettri." A slight bow. "What an honor. My name is Rathore. We've been trying to reach you — the Meridian Development Group has long admired your service to this community. We'd hoped to discuss a settlement regarding certain historical claims. Generous terms. An education fund in your name, perhaps. A building. Your students would—"

"Mr. Rathore." Meera's voice carried the way a schoolteacher's voice carries — not loud, but structured to fill a room and tolerate no interruptions. Half the market was already listening. "In forty years, how many of my letters did your group answer?"

Rathore's smile held. Barely. "Madam, I'm not familiar with—"

"Nineteen. You answered none. But today, suddenly, there is a fund. A building." She tilted her head. "That is not generosity, Mr. Rathore. That is fear with money in its hand."

A murmur went through the crowd. Elodie watched it move — stall holders leaning in, the cloth seller turning down his radio, mothers lifting children onto their hips so they could see. This was the thing no office ever understood: in a place like Sarnali, a public conversation belonged to everyone in it.

Rathore's eyes slid to Elodie. Took in the gear, the scars, the pack. "And you are?"

"The guide who climbed to the cairn," Elodie said. "The one whose father your people erased from the disaster report twenty years ago." She said it loudly. Clearly. For the crowd, not for him. "My name is Elodie Montrose. Maybe you've read the blacklist."

Something flickered across his face — recognition, and behind it, recalculation. The blacklist had made her infamous in exactly the circles that employed men like Rathore. Infamy, she was discovering, cut both ways.

"Ms. Montrose. Whatever you believe you found on that mountain—"

"It's not what I believe." Elodie swung the pack off her shoulder. Her hands were steady. Twenty years of measuring life in miles had taught her this much: you do the next hundred meters, and then the next. This was just another hundred meters, vertical instead of horizontal. "It's what's documented. Sealed in a tin box at a cairn above the icefall until eleven days ago. Land surveys. Deeds. Purchase orders for villages that officially never existed. Signatures — including, Mr. Rathore, I'd guess, some belonging to people who sign your paychecks."

"Those documents are stolen property," Rathore said, and his voice dropped its warmth all at once, showing the metal underneath. "Meridian will pursue legal action—"

"Against whom?" Priya asked pleasantly, from the flank. "The dead headman's granddaughter? In front of all these witnesses?" She gestured, lazy and wide, at the market. "Go ahead. File the paperwork."

The crowd had gone quiet in the specific way crowds go quiet when they've decided which side they're on. The cloth seller folded his arms. The spice woman set down her ladle. An old man near the front — Elodie saw him clearly, weathered face, Lumsa features, the same bones Meera carried — spat deliberately into the dust.

Rathore felt it. Professionals always do. His younger colleague had gone pale. And Elodie understood that they had miscalculated at the level of terrain: they thought this was an office, where documents could be subpoenaed and witnesses could be bought. But this was a market, and markets run on memory, and memory here was fifty years deep.

So she did the thing she had decided to do in the schoolroom, the thing she'd rehearsed on the traverse with her heart hammering harder than it had on any ledge.

She opened the pack, took out the ledger wrapped in her father's route notes, and put it in Meera Devi Chettri's hands.

Not handed aside. Not slipped discreetly. She held it out in full view of the market, and Meera received it in both hands, the way you receive something sacred, and Elodie said, in a voice pitched to reach the last row of stalls:

"This belongs to the people of Lumsa, Thakoli, Renchek, and every village your maps deleted. It names the living heirs. It proves the claims. It's yours, ma'am — and whatever happens next happens in daylight, with witnesses, or it doesn't happen at all."

Meera weighed it. Looked up. And then, magnificently, she turned to the crowd and raised the ledger overhead like a schoolteacher holding up the correct answer.

"Do any of you remember Lumsa?" she called out.

The silence lasted three seconds. Then the old man who had spat spoke first — "My mother was born there" — and after him came others, voices overlapping, names being called, someone starting toward the front, and the market river closed around them all.

Rathore stood in the middle of it, smiling his millimeter smile at nobody, and Elodie watched him understand, with the slow dread of a man watching a cornice crack, that there was nothing left to buy. You cannot purchase a crowd that has already remembered.

He withdrew the way professionals withdraw — smoothly, backing a step, murmuring something about *this isn't concluded* — and Elodie let him go. Corpses made martyrs of employers; witnesses made corpses of lies. Let him report back. Let them send whoever they liked next.

* * *

Evening found them on the schoolhouse porch, the whole crew, eating rice off banana leaves while Meera held court inside with a growing delegation of elders and the ledger open under lamplight, page by page, name by name, fifty years of erasure being read back into existence one line at a time.

Tomas sat on the steps beside Elodie, shoulders finally down from around his ears.

"You could have given it to her privately," he said. "Safer."

"Safer for whom?"

He huffed something that was almost a laugh. "Fair." He was quiet a moment. "My uncle used to say the mountains keep honest books. Every gram weighs, every lie costs." He nodded toward the lit window, the silhouettes bent over the pages. "Took me thirty years to learn he meant the low country too."

Inside, Meera's voice rose suddenly — sharp, triumphant — and the elders' murmur broke into something louder. Priya leaned out the door. "Elodie. You need to hear this. There's a clause in the seizure orders — a restitution provision, government-issued, never enforced. If the original heirs file formally, with documented lineage—" she grinned, feral, "—the land titles revert. Legally. All of them."

Meera appeared in the doorway, spectacles fogged, holding a single page like a live coal.

"Child," she said to Elodie, and her eyes were burning, "there is a waterfall below my grandfather's village. Where the current falls past the shrine. My grandmother took the deeds there the night before the soldiers came, to swear an oath on them." She gripped the page. "Tomorrow, I file the claim. And I would like the woman who carried the truth down off the mountain to stand beside me when I do."

Elodie looked past her, through the darkening valley, to where the river ran somewhere below the terraces, falling toward the lowlands — falling, the whole way down, the way water does, the way truth apparently did too, given enough slope and enough time.

"Ma'am," she said, "I wouldn't miss it."

But sleep didn't come easily that night, and near midnight, footsteps on the porch stairs — Priya, lantern in hand, face grim.

"Elodie. The tea house boy says Rathore didn't leave town. He's sent a rider east, fast, hours ago." She paused. "And Keshav's gone missing from the room we gave him."

Elodie sat up slowly, the old cold settling along her spine.

Twenty years of reading weather. She knew a building storm when she felt one.
