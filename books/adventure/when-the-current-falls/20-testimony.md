## 20. Testimony

The hearing room in the valley town had once been a granary, and it still smelled faintly of grain and dust and old decisions. Forty folding chairs, a table scarred by decades of tea rings, a ceiling fan turning with the lazy conviction of a thing that had never once been in a hurry. Tomas Vey sat in the third row and watched the former surveyor walk to the front of the room on legs that shook, and thought: *this is what courage looks like when it's too tired to perform.*

The surveyor's name was Anselm Roth. He was sixty-one years old, and for twenty years he had been a small gray man in a small gray office in a city none of them could afford to live in, filing reports that made mountains disappear on paper. Now he stood at the front of the granary in a wrinkled shirt, holding a sheaf of documents like a man holding a live wire, and he began to speak.

"I was the assistant surveyor attached to the Karveth assessment team," he said. "Nineteen years before the disaster. My signature appears on forty-one land reclassification documents. I want the record to show that I signed them."

A murmur went through the chairs. At the back, two of the consortium's local men — the ones who hadn't fled when word came down from the coast that the ledger was public — shifted like men sitting on nails.

Tomas watched Elodie instead. She sat near the window in the second row, arms crossed, wearing the same expression she wore reading weather: patient, measuring, waiting for the sky to tell her the truth. She had not looked at him all morning. He deserved that. He was learning to sit inside what he deserved.

Roth laid the documents on the table one at a time, each with a small precise click, like stones being placed.

"The reclassifications moved village common land into 'uninhabited hazard zones' — zones that were then leased, at nominal cost, to the Karveth Development Consortium. The villages weren't abandoned after the disaster. They were emptied *before* it. The disaster report — the official one — was drafted in our offices six weeks before the mountain failed. We knew where the slide would run. We'd mapped it. The villages were in the runout. That was not an obstacle." His voice cracked, steadied. "That was the plan."

At the front of the room, an elderly woman in a faded blue sari sat very straight with the ledger open on her knees. Meera. The last heir. She did not weep and she did not speak; she simply turned pages, following Roth's testimony through the ledger like a woman following a route she'd memorized long ago and never believed she'd walk.

Tomas understood then why Elodie had handed her the book in the market instead of a lawyer's office. A courtroom would have taken this truth and processed it, delayed it, buried it under procedure until it was safe to forget. Meera had taken it the way you take a rope — hand to hand, weight already on it.

* * *

By afternoon, the story had outrun the building.

Priya came in from the street with her phone lit up like a signal fire and read the headlines aloud while the crew ate rice off their knees in the granary's side room. The regional paper had gotten copies of the photographs from the cairn — the villages before, the villages after, the survey maps with the runout zones drawn in red ink twenty years before the red became real. A national outlet had picked it up by noon. By evening there was a government statement, carefully worded, announcing a "formal review" of the disaster-era records and, buried in the fourth paragraph, a review of the professional blacklist against one *Aldric Montrose*, guide, deceased.

Tomas read that paragraph four times.

"They're going to clear his name," Priya said quietly, watching him. "Posthumously."

"They're going to *consider* clearing his name," Tomas said. "Governments consider things the way glaciers move. Slowly, and only downhill."

Dev laughed — a short, surprised bark — and then covered his mouth, glancing at Elodie. But Elodie didn't look offended. Something in her face had loosened, some load-bearing tension finally set down, and she said, "He would have liked that joke," and the whole room exhaled.

It was the first time Tomas had heard her speak of her father without flinching. He catalogued it the way he catalogued weather changes: significant, possibly temporary, do not build on it yet.

* * *

The consortium's collapse took three days and looked, from the inside, like watching a snowpack fail.

First the local partners withdrew — the valley merchants and land brokers who had brokered the leases, issuing statements so fast they tripped over each other, each claiming they had always suspected, always objected, had merely been *waiting for evidence*. Then the international financiers froze the accounts pending investigation. Then the two remaining board members resigned within hours of each other, and the company's website went down, and when it came back up it was a single page: a law firm's contact information and nothing else.

The armed men who had followed them up the mountain simply evaporated. Their employer had stopped paying, and mercenaries are honest in exactly one way.

But lawyers, Tomas knew, were another species entirely. On the fourth day, a letter arrived at the guesthouse where the crew was staying — heavy paper, real ink, the kind of stationery that costs more per sheet than Dev made in a day. It was addressed to Elodie Montrose, care of the crew, and it was copied to Tomas Vey, and its message was simple:

*Certain contractual irregularities surround the recent expedition. The crossing of the Karveth Range without permit constitutes a violation of national access regulations. Parties found responsible may face prosecution, fines, and permanent revocation of guiding credentials. We would welcome the opportunity to discuss which parties those might be.*

They read it aloud in the side room, all of them together, because that was how this crew did things now — no more secrets carried alone up mountains.

"They can't touch me," Priya said. "I'm licensed in three jurisdictions and none of them recognize each other's paperwork. It's beautiful, actually."

"They can't prove anything about Dev or the porters," Ravi added. "Hired labor, no contracts signed on national soil."

"They can touch me," Elodie said. Her voice was flat, practical. "Blacklisted twice. Hired for a forbidden crossing. If they want a criminal, I'm the easiest one to build."

"No," Tomas said.

Everyone looked at him. He hadn't meant to say it out loud. But now that it was out, he found he had no interest in taking it back.

"It was my permit," he said. "My money. My uncle's confession that started this. Every document, every arrangement, every decision to climb — mine. Elodie refused the job. You all remember she refused it. Priya accepted it, but the contract was with me, and the contract named me as expedition principal. Let them prosecute the principal."

"Tomas—" Elodie started.

"You've been paying for your father's name for twenty years," he said. "You paid in miles. I've watched you pay. I'm the reason you got blacklisted in the first place — my family's company wrote half the disaster report, my uncle carried the ledger off that mountain and sat on it while your father rotted in disgrace. If someone has to be guilty here, it was never going to be you. I've just been slow about admitting it."

The room was very quiet. Outside, monsoon rain worked the tin roof like fingers working a knot.

Elodie stood. She crossed the room, took the lawyer's letter out of his hands, and read it again, slowly, the way she read terrain.

"If you take sole blame," she said, "they'll take your license. Your company. Everything you have left."

"Yes."

"And you'll do it anyway."

"Yes."

She looked at him for a long moment, and Tomas braced himself for the fury — twenty years of it, earned, banked, ready. Instead she folded the letter in half, set it on the table, and said, "Then we do it properly. Nobody takes blame alone. Not on my rope team."

"That's not how prosecutions work."

"I don't care how prosecutions work. I care how crews work." She turned to the others. "We all signed on. We all climbed. If there's a price, we split it the way we split loads. That's the whole point of everything we just did — no more invisible people carrying weight for other people's profit."

Priya grinned slowly. "She's going to be insufferable now that she's decided to have principles publicly."

"She's always been insufferable," Ravi said. "Now it's just documented."

And Dev — Dev who had frozen on a ledge above a four-hundred-meter drop five days ago and walked across anyway — raised his teacup and said, "To the expedition principal," and Tomas felt something in his chest crack open that wasn't pain, or wasn't only pain.

* * *

The formal response went out the next morning, drafted at the guesthouse table over breakfast, with Meera consulting and Roth — who had stayed in town, unable to leave, unable to stop testifying — adding clauses in his careful surveyor's hand.

They acknowledged the crossing. They acknowledged it was unauthorized. And then they stated, plainly, that the authorization had been impossible to obtain, because the authority that controlled access to the Karveth Range was the same authority whose officials had profited from the erased villages, and that a permit system built by the guilty is not a legal framework but a lock on a door the guilty installed. They submitted the full ledger, the photographs, Roth's testimony, and the crew's route log as a single package, and they requested — demanded — that any review of the crossing be conducted alongside the review of the disaster, in public, with the erased villages' descendants present.

Meera read the final draft twice and said, "This will make them furious."

"It's supposed to," Elodie said.

"They may take everything from you anyway. Institutions defend themselves. It's what they're for."

"Then they'll take it in daylight," Elodie said, "in front of witnesses, on the record. I spent twenty years learning that the difference between a disaster and an accident is whether anyone writes down the truth. So we write it down."

Tomas signed his name last, beneath hers, and found that his hand did not shake.

That night he sat on the guesthouse steps while the rain came down and the valley smelled of wet earth and woodsmoke, and he thought about his uncle — dying in a hospital bed, pressing a photograph into Tomas's palm, saying *find it before they do, boy, find it and give it back.* He had thought the job was retrieval. Carry the ledger off the mountain, deliver it, done. He had not understood that giving something back means standing in front of everyone and saying *this is what my family took,* and staying there while they look at you.

His uncle had never managed it. Had carried the guilt for thirty years and died with it still in his hands, unspent.

Tomas was not going to die with it in his hands.

From inside the house came laughter — Priya arguing with Ravi about route grades, Dev practicing knots, Meera telling some story that had the whole room listening. And underneath it, Elodie's voice, low and steady, reading something aloud. Her father's route notes, maybe. Or the letter from the cairn. He couldn't tell, and he found he didn't need to.

In the morning, a motorcycle came up the valley road through the rain, mud to the fenders, and the rider — a government courier, soaked through, official envelope in a waterproof sleeve — asked for Elodie Montrose by name.

The envelope held a single page. Official letterhead. The review board's seal.

*Aldric Montrose: posthumous reinstatement approved, pending final ratification.*

And below it, a second paragraph, addressed not to the dead man but to the living one standing in the doorway behind him:

*Ms. Montrose — the board further requests your presence at the ratification session, and extends an invitation regarding the review of your own standing. Please present yourself in the capital within fourteen days.*

Elodie read it standing in the rain, letting the water spot the paper, and Tomas watched her face do something he had never seen it do in twenty years of knowing her from across ridgelines and rivalries and funerals.

She laughed.

Short, disbelieving, cracked right down the middle — the laugh of a woman who had planned her whole life around a door being locked and had just heard the bolt slide back.

"Fourteen days," she said. "They want me in the capital in fourteen days." She looked at the letter, then at the mountains hidden somewhere above the rain clouds, then at Tomas. "I haven't been past that checkpoint since I was nineteen years old."

"Do you want to go?" he asked.

She was quiet for a moment. Rain ran off the eaves between them like a curtain, and beyond it the whole green valley fell away toward the waterfall, toward the shrine, toward the current that fell and fell and never once stopped falling.

"There's one place I have to stop first," she said. "Before any capital. Before any board." She folded the letter carefully, dried it against her shirt, and looked east, up-valley, where the river came down out of the high country. "There's a waterfall where his notes end. And I've been carrying something of his for twenty years that needs to go home."

Tomas nodded slowly. "When do we leave?"

"Tomorrow," Elodie said, and pocketed the letter, and turned back toward the light and the noise of the house where her crew waited. "Tomorrow, at first light. Before the weather turns again."

Behind them, up in the hidden heights, the glacier fed the river, and the river ran down through everything — through the terraces, through the town, through the ruins and the rebuilt walls — carrying, the way water always does, every stone it had ever moved, toward the fall.
