## 21. When the Current Falls

The route notes ended at a bend in the river, three days below the snowline, where the water dropped out of a notch in the granite and fell a hundred meters into a pool the color of bottle glass.

My father had written it in his cramped, slanted hand, the ink faded to the brown of old tea: *Follow the sound. When the current falls, you're there. Tell them I said the valley keeps its promises.*

I had read that line a dozen times since the cairn. I read it again now, standing on the trail with the letter folded soft as cloth in my pocket, and I still didn't know what he'd meant. The valley keeps its promises. He'd been a man who believed mountains kept theirs, and I had spent twenty years believing they didn't.

"You're smiling," Priya said beside me.

"I'm not."

"You are. It's terrifying. Do it again."

Below us, through a screen of wet fern, the falls made a sound like the mountain clearing its throat. Not the roar of the icefall, not the white noise of the storm wall we'd sheltered through — something older and more patient. Water that had been falling since before my father was born and would fall long after everyone who remembered him was gone.

The crew came down the trail behind us in loose order. Dev carried the small brass urn wrapped in his spare shirt, because I had asked him to and because he had understood without being told that I could not carry it the last kilometer. My hands had been steady on the traverse, steady on the icefall, steady through thirty hours in a crevasse listening to the mountain try to shrug us off. They would not have been steady holding what was left of him.

Tomas walked at the rear, which was where he'd been walking for three days. Not hiding. Keeping watch. The pursuers' party had broken apart at the col — the lawyer had turned back with his guides, the muscle had scattered into the rain like men released from a spell — but Tomas still checked the back trail every hour, out of habit or penance or both.

Meera was waiting for us at the falls.

She had walked up from the valley town that morning with two of her students carrying her pack, an old woman in a grey shawl moving at the pace of someone who had decided exactly how long she intended to live and was in no hurry to shorten it. When she saw us she didn't wave. She stood beside the shrine — the rebuilt one, stone on stone, new wood on the prayer frames, the whole small structure bright against the wet rock — and she waited the way you wait for weather you've been promised.

"You came," she said, when I reached her.

"You asked."

"I asked a lot of people over a lot of years." She looked at the falls, then at me. "Most of them sent lawyers instead. Lawyers are cheaper than climbing."

"We had a guide who knew the route," Dev said, and grinned at me, and I let him.

* * *

We built the ceremony out of almost nothing, the way you build a camp in bad weather.

Meera lit butter lamps at the shrine, and her students hung new prayer flags, and the wind off the falls took them immediately, snapping them out like sails. The crew stood in a half-circle with their hats off, water beading in their hair, and nobody spoke for a while because nobody needed to. Twenty years of silence had already been spoken. This was just the place where it landed.

I took the urn from Dev.

It was lighter than I expected. It had been lighter every time I'd picked it up on the descent, as if the mountain had already taken most of him and was only returning what it could spare. I had scattered a pinch of him at the cairn, because the cairn was his too — *where the truth sleeps*, he'd written, and he'd been right, and the truth had slept there twenty years waiting for someone with the courage to climb for it, and I had finally been that someone, and it had nearly killed all of us, and it had been worth every meter.

But the rest of him belonged here. At the falls. Where his route notes ended.

I walked to the lip of the pool. The spray came off the falling water in a cold fine veil, and the noise was enormous up close, and underneath the noise there was a rhythm, a pulse, the same beat over and over like a heart that had never once considered stopping.

"I don't have anything prepared," I said, and my voice cracked on the second word, and I let it. "He wouldn't have wanted a speech. He used to say the mountain doesn't listen, it just remembers."

Priya made a sound that was almost a laugh.

"So I'm going to read his letter. All of it. Out loud. Because he wrote it for whoever found the cairn, and it turns out the whoever was me, and I've been reading it alone in my tent like a coward, and I'm done being a coward in front of my own crew."

I unfolded the pages. My hands shook. I let them shake.

*If you're reading this, then you climbed for it, and I'm glad, and I'm sorry, because I know what it cost. I knew when I wrote it. I knew I wasn't coming down, and I hid the truth anyway, because the truth was worth more than my life and less than my daughter's, and I chose wrong. I chose the mountain over going home. If you're Elodie — if my stubborn girl climbed my route against every sane instinct — then I hope you found it in yourself to forgive me, and I hope you found people to climb with, because a rope team is the only family that never lies to you about who you are.*

I stopped. The falls went on falling.

The crew stood very still. Dev had his eyes closed. Priya was crying without any embarrassment at all, tears cutting clean lines through the spray on her face, and I thought: twenty years I hid from these people. Twenty years I kept my life shallow so it couldn't break, and here was the break, and here they all were, holding the edges.

*The men who made the ledger will tell you the disaster was the mountain's fault,* the letter went on. *The mountain doesn't take sides. It only keeps what's given to it. I gave it the truth. Go and take it back.*

I read the rest — the names of the villages, the survey numbers, the sentence he'd underlined twice: *Tell Meera's family the land remembers them even if the maps don't.* — and when I finished I stood at the lip of the pool and opened the urn and let the wind off the falls take him.

Ash is heavier than you think. It didn't blow away. It went out and down and into the falling water in a grey ribbon, and the current carried it into the pool, and the pool gave it to the river, and the river gave it to the valley — the valley that keeps its promises — and I understood the line at last. He hadn't been talking about the mountain. He'd been talking about the people. The valley had kept his truth for twenty years in stone foundations and an old shepherd's memory and a schoolteacher's stubbornness, and it had kept it because the living had refused to let the dead be erased.

I sat down on a wet rock and I cried.

Not the private kind, not the kind I'd done in tents and outhouses and once, memorably, in a customs office in a country that had banned me. The full kind. The kind with sound. Twenty years of frozen grief moving all at once, like an icefall letting go, and my crew — my beautiful, debt-ridden, loyal crew — sat down around me on the wet stones and let me do it, and nobody said *there, there*, because they were climbers and they knew that sometimes the only thing to do with an avalanche is stand still and let it pass over you.

Dev put his hand on my shoulder. Tomas, standing apart at the edge of the circle, took off his hat and held it against his chest, and I looked at him through the blur and he mouthed something I couldn't hear over the water, and I decided it didn't matter. I'd hear it later. There was going to be a later. That was the whole point of everything.

* * *

Meera filed the claim the next morning, and she filed it the way she did everything — in public, at full volume, with witnesses.

The valley town's market was already awake when we came down: tarps steaming in the early sun, women sorting chilies, a boy leading a recalcitrant goat between the stalls. Meera had set up a table in front of the tea house — an actual table, with a cloth, and on it the ledger, open, and beside it the photographs from the tin box, the ones my father had taken of the seized villages, laid out in a row like evidence at a trial, which is exactly what they were.

She'd invited everyone. That was the point. She'd sent word down-valley for three days, and word travels faster than climbers, and faster than lawyers too.

The consortium's agents were already there when we arrived. Two of them, in clean shirts that had no business being clean in a monsoon valley, standing at the edge of the crowd with the particular stillness of men who have been told to be polite and are finding it expensive. The taller one had a document folder and a smile he'd practiced in a mirror.

"Madam," he said to Meera, "if we could discuss this privately—"

"Everything I do is public," Meera said. "That's the whole idea. You people taught me that. You did your work in the dark, so I do mine in the light." She raised her voice to carry across the market. "You all know what happened here. Your grandparents knew. The maps say the village never existed. This book says otherwise, and the book has names, and the names have heirs, and I am one of them."

The taller agent stepped forward. "The consortium is prepared to offer a settlement. Substantial. In exchange for the return of stolen property and a mutual—"

"Stolen property." Priya said it from beside me, flat and dangerous, and half the market turned to look at her. "You want to call a ledger of land seizures *stolen property*. That's the bravest sentence I've heard on this whole mountain."

"The documents were removed without authorization—"

"They were removed from a cairn on a mountain," I said, and stepped forward, and the crowd made room for me the way crowds do when they've already decided whose side they're on. "My father carried them up there and left them for whoever had the courage to climb for them. Nobody stole anything. Somebody finally came to collect."

And that was when the international observers arrived.

Meera had written to them too — a human rights office, a land-rights NGO, a journalist from the coast who'd been chasing the consortium for a decade and had never had the documents to land a blow. They came up the market street in a loose knot, rain-jackets and cameras and one elderly man with a walking stick, and the agents saw them and I watched the fight go out of them the way air goes out of a tent. Not all at once. Slowly, from the corners. They'd been sent to buy one old woman's silence in private. They were standing in a market with forty witnesses, three cameras, and a ledger that named their employers.

The taller agent closed his folder.

"You understand," he said, quieter now, to Meera, "that this will be a long process."

"I'm seventy-three," Meera said. "I've been waiting since I was fifty-three. I'm very good at long." She signed the claim form on the table — the observers had brought one, prepared, notarized, the journalist filming over her shoulder — and when she finished she looked up at the agents with an expression I recognized, because I'd worn it for twenty years in border towns and permit offices. The expression of someone who has been told *no* by every institution that exists and has decided, finally and completely, to stop asking.

"Tell your principals," she said, "that the mountain kept the truth for me. They should have known better than to bury anything on a mountain. Mountains give things back."

The agents left. Nobody followed them. There was no need. The market went back to its noise — chilies, goats, tea, the ordinary machinery of a morning — and somewhere in the middle of it I realized my hands were steady, completely steady, for the first time in twenty years.

* * *

That evening the crew sat on the tea house roof and watched the light go off the peaks we'd crossed.

Dev was asleep against his pack, worn out and grinning even in sleep, a junior guide now whether the paperwork said so or not. Priya was writing in the logbook — the real logbook, the one she kept for the record — and I could see her choosing her words carefully, because this entry would be read by permit offices and, if Meera's claim went the way it looked like it was going, by people who decided things like blacklists.

Tomas sat beside me at the roof's edge with a mug of tea he wasn't drinking.

"The surveyor's testimony lands next week," he said. "And my statement. I told them everything — the permit, the hire, all of it. My name goes first on the violations. Yours shouldn't be on it at all."

"It'll be on it. I climbed the route."

"You climbed it because I lied to get you there."

"I climbed it," I said, "because my father asked me to, twenty years before you were born into this story. You're a footnote, Tomas. A well-meaning footnote." I took a sip of tea. "But you're a footnote who carried an urn for a stranger and held the rope when the ladder went. So when they ask me, I'll say the crew bears it together or not at all. That's how rope teams work."

He looked at the peaks. The last light was coming off the Karveth, and the glacier below the icefall was catching it, burning orange, and for a moment the whole route we'd survived was lit up like a signature across the rock.

"Your father's letter," he said. "The part about rope teams being the only family that never lies. Do you believe that?"

"I believe a rope team is the only family that *can't* lie," I said. "Whether it does is up to the people on it." I looked at him. "Ask me again in a year."

He smiled — a real one, the first I'd ever seen on him that wasn't armor — and below us in the dark the river ran on toward the falls, and the falls went on falling, patient as a promise, and the valley held all of it: the ash and the ledger and the old woman's claim and the sleeping boy and the light going off the ice.

Priya looked up from the logbook. "Last entry of the crossing," she said. "Give me one line. Your words, for the record."

I thought about it. Twenty years of miles, and here at the end of them, one line.

"Write: *The current falls. We came down. Everyone came down.*"

She wrote it. And in the valley below, in a grey shawl, by lamplight, an old schoolteacher was writing too — the first page of a claim that would take years and win, because the land remembered, and now so did everyone else.
