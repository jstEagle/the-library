## 22. Years, Not Miles

The sign went up on a Tuesday, because Elodie insisted that anything worth doing should be done on an ordinary day.

PRIYA OKONKWO & E. MONTROSE — MOUNTAIN GUIDES, LICENSED. TRAINING AND EXPEDITIONS. BELOW THE SIGN, smaller, hand-painted by Dev in letters that leaned slightly uphill: *We measure in years now.*

Priya had argued against the tagline for three weeks. It was too sentimental, she said. Clients would think it was a yoga retreat. Elodie had stood in the doorway of the converted stable that served as their office, arms crossed, and said, "It stays," and that was the end of the argument, because when Elodie Montrose said *it stays* in that voice — the one she'd learned somewhere between the icefall and the storm wall, the voice of a woman who had stopped asking permission from her own grief — you let it stay.

One year. Priya caught herself counting it constantly, the way you'd count stitches in a rope: one year since the col, since Meera's weathered hands closing around the ledger in front of forty witnesses at the market; since the restitution claim went in with international observers standing shoulder to shoulder in the dust; since they'd walked down out of the Karveth Range carrying something heavier than any cargo and lighter than anything Priya had ever held.

One year since the waterfall, where Elodie had read her father's letter aloud with her voice breaking on every third word, and none of them had looked away, because looking away would have been the old world's habit, and they were done with the old world.

Now the morning light came through the stable's east window and lay across the gear wall like a blessing nobody had asked for. Ropes coiled by diameter. Ice tools racked by length. The training yard beyond, where six local kids were already hauling on fixed lines under Dev's supervision, his voice carrying across the gravel in a cadence Priya recognized with a private smile — patient, precise, occasionally exasperated. He'd stolen it wholesale from Elodie. She pretended not to notice. She noticed everything.

"Your nine o'clock cancelled," Dev said, ducking into the office, cheeks flushed from the cold. "Family thing. Harvest."

"Good," Priya said. "Tell them to take two days. The mountain will still be there."

"That's what I said." He grinned, dropped onto the bench by the door, and started re-lacing a boot with the concentration of a surgeon. "Elodie's been up since four."

"Since four?"

"Went up to the shrine. Came back down before light." He glanced up. "Is today—"

"It is."

He nodded slowly, and Priya watched him decide not to say anything else, which was its own kind of graduation. A year ago he'd have asked ten questions. Now he just tied his laces and said, "I'll keep the kids on the low walls till noon," and went back out into the sun.

* * *

Priya found Elodie at the kitchen table behind the office, a pot of tea going cold between her hands, an envelope lying flat in front of her like something that might bite.

"Tomas?" Priya asked.

"Tomas." Elodie didn't look up. "Came two days ago. I've read it twice."

"And?"

"And I'm deciding whether to read it a third time or burn it."

"You'll do neither. You'll keep it." Priya sat down across from her, uninvited, which after eleven years of rope teams and shared tents was less rudeness than grammar. "You keep everything now. It's disgusting. You have a drawer."

"I have a *file*."

"You have a drawer full of letters and newspaper clippings and the first client check we ever cashed, and last month I saw you smoothing out a bus ticket from the valley town like it was a relic." Priya poured herself tea from the cold pot anyway. "Twenty years ago you'd have burned that bus ticket and the town with it."

Elodie almost smiled. Almost. Her face did this thing now, this small thawing at the corners, that Priya had spent a year learning to read the way she once read cloud formations over the Karveth — carefully, hopefully, knowing how fast it could change.

"He writes differently than he talks," Elodie said. "On the mountain he was all momentum. Get to the cairn, get down alive, get ahead of the lawyers. But the letters—" She turned the envelope over. Her thumb moved along the flap. "The letters are slow. Like he finally has time to mean things."

"What does this one say?"

"He's testifying again. In the capital. The formal review of my father's blacklist — the hearing's next month. He's offered to go alone. Take sole blame for hiring me illegally, for crossing without permits, all of it. He says—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "He says I've given enough years to other people's lies."

Priya set down her cup. Outside, Dev's voice rose and fell over the training yard, and somewhere a kid laughed — bright, unguarded, the sound of someone who had never been blacklisted from anything.

"And what did you write back?"

"Nothing yet. That's the problem." Elodie pushed the letter an inch toward the center of the table, toward Priya, an offering or a surrender. "Every draft sounds either grateful or furious, and I'm not sure which one is true anymore. Maybe both. Maybe that's what he left me with — both things at once, permanently."

"Welcome to being a person," Priya said. "It's terrible. There's no route around it, only through."

* * *

They worked the morning the way they worked everything now — together, unhurried, with the particular efficiency of people who no longer needed to prove anything to anyone. Priya ran inventory. Elodie repaired the stitching on a haul bag with the focus of a monk. At eleven, Dev brought the kids in for water and a lesson on knot inspection, and Priya stood in the doorway watching him correct a fourteen-year-old girl's prusik grip with Elodie's exact words — *"the mountain doesn't care if you're tired, so build habits that work when you are"* — and felt the strange vertigo of watching your own history get handed down like heirloom silver.

At noon, Elodie washed her hands, combed her hair back, and said, "Walk with me," and Priya went, because some invitations aren't requests.

They climbed the low ridge behind the village, the one the locals called the Shepherd's Spine, to the rebuilt shrine — whitewashed walls, new timber roof, prayer flags Meera's grandchildren had strung only last month, still sharp-colored against the sky. Below them the valley opened green and terraced, the river running high with monsoon melt, and beyond it, far off, the white line of the Karveth Range, wearing its clouds like a closed door.

Or like a window. Priya wasn't sure anymore which one it was.

"Meera came by yesterday," Elodie said. They sat on the warm stone, shoulders almost touching. "First restitution payment cleared. She's having the school roof redone. She wanted to know if we'd come to the ceremony."

"Did you say yes?"

"I said yes." Elodie's hands were quiet in her lap. No gloves, no tape, no scars hidden. Just hands. "She also asked me something I didn't know how to answer. She asked how old I am."

Priya laughed before she could stop herself. "What did you tell her?"

"I told her I'd have to check." Elodie stared out at the range. "And then I sat here for a while doing arithmetic, and do you know what I found? I've spent twenty years able to tell you the exact distance from any point on earth to any other point. Kilometers to the trailhead. Vertical meters to the col. Days of margin before the snows. I could measure anything." Her voice stayed level, but Priya heard the tremor underneath it, the way you hear loose rock under firm footing. "But I couldn't tell you how many birthdays I'd skipped. I genuinely didn't know. I'd deleted them. All of them. Every year since the disaster, I just... didn't count myself."

"And now?"

"Now Meera's granddaughter is making me a cake tomorrow, apparently, because Meera decided these things by decree, and Dev has already told half the crew, and Ravi bought candles in the valley town — actual candles, Priya, he climbed the Spine last week to ask Meera what day it should be, like planning a summit push." Elodie shook her head, but the thawing was at the corners of her mouth again, wider this time. "Tomorrow. On the anniversary of the descent. Because Meera says that's the day I was born — the real one — the day I came down off the mountain instead of becoming part of it."

"She's not wrong."

"No," Elodie said softly. "She's not wrong."

The wind came up the valley, warm and wet, smelling of rain and rice and woodsmoke. Somewhere below, the river fell over the rocks near the rebuilt shrine in the lower gorge — the waterfall, *their* waterfall now, though no map named it. When the current falls, her father's notes had ended, look for what the water keeps. What the water kept, it turned out, was everything: the ledger, the letter, the names of villages that maps had erased, the truth that twenty years of silence couldn't quite drown.

"Priya." Elodie was looking at her directly now, and there was something in her face that Priya had only seen once before — at the cairn, in the wind, kneeling over a tin box in the snow. "Do you remember what I said at the col? When the lawyers blocked us and I proposed the traverse?"

"'No legitimate guide would dare it,'" Priya quoted. "'Fortunately, I'm not legitimate.'"

"I said it as a joke. Mostly." Elodie turned her palm up against the sun, as if weighing it. "But it was true, and it's still true, and I've been thinking about what it costs to be illegitimate your whole life. You learn routes no one else can follow. You survive things that should end you. And then one day the ban lifts — formally, officially, with documents — and you discover the blacklist was never really about the mountains. It was about believing you deserved to be anywhere at all."

"Tomorrow you'll be fifty-one," Priya said. "Or whatever the number is. And you'll deserve the cake."

"Fifty-two."

"Fifty-two. See? You checked."

"I checked." Elodie laughed — actually laughed, head back, the sound startling a pair of choughs off the shrine roof — and Priya thought: *there she is.* Not the guide. Not the survivor. Just Elodie, fifty-two years old, sitting in the sun with a friend, laughing at nothing, at everything, at the absurd arithmetic of a life that had finally agreed to be counted.

* * *

The letter stayed on the kitchen table all afternoon. Priya saw Elodie glance at it twice while they planned next season's training calendar, and the second time, she picked it up, read it straight through a third time — lips moving slightly, which Priya had never seen her do — and then folded it along its existing creases and carried it to the office.

Priya followed, ostensibly to check the mail log, actually to watch.

Elodie opened the bottom drawer. The file. Inside: clippings from the trial coverage. The photograph of the crew at the market, Meera holding the ledger overhead like a lantern. A pressed sprig of juniper from the shrine. The bus ticket. And a thin stack of letters, all in the same careful hand, each envelope worn soft at the corners from rereading.

She put Tomas's letter on top of the stack, squared the edges, and closed the drawer.

"He asks a question at the end," Elodie said, without turning around. "Every letter, he asks a question. This time it's whether I'd meet him in the capital after the hearing. He says there's a route he wants to show me. In the eastern Karveth. One nobody's climbed because everyone who knew it is dead." She paused. "He says my father wrote about it in the margins of the route notes. A way through the eastern wall that the surveyors never mapped."

Priya leaned against the doorframe. "And?"

"And I don't know yet." Elodie turned around. Her eyes were dry, but her voice wasn't quite steady, and for once she didn't try to make it steady, and that — more than the drawer, more than the cake, more than the sign with the tagline Dev had painted — was the thing that told Priya the woman standing in front of her had truly come home. "A year ago I'd have said no before he finished writing. Six months ago I'd have said maybe and meant no. Today I read it three times and I haven't answered, and the fact that I haven't answered means—"

"Means yes," Priya said. "Eventually. You're just letting yourself arrive there at walking pace instead of running."

Elodie looked at her for a long moment. Then she nodded, once, the way she used to nod at the top of a pitch — not triumph, exactly. Arrival.

Outside, the training day was ending. Dev's voice called the kids in for debrief, and their chatter drifted up like birdsong, and the light went gold across the yard, and somewhere in the valley Meera's kitchen was already filling with the smell of tomorrow's cake.

"One condition," Elodie said, reaching for her jacket. "If I go to the capital — when I go — you're coming with me. And Dev. And Ravi. The whole ridiculous crew."

"We wouldn't fit in the hearing room."

"We'll stand outside the windows. We're good at windows." She smiled, and it reached all the way to her eyes, no miles left in it at all. "Come on. Walk me down. Tomorrow I turn fifty-two, and I intend to be measured properly — in years, with witnesses."

They went down together, two guides on an ordinary evening path, while above them the flags snapped in the wind and the great white range held its silence, no longer a sentence, only a place — and somewhere past its eastern wall, a route waited, unmapped, unwalked, patient as truth, for whoever had the courage to climb for it.
