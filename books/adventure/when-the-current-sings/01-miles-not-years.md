## 1. Miles, Not Years

Jude Okafor measured his life in miles, not years.

Years were slippery things. They pooled and stagnated, they carried birthdays and anniversaries and the soft accusations of people who remembered your face. But a mile was honest. A mile had a beginning and an end, and you could feel every foot of it in your knees by the time you finished one.

He was forty-one years old, which meant nothing to him, but he had walked eleven thousand miles of the Kestrel Range's dead rail lines since he'd come up here, and that number meant everything.

The morning of the supply drop began like every other morning: cold water in a tin basin, coffee boiled on a wood stove, and the ritual inventory of a man who maintained things no one else wanted to exist. He kept a ledger — actual paper, actual ink — of every closed line in his care. The Wolf's Tooth spur. The Mercy Pass switchbacks. The old Kestrel trunk that ran upriver into country so remote the maps printed it as blank space with a polite apology.

*Line 7, mile 41: culvert washed out, cleared October 3.*
*Line 4, mile 12: trestle decking rotting through, third plank from north end. Flagged.*

Nobody paid him for this. That was the point. The consortium had decommissioned these lines eleven years ago, written them off as liabilities, and Jude had signed the last inspection report of his career and walked into the mountains like a man stepping out of a burning building. He told himself it was retirement. He told himself it was escape. He told himself a lot of things at twenty-nine that sounded less true every year.

What he actually did was keep the bones of the railway alive so that nobody could use them.

That distinction mattered. He repeated it to himself sometimes, walking a grade at dawn with frost in his beard, when the loneliness got loud enough to have a voice. *You're not maintaining them. You're burying them properly.* A culvert cleared was a culvert that wouldn't flood and shift a hillside. A rotten plank flagged was a plank that wouldn't drop some fool prospector into a gorge. And a route known only to him was a route the ore companies couldn't follow into the high country, where the last unclaimed mineral ground in the range still slept under stone and snow.

His father had spent thirty years building railroads for men like that. Jude had spent twelve helping them do it faster. Then came the Voss Creek disaster — fourteen men, a tunnel that shouldn't have been driven, a company that called it geology instead of greed — and Jude had understood what his family legacy actually weighed. So he'd erased himself the way he now erased lines: carefully, completely, leaving nothing behind worth following.

* * *

The supply drop came twice a season, spring and fall, and it was the closest thing he had to a conversation.

The pilot — a heavyset woman named Odile who flew a battered bush plane out of the riverhead settlement — would swing low over the meadow where Line 9 crossed the flats, and Jude would stand in the grass with his arms crossed, and she would kick out whatever crates the survey expedition upstream had paid him to forward. It wasn't charity and it wasn't quite work. Three years ago, a survey crew working the upper river had found his name in a decommissioned-records archive — the only inspector who knew the old pack routes — and started paying him a modest retainer to receive their freight and haul it as far as the Kestrel fork. He never saw them. They never asked questions. It was, both parties seemed to agree, the perfect arrangement between human beings.

This morning the plane came in low against a sky the color of unpolished tin, and Odile kicked two crates and something extra — a canvas-wrapped bundle that tumbled end over end into the wet grass.

Jude retrieved the crates first. Rice, fuel cells, batteries, the usual grammar of expedition life. Then he crouched over the bundle and unwrapped it with the caution of a man defusing something.

A radio set. Field-grade, new, its packing slip marked in a hand he didn't recognize: *FOR THE ENGINEER AT LINE 9. REPLACEMENT FOR YOUR OLD UNIT. EXPEDITION REQUESTS WEEKLY CONTACT, THURSDAYS, 1900 HOURS. CHANNEL 6.*

He sat back on his heels in the grass and read the note three times.

They'd never asked for contact before. Three years of clean, silent commerce, and now someone upstream wanted his voice in their ears once a week. He should have refused. He should have repacked the radio and left it in the crate like a snake in a basket.

Instead, that evening, he strung the antenna wire between two lodgepoles outside his cabin, and on Thursday at seven o'clock he keyed the mic and said, "Line nine receiving," into the static, feeling like a man knocking on a door he'd nailed shut from the inside.

The voice that answered was dry and precise, a man used to being obeyed. "Good evening, engineer. Elias Voss. I'm told you're the man who knows where all the bodies are buried."

"Depends who did the burying."

A pause, then a laugh — short, surprised, genuine. "I've read your inspection reports. All of them. You have a gift for making a mountain legible, Mr. Okafor. I hope you'll extend the courtesy to us. We're surveying the upper gorge, and I'd sleep better knowing someone downriver knows where we are."

Jude looked out the window at the dark mass of the range, at the passes already wearing their first white hoods of the season. "Where you are is your business."

"My daughter would say the same about me." Another pause, softer this time. "Thursdays, then. Seven o'clock. Just so somebody knows."

And that was how it went, all through the late summer and into the fall. Thursdays at seven, five minutes, sometimes less. Voss would report his position in clipped coordinates; Jude would grunt acknowledgment; occasionally Voss would ask some question about an old grade or a bridge site, and Jude would answer because not answering felt crueler than answering. He learned almost nothing about the man except that he was thorough, restless, and spoke of his daughter Mara the way other men spoke of saints or debts — with reverence and regret in equal measure.

It was, Jude admitted to himself around the second week, the most human contact he'd had in three years, and he hated how much he didn't hate it.

* * *

October thinned. The larches went gold and then bare. The mornings came later and colder, and Jude's ledger filled with entries in his small square hand, and every Thursday at seven he climbed the rise behind his cabin and pressed the transmit key and listened to a stranger tell him he was still alive.

On the fourth Thursday of October, the static answered back.

He tried again at 1905. Again at 1915, walking a slow circle with the handset raised like a man trying to catch rain in a drought. The channel hissed. Somewhere far upriver, weather was moving in — he could taste it, iron and ice on the wind — and radios failed in weather, everyone knew that, it was probably nothing, it was probably the ridge between them full of storm.

But Voss had never missed a check. Not once. In nineteen weeks, through squalls and equipment failures and one memorable Thursday when the man reported he'd fallen into the river and was transmitting while soaked and shivering, Elias Voss had never missed a Thursday.

At 1930, Jude set the handset down on the cabin table and stood looking at it for a long time.

Then he opened the ledger, and beneath the last entry — *Line 4, mile 12: decking failing* — he wrote a new line in ink that trembled only slightly:

*Upper gorge party: contact lost, Oct. 24, 1900 hrs. Last known position, mile 61 above the fork.*

He read it back and heard what it was. It was the first sentence of a story he had sworn never to be in again. Fourteen men at Voss Creek. A tunnel that shouldn't have been driven. A company that called it geology. He had walked away from exactly this — from the moment when a man in the field goes quiet and someone has to decide whether to go after him, and going means the mountains, and the mountains mean the routes, and the routes mean the map.

Because there was a map. Of course there was a map. Eleven thousand miles of walking distilled onto oilcloth and folded into a tin box under his floorboards — every closed line, every dead trestle, every pass and pack route and washout in the Kestrel Range, drawn in his own hand over eleven years. The consortium thought those lines were forgotten. The mountains themselves barely remembered them. Only Jude knew, and the map was the only thing he owned that was worth anything to anyone, which is precisely why it had to be worth nothing.

He put the radio in its case. He filled a kettle he didn't drink from. Outside, the wind had turned and was coming down out of the high country carrying the smell of snow, and somewhere up there, past the fork, past mile 61, a man who said *just so somebody knows* had stopped being known.

Jude took down his pack and began, without deciding to, to load it.

* * *

He held out four more days.

He told himself the storm explained it — four days of sleet and low cloud, radios dying in weather like flies in frost. He told himself the expedition had moved camp beyond the ridge shadow, out of signal. He told himself the story so many times it wore smooth as river stone, and then on Monday morning he walked the six miles to the relay station at the old Kestrel junction, keyed the long-range set he'd sworn never to touch, and put a call through to the riverhead office of the Meridian Consolidated Railway — the consortium, the beast itself, the reason he lived alone.

The voice that answered was young and corporate and bored until he gave his name. Then it changed.

"Okafor. The Okafor? Hold please."

Hold. Music. Then a different voice, older, smoother, a voice like a handshake that takes too long. "Mr. Okafor. This is a surprise. We haven't heard from you since you resigned your commission — dramatically, if I recall."

"The survey party upriver's gone quiet. Nineteen weeks of Thursday checks, missed the last two. Somebody should know."

"We appreciate the concern. We'll flag it with the appropriate—"

"They're your people. On your concession. Above the fork, past mile sixty-one, in country that closes for winter in three weeks."

A silence on the line, long enough that Jude could hear the man recalculating something. When he spoke again, the boredom was gone, replaced by something attentive and cold that Jude recognized from boardrooms a lifetime ago.

"That's very observant of you, Mr. Okafor. Very detailed. Almost as if you knew that country yourself." A pause. "As it happens, we're organizing a search. Small, fast, before the snows. We could use a man who knows the old grades."

"No."

"We haven't offered terms yet."

"There are no terms. I called to report a silence, not to join a posse. Send whoever you send. I won't be among them."

He cut the call and stood a moment in the relay station's rusted quiet, heart hammering like a boy's, furious at himself — for calling, for caring, for the way the word *no* had come out too fast, the way it always does when it's lying.

* * *

That night he almost didn't check the channel. Habit won, the way habit always wins with men like him. At 1900 hours he climbed the rise, keyed the mic, and let the static pour through him like cold water.

He was about to sign off when the static stuttered — broke — and a fragment of voice surfaced through it, thin and warped, half-drowned in interference. He couldn't make out words, only cadence: a man speaking steadily, deliberately, the way you speak when you know you might not be heard.

Then, clearer, a single phrase punched through the noise like a nail through cloth:

"—for Okafor. Repeat, this transmission is for Okafor—"

And then the static rolled back in and closed over it, and the channel went dead, and Jude stood on the frozen rise with the handset shaking in his grip while the first flakes of the season came down out of the dark and settled, one by one, on the shoulders of a man who had just run out of miles to hide behind.

Below the floorboards, wrapped in oilcloth, the map waited.
