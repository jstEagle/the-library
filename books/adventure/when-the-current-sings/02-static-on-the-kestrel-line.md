## 2. Static on the Kestrel Line

The radio check was due at six.

Jude knew this the way he knew the weight of his own boots, the way he knew that the third rail spike from the old mile marker forty-one had worked loose again and needed reseating before frost. Six o'clock, every Thursday, the Voss expedition checked in on the Kestrel relay band. He'd set his watch by it for eleven weeks without admitting to himself that he'd done so. A man who measured his life in miles walked did not, by rights, measure it also in radio checks. But he'd stopped pretending around week four.

At 6:04 he turned the dial through the static and heard nothing but the mountain breathing—white noise, the long hiss of altitude, the sound the Kestrel Range made when it wanted to be left alone.

He tried again at 6:15. Then at 6:30, standing at the window of the line shack with the kettle going cold behind him, watching the river gorge darken below. The expedition was camped somewhere upriver past Wolf's Tooth, beyond the trestles, in country Jude had walked alone for five years and mapped alone for five years and told no one about for five years. Eleven weeks ago a floatplane had dropped supplies on the gravel bar below his shack, and a lean man with gray in his beard and his daughter's stubborn jaw had shaken Jude's hand and said, *Okafor. Your charts got us this far. We'll check in Thursdays.*

Elias Voss. The name still sat strangely in Jude's mouth, like a coin from a country he'd emigrated from.

At seven he keyed the transmitter himself. "Kestrel base to Voss party. Come in, Voss."

Static. The patient, indifferent static of a range that swallowed signals the way the river swallowed stones.

They could be out of range. Weather in the high country made liars of radios; a storm wall between the peaks could kill a transmission as cleanly as a knife. They could have moved camp. Elias Voss struck him as a man who moved camp the way other men changed shirts. There were a dozen innocent explanations, and Jude recited them to himself while he ate standing up, while he banked the stove, while he lay awake in the dark listening to the wind come down the gorge and finding, beneath it, no sound at all from upriver where there should have been—no blast echo, no generator drone, nothing. The mountain had gone quiet in a way mountains didn't unless they were keeping something.

By morning he had decided. Not to go—he had decided that absolutely, the way a man decides not to open a door he can hear knocking behind—but to report. Reporting was maintenance. Maintenance was his whole religion now: keep the lines clear, keep the records straight, keep your hands busy and your history buried. He keyed the consortium's regional frequency, the one printed on the supply manifests, and gave his call sign, and waited the better part of an hour for the relay to find a human being.

"Base copies," said a voice finally, clipped and corporate and very far away. "Voss party missed scheduled contact?"

"Missed it. Fourteen hours now. Weather's clean on my end. No storm traffic in the gorge."

A pause with the texture of paperwork. "Understood. Stand by, Okafor."

"They could be hurt." He heard his own voice sharpen and didn't rein it in. "If someone went down a slope, if the med kit's short—if they're walking out injured, every hour matters."

"Stand by, Okafor."

* * *

They came by helicopter two days later, which was the first wrong thing, because the consortium did not spend helicopter money on missing surveyors. It spent helicopter money on assets.

Jude watched it come up the gorge low over the water, rotors flattening the river to white lace, and felt something in his chest close like a fist. Two men stepped out onto his gravel bar in matching shells the color of wet slate, and the younger one smiled the way men smile when they've been trained to. The older one didn't smile at all. He had a face like a closed ledger, and he looked at Jude's shack—the antenna, the charts pinned under the window, the boots drying by the door—and catalogued all of it without appearing to.

"Okafor." The older man offered a hand Jude didn't take. "Halloran. Regional operations. You reported the Voss silence."

"I reported it. Twice."

"Appreciated." Halloran's eyes went past him, upriver, to where the gorge bent into shadow. "We're mounting a search. Immediate. And we need you on it."

The words landed wrong, the way a load lands wrong on a flatcar—all the weight on one side. "You have search teams."

"We have teams coming up from the valley. None of them know the range." Halloran turned fully to him then, and Jude saw the calculation arrive behind his eyes like a train coming around a bend. "You do. Five years of solo maintenance on the abandoned lines. Every trestle, every cut, every washout. There is no one else alive who knows those routes. That's not flattery, Okafor. It's logistics."

"And if I say no?"

"Then we go in blind, and if Voss's people are alive, they wait longer." Halloran let that sit. Then, softer, almost kind, which was worse: "You supplied them. Your charts took them up there. You must feel some—"

"Don't." Jude heard how fast it came out of him, how much it gave away, and hated it.

Halloran nodded slowly, as if Jude had confirmed something on a form. "We stage at the riverhead tomorrow, oh-six-hundred. Think about it tonight." He paused at the helicopter's door, one hand on the frame, and added without turning around: "There'll be compensation, of course. Hazard rates. And the company remembers who helps."

The helicopter lifted away and the river healed itself behind it, and Jude stood on the gravel bar for a long time doing arithmetic he didn't like. Because here was the thing he couldn't say aloud: he knew what the consortium searched for, and it wasn't men. He'd read the supply manifests. Ore assays, core samples, spectral gear—equipment for finding things in the ground worth more than the people standing on it. The Kestrel Range had been abandoned by every railway that ever touched it, and companies did not send helicopters back to places they'd abandoned out of nostalgia.

And he knew the routes. God help him, he knew them better than anyone living, because he'd spent five years learning them the way you learn a wound—slowly, alone, with your fingers in it. If he went, he led the consortium straight up the spine of everything he'd tried to let the mountain keep. If he stayed, and Voss's people were bleeding out in some cut above the tree line—

He refused. He said it out loud to the empty shack, to make it real. "No."

* * *

That night he pulled the radio log, because maintenance meant records and records meant he could at least give the searchers something true. He sat with the lamp down low and thumbed back through eleven weeks of Thursday check-ins, timestamps and signal strengths, and found the last one.

It wasn't a routine check-in.

The final transmission from the Voss party had come three days ago—not Thursday, off-schedule, low power, flagged in the relay's automatic log as *incomplete*. He tuned to the recorded frequency, hands suddenly not steady, and let the tape run.

Static first. Then Elias Voss's voice, thin through the interference, breathless in a way that had nothing to do with altitude:

*"—if this reaches base, tell them the vein is real, tell them—" A burst of noise. When the voice came back it was lower, closer to the microphone, and it was no longer talking to base at all.*

*"Okafor. Jude Okafor, if you're hearing this—I know you monitor the band, don't lie to yourself. You're the only one who will. Listen to me. We found it because of your map. Every route, every crossing—you drew them a road and they'll follow it, and what's down here isn't ore, it's—" Static, long, swallowing. Then, faintly, almost lost:* 

*"—you always knew where to bury things. So bury this one deep. Burn the routes, Jude. Before they—"*

The tape ran on into static and kept running, and Jude let it, because moving required deciding what to do with his hands and he didn't trust them.

*Burn the routes.*

His map. His life's work—not the consortium's surveys, not the dead railways' ledgers, but the thing he'd made alone in five years of silence: every trestle, every grade, every hidden crossing of the Kestrel Range, drawn in his own hand because no one else would ever care enough to draw them. The reason he'd told himself he kept it was maintenance. The truth was simpler and worse: it was the only thing he'd ever finished, and finishing it had been the closest he'd come, since leaving his family's world, to mattering.

And Elias Voss—a man he'd met once, shaken hands with once—was asking him to burn it. Was telling him, in a voice already half-swallowed by the mountain, that the map was why they were dying.

Jude sat until the lamp burned low. Outside, the wind shifted and came down the gorge carrying, or so his ears insisted, a smell of snow that had no business arriving yet. Early snows. The passes would seal within weeks, maybe days. Whatever window existed for reaching the Voss party—and for whatever Elias had rigged or broken or hidden up there—it was closing at the speed of weather.

He thought about refusing Halloran. He thought about how easy refusal was from inside a locked room, and how the knocking never stops just because you don't answer.

Then he got up, took down the oilcloth packet from behind the loose panel above his bunk, and unwrapped his map for the first time in a year. The lines looked back at him, patient and damning. Every route a promise he'd made to no one. Every crossing a way in.

He packed it.

Not for Halloran—for himself, or for the dead man's voice, or for whatever was left alive upriver that a map might reach before the snow did. He told himself all three in turn and believed none of them completely, and that was its own kind of honesty.

At dawn he stood on the gravel bar with a rucksack and a coil of rope and watched the helicopter come back up the gorge, and the last thought he had before it landed was Elias Voss's voice saying *bury this one deep*, and the question underneath it that he'd been avoiding for three days:

Bury it deep enough to keep everyone out—or deep enough that no one, ever, could dig it back out?

The rotors flattened the river to white lace, and Jude Okafor picked up his bag, and answered neither.
