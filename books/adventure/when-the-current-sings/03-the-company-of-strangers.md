## 3. The Company of Strangers

The riverhead was a wound in the valley where the Kestrel Line died. I'd walked it a hundred times in the years since the last train ran, and it always looked to me like something amputated badly—the rails ending in a rusted buffer, the ties sinking into silt, the water sliding past without apology. That morning it looked worse, because there were people in it.

Four people, counting me.

The consortium's floatplane had put me down on the gravel bar at dawn, and the pilot hadn't even cut his engine before I understood the search party was a lie told badly. There was no climbing team. No survey gear. No radios worth the name. There was a canvas tent pitched crooked against the wind, a cook fire burning wet wood because nobody had thought to split it, and three strangers who watched me come up the bank like I was something the river had coughed up.

The woman spoke first. She was crouched over a supply crate, sorting vials into a leather roll with the economy of someone who'd done it ten thousand times, and she didn't look up when she said, "You're the engineer."

"Jude Okafor."

"Ruth Adeyemi." She tied the roll, stood, and only then gave me her whole attention, which was a lot of attention. She was maybe fifty, gray at the temples, with the forearms of someone who did heavy work and the eyes of someone who'd seen the results of it done badly. "They struck me off the medical registry four years ago. You should know that before you let me set your bones."

"Was it malpractice?"

"No. It was telling the truth about a company's accident report." She almost smiled. "The consortium hires me because I'm cheap and available and I don't ask them to like me. You can decide what that says about them."

It said plenty. I looked past her at the other two.

The kid was sitting on the buffer at the end of the dead line, legs dangling, headphones clamped over his ears, a radio rig balanced on his knees that had been soldered and resoldered so many times it looked like a wasp's nest made of copper. Fifteen, maybe sixteen. He pulled one earphone off when he noticed me staring.

"You're Okafor?" His voice cracked in the middle of the name, and he covered it by grinning. "The Okafor? I've read your survey notes. The Wolf's Tooth trestle load analysis is a masterpiece. Nobody else bothered to—"

"Pip." The third one said it from the shadow of the tent, and it wasn't a greeting. It was a warning.

She came out slowly, and I knew her before she said her name, because she had her father's face the way a knife has its maker's mark. I'd met Elias Voss exactly twice—once at a consortium function I'd attended under protest, once on a platform in the rain where he'd asked me, with terrifying sincerity, why a man with my gifts would choose to erase himself. I'd had no answer then. His daughter had the same gray eyes that asked the question without asking it.

"Mara Voss," she said. "You knew my father."

"I met him twice."

"You mapped the route he followed." She said it flat, no accusation in it yet, which somehow made it worse. Accusation I could have answered. "The old Kestrel spur. The passes everyone else wrote off. My father spent eleven years trying to get funding to follow your maps, and when the consortium finally said yes, he went up that river with nine people and a radio schedule. You knew that route better than anyone alive. You could have led it. Instead you sold him the map and stayed home."

"I didn't sell him anything. I gave him the survey."

"Why?"

It was the first question anyone had asked me in years that I couldn't answer with a number. I looked at the river, because it was easier than looking at her. "Because he asked. Because he was the only one who ever understood what the lines were. Because—" I stopped. Because I couldn't stand the thought of the routes dying with me. Because a map in a drawer is a kind of grave, and I'd dug enough of those.

"Because," she repeated, and let it hang there like smoke.

* * *

We broke camp within the hour, because the consortium's man—a smooth-voiced dispatcher named Calloway, who'd given me my instructions by radio the night before—wanted us moving before the weather window closed. Ruth shouldered a medic's pack that weighed as much as the kid. Pip carried the radio and a coil of wire antenna and asked questions the whole time, about the line, the grades, the bridges, whether I'd really walked every mile of the closed system, and I answered the ones I could and pretended not to hear the ones I didn't want to.

Mara walked at the front and didn't talk to anyone.

I fell into step beside her around the second bend of the gorge, where the river narrowed and the old rail bed climbed onto a shelf of cut stone my grandfather's crews had blasted out of the cliff face in a single season, back when the company spent money like water and men like gravel.

"You should know the route," I said. "Three days upriver to the last confirmed camp. Two passes after that. If the weather holds."

"If."

"If it doesn't, we turn around and come back in spring, and your father spends a winter in a tent with nine people and full supplies, annoyed but alive." I'd been telling myself that for two days. It sounded thinner every time.

She stopped walking. The river filled the silence, and I let it, because I'd had a lot of practice letting rivers talk for me.

"My father wrote me a letter before he went up," she said. "I didn't open it until after the checks went silent. He wrote that he'd found the route at last—the real one, the one the old surveys missed—and that he owed it to the engineer who erased the lines. Those were his words. *Erased the lines.* He thought you were a man who destroyed things on purpose. He admired it." She turned to look at me, and up close her father's eyes were harder than his had been, or maybe just younger. "So which is it, Okafor? Did you erase them, or did you just hide?"

"Is there a difference?"

"Yes. One of them can be forgiven."

I didn't have an answer for that either. I walked on, and after a while she walked too, a half-length behind me, close enough to watch my hands when I checked the cut slopes and the drainage and the frost-cracked rock. I caught her watching. She didn't look away. There was going to be no privacy on this river, I understood then, not from her. She'd learned to read people the way I read terrain, looking for the places where the surface gave way.

* * *

We made camp that night under an overhang of granite where the old crews had once parked a rotary snowplow, its rusted blade still facing upriver like a thing that meant to keep going. Ruth built a fire that actually burned, having split the wood herself without comment while the rest of us stood around. Pip strung the antenna between two boulders and got the set humming, and by full dark he had the schedule frequency dialed in, scanning the bands with his headphones on and his whole body tilted toward the sound like a compass needle.

"Anything?" Ruth asked.

"Static on the schedule band. But—" He pulled the headphones half off. "There's a carrier wave up around here that comes and goes. Weak. Somebody's got a transmitter running upriver. Low power. Intermittent."

Ruth and I looked at each other. The expedition's long-range set, if it still worked, would be loud and steady. A weak intermittent carrier meant something else—a hand-cranked unit, a damaged one, someone rationing their battery in the dark.

"Could be the consortium's people," Ruth said carefully. "They said they had a second team staging at the divide."

"They said a lot of things," I said.

Mara had been silent by the fire, turning her father's letter over in her hands. She'd read it four times since the gravel bar. Now she smoothed it flat on her knee. "He signed off his last scheduled transmission with a code word," she said. "The expedition used them. *Kestrel* meant all's well. *Current* meant trouble. And there was a third one, for—" She stopped. Her jaw worked. "For if the trouble was the company itself. If they couldn't say it out loud."

"Did he use it?" Ruth asked.

"He used the third one. On the last check. Nine seconds of carrier and then the word." She looked up at me across the fire, and the light made her father's eyes into something fierce and young and unbearably certain. "*Home.* That was the word. *Home.* He was telling whoever was listening that the danger was the people who sent him. And the consortium sent you four instead."

"Not four," Pip said quietly. He'd gone pale under his freckles, headphones around his neck. "I wasn't sent by anybody."

"No," Mara said, not looking away from me. "You stowed away. Which makes you the only honest one on this river." She folded the letter along its old creases, deliberate as a surgeon. "So tell me, engineer. When we find my father's camp—what exactly did the consortium tell you to do if you found him alive?"

I fed a stick into the fire and watched it catch, and I told her the truth, because lying to this one was going to be impossible and I'd known it since the gravel bar.

"They told me to confirm the route's condition. To assess the ore samples if any were on site. And to secure the survey materials." I met her eyes. "They didn't tell me what to do about your father. Which means whatever happens upriver, they've already decided it doesn't matter."

The fire popped. Down the gorge, faint as breath, the river kept saying the same thing over and over, and for the first time in years I heard it as a warning instead of a comfort.

Pip's hand came up suddenly. "The carrier wave," he said. "It just changed. It's not intermittent anymore." He swung the antenna toward the dark upriver, and his voice dropped. "Somebody up there is sending. Same three pulses, over and over. Three, pause, three, pause, three."

Ruth stood. "Distress."

"Or a lure," Mara said.

Nobody answered her. Above us the first wind of the front came down the gorge, smelling of snow, and the fire bent flat, and we all looked up the black river toward the sound that only the kid could hear.
