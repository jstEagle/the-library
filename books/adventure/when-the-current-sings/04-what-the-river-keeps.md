## 4. What the River Keeps

The gorge swallowed sound the way it swallowed light. Mara Voss walked at the front of the line because she couldn't stand to walk anywhere else, and the river talked to her the whole way down—low, constant, indifferent. It had been talking like that for a hundred thousand years and would go on talking long after anyone remembered her father's name.

She hated how much comfort she took from that.

Behind her, boots on wet stone: Ruth, steady as a metronome; Pip, lighter, quicker, half-skipping to keep the radio case from swinging; and Jude Okafor, last in line, always last, as if distance were something he could put between himself and other people even when they were all moving the same direction.

Three days out of the riverhead, and she still didn't know what to make of him.

Her father had trusted him. That was the part that sat wrong, the splinter under the skin of everything. Elias Voss did not trust people. He'd taught her that himself, over twenty years of packed duffels and short goodbyes—*never let the crew see you doubt, never let the company see you hope*. He'd trusted his daughter with his silences and no one else with anything. And yet somewhere up this river, in his last transmissions before the static took him, he'd asked for Jude Okafor by name. She'd read the transcript three times on the boat upriver. *If anything goes wrong, tell the engineer. He'll know the lines.*

*He'll know the lines.* Like her father had known him. Like they'd shared something she'd never been given the key to.

"Cache should be past this bend," Jude said, and his voice was exactly what she'd expected from the transcript: flat, careful, a man reading coordinates aloud. "Elias marked it on my survey copy. Left bank, above the flood line."

"You've seen his handwriting," Mara said. Not a question. She watched his shoulders take it.

"I've seen his field notes." He didn't look at her. "He annotated my maps for years. Corrections, mostly. He was thorough."

"For years." She kept her voice level, matched his flatness. "And in all those years, he never once mentioned you."

The river filled the silence for him. Ruth glanced back, then away, professional enough not to stare. Pip, who was seventeen and had no such discipline, stared openly.

"There wasn't much to mention," Jude said finally. "I sold maps. He bought them."

"That's all?"

"That's all."

She let it go, because the bend was coming, and because she'd learned long ago that men like Jude Okafor gave you nothing until you stopped asking—but she filed it, the way her father had taught her to file things. In the drawer marked *lies of omission*.

* * *

They found the cache an hour before dark, wedged in a cleft of granite above a gravel bar, exactly where a man who annotated maps for years would have put it: findable by design, invisible by accident. A canvas cache bag, oilcloth-wrapped, stenciled with the expedition mark—a kestrel in a circle—and beneath the stencil, in her father's blocky hand: *VOSS / 3 OF 4*.

Mara knelt in the gravel and didn't touch it for a long moment. Three days of walking, and here was the first thing in the world her father's hands had actually touched. The canvas was stiff with cold. Her fingers shook, and she hated that too.

Ruth crouched beside her without crowding her. "Want a minute?"

"No." She opened it.

Inside: spare rations, two tins of fuel, a coil of rope still bright and unused. A medical kit, sealed. And underneath, wrapped separately in waxed paper, a notebook.

She knew the notebook. She'd grown up watching him fill them—the green cloth-bound ones, the pencil worn to a stub between his fingers, the smell of graphite and kerosene that meant *home* in whatever language homes spoke. This one was half full. The last entry was dated eleven days before the radio went silent.

She read it by headlamp while the others made camp above the flood line, and she read it twice, because the first time she couldn't breathe properly.

It started ordinary. Elevations, water levels, crew grumbles, the price of everything going up. Then, six days in:

*Crossed the divide at the old cut. The engineer's line runs true—of course it does, the man draws like God measures. We followed the abandoned grade into the valley he marked as impassable and found why he marked it so.*

*There is ore in this mountain such as I have not seen in thirty years of looking. Copper, but not only copper. The seam runs through the whole shoulder of the peak. If the consortium sees what we saw, they will bring ten thousand men and a town will grow here like a tumor, and the river will die first.*

*I have told the crew we found nothing. Half of them believe me. Half of them are already calculating.*

Mara lowered the notebook. The fire crackled. Pip was showing Ruth some trick with the antenna wire, laughing at something, alive in a way that felt obscene and necessary at once.

*Half of them are already calculating.*

Her father had stood in a valley no map said was passable, holding the richest find of his life, and his first thought had been to hide it. His second thought—

She turned pages forward. The entries got shorter. Colder.

*Riggs wants samples sent downriver. I refused. He says the consortium has rights to any strike within the concession. I say the concession ends where the mountain begins.*

*Two of the crew sided with Riggs tonight. I moved the food cache.*

*The engineer erased these lines once. I begin to understand why. Some routes should not exist. I am going to ask him to erase them again.*

Mara closed the notebook and pressed it flat against her sternum, against the parka, as if she could push the words through her own ribs and into whatever was left of him.

*The engineer erased these lines once.*

She looked across the fire at Jude Okafor, who was sitting apart, as always, mending a strap on his pack with small precise hands, and she understood suddenly that she had been reading him wrong. She'd thought he was hiding from the world. Men who hid from the world didn't get asked for by name in a dying man's final transmission. Men who hid from the world weren't described in a journal as someone you'd trust to bury a mountain.

He felt her looking. He always seemed to feel it, and never turned.

"Eat," Ruth called to her gently. "You can grieve and chew at the same time. I've seen it done."

Mara almost smiled. Almost. She ate.

* * *

She waited until the watch changed. Second watch, when Pip slept like the dead teenager he was and Ruth sat wrapped in her coat facing the river, giving them privacy the way medics do—by pretending not to be there.

Jude was awake, of course. He was always awake during second watch, sitting up with his back against a boulder, and Mara suspected he slept the way her father had: in installments, guiltily, one ear open.

She sat down across the coals from him and held up the notebook. "Read the last page."

"I don't need to."

"He wrote about you."

"I know what he wrote." For the first time since the riverhead, something moved behind his face—not anger, something older and more tired than anger. "I read his field notes for nine years, Ms. Voss. I know how he wrote."

"Then you know he trusted you. You knew what he found—or you guessed it, the moment you saw which valley your own map led them into. And you sat at the riverhead for three days arguing about whether to come."

"Yes."

The word landed clean, no defense attached, and it infuriated her more than any lie would have.

"My father is up this river," she said, "dying or dead or worse, and you knew—"

"I knew a route existed that shouldn't." His voice stayed low, stayed flat, but there was iron under it now. "I drew that grade when I was twenty-six years old, and I have spent fifteen years trying to make sure nobody ever used it. Your father found it anyway, because he was the best field man I ever annotated for, and because mountains don't keep secrets from men like him. So yes. I knew. Knowing isn't the same as being able to stop it."

"What does that mean? What did he want you to erase?"

Jude looked at the fire for a long time. When he spoke again, his voice had dropped to nearly nothing, and the river nearly took it.

"It means your father understood something it took me fifteen years and a lot of miles to learn. Some things you don't protect by guarding them. You protect them by making sure no one can ever reach them again." He looked up at her then, and his eyes in the coal-light were the flattest, saddest thing she'd seen since the telegram came. "When we find him, Ms. Voss—if we find him—you may wish he'd never written any of it down."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I have tonight."

She wanted to shake him. She wanted to grab the collar of that ruined coat and demand the whole architecture of his silence, every beam of it, the way you'd demand the truth from a bridge before you trusted it with your weight. Instead she heard herself say, quietly, in a voice she barely recognized as hers:

"When I was twelve, he missed my birthday because a vein looked promising. When I was sixteen, he missed the funeral of the only grandparent I had. I spent my whole life competing with rock. And now I find out the one person he trusted with his last secret was a stranger who sells maps and walks alone." Her breath came out white in the cold. "So you'll forgive me if 'that's all' doesn't hold."

Jude was quiet for so long she thought he wouldn't answer. Then he reached into his jacket—not fast, deliberately slow, letting her see—and drew out a folded square of oilskin, and inside it, even in the dark, she caught the gleam of drafting paper. Lines. Contours. A lifetime of them.

"This stays with me," he said. "Not because I don't trust you. Because the more people who know what's in this range, the fewer people walk out of it." He refolded it, unhurried, and put it away. "Ask me again when we find your father. I'll answer what I can. But not tonight, and not here, where the river carries sound farther than you'd think."

Mara held his gaze, and he held hers, and neither of them blinked, and she realized with a strange, unwelcome clarity that she was beginning to be able to read him—that the flatness was load-bearing, that the deflections were walls around something, and that she could spend the rest of this river learning the shape of what was behind them whether he liked it or not.

"Fine," she said. "Tonight, the river keeps it."

She stood, and was three steps toward her bedroll when his voice caught her, low, stripped of its flatness for just one word:

"Mara."

She turned.

"The journal," he said. "The entry about the crew splitting. Which names did he write down?"

She frowned, pulling the memory of the page back up. "Riggs. And two others—he didn't give their names. Just said two sided with him."

Jude exhaled slowly through his nose, and in the coal-light his face did something she'd never seen it do: it aged.

"Then we have a problem," he said, "because Riggs isn't upriver anymore."

"What do you mean? Where would he—"

Jude nodded downstream. Back the way they'd come. Toward the riverhead. Toward the world.

"He's between us and home," Jude said. "And he knows we're coming."
