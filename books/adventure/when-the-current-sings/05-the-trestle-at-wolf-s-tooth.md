## 5. The Trestle at Wolf's Tooth

The storm came down the gorge like something with a grudge.

I'd watched weather build over the Kestrels for eleven years, and I knew the look of it—the green-black bruise stacking up behind Wolf's Tooth, the way the river went the color of gunmetal and started talking in a lower voice. By two o'clock the light had gone strange and yellow. By three, Pip's radio was screaming static in every direction at once.

"We should move," I said.

Mara didn't look up from her father's journal. "We've been moving since dawn."

"Not fast enough." I pointed upstream, where the gorge pinched to a throat of granite no wider than a rail bed. "That's the Narrows. When this hits, the river'll rise six feet through there in an hour. We camp here, we wake up swimming."

Ruth was already repacking her kit with the economy of a woman who'd done it a thousand times in worse places. "Where do you propose we go, engineer? There's nothing between here and the pass but rock."

"There's one thing." I hated saying it. Saying it made it real, made me responsible for what happened next. "The Wolf's Tooth viaduct. It crosses the Narrows about four miles up. There's a maintenance shelter on the far abutment—stone walls, iron roof. Solid enough."

"How solid is the viaduct?" Mara asked.

She asked it flatly, watching me, and I understood she already knew the answer would be bad. She'd been reading me for days now, the way she read her father's handwriting—looking past the words to the tremor underneath them.

"Last inspected five years ago," I said. "It passed."

"Passed," Pip repeated, delighted. "Like a school exam?"

"Like a condemned building that hasn't fallen down yet," Ruth said.

* * *

The rain found us before the trestle did. It came sideways off the peaks, hard as thrown gravel, and inside twenty minutes we were all soaked through and not speaking, because speaking meant opening your mouth to water. The old rail grade clung to the gorge wall above the rising river, and I walked it the way you walk a hallway in your own house at night—by memory, by feel, by the count of ties under my boots.

Four hundred and twelve. That's where the grade ended and the viaduct began.

Wolf's Tooth spanned the Narrows on nine steel spans set on stone piers, three hundred feet of lattice and rivets built in 1911 by men who expected it to outlast their grandchildren's grandchildren. For eighty years it did. Then the trains stopped coming, and the mountain started taking its cut—rust creeping up from the bolt holes, frost splitting the piers one winter at a time, wind worrying the bracing like a dog with a bone.

Five years ago I'd stood on it with a hammer and a notebook and called it sound. Five years is a long time to a bridge nobody loves.

"Stay on the centerline," I said, raising my voice over the storm. "Single file. Don't touch the rails—they're not grounded, and if lightning comes down this gorge we'll all know about it. Step where I step."

"Why?" Pip shouted.

"Because I'm the only one who knows which boards are lying."

Mara went first after me, then Ruth, then Pip, who kept up a running commentary of numbers under his breath—I counted his steps, he told me later, because counting was better than looking down. The river was directly beneath us now, forty feet down and climbing, white water chewing at the pier bases with a sound like grinding teeth.

At the third span, the wind hit us broadside and the whole structure sang—a low groaning chord through the steel, deep enough to feel in my back teeth. Everyone stopped.

"It does that," I said. "Keep moving."

"You knew that," Mara said. Not a question. "You knew it groans, you knew which boards are rotten, you knew the river rises. You've known all of it for years and you never told anyone these lines were still walkable."

"I told the consortium they were closed."

"And they believed you?"

I stepped over a gap where a tie had rotted clean through, and turned to offer her a hand across. She looked at my hand for a long second, then took it. Her grip was cold and strong.

"They believed what I wrote in the ledger," I said. "Condemned. Unsafe. Remove when budget allows. Nobody budgets for removing things nobody wants."

"So you erased us," she said. "Same as you erased the lines."

Before I could answer—which was probably good, because I didn't have an answer that wasn't a confession—the world came apart behind us.

* * *

I heard it before I saw it: a crack like a rifle shot, but wrong, too long, wood tearing instead of splitting. I turned in time to see the tie under Pip's left foot fold like wet cardboard, and then his leg punched through the deck to the hip, and then the board beside it went too, and he dropped.

He caught himself on his elbows, arms spread across the remaining ties, half his body hanging into the storm. Below him the river reached up, hungry, forty feet of falling air between his boots and the white water.

"Pip!" Ruth was moving before anyone else, dropping to her belly on the deck, crawling toward him along the centerline. "Don't move. Do you hear me? Don't move anything."

"My legs—" His voice cracked high. "I can't feel—"

"You can feel panic, that's all. Stay with me, kid."

I got down beside Ruth and made myself look at what we were dealing with. Two ties gone, maybe three. The stringers beneath were original timber, black with age, and I could see daylight through a split in the near one that shouldn't have been there. The deck around Pip was a trapdoor waiting to happen.

"Distribute his weight," I said. "Get a rope under his chest. If we pull straight up we tear him loose with the deck."

"Then how?"

"Sideways. Along the rail." I pointed. "The rails sit on steel plates bolted direct to the stringers. Strongest line on the whole bridge. We slide him onto the rail bed and move him plate to plate."

Ruth didn't argue, didn't hesitate—she just looked at me once, and whatever she saw in my face, she trusted it. Later I would think about that moment more than any other on the mountain: a woman struck off the registry for reasons she never explained, betting a boy's life on the word of a man who lived alone with bridges.

Mara anchored the rope around the far rail stanchion. Ruth talked to Pip the whole time, steady as a metronome—"That's it, breathe, you're doing fine, eyes on me, not down"—while I bellied out onto the broken section and got a sling under his arms. The wind shoved at us. The steel sang its groaning chord again, louder, and somewhere below a pier shed stone into the river.

"On three," I said. "One—"

The tie under my own knee let go.

I dropped six inches, caught the rail, and hung there with one arm while the gorge yawned. And the thing I remember, the thing I keep even now, is that my first thought wasn't *the map, the map is in your pack, if you go in the map goes in*. My first thought was *Pip*, and whether the sling would hold without my weight anchoring it.

Mara had my wrist before I knew she'd moved. She hauled with a fury that had nothing to do with forgiveness, and I came up over the rail bed gasping, and then we pulled together, all four of us, and Pip slid across the broken gap onto solid steel and lay there shaking, alive, whole, his legs reporting in one pins-and-needle complaint at a time.

Ruth checked him with quick hands—pulses, pupils, the press of fingers against his ribs—and pronounced him bruised, sprained, and grounded until the far side. Then she sat back on her heels in the pouring rain and laughed, once, a short bark of a sound, like it had escaped custody.

"He's fine," she said, to no one, to the storm. "He's actually fine."

* * *

The maintenance shelter was exactly as I remembered it: a stone box with an iron roof, a rusted stove, and a view of the viaduct standing against the storm like a skeleton refusing to lie down. We got a fire going. We got Pip wrapped in every dry thing we owned. We got tea from snowmelt, and nobody spoke for a long time.

It was Pip who broke it.

"You came back for me." He was staring into the stove. "On the bridge. You went out on the broken part. You didn't have to."

"Yes, I did," I said. "I'm the one who said the bridge passed inspection."

"That's not what I mean."

I knew it wasn't. I looked at the fire and tried to find the honest answer, and discovered, uncomfortably, that I had one. Somewhere in the last four hundred miles these three strangers had stopped being cargo I was escorting and become people whose deaths I would carry. I hadn't agreed to it. It had happened anyway, the way rust happens, quietly, in places you don't inspect.

"The map says this route ends at the pass," I said finally. "It doesn't say anything about what's on it."

Mara looked up sharply. Across the fire, her face was lit orange and unreadable, and I thought she was going to say something cutting—she had a talent for it—but instead she said:

"My father used to talk about you. Before he left. He said there was an engineer who walked the dead lines every year, checking on them, keeping them honest. He said if anything ever happened to him upriver, that man would come." She paused. "I thought he was being sentimental. He wasn't, was he. He knew you'd come."

"I didn't come for him."

"No," she said. "You came for us."

The stove ticked. Outside, the storm worked at the viaduct, and the viaduct held, and I thought about how many things in this world hold only because somebody keeps checking on them.

Ruth stood, stretched, and went to the doorway to watch the weather. After a minute she said, quietly, in the voice of a woman reading a signpost:

"Storm's breaking. And we've got company."

I joined her at the door. Far downstream, on the rail grade we'd climbed that morning, lights were moving. Three of them. Steady, spaced, disciplined—not the wandering beams of lost hikers.

Nobody walks the Kestrel grade at night in a storm unless they're following someone.

And nobody knew this route except the man who'd written it off.
