## 6. Snowline

The snow found us on the third morning above the cache, fine as sifted flour, drifting down through the pines without wind. Pip caught it on her tongue like it was a gift. Ruth pulled her collar up and said nothing. Jude stopped walking and looked north the way a man looks at a door he knows is about to close.

"Early," he said. "Weeks early."

"How long do we have?" I asked.

He turned his face up to the gray. I watched him do the arithmetic behind his eyes—the pass elevation, the drift lines, whatever private ledger he kept of this mountain's moods. "Four days to Mercy Pass if the weather holds. Two if it doesn't."

It didn't hold. By noon the flakes had gone fat and wet, then small and dry, and by the time we made camp in the lee of a granite shoulder, the world had gone quiet in that particular way snow quiets things—swallowing our footprints behind us as fast as we made them, erasing us from the mountain even while we were still on it.

I should have slept. Instead I sat with my father's journal open on my knees inside the tent, reading it again by headlamp, because I'd read it three times now and it kept not making sense.

*Day 19. The vein is real. More than real—it runs like a river under the ridge. Halvard wants to flag it, mark coordinates, radio the consortium at first chance. He says we'll all be rich men. He says they'll build a spur line within two years.*

*Day 20. I told him no.*

*Day 21. Halvard took four men and half our food downriver before dawn. Left us the tents, the instruments, and a note that said I was a sentimental old man throwing away a fortune for a principle.*

That was where the handwriting changed. Not just the ink—my father had switched pencils somewhere around day nine—but the pressure of it, the letters going small and cramped, a man writing against something.

*They will come back with company. Consortium men don't file claims; they take them. If anyone reads this: the route out is the engineer's route. The one he erased. Trust the lines that aren't on any map, because those are the only ones the consortium hasn't bought.*

*Trust the engineer who erased them.*

I read that sentence until the words stopped being words.

My father had spent twenty years teaching me that maps were contracts, that every line drawn was a promise someone intended to keep or break, and that the railway consortium broke every contract it ever signed. He'd built his whole expedition on old survey charts—charts, I realized now with a cold sliding feeling, that had come from *him*. From Jude Okafor. The hermit who maintained dead rail lines. The man walking thirty feet ahead of us all day, silent as the snow, carrying a pack he never let anyone else touch.

He hadn't just known the way. My father had *asked him*. Had trusted him enough to bet all our lives on a map that didn't officially exist.

And I had spent six days blaming him for it.

* * *

We found the body the next afternoon, in a hollow below the ridgeline where the snow lay shallow against a shelf of rock.

Ruth saw him first—a dark shape wrong against the white—and she was moving before any of us, dropping to her knees, hands already working. But there was nothing to work on. He'd been dead for weeks. The cold had kept him, the way the cold keeps everything up here, honest and preserved.

Halvard. It had to be. The journal described him: big man, red beard, a scar through one eyebrow. The frost had taken the color from everything but the scar.

"He didn't freeze," Ruth said quietly. She'd turned him gently, professionally, and her voice had the flatness she got when she was keeping herself together with procedure. "There's a wound here. Under the ribs. Blade, probably. And look—" she pointed, careful, clinical, "—he's holding this."

A canvas pouch, frozen stiff in the dead man's grip. Inside: ore samples, maybe eight pounds of glittering black stone shot through with veins of something that caught the light like oil catches fire. And folded beneath them, a consortium claim marker, the kind they drive into ground like a flag—except this one was unstruck, unused, wrapped in its oilcloth like something a man had decided, at the end, not to plant.

"He was carrying it *out*," Pip said. Her voice cracked in the middle. She was seventeen and trying very hard not to be. "He was taking it back to them."

"Or running from them," Ruth said. "With proof of what they did."

Nobody answered that. The snow ticked down through the branches. Somewhere below us, the river went on talking to itself under its skin of ice, patient as ever.

Jude crouched beside the body for a long time without touching anything. Then he looked up-slope, toward the high country, and I watched him read the ground the way other people read print.

"There were more than one," he said. "See the tracks under the drift? At least three sets, heading for the pass. One of them dragging." He stood, brushing snow from his knees. "Whatever happened here, it happened fast, and the rest of them ran."

"Toward the mine," I said.

"Toward the mine," he agreed.

"And the consortium crew following *us*?"

He met my eyes. For once he didn't deflect, didn't retreat into that flat surveyor's calm. What I saw in his face was worse than deflection. It was confirmation.

"They're not following your father," he said. "Your father's past helping anyone. They're following the samples. And the journal. And us." A pause. "Cleanup crews don't leave witnesses, Mara. That's the whole job."

* * *

That night we burned nothing and buried Halvard under a cairn of stones, because Ruth insisted and because none of us could think of a reason not to. Pip said a few words she clearly improvised and immediately regretted. I put the claim marker in my pack—not the ore, we left the ore with him, let the mountain have its due—but the marker, because it was evidence, because it was the closest thing to a will my father's enemy had left, and because I wanted the consortium to know someday that I knew.

Then I took the journal to Jude's fire.

He was sitting alone at the edge of camp, the way he always did, positioned exactly between us and the dark. I dropped the journal in his lap.

"Page forty-one," I said. "Read it."

He read it. I watched his face while he did, and there—there it was, the thing I'd been hunting for six days, the guilt I'd been sure was hiding under all that silence. But what moved across his features wasn't guilt. It was grief. Old grief, worn smooth like river stone, the kind you carry so long it stops hurting and starts defining you.

"He wrote to me twice a year," Jude said finally. "Did you know that? Every spring and every fall. Letters about rail grades and snow loads. Never once mentioned an expedition." His thumb moved over the cover of the journal. "Until last fall. Last letter said: *I've found something in the Kestrels worth more than the railway, and less than my daughter's forgiveness. If anything happens to me, find Okafor. He'll know what to bury.*"

"That's why you came," I said. "Not the radio silence. The letter."

"The letter." He closed the journal and held it out to me, and when I took it his hand stayed on it a moment longer than necessary. "Mara, I erased those lines for a reason. Twelve years ago I mapped every trestle, every grade, every cut in this range for the consortium, and I watched them use my work to gut three towns and buy a senator. So I walked back in and I unmade it. Every route. Every crossing. I spent ten years making sure nothing I drew could be used to hurt anyone again." He looked into the fire. "And then your father asked me to draw him one last line anyway. Because he said he needed it for something worth saving."

"And you believed him."

"I believed *him*," Jude said. "Not the mountain. Not the ore. Him."

The fire popped. Up-valley, faint but unmistakable, came a sound I'd been hearing for two days and pretending was wind: a whistle. Two notes, low, repeated. Someone signaling someone else in the dark.

Pip appeared at the edge of the firelight, radio clutched to her chest, face bloodless. "I picked up a transmission," she said. "Short-range. They're close—they're closer than close, they're *behind us*, maybe two miles. And Mara—" her voice shook, steadied, "—they were using our names. They said 'the girl has the journal.' They know we have it. They know exactly what we're carrying."

Jude was already on his feet, kicking snow over the fire, and the speed of him, the certainty, told me everything the last six days hadn't: this man had run from people like this before, and he remembered how.

"Break camp," he said. "Now. We move for the pass tonight, in the dark, off the trail."

"In this?" Ruth said, gesturing at the snowfall thickening out of the black.

"In this," Jude said. "Because by tomorrow night the passes start closing, and the day after, the only thing sealed up here besides the mine—" he looked at each of us in turn, and his voice went quiet and hard, "—will be us."

I shoved the journal deep into my pack, next to the claim marker, and for the first time since the riverhead I wasn't angry at the engineer who erased the lines.

I was angry at the men who'd taught him why he had to.
