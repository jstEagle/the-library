## 7. The Men from the Consortium

Jude counted them by their fires.

Three points of light in the valley below the ridge, spaced the way soldiers spaced them—close enough to signal, far enough to survive a single bad night. He'd been watching for an hour from the shoulder of the mountain while the others slept under a lean-to of spruce boughs, and in that hour he'd learned everything he needed to know about the men following them upriver.

Rescue parties didn't post watches. Rescue parties burned big fires and sang to keep their spirits up and left their gear scattered like an apology. These men banked their coals low, strung their packs on lines off the ground, and rotated sentries with a discipline that belonged to payroll, not mercy.

"They're not looking for survivors," he said when Mara crawled out beside him, her breath smoking in the dark.

She followed his gaze down to the fires. "You're sure."

"Look at the spacing. Look at the watch rotations." He handed her the binoculars from his pack—the one luxury he allowed himself, brass-cased, older than Pip. "Third fire from the left. The man standing up just now. See the rifle?"

She saw it. He watched her shoulders change, the way a bridge changes under load.

"My father's expedition went silent eleven days ago," she said quietly. "The consortium sends four armed men and no medics?"

"The consortium sends exactly what it sends." Jude took the glasses back. "I've spent nine years pretending they didn't know my name. I'm done pretending."

* * *

They made the fire lookout at dusk the next day, because there was nowhere else to go.

It stood on a granite knob above the tree line—an old Forest Service tower, sixty feet of creosoted timber and glass, its cab listing slightly east like a tired man leaning on a fence. The stairs were gone below the third landing; someone had pulled them decades ago when the service abandoned the line. But Jude had climbed it once, years back, on a day he'd rather not remember, and he remembered the iron rungs bolted into the corner posts, rusted but true.

"We can see both approaches from up there," he told them. "And nobody comes up those rungs quietly."

Ruth looked at the tower, then at him. "How long do we hold it?"

"As long as we need to. Pip's radio is dead weight until we reach the pass relay anyway—" He stopped. He'd been about to say something else, something about how the tower's height might catch a stray signal, and he caught himself because Pip was already grinning.

"It won't be dead weight," Pip said. "I fixed the antenna coupling at the cache yesterday. If I get thirty feet of elevation and clear sky, I can raise the valley station at Corben's Ford. They keep a weather watch on that band."

"You said the set was ruined," Mara said.

"I said it was *broken*. There's a difference." Pip shouldered the radio like it weighed nothing, which it didn't—it weighed forty pounds of bakelite and batteries—and started up the rungs before anyone could argue. That was Pip all over. Ruth called it recklessness. Jude was starting to understand it was something closer to faith: the kid had decided these people were worth climbing for, and acted accordingly, without waiting for permission.

Ruth went next, testing each rung with a small hammer she carried for exactly this purpose, listening to the ring of sound metal versus the dull knock of rot. Then Mara. Jude came last, hauling the packs on a rope, and stood in the cab as the last light died over the Kestrels and the whole broken spine of the range turned the color of old iron.

From up here he could see the route he'd drawn twenty years ago and never walked since—the line of his map running through the passes like a scar through skin. Somewhere north, past the saddle they'd cross tomorrow, his father's crew had laid track that lasted six winters before the mountain shrugged it off. Somewhere south, the consortium's men were climbing toward them with rifles and a mandate.

He wondered, not for the first time, whether a map could be a kind of sin. Whether drawing a line through wilderness was the same as promising the world would follow it.

Below, in the blue dark, the three fires had become two. And then, an hour later, one.

"They've consolidated," Mara whispered. "That means they know where we are."

"It means they're done being careful," Jude said.

* * *

They came at midnight, and they came quiet, and they still weren't quiet enough.

Jude heard the first rung flex—a creak of iron under a careful boot, forty feet down in the black—and had his hand on Mara's shoulder before her eyes opened. He put a finger to his lips and moved to the stairwell opening with the crowbar he'd taken from the cache, and by then the first man was already through the hatch.

He was younger than Jude expected. Clean-shaven, dry-eyed, wearing a consortium field jacket with the insignia cut off. He held a pistol low and professional and said, in a voice pitched for conversation rather than command:

"Evening. Nobody needs this to go badly."

Behind Jude, in the dark of the cab, Pip made a sound—a small intake of breath, the sound of a kid realizing the story has teeth—and the man's head turned toward it, and that was the whole mistake, the whole terrible economy of the moment. Because Jude moved on the turn, crowbar sweeping for the gun hand, and connected hard enough to send the pistol spinning into the corner, and then the second man was coming up the rungs behind the first and the cab was full of noise and elbows and Mara shouting Ruth's name, and somewhere in it all—

Somewhere in it all, a third man who had never come up the stairs at all simply reached through the open window on the windward side and took Pip by the collar of his coat.

Jude saw it happen the way you see a span fail: too late, all at once, inevitable. One minute Pip was pressed against the far wall clutching the radio; the next the kid was hauled half through the window frame, boots scrabbling on the boards, forty pounds of radio clattering loose across the floor, and a voice from outside said, calm as a foreman reading a gauge:

"That's far enough, engineer."

The fighting stopped the way water stops when the tap closes. The first man retrieved his pistol and rubbed his wrist and did not point it at anyone, which was somehow worse than pointing it. The second man held the window. And out on the catwalk, silhouetted against the stars, the third man held Pip over open air with one fist in the coat and the patience of a man being paid by the hour.

"I'll introduce myself," the first man said. "Name's Corliss. I work for people who are very sorry about your friend Voss, and very interested in what he found, and completely uninterested in anything else. Including you. Including the girl. Including the child."

"He's sixteen," Ruth said.

"Then he's old enough to fall." Corliss said it without heat, which was the worst thing about him. "Here's the shape of the evening. We want the survey data. Voss kept notes—we know he kept notes, he was a meticulous man—and we know you have them, because we watched you dig up his cache two days ago. You give us the journal and any maps, and the boy comes inside, and we part ways at the pass. You refuse, and—" he nodded toward the window, toward the small dangling weight of a human being, "—accidents happen in mountains. Everyone knows that. No one will ever ask why."

Jude's heart was going like a trip-hammer, and underneath the fear was something colder and more lucid, the way it always got for him at height, at speed, at the moment of load transfer. He thought: *they don't know about the mine entrance. They don't know Voss rigged it. They think the journal is the prize.*

He thought: *the journal is not the only copy of what matters.*

And then he thought about the map. His map. Twenty years of walking dead lines alone so that no one else would have to, twenty years of measuring his life in miles because miles couldn't leave him, and folded in oilcloth inside his jacket, the only document in the world that showed every trestle, every tunnel, every forgotten approach through the Kestrel Range—including the one his own father had died surveying, including the route that had led Elias Voss to the vein that was going to get everyone in this tower killed.

Corliss watched him think. "You're the cartographer," he said. "Okafor. We know your work. It's good work. It's the reason any of this is possible." A thin smile. "Give us the maps, engineer. All of them."

Pip's breath was fogging fast out there in the cold, small and quick, a signal flag of a life. Below the catwalk was sixty feet of granite and starlight.

Jude reached inside his jacket.

Mara's hand closed on his wrist. "Jude." Barely a whisper. "That's the only—"

"I know what it is," he said.

He knew better than she did. He knew every contour of it the way he knew his own hands. He had redrawn it four times over the years, refining, correcting, keeping it alive the way you keep a grave tended. It was the closest thing he had to a self. Handing it over felt less like losing a possession than like being unwritten.

But Pip's fog-breath was getting faster, and the arm holding the kid wasn't getting tired, and Jude Okafor had spent nine years learning the difference between things that could be rebuilt and things that couldn't.

He pulled the map out—oilcloth, string-tied—and held it over the stove in the center of the cab, over the cold iron plate where a fire hadn't burned in fifty years.

"Inside first," he said. "Then you get it. Not before. And if anyone follows us past the pass, I promise you the consortium will spend ten years and a hundred men finding routes this map took me a lifetime to learn—and they will find them, because I'll make sure of it, personally, for as long as I'm breathing. My knowledge walks out of these mountains with me either way. The paper is the discount. Take the discount."

For a long moment the only sound was wind and Pip's breathing.

Then Corliss nodded at the window man, and Pip came stumbling backward through the frame into Ruth's arms, and Jude set the map on the cold stove and stepped away from it and did not look at it again, because if he looked at it he would not let it go.

Corliss picked it up, weighed it in one hand like a man judging ore, and tucked it inside his jacket.

"Wise," he said. "We'll be behind you to the pass. Don't hurry on our account." At the hatch he paused. "Voss found something worth more than railroads, engineer. Men like you always end up choosing which side of that gets buried. Choose carefully."

Then they were gone, down the rungs, three lantern-points shrinking into the dark, and the tower was quiet except for Ruth checking Pip's ribs with quick competent hands and Pip saying, twice, *I'm fine, I'm fine,* in the voice of someone who was not fine.

Mara stood by the window, staring after the lights.

"He has my father's journal," she said. "Everything Dad wrote. Everything he—" Her voice cracked and reformed. "We gave them everything."

"No," Jude said.

She turned. In the starlight through the glass her face was fierce and wet and young.

"The journal's real," he said. "I gave them the truth. But the truth isn't the whole of it. There's a page missing—the last page, the one with the mine entrance and the charge lines. Your father tore it out before we ever reached the cache. I found it sewn into the binding of my own field book three days ago." He met her eyes. "Elias Voss trusted me with it. 'The engineer who erased the lines,' he wrote. He knew they'd come for the journal. He wanted them to get it."

Mara stared at him. "Then what's left for us?"

Jude looked north, toward the pass called Mercy, where the high country waited white and blind under the coming snow, and where a dying man was holding a door shut with his last strength.

"A burial," he said. "Ours to finish."

* * *

They left the tower an hour before dawn, moving fast and light along the ridge while the consortium's fires slept below, and the first flakes of the season came down around them like static, soft and early and wrong, weeks ahead of schedule.

Jude walked at the front with the torn page against his chest and no map at all in his jacket, and found—to his surprise—that his legs knew the way regardless. Some things, it seemed, you couldn't surrender even if you tried.

Behind him, small and steady in the dark, he could hear Pip counting steps aloud to keep the fear away, and Ruth answering each count like a heartbeat, and Mara saying nothing at all, walking like a woman carrying a match into a room full of dynamite.

The snow thickened. The pass opened ahead of them, a black notch in the white shoulder of the world.

And somewhere beyond it, faint on the wind, came a sound Jude hadn't heard in twenty years: the deep, patient groan of old timbers settling under weight they were never meant to hold again.
