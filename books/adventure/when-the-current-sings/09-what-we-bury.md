## 9. What We Bury

The fuse was a length of surveyor's cord soaked in lamp oil, and it would burn for maybe four minutes. Jude knew because he'd timed everything in his life by burning things—candles down to stubs, matches to the quick, the slow consumption of a man's own patience.

"Say it again," Mara said behind him. Her voice was raw from the climb, from her father's body they'd wrapped in canvas and left where the mountain could hold him. "Say what you're going to do."

"I'm going to bury it." He knelt at the mouth of the adit, the old mine entrance Voss had rigged with charges and never lit. The vein glittered in the headlamp's spill like something alive, like the current that sang under the Kestrel Range and had sung there since before any of them were born. "The route, the ore, all of it. The consortium gets nothing. Nobody gets anything. The mountain keeps its silence."

"And your map?"

He didn't answer. The map was folded against his chest, inside his coat, where it had ridden for nine days—the last copy, the master, forty years of abandoned lines and dead trestles and the secret ways through the range that only he had ever walked. His life's work. The thing he'd fled into and been made by.

Ruth stood at the tree line with Pip's radio, watching the switchbacks below. The consortium crew was two hours back and closing. They'd taken Pip once already; they wouldn't get the chance again.

"Jude." Mara crouched beside him. Up close her eyes were her father's eyes, and that was unbearable. "Four minutes. Then we run."

He touched the flame to the cord.

* * *

They ran.

The pass called Mercy lived up to its name the way a knife lives up to being sharp—it got you through, but it cut the whole way. Snow came in sideways off the ridge, the first real snowfall, fat flakes that meant the passes were closing behind them and ahead of them both. Jude counted strides because counting was what he did instead of praying. Two hundred strides to the old service trail. Three hundred to the trestle.

The trestle at Wolf's Tooth had nearly killed Pip once already. Now it stood between them and the descent, a hundred and forty feet of rotting timber over the gorge, and the charges going off behind them sounded like the mountain clearing its throat.

The boom came as they reached the near end. Jude turned once. Above the mine, the whole shoulder of the ridge shrugged and slid, tons of rock folding down over the adit, over the vein, over the route no one would ever find again without a map—and the only map left in the world was against his chest, getting wet.

"It's done," Mara said. She wasn't looking back. She was watching him. "It's done, Jude. It's over."

It wasn't over. Below them, on the switchbacks, torchlight moved. Six men, maybe seven, climbing fast, and between Jude's party and the valley lay one hundred and forty feet of timber that hadn't been inspected in five years.

"We cross single file," he said. "My weight first. I'll test each span. Mara, you step exactly where I step. Ruth—"

A shot cracked off the rocks above them. Not aimed at them—a warning, or an argument among the men below about whether warnings mattered.

"Ruth takes Pip across first," Jude finished. "Go."

* * *

The trestle sang under them. That was the worst part—that it sang, a low groaning chord of old wood and iron bolts, the same note it had held five years ago when Jude had walked it alone with a clipboard and a conscience. He'd written *condemn* in the margin of his report. The company had filed the report and done nothing, because nobody rode this line anymore, because the line was dead, because Jude Okafor had spent twenty years making sure the lines he loved stayed dead.

Ruth went first with Pip, quick and light, and the structure held its breath and let them. Then Mara stepped out onto the first span, and Jude came behind her, testing each board with his heel before she trusted it with hers.

Halfway across, the wind changed.

It came down the gorge in a long white gust, and it took the snow and turned it horizontal, and it hit the trestle like a hand hitting a table. The whole structure leaned. Somewhere below them a cable parted with a sound like a rifle shot, and the third span—the one Jude's report had flagged, the one with *condemn* in the margin—dropped four inches and stopped, held by nothing but habit.

"Mara—" 

She was already moving, already running the last thirty feet, because she'd understood before he said it that the choice was no longer careful steps or careful anything. She ran, and he ran, and the span let go beneath her like a floor giving up.

She didn't scream. That was the thing he would remember afterward—not screaming, just her hand shooting out and catching the broken stringer, her boots swinging over a hundred and forty feet of dark, snow whirling up past her face out of the gorge.

Jude dropped to his knees at the edge. Below him the gorge breathed snow and shadow. Above, torchlight was coming up the switchbacks faster now, drawn by the noise.

There was a moment—one breath, maybe less—when everything he was stood very still and looked at itself. Forty years of mapping. The last copy of the only map. The route buried, the vein buried, the work of his whole life reduced to paper against his ribs, and the consortium men close enough now that he could hear their voices arguing on the wind. If he went down after her, he went down carrying the map. If he sent it up instead—

He took the map out of his coat.

He didn't think about it. Thinking was for men with time. He struck his last match against the frozen stringer, and he touched it to the corner of the paper, and he held it there while the fire ate inward—forty years of lines, every secret way through the Kestrel Range, curling black and rising as sparks into the snow. The men on the switchbacks saw it. Of course they saw it. A fire on a condemned trestle in a whiteout, burning the only thing they'd climbed all this way to take.

He heard someone shout. He dropped the burning remnant into the gorge, where it spun down like a dying star, and then he swung himself over the edge.

* * *

The descent was not climbing. Climbing had rules. This was negotiation with a cliff, hand over hand down rotten pilings, snow filling his collar, Mara fifteen feet below him clinging to a cross-brace with arms that were shaking and would stop shaking soon.

"Jude." Her voice came up thin through the wind. "You burned it. Tell me you didn't—you didn't burn it for me."

"I'll tell you at the bottom."

"You *idiot*, that was your—"

"Move your left foot six inches right," he said. "There's a bolt head. Trust me. I put that bolt there."

She found it. He watched her find it, watched her boot settle on steel he'd driven himself at nineteen years old, working his first summer on the line, and something in his chest cracked open that he suspected would never close again.

He reached her at the eighty-foot mark, where a piling leaned out over the void at an angle that made the whole gorge feel tilted. He got his arm around her waist and her arm over his shoulders, and then he did the arithmetic he'd been avoiding: eighty feet up a rotten structure, carrying another person, in a storm, with seven men above them who had lost their prize and might want a witness instead.

"Can you hold on?" he asked.

"To you?"

"To me."

"Yes," she said, and there was no argument left in it.

He climbed. It was not graceful and it was not fast and several times the trestle groaned so deeply he was sure it was the last sound either of them would hear. But the trestle had been built by men like him, stubborn men driving bolts into stone because the drawing said so, and it held long enough to be sorry about it later. At the top of the embankment Ruth and Pip were waiting, hauling them over the edge, and Pip was crying and talking at the same time, and Ruth's hands were steady as she checked Mara's fingers for frostbite and found Jude's hands shaking too hard to check his own.

Below them, on the switchbacks, the torches had stopped.

Jude looked down through the snow. The lead man stood at the base of the trestle, looking up at the burned gap in the spans, at the gorge where a map's ashes had gone out somewhere in the dark water. Then the man turned, and the torches turned with him, and began the long climb back down toward the valley, toward nothing.

"He saw it burn," Pip whispered. "He saw the whole thing."

"The mountain keeps its silence," Jude said. His voice came out ruined. "Now so do I."

Mara's hand found his in the dark. Her fingers were freezing and her grip was not.

"The passes are closing," Ruth said quietly, nodding up-valley, where the snow was thickening from weather into wall. "Whatever we're going to be to each other, we'd better decide it walking."

Jude shouldered the pack. Mara's arm went around his waist, his arm around her shoulders, and the four of them turned downhill together into the first real snowfall of the year, leaving the trestle groaning behind them in the wind like something finally allowed to rest.

Ahead, miles below, the valley lights were coming on.

One mile at a time, Jude started counting again—but for the first time in forty years, he wasn't counting away from anything.
