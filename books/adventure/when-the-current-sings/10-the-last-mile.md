## 10. The Last Mile

**JUDE**

The snow came down like a verdict, and we walked out from under it.

I carried Mara on my back for the first six miles of the descent, her arms looped over my collarbones, her breath fogging past my ear in slow, even pulses. She'd broken nothing in the fall—Ruth checked twice, hands sure despite the tremor she tried to hide—but her ankle was a ruin and the gorge had taken something out of her that walking wouldn't fix. So I carried her. It was the easiest weight I'd ever borne, which frightened me more than the trestle had.

Behind us, the pass closed. I didn't look back. You don't look back at a door you've nailed shut.

Pip walked ahead with the radio strapped to their chest like a shield, calling intervals into static that was slowly, grudgingly becoming signal as we dropped below the ridgeline. Ruth brought up the rear, counting our steps aloud in a medic's murmur, keeping Mara's pulse honest. Four strangers who weren't strangers anymore, moving down through the first real snow of the season while behind us the Kestrel Range swallowed everything—the vein, the route, my map, Voss's grave, and whatever the consortium men had come to claim.

They'd turned back two days ago. I'd stood on a granite shelf above their camp and burned the last copy of my life's work in an open flame, page by page, letting the wind carry the ash down into their valley so there could be no mistake about what it meant. *The route is dead,* the smoke said. *Go home.* By morning they were gone, and the mountain kept its silence, and so did I.

Mara stirred against my shoulder. "Put me down."

"You'll fall."

"I'll fall slower than you will." But she didn't fight it when I eased her onto a fallen trunk by the trail, and she let Ruth rewrap the ankle without complaint, jaw tight, eyes on the river below us where it came out of the gorge and widened and went silver in the flat light.

"That's the Kestrel," Pip said, like an introduction.

"I know what it's called," Mara said. Then, quieter: "My father used to say rivers are just mountains learning to let go."

Nobody answered that. Some sentences you carry instead.

We made the valley town at dusk on the third day—a scatter of roofs and smoke along the rail spur where the line died years ago, one hotel, one diner, one depot with weeds growing through the platform boards. The consortium's survey stakes were already being pulled up by men who wouldn't say why. The claims were worthless now. A route no one can reach is a rumor, and rumors don't hold title.

The hotel keeper looked at the four of us—mud to the eyebrows, frostbitten, one of us carried—and asked no questions, which is the local currency in towns like this. He gave us four rooms and a pot of coffee that tasted like engine grease, and it was the best thing I'd ever drunk.

* * *

**MARA**

I slept fourteen hours and woke to the sound of Ruth arguing with someone downstairs.

By the time I got there, favoring the ankle, it was over. Ruth stood at the hotel desk with a leather bag open, laying out instruments on the wood—forceps, a stethoscope gone gray with age, a folding scale—with the particular defiance of a woman daring the world to object. The hotel keeper had mentioned, apparently, that the town's doctor left in the spring and nobody replaced him.

"They struck me off the registry," Ruth said, not looking at me. "Said I falsified a report. Said I put a mine's production numbers above a man's life." Her hand paused over the stethoscope. "They were right about the report. They were wrong about which numbers mattered."

"So reopen," I said.

She finally looked at me. "Here?"

"Here. There's nobody else."

She closed the bag slowly, and when she lifted it off the counter her shoulders were different—straighter, or maybe just set down from something heavy. "There's a building on the corner with a faded cross on it. Looked like it used to be a clinic."

"It did," the hotel keeper said from the doorway. "Rent's cheap. Roof leaks."

"Everything worth having leaks," Ruth said, and walked out to go look at it.

Pip found me on the porch an hour later, sitting with my foot propped on a crate, watching the river. They sat down without asking, radio still slung across their chest out of habit, though there was nothing left to call.

"My mom's in Corvane," Pip said. "Two hundred miles east. I ran because—" They stopped. Started again. "Because she remarried and the new house had rules and I've never been good at rules. I thought the expedition would be different. Radio doesn't care whose kid you are."

"How old are you?"

"Sixteen in March."

I looked at this child who had crossed the Kestrel Range in early snow and held a lifeline open with bare hands and frozen fingers, and I thought about my father, who had also been sixteen once, and run toward mountains instead of away from them.

"Corvane has a rail line," I said. "The spur here connects. There's a freight that runs Tuesdays."

Pip's face did something complicated—hope and terror fighting for the same ground. "You'd come with me? To—to introduce me? In case she—"

"I'll come," I said. "Jude too, if he'll come. And Ruth can write down your medical history, which is apparently extensive."

Pip laughed, wet-eyed, and bumped their shoulder against mine, and stayed there, leaning, like a compass finding north.

* * *

**JUDE**

On the fourth morning I walked to the depot out of habit and stood on the platform where the rails ended in rust and weeds, and I realized I didn't know how far anything was anymore.

That was the truth of it, the thing I'd been circling since the fire on the granite shelf. For twenty years I had measured my life in miles—miles of closed line walked and logged, miles between supply drops, miles between myself and every person who might ask me a question I couldn't answer with a number. My maps had been a way of holding the world at arm's length: if I could chart it, I didn't have to live in it. Burn the map, and what's left?

What's left is a man standing on a dead platform with no idea how far it is to anywhere, feeling—for the first time in two decades—not empty but *open*. Like country that hasn't been surveyed yet.

Mara found me there. She walked with a stick now, cut from birch, and she leaned on it and looked down the dead line the way I used to look at living ones.

"The Tuesday freight," she said. "Pip told you?"

"Told me."

"We leave tomorrow, if the weather holds."

I nodded. We stood in the cold together, and the river talked to itself below the embankment—that low continuous voice it had kept the whole journey, the one I'd stopped hearing somewhere around Wolf's Tooth because I'd been listening to other things. To people.

"I spent my whole life measuring," I said. "Distances. Grades. Load limits. How much weight a span will take before it gives." I turned to face her. "I don't know how to measure this. Whatever this is. Us. There's no unit for it."

"No," she agreed. "There isn't."

"And that should terrify me. It would have, a year ago. Six months ago. Last week."

"But?"

"But I burned the map," I said. "So I'm done pretending I know what's coming. I'm asking you to walk into it with me anyway. Not because I know the route." I shook my head. "Because there isn't one. Because for the first time in my life I'd rather not know, with you, than know alone."

Mara looked at me for a long moment. Snow started again—light, unhurried, the kind that means the passes have sealed and the mountain has said its last word for the season.

"My father wrote about you," she said. "In the journal. 'The engineer who erased the lines.' He trusted you because you understood that some things shouldn't be found." Her voice caught, steadied. "He'd have liked this. You, asking me to get lost together."

"Is that a yes?"

She smiled, and it was the first time I'd seen it reach all the way up, grief and all. "That's a yes. But I'm setting the pace. My ankle's wrecked and you walk like a man being chased by his own thoughts."

"I am."

"Not anymore." She took my hand. Her fingers were cold and certain. "Come on. Ruth's cooking breakfast, Pip's trying to raise Corvane on a dead frequency out of pure stubbornness, and the freight leaves tomorrow. We've got one last mile to walk before any of it starts."

We walked it slowly, the two of us, down along the river as it came out of the mountains and spread wide and easy across the valley floor, running bright under the new snow. All winter it had been silent under ice up in the gorge, choked and dark. Down here it moved free, and if you listened—really listened, the way I was only now learning to—it wasn't silence at all.

It was singing.

And for the first time in my life, I didn't need to know how many miles it had left.
