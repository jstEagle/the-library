## 1. Miles, Not Years

Zephyrine Sandoval measured her life in miles the way other people measured it in birthdays.

Four hundred and twelve miles from the last dig. Ninety from the one before that. Six thousand from the house in the capital where she'd grown up under a framed photograph of her mother shaking hands with a president, and eleven from the rooming house in Puerto Cobre she'd sworn she'd never go back to and hadn't, not once, not even to collect the box of things she'd left behind like a snake leaving skin.

She kept the tally in the back of her field notebook, small figures in pencil, updated every time she moved on. It was a private arithmetic. Nobody asked about it because nobody knew it existed, and that was the point. A person who counted miles was a person who could always answer the question *where are you* without ever having to answer the harder one underneath it.

The current number was thirty-one.

Thirty-one miles inland from the coast, at the end of a road that turned to corduroy planking and then to mud, sat the town of Estero Salado — population somewhere north of two hundred if you counted the fishermen's dogs, which the census did not but which Zephyrine privately did, because the dogs were better company than most of the contractors she'd worked for. The marsh around it spread out flat and silver-green to the horizon, cut by channels that moved overnight like slow animals turning in their sleep. In the wet season half the peninsula drowned. In the dry season the other half threatened to.

It was, in other words, exactly the kind of place Zephyrine chose on purpose.

The contract was a salvage survey — a pre-construction assessment for a seawall the government wanted to build and would probably never fund, commissioned by an engineering firm that needed a name on a report more than it needed accurate data. She'd taken it because it paid in full up front and because the site was a midden: four thousand years of oyster shells and broken pottery and fish bones layered like a cake nobody had ordered, slowly eroding into the channel. Middens didn't make headlines. Middens didn't get named after anyone. A midden was just people, eating, for a very long time, and then stopping.

Zephyrine had developed a strong professional preference for things that stopped quietly.

She'd been in Estero Salado nine days. Her crew consisted of one local teenager named Beto who showed up when he felt like it and dug with the focused violence of someone taking out a grudge on the earth, and a retired schoolteacher named Doña Rufina who came for the conversation and stayed for the shade of the tarp. Between the three of them they had opened two test pits and catalogued three hundred and forty artifacts, none of which would change the world, all of which were exactly what they appeared to be.

That last part mattered more than Zephyrine ever said out loud.

She knelt in Pit 2 with a hand trowel, working the interface between the shell layer and the darker soil beneath it, when the thought arrived — as it did most mornings, uninvited, punctual as the tide — that her mother would have done this differently. Imelda Sandoval would have had six students and a drone and a foundation grant and a documentary crew, and by now there would be a press release with the word *remarkable* in the second sentence. Zephyrine had a trowel and a teenager and a report due in November that four people would read.

Some mornings this felt like freedom. Some mornings it felt like the smallest possible life, lived deliberately, the way you might hold your breath.

Today it felt like neither. Today it just felt like Tuesday.

"Señora Zephy." Beto crouched at the edge of the pit, holding his phone at arm's length like it had bitten him. "There's a woman at the cantina asking for you. She says—" he squinted at the screen, where Doña Rufina had evidently texted him a transcription, "—she says she's from the Sandoval Foundation."

The trowel stopped moving.

Zephyrine looked at the profile of the pit wall instead of at the boy. The stratigraphy was clean. Shell, ash, shell, a lens of charcoal where somebody's fire had guttered out four millennia ago, shell again. Layers lay on layers and nothing argued with anything above or below it. That was the whole appeal of dirt, she'd come to understand. Dirt didn't inherit. Dirt didn't call.

"What does she want?" she asked.

Beto shrugged with his whole body. "She wouldn't say. She's been sitting there since eight. Doña Rufina gave her coffee so she'd stop looking at everyone."

Of course she had. The women of Estero Salado ran hospitality like a defense system.

"I'm working," Zephyrine said, and went back to the interface.

But her hands had changed. She noticed it the way you notice a current shift under a boat — nothing visible, everything altered. The trowel cut shallower. Her breathing sat higher in her chest. Because here was the thing about seven years of chosen exile: it only worked if the exile held. One visitor from the old world, sitting in a cantina with a folder and a foundation business card, and the whole careful architecture of distance started to groan like a dock at low tide.

Her mother's foundation. Not her mother. Imelda Sandoval herself communicated through institutions the way other people communicated through weather — remotely, massively, and with total indifference to whoever got soaked. If the foundation had sent someone, it meant paperwork. It meant a signature she'd refused to give seven years ago on some form or other, resurfacing like a cork. It meant, almost certainly, money she didn't want and obligations she liked less.

It did not mean her mother missed her. Zephyrine had learned that particular lesson at nineteen, thoroughly, and had never needed a refresher.

She finished the unit. Photographed it. Drew the profile in her notebook, adding the day's mileage figure to the running tally out of pure habit — still thirty-one; she hadn't gone anywhere, and the notebook didn't lie — and then, because the alternative was letting the woman sit in the cantina until dark growing roots, she walked into town.

* * *

The cantina smelled of fried plantain and diesel from the boats outside, and the woman from the foundation sat at the corner table with the posture of someone who had been trained to sit and had never once enjoyed it. Mid-forties. Linen blazer in a color that had probably been white that morning. A leather portfolio squared precisely to the table's edge.

"Ms. Sandoval," she said, standing, extending a hand.

"No," Zephyrine said.

The woman blinked. Around them the lunch crowd went on eating, incurious. In a town this size, a stranger in linen was entertainment enough for a week, but the locals had apparently already priced her in.

"I'm sorry?"

"You're looking for Dr. Sandoval's daughter. I'm not her." Zephyrine pulled out the opposite chair and sat down anyway, because walking away would take longer than this was going to. "I don't use the name. I haven't signed anything for the foundation in seven years. Whatever form you need, send it to the office and let it die in a drawer."

The woman recovered well, Zephyrine had to give her that. She'd been sent to remote places before; you could tell by the way she didn't flinch at the flies or the volume of the radio behind the bar.

"My name is Cora Mendez. I'm the expedition coordinator." She opened the portfolio. "I'm not here about a form."

Something in the flatness of the sentence made Zephyrine's hands go still on the tabletop.

"The Río Bravo expedition," Mendez said. "Your mother's survey of the colonial wreck sites in the upper gorge. I don't know how much reaches you out here."

"It doesn't reach me at all. That's the arrangement."

Mendez looked at her for a moment — a long, assessing look, the kind Zephyrine usually got from people deciding whether she was really as unimpressive as she presented. Then she slid a single sheet of paper across the table. A telegram, actual paper, actual telex formatting, the kind of thing the foundation used only when the satellite network failed and they'd had to route through the coastal relay station.

Three lines. Zephyrine read them twice.

**RIO BRAVO EXPEDITION MISSED SCHEDULED CHECK-IN 14TH. MISSED CHECK-IN 21ST. MISSED CHECK-IN YESTERDAY. NO RESPONSE ON WORKING FREQUENCIES. LAST KNOWN POSITION ABOVE THE GORGE MOUTH. SALVAGE INTERESTS MOVING INTO SECTOR. REQUEST FAMILY GUIDANCE.**

Family guidance. As if Imelda Sandoval were a policy question.

"She wouldn't miss check-ins," Mendez said quietly. "Not three. I've coordinated eleven expeditions for her. When she misses a window, there's a reason, and the reason is always in the message that follows within the hour. There's been no message. Nothing on any band. The last transmission we logged was eighteen days ago — routine position report, all nominal, she even joked about the cook burning the rice." Mendez's composure cracked, just at the corner of her mouth. "Then nothing."

Zephyrine stared at the telegram. The paper was cheap and slightly damp from the marsh air, the ink feathering at the edges, and somehow that made it worse than a phone call would have. Paper could be held. This could be held, and it weighed nothing, and it said her mother had been silent for twenty-two days above the gorge mouth while salvage interests moved into the sector.

Salvage interests. She knew what that meant without asking. It meant Aurelio Vance, or men like him — dredges and lawyers and divers paid by the hour, stripping wrecks down to the nails before any academic claim could be filed. Her mother had spent her whole career fighting that war from conference rooms and ministry offices, winning some rounds, losing most, always certain the system would bend if you pushed correctly.

And now the system's silence had swallowed her.

"She's fine," Zephyrine heard herself say. "She's Imelda Sandoval. She has a first mate, a fleet radio, a satellite beacon, and an ego the size of the watershed. Three missed check-ins means the equipment broke, or the schedule changed, or she decided the check-ins were beneath her. Which, honestly, is the most likely option."

"That may be true," Mendez said. "I've run that possibility myself, every hour, for three days." She closed the portfolio. "But the family guidance request isn't mine. It came from the river authority, through the ministry. They want to know whether to authorize a search before the salvage fleet's permits activate. The permits activate in six days. After that, the gorge is legally theirs to work, and a search becomes a trespass."

Six days. Six tides, though the telegram didn't say that; Zephyrine supplied it herself from some old childhood memory of her mother explaining tidal windows over breakfast, drawing the moon's pull in syrup on the table. Six windows, and then the river belonged to the dredges.

"And my mother's daughter," Mendez said, "is the only family listed. So I found the daughter." She paused. "Or the person who used to be."

The words should have stung. They landed instead like a diagnosis Zephyrine had already written for herself years ago and never shown anyone.

"I'm not going upriver," she said.

"I didn't ask you to." Mendez stood, buttoned the blazer, squared the portfolio under her arm. Everything precise, everything professional, everything screaming institutional calm over what Zephyrine could now see was barely managed fear. "I asked you to know. There's a difference. I've spent three weeks watching your mother refuse help because asking for it would look weak, and I'll be damned if I do the same thing to her blood." She put a card on the table. "The relay station in Puerto Cobre monitors the expedition band around the clock. Frequency's on the back. If you hear anything — anything at all —"

"I won't be listening."

"No," Mendez said. "You won't." And there was something almost gentle in how she said it, the way you confirm a fact you both know is temporary. She left money on the table for the coffee she hadn't drunk, nodded to Doña Rufina behind the counter like they were old friends, and walked out into the glare.

* * *

Zephyrine went back to the site. Of course she did. That was the whole discipline of it — you felt the pull and you put your hands back in the dirt, because the dirt didn't leave, and the dirt didn't go silent, and the dirt had never once in four thousand years needed anyone to come find it.

She opened Pit 3 in the afternoon. Beto dug like a man avenging something. Doña Rufina told a long story about the flood of '61 that Zephyrine had heard twice already and listened to fully anyway, because listening was easier than thinking.

At dusk she sat on the sea wall with her notebook and updated the tally. Thirty-one miles. Same as yesterday. Same as tomorrow, she'd assumed that morning.

The page stared up at her. Four hundred and twelve. Ninety. Six thousand. Eleven. Thirty-one. Seven years of distances, each one chosen, each one a door closed behind her, each one proof — to whom? she'd never quite answered — that she was not her mother's daughter in any way that required her to show up.

Except.

Except that at nineteen she had memorized the expedition frequencies without meaning to, the way you memorize a lullaby. Except that she could read tide tables the way other people read horoscopes, fluently, compulsively, a skill that existed for no reason except that a famous archaeologist had taught it to a bored child on a boat deck. Except that right now, holding a telegram about her mother's silence, the thing Zephyrine felt in her chest wasn't grief or duty or love, exactly. It was motion sickness. The specific nausea of a body that has spent seven years standing still being told the current has changed.

She took out the card. Cora Mendez, Expedition Coordinator, Sandoval Foundation. On the back, in neat block capitals: **EXPEDITION WATCH — 4187 kHz — CONTINUOUS.**

4187 kilohertz. She knew the band. She'd known it before she could ride a bicycle. Night after night in a dozen field camps, falling asleep to her parents' voices threading through static — position reports, weather, her father reading the shipping news aloud in his terrible Spanish until he died and the ritual became her mother's alone, one voice on one frequency, steady as a lighthouse, reaching every place Zephyrine had ever run to.

Her mother was on that frequency. Or her mother's absence was. Either way, it was the closest thing to her that existed within six hundred miles.

Zephyrine didn't decide anything. Deciding was for people whose lives happened in rooms. What she did was simpler and more damning: she looked at the marsh going dark, at the channel filling with the evening tide, at the little town where she'd planned to stay through November and maybe past it, and she caught herself calculating.

Puerto Cobre was ninety miles away. The mail steamer ran Thursdays. Today was Monday.

Three days. Six tides minus three.

Beto appeared beside the sea wall, drawn by whatever instinct makes teenagers materialize when adults are behaving strangely. He looked at the card in her hand, then at her face.

"Are you leaving?" he asked.

"No," Zephyrine said.

The tide turned below them, right on schedule, the whole silver marsh beginning its slow nightly rotation toward the sea. She watched it happen and understood, with the clarity she usually reserved for stratigraphy, that she had just lied for the first time in seven years about something that mattered.

In her notebook, under the column of miles, she wrote a new line. Not a distance. A frequency.

**4187.**

Then she closed the book, and the ache of all those chosen miles sat in her chest like ballast, and Thursday was coming whether she counted it or not.
