## 2. The Silent Frequency

The radio lived in the survey shed because nobody else wanted it.

It was a Hallicrafters S-38B, older than Zephyrine by two decades, with a bakelite case gone amber and a dial that glowed the color of old honey when it warmed up. It had come with the dig — left behind by some previous contractor who'd decided the marsh was quieter without voices in it — and for three months it had sat on a shelf between a box of plotting pins and a jar of fossilized rubber bands, doing nothing but gathering salt film.

Zephyrine had walked past it every day. She had not touched it once.

Now she stood in the doorway of the shed at eleven minutes past midnight, still dressed, boots muddy from the evening's last round of stakes, holding her notebook against her chest like something she was afraid might be taken from her. The number was written there in her own hand: **4187**. Kilohertz. The expedition band. The frequency Imelda Sandoval had used since before Zephyrine was born, back when it was just mother and daughter and a borrowed transmitter on the porch of some field station, calling check-ins across distances that felt romantic then, before they came to feel like the whole architecture of their estrangement.

She went in. She shut the door. She set the notebook down where she could see the number.

"You're being ridiculous," she said aloud, to no one, in the flat tone she used on herself when she caught herself performing an emotion instead of having one. "You're checking whether the equipment works. That's all. It's due diligence."

The lie tasted familiar now. Two lies in one day. At this rate she'd be unrecognizable by spring.

* * *

The radio took four minutes to warm up. She knew this because she'd read the manual as a child, sitting cross-legged on the floor of a field station in a country whose name she could no longer say without tasting river water, while her mother's hands moved over the dials with the certainty other mothers reserved for braiding hair. *Patience, Zeph. The tubes have to wake up. Everything worth hearing has to wake up first.*

She had hated that sentence at fourteen. She hated it now, standing in a salt-rotted shed at midnight, waiting for tubes to wake up so she could hear nothing.

Because that was the rational expectation: nothing. Static. The expedition had missed three scheduled check-ins — the telegram had said it in the clipped grammar of institutions, *EXPEDITION UPRIVER NONRESPONSIVE X3 CYCLES ADVISE*, as if her mother were a line item that had failed to reconcile — and three missed check-ins meant a dead radio, a dead battery bank, or a dead operator, and two of those three possibilities were survivable and one was not, and Zephyrine had spent the entire evening refusing to do the arithmetic.

Instead she did what she always did with unbearable things. She turned them into procedure.

Step one: confirm the frequency. Step two: log the result. Step three: decide nothing until morning.

The dial glowed. She tuned past the low murmur of a station playing boleros somewhere far south, past the whine of atmospheric noise, down into the shortwave bands where the world's official traffic lived — weather bulletins, time signals, the dry liturgy of numbers stations — and stopped at 4187.

Static.

Not even interesting static. No skip, no ghost voices bleeding through from another hemisphere. Just the marsh's own white noise, the sound of distance with nothing in it, like snowfall on an empty page.

"Good," she said. "Confirmed nonresponsive. Logged." And she reached to turn the dial down, and her hand stopped, because underneath the static — so faint she might have manufactured it out of longing, and God knew she'd manufactured worse out of less — there was a rhythm.

A carrier wave. Weak, wavering, but present. Someone, somewhere upriver, had a transmitter powered and keyed.

Zephyrine sat down on the crate in front of the radio very slowly, the way you sit down when your knees have made the decision independently of your judgment.

* * *

For twenty minutes she rode the signal, easing the tuning knob in increments fine enough to make her fingertips ache, chasing the carrier as it drifted with the temperature of whatever failing circuit was pushing it. The static breathed around it. Once, twice, she thought she heard structure inside the noise — the ghost of a voice, syllables worn down to texture — and twice she decided she'd imagined it, and the third time she didn't decide anything at all, she just leaned closer, close enough that her breath fogged the glass over the dial, and waited.

At 11:49 the drift slowed. The carrier steadied. And through the static, torn and reassembled like a photograph dropped in water, a voice said:

"—do not—"

Zephyrine's whole body went cold and then hot, in that order, blood making its argument with fear and winning.

"—repeat, do not—" A gap, filled with roar. Then, clearer, as though the speaker had turned toward the microphone, as though she were leaning in the way she used to lean over maps: "—coordinates follow. Seven-one-three. Four-zero-nine. Mark it, mark it—" Static swallowed the rest. Then, from deep inside the noise, one word surfaced whole and unmistakable, in a voice Zephyrine would have known underwater, in a fire, in her sleep, at the bottom of any river in the world:

"*Return.*"

Then the carrier folded, and the static closed over it like water over a stone, and the band was empty again, and the shed was very quiet, and Zephyrine Sandoval discovered that she had been holding her breath long enough to feel dizzy.

She wrote the coordinates down. Her hand shook only on the second number, and she made herself rewrite it, clean, because a sloppy coordinate was a sin her mother had beaten out of her before she could ride a bicycle. 713. 409. Grid references, probably, in whatever local system the expedition was using. Meaningless without the key. Meaningless except for the voice attached to them.

Her mother's voice. Alive enough to transmit. Three days after missing three check-ins.

Zephyrine put both hands flat on the crate and breathed until the shed stopped tilting.

* * *

Here is what she should have done, and knew she should have done, and told herself she would do: nothing. Log the intercept. Wire the foundation — they had people for this, search protocols, insurance, a director whose entire job was managing Dr. Imelda Sandoval's legend. Let the institution earn its ten percent. Take the morning steamer back down the coast to the next contract, the next trench, the next town where nobody knew the name, and let the miles keep doing what they had done for seven years, which was stand between her and everything that bled.

That was the plan. That had always been the plan. The plan had kept her alive and employed and safely unloved across four countries and eleven dig sites, and it deserved better than to be abandoned over thirty seconds of static.

She was already packing when she noticed she was packing.

It happened the way these things happen — not as decision but as discovery. She was in her room pulling the field kit off the top shelf, the good canvas roll with the brass buckles, and she had the trowel wrapped and the calipers nested and her survey notebook squared into the waterproof sleeve before her brain caught up with her hands and objected.

*You are running,* it said. *You understand that. This is running. You're just doing it upstream instead of away, which is the same motion wearing a different hat.*

"I'm going to get answers," she said to the empty room, in the voice she used on recalcitrant instruments. "There's a difference."

*Is there?*

"There is." She stuffed a sweater in hard, as punctuation.

The truth, which she examined for exactly as long as she could stand — about four seconds — was this: she did not know which outcome she feared more. That her mother was dead, and the voice was a recording, a dead-man's loop ticking out coordinates into the dark for no one. Or that her mother was alive, and had missed three check-ins on purpose, and had sent that fragment — *return* — knowing, *knowing*, that the only person on earth who would hear it and move was the daughter she hadn't spoken to in seven years. That her mother had reached across all those silent miles not with a letter, not with a call, not with the apology that seven years owed, but with a radio transmission, terse as a work order, assuming — *assuming* — that Zeph would come.

And the worst part, the part that made her slam the kit shut harder than the buckles required, was that the assumption was correct. Her mother still knew her better than anyone alive. Seven years of chosen exile, and Imelda Sandoval could still predict her daughter's behavior from three hundred miles upriver with more accuracy than Zephyrine could manage from inside her own skull.

That was the thing she couldn't forgive. Not the absence. The accuracy.

* * *

The mail steamer *Coronel* ran upriver from the coastal port every Thursday, carrying sacks of post, drums of diesel, crates of condensed milk, and whatever passengers were desperate or foolish enough to pay for six days of open-deck travel into the interior. Zephyrine knew the schedule the way she knew tide tables — involuntarily, permanently, the way you know things from childhood. She also knew that Thursday was two days away, and that two days was either plenty of time to think clearly or exactly enough time to lose her nerve, depending on how she spent them.

She spent them badly, which is to say honestly.

Thursday morning found her on the Coronel's aft deck with her kit at her feet and the marsh falling away behind her, watching the town she'd lived in for three months shrink to a gray smudge, and she felt — she catalogued the feeling precisely, because cataloguing was how she survived feelings — irritated. Profoundly, comprehensively irritated. Irritated at the telegram for existing. Irritated at the foundation for sending it. Irritated at the radio in the shed for working after three months of dignified silence. Irritated at her mother for transmitting coordinates instead of words, for saying *return* instead of *I'm sorry* or *I was wrong* or even *hello, Zeph, I hope you're well*, irritated most of all at herself for coming, for booking passage against every instinct she'd spent seven years sharpening, for being so transparently, mechanically predictable that a woman three hundred miles away could steer her with thirty seconds of static.

"You're not angry," said a voice behind her. "Angry people don't itemize."

She turned. The ship's purser — a heavy, kind-faced man who'd stamped her ticket without comment at dawn — was leaning on the rail beside her with two cups of coffee that smelled like it had been brewed from rope.

"I'm not angry," Zephyrine said.

"No," he agreed, and handed her a cup. "Where you headed?"

"Upstream."

"That's not a place."

"It's the only direction I've got."

He nodded as if this were a normal answer, which, on a mail boat, it probably was. Below them the river took the Coronel into its grip — brown, broad, unhurried, carrying silt from places she'd never seen toward deltas she'd never visit — and Zephyrine drank the terrible coffee and opened her notebook to the last page.

Under **4187**, under the coordinates, she wrote the only honest sentence of the week:

*If this is a trap, it's a good one, because I'd walk into it twice.*

Then she looked upriver, at the haze where the water narrowed and the country began, and allowed herself, for the first time in seven years, to count forward instead of backward. Six days to Puerto Cobre. Whatever was waiting there, it wasn't going to wait politely.

Somewhere ahead, her mother's voice was still repeating itself into the dark. *Return.* As if Zephyrine had ever needed telling. As if the whole exile had been anything but a circle drawn too wide.

The Coronel blew its whistle at a fishing skiff, a long rude blast, and turned its bow upstream, and Zephyrine turned her face into the wind so that whatever her expression was doing, no one could log it.
