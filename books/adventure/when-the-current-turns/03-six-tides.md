## 3. Six Tides

Puerto Cobre smelled like diesel and dead fish and money changing hands too fast to count. Zephyrine came down the gangplank of the mail steamer with her mother's field kit on one shoulder and a headache she'd carried since the river mouth, and the first thing she saw was the fleet.

Three dredge barges moored at the commercial quay, their cranes folded like sleeping insects. Behind them, riding higher and prouder than anything else in the harbor, a white-hulled salvage ship with *AURELIA* painted across its stern in letters you could read from the far bank. Men in matching coveralls moved along the docks with the unhurried confidence of people who had already bought everything worth buying.

She'd known Vance was moving. The telegram hadn't said so, but telegrams never say the important part; they just give you enough rope to hang your own conclusions. Still, knowing it abstractly and seeing the Aurelia's chrome railings throwing sunlight into her eyes were two different animals.

"Passage upriver?" a boy asked, materializing at her elbow with the practiced hunger of a port kid. "Charter? Guide? I know everyone."

"I know where I'm going."

"Everyone says that." He fell into step anyway. "You want the gorge, you want to know about the tides. Six tides."

Zephyrine stopped. "What?"

"The bar at the gorge mouth." He pointed upstream, where the river narrowed between two shoulders of black rock maybe three kilometers off. "Dredges draw too much water for the low channel. They need a spring tide stacked on a flood current to push the big equipment through. Tide tables are posted at the harbormaster's office — free information, but nobody reads it right." He grinned, gap-toothed, businesslike. "Six tides until they can bring the machines in. After that, whatever's up that river belongs to Aurelio Vance, and everybody knows it."

"How much do I owe you?"

"For what? I told you before you asked." He shrugged and drifted toward the next gangplank, already working another mark. Zephyrine stood a moment in the churn of the quay, doing arithmetic she didn't like.

Six tides. On this coast, roughly two and a half days. Her mother's expedition had been silent for eleven.

* * *

The harbormaster's office was a wooden shack with a ceiling fan turning at the speed of resignation. Inside, a tide table was indeed nailed to the wall, hand-lettered, and the man behind the desk was indeed not reading it. He was reading a ledger, and when he looked up at Zephyrine his face did something complicated — recognition, then calculation, then a careful blankness.

"You're Sandoval," he said. Not a question. "The daughter."

Word traveled faster than steamers. She set her elbows on his counter. "Then you know why I'm here."

"I know a lot of things." He closed the ledger. "I know Dr. Imelda Sandoval's expedition checked in through this office every forty-eight hours, regular as church, because she was the kind of woman who believed protocols existed for a reason. I know they missed three. And I know that two days after the third missed check-in, Mr. Vance's people arrived and bought up every charter license on this waterfront inside of six hours." He tapped the tide table. "I also know that if you're thinking of going up there ahead of them, the back channels are the only channels left. And most of the men who used to run them won't take the work."

"Why not?"

"Because the ones who tried are no longer pilots." He said it flatly, without relish. "Vance's lawyers found technical violations. Drafting errors on charts, expired endorsements, an incident report filed late. Paper crimes. But a pilot without a license is a pilot without insurance, and a pilot without insurance can't carry passengers, and a passenger who hires him anyway is liable for everything that goes wrong." He met her eyes. "Which is how a man like Vance clears a river without firing a shot."

"Names," Zephyrine said. "Give me names."

The harbormaster looked at her for a long moment, weighing something. Whatever he weighed, it tipped. He wrote a single name on the corner of a chart scrap and slid it across: **IBARRA**.

"He drinks at the Cantina del Faro after dark. Don't mention I sent you. He's proud, and pride is the only thing the lawyers didn't manage to strip off him."

* * *

The Cantina del Faro sat under the skeletal lighthouse at the harbor's edge, and it was the kind of place where the light was dim enough to be merciful. Zephyrine took a stool at the end of the bar and ordered what the man beside her was drinking, which turned out to be a mistake involving rum and something medicinal, but it accomplished its purpose: he glanced over.

Cass Ibarra was younger than she expected — mid-thirties, maybe, with a riverman's forearms and a face that had been handsome before the river got done arguing with it. A faded license badge still hung from his neck on a lanyard, which struck her as either grief or defiance or both.

"You're staring," he said.

"You're wearing a revoked license around your neck."

He touched the badge like a man touching a scar. "And you're the Sandoval girl. Walked off the mail steamer this morning asking questions about tides." He turned back to his glass. "Whatever you're selling, I'm not buying."

"I'm not selling. I'm hiring."

"Can't hire me. Legally speaking, I don't exist." He said it lightly, but the lightness had rust under it. "Ask anyone at the harbormaster's. Ask Vance's lawyers — they'll give you the full list of my crimes. Very thorough men."

"They took your license over paper."

"They took my license over a survey I ran for the wrong people." Now he did turn, fully, and his eyes were harder than the rest of him. "Three years ago I mapped the back channels above the gorge for a government hydrographic office. Honest work. Then it turned out Aurelio Vance wanted those same channels unmapped, because unmapped water keeps salvage competitors out and keeps the villagers in San Remedios cut off from any legal traffic. My charts made the river legible. Legible rivers get claimed. So the lawyers went hunting through my filings until they found ink blots to hang me with." He lifted his glass in a bitter toast. "To progress."

Zephyrine turned her own glass slowly on the bar. Everything in her wanted to keep this transactional — hire the boat, pay the man, feel nothing. That had been her method for seven years: distances, contracts, clean exits. But the harbormaster's voice was still in her ear, and underneath it, garbled by static, her mother's: *coordinates, return.*

"My mother's expedition has gone silent," she said. "Eleven days. And Vance's fleet needs six tides to get its dredges into the gorge. Which means I have six tides to get there first, through water that's apparently been erased from the record." She looked at him. "You're the only man on this waterfront who ever mapped it."

"Was." He drained the glass. "Past tense is the whole story of my life."

"What's the fee?"

"Doesn't matter. Answer's no."

"Name it anyway."

"No." He set the glass down with a click of finality. "You're a Sandoval. Your family's name opened this river to people like Vance in the first place — famous discoveries, famous expeditions, half the coastline named after your mother's career. Men like Vance learned how to work this water watching people like her show them it could be worked." He stood, dropped coins on the bar, and pulled his jacket off the hook. "I don't pilot for the dynasty that taught the pirates to sail."

The words landed lower than she wanted them to. Because he wasn't wrong, exactly — she'd spent seven years running from exactly that arithmetic, and she still hadn't solved it. She watched him head for the door, and something in her — the part that read current lines for a living, that knew when a surface disturbance meant deep water moving underneath — made her speak before she could stop herself.

"She missed three check-ins," Zephyrine said to his back. "My mother has never missed a check-in in thirty years. She invented half the protocols this coast runs on. And the last thing I heard from her band was her voice saying coordinates and one word — *return*. You can hate the name all you want. But somewhere up that river is a woman who stayed silent on purpose, and a fleet full of dredges coming to strip whatever she found, and six tides." She stood. "I'm going with or without you. Without you, I drown in the back channels, and Vance gets everything, and the river stays exactly as illegible as he wants it. With you—" she paused, hating the next word and meaning it anyway, "—maybe not."

Cass stopped with his hand on the door. For a long moment the cantina noise filled the silence between them — dice, a radio playing boleros, someone laughing too hard.

"You heard her voice," he said quietly. "On the radio."

"Fragment. Garbled. Could have been a replay, could have been a ghost, could have been bait." She held his gaze. "But I'm the only person alive who knows what her voice sounds like when she's scared. And she was scared."

Something shifted in his face — not softening, exactly. Recalculation. A man redoing his sums and disliking the new total.

"The Mariposa," he said finally. "Berth nine, down past the fish market. She leaks, she's ugly, and she's the fastest thing on this river below twenty tons." He jabbed a finger at her. "Three conditions. One: my boat, my rules on the water, no exceptions, including especially for Sandovals. Two: you pay half up front, and if we get seized by Vance's people, you tell them you shanghaied me. Three—" he hesitated, and the pride flickered visibly, costing him something, "—you tell me everything you know about those coordinates. All of it. If I'm running black water for a Sandoval, I'm doing it with the chart in front of me."

"Two and a half are reasonable."

"And the half?"

"I decide what I share when I know what it means. Same as you'd do."

He almost smiled. It was a narrow, reluctant thing, gone as fast as it came. "Berth nine. We cast off at slack water before the fourth tide turns — no, earlier. Third slack. If we run the upper channel at night we gain a tide on them." He pushed open the door, then paused. "For the record, Sandoval — I still think this is a mistake."

"Noted."

"And for the record," he added, "if your mother is alive up there, she'd better be worth it. Because the last time I trusted a legend on this river, it cost me everything I had."

He was gone into the dark before she could answer that, and Zephyrine stood alone in the cantina's merciful dimness, two and a half days from the gorge, three tides from casting off, holding a partnership neither of them had quite agreed to and both of them needed.

Outside, the tide bell at the harbor mouth rang once, flat and patient, counting down.

* * *

She walked the waterfront instead of sleeping. It was an old habit — measuring places by walking them, getting the lie of the land into her hands the way other people got it into notebooks. Berth nine held a boat that matched Cass's description and exceeded it in ugliness: a stubby river freighter with a patched wheelhouse and an engine housing wrapped in what appeared to be a retired truck tarp. The name on the bow had been repainted so many times it read as *MARI—OSA*, the P worn away entirely.

At berth twelve, larger and newer and lit like a hotel lobby, a salvage tender flew a small pennant with the Vance corporate crest. Two crewmen on deck were loading dive tanks, and their conversation carried across the water with the unguarded ease of men who assumed no one was listening.

"—sonar team says the gorge readings are garbage anyway. Caverns, they're saying. Whole drowned system under the ridge, plays hell with the returns."

"So how do we find anything?"

"Find the old lady's camp first, idiot. She marked everything. Vance wants her maps more than he wants the gold." A laugh. "Dead or alive, Sandoval's still the best surveyor on this river."

Zephyrine stood very still in the dark between the berths, her mother's field kit heavy on her shoulder, and felt the sentence go through her like cold water through a hull seam.

*Dead or alive.* They weren't searching for a missing expedition. They were hunting a woman — and her maps.

The tide bell rang again. Five tides now, if the boy at the gangplank had counted right, and counting right was suddenly the only thing that mattered. She turned back toward the town, toward a cheap room and a sleepless night and a battered boat called the Mariposa, and began, for the first time in seven years, to count time in tides instead of miles.
