## 4. What the Mother Kept

The mail steamer's cabin smelled of diesel, mildew, and someone else's cigarettes, and Zephyrine Sandoval spent her first hour aboard pretending it was an office.

She'd worked worse. She'd worked a trench in the Yucatán lowlands where the humidity rotted the survey stakes before they could be driven, a season in a wind-scoured Atacama camp where the tents shredded like old flags. She had learned, over seven years, to make a workspace out of anything—a crate top, a bunk, a folded tarp on gravel—because a workspace was the one thing she could always control. You couldn't control a mother who missed check-ins. You couldn't control a river you'd never seen. But you could control what went into a pack, and in what order, and that was enough to keep the hands busy while the mind did its quieter, more dangerous work.

So she laid out her mother's field kit on the bunk and began to inventory it.

The kit itself was a legend in the profession, or had been. A leather rucksack gone the color of dark honey, straps replaced twice, buckles polished by thirty years of thumbs. Imelda Sandoval had carried it on every expedition from the coastal surveys that made her name to the deep-river work the textbooks still cited. There were photographs of it. Zephyrine had grown up watching strangers ask to hold it, the way people ask to hold a sword in a museum, and her mother always handed it over with a smile that never quite reached her eyes.

It had arrived at Zephyrine's last dig site eleven days ago, forwarded twice, wrapped in sacking, with a note in her mother's handwriting: *If anything happens, you'll want this. — I.S.* Not *love*. Not *Mama*. Just the initials, like a signature on a contract.

*If anything happens.* Zephyrine had stared at those three words for a long time in the marsh town's post office, and then she had done what she always did with anything her mother sent her: she had put it in a corner and refused to open it for two days.

Now, on the steamer's lurching bunk, she opened it.

The first layer was ordinary, and that was somehow the worst part. Brass calipers worn smooth. A folding ruler with one cracked segment. A compass—no, not the compass, that was with her mother, or should be—the backup compass, the one Imelda called "the understudy," its needle sluggish but true. A tin of pencils sharpened to needles. A rock hammer with a grip wrapped in bicycle tape. Zephyrine knew every item the way you know the furniture of a childhood house: not because you studied it, but because it was simply always there.

Beneath the tools, wrapped in an oilcloth that crackled like old leaves, she found the maps.

She unfolded the first one and her breath caught, not because of what it showed but because of what it *was*—a hand-drawn hydrographic chart of the upper river, ink on linen, annotated in her mother's small, ruthless script. Depth soundings in fathoms. Current arrows. Little crosses at points along the gorge with notations Zephyrine had to hold up to the porthole light to read: *silt shelf, unstable* and *cavern mouth, unverified* and, at one bend, simply *NO*.

Her mother had drawn this map years ago. The linen was foxed with age, the ink faded unevenly. Which meant Imelda Sandoval had surveyed the upper gorge long before the salvage fleet ever filed its permits, long before the galleon's existence was announced to the world, long before—

Long before the discovery that built the family name.

Zephyrine sat with that thought while the steamer's engine knocked beneath her like a second, dumber heart.

She unfolded the second map. This one was older. The paper had gone soft as cloth, and the handwriting on it was not her mother's. It was rounder, more patient, annotated in a language Zephyrine half-recognized—not quite Spanish, not quite anything the academies would certify—and it showed the same stretch of river, the same gorge, the same bend marked *NO*. But where Imelda's map had crosses, this one had names. Small ones, careful ones, written along the banks like a litany: *San Remedios. La Boca del Cielo. Casa de los Ahogados.* The House of the Drowned.

Zephyrine had never heard of any of them. And she had grown up on this river's history the way other children grew up on bedtime stories.

She turned the old map over. On the back, in pencil, in her mother's hand, added years later by the look of it: *Ask them. Don't take. ASK.*

Ask whom? Take what?

The third thing in the oilcloth was not a map. It was a page torn from a journal—torn roughly, the ragged edge still showing the ghost of the binding's stitching—and Zephyrine recognized the journal itself by its absence. It was the green one. The one her mother had kept through the years Zephyrine was small, the one that had vanished from the family shelves sometime during Zephyrine's adolescence, the one she'd assumed was lost or sold or simply packed away with everything else her mother decided was finished.

The torn page was dated twenty-two years ago. Zephyrine did the arithmetic without wanting to. She would have been four.

The handwriting was her mother's, but younger—looser, less armored, the script of a woman who hadn't yet learned to write like a monument. Zephyrine read it twice, and then a third time, because the first two times she kept flinching off the middle of it.

*The elders came to me again tonight. Old Eusebio does not raise his voice, which is how I know he is serious. He says the church gives up what it gives up, but the codex is not the church's to give and it is not mine to take. He says the river kept it for them and the river will ask for it back.*

*I told him about the funding. I told him the foundation expects a find. He looked at me for a long time and said: "Then you must decide which debt you will owe."*

*I have decided. I will tell them the codex came from the galleon. The galleon is real, the cargo manifests are real, and no one will question a colonial wreck. The village is gone anyway—Vance's dam took it, and no one asks questions about things that are underwater. It is not a lie exactly. It is a redirection.*

*God forgive me. Or don't. I have work to do.*

Zephyrine lowered the page.

The steamer's cabin seemed to tilt, though the river was calm. She read the date again. Twenty-two years. The Sandoval Codex—the find that had put the family name on three generations of syllabi, the artifact that had funded the institute that had funded every dig Zephyrine had ever escaped to—the codex had not come off the galleon at all.

It had come from a drowned church. From a village called San Remedios. From people who had told her mother, plainly, that it was not hers to take.

And her mother had taken it anyway, and built a legend on top of it, and raised a daughter inside that legend like a bird raised in a painted sky.

Zephyrine's first feeling was not anger. It was something colder and more disorienting: the vertigo of a floor that turns out to have been a ceiling. All her life she had defined herself against her mother—the famous one, the incorruptible one, Dr. Imelda Sandoval who never fudged a provenance, who testified against looters, whose integrity was cited in ethics seminars. Zephyrine had built her whole drifting, refusing, seven-years-running exile on the belief that she could never measure up to that particular monument.

And the monument had a crack in it, and through the crack she could see not a monster, which would have been easier, but a woman. A young woman with funding deadlines and a foundation breathing down her neck, who had looked at an impossible choice and made the wrong one for reasons that were almost—*almost*—understandable.

*It is not a lie exactly. It is a redirection.*

Zephyrine almost laughed. She knew that sentence. She had been saying versions of it her entire adult life. *I'm not running, I'm repositioning. I'm not avoiding her calls, I'm prioritizing fieldwork.* Her mother's talent for the elegant evasion hadn't come from nowhere. It was the family inheritance, passed down like the calipers and the compass and the honey-colored rucksack.

She looked at the kit again, at the tools laid out in their careful rows, and now she saw them differently. Not as relics. As evidence.

And then the memory came, the way memories do when you've finally made room for them—rising not from the mind but from the body, from the smell of the oilcloth and the pencil shavings, from the particular weight of linen maps in the hands.

* * *

The summers had a color. That was how Zephyrine remembered them—not as dates but as light: the green-gold of the river compound in late afternoon, when the funders' boats had gone and the generators were off and the whole expedition ran on kerosene and her mother's restless energy.

Zephyrine would have been eight, nine, ten. Old enough to be useful, young enough to be flattered by it. Her job was the maps. Not the real ones—the *other* ones.

"Here's what we'll do," her mother would say, when a funding delegation was due. She'd spread the official charts across the big table in the map room, the ones with the survey grids and the tidy site markers, and then she'd take Zephyrine's small hands and put a pencil in them. "You're going to draw me a map of the camp. Every tent, every crate, the cook's dog, everything. And when the visitors come, you're going to show them this map, and you're going to be very serious about it."

"And the real ones?"

"The real ones go under the floor." Imelda said it lightly, the way she said everything, but Zephyrine remembered the specific care with which her mother rolled the linen charts and slid them into the oilcloth sleeve, and the specific care with which she pried up the loose board beneath the map table's leg, and the way the board went back down with a sound like a held breath released.

"Why?" Zephyrine had asked once, the way children ask, without any diplomacy at all.

Her mother had crouched down to her level, which she almost never did with adults, and considered the question seriously, because she considered everything seriously, that was the whole problem with her.

"Because maps are promises," she'd said. "Everything on a map is a promise to somebody. And I haven't decided yet who I'm making my promises to."

Zephyrine, at nine, had thought this was wonderful. She'd thought her mother was a spy in a romance, hiding treasure from villains. She'd drawn her decoy maps with fierce dedication, adding invented features—a crocodile pen, a well that went to the center of the earth—and the funders had admired them, and her mother had praised her, and the real maps had slept under the floor through every visit.

It was only now, twenty years later, holding a map that had once slept under that same kind of floor, that Zephyrine understood what her nine-year-old self had been helping to hide.

Not treasure. *Debts.* Every one of those hidden charts was a promise her mother hadn't decided who to make. And the funders—the patient, well-meaning, moneyed people who'd admired a child's crocodile pen—they had been shown a painted sky too. Everyone had. The whole shining edifice of the Sandoval reputation had been built on a redirected lie, and the little girl with the pencil had been its unwitting first collaborator.

There was another summer, later—Zephyrine fourteen, already chafing, already counting the miles to anywhere else—when she'd come into the map room at night and found her mother sitting alone with a chart unrolled, holding a pencil over it and not writing. Just holding it. For a long time. And when Zephyrine had asked what she was doing, her mother had said, "Deciding," and nothing else, and Zephyrine had rolled her eyes and gone to bed and told herself her mother was being dramatic, because dramatic was what famous people were.

Now she knew what the pencil had been hovering over. The same choice Old Eusebio had named: which debt to owe. And Zephyrine understood, with a sick lurch, that her mother had been deciding for twenty-two years and had never once finished deciding—until, perhaps, whatever had happened upriver three weeks ago. Until *if anything happens, you'll want this.* Until a radio band full of static and one word: *return.*

Return the codex. Return to the river. Return the maps to the people whose names were written on them.

*Ask them. Don't take. ASK.*

* * *

The steamer's horn sounded somewhere below, a long low note that shivered the porthole glass, and Zephyrine realized she'd been sitting motionless for the better part of an hour. Outside, the banks had changed—the coastal marsh giving way to firmer ground, the first low hills of the interior shouldering up through the haze. They were making for the river port. Puerto Cobre, the ticket agent had called it, in the tone of a man naming a place you pass through, never a place you arrive.

Zephyrine began packing the kit back together, but she did it differently now. Slower. She wrapped the maps in their oilcloth and put them not at the bottom, where her mother had kept them, but at the top, where her own hand would find them first. She set the torn journal page inside them, folded along its old creases. The tools went back in their loops. The understudy compass she clipped to her belt, because some inheritances you carry on your body.

At the very bottom of the rucksack, beneath a false panel her mother had surely sewn—of course she had; the woman had been hiding things in this bag for thirty years—Zephyrine found one last item.

A radio frequency, written in grease pencil on a strip of linen: 8364 kilocycles. The expedition band. The one she'd already tuned through in the marsh town, the one that had given her static and coordinates and that single word.

But beneath the frequency, in her mother's hand, smaller: *If the signal repeats, it is not me. Do not follow it alone.*

Zephyrine sat back on the bunk and let the words do their work.

So her mother had known. Had anticipated this—had rigged some signal, or known someone who would send one, or feared that someone would. The message in the static was either a lifeline or a lure, and the woman who knew her own voice best of anyone alive had written a warning against trusting it.

*It is not me.*

Zephyrine looked out the porthole at the brown water sliding past, at the hills gathering themselves into the distance where the gorge waited, where six tides were draining away somewhere without her, where a salvage fleet was already moving.

Her mother had always been more complicated than the legend. That was the day's hard lesson, and Zephyrine took it the way she took all field data—without flinching, without editorializing, recorded and carried forward. The woman who hid maps under floors and redirected lies and wrote warnings against her own voice was not the monument and not the monster. She was a surveyor of debts, still out there in the dark water, still deciding.

And Zephyrine, for the first time in seven years, was not running from her or toward her.

She was going upriver to read the current for herself.

The steamer's horn sounded again. Somewhere ahead, at a port called Puerto Cobre, there would be tide tables, a boat, and—she had a feeling, the kind her mother used to trust and she herself had never permitted—someone who knew the back channels.

Zephyrine clipped the rucksack shut and went up on deck to watch the river come.
