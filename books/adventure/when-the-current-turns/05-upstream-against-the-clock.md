## 5. Upstream Against the Clock

The Mariposa had a heart like a bad debt—she'd run for you when she felt like it, and only if you asked nicely. Cass Ibarra had bought her rusted hull for less than the price of a decent lie and spent four years learning her moods: the way she pulled starboard under load, the way her old diesel coughed when the fuel line ran warm, the way her wheel fought him in a following sea like something that wanted to be anywhere but here.

Here was the mouth of the Cobre Gorge at slack before flood tide, mist still burning off the water, and a woman standing at his bow rail reading the river like it owed her money.

"You're going to want to hug the left bank for the first two hundred meters," Zephyrine Sandoval called back to him. "There's a scour channel cut into the point where the current doubles back."

Cass didn't look up from the engine hatch he was nursing. "I've run this stretch since I was fourteen."

"Then you've been running it wrong." She crouched at the rail, one hand on the cable, watching the water with an expression he recognized from mirrors—the look of someone who trusted nothing that couldn't be measured. "See the foam line? It's not breaking parallel to the bank. It's angling out. That's a countercurrent eddy stacked on top of the main flow. Your draft is what, four feet loaded?"

"Three and a half, if you don't count my patience."

"Then the eddy will grab your stern and swing you into the point unless you cut inside it early. Hug the left bank."

He wiped his hands on a rag that had given up being clean sometime last year and climbed to the wheelhouse. The truth—which he'd have chewed glass before admitting—was that he'd already seen the foam line doing exactly what she said. He'd seen it and filed it under *river being the river*, the way you file away a toothache. She'd looked at the same water and named it.

"Left bank," he muttered, and put the wheel over.

The Mariposa swung wide, then tucked in close enough to the bank that overhanging fig branches dragged their fingers along the gunwale. The eddy grabbed for them and found nothing. They slid through the narrows below the first bend clean as a coin through a slot.

Zephyrine glanced back at him. Said nothing. Which was its own kind of commentary.

Cass had known her three days and disliked her for two of them. The third day was this morning, and he wasn't ready to call it yet.

* * *

She'd come to his table at Doña Fermina's cantina with a rolled chart under her arm and Vance's name in her mouth like a stone she was daring him to take. Everyone in Puerto Cobre knew who Cass was—or had been, before the lawyers came. Pilot's license number 4471, revoked on paper so clean you could frame it: an "unauthorized deviation from the marked channel," they'd written, as if the marked channel hadn't been moved six months earlier to serve the salvage fleet's dredges, as if half the pilots on the river hadn't been quietly pushed out the same way, one license at a time, until the only boats running the upper Cobre belonged to men who signed whatever Aurelio Vance put in front of them.

"I need someone who knows the back channels," she'd said. "I can pay."

"Everyone who comes to me says that. Then they find out what it costs."

"It costs more than money. I know. That's why I'm not offering any more than I have."

He'd almost laughed. Almost. Instead he'd looked at the chart she unrolled on the sticky table—hand-annotated, soundings in her own cramped script, corrections dated within the year—and felt something he hadn't felt since the license went: the itch of a river he wasn't allowed to touch.

Now she stood at his bow rail doing it again, pointing upstream past the second bend. "Scout boat. Anchored."

He saw it a moment later—a low gray workboat with a radar mast and too much antenna for a survey vessel, sitting dead in the water a full kilometer below the gorge entrance. No lights. No anchor light, even, which on a working boat after dark meant either carelessness or confidence.

"That's wrong," he said, cutting the throttle to let the words land.

"How?"

"She's anchored too far down. Anybody scouting the gorge entrance wants to sit above the bend where they can see traffic coming out of the narrows. Down here you're blind—you get the current noise, the echo off the cliffs, and nothing else." He eased the Mariposa toward the shadowed southern bank, killing their silhouette against the last of the daylight. "Unless they're not scouting the gorge."

"Then what are they scouting?"

"The approach. Us." He watched the gray boat slide past their stern, half a kilometer off, close enough now to see a figure on her deck wearing a headset, motionless, facing downstream. Facing them. "Vance doesn't waste boats. If she's parked there, somebody told her to watch this stretch of river and report anything moving against the current."

Zephyrine turned, and in the dimness her face did something complicated. "How long before they make us?"

"We're a smudge on the bank in failing light. Ten minutes, maybe fifteen, before whoever's on that deck decides we're worth a radio call. After that—" he jerked his chin at the darkening gorge ahead, all cliff-shadow and white water, "—after that we're either through the first gate or we're a story they tell at dinner."

"Can we make the first gate in ten minutes?"

"No." He fed the throttle forward and felt the old engine answer with a rattle like dice in a tin cup. "But we can make it in eight if the river cooperates, and the river never cooperates. So talk to me about that eddy at the gate, because I've only run it at night twice, and both times I was younger and stupider."

* * *

The first gate of the Cobre Gorge was a lie the river told about itself. From downstream it looked like a gap forty meters wide between limestone shoulders, plenty of room for anything short of a barge. What it actually was, was a gap forty meters wide with a rock shelf lying just under the surface on the right side, invisible until the water folded over it in a smooth green hump that meant death delivered politely, and a boil of standing waves on the left where the full force of the flood tide hit the cliff wall and rebounded. The safe line—if you could call anything safe in a gorge at night—was a slot maybe eight meters wide between the shelf's edge and the boil, and the slot moved with the tide, because everything in the Cobre moved with the tide.

Six tides. That was the arithmetic Zephyrine had laid out in Puerto Cobre, tapping the tide table with one finger while Cass drank coffee that tasted like it had been brewed during the previous administration. Six tides until the dredges could enter the gorge in force. Vance's fleet needed deep water and daylight and a window of calm, and the river would give it to him on the sixth flood. Everything they did, they did inside that countdown.

Tide one was tonight.

"Slot's going to sit closer to the shelf than you think," Zephyrine said, coming into the wheelhouse without knocking, which was apparently a thing she did. She'd spread her chart across the chart table and weighted the corners with a coffee mug, a wrench, and Cass's own lucky bolt, which she handled without asking and he decided not to kill her over. "Flood stream hits the outside wall, reflects, and pushes the eddy line toward the obstruction. The calm water isn't where the calm looks like it is. It's offset."

"Offset how much?"

"Two meters. Maybe three. It'll read as slack water hugging the shelf, which is going to feel like steering at the thing most likely to kill you."

Cass stared at her. "You've run this gate."

"I've never been within fifty kilometers of this gate. I'm reading the physics." She tapped the foam lines sketched on her chart—she'd drawn them from memory of the last hour's water, annotated with times and headings in that cramped engineer's hand. "Your mother teach you the river, or did you learn it the way most pilots do, by nearly dying until the lessons stuck?"

"My father. Same method, fewer survivors in the class." He took the wheel. Ahead, the cliffs had closed to a black corridor stitched with the last gray light, and somewhere in it the gate waited, making its small cold sounds. "If I put us on the shelf, grab the bow line and jump, because I will."

"Noted."

"And if I put us in the boil—"

"I'll be busy swimming," she said. "Go."

He went.

The Mariposa gathered herself in the last open water, engine hauling against the building flood, and then the walls were rushing past and the gate was there—not the gap he'd expected but a churning seam of shadow, the shelf's green hump gleaming wet and false on the right, the boil smoking white on the left, and between them a lane of darker water that was not where his eye said it should be. His eye said mid-channel. Her voice in his head said *offset*, and he hated her for three full seconds while he steered at the rock that wanted to eat them.

Two meters from the shelf's edge, the water went smooth.

Not calm—nothing in the Cobre was calm—but smooth, a ribbon of quieter current threading between the hump and the chaos, and he put the Mariposa on that ribbon like a needle onto a groove. The hull shuddered once as the reflected wave slapped her quarter. The boil reached for them with a long white arm and missed by the width of a decision. Then the gate was behind them, the cliffs fell back, and the river opened into the long dark reach above, breathing fog.

Neither of them spoke for a full minute. The engine settled into its working rhythm. Somewhere overhead, a nightjar started its ragged song.

"Offset two meters," Cass finally said. "Maybe two and a half."

"Three," she said. "I'll allow I was estimating."

He laughed—one short bark, surprised out of him—and immediately regretted it, because she turned and looked at him with an expression that suggested she'd just catalogued the sound for future use. People didn't laugh much around Cass Ibarra these days. He'd gotten good at not giving them reasons.

"Where'd you learn to read water like that?" he asked, because not asking would have been worse.

"Survey work. Coastal mostly. Marshes, deltas, a season on a harbor project nobody talks about anymore." She kept her eyes on the reach ahead. "Current is current. Salt or fresh, it obeys the same rules. Most people look at a river and see scenery. I look at it and see a machine with every part showing."

"And your mother?" The question was out before he could weigh it. He'd heard the name Sandoval whispered along the whole river for twenty years—the great Dr. Imelda, the woman who found the galleon, whose face had been on stamps and museum plaques and the covers of magazines Cass used to wrap fish. "She raised you on boats, I'd assume."

Something closed in Zephyrine's face, quick and total, like a shutter going down over a lit window.

"My mother raised me on waiting," she said. "In cars outside dig sites. In hotel lobbies in towns she flew out of before I woke up. The river taught me what I know. Not her." She adjusted the chart a centimeter it didn't need adjusting. "Is there anchorage ahead, or do we run all night?"

"There's a cutbank below the old customs station, four kilometers up. Good holding, out of the main stream." He let the silence stand, because he recognized it—he wore one himself, most days. Two loners on a battered boat, each carrying a grudge shaped differently but weighing about the same. Hers against a mother. His against a man with lawyers. The river didn't care. The river just wanted to know if you could read it.

They tied up at the cutbank under a sky going hard with stars, and Cass killed the engine, and in the sudden quiet he heard the thing he'd been half-listening for since the gate: far downstream, faint but unmistakable, the thump-thump-thump of a dredge pump testing itself in the dark.

"They're warming up," Zephyrine said.

"Tide two is tomorrow noon. Tide three tomorrow midnight." He coiled the mooring line slowly, thinking about the scout boat sitting blind below the bend, reporting nothing yet—or reporting everything, to ears he couldn't hear. "Which means Vance's people know we're on the river by now. Question is whether they think we're a problem or a joke."

"What do you think they think?"

Cass looked upriver, where the gorge went on climbing into country he'd been barred from for four years, country that held a sunken ship and a missing woman and whatever confession Imelda Sandoval had sent down the radio bands to the daughter who'd spent seven years pretending she didn't care.

"I think," he said, "that tomorrow we find out whether that scout boat was alone."

Zephyrine followed his gaze, and in the starlight he saw her jaw set in a way that reminded him, uncomfortably, of every photograph he'd ever seen of her mother.

"Then we'd better make tide two," she said.
