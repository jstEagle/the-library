## 6. The Drowned Village

The rain found us an hour before the village did.

It came down in sheets, the kind of rain that erased the river's edges until water and sky were one gray argument. Cass stood at the wheel of the Mariposa with his collar up and his eyes narrowed at a shoreline that didn't exist yet, reading something in the downpour I couldn't see.

"There." He pointed his chin left. "Chimney."

I followed his finger and saw it: a single brick chimney rising out of the river like a drowned man's last breath. Then another. Then a whole row of them, tilted at angles, their rooftops gone soft with rot or sheared away entirely, the upper stories of houses standing waist-deep in moving water.

"San Remedios," Cass said. "Half of it, anyway."

I'd read about the dam in the foundation files—skimmed, really, the way you skim anything that doesn't have your name on it. The Cobre River had been impounded forty years ago to feed a hydroelectric scheme, one of Vance's earliest acquisitions, back when he was still buying politicians instead of salvage fleets. The engineers had promised the flooding would be minimal. The maps had said the same. The maps had been drawn by men who'd never lived here.

"What happened to the rest of it?" I asked.

Cass cut the engine to half and let us drift. "What happens to any place the river takes? The government moved who'd move. The rest stayed until they couldn't." He glanced at me, and there was something in his face I hadn't seen before—not the flat professional wariness of the pilot, but something older and more tired. "You didn't know."

"I knew there was a dam."

"Everybody knows there's a dam. Nobody asks what's under it." He swung the wheel and we slid toward a break in a concrete embankment where a newer settlement clung to higher ground—a scatter of tin roofs and drying nets, a church with a bell tower that leaned like a question. "They rebuilt uphill when the water came for the old town. Called it San Remedios still. Same name, new mud."

We tied off at a rickety dock built from salvaged lumber, some of the planks still bearing paint from the houses they'd once been. The rain eased as if it had only wanted to make its point. I shouldered my pack and followed Cass up a path of packed clay, past a fence made from a rowboat sawed in half, into the smell of woodsmoke and wet earth and frying plantains.

The village watched us arrive. It wasn't hostile, exactly. It was worse than hostile—it was the look of people who had learned long ago that strangers meant paperwork, and paperwork meant men in boats, and men in boats meant the river would take something else.

An old woman selling cassava from a basket gave me a nod that cost her something. A boy on a rooftop stared until his mother called him down. Dogs took our measure and decided we weren't worth barking at.

Cass handled the negotiations. There was a comedor near the church run by a woman named Doña Fermina, wide as a doorframe and twice as immovable, who took one look at Cass and said, "Still banned from the river, Ibarra?" and then, before he could answer, "Two rooms. No credit. You eat what we eat."

"We'll pay," I said, and reached for my bag.

Doña Fermina looked at the money, then at me, then at the money again, and took it with the expression of someone accepting a bribe she intended to report later.

* * *

The food was fish and rice and a pepper sauce that made my eyes water, and it was the best meal I'd had in months, though I suspected that had less to do with the cooking than with the fact that I hadn't realized how hungry I was until I started eating. We ate at a long table under an awning with three fishermen who ignored us magnificently until one of them, a leathery man missing two fingers on his left hand, asked Cass where we were bound.

"Upstream," Cass said.

"Past the gorge?"

"Past the gorge."

The fishermen exchanged looks. The two-fingered one said, "Vance's dredges are staging at the mouth. Six tides, they say, and then nothing comes out of that gorge but silt and dead fish." He looked at me for the first time. "You going after the galleon?"

"I'm going after my mother."

"And who's your mother?"

I hesitated. It was a hesitation I'd performed a thousand times in a thousand dig-site cantinas—the small deliberate pause that let other people fill in the name, so I never had to say it myself. Usually it worked. Usually people guessed wrong, or guessed right and pretended otherwise.

This time, the fisherman's eyes went over my face like a surveyor checking bearings, and I watched him find the resemblance I'd spent seven years outrunning.

"Sandoval," he said.

The word dropped onto the table like a stone into still water.

The awning went quiet. Not dramatically—there was no gasp, no sudden movement—but the conversation around us simply stopped, the way a current stops when it hits a rock. Doña Fermina, wiping plates behind the counter, set one down very carefully. The boy from the rooftop appeared at the edge of the awning, drawn by whatever instinct tells children where trouble is forming.

"You're Imelda Sandoval's girl," the fisherman said. Not a question anymore.

"Yes," I said. Seven years of practice kept my voice level. "I am."

He stood up. Slowly, the way old men stand, each joint negotiated separately. He was not a large man, but he suddenly seemed to occupy all the space under the awning.

"My name is Eusebio Ríos," he said. "My father fished this bend of the river for thirty years. My grandmother is buried in the church down there—" he pointed with his whole arm, toward the drowned town, toward the chimney tops standing in the current, "—under forty feet of water, because when the engineers came with their maps, the maps said her grave was above the flood line. The maps were wrong. Or the maps were right, and the men who paid for them moved the line."

"Cass," I started, "maybe—"

"No. Let her hear it." Eusebio turned back to me. "Your mother came here. Twenty years ago, maybe more. Before the book, before the fame. She came with a permit and a team and she asked to dive the old church, and the elders said no, because the church is where our dead are, and our dead are all we were allowed to keep. And she dove anyway. Or her permit let her dive anyway. And she brought up things from the nave—records, ledgers, a codex, papers with names on them—and she carried them away in a crate with a university stamp on it, and nobody in this village ever saw them again."

The rain started again, softly, as if it had been waiting for its cue.

"But she found the galleon," I said. "That's—that's documented. The survey records—"

"The survey records say she found it where she said she found it." Eusebio's voice didn't rise. That was the terrible thing about it; it just got heavier. "I'm sure they do. She was a great woman. Everyone says so. Great women don't need to lie, isn't that how it goes?" He leaned closer, and I could see the river in his face, decades of it, sun and spray and grief. "Ask yourself, Sandoval girl. How does a woman find a colonial wreck in a stretch of river that was dammed, flooded, dredged, and dragged by sonar for forty years, in a spot every salvage company in the country has scanned since before she was born? Either she got lucky in a way that insults mathematics. Or she knew where to look because she'd already been looking here. In our water. Over our dead."

I opened my mouth. Closed it. Somewhere behind me, Cass shifted his weight, and I understood that he had known—had known or suspected—all day, and had said nothing, and had brought me here anyway.

Eusebio spat. It wasn't theatrical. It was quick and precise and landed in the mud between my boots, and it said everything his voice had chosen not to.

Then he walked out into the rain, and the awning exhaled, and the conversations resumed around us at a lower register, like a fire banked for the night.

* * *

I didn't sleep. I sat on the dock with my knees drawn up while the river did its patient work below me, sliding against the drowned chimneys, wearing the old town down one grain at a time.

Seven years. Seven years of measuring my life in miles specifically so I wouldn't have to measure it in bloodlines. I had told myself the distance was about her—the silences, the standards, the way love in our house had always arrived disguised as evaluation. And maybe it was. But sitting there in the dark with the taste of that stranger's contempt in my mouth, I had to admit the uglier truth: I had also loved what the name bought me. The grant committees that leaned forward when they heard it. The dig directors who assumed competence. The doors that opened before I knocked.

I had spent seven years running from the shadow while standing squarely inside its shelter.

And now the shadow itself was missing, and the name it cast was stained, and the woman who'd made it might be alive upriver, guarding something she'd taken from these people—or dead, having died for it, which would be worse, because then the confession in that garbled radio fragment wouldn't be a plea. It would be a will. And I would be the executor.

*Return,* she'd said. One word through the static.

I'd thought she meant come home. Come to me. Standing on the dock in San Remedios, listening to the river move through the streets where Eusebio's grandmother lay buried, I began to suspect she'd meant something else entirely. Return the codex. Return the record. Return what was never ours.

Footsteps on the dock planks. Cass, carrying two cups of coffee that smelled like it had been boiled into submission. He handed me one and sat without asking, which I was beginning to understand was his way of asking.

"How long have you known?" I said.

"That your family's name opens some doors and closes others?" He blew on his coffee. "Everyone on this river knows. You're the first Sandoval to come up here in twenty years, and you did it in a boat with a banned pilot, so I figured either you didn't know what the name means here, or you did, and you came anyway." He shrugged. "Either way, I wanted to watch."

"Glad I could entertain you."

"You didn't." He said it plainly, no cruelty in it. "That old man spitting at you—that wasn't entertainment. That was history. Vance dammed this river and drowned this town to light his own city, and when the academics came, they took the only thing left worth taking." He drank. "Your mother's expedition going silent, Vance's fleet moving in the same week—I've been running this river long enough to know coincidences that size don't happen. Somebody's lying. Maybe everybody."

"Maybe," I said.

Below us, the current curled around a chimney and moved on. I thought about the journal page I'd found torn from her field kit, the edge ragged where she'd ripped it free. I thought about childhood summers, my mother burning maps in a coffee can behind the tent while funders slept, telling me: *Some things get found twice, Zephyrine. Once by the person who takes them, and once by the person who gives them back.* I'd thought it was a lesson about archaeology.

I looked at the drowned village and understood it had been a confession, delivered years early to an audience too young to read it.

"Cass," I said. "How far to the transmitter?"

His head came around. "What transmitter?"

"Doña Fermina mentioned the village keeps the old relay station running. Storm warnings, river traffic." I stood, joints stiff from the cold, and felt something in me shift from grief to motion, which was the only direction I knew how to convert it. "If my mother's signal is repeating—if someone is still sending it—then it's broadcasting on a band every operator on this river can hear. Including whoever's listening for it upstream."

"So?"

"So I want to hear it again." I drained the coffee. It tasted like punishment. "All of it. Every word. And I want to know if the bearing it gives tonight is the same bearing it gave last night—because if it moves, it's not a distress call. It's a trail. And somebody laid it."

Cass looked at me for a long moment, and then at the dark water sliding past the drowned chimneys, and I watched him do the arithmetic every pilot on this river must carry: what a thing costs, versus what it's worth.

"Relay station's on the hill," he said finally. "Generator's older than both of us. If the tube's still good, we can raise it." He stood, and there was something new in his voice—not warmth exactly, but the absence of distance, which on Cass Ibarra was nearly the same thing. "But Zephyrine. Whatever your mother did here, whatever she took—you should know the village isn't going to care why. They're going to care whether you give it back."

"I know," I said.

And for the first time in seven years, the thought didn't feel like exile. It felt like coordinates.
