## 7. Radio Ghosts

The transmitter lived in the church tower, which was the only part of San Remedios that still stood entirely above the waterline.

Everything else about the village was a negotiation with the river. Houses on stilts with their lower rooms bricked shut and abandoned to the flood. A plaza where the water lapped at the base of the statue of some saint whose face had gone green with patience. Canoes tied where cars would have parked. The people of San Remedios had not moved when the dam went in forty years ago; they had simply climbed, floor by floor, like tenants of a building slowly filling from the bottom, and dared anyone to call them displaced.

Zephyrine climbed the tower stairs behind Doña Eufemia, the elder who had spat at the Sandoval name that morning and then, in an act of contradiction Zephyrine was still turning over, offered her a bowl of fish stew anyway.

"The radio is my husband's," Eufemia said, climbing slowly, one hand on the rail. "Was. He kept it working through everything. Said a village that can't call out is a village that's already drowned." She paused on the landing, catching her breath. "He has been dead nine years. The radio still works better than I do."

The tower room was small and hot, one window open to the river haze, a desk, a chair, and the transmitter itself — a military surplus rig older than Zephyrine, its casing scarred with stickers from ports she'd never heard of, its dial faces yellowed like old teeth. A hand-lettered card taped above the frequency readout: *Habla claro. Escucha más.* Speak clearly. Listen more.

Zephyrine set down her mother's field kit — the canvas bag she'd hauled out of the Mariposa's hold that afternoon, the one she'd been avoiding opening since Puerto Cobre — and sat in the dead man's chair.

"You know how to run this?" Eufemia asked from the doorway.

"I grew up on expedition radios." She touched the power switch. "Before I grew up on anything else."

It was true, and hearing herself say it made something twist in her chest. Her first language hadn't been English or Spanish. It had been the clipped phonetic alphabet of field communications, learned at seven years old sitting cross-legged under a tarp table while her mother's voice came crackling out of a handset half a jungle away: *Sandoval base to survey two, come back, over.* She had thought, as a child, that all mothers lived inside radios. That they traveled by frequency the way other mothers traveled by car.

She keyed the power. The set hummed, drew breath, settled into its working whine. The dial light glowed amber.

"Which band?" she asked.

Eufemia's mouth thinned. "The one your mother used." Of course the village knew it. Of course they'd memorized it, the way you memorize the license plate of the truck that ran over your dog. "Forty-one meters, upper sideband. Every night for three weeks now, someone has been talking on it."

Zephyrine's hand stopped on the tuning knob. "Someone."

"A woman's voice." Eufemia folded her arms. "Repeating numbers. Bearings. The same message, over and over, until midnight, then nothing until the next night. My grandson says it's ghosts." Her eyes were dry and hard and very old. "I do not believe in ghosts. I believe in people who want something."

* * *

Zephyrine tuned through the band slowly, letting each frequency settle before moving on. Static, static, a Filipino seaman's net chattering in Tagalog, static, a time signal, static. Her hands were steady. She noticed that they were steady and distrusted it, the way you distrust calm water over a submerged rock shelf.

Cass had stayed with the Mariposa, running fresh caulking into the seam that the gorge passage had opened, refusing help, refusing conversation, refusing everything except work. Tomás was somewhere in the reeds downstream, keeping watch on the Aurelia's anchor lights, and Zephyrine had decided — provisionally, angrily, without evidence — to trust him, because the alternative was to trust no one, and that way lay the kind of paralysis that got boats sunk.

And her mother was either alive or dead, and either answer required Zephyrine to keep functioning, so functioning was what she did.

The needle swung. She backed off the tuning, fine, finer.

Static — and then, inside the static, a voice.

Not words yet. Just the shape of a voice, buried in noise, rhythmic, patient. Zephyrine's whole body went cold and hot at once, blood draining from her face and flooding into her ears. She knew that shape. She had known it across thirty years of distance and seven years of silence and one childhood spent listening for it through thunderstorms.

She eased the dial a hair further. The noise floor dropped. The voice rose.

"—bearing zero-four-seven, distance eleven kilometers, mark the third channel marker past the sunken chapel. Repeat. Bearing zero-four-seven—"

Her mother's voice.

Not live. Zephyrine heard it within four seconds, the flatness of it, the even spacing between phrases, the absence of breath. This was a recording, looped, played into the night sky on a schedule. Imelda Sandoval had always spoken like she was arguing with the future — fast, sharp, sentences that leaned forward. This voice didn't lean anywhere. It just repeated.

*Bearing zero-four-seven. Distance eleven kilometers. Mark the third channel marker past the sunken chapel.*

Zephyrine wrote it down. Her handwriting came out strange, too large, a child's scrawl. She made herself listen through the full loop — bearing, distance, marker, repeat — six times, timing it. Four minutes eleven seconds per cycle. Clean splice points. Professional equipment on the transmitting end, or at least well-maintained equipment, which in this part of the world amounted to the same thing.

"Three weeks," she said aloud, to no one. "Every night."

Eufemia had gone. Or maybe she'd never really been there; old women in this village seemed to move like weather. But her words stayed, hanging in the tower room heat. *I do not believe in ghosts. I believe in people who want something.*

Zephyrine pulled the field kit onto her lap and finally opened it.

* * *

The kit smelled like every dig of Zephyrine's childhood: canvas and mildew and the mineral ghost of river mud, and underneath it, faintly, her mother's soap, the harsh brown kind that came wrapped in paper. Inside were the things Zephyrine expected — the brass compass, the folding rule, the grease pencils worn to stubs, the waterproof notebook swollen with age. And the things she didn't: a child's hair ribbon, faded red, that Zephyrine had no memory of ever owning but recognized instantly. A photograph of a young Imelda standing on the deck of a research vessel, arm around a man Zephyrine had never seen before, both of them laughing at something outside the frame.

And a page torn from a journal.

Torn, not removed cleanly — ripped, in haste or fury, leaving a ragged edge and, caught in the binding groove, a fragment of the next line: *...if they come for it, the river keeps what...*

The rest was gone. Zephyrine turned the fragment over twice, held it to the window light, found nothing written on the reverse. She thought about the journal itself, which she'd paged through on the steamer and again in Puerto Cobre, looking for the missing entry, finding instead a sequence of dates with gaps like knocked-out teeth. Her mother had been meticulous her entire life. Imelda Sandoval did not lose records. She destroyed them, selectively, deliberately, the way a surgeon removes tissue — and she had done it long before the upriver expedition ever launched.

Which meant the confession wasn't recent. Which meant whatever her mother had to hide, she'd been hiding it for years, possibly decades, and the torn page was less a slip than a message left where only someone who knew her habits would look.

Only someone who knew her habits. Only someone who'd spent summers watching her hide maps from funders, sliding survey sheets into oilcloth sleeves taped under chart tables, lying with a straight face to men in linen suits about how far upstream the team had actually gone. *Never show them the real map,* Imelda had told eight-year-old Zephyrine once, crouched beside her in the shade of a supply tent, tapping the fake route drawn in confident ink. *Show them the map that keeps them happy. Keep the true one where your heart is.* And she'd tapped her daughter's sternum, once, lightly, like knocking on a door.

Zephyrine put the fragment in her breast pocket, over the sternum, and went back to the radio.

* * *

The loop played on. She listened to it until she could recite it, until the words lost meaning and became pure sound, and then listened more until they regained meaning and curdled.

Because here was the thing she couldn't stop noticing, the thing that sat in her stomach like swallowed ballast:

The bearing pointed upstream. Into the gorge. Toward the wreck site — toward the coordinates her mother had sent in that garbled final transmission, the ones Zephyrine had carried upriver like a bone she couldn't stop chewing. Eleven kilometers. Third channel marker past the sunken chapel. Anyone with a chart and a receiver could plot it. Anyone with a boat could reach it.

Anyone with a fleet could strip it.

The Aurelia had salvage sonar, dive tenders, a dredge barge riding at anchor downstream like a patient predator. Vance's operation didn't need luck to find the galleon. It needed a signal. A clean, repeating, authoritative signal, broadcast nightly on a frequency known to be associated with the missing expedition, in the voice of the famous Dr. Imelda Sandoval, giving bearings that led straight to the prize.

Bait doesn't have to be subtle. It just has to smell right.

But if it was bait, why hadn't the fleet taken it? Three weeks of nightly broadcasts. The Aurelia had been on station for days. Either Vance hadn't intercepted the signal — unlikely, given his money — or he had, and was waiting, letting someone else lead him in. Letting *her* lead him in. Cass had said it himself in Puerto Cobre, spitting tobacco into the brown water: *Vance doesn't chase. He lets the current bring things to him.*

Or — and here the hope came back, treacherous, warm, worse than the dread —

Or it was her mother. Alive. Broadcasting blind, on a loop, from somewhere she couldn't stay on air continuously, using a recording because she was injured, or hiding, or rationing battery power, sending bearings not to the wreck but to something near it — the third marker past the sunken chapel, not the chapel itself, not the wreck itself, an offset, a displacement, the kind of misdirection her mother had been playing against funders and rivals for thirty years. *Show them the map that keeps them happy.*

A trap for Vance that looked identical to a lure for Vance. A message meant for family that any enemy could read. That was exactly the kind of problem Imelda Sandoval would build, because it was the kind of problem she was: brilliant, arrogant, certain that she could control who understood her.

Zephyrine pressed her palms flat against the desk and breathed.

The truth was she couldn't tell. Not from a loop. Not from a bearing. The recording gave her nothing to interrogate — no pauses to read, no hesitations, no choice of words to parse. Whoever had cut this tape had cut away everything human in it. All that remained was information, and information could point two directions at once, and Zephyrine had spent seven years learning that the distance between those directions was exactly wide enough to destroy a person.

Hope sharpening into dread. Dread sharpening back into hope. Around and around, like the tide.

* * *

Midnight came, and the transmission stopped mid-cycle — clean cutoff, carrier drop, silence slamming down like a door. Zephyrine sat in the amber afterglow of the dial light and did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding.

Six tides until the dredges could enter the gorge. They'd burned one getting here. The broadcast had been running three weeks, which meant whoever ran it had power, discipline, and a schedule — and schedules could be predicted. Tomorrow night, same band, same hour. If she monitored the shutdown, timed the carrier drop, measured drift in the loop timing night over night, she could fingerprint the transmitter. A generator sagged differently than batteries. A hand-keyed changeover sounded different than an automated timer. Radio operators had tells, the way poker players did, and Zephyrine had grown up reading them through static.

And there was one more thing. She picked up the handset, keyed it, and spoke into the dark band, knowing full well that anyone tuned to 41 meters upstream or down might hear her — including the Aurelia, including Vance, including God.

"Sandoval base calling survey two." Her voice cracked on the second word. She steadied it. "Come back. Over."

Nothing. Static with the texture of deep water.

She tried again, and this time she didn't use the formal calls. "Mom. If that's you — if you're the one sending the bearings — I'm coming. Third marker past the chapel. I'll be there before tide five." A breath. "You don't get to disappear on me twice. Over."

Silence. The river muttered against the tower's foundations, patient as erosion.

Then, from the stairwell, Cass's voice, low: "You just announced our position to every receiver in the valley."

Zephyrine set the handset down. Through the window, far downstream, the Aurelia's anchor lights burned in a neat line, and as she watched, one of them detached itself from the others and began, very slowly, to move.

Upstream.

"I know," she said. "Now we find out who takes the bait first."
