## 8. Vance's Wake

Cass Ibarra had spent eleven years reading the river, and the river was telling him something ugly.

He felt it before he saw it—a change in the water's texture downstream, a long smooth swell where there should have been chop. The Mariposa bucked through the current at half throttle, her cracked exhaust manifold coughing blue smoke into the morning haze, and Cass kept one hand on the wheel and one on the throttle lever the way he'd done since he was fourteen, since before Vance's lawyers took his license and made his name a legal fiction.

"You feel that?" he said.

Zephyrine looked up from the chart she'd been annotating with a grease pencil. She'd been at it for two hours, cross-hatching depth soundings against the tide tables he'd given her, working with a precision that annoyed him because it reminded him of surveyors—of the people who'd drawn the maps that drowned San Remedios in the first place. But she was good. He'd stopped pretending otherwise somewhere around the second narrows.

"Feel what?" she said.

"Flat water. Downstream. Something big is pushing a wake ahead of it."

She stood, shading her eyes against the glare off the water. The gorge walls rose on either side of them, red and green striated stone dripping with vines, and the channel bent ahead around a spur of rock so they could see perhaps four hundred meters before the river turned blind again.

Then the bend gave up its secret.

The ship came around the spur like a wall moving. White superstructure, black hull, cranes folded along her deck like sleeping insects, and painted on her bow in letters tall as a man: AURELIA. Behind her, smaller shapes resolved out of the haze—two barges, a tender, the blunt silhouettes of dredges lashed down for transit. The salvage fleet. Not waiting for the tides at Puerto Cobre after all. Coming to meet the river halfway.

"They're early," Zephyrine said.

"They're always early." Cass cut the throttle and let the Mariposa drift, killing their smoke trail as best he could. "Vance buys time the way other men buy drinks. There'll be a permit office somewhere that suddenly found the paperwork."

The Aurelia filled the channel. She drew too much water for the upper river—that was the whole point of the six-tide window, the whole reason the dredges needed spring high water to make the gorge—and yet here she was, pushing upstream against the ebb with the arrogant confidence of something that had never been told no by a river in its life. Twin diesels thudded across the water, a heartbeat you could feel in the deck plates under Cass's boots.

"She can't run this stretch at speed," he muttered, more to himself than to Zephyrine. "Channel's too narrow past the S-curve. She has to slow to walking pace or she puts her bow through the bank." He glanced at the shoreline, calculating. "There's a backwater behind that gravel bar. If we tuck in and kill the engine—"

"Do it."

He did it. The Mariposa slid off the current's shoulder and into the still brown water behind the bar, and Cass let her drift until the overhanging ceiba branches swallowed them whole. Green light. Insects. The distant thunder of the Aurelia's engines growing.

Zephyrine crouched at the rail with binoculars she'd produced from her mother's field kit—the old brass Zeiss ones, dented, the left eyepiece loose. Cass watched her watch the ship and thought about how strange it was, the way grief and purpose looked identical from the outside. Same stillness. Same locked jaw.

The fleet came abreast of them at less than eighty meters. Close enough to count rivets. Close enough to read the names stenciled on the dredge hulls: CONDOR, HALCÓN, and a third barge with its name plate freshly painted over, some previous owner's identity erased under Vance's white enamel.

And close enough, Cass realized an instant too late, to be seen from the Aurelia's bridge if anyone happened to be looking.

"Down," he breathed.

Zephyrine didn't go down. She went rigid instead, the binoculars locked on a point near the ship's stern rail, and her face did something Cass had no word for—a collapse and a hardening happening in the same second, like ice forming over moving water.

"What," he said. "What do you see?"

Her voice came out flat. "Tomás Reyes."

* * *

Cass knew the name the way everyone on the river knew it. Tomás Reyes, first mate of the Sandoval expedition, the loyal right hand, the man who'd radioed the foundation when the expedition went silent. His face had been on the news feeds for a week—hollow-eyed, grieving, appealing for information about Dr. Imelda Sandoval's disappearance while standing in front of a wall of microphones.

Now he stood at the Aurelia's stern rail wearing a Vance Maritime uniform shirt, khaki and pressed, a clipboard under one arm, supervising the lashings on a dredge crane with the easy authority of a man who belonged exactly where he was.

"He's supposed to be searching for her," Zephyrine said. Each word came out separately, like stones placed one at a time. "He's supposed to be—" She stopped. Started again. "I've known him since I was nine years old. He taught me to read current lines. He carried me on his shoulders through the market at Puerto Cobre. He—" Her jaw worked. "He was the closest thing to family I had besides her."

Cass said nothing. There was nothing useful to say. He'd watched enough men sell each other out on this river to know that the arithmetic was almost always simple: fear plus money equals loyalty for sale. He watched her grip the rail and waited for the arithmetic to land on her too.

The Aurelia's engines changed pitch. The ship was slowing for the S-curve, just as Cass had predicted, her bow wave flattening, the whole convoy settling into the cautious crawl of deep draft in shallow water. They would hang in view for long minutes. Too long. Every second increased the odds that someone on that bridge would notice the shape of a boat tucked in the backwater, the glint of binoculars in the shade.

"We should move farther in," Cass whispered. "Under the branches. Now."

But Zephyrine wasn't listening. She was watching Tomás Reyes walk the length of the stern deck, checking lashings, and Cass saw her expression shift from betrayal toward something colder and more deliberate—the look of a woman memorizing a face she intended to find later, under circumstances of her own choosing.

Then Reyes stopped.

He stopped mid-stride, clipboard forgotten, and turned his head—not toward the shore generally, but toward their specific patch of shadow, with the unerring accuracy of a man who had spent thirty years reading rivers and knew exactly where a shallow-draft boat would hide behind a gravel bar.

Cass's hand moved to the throttle.

"No," Zephyrine breathed. "Wait."

Reyes raised the clipboard. Made a show of consulting it. And then, in a sequence so casual it might have been nothing at all, he lifted his free hand and scratched his head—once, twice—and then held up three fingers as if counting lashings, folding them down one at a time, slow, deliberate, facing their direction.

Three fingers. Then a flat palm swept low, twice, toward the water.

Cass felt the hair rise on his arms. He'd grown up with river signals; every pilot who ran the unofficial channels had. Three fingers meant three hours, or three kilometers, or third watch. The low sweep meant *go quiet, go low, stay hidden*. It was the oldest code on the water, older than radios, older than licenses, passed from smugglers and fisherfolk and anyone whose survival depended on not being seen by the wrong boat.

Or it meant nothing. Or it meant bait.

"That's either a warning," Cass said quietly, "or he's telling Vance exactly where we are and enjoying the theater."

"I know." Zephyrine hadn't lowered the binoculars. Through the lenses, Cass knew, she'd see what he saw: Reyes turning away, resuming his inspection, but leaving his body angled toward their bank a few degrees longer than chance allowed. A door held open a crack. "I know it could be either."

"And?"

"And my mother trusted him with her life for twenty years." Her voice was steady now, which frightened Cass more than the shaking had. "Either he betrayed her, in which case I need to know why. Or he's betraying Vance right now, in which case I need to know what it costs him. Either way, the answer isn't going to come to me from behind a gravel bar."

The last finger of the convoy—the repainted barge—slid past their hiding place, dragging a wake that rocked the Mariposa against the bar with a soft grinding kiss. Then the bend took the fleet, and the engine thunder faded upstream, and the river closed over the moment like water over a dropped coin.

They sat in the green silence. Insects resumed. Somewhere a kingfisher rattled.

"He slowed the ship down," Cass said finally. The thought had been assembling itself in the back of his skull and now it came out whole. "Back at the S-curve. He was standing next to the helmsman. I saw him lean in before we reached the bend. A first mate doesn't advise the bridge on local waters unless he wants to be heard." He turned to look at her. "He bought us those minutes. Maybe without knowing we were there. Maybe knowing exactly."

Zephyrine lowered the binoculars at last. Her knuckles were white around them.

"Three fingers," she said. "Three hours, or three kilometers, or third watch."

"Third watch," Cass said. "Tonight. If it's tonight, he means to come to you—or he means for you to come to him. Either way, you'll want to decide before dark which it is, because the answer changes what river we're sitting on when it happens."

She looked at him then, really looked, and Cass understood that he had just crossed some line he couldn't uncross—from hired pilot to accomplice, from man-for-pay to the other person in the boat. He found, examining himself honestly, that he didn't mind. Vance had taken his license, his livelihood, and eleven years of his life. Any enemy of that arithmetic was worth drifting in the shadows with.

"Third watch," Zephyrine repeated. She tucked the binoculars into the field kit with hands that were almost steady. "Then we don't run the open channel tonight. We anchor in the reeds above the old ferry crossing, lights cold, engine cold, and we wait to see whether Tomás Reyes is a traitor or the only honest man on that ship."

"And if he's a traitor?"

She was quiet for a moment. The current worried at the gravel bar, patient as everything else on this river.

"Then I'll know," she said, "that everyone my mother ever loved can be bought. And I'd rather learn it from him, looking him in the eye, than from a salvage manifest."

Cass pushed the Mariposa out of the backwater and turned her nose upstream, into the fading wake of the fleet, running dark water and shallow channels he could navigate with his eyes closed. Ahead, around the next blind bend, the Aurelia's smoke hung in the windless air like a question mark.

Three fingers. Third watch.

By nightfall, one of them would have an answer—and Cass had been on this river long enough to know that answers, up here in the gorge, almost always arrived with a price attached.
