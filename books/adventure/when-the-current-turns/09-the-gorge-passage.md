## 9. The Gorge Passage

Cass wouldn't take the narrows until the tide was exactly wrong.

I told him that. I stood on the deck of the *Mariposa* with the survey tables open on my knees and the last light bleeding out of the gorge walls and said, plainly, "The water drops for another forty minutes. Every pilot on this river waits for slack."

"Every pilot on this river," Cass said, feeding fuel to the engine like a man feeding a fire he didn't trust, "waits for slack because every pilot on this river learned from the same manual, and the manual was written by a man who never ran this stretch at night." He spat over the rail. "The slack is a lie here. The gorge holds a back-eddy below the second bend. When the surface goes flat, the undertow's still running hard enough to pin you to the shelf. You want to go through when the river's still falling — fast enough that the eddy can't set."

"That's not in any table I've ever read."

"No," he said. "It's in my hands."

I should have argued. Seven years of contract work had taught me that the men who said *trust me, I know this water* were the same men who left you swimming. But I'd watched Cass read the current for two days now — watched him read it the way I read contour lines, not looking at the water but through it, at the shape of the channel underneath — and I was beginning to suspect that his disgraced license and my forged credentials had been issued by the same office: the one that punishes people for being right about things powerful people don't want to hear.

So I said, "Then we go now," and I took the bow with the floodlamp and let him take the wheel, and the *Mariposa* turned her nose into the dark mouth of the gorge.

* * *

The narrows were a kilometer of river squeezed between walls the color of wet slate, close enough that I could have thrown a line to either bank. The floodlamp carved a white cone out of the black and the current filled it — not water so much as argument, the whole river's weight funneled through a throat too narrow for it, folding and refolding over rocks we'd never see until we hit them.

"Read for me," Cass called from the wheel. "You see foam, you call it. You see glass, you call it. Don't interpret. Just call."

"Foam, starboard, ten meters."

"Foam's shallow. Good. Keep us off it anyway."

"Glass — dead center, past the bend."

"Glass is deep or it's a lie. Watch it."

The boat bucked. My teeth clicked shut. Somewhere behind us, downriver, the running lights of the salvage fleet's scout boat sat at anchor exactly where a cautious man would anchor — too far back, waiting on the tide tables, waiting on permission. Vance's people ran on schedules and lawyers. We were running on a disgraced pilot's hands and a falling tide, and for the first time since Puerto Cobre, I liked our odds.

"Second bend," Cass said. "Here's where the eddy lives. When I kill the engine, don't say a word. Just watch the water and tell me what it does."

"You're going to *drift* it?"

"I'm going to let the river carry us through the part of the river that's still honest."

He cut the engine. The silence was enormous — gorge silence, the kind that has weight — and the *Mariposa* swung her stern toward the starboard wall and began to slide. I watched the water off the port bow. It looked flat. It looked like every slack-water photograph in every manual I'd ever studied. And then the floodlamp caught it — a seam in the surface, a long unbroken line of glass running diagonal from the port wall toward midstream, and beneath the glass, faint as a watermark, a suggestion of movement. Not foam. Not ripples. The surface holding perfectly still while something underneath moved against it.

"Cass. The glass — it's moving. Underneath. It's running *upriver*."

The eddy. The back-current he'd sworn by, setting exactly as he'd said it would, invisible on the surface, strong enough below to take a boat and fold it against the rock.

"Engine," he said, and I'd already thrown the starter, and the *Mariposa* caught on the second crank just as the stern began to walk. Cass hauled the wheel hard over and we came out of the eddy's grip with the hull groaning and the floodlamp swinging wild across the walls, and for three full seconds we were sideways in the current, a leaf on its edge, and then the nose came around and we were through the second bend and running.

Cass let out a breath that sounded like it had been held since Puerto Cobre.

"Nobody calls that seam," he said. "Twenty years on this river, I've never once seen anyone call that seam."

"My mother taught me to read water before she taught me to read," I said. It came out before I could decide whether it was true. It was true. "She said the surface is the river's public face. You survey the private one."

He glanced back at me — one second, no more, the wheel steady in his grip — and something in his face rearranged itself. Not warmth, exactly. Recognition. The look of a man discovering that the stranger in his boat has the same scar in a different place.

"Third bend's the shelf," he said. "Low water, it's a foot under. High water, you can't see it at all, which is how it kills people. We'll be somewhere in between."

* * *

The shelf announced itself as a change in the sound.

I'd been listening without deciding to — the way you do after years of listening to rivers through the hull of a workboat, cataloguing frequencies. The gorge roar had a texture to it, and two hundred meters past the third bend the texture changed: a new note underneath, thin and hard, like wire under tension. Cass heard it too. I saw his shoulders come up.

"Rocks," he said. "But where?"

I got the floodlamp down off its bracket and held it low, near the waterline, almost horizontal. High light off black water shows you glare. Low light shows you what's actually there — the difference between a photograph and a survey. The beam raked the surface and there it was, a half-meter of dark water lying over a paler dark, a seam of color where the river shallowed from three meters to less than one, running not where the foam said it should but a full boat-width to port.

"Shelf's port. It runs diagonal. The channel's a boat-width, maybe less, hard to starboard — but the current's pushing us port."

"Then we go against it."

"At night. In this."

"You have a better channel?"

I didn't. I got to the bow cleat, ran the line through, and braced. "I'll call the line. You hold it. If we touch, we touch stern-first, engine up."

"Engine stays down. If I lift it we lose steerage and the river decides."

"The river's already decided. It decided to kill us. I'm overruling it."

He laughed — one short bark, no humor in it, all adrenaline — and brought the *Mariposa* around, and we entered the channel.

It was like threading a needle held by a hand that wanted to close. The current shoved us port and Cass countered and I called the line — *starboard, starboard, hold — port, two meters, port* — and the pale seam of the shelf slid past our hull close enough that I could have trailed my fingers along it. The boat's draft kissed it once. Not a hit. A kiss. A long shuddering scrape along the keel that I felt in my back teeth, and then the channel widened, and the shelf fell away beneath us, and the gorge opened its throat and let us go.

We came out into the lower pool under a sky suddenly full of stars, the walls dropped back on either side, and the engine note changed — and I heard it before Cass did. A new rattle in it. A rhythm gone slightly wrong, like a heartbeat with a skipped beat.

He throttled down and put his ear to the deck plates and his face, when it came up, was grim in the floodlamp's spill.

"We're taking water."

* * *

The crack was in the hull seam, port side, aft of where the shelf had kissed us — a weep, not a gush, a thin bright thread of river finding its way in through a joint that twenty years of patches had held together through worse. Cass got the bilge pump running and stuffed the crack with a bar of soap and a strip of canvas, the oldest trick on the river, and I held the lamp while he worked, and neither of us said anything for a long time.

The water in the bilge stopped rising. The pump held it there. It would hold until we reached somewhere with tools, and somewhere with tools was two hours downstream of where we needed to be.

"That shelf," Cass said finally, sitting back on his heels. "The manuals put it midstream."

"I know."

"Twenty years I've run this gorge and I've never once hit that seam, because I never once ran it this close to low water, and every chart I've ever seen has that shelf a boat-width to starboard of where it actually is." He looked at me across the bilge water and the lamplight. "The river moved it."

"Rivers do that."

"Rivers do it slow. That shelf moved in one flood season. Somebody blasted it." He wiped his hands on his trousers. "Vance's survey crews. Making the narrows hostile for anyone running without his tide tables. He doesn't just own the salvage fleet. He's been editing the river."

I sat down on the engine housing, and the two of us sat there in the rocking dark, the pump chugging its stubborn rhythm, and I thought about my mother's field kit in the hold below, the torn journal page, the maps she'd hidden from funders all those summers. A woman who surveyed the river's private face. A magnate who rewrote it.

"He's not just stripping a wreck," I said. "He's rewriting the evidence."

"Then we'd better get to it before he finishes the edit." Cass stood, tested the pump, nodded at the sound of it. "Camp's an hour up this pool, around the point. Expedition site. Your mother's people."

The word *camp* landed in my chest like a dropped anchor. Three missed check-ins. A garbled fragment of her voice saying *return.* A scout boat anchored suspiciously far downstream, as if its job was not to find the wreck but to watch the approaches.

"An hour," I said.

"An hour," Cass said, "if the pump holds."

I went forward to take the bow watch, and behind me the *Mariposa* limped up the black pool with her cracked seam singing, and ahead, around the point, there was a light where there should have been no light at all — a single white flare, rising slow, hanging in the gorge dark like a question nobody upriver had answered in three weeks.

Cass saw it too. He cut the engine to idle, and we drifted toward it in silence, both of us staring, neither of us saying the thing we were both thinking:

That nobody fires a flare for a camp that's fine.
