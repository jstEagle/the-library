## 10. Camp of the Missing

The gorge narrowed for two hours after the narrows, walls closing in like a throat, and then it opened without warning into a shelf of flat gray stone where the river slowed and spread and forgot itself. Cass cut the engine and let the Mariposa drift.

"There," he said.

I saw it before he pointed. A cluster of canvas domes on the gravel bar at the river's edge, maybe forty meters upstream. Expedition tents—my mother's expedition tents, the pale green ones with the foundation's crest stitched on the fly. I'd grown up under those tents. I'd learned to read tide tables on the crates they came packed in.

Nobody moved among them.

"Could be they're upriver," Cass said. "Working the site."

"No." My voice came out flat, certain, the way my mother's voice went when she knew something before she could explain it. "She never leaves camp half-struck. If they'd moved operations, the mess tent would be down and the latrine trench filled. Look at the guy lines."

He looked. He didn't ask how I knew. That was one of the things I was learning to tolerate about Cass Ibarra—he absorbed information instead of demanding it, banked it like a man who'd been burned by asking questions at the wrong moments.

We tied off below the camp and walked up through shallows that sucked at our boots. The light was going long and amber, that hour when everything looks preserved, embalmed in honey. The first tent's flap hung open. Inside: two bedrolls, unrolled but unslept-in, still tight as bandages. A lantern with a full fuel gauge. Someone's paperback swollen with damp, abandoned face-down on a crate.

The mess tent told the rest of it. Four tin plates on the folding table. Rice gone to concrete. Beans skinned over with a gray film. A pot of coffee that had evaporated down to tar. Mugs beside the plates, one of them tipped on its side, dried in a brown starburst across the table's aluminum skin.

"They left mid-meal," I said.

"People leave mid-meal when something spooks them," Cass said. "Animal comes into camp. Rockslide noise up the gorge."

"Four people don't all forget their boots." I nodded at the tent entrance, where four pairs of heavy rubber work boots sat in a neat row, laces tucked in, the way you store them overnight. Not the way you flee in.

Cass crouched by the fire ring. Cold ash, weeks old at the bottom, but a thinner layer above it—someone had burned paper recently. He sifted it with a stick. Fragments. I took one between my fingers: heavy bond paper, watermarked. Foundation letterhead. The corner of a printed chart, its contour lines still legible where the flame hadn't reached.

"They burned their own documents," I said.

"Or someone made them."

* * *

The radio tent was the worst.

It stood at the camp's high point, which was correct protocol—elevation for transmission—and its flap was torn rather than opened, ripped along the seam like something had come through it fast. Inside, the equipment had not simply been disabled. It had been executed. The main transceiver lay on its back with its chassis staved in, tubes shattered, the tuning dial cracked clean through. The backup unit had been hammered against the support pole until the casing folded. Cables were cut, not unplugged—cut short, close to the housing, so nothing could be spliced. The antenna wire had been dropped from its mast and slashed in six places.

"You don't do that to stop a call," Cass said quietly. "You do that to make sure nobody ever tries again."

I stood in the wreckage and felt something cold move through me that wasn't fear exactly. Fear is about what might happen. This was about what already had. My mother had built that radio schedule herself—three check-ins a week, 8 megacycles, dusk and dawn windows, drilled into every crew member since before I was born. *The river doesn't forgive silence,* she used to say. *Neither do I.* Three missed check-ins had brought me here. And someone had made sure there would be no fourth, no fifth, no explanation, no distress call. Just static, and me listening to it a thousand miles downstream, imagining the worst, because the worst was the only thing left to imagine.

There was a logbook clipped to the tent pole. Water-stained, but the last entries survived. I read them twice.

The final entry was in Tomás Reyes' hand—I'd know that cramped backward-slanting script anywhere; he'd written out my tenth-birthday card in the same hand, *to Zephyrine, who steers by her own stars*, and I had hated him a little even then for knowing me so well. The entry read:

*Day 41. Site confirmed. Dr. S. orders halt to salvage ops pending review. Crew uneasy. Vance's scouts sighted downstream again. Dr. S. says let them watch.*

Pending review. My mother had found whatever she'd come for and ordered a full stop. Imelda Sandoval did not stop. Imelda Sandoval once worked through a broken wrist and a fever of a hundred and three because a trench wall wouldn't hold another day. A halt order meant she'd seen something that frightened her more than failure did.

Below that entry, in different ink, fresher: a single line in my mother's hand, the sharp forward slant I'd spent seven years trying not to imitate.

*If anyone reads this—we were wrong about what we found. It was never cargo.*

* * *

We searched the rest of the camp while the light died. Six tents total, a supply cache, a surveyor's bench with instruments still laid out under an oilcloth. No weapons missing—the rifle rack held all four rifles, bolts closed, unfired. Whatever had emptied this camp hadn't been met with force. It had been met with obedience, or surprise, or both.

Cass found the trail heading upriver from the far tent line: boot prints in the mud, several sets, adult-sized, walking—not running—toward the gorge's upper reach. Some of the prints were shallow, uneven. Someone had been limping.

And then I found the grave.

It was set back from the camp on a rise of dry ground, where the foundation would have put it, because my mother had written the field manual that said graves go on dry ground above the flood line, marked and recorded, always. It was fresh—days old, not weeks. The earth was mounded with care, edged with stones pried from the riverbank, and at its head, driven into the soil upright, was a compass.

Not just any compass. A brass surveyor's compass with a cracked crystal and a lanyard worn smooth as rope. I had watched that compass swing on my mother's neck my entire childhood. She'd tapped it against her teeth when she thought. She'd pressed it into my palm once, at fourteen, and said *you always know which way you're facing, Zephyrine, that's your gift and your curse,* and then taken it back because she wasn't finished needing it.

It was hers. There was no version of events in which Imelda Sandoval surrendered that compass voluntarily.

Cass came and stood beside me. He read the mound the way he read water, silently, thoroughly. Then he said, gently, in the voice of a man delivering bad news to a stranger whose name he doesn't know yet:

"It's a proper grave. Somebody cared enough to do it right."

"I know what it is."

"A body's under there, most likely. Given the limp in those prints—"

"Don't." The word came out of me like a round leaving a chamber. Cass stopped talking. Good. Because if he kept going, if he said *your mother* or *I'm sorry* or anything with grief in it, I was going to break apart on this gravel bar, and I could not afford to break apart, because breaking apart was a luxury for people whose mothers were alive.

I knelt at the edge of the mound. The soil was dark river loam, turned recently, settled by rain but not much rain. I put my hand flat on it. Cool. Damp underneath.

"She's not here," I said.

"Zephyrine."

"She's *not here.*" I started pulling stones from the edge, fast, my fingers scraping dirt. "Look at the depth. Look at it. This mound is eight inches high over a pit maybe two feet deep. You don't bury a person in two feet of soil on a riverbank—you dig down four, five feet, or the water table floods the grave and everything surfaces in a week. My mother wrote the manual. Whoever dug this knew the manual. They wouldn't bury her shallow."

"Then why dig at all?"

"For a marker." I was digging now, both hands, throwing soil aside, and I didn't care. "For a message. For *me.*"

Cass hesitated one second longer—long enough that I understood he thought I'd lost my mind, and long enough that he decided to help anyway. He pulled his knife and cut into the near side of the mound, and we opened it together, the two of us, in the last gray light, like gravediggers in reverse.

Eighteen inches down, wrapped in oilcloth, we found a surveyor's field case. Brass corners. Dented. Familiar.

Inside: my mother's spare notebook, the waterproof kind, half-full. A folded chart of the upper gorge with a cavern system inked in by hand, annotations in her shorthand—*sonar-blind, air pocket likely, approach only at slack*. And beneath the chart, sealed in a waxed envelope, a single page torn from her personal journal, addressed in block letters:

*FOR ZEPHYRINE. NO ONE ELSE.*

My hands shook so badly I nearly tore it getting the flap open. One page. Her handwriting, hurried, the letters crowded, written by penlight or dawn or some other thin light:

*They know what I have. Vance's men gave me a choice: hand it over or the crew pays for it. So I sent the crew downriver and stayed. The grave is a lie I asked Tomás to build—bury the case, mark it with my compass, let them think me dead and lose interest. If you are reading this, you got past the lie, which means you never believed it, which means I owe your stubbornness an apology I'll deliver in person.*

*I am alive. I am north of the false site, in the drowned church country. Trust Reyes. Trust no one else. Do not bring the fleet. Do not bring the law. Bring only someone who can read water.*

*And Zephyrine—what I stole, I mean to give back. Help me finish it.*

I read it three times. The third time my vision blurred and I let it, kneeling in the ruined grave with the dirt of a fake burial under my nails, laughing once—a hard, ugly, disbelieving sound that scared a heron off the far bank.

"She's alive," I said. "She faked her own death and used her own compass as a headstone, because she knew—" My throat closed. I pushed through it. "Because she knew I'd come. She knew I'd dig. Seven years of not calling me back and she still bet everything on me reading her dirt."

Cass was quiet a moment. Then: "Tomás Reyes. First mate. The man you said—"

"Who signed the logbook. Who built the grave. Who is, according to every scrap of evidence in this camp, either the most loyal man on the river or the best liar in it." I stood, wiping my hands on my thighs, and felt the grief drain out of me and something hotter pour in to fill the space, something with direction and velocity. Anger, but aimed. Anger with a bearing. "Vance thinks my mother is dead. Vance's divers are working a false site. And somewhere north of here, in flooded church country, Imelda Sandoval is waiting for a pilot who can read water."

Cass looked at the Mariposa, riding low on her mooring in the dusk. "Tide turns in four hours," he said. "Current runs north through the upper gorge till midnight."

"Then we're not spending them here."

I took the compass from the headstone—it was mine now, apparently, by right of belief—and drove it into the empty grave sideways, blade-deep in the soil, pointing north. Let Vance's scouts find a robbed grave and wonder. Let them waste a tide on it.

Behind us, the camp sat silent in the dark, plates molding on the table, radios murdered in their tent, holding its breath around the question I refused to ask: *where is Tomás Reyes now, and whose side is he standing on tonight?*

The river answered first. Far upstream, around the gorge's northern bend, a searchlight swept the water—slow, methodical, the sweep of a ship looking for something small.

The Aurelia was ahead of us.
