## 11. Tomás in the Reeds

The Aurelia smelled of money and rot, and Tomás Reyes had learned to breathe both without flinching.

He stood at the aft rail past midnight, watching the searchlights sweep the black water below the gorge. Vance's fleet rode at anchor like a string of lit lanterns strung too far upriver—arrogant, certain that nothing in this drowned country could touch them. Sixty meters of salvage ship, helipad, decompression chamber, a winch system worth more than San Remedios had seen in its entire flooded history. And beneath all that chrome and diesel, a cargo manifest with one line item that made Tomás's hands shake every time he read it: *Sandoval Codex — provenance pending.*

Provenance pending. As if you could file paperwork on a lie thirty years old.

Behind him, the card game in the crew mess was breaking up. He heard Vance's voice, smooth as bilge oil, telling some junior surveyor about the tide schedule. Three tides left before the dredges could work the main channel. Three tides, and then the machines would start eating the river.

Tomás checked his watch. Shift change at 0100. The night dive crew would be sleeping off their bottom time, the deck crew would be short-handed, and the man on the tender dock was a kid from Puerto Cobre who took bribes in cigarettes and didn't ask questions.

He'd planned this for nine days. He'd told himself he was waiting for the right conditions. That was a lie too. He'd been waiting for courage, and courage, he'd learned, doesn't arrive—you have to go collect it yourself, usually at night, usually alone, usually from a place you swore you'd never go back to.

* * *

He went over the side at 0112, into water so dark it felt like falling.

The swim to shore was four hundred meters. He'd done it once before, three weeks ago, when he'd first understood what Vance intended—not salvage but strip-mining, not archaeology but theft with a permit—and he'd swum back shaking, telling himself there was still time, still options, still a way out that didn't end with him in the reeds with nothing but the clothes on his back.

There wasn't. There never had been. He'd known it since the day Imelda Sandoval looked him in the eye aboard her own expedition boat and said, *Tomás, I need you to hate me convincingly.*

The mud took him to the knees. The reeds closed over his head, eight feet of dry whispering stalks, and he stood there in the shallows breathing hard, listening to the Aurelia's generators fade behind him, feeling the river press against his chest like a hand deciding whether to hold him or push him under.

His duffel was where he'd cached it, wedged in the roots of a fallen ceiba: dry clothes, a waterproof bag with the documents, a machete, and the thing he'd carried for three weeks without being able to look at directly—a brass compass, its needle trembling, that he'd pried out of a grave marker with his own two hands.

He shouldered the bag and started walking north along the bank, toward the abandoned camp, toward the place where the Mariposa would be hiding if Cass Ibarra was half the pilot people said he used to be.

Toward Zephyrine Sandoval, who hated him, and had every reason to.

* * *

He found their fire at dawn. Or rather, he found the smoke—or the absence of it, a cold ring of stones tucked under an overhang where no aircraft would spot it, which told him they were smarter than he'd hoped. He circled the position twice, hands raised, calling out softly.

"Zephyrine. It's Tomás. I'm alone. I'm unarmed."

The machete came out of the brush at his throat before he finished the sentence. Not Zephyrine—the pilot, Cass, moving quiet as water, eyes flat with a hatred Tomás recognized because he wore it himself these days.

"You've got nerve," Cass said. "Walking in here after what your signature did."

"My signature didn't do anything. That's rather the point." He kept his hands wide. "Zephyrine. Please. Your mother is alive."

The silence that followed lasted long enough for the birds to start up again in the canopy. Then she stepped out of the shadow of the overhang, and Tomás felt twenty years fall away and land on him all at once. She had Imelda's jaw. She had Imelda's way of going absolutely still when the world hurt, as if stillness were armor.

"The grave," Zephyrine said. Her voice didn't shake. "We saw the grave."

"Is empty." He crouched slowly, keeping his movements deliberate, and unslung the duffel. "I dug it myself. Four days after the expedition 'vanished.' Vance wanted a body. A body ends questions. So we gave him a marker instead of a corpse, and I put your mother's compass on it because—" He stopped. Swallowed. "Because she asked me to. She said you'd come looking eventually. She said you'd know what it meant."

"She left her compass on a fake grave," Zephyrine said, "as a message."

"She left it as proof the grave was fake. You're her daughter. You'd check. Everyone else would grieve. You'd dig."

Something moved through Zephyrine's face—relief trying to be born and getting strangled by everything else. Tomás knew the feeling intimately. He'd been living inside it for three weeks.

"Sit down," she said finally. "All of you talk fast, and then you're going to tell me everything, starting with why my mother faked her own death instead of just leaving."

* * *

They gave him coffee that tasted like bark, and he talked with his hands around the tin cup because his mouth kept wanting to stop.

"After the discovery—the real discovery, the codex—Vance's lawyers came aboard within seventy-two hours. Not to negotiate. To own. They had papers tying half the expedition's funding to his shell companies, loans your mother signed years ago when nobody thought the galleon was real. Either she signed the codex over, or the fleet seized everything and the expedition became his, legally, retroactively." He turned the cup. "Your mother signed something. It wasn't what he thought."

"What did she sign?"

"A confession. Dated, witnessed, sealed. Stating that the Sandoval Codex was recovered from the flooded church at San Remedios eleven years ago—that the find that built your family name was taken from these people, undocumented, undisclosed, and sold into private collection. She named herself. She named the funders. She named me." He met Zephyrine's eyes. "She named everyone, Zeph. Including the part where Vance's money had been laundering the proceeds since before you were born."

Cass let out a low breath. "Blackmail goes both ways."

"That document is the only reason she's alive. Vance can't touch her while it exists—he needs to know where it is. So she staged the disappearance. Radio silence, abandoned camp, a story big enough to pull his whole fleet sixty miles upriver, away from the true site." He paused. "Which isn't the galleon. The galleon is a decoy. It always was. Your mother found it years ago and let it sit because there was nothing in it worth the trouble—it was cover. The real site is the old church itself. The codex came out of that nave, and there are things down there she never reported. Things the village has a better claim to than any museum."

Zephyrine had gone very still again. "Where is she now?"

"In an air pocket in the lower gallery. Injured—her leg, a rockfall during the staging. There's a diver among Vance's crew, Elena Vasco, who owes your mother her life from a cave collapse in '19. Elena's been running supplies down on pretense of survey dives. Food, antibiotics, a radio." He reached into the waterproof bag. "And this."

He set the brass compass on the stone between them. Then, beside it, a folded page torn from a field journal, soft as cloth from handling.

Zephyrine didn't touch either. "You said Vance coerced you. Say it plainly."

"He owns my debt. My sister's medical debt, specifically, and my house, and my license history—he bought the paper on all of it the year before the expedition. I signed on as first mate knowing exactly what I was. His man inside." Tomás's voice went rough. "I told myself I'd protect the crew from the inside. Then your mother handed me that confession and asked me to help her disappear, and I realized protecting the crew and betraying her had become the same act. So I dug the grave. I fed Vance the story he wanted. And I've spent three weeks feeding him just enough truth to keep him confident and just enough delay to keep him off balance." He laughed, and it came out bitter. "I'm very good at my job. It's the only thing I've ever been good at. It turns out the job was treason the whole time—I just changed whose."

"Why come to us?" Cass said. "Why not run? You had the documents. You had a head start."

"Because documents don't dive." Tomás nodded at the compass. "Because the tide clock is against us—three tides, maybe less, before Vance stops playing patient and starts dredging blind. Because Elena can't hold the air pocket much longer; the silt load is rising and the gallery entrance is narrowing by the day. And because—" he looked at Zephyrine, and made himself finish it— "because your mother wrote a letter, and she addressed it to you, and I promised I'd put it in your hands even if it cost me everything. Which it will. By tomorrow night, Vance will know I'm gone, and he'll know what I took, and there is nowhere on this river I'll be safe."

Zephyrine picked up the page at last. Unfolded it. Read.

Tomás watched her read it and saw the exact moment the words landed—saw her shoulders drop a centimeter, saw her breath go out of her like a tide pulling back. Whatever Imelda had written, it wasn't apology. He knew Imelda. It would be coordinates, and instructions, and one line somewhere near the end that would cut her daughter open and stitch her shut in the same stroke.

"Well?" Cass asked quietly.

Zephyrine folded the page along its original creases, precise as a surveyor, and when she looked up her eyes were wet and furious and more awake than Tomás had ever seen them.

"She's alive," Zephyrine said. "And we have three tides to get to her before Vance's divers do." She stood, already moving, already packing. "Tomás—you know the Aurelia's dive rotations, their sonar gaps, their shift changes. You're coming with us, and you're going to earn back every ounce of what you cost this family. Cass—how fast can the Mariposa run loaded?"

Cass was already on his feet. "Fast enough. Barely."

"Then we move now." She slung her mother's field kit across her shoulder, and for a moment she looked so much like Imelda that Tomás had to look away. "Three tides. We dive on the second one, at slack water, through the cavern mouth before their sonar recalibrates. Which means tonight we sleep in shifts, and tomorrow we make the gorge passage in daylight, in plain view, and pray the river is on our side."

"The river," Cass said, "is on nobody's side."

"No," Zephyrine agreed, and almost smiled. "But it remembers who belongs to it."

Tomás gathered his duffel and followed them down to the water, and behind them the sun came up over the gorge like a coin dropped into a well—gold sinking, light rising, and somewhere beneath it all, in the cold and the dark, a woman with a broken leg holding a stolen truth in her two bare hands, waiting to see if her daughter would come.
