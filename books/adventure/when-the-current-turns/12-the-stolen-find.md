## 12. The Stolen Find

Tomás Reyes had not stopped shaking since he'd crawled out of the reeds an hour before dawn.

He sat on the Mariposa's deck planks with a tin cup of coffee he hadn't touched, wrapped in Cass's spare blanket, and told it in pieces — the way men tell things they've rehearsed in their heads so long the words have worn smooth. Vance's lawyers. The debt Tomás owed from his brother's medical bills. The night Imelda Sandoval stood in the chart room of her own expedition ship and told him, very quietly, that she was going to disappear.

"She said, *You'll know what to do when the time comes.*" He laughed, a dry sound with nothing in it. "I didn't. I still don't."

Zephyrine crouched across from him, forearms on her knees, watching the river lighten behind him. The grave with her mother's compass was two hundred meters back through the trees, and she had made herself walk past it this morning without stopping. That had cost something. She kept the receipt folded away where it couldn't reach her voice.

"You're telling me she faked her own death," Zephyrine said. "To pull the fleet off the real site."

"To buy time." Tomás set the cup down because his hands couldn't hold it steady and lie about it at the same time. "Vance wanted the wreck stripped inside a season. Your mother wanted — I don't even know what she wanted. Not the gold. I'll tell you that much for free. She spent three nights after we found the hold just sitting in the dark with the manifest, not saying anything. Then she started moving equipment at night. Then she was gone."

Cass leaned against the rail, arms crossed, chewing on the inside of his cheek. He'd been quiet since Tomás surfaced out of the black water like something the river had decided to give back. "The grave," Cass said. "Empty?"

"Sandbags and a coat. I helped dig it." Tomás looked at Zephyrine then, and there was no flinching in him, which she found worse than flinching would have been. "I'm sorry. I know what you thought you found yesterday."

"I thought I found my mother dead." Her voice came out flat and strange, like someone else's. "Now you're telling me I found a prop."

"I'm telling you she's alive, and that's not mercy either. Because if she's alive, she's been down there alone for eleven days with a broken wrist and half a tank of air cycling through a scrubber she rigged herself, and she did it knowing Vance would come for her the moment he figured out the trick." Tomás dragged a hand down his face. "She counted on you, Zeph. That message on the old band — the coordinates, the word *return*. She keyed it to your call sign. Nobody else's. She wasn't calling the foundation. She wasn't calling me. She was calling you."

Zephyrine stood up. Walked to the rail. The river went on doing what rivers do, which is pretend to be indifferent.

*Return.*

Seven years of not answering her calls, of letting the foundation's letters pile up unopened in rented rooms from Belize to Barranquilla, and the first thing her mother ever asked of her directly was one word, broadcast into static, trusting that Zephyrine would be listening.

She had almost not been listening. That was the part that sat in her chest like swallowed glass.

* * *

They worked through the morning because work was the only thing any of them could do that didn't feel like waiting to die. Cass patched the cracked seam with resin and canvas while Tomás, who knew engines from twenty years on the river, held plates and swore fluently in three languages. Zephyrine ran the survey lines again, plotting the bearing from the repeating signal against the gorge topography she'd sketched from the ridge.

It was while she was cross-referencing the signal's shifting bearings — they converged, all of them, on a point upstream of the wreck's registered position, tucked into the north wall where the sonar returns went to mush — that Tomás came and stood over her chart table and went very still.

"What?" she said.

"Show me the convergence point."

She tapped it. He stared at it for a long moment, and something moved across his face that she'd only seen once before, years ago, on a dock in Puerto Cobre when a storm report came in wrong: the look of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't want the answer to.

"That's not the galleon," he said.

"It's within sixty meters of—"

"It's not the galleon." He pulled the pencil out of her hand and drew, fast, a rough profile of the north wall: the shelf, the scree fan, the fissure system the villagers' guides had described. "The *Santa Elena*'s hold sits in the channel scour, forty meters down, open to the river. We mapped it. I was on the mapping team. There is no air pocket anywhere near the wreck — I'd stake my license on it, what's left of it. Whatever your mother is hiding behind, whatever she's breathing, it's in the caverns. And the thing she wouldn't let Vance touch—" He stopped.

"The codex," Zephyrine said.

Tomás looked at her.

"San Remedios," she said. "The elder, Doña Eufemia. When she spat at our name, she said something I didn't understand. She said, *Ask her what she took from the church.* I thought she meant the salvage rights. I thought she meant the dam." Zephyrine's mouth had gone dry somewhere in the last ten seconds. "Tomás. What did my mother take from the church?"

He sat down slowly on the chart bench, like a man whose legs had received new information.

"You have to understand what the family name was before the Codex," he began. "Your grandfather was a competent surveyor and a mediocre writer. Your mother was brilliant but unpublished, unfunded, thirty-one years old, scraping by on contract bathymetry. Then in '09 she announces the recovery of a pre-Columbian screenfold manuscript, recovered — her words — *from the silt matrix of a colonial wreck site*, carbon-dated, partially waterlogged, extraordinary. The Sandoval Codex. It made her career overnight. Museums fought over the exhibition rights. The foundation exists because of it. You exist — professionally — because of it."

"I know its provenance," Zephyrine said. "I grew up on its provenance. It was in every interview she ever gave. Recovered from the wreck site during preliminary survey, 2009—"

"And now ask yourself," Tomás said gently, "what a pre-Columbian screenfold was doing aboard a Spanish galleon that sank in 1743."

The question landed and kept landing, opening outward like a crack in ice.

"They didn't carry things like that," Zephyrine said slowly. "By 1743 the Church was burning them. A screenfold on a treasure ship would be — ballast at best. Contraband at worst. It doesn't make sense as a wreck find."

"No," Tomás agreed. "It makes perfect sense as a different kind of find entirely."

* * *

He told it walking, because sitting still had become impossible for him; he paced the deck while the Mariposa lay moored in the willow shadow, and Zephyrine followed him with her mother's journal page in her hand — the torn page she'd found in the field kit, the one with the sketch of a bell tower rising out of water and a single line of her mother's handwriting: *I should have told them the truth the first day.*

"In '08, a year before the announcement, your mother ran a private survey upriver. No funding, no permits, no team beyond me and two local hires from San Remedios. Officially it was a bathymetric study for the dam consortium — Vance's people, though he wasn't Vance yet, just money with ambitions. Unofficially, she was chasing flood legends. Every drowned village has them. Stories about the old church that never fully went under, that divers sometimes saw on clear-water days. Bells that rang underwater. Your mother didn't believe the bells." He smiled briefly, without humor. "She believed in the archive."

"The village records," Zephyrine said. "Parish archives survive flooding if the building structure holds. If the nave stayed even partially sealed—"

"The nave sealed itself. When they flooded the valley in '61, the church was the tallest structure, and the silt chose it. Forty years of deposition built a shell around the walls. Inside — dry-ish pockets, collapsed roof, standing water to the clerestory. And in the crypt below the baptistery, in a lead-lined chest the parish priest had packed himself before the waters came, the village's whole movable history. Chalices. Registers. And three screenfold manuscripts the community had hidden from the Church two centuries earlier and hidden from the flood a second time." Tomás spread his hands. "Your mother found them. All of them. In '08."

Zephyrine felt the deck tilt under her, though the river was flat as poured metal.

"There were three," she said.

"There were three. Two she documented, photographed, and reported — properly, to the cultural ministry, with San Remedios listed as source community. Those are in the national archive under joint stewardship. Small footnote items. Nobody writes books about footnotes." His voice roughened. "The third was the finest. Figured narrative, painted bark paper, maybe four hundred years old, the story of the river people themselves — their migration, their floods, their names. Your mother held it in her hands in a flooded crypt with me standing beside her, and I watched her make a decision. I just didn't understand until later what decision I'd watched."

"She took it," Zephyrine said.

"She took it. And eighteen months later the world met the Sandoval Codex, recovered from the silt matrix of a colonial wreck site." He recited the phrase with bitter precision. "She fabricated the find spot. She fabricated the recovery date. She buried the true provenance so deep that even I signed off on the paperwork, because she told me the ministry would tie it in litigation for a decade and the dam consortium would seize it as collateral — and maybe that was even true, Zeph, maybe some of it was true, but the core of it was that she wanted it. She'd spent her whole life being brilliant and broke, and here was the thing that would fix both, and she told herself the lie that mattered: that she'd take care of it better than anyone else could. That possession was protection."

The journal page trembled slightly in Zephyrine's grip. She made it stop.

"My name," she said. "Every grant I ever lost, every door that opened because of who my mother was — it was built on this. On a drowned village's book in a museum case with a plaque that says *recovered from wreck site, 2009*." She heard her own voice go thin and hard. "Doña Eufemia spat at us because her grandmother's stories are sitting in a climate-controlled vitrine in the capital with our family name on the label."

"Yes."

"And my mother knew. All these years she knew, and she—" Zephyrine stopped. Turned. Looked upstream, toward the gorge, toward the convergence point where a woman with a broken wrist was breathing recycled air in the dark. "That's why she went back. That's why she wouldn't let Vance near the site. It was never about the gold."

"When the *Santa Elena* turned up in the gorge last year, your mother saw it for what it was," Tomás said. "A gift. A genuine colonial wreck, cargo intact, worth a fortune — and sitting practically on top of the cavern system where the Codex originally came from. She took the expedition contract knowing Vance would come sniffing. She planned to use the wreck as cover to move the Codex out of the museum loan circuit and return it — quietly, legally if possible, illegally if not — to San Remedios. Repatriation disguised as salvage logistics." He shook his head. "Then Vance accelerated the timeline, brought in the dredges, and started demanding full cargo access including anything 'anomalous.' He'd gotten wind there was more in that gorge than gold. So your mother cut the anchor line. Faked the disappearance. Drew the whole fleet upriver on a rescue-and-salvage frenzy, and bought herself time to finish what she should have finished fifteen years ago."

"*Return,*" Zephyrine said. "Not return to port. Return *it*. Return *to them*."

"She wasn't asking you to save her," Tomás said. "Or maybe she was. But the mission was always the same mission. Give it back."

* * *

Zephyrine walked to the bow and stood there a long time with the sun climbing and the water talking its endless indifferent talk against the hull.

She tried to locate the edges of the thing she was feeling and couldn't. It wasn't grief exactly, and it wasn't anger, or rather it was both of those dissolved into something larger and colder — the specific vertigo of watching a load-bearing wall of your life turn out to have been painted cardboard all along.

Her whole childhood had been organized around the Codex. The replica in her mother's study she'd been forbidden to touch. The lecture tours. The way strangers at dinners leaned in when the name came up. *The girl who grew up with the Codex.* She had rebelled against everything else — the expectations, the shadow, the famous name — but she had never once questioned the discovery itself. It was the family's one unquestioned miracle. The bedrock under all the arguing.

And the bedrock was a theft. Committed by the person she'd spent seven years resenting for reasons that suddenly looked small, against people who had every right to the resentment she'd been hoarding.

There was a horrible symmetry to it, and she saw it clearly and hated seeing it: her mother had stolen a past and called it a future. Zephyrine had spent seven years running from that future and calling it freedom. Neither of them had ever once, until now, stood in front of the actual thing — a village, a book, a debt — and simply paid.

"Doña Eufemia said one other thing," Zephyrine said, when Tomás came to stand beside her. "Before we left. She said, *Tell the daughter the debt isn't hers, but neither is the forgiveness.* I thought she was being cruel."

"She was being precise," Tomás said. "River people usually are."

Zephyrine folded the journal page along its old creases and put it in her breast pocket, over her heart, where her mother's compass used to ride.

"Then we finish it," she said. "All of it. The Codex goes back to San Remedios with the full truth attached — the real provenance, the real dates, her name on the fabrication and mine on the correction. Publicly. In writing. And then we get my mother out of that cave before tide six, because whatever she owes these people, she doesn't owe them her life, and I am done letting this family pay its debts in other people's currency."

Tomás studied her for a moment, and something in his weathered face eased — not relief exactly, more like recognition, the look of a man seeing a third Sandoval emerge from the wreckage of the first two.

"Your mother said you'd say something like that," he said. "Word for word, nearly."

"When?"

"The night she planned the disappearance. I asked her why she didn't just tell you everything years ago, spare everyone the mess. She said—" he closed his eyes, retrieving it exactly, "—she said, *Because Zephyrine would do the right thing immediately, and I needed time to learn how.*"

Zephyrine turned away so he wouldn't see her face, and that was when Cass's voice came down from the wheelhouse, flat and urgent:

"We've got company. Trawler, coming down-current, flying no colors — and she's running a tow-sonar array I last saw bolted to the stern of the Aurelia."

Behind the trawler, faint in the heat shimmer, the gorge walls waited, and somewhere inside them, three tides from now, the water would start to rise.
