## 13. Tide Three

Cass Ibarra had spent thirty years reading the river, and in all that time he'd learned one thing worth teaching: the water never lied, but it never told you everything either.

He stood on the Mariposa's deck at first light, watching the tide drain out of San Remedios like the village itself was exhaling. The flooded church tower broke the surface a hundred meters off his port bow, its bell arch crusted white with mineral stain, and below it—somewhere beneath forty feet of green water and three hundred years of silt—the elders said there were passages that went back into the hillside like roots. The codex had come out of that drowned nave once already. Now it sat deeper still, inside a cavern system no sonar could read, guarded by a woman who'd faked her own death to keep it from Aurelio Vance.

Three tides left. He counted them the way he counted everything now: in hours of water.

Behind him, Zephyrine Sandoval emerged from the cabin with her mother's field journal open against her forearm, hair still wet from washing at the village pump. She hadn't slept much. None of them had. But she moved like someone who'd made a decision and was done grieving over it, and Cass found he respected that more than he could say without saying it.

"The elders are meeting at the schoolhouse," she said. "They want us there."

"They want *you* there," Cass corrected. "I'm still the man who ran guns through this delta in '09."

"You were twenty-two."

"I was paid." He spat over the rail, out of habit more than malice. "River doesn't forget what a boat carries. Neither do villages."

Zephyrine closed the journal. "Then maybe it's time somebody told them what this boat carries now."

* * *

The schoolhouse of San Remedios stood on the last dry finger of land, its walls stained to the height of the old flood line—a brown tide mark running around the room at chest height, so that anyone who sat in those desks for the past forty years had done their learning inside the memory of the water. Cass had been in rooms like it all up and down the delta. Rooms where the dam engineers' promises hung in the air like the smell of creosote.

Doña Esperanza presided from the front, the elder with the carved cane and the face of a woman who had personally outlived every argument ever raised against her. Around her sat maybe fifteen others, old men in straw hats, women with hands like tree bark. On the wall behind them, half-faded under newer paint, someone had lettered a motto in the old style: *EL RÍO DA Y EL RÍO QUITA.* The river gives and the river takes.

Cass had always thought it should read the other way around.

Zephyrine stood when they were brought in. She didn't sit until Esperanza nodded her into the chair, and Cass noted that—manners, or instinct, or both. The girl had her mother's spine but not yet her mother's armor.

"You carry the Sandoval name," Esperanza said. Not a question. Her Spanish was slow and deliberate, each word set down like a stone in a ford.

"I do."

"Your mother took our codex."

The room went very quiet. Cass shifted his weight, ready to be the wall between the girl and whatever came next, but Zephyrine didn't flinch.

"She did," Zephyrine said. "And she built a career on it. And she hid it from the people it belonged to, because she told herself she was protecting it—from looters, from collectors, from men like Vance. And then a man exactly like Vance came anyway, and she discovered that hiding a thing is just another way of losing it."

Esperanza's eyes narrowed. "You speak plainly for a daughter defending her mother."

"I'm not defending her." Zephyrine's voice didn't rise. "I'm accounting for her. There's a difference. What she took can't be un-taken. What Vance wants to take can still be stopped. I'm here for the second part. But I won't pretend the first part didn't happen, because pretending is how we got here."

An old man near the window—Cass recognized him as Tomás Chirino, who kept the village's one remaining boat and knew the submerged channels better than any chart—made a low sound that might have been approval. Or indigestion. With Chirino it was always hard to tell.

Esperanza studied Zephyrine for a long moment. Then she turned to Cass, and the weight of her attention landed on him like ballast.

"And you. Ibarra. They say your license is gone."

"Vance's lawyers took it. Said my charts were falsified." He shrugged. "My charts weren't falsified. My charts were *accurate*, which is worse."

"Can you get them into the caverns before the fleet's dredges enter the gorge?"

"With three tides?" Cass looked at the flood line on the wall, then back at her. "With three tides I can get them to the mouth. Inside—" he spread his hands, "—that depends on guides who know passages that were dry land when your grandparents walked them."

Silence again. Then Esperanza did something Cass had not seen an elder of San Remedios do in thirty years of dealing with outsiders.

She smiled.

It was a small thing, dry as driftwood, but it changed her whole face.

"My grandmother walked those hills," she said. "Before the water. Chirino's father swam them after. Between the two of us, we remember every passage the river tried to erase." She tapped her cane once against the floorboards, a sound like a door unlocking. "You will have your guides, pilot. Both of you will. But hear me first."

She leaned forward, and the whole room leaned with her.

"Do not go down there for revenge. Revenge sinks boats. Men who dive angry hold their breath wrong, they move fast when they should move slow, they die in places their mothers cannot bury them." Her voice dropped. "Go down there to *repair*. Repair is slower. Repair is heavier. But repair floats."

* * *

They spent the middle of the day preparing, and Cass learned something about Zephyrine Sandoval in the way she prepared: she inventoried.

Lines, coiled by length. Tanks, checked twice and logged. Her mother's survey instruments laid out on the Mariposa's deck plates in exact rows, each one tested, each one returned to its case in the same orientation it had come out. It was archaeologist discipline, sure—but it was also, Cass realized, the discipline of a woman who had grown up measuring distances because distances couldn't lie to her the way people did.

He said as much, or a version of it, while they rigged the guide lines.

"You count things," he said. "When you're nervous. You count everything."

"Tide intervals. Air consumption. Meters of line." She didn't look up from the reel she was spooling. "Numbers don't leave."

"No," Cass agreed. "But they also don't stay."

That got her to look up. "What's that supposed to mean?"

"A number's only true at the moment you take it. River moves. Tide turns. You take a sounding at slack water, it's a lie by the next hour." He knotted off the line and sat back on his heels. "Your mother understood that. That's why she stayed down there instead of running. Some things you can't measure from far away. You have to be in the current."

Zephyrine was quiet a moment. "Tomás says she's injured. Says she's been living in an air pocket for weeks guarding the codex."

"Tomás also worked for Vance for two months. So we verify everything and trust selectively."

"That's the most honest thing anyone's said to me since Puerto Cobre."

"Don't get used to it. Honesty's bad for business." He tossed her the spare mask. "Besides, you've got three tides left. After that, honesty's free for everybody, because we'll all be dead or rich or famous, and none of those improve a man's character."

Something that was almost a laugh escaped her. Cass filed it away. First one he'd heard from her since the grave.

* * *

Chirino came aboard at slack before the third-to-last ebb, bringing his granddaughter Lucía—a girl of maybe nineteen with a swimmer's shoulders and the flat, unimpressed stare of someone raised on water that had drowned her own history. She carried a coil of braided palm-fiber line older than Cass and a wooden float carved in the shape of a fish.

"She marks the passages," Chirino explained. "The way her family always has. Before the dam, they were the ones who guided the loggers through the narrows. After, they guided the smugglers. Now—" he shrugged, "—she guides whoever remembers to ask."

Lucía looked at Cass. "Do you ask?"

"I'm asking."

"Good." She stowed the float without another word and began checking his hull seams with the brisk familiarity of someone who'd already decided the Mariposa was salvageable and intended to inform him of any deficiencies.

Zephyrine watched her work with an expression Cass couldn't quite name. Something between envy and recognition.

"She's younger than me," Zephyrine murmured, "and she knows this water better than I'll ever know anything."

"She knows it because it took something from her and she refused to let go of the shape of it." Cass started the engine, felt the old diesel catch on the third try, the way it always did, the way he always did. "That's not knowledge. That's stubbornness with a memory."

"Is that what I have?"

Cass considered lying, decided against it, and considered being gentle, decided that would be worse.

"You have the anger version of it," he said. "Hers settled into something useful. Yours is still deciding what it wants to be when it grows up."

Zephyrine held his gaze for a long three seconds. Then she nodded, once, and turned to the bow line, and Cass understood that he'd passed some kind of test he hadn't known he was taking.

* * *

They gathered at dusk on the schoolhouse steps for the final briefing, the whole strange expedition of them: Cass, Zephyrine, Lucía with her fish-float, Chirino, and two of the elders' sons who would handle the surface lines. Esperanza stood at the top of the steps with her cane planted like a flagstaff.

Below them, the river lay flat and pewter-colored, holding the last light. Somewhere upriver, past the gorge, past the narrows, the lights of the Aurelia's fleet burned like a floating town. Sonar pings, Cass knew, sweeping the deep channel every ninety seconds, mapping nothing, finding nothing, because the true site wasn't in the channel at all—it was behind limestone, inside the hill's drowned throat, where sound went to die.

"Three tides," Esperanza said. "Tonight's ebb takes you to the mouth. Tomorrow's flood covers your entry. The sixth tide—" her voice caught, just slightly, the only crack in the stone, "—the sixth tide brings the surge. If you are not out before the surge, the caverns close for good, and they will close with whatever is inside them."

"Including Vance's divers," Zephyrine said quietly.

"Including whoever chooses gold over breath." Esperanza's eyes found Zephyrine's and held them. "Which brings me to my last word, Sandoval girl. When you find your mother—and you will find her, because mothers who guard things do not die easily—you will want to hate her for what she took. And when you bring the codex home, you will want the world to punish her for it."

She descended one step, slowly, until she stood level with Zephyrine.

"Punishment is the river's business. It does that work whether we help or not. Our business is putting back what was carried off. Do you understand the difference?"

Zephyrine was silent long enough that Cass thought she wouldn't answer. Then:

"I understand that I'm going to spend the rest of my life doing repair work," she said. "Starting with this."

Esperanza studied her, then turned to Cass, and gave him the smallest nod—the nod of a woman transferring cargo of considerable value and expecting it delivered intact.

"Pilot," she said. "Take them in. Bring them back. In that order."

* * *

They cast off under the first stars, the Mariposa riding low with tanks and line and three generations of San Remedios' memory aboard. Lucía stood at the bow with her fish-float, reading the dark water the way other people read print. Chirino handled the stern line. Zephyrine crouched beside Cass at the wheel, her mother's journal open on her knees, the coordinates glowing faintly under his instrument light.

Ahead, the gorge opened its black mouth, and somewhere inside it, a woman who had chosen the river over her reputation waited with the truth in her hands.

Cass throttled up and steered for the dark.

"Third tide," he said. "Two left after this. Nobody wastes a minute."

Nobody answered. But nobody argued either, and on the Mariposa, that was as good as a prayer.
