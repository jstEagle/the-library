## 14. Diving the Old Church

The bell tower of San Remedios broke the water like a drowning man's last raised hand.

Zephyrine stood at the bow of the Mariposa and looked up at it — a stone finger pointing at nothing, crusted white with mineral stain where the river had been chewing on it for forty years. Below the surface line, she knew from the elder Mateo's hand-drawn chart, the tower went down another ninety feet to its base, and past it, the roofline of the church of Santa Remedios de los Ríos, swallowed whole when the dam gates closed in the spring of 1979.

"You don't have to do this today," Cass said behind her. He was coiling rope with the exaggerated calm of a man who'd already argued and lost. "Tide four comes tomorrow. We could rest."

"The Aurelia's divers won't rest."

"No," he admitted. "They won't."

Tomás crouched at the stern, checking her tanks for the third time, his hands moving with the automatic tenderness of a man who'd spent thirty years maintaining dive gear for someone else's expeditions. He hadn't said much since the night in the reeds. Confession had emptied him out, and what remained was quieter than the man she'd watched signal covertly from the deck of Vance's flagship. But his hands were sure, and that was enough.

"Air's good," he said. "Regulator's good. The line's marked every ten meters." He paused, then added, not looking at her: "Your mother dove this same entry point. April of '81. I held the line."

Zephyrine looked at him. "You were there."

"I was there for all of it." His jaw worked. "That's rather the problem, niña."

* * *

The briefing had happened the night before, in the elder Mateo's kitchen, around a table scarred by decades of elbows and oil lamps. Mateo was eighty-one and had been fourteen when the water came. He drew the village from memory on the back of a flour sack: the plaza, the schoolhouse, the arcade of shops along the Calle del Mercado, and there, at the end of everything, the church with its twin towers and its nave long as a ship.

"The dam was sold to us as progress," he said, tapping the paper. "Vance's grandfather bought the concessions, the government bought the promises, and we bought a bus ticket and a number for a resettlement lot that flooded too, two years later, but nobody wrote that down anywhere official." He looked up at Zephyrine, and his eyes were the flat brown of river stones. "When your mother came back in '81, we thought maybe someone had finally come to write it down. She asked about the church records. The parish books. What happened to them when the water rose."

"What did you tell her?"

"That Father Anselmo took what he could carry and drowned doing it. That the rest went down with the building." Mateo folded the flour sack with great care. "She came back three times that year. Always alone, always at night, always asking questions she didn't explain. And then she stopped coming, and a year later the whole world knew the name Sandoval because of a codex pulled out of a colonial wreck two hundred miles downstream." His voice didn't rise. It didn't need to. "You understand what we concluded."

Zephyrine understood. She'd understood since Tomás told them the truth in the reeds: that the codex — the illuminated missionary record that had made her mother famous, funded the foundation, built the legend Imelda Sandoval had spent a lifetime outrunning or embodying, depending on the day — hadn't come off any galleon. It had come out of this church. Out of this drowned parish. Out of a community that lost everything and got a bus ticket and a flooded resettlement lot in exchange.

Her mother had found it here, in the dark, among the bones of a village the river had eaten. And then she'd written a discovery report that said otherwise.

"She couldn't publish the truth," Tomás had said. "If she said where it really came from, Vance's company would have moved on San Remedios within the year — salvage rights, dredging permits, whatever legal fiction they needed. The codex would've ended up in a private vault in Geneva and the village would've gotten nothing but a deeper hole in the riverbed. So she buried the truth inside a bigger lie and spent twenty years hating herself for it." He'd rubbed his face with both hands. "I helped her write the false provenance. I typed half of it myself. That's my confession, and you can have it for free."

Now, standing over the water where the village used to be, Zephyrine tried to hold both things at once: the woman who had stolen a miracle from a drowned town, and the woman who had spent seven years and finally her own life trying to give it back. She wasn't sure they fit in the same person. She wasn't sure people did.

"Line's set," Cass called. "Entry's off the north tower — there's a window at fifteen feet, big enough. Current's slack for another ninety minutes. After that, don't be down there."

Zephyrine shrugged into the harness. The weight of the tanks settled across her shoulders like a familiar argument.

"I'll be back before the current remembers us," she said.

* * *

The water closed over her head and the world went green-gold and silent.

Descent along the shot line, meter marks ticking past — ten, twenty, thirty — and the light thickened from amber to tea to something like dusk underwater. Her gauge read fifty-one degrees. Cold enough to bite through the wetsuit at her wrists and throat. She breathed slow, the way her mother had taught her when she was twelve, in a quarry in the north: *the tank is not air, mija, it is time. Spend it like money.*

At sixty feet the tower wall slid past her shoulder and she saw the window.

It was an arched opening, tall as a door, its wooden frame long dissolved, its stone sill furred with silt and a fine black mold that grew only where the river ran slow. Through it, the interior of the nave opened up like the inside of a lung.

Zephyrine hung in the water and looked at a church.

The roof had partially collapsed — beams lay across the crossing like fallen masts — but the walls stood, and the light from above fell in slanted columns through gaps in the timbers, and in those columns the silt drifted like snow falling upward. There were pews still. Rows of them, oak gone soft and gray, tipped and tumbled as if the congregation had left in a hurry, which, she supposed, they had. An altar rail. A pulpit leaning against a pillar. And everywhere, everywhere, the small honest debris of lives interrupted: a boot. A child's chair. A lantern with its glass still intact, full of river instead of flame.

Her depth gauge read seventy-eight feet. Bottom time burning. She swam the central aisle.

This was the part no survey had prepared her for. Zephyrine had worked wrecks — hulls, cargo holds, the geometry of ships, which were designed to be entered, mapped, stripped. A church was different. A church was built to be believed in. Every arch pointed at heaven, and now every arch pointed at sixty feet of brown water, and the effect was not eerie so much as accusatory. The building itself seemed to be asking her what she intended to do here.

She reached the altar.

Someone had cleared it. That was the first thing she noticed — the altar stone was swept clean of silt, and on it, arranged with deliberate care, sat objects that had no business surviving forty years underwater in such condition: a ceramic figurine of the Virgin, furred white with concretion but whole; a rosary of river pearls draped over a standing crucifix; and beneath these, flat and square and wrapped in waxed canvas tied with cord, a bundle the size of a hymnal.

Not the codex. Tomás had been clear — Imelda had taken the codex out in '81, hidden it inside the galleon find, let the world believe what it wanted. This was something else. Something left.

Zephyrine hovered over the altar, and her breath sounded enormous in her own ears, and she thought: *she came back.* Not once. Three times that first year, Mateo had said. And after the lie was published and the fame arrived and the foundation opened its doors — she must have kept coming back. Quietly. For years. Leaving offerings on the altar of a church she'd helped rob, the way you visit a grave you're ashamed of.

The resentment Zephyrine had carried for seven years — the cold, well-organized resentment of a daughter who'd measured herself against a legend and found the legend hollow — shifted in her chest like ballast.

Because she could see it now, from down here, from the other side of the discovery. She could see her mother at this altar at night, young, thirty-four years old, holding a manuscript that could make or unmake her, with Vance's lawyers circling and the truth unspeakable and the only exit a lie. And she could see the older Imelda, returning again and again, unable to put it down, unable to pick it up. The codex hadn't been treasure. It had been a debt. And debts like that don't get paid once; they compound.

*You thought she abandoned the truth,* Zephyrine thought, turning slowly in the green water. *She was guarding it. Badly, clumsily, for twenty years — but guarding it.*

Her light caught movement at the edge of the aisle.

For one electric second she thought it was a diver — Vance's people, already here — and her hand went to the knife on her calf. Then the shape resolved: a catfish, huge and unhurried, longer than her arm, cruising between the pews like a verger making rounds. It regarded her with prehistoric indifference and poured itself through a broken window into the open water beyond.

Zephyrine laughed into her regulator, and the sound came out as a shudder, and she realized her eyes were stinging and that at seventy-eight feet, crying was a genuinely stupid thing to do.

She turned back to the altar. Carefully, keeping her fins high, she lifted the waxed bundle. It was heavier than it looked, dense, and through the canvas she could feel the corners of something rigid — wood? metal? She untied the cord with numb fingers.

Inside was a ledger. Parish-sized, leather-bound, its cover blistered but intact, protected by whatever providence governs objects meant to survive. She opened it. The pages were stuck in places, illegible in others, but the early entries were clear enough — columns of baptisms, marriages, deaths, in a curate's careful hand, dates running backward into the 1700s. The register of San Remedios. The book Father Anselmo hadn't saved, because he couldn't carry everything, and someone — her mother — had recovered it and brought it home and left it where it belonged.

On the flyleaf, in pencil, faded but legible, a single line in handwriting Zephyrine had known since childhood:

*For the record that was never allowed to speak. — I.S.*

Below it, a second line, newer, the graphite barely smudged:

*Tell Zephyrine the tide will turn. Tell her I'm sorry it took so long.*

Zephyrine stayed very still. Somewhere above her, faint as a heartbeat through forty feet of water, she felt the first tug of the turning tide against the shot line.

Her mother had written that message knowing who would find it. Knowing her daughter would follow the coordinates, thread the gorge, dive the church — because Zephyrine had inherited exactly one thing from Imelda Sandoval without dispute, and it was the inability to leave a question unanswered. The message wasn't a note. It was a key cut precisely to her shape.

*I'm sorry it took so long.*

Seven years of silence, and the apology was waiting for her at the bottom of a drowned church, penciled on a flyleaf, patient as stone.

Zephyrine wrapped the ledger back in its canvas, cinched it to her harness, and checked her gauge. Twelve minutes of bottom time left. Enough.

She made one more pass down the aisle, slower now, not surveying but witnessing — the pews, the lantern, the small interrupted lives — and at the baptismal font near the entrance she stopped. The basin was full of silt, and rising out of it, planted upright like a flag, was a child's toy boat. Wooden, hand-carved, its paint long gone, its little mast snapped. Someone had placed it there recently — the silt around its hull was disturbed, the placement deliberate.

A marker. Or a message. Or just a village's way of saying: *we remember this place was ours.*

She memorized it. She filed it away with everything else — the flour-sack map, Mateo's flat stone-colored eyes, the ledger against her ribs — and kicked for the window and the light.

* * *

She broke the surface gasping, and Cass's hand was on her harness before she'd fully cleared the water, hauling her over the gunwale with a roughness that was its own kind of relief.

"Tide's turning," he said. "You were down to your last ten minutes, you know that?"

"I know."

"You always know." He shoved a thermos at her. "What'd you find?"

She unwrapped the ledger and set it on the thwart between them, and watched his face change as he read the flyleaf — first the old line, then the new. Tomás leaned in over her shoulder, and when he reached the second line his breath went out of him like a punctured tire.

"She knew you'd come," he said quietly.

"She counted on it." Zephyrine stared at the pencil words until they blurred. "All those years I thought she was pushing me away. She was leaving a trail." She laughed, and it came out wrong, wet. "God. She couldn't even apologize like a normal person. It had to be a treasure hunt."

"That's Imelda," Tomás said, and there was a whole history in the way he said it.

Cass was already studying the sky, the water, the angle of the current sliding past the hull. "Tide four starts in six hours. The Aurelia's divers hit the cavern mouth at slack water — Tomás says Vance has them on a strict schedule. If we're going to beat them to the true site—"

"We go tonight," Zephyrine said. "We go before they finish their surface interval."

But even as she said it, her eyes went upstream, toward the gorge, toward the black water where the galleon lay and the cavern system wound beneath it like a secret under a secret. And that was when she saw it — far off, at the limit of sight where the river bent — a searchlight sweeping the water. Then a second. The Aurelia, moving early. Moving now.

And moving, unless Tomás's information was already stale, straight toward the cavern mouth.

"They're not waiting for slack," she said.

Cass was already at the engine.
