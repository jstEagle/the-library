## 15. Interception

The trouble with a plan built on silence is that silence cuts both ways.

We'd been running dark since the gorge—no radio traffic, no lights after dusk, the Mariposa's engine throttled to a murmur that Cass swore sounded like rain on the water rather than machinery. Tomás had gone back over the side two nights ago with a promise to signal us from the Aurelia if Vance's divers moved on the cavern. The village guides had led us to a slough half a mile downstream of the true site, screened by dead cypress and a curtain of hanging moss so thick it swallowed sound. Everything was positioned. Everything was timed.

And then, on the morning of tide four, the river gave us away anyway.

I heard it first as a wrongness in the water—a pressure change against the hull, the kind of shudder I'd learned to read in seven years of survey work. Not current. Displacement. Something big moving upstream at speed, and not bothering to hide it.

"Cass." I put my hand on his shoulder where he dozed against the wheel housing. "Wake up."

He came awake the way he did everything—fast, economical, no wasted motion. One look at my face and he was at the rail, listening the way he listened, whole body tilted toward the water like an instrument himself.

"Two engines," he said. "Shallow draft. Patrol boats." He was already untying the mooring line from the cypress root. "They've been sweeping this stretch since dawn. Somebody talked."

"Nobody talked."

"Somebody always talks." He didn't say it bitterly. He said it the way he said everything about rivers: as weather. "Vance has been paying informants along this water for twenty years. A fisherman sees our wake, makes a call, buys his family a month of rice. You'd do worse."

The Mariposa was too slow. We both knew it. In open water she might make six knots with the current's help; the patrol boats would do fifteen without breathing hard. Our only chance was the maze—the braided channels behind the cypress islands where the charts went blank and the water went skinny, where a boat drawing three feet could follow one drawing eighteen only so far before the river itself became a weapon.

Cass took us into it at full throttle, which felt like madness until I understood it wasn't. Speed here wasn't about escape. It was about buying the next thirty seconds, over and over, thirty seconds at a time.

I stood at the bow reading the water while he drove. This was the thing we'd found in each other somewhere between Puerto Cobre and the narrows—I could see what the river was doing underneath, the shelves and seams and buried snags, and he could put a hull exactly where I pointed, faster than I could point. We'd stopped being surprised by it around tide two. Now it was just how we worked, wordless, my hand rising or falling and his wheel answering.

"Left," I called. "Hard left—sandbar shifting, there's a channel behind it."

He took it. The Mariposa heeled, her cracked seam groaning—that repair job in the narrows had been solder and prayer—and then we were through into a corridor of drowned timber so narrow the moss brushed both rails.

Behind us, engines. Closer than they should have been.

"They know these channels," I said.

"Their pilots do. Mine are better." But his jaw was tight, and I knew what he knew: better wasn't infinite. We were deep in territory the fleet had dredged around for years, and every minute we ran, their boats were learning the maze alongside us.

* * *

They caught us not because we were slow but because we stopped.

It was my decision, which is the part that still sits wrong in my chest. Ahead, the channel forked, and the right fork looked open—but open meant exposed, a quarter mile of clear water with the patrol boats' wake visible beyond the far bend. The left fork narrowed into a slough I couldn't read. Too much silt. Too many variables. My surveyor's eye wanted data I didn't have time to gather, and in that hesitation—three seconds, maybe four—Cass made the call I should have made.

He cut the engine.

We drifted into the shadow of a collapsed boathouse, its roof half-submerged, its pilings furred with weed, and let the current carry us sideways into the reeds. He killed even the bilge pump. We sat in a silence so complete I could hear my own pulse, and through the reed stems I watched the first patrol boat come around the bend.

It was painted the gray-green of official indifference, VANCE SALVAGE CONSORTIUM stenciled on the bow in letters you could read from a hundred meters, because Aurelio Vance had never once in his life pretended to be subtle. Men lined its rail with binoculars. It passed within forty yards of us. Close enough that I could see the coffee cup in one man's hand, the boredom in his shoulders.

It kept going.

Cass let out a breath that had been held so long it came out shaking. "Tide four," he murmured. "We're still ahead of them. If we hold here till dark—"

The second boat hadn't gone past.

I realized it a half-second before the loudspeaker crackled, and by then it was arithmetic, not hope. The second boat had been waiting at the mouth of the slough the whole time, sitting quiet with its engine idling, letting its partner flush us toward it. They hadn't been chasing us at all. They'd been herding.

"*Mariposa*," the loudspeaker said, in a voice fat with satisfaction. "Cut your engine and step out where we can see you. Mr. Vance would like a word."

Cass looked at me across the wheel housing, and what passed between us wasn't words. It was the whole geometry of the moment laid flat: run and they run us down in open water; fight and there were six of them and two of us and the village elders were a full day's pole away; surrender and—

Surrender and what? That was the question neither of us could answer, and the loudspeaker asked it again, more pleasantly, the way power always asks twice.

"We give them the boat, they find the survey notes," Cass said quietly. "Your mother's bearing. Everything."

"I'll sink the papers first."

"And then they take us anyway, and we're two prisoners instead of two free ones with wet paper." He rubbed his jaw. "Zeph. Listen to me. Whatever happens on that boat—whatever they offer you—you say yes."

"What?"

"You say yes to everything. You hear me?" His eyes were very steady. "A man like Vance doesn't stop at offers. He stops at obstacles. Don't be an obstacle. Be a door he thinks he's walking through."

"Cass—"

"Ms. Sandoval." The loudspeaker again, and now the voice had changed—it knew something, and it wanted me to know it knew. "Mr. Vance is aware your mother is alive. He'd very much like to discuss the terms of keeping her that way."

* * *

They took Cass in irons and me in courtesy, which told me everything about which of us Vance feared. The patrol boat's deck was scrubbed white and smelled of diesel and floor polish, money trying to pass as order. They sat us facing forward, side by side, hands zip-tied—not behind our backs, I noticed. In front. Someone had decided we should be comfortable. Someone had decided comfort was leverage.

The run upstream took under an hour. The Aurelia grew out of the river haze the way bad news grows out of a phone call: gradually, then all at once. She was a converted coastal freighter, three hundred feet of rust-streaked steel bristling with crane arms and coiled hose, her decks crowded with dive gear and dredge equipment and men who moved with the unhurried confidence of people whose paychecks don't depend on questions. Her name was picked out in gold leaf on the stern. *Aurelia*. He'd named his flagship after himself. Somewhere below us, I thought, was a woman named Aurelia Vance who had either died young or left him, and either way he'd turned her into a machine for pulling gold out of drowned places.

They separated us at the gangway. Cass went down, escorted, not resisting—he'd told me not to be an obstacle, and he was taking his own advice with a discipline that scared me more than struggle would have. I watched him go and made myself believe the door metaphor. Doors can be walked back through.

They took me up.

* * *

Vance received me in the ship's former cargo office, a room that had been refitted with mahogany paneling and air conditioning and a desk carved from a single slab of some dark wood, all of it slightly absurd aboard a working vessel, all of it deliberate. He wanted me to understand that he could build rooms anywhere. That he could build anything anywhere. That the river, the law, the wreck, the truth—all of it was furniture to him.

He was smaller than I expected. That's the thing nobody tells you about powerful men: the power does the sizing, and when you finally stand in front of the actual body, there's a strange deflation, a sense of having been lied to by scale. Aurelio Vance was maybe five-six, silver-haired, neat as a banker, with reading glasses pushed up on his forehead and eyes that did all the work his voice refused to do. The voice was warm. The eyes were cold as ballast.

"Zephyrine Sandoval." He pronounced it correctly, which meant he'd practiced. "Please sit. Can I offer you coffee? Real coffee, not the swill my crew drinks. I have standards. It's why I'm successful."

"I'm fine standing."

"Of course you are." He sat himself, folding his hands, and studied me with the frank appraisal of a man pricing an object. "You look like her. Did you know that? Imelda, at your age. Same jaw. Same way of standing like the ground owes you rent." He smiled. "I've admired your mother for twenty years. She's the only person in this business I ever considered a peer. Which is why this situation disappoints me."

"What situation is that?"

"The situation where she's sitting in a flooded hole guarding a box of paper like a dragon on a hoard, and I'm forced to consider unpleasant methods of eviction." He said it lightly, conversationally, the way you'd discuss a plumbing problem. "She's injured, you know. Leg. Badly enough that she shouldn't be diving, and she knows it, and she's still down there, because your mother would rather drown than lose an argument. I respect that. I also intend to win it."

My hands wanted to shake. I kept them still by gripping the back of the chair I refused to sit in. "If you've hurt her—"

"I haven't. Yet." He removed his glasses, polished them on his lapel, replaced them. "Let me tell you what I actually want, Ms. Sandoval, because I suspect you've been fed a story about me—by Reyes, probably, or those sanctimonious relics in San Remedios—and the story is wrong. I'm not a villain. Villains want destruction. I want *completion*. There is a galleon in a cavern under this river carrying the richest colonial cargo ever found in this hemisphere, and the paperwork that makes it legally mine is sitting in a filing cabinet in the capital, waiting on one thing: provenance. A credible chain of custody. An authority the courts will trust." He spread his hands. "And then you paddled up my river."

"No."

"You haven't heard the offer."

"I don't need to. You want my name on your claim. You want Sandoval provenance on stolen goods, and in exchange you're selling me my mother's life, and you think that's a negotiation because you've never met anyone who wouldn't pay it." I heard my own voice go flat and hard, my mother's voice, and hated it and used it anyway. "Here's what you missed, Mr. Vance. I spent seven years running from that name precisely so that no one could ever buy me with it. You're holding collateral that has no market."

Something flickered behind the cold eyes. Recalculation. Men like him aren't used to assets refusing valuation.

"Then let me adjust the terms." He opened a drawer and set a photograph on the desk, face up, sliding it across the wood with one finger. "Because I don't think you fully grasp your position."

I looked down at the photograph and the floor of my chest dropped out.

It was Cass. Taken an hour ago, on this deck—his shirt torn at the shoulder, blood at his hairline, kneeling between two of Vance's security men with his hands still zip-tied. But it wasn't the blood that stopped my breath. It was the background. Behind him, being carried up the Aurelia's gangway in sealed waterproof cases marked with the consortium's stencil, were instruments I recognized. Survey transits. Sediment cores. My mother's field kit—the real one, the one from San Remedios' storeroom, the one I'd packed my own notes inside.

They had the journal page. They had the bearings. They had everything they needed to work the cavern themselves, and they'd taken it off a half-sunk boathouse while we were busy hiding from the wrong boat.

"You see the problem," Vance said gently. "I no longer strictly need you. Or your pilot. Or, frankly, your mother—though I confess I'd prefer her alive, for reasons of sentiment and also because a dead Imelda Sandoval becomes a martyr, and martyrs attract journalists." He leaned back. "So here are the real terms. Sign the provenance affidavit. Appear at the hearing in the capital—your presence, your signature, your family name legitimizing a salvage operation that will employ three hundred people and rescue a drowning regional economy, which is how I'll frame it, and the framing will work because framing always works. Do that, and I pull my divers out today. Your mother walks—or swims—out of that cavern untouched. Your pilot goes home with his debts paid and his record cleaned. Everyone lives. The codex stays in a vault where paper belongs, and the villagers get a settlement package that will look generous in the newspapers."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then tomorrow morning my divers go in with cutting torches, and I stop caring who's in the air pocket." He shrugged, a small, tidy shrug. "I'm offering you the version of this story where everyone gets to keep their skin, Ms. Sandoval. There is another version. I've lived long enough to know that people almost always choose the first one, and I've stopped pretending to be disappointed about it."

The cabin was very quiet. Through the porthole I could see the river, brown and patient, going about its business around the hull of his ship as though the ship were a rock it had decided to flow past. Tide four was turning. I could feel it in the way the water moved—slowing, hesitating, gathering itself for the turn. Six tides, the elder had said. Three left after this one. And my mother was down there in the dark with a broken leg, counting the same water, betting everything on a daughter she hadn't seen in seven years.

Sign the paper. Save her today. Then fight him from inside, with lawyers, with time, with the affidavit torn up the moment she was safe. It was right there. It was *easy*. That was the horror of it—not that the bargain was corrupt, but that it was reasonable. Any sensible person would take it. Any sensible person would sign, and tell herself the betrayal was temporary, and discover, later, that temporary is the most permanent word in the language. My mother had taught me that without meaning to. Seven years of exile had taught me the rest.

I picked up the photograph of Cass and set it face down.

"Here's my counteroffer," I said. "You put me on a boat to that cavern right now, alone, and I'll bring my mother out myself. She trusts me. She won't torch your divers if I'm the one asking her to surface. You get your wreck without a firefight, I get my mother without your torches, and we settle the paperwork question afterward, in daylight, with witnesses."

Vance studied me for a long moment, and I watched him decide—not whether I was lying, but whether it mattered. Whether a Sandoval in hand was worth whatever I was concealing in the other.

"You'll sign," he said. It wasn't a question. It was a forecast.

"When she's standing on your deck," I said. "Not before."

He smiled then, warm as the voice, and pressed a button on his desk, and somewhere below us a bosun's whistle shrilled.

"Take Ms. Sandoval down to the dive deck," he told the man who appeared. "Full kit. And send word to the cavern team—we have a family reunion to arrange."

As they marched me out, past the rail where Cass had knelt, past the cases holding my mother's life in labeled boxes, I kept my eyes on the water and did the only arithmetic that mattered: they were putting me in a wetsuit, handing me a regulator, and pointing me at the one place on earth my mother was.

They thought they were sending me in as their negotiator.

Every door, Cass had said. Be a door he thinks he's walking through.

I checked my gauge straps, and I started counting breaths.
