## 16. The Bargain Refused

The Aurelia's launch came for me at dawn, a black-hulled tender cutting the river's brown silk into two long scars. I stood on the Mariposa's deck with my hands cuffed behind my back—Cass's idea, and a good one—and let them think they'd taken me.

They had taken Cass too. That was the part that made my teeth ache.

"Miss Sandoval." The man in the tender wore Vance's colors, white shirt pressed despite the humidity, a radio clipped to his shoulder like a badge of office. "Mr. Vance extends his compliments."

"Tell him to keep them."

He smiled the way men smile when they're being paid to. "You'll want to deliver that in person."

* * *

They brought us up the boarding ladder separately, Cass first, then me, so we wouldn't have time to speak. The Aurelia was everything I expected from a ship named after its owner's vanity: teak decks scrubbed pale, brass fittings polished to a shine that mocked the working boats downstream, a superstructure like a wedding cake stacked three tiers high. Air conditioning bled cold through an open hatch. Somewhere below, generators throbbed.

Vance received me on the aft deck, under an awning, at a table set with coffee that smelled real. He was smaller than I'd imagined—a compact man, silver-templed, with the stillness of someone who had never once needed to hurry because other people did his hurrying for him.

"Zephyrine Sandoval." He didn't stand. "You look like your mother around the eyes. It's a useful face. People trust it."

"You've met my mother?"

"I've read her books." He poured a second cup without asking if I wanted one. "I've also read her salvage claims. Sit down. Your pilot is fine, by the way. Uncomfortable, but fine. His comfort is entirely up to you."

I sat. The coffee was excellent and I hated it.

"Here's the shape of things," Vance said. "Your mother filed a claim on that wreck eleven days ago. A valid claim—her name carries weight with the maritime board, more weight than mine, frankly, which annoys me. But she's missing. Presumed dead, some say. And a claim held by a dead woman expires." He turned his cup a quarter turn on its saucer, precise as a surgeon. "Unless it's transferred to next of kin."

"And you need my signature."

"I need your *name*. The Sandoval name on the transfer makes this legitimate instead of merely profitable. There are people upstream who watch legitimacy very closely." He leaned back. "In exchange: your pilot walks free, your mother—if she's alive—is found and brought home, and you take ten percent of a cargo worth more than either of our reputations."

"The codex isn't for sale."

"The codex," Vance said mildly, "is a book. Books don't drown. Everything else down there does, every day the tide works on it. Think about what your mother would want. She spent her life pulling things out of rivers. You'd be finishing her work."

That was the hook, and he knew it, and he watched me swallow it. Because he was right about one thing: my mother had built her legend on salvage, on bringing the drowned past into the light. Every argument I had against him could be bent into an argument for him. That's what made him dangerous—not the money, not the muscle, but the way his offer sounded almost like inheritance.

"I want to see where Cass is being kept," I said. "And I want proof my mother's alive before I sign anything."

"Reasonable." Vance signaled to the man in the white shirt. "Take Miss Sandoval below. Show her the pilot. And—" a pause, delicate as a knife laid flat, "—show her the dive schedule. Let her see how far along we already are. I find that clarifies negotiations wonderfully."

* * *

They kept Cass in a storage compartment aft of the engine room, a steel box with a pipe bunk and no porthole. When they opened the door he was sitting cross-legged on the deck doing something to the hinge with a bent spoon, and he looked up at me with an expression of profound theatrical disappointment.

"Took you long enough."

"They grabbed you off my boat."

"They grabbed *me* because I let them. Someone had to get aboard this tub, and they weren't going to invite the pilot." He stood, rolling his shoulders. "You took the deal?"

"I'm considering the deal."

"Good. Keep considering. Slowly." His voice dropped. "Dive schedule. Ask about the dive schedule."

The escort cleared his throat. I asked.

The white-shirted man—his name tag said ORTIZ—walked me forward along a corridor lined with charts and sonar repeaters, talking with the pride of a man showing off his own house. "Mr. Vance located the cavern system eight days ago. Side-scan can't resolve the interior—the rock overburden scatters the return—but the mouth was mapped by your mother's own survey team, before she went quiet. Convenient of her." He tapped a chart pinned to the bulkhead. The gorge, the bend, the drowned church upstream—and there, marked in red grease pencil, a fissure in the cliff wall below the waterline, exactly where Tomás had told us it would be. Exactly where Imelda had sent her coordinates.

Two divers were already committed. Ortiz said it casually, the way you'd mention laundry. "First pair went down at slack water. They'll be in the gallery by now. Second shift follows at the turn."

"When's the turn?"

"Nineteen hundred. Shift change takes forty minutes—decompression rotation, new bottles, new tenders. Mr. Vance doesn't cut corners on air." He smiled. "Safety is a core value of this operation."

* * *

I found a bench near the rail and sat with the coffee I hadn't drunk and thought about what choosing looks like when it's happening.

There was a version of me who signed. She was tired; she'd been tired for seven years. She signed, took her ten percent, pulled her mother out of whatever hole she was hiding in, and they fought about it for the rest of their lives but at least they were both alive to fight. That version had a bank account and a bad conscience and a mother who never quite looked at her the same way. That version was safe.

Then there was the arithmetic. Two divers in the gallery now. Shift change at nineteen hundred. Forty minutes of gap. And somewhere in that flooded dark, my mother—or her ghost, or her trap—waiting with a book that belonged to a village that had been drowned twice: once by the dam, once by the theft.

Tomás had said it on the night he slipped away from this same ship: *She faked her disappearance to draw the fleet away from the true site.* Which meant every hour the Aurelia sat here chasing divers through the wrong end of the cavern was an hour bought with my mother's blood and broken ribs. Vance hadn't found the true site yet. The red grease pencil marked the mouth, not the church. If I signed, the search would stop being a search and become a harvest.

I went looking for Ortiz.

* * *

"One condition," I told Vance, that evening, in his cabin with its cold air and its framed photographs of himself shaking hands with governors. "Before I sign anything, I inspect the operation. All of it. My mother taught me that a claim is only as good as its survey. If I'm putting my name on this wreck, I want to see the winch gear, the lift bags, the decompression station. If your equipment can't bring that cargo up intact, my signature is worthless and you know it."

Vance studied me for a long moment. I let him study. I let him see the tiredness, the seven years, the daughter who wanted her mother back—I gave him all of it, because all of it was true, and truth is the best camouflage there is.

"A survey inspection," he said finally. "You'll be escorted."

"Of course."

"And the pilot stays aboard until the papers are signed."

"Done."

He believed me. That was the terrible part—that it was easy. Seven years of running from my mother's name had taught me exactly how to wear it, and Vance saw what everyone always saw: Imelda Sandoval's daughter, wanting to come home.

* * *

The dredge deck was amidships, aft of the dive station: two massive winches spooled with wire cable thick as my wrist, each feeding a clamshell dredge bucket rated to tear a riverbed open. They were moored over the gorge bend, not the cavern—dredging the galleon's original reported position, stripping the decoy site while the divers worked the true one. Even Vance's greed had a hierarchy.

Ortiz walked me through it, reciting tonnage ratings and cable gauges while I nodded and asked dull questions and touched things a surveyor would touch: the brake housings, the clutch linkages, the hydraulic lines running down into the winch guts. He watched my hands the whole time, so I kept them honest—tapping gauges, tracing welds, nothing that would raise an eyebrow.

Shift change began at 18:58. I knew because I'd asked Ortiz to walk me through the rotation timing twice, and surveyors are allowed to be pedantic.

The dive station emptied. Tenders ferried the exhausted first pair back toward the decompression chamber; fresh crews mustered at the rack, checking bottles, arguing about weights. The deck between the winches and the dive station became a corridor of moving bodies, radios crackling, nobody watching the woman in the borrowed coveralls taking notes on a clipboard she'd liberated from the chart room.

The brake housings were the answer. Each winch drum carried a band brake, and each band brake was adjusted by a threaded tensioner rod locked with a cotter pin. Standard gear, standard pins. I'd grown up on dig sites where a jammed winch meant a collapsed trench wall, and my mother had drilled one rule into me before any other: *if you can't stop the machine, make sure it stops itself.*

The first pin came out with pliers from my field kit, quick as pulling a tooth. I dropped it into the river. Then I backed the tensioner rod off four full turns—not enough to show at a glance, not enough to bind the brake now, but enough that under load, with two tons of dredge bucket swinging on the wire, the band would slip, then chatter, then fail. Not sabotage that looked like sabotage. Sabotage that looked like maintenance error. Like rust. Like the river itself refusing.

The second winch was harder. A rigger stood ten feet away coiling hose, and I had to kneel at the housing and pretend to photograph the weld seams with a camera I wasn't sure worked. My hands were steady. That surprised me. Somewhere underneath the fear was a strange, clean calm, the feeling I'd only ever gotten reading current lines in bad water—the feeling of knowing exactly where the river was going and putting myself in front of it on purpose.

Four turns. Pin out. Pin gone.

"There's a problem with your third cable guide," I told the rigger, pointing hard at the bow side, and while he looked I closed the housing and stepped back.

By 19:40 both winches were dressed, inspected, and quietly dying. Ortiz walked me to my cabin—"for your comfort until signing"—and I thanked him with my mother's smile, the useful one.

* * *

The cabin had a porthole. Through it, the gorge was going dark, and beyond the headland, faint as breath on glass, the running light of a battered river boat crept along the shadow line of the cliffs. Cass's cousin's boat—the one we'd arranged, the one Cass had let himself be captured to buy time for. They were coming for the extraction, and they were early, and they were trusting me to make the cavern mouth safe to enter.

Below me, muffled through eighty feet of steel and river, the second dive shift was suiting up. In forty minutes they'd be in the water, swimming toward my mother with lights and knives and a manifest for a cargo that was never theirs.

I sat on the bunk and listened to the engines and understood, finally, what my mother must have felt the day she decided to vanish. Not bravery. Just arithmetic—the kind where every column sums to the same number, and the number is *no*, and after that the only question left is how loudly to say it.

I checked my watch. Nineteeen fifty-two.

Eight minutes to the tide turn. Eight minutes to decide whether those divers swam into a guarded gallery or a trap.

I pulled the clipboard onto my lap and started writing, fast, in surveyor's shorthand only Cass would read: *Winches dead by morning. Divers due in gallery 20:10. Mother's position unknown. Coming in through the low channel—tell them to kill their lights.*

Then I folded it small, and went to find a window that opened.
