## 17. Flooded Hold

The fifth tide began to turn at 3:40 in the morning, and Zephyrine was already in the water.

She'd timed it against Cass's hand-drawn chart and her own soundings from two nights ago, when the Mariposa had limped into the hidden lagoon behind San Remedios with a cracked seam and a crew of three who hadn't slept in thirty hours. The cavern mouth opened only on the ebb—a black slot in the limestone, four meters down, wide enough for a diver with gear but not for a man carrying a dredge head. That was the whole arithmetic of the next ninety minutes: she could fit through; Vance's machinery could not. If she reached her mother before his divers did, the codex stayed out of his hands. If she didn't—

She didn't finish the thought. She'd stopped finishing that thought somewhere around the second sleepless night.

The water was cold in a way river water shouldn't be, cold rising off the deep stone like breath off ice. Her lamp cut a cone through the green-black and found nothing, then nothing, then the wall—limestone scalloped by centuries of current, slick with a silt so fine it hung in the water like smoke. She kicked down past the slot's lip, angled her body sideways, and slid into the throat of the mountain.

Tomás had drawn the route from memory on the back of a supply manifest: forty meters of descending gallery, then a chamber where the old church's bell tower had collapsed inward when the valley flooded, sealing a pocket of air behind a shelf of stone. *Your mother knew about the pocket before anyone,* he'd said, pencil trembling. *She mapped this system in '09 and told no one. Not even me.* Zephyrine had looked at him then—at the man who had stood at Imelda Sandoval's right hand for twenty years and been lied to anyway—and understood something new about her childhood: the secrecy wasn't a phase or a paranoia. It was a discipline. Her mother had been practicing for this her entire life.

Forty meters. She counted kicks because counting kept the fear in its box. The gallery narrowed, widened, narrowed again. Somewhere above her, miles back downriver, the Aurelia sat at anchor with its floodlights burning, and Vance's dive team was staging on the lower deck, and Cass was locked in a hold below the waterline with a split lip and a grin he'd aimed at the guards like a weapon. *Go get her,* he'd said through the hatch gap, before they took him away. *And then come get me. In that order.*

*In that order,* she'd promised.

Her gauge read nine minutes of bottom time when the shelf appeared—a slab of fallen masonry angling up out of the dark like the rib of something enormous. Beyond it, her lamp caught movement: not current, not fish. A shimmer. The boundary between water and air, trembling where her light touched it.

An air pocket. Real. Exactly where a dead woman's first mate said it would be.

Zephyrine surfaced into darkness and the smell of wet stone and candle smoke.

Candle smoke.

Her mind snagged on it—there was no fuel down here, no reason for fire—and then her lamp swept the ledge and found the answer sitting in a tin dish, guttering, half-melted, and beside it a figure propped against the collapsed bell tower's stone, one leg bound in a field dressing gone gray with age, a dive knife loose in one hand and raised before Zephyrine had finished taking a breath.

The knife stopped mid-air.

"Zeph."

Seven years. Seven years of unanswered letters, of foundation calls declined, of measuring her life in miles specifically so she wouldn't have to measure it in years, and the first word her mother said to her was the nickname Zephyrine had forbidden everyone on earth to use since she was nineteen years old.

It should have made her angry. It did make her angry. But anger, she discovered, takes time to assemble—all those grievances lined up in order, all those speeches rehearsed in hotel rooms across four continents—and there was no time. There was only the candle, and the knife lowering, and her mother's face half-lit and older than seven years should have allowed, hollowed at the cheeks, a fresh scar over one eyebrow, eyes that were still, impossibly, exactly her mother's eyes: assessing, dry, already cataloguing Zephyrine's gear, her air consumption, the state of the passage behind her.

"You came down the north gallery," Imelda said. Her voice was roughened, underused. "Good. The south entrance silts at slack water. You'd have been trapped."

That was it. That was the greeting. No embrace, no explanation, no *I'm sorry I let the world believe I was dead for eleven days while you crossed a country on a mail steamer*. Just hydrology. Just the same voice that had corrected Zephyrine's stratigraphy drawings when she was twelve, precise and unsoftened, as if the intervening years were an inconvenience both of them had agreed to overlook.

Zephyrine hauled herself onto the ledge, pulled her mask down around her neck, and discovered she was shaking—not from cold, though the cold was real, but from the sheer pressure of everything she'd carried upriver finally finding its target and having nowhere to go.

"You're alive," she said. It came out accusatory.

"For now." Imelda shifted, and Zephyrine saw the full damage: the bound leg swollen above the knee, the improvised splint, the way she braced every movement against the stone like a woman who had learned exactly how much weight it would bear. "Broken, not shattered. I set it myself on day three. Crude work." A pause. "You always were better with your hands than I am."

"Don't." Zephyrine held up a hand. "Don't do that. Don't make small talk like we're at a dig briefing. You faked your death. You let them put a compass on an empty grave. You let *me*—" Her voice cracked and she hated it, hated the crack, hated that her mother watched it happen with those patient cataloguing eyes. "I planned your funeral, Mamá. In my head. On the boat. Twice."

"I know." No apology in it. No deflection either. Just acknowledgment, flat and honest, which was somehow worse and better than anything else she could have said. "I needed the fleet to believe the site was here and I was gone. Vance doesn't chase ghosts. He chases assets. A dead Imelda Sandoval is a closed file; a missing one is a search party, and search parties bring sonar, and sonar finds caverns." She nodded toward the dark water below the ledge. "As long as I was dead, this place was just rock."

"And Tomás?"

"Knew enough to keep the story moving and not enough to blow it. He's better at loyalty than I ever gave him credit for." Something moved across her face—regret, quickly filed away. "He'll have told you the rest. About the codex."

"He told me." Zephyrine's jaw tightened. "San Remedios. The flooded church. You found it eight years ago and never logged it. You built the family name on a discovery you'd already made and buried."

"Yes."

"That's it? *Yes?*"

"What would you like, Zeph? A speech?" Imelda leaned her head back against the stone, and in the candlelight Zephyrine saw how exhausted she was—not injured-woman exhausted, but something deeper, the exhaustion of a person who has been alone with a secret long enough for the secret to become the whole room. "I stole it. Or my generation stole it—my teachers, my mentors, the whole architecture of the profession that taught us a find belonged to whoever wrote the paper. I was young and ambitious and the village was drowned and voiceless, and no one was going to stop me, so I didn't stop myself. Then I spent eight years discovering that I couldn't live with it. That's the confession. It's not noble. It's just true."

Zephyrine had imagined this conversation a hundred times on the river—in the Mariposa's wheelhouse, in the reeds outside the abandoned camp, staring at that grave marker with its lying compass. In every version she was magnificent. She laid out the abandonment, the exile, the childhood summers spent watching her mother hide maps from funders and understanding even then that some part of the family legend was load-bearing fiction. In every version, Imelda broke, or apologized, or at minimum flinched.

In the actual cave, Imelda did none of these things. She simply sat with it, breathing, letting the truth stand between them like a third person neither of them introduced.

And Zephyrine found—to her fury—that the fury was running out of fuel. Because underneath the anger was the thing she'd felt the moment she smelled candle smoke: her mother was alive, and her mother had set her own broken leg alone in the dark, and her mother had guarded a stolen book with a dive knife for eleven days rather than trade it for her own rescue. Whatever else Imelda Sandoval was, she was not a coward, and she was not a liar in the small ways. Only in the enormous ones.

"The divers," Zephyrine said. "Vance has a team staging. They'll run the south gallery when the tide gives them the window. Two hours, maybe less."

"They won't fit the equipment through the squeeze at twelve meters. I collapsed part of it deliberately." A ghost of the old smile, sharp as a trowel edge. "Your mother does her best work with a pry bar."

"It'll slow them. It won't stop them. And Cass is still on the Aurelia."

Imelda went very still. "You left him aboard?"

"He told me to. We're getting him back tonight—I need the Mariposa positioned at the north mouth for extraction, and Tomás is turning the searchlights on the fleet's own bridge watch at shift change. After that—" She stopped. Recalibrated. "After that, we take the codex out through the north gallery and give it to San Remedios. All of it. Publicly. You sign over any claim you have, and you say so on record, and you let the family name take the hit it's earned."

Silence. The candle flame bent in some imperceptible draft. Zephyrine braced for the argument—the great Imelda Sandoval surrendering her life's work, her reputation, the Sandoval Codex itself, the artifact that had funded twenty years of expeditions and bought the house in the capital and paid for Zephyrine's education and built the pedestal Zephyrine had spent seven years climbing down from.

"All right," Imelda said.

Zephyrine blinked. "All right?"

"It was never mine. That's the entire point, and it took me eight years and a broken leg to learn it properly." She reached beneath the ledge, slow with pain, and drew out a waterproof case—scuffed, mud-stained, wrapped in a fishing net for buoyancy—and set it on the stone between them without ceremony. "There it is. The thing that made us. Forty-one pages of pre-colonial river law, land records, the names of families who farmed this valley for six hundred years before anyone dammed it. The elders of San Remedios can read their own history in it. Vance wants it because a provenance that old makes his salvage claim unassailable." Her mouth twisted. "I want it returned because it was never salvage. It was theft with a bibliography."

Zephyrine stared at the case. Eight years of her life had been organized around the shadow of this object without her knowing it existed. Every grant denied, every colleague's condescension, every time someone said *ah, the daughter* —all of it traced back to a book her mother had lifted from a drowned church and hidden in plain sight inside the family legend.

"Why tell me?" she asked quietly. "Why signal me? You could have waited out the fleet alone. You've clearly got the supplies. Why pull me upriver?"

Imelda looked at her for a long moment, and for the first time the cataloguing gaze faltered, and what was underneath it was just a tired woman in a cave looking at the child she had driven away.

"Because a confession isn't finished until someone hears it," she said. "And because if I died down here holding this thing, it would have died as mine. I wanted it to die as theirs. Someone had to carry it out who wasn't already compromised." A pause. "Also, I missed you. In that order."

Despite everything—despite seven years and the empty grave and the crack in her own voice—Zephyrine laughed. It came out ragged and surprised, and Imelda's mouth twitched in response, and for three seconds they were just two people in a ridiculous situation, laughing at the absurdity of *in that order*, of a family that communicated exclusively in prioritized logistics.

Then Imelda winced, shifting the leg, and the moment passed—but it left something behind. Not absolution. Zephyrine wasn't ready for that, and suspected she might never be, and understood suddenly that her mother wasn't asking for it. Reconciliation without absolution: two women agreeing to move forward while leaving the debt on the table, visible, unpaid, acknowledged. It wasn't forgiveness. It was maybe better than forgiveness. It was honest.

Zephyrine checked her gauge. Eleven minutes of reserve. Outside, the tide was nearing slack, and somewhere downriver a dive team was checking seals and checking watches, and a river pilot with a split lip was waiting for her to keep a promise.

"We move on the next ebb," she said. "You can't walk the gallery, so I rig a haul line and we float you through the squeezes. The Mariposa will be at the north mouth by then—Tomás is handling the lights, and the elders are sending two guides to cover our wake. Once we're clear of the gorge, we go straight to San Remedios and you sign the transfer in front of the whole village."

"In front of the whole village," Imelda repeated. She looked at the case, then at her daughter, and something in her face settled, like sediment finding the bottom after a storm. "You've thought this through."

"I've had five tides."

"So you have." Imelda began unwinding the netting from the case, testing its seals with practiced fingers, and then paused. "Zeph. When we surface—if Vance's people are already at the north mouth—"

"They won't be."

"If they are." Her mother's eyes came up, steady and terrible in the candlelight. "You take the case and you swim. You don't negotiate, you don't bargain, you don't come back for me. Do you understand? This ends tonight, one way or the other, and it ends with the book in San Remedios. Not with sentiment."

Zephyrine met the look and held it, and realized she was no longer the girl who had fled that gaze at nineteen. "Understood," she said. "But it won't come to that. Because in about twenty minutes, the Aurelia's going to lose its searchlights, its dive schedule, and its pilot's patience—all at once."

Above them, faint through sixty feet of stone and water, came a sound neither of them could possibly have heard: the deep-throated groan of the Aurelia's generators stuttering as Tomás Reyes threw the master breaker and turned his employers' own lights against them.

Tide six was coming.

They had seventy minutes to be gone.
