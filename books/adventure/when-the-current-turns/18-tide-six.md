## 18. Tide Six

### Tomás

The searchlights of the *Aurelia* were Tomás Reyes's to command, and for eleven days he had aimed them exactly where Aurelio Vance wanted them pointed — at black water, at empty shoals, at the theater of a search that was never meant to find anything until the dredges arrived. Eleven days of playing the loyal traitor. His hands had learned the console's switches so well he could run the light array blind.

Tonight he would do it literally.

He stood on the aft deck while the river breathed beneath him, that slow tidal exhale that meant the sixth tide was building out at the gorge mouth. Somewhere below, in a cavern no sonar could read, Imelda Sandoval was alive — he'd bet his remaining soul on it — and her daughter was down there too, because of course she was. Zephyrine Sandoval ran from her mother for seven years and then swam straight into the dark after her. He almost admired it. Almost. Admiration was a luxury; what he had was a debt, and debts on this river were paid in motion, not words.

Vance came up behind him, leather shoes loud on the steel grating. "Status."

"Patrol boats report the Mariposa anchored off San Remedios. Girl's aboard. Pilot too." He kept his voice flat, bored, the voice of a man who had stopped caring — which was itself a performance, because his heart was hammering like a dredge bucket against stone.

"And the cavern?"

"Sonar still reads it as silt shelf. If there's an opening, we haven't found it."

Vance leaned on the rail beside him, close enough that Tomás could smell the cigar and the cologne over it, money trying to drown its own rot. "I have divers in the water, Reyes. Three teams. They'll find the opening if your lights don't beat them to it."

"Then give me the bearing," Tomás said, and hated how easily the words came. Seven years of loyalty to Imelda, undone in one season by a man who owned his brother's medical debt and knew precisely what a first mate's signature was worth. "Give me where you want the light."

Vance gave him coordinates. Wrong ones — deliberately wrong, Tomás realized, which meant Vance suspected him, or wanted to see whether he'd flinch. Tomás entered them without flinching. Let the old man watch his own searchlights sweep a stretch of empty gorge all night while his divers groped through cold water toward a hole that would swallow them.

At 0300, when the tide turned and the current began its long pull seaward, Tomás made his move. Not a dramatic one. He simply reprogrammed the light array — a maintenance override, thirty seconds of keystrokes he'd rehearsed in his bunk for a week — and sent every lamp on the *Aurelia* sweeping inward, crossing, strobing, washing out the patrol boats' night vision, whiting out the dive tenders' marker buoys, turning the whole fleet blind at the exact hour the Mariposa needed darkness most.

An alarm shrilled somewhere below decks. Boots on stairs.

Tomás keyed the radio to the frequency only three people alive knew — Imelda's frequency, the one she'd used to whisper coordinates to a daughter she thought wasn't listening — and said, calm as a weather report: "Lights are yours. Go now. I'll keep them busy."

Then he pulled the main breaker, plunged the *Aurelia*'s deck into blackness, and walked toward the sound of running men with his hands raised like a man surrendering — or like a man who had finally, after one long cowardly year, decided what he was willing to be caught for.

### Cass

Cass Ibarra had threaded boats through the gorge for nineteen years, and he had never done it like this.

No moon. No running lights — Zephyrine had taped cardboard over every lamp on the Mariposa herself, methodical as a surgeon, because the cavern mouth sat behind a curtain of falling water and any glow would bounce off the mist like a signal fire. The flood tide was shoving them upstream at four knots, faster than safe, faster than sane, and the roar of the falls ahead filled the wheelhouse so completely that he was steering by memory and by the girl's voice.

"Hard to port," Zephyrine called from the bow, one hand on the rail, surveyor's eyes reading water he couldn't see. "There's an eddy line — you'll feel it in a second — trust it."

He felt it. The hull shuddered, swung, and the current that had been trying to fold them against the north wall let go all at once, and they were through, into the relative hush behind the waterfall, the cavern mouth yawning black and dripping ten meters off the starboard bow.

"Madre de Dios," he muttered, and brought her in.

Inside, the air changed — cold, mineral, ancient. The Mariposa's bow nudged against a submerged ledge that some drowned congregation had built as a church landing two hundred years ago, and Cass got a line around a stone stanchion with hands that wouldn't quite stop shaking. Not fear. He'd tell anyone who asked it wasn't fear. It was the shaking of a man who has just spent everything he has left — license, boat, life — on one passage, and hasn't yet learned whether the spending bought anything.

Zephyrine came aft, dripping, wetsuit peeled to the waist, hair plastered flat. Behind her, two of San Remedios's young men hauled a stretcher up from the dive platform, and on the stretcher —

Cass took off his hat. He didn't know why. It seemed right.

Imelda Sandoval looked smaller than her legend. Gray in the braid, a splinted forearm, a face carved down to angles by six weeks underground. But her eyes opened, found Cass, and sharpened.

"Ibarra," she rasped. "They stripped your license."

"They did."

"Good," she said. "Licensed pilots follow rules. We're past rules." And then her eyes rolled back and she was asleep again between one breath and the next, and Zephyrine caught her mother's head before it hit the stretcher rail, cradling it with a tenderness she would have denied under torture.

"Get us out of the narrows before the tide drops," Zephyrine said, voice steady, hands not. "She needs a hospital. Real light. Real air."

"The tide drops in forty minutes and the *Aurelia* will have its lights back by then, assuming Reyes is still breathing."

"Assuming," Zephyrine agreed, and something passed between them — the thing they'd been building since Puerto Cobre, wordless, made of shared enemies and complementary skills and the particular intimacy of people who have nearly died together twice. She put her hand once, briefly, on his shoulder. "You got us in. Get us out."

Cass put his hat back on and went to the wheel. Outside, far downstream, the great searchlights of the salvage fleet swept the gorge in wild, crossing arcs — then stuttered, crossed again, and went dark all at once, the whole fleet blinking blind like a lighthouse having a seizure.

"That's our window," he said. "That's Reyes paying his debt. Hold on to something."

### Zephyrine

Her mother weighed nothing.

That was the thought that kept arriving, absurd and unbearable, as Zephyrine braced the stretcher against the wheelhouse bulkhead while Cass drove them back out through the falls' thunder. Dr. Imelda Sandoval — whose name was on three textbooks, whose signature had launched a thousand funding proposals, whose shadow had been the entire geography of Zephyrine's childhood — weighed nothing at all. Six weeks in a wet cave had boiled the legend down to bone and stubbornness.

The codex case sat wedged between Zephyrine's boots: titanium, sealed, heavier than sin. Her inheritance. The thing that had built the family name out of other people's drowned church. She'd held it for an hour now and hadn't opened it, and understood suddenly that she might never open it, that some things you carry closed.

In the flooded gallery below, Vance's third dive team was still coming. She'd heard their tanks knocking against rock in the dark, heard the garble of their comms through the water. The silt was rising — the surge tide was chewing the lower gallery apart, visibility gone from bad to biblical, and those divers were swimming blind into a collapsing throat. One of them had grabbed her ankle in the blackout. She'd kicked free and felt, absurdly, guilty about it.

"Contact!" one of the village boys shouted from the bow — Mateo, seventeen, who'd volunteered for this run with the serene fearlessness of someone who grew up on a drowning river. "Boat, port side, no lights!"

Cass killed their engine and let them drift. Through the curtain of falling water Zephyrine saw it: a dive tender, adrift, rolling badly, one man clinging to its rail and another in the water holding a line, both of them being dragged seaward by the ebb, straight toward the rocks at the gorge mouth.

Vance's men. The ones her mother's collapse had orphaned.

For one clean second, Zephyrine considered letting the river do what rivers do. These were the hands stripping the wreck, the hands that had smashed the base camp radios, the hands of the machine that had taken San Remedios twice — once its village, now its past. The current was already deciding. All she had to do was nothing.

Her mother stirred on the stretcher and said, barely audible under the falls: "The diver. Not the gold. Never the gold."

Zephyrine was moving before the sentence ended.

"Cass! Hard aport, get us alongside — Mateo, throw lines, both of you, NOW—"

The Mariposa swung across the ebb like a gull riding wind, and for ninety seconds it was all rope and shouting and the river hauling at everyone equally — the salvager's man hauled gasping over the gunwale, then his partner, then the empty tender cracking itself to kindling on the outer rocks behind them. The rescued diver lay coughing on the deck, maybe forty years old, sobbing with cold and terror, and Zephyrine crouched over him and wrapped a thermal blanket around his shoulders with her own hands.

"Where's Vance?" she asked.

The man stared at her. Recognition moved across his face — the name Sandoval doing its famous work even here, even now. "Below," he choked. "He wouldn't come up. He's in the lower gallery with the last team. He says—" another spasm of coughing, "—he says the case is worth more than the whole fleet. He's going for it himself."

Zephyrine looked down at the titanium case between her boots. Then at her mother, gray and breathing. Then at the black water rising in the gorge, the sixth tide at its height, the river gathering itself for the turn.

"He can have the gold," she said. "It was never ours either."

But her hands were already checking her tank, because the lower gallery was flooding, and whatever else Aurelio Vance deserved, Zephyrine had learned something in the last six tides about what you owe the drowning — even the ones who never learned to swim in anything but money.

"Cass," she said, pulling her mask down. "Turn us around."

* * *

The river turned with them, and did not care what any of them owed.
