## 19. The Current Turns

The water told her first.

Zephyrine felt it before she heard it—a pressure change in the flooded gallery, a low groan rolling through the limestone like the mountain clearing its throat. She had spent seven years learning to read rivers, and every river she'd ever known spoke the same language before it turned violent: a hush, then a shudder, then the roar.

The surge tide was coming.

She kicked hard for the air pocket where her mother waited, the codex case strapped across her chest in a harness Imelda had rigged from dive weights and prayer. Behind her, somewhere in the black throat of the cavern system, Vance's divers were pushing inward, their lights cutting pale blades through the silted water. Cass was threading the *Mariposa* toward the cavern mouth above. Tomás had turned the *Aurelia's* searchlights on its own fleet. Every debt in this river was being paid at once, and hers was due now.

The air pocket opened around her like a held breath. Imelda Sandoval sat against the stone shelf where Zephyrine had found her an hour ago—leg broken below the knee, splinted with a survey stake and neoprene wrap, face gray with pain and six days of waiting in the dark. She looked smaller than the legend. She looked, for the first time in Zephyrine's memory, like just a woman.

"You feel that?" Zephyrine said, pulling off her regulator.

Imelda tilted her head, listening to the mountain groan. "Surge tide. The gorge narrows at the lower bend—it'll funnel the whole thing straight through here." Her eyes went to the case on Zephyrine's chest. "You should be gone."

"So should you. Can you swim?"

"I can float with assistance." A ghost of the old dry humor. "Barely."

"Then we go together, and we go now."

They were moving—Zephyrine hauling her mother's arm across her shoulders, both of them half-swimming through the shallowing gallery toward the upper passage—when the second sound came. Not the mountain this time. A voice, distorted by rock and water, but unmistakable: Aurelio Vance, shouting from the lower gallery entrance, his dive team's lights strobing below them.

"The case!" His voice bounced off the wet stone, ragged with something worse than greed—panic. The man had finally understood what the tide was going to do to him. "Sandoval! You bring that case up or your pilot drowns in my hold!"

Zephyrine stopped.

Cass. Taken once already, escaped once already, and Vance had him again—or claimed to. She couldn't know if it was true. That was the whole architecture of a man like Vance: he built his bargains out of things you couldn't verify, so that fear did the verification for him.

Her mother's hand closed on her wrist. "Don't," Imelda said quietly. "He's lying. He has to be lying, because if he had Cass he wouldn't need the case—he'd have traded him already."

"Or he's telling the truth because he knows I won't gamble."

"That's exactly what he's counting on. Both can't be true, Zeph. Choose which man you think he is."

The water chose for her.

The surge hit the lower gallery like a freight train made of river. Even forty feet up and fifty back, they felt it—a wall of displaced water slamming through the narrow channel, and with it came the sound Zephyrine would hear for the rest of her life: the deep, structural crack of the old church roof finally giving way under two centuries of patience and one tide too many.

Stone came down. The lower gallery—the one Vance's divers had entered, the one that led to the galleon's drowned hold and its three hundred years of silver—folded in on itself with a grinding finality that turned the water brown and then black.

The shouting stopped.

For three heartbeats there was only the hiss of settling silt and the thunder receding upriver through channels no map had ever charted.

Then, faintly, a new sound. A diver's emergency whistle. One blast. Two. Weak, rhythmic, desperate—and coming not from the collapsed gallery but from the side channel, the narrow fissure where one of Vance's men had evidently been scouting when the roof let go. Cut off from his exit, cut off from his surface line, pinned behind a rockfall that was still shifting.

Zephyrine looked at her mother. Her mother looked at the water.

"Go," Imelda said.

"He'll drown."

"Yes. In about four minutes, if the air pocket in that fissure doesn't collapse first. And the case will survive either way—the river's wanted it longer than we have." Imelda's voice was steady, clinical, the voice she used on funders and rivals alike. But her eyes weren't. "I'm telling you what the arithmetic says. I'm not telling you what to do. I've done enough of that for both our lives."

Zephyrine unclipped the harness.

The codex case slid from her chest into her mother's arms, and for one absurd moment they were two women in a flooded cave passing a family heirloom like a casserole dish, and Zephyrine almost laughed—almost—but the whistle blew again, weaker, and there was nothing funny left anywhere in the world.

"San Remedios gets it," she said. "Whatever happens down there. San Remedios gets it, and you sign the claim over, and you do it publicly, or I will spend the rest of my life making sure everyone knows exactly how the Sandoval name was built."

"I know." Her mother's voice cracked on something that might have been pride. "I've known since the day you were born. Go save the man, Zeph. It's the first thing you've ever done that wasn't about me."

Zephyrine dove.

* * *

The side channel was a nightmare of compressed space—barely wide enough for her shoulders, the rockfall still ticking and settling overhead like a clock running down. Her light found him fifteen feet in: a young diver, maybe mid-twenties, wedged behind a slab of fallen roof stone, his mask fogged, his tank gauge deep in the red. Not Vance. Some hired kid from the salvage crew, the kind who took dangerous contracts because the safe ones didn't exist for men without names.

His eyes went wide when her light hit him. Then wider when he registered who she was—or rather, whose daughter. Everyone on that fleet knew the Sandoval name. It was the whole reason Vance had wanted her aboard.

She pointed at his regulator, then at her own spare octopus, and mimicked: breathe slow. He nodded, shaking so hard the vibration traveled through the water between them.

The slab pinning his leg was the size of a door. She couldn't move it. She could see that immediately—two hundred pounds of colonial masonry seated into the channel floor. What she could move was the smaller stone beneath it, the one acting as its fulcrum, the one the surge had half-scoured away. Surveyor's instinct, her mother's training repurposed: don't fight the mass, find the pivot.

She wedged herself under the slab's edge, got her shoulder against it, and pushed with her legs against the channel wall. For a horrible second nothing happened. Then the fulcrum stone rolled, the slab tipped six inches, and the kid screamed through his regulator as he wrenched his leg free.

His tank was nearly empty. Her octopus reached him just as his own reg started delivering nothing but resistance. They breathed share-air up the channel, his arm locked in hers, the rockfall groaning above them like a thing waking up angry.

Twice the passage narrowed and she had to strip gear to get them through. Once the ceiling dropped so low they went through on their backs, faces inches from stone, sharing one mouthpiece in the rhythm divers call buddy-breathing and survivors call something else entirely.

And all the while, behind them, the river was taking everything else.

She could hear it—the deeper booms of further collapse, the galleon's last hold surrendering, three centuries of silver and gold and stolen history being swallowed back into the geology that had briefly, accidentally, given it up. Vance's fortune folding shut like a book nobody would ever read again.

At the mouth of the fissure, open water. Above them, far above, a wavering point of light: the *Mariposa's* floodlamp punching down through thirty feet of churning tide, Cass refusing to leave, Tomás holding the boat on station against a current that wanted it gone.

They rose together.

* * *

Hands pulled the kid over the gunwale first—Tomás, soaked and shaking, working with the efficiency of a man expiating something. Then Zephyrine, hauled aboard by arms that belonged to Cass Ibarra, who looked at her with an expression she'd never seen on him before and couldn't name and didn't try to.

"Vance," she managed. "Where is he?"

Cass's jaw worked. "On the *Aurelia*. He surfaced before the collapse—made it back with two of his crew. He's screaming for divers to go back in." He paused. "There's no back to go into. That whole gallery is a tomb now. He lost four men inside before the roof finished coming down."

"And the gold?"

"Gone." Cass said it simply. "All of it. The river closed the account."

Zephyrine sat down hard on the deck plates, next to the rescued diver, who was coughing river water and staring at her like she was a question he'd never be able to answer. Upstream, the *Aurelia's* searchlights swept the churning water in frantic, useless arcs. Somewhere on that bridge, Aurelio Vance was watching three hundred years of leverage dissolve into silt, and she found—to her own surprise—that she felt no triumph at all.

Only the strange, weightless clarity of a woman who had chosen the harder arithmetic and discovered it was the lighter one.

Her mother surfaced beside the boat ten minutes later, hauled up by Tomás with the codex case still lashed to her chest, and the first thing Imelda Sandoval said, wrapped in blankets on the deck of a battered river boat while the wreck of her greatest discovery collapsed behind her, was:

"Well. Now we find out who you are when there's nothing left to inherit."

Zephyrine looked at her—at the gray face and the broken leg and the seven years of silence sitting between them like a third passenger—and realized the answer had been underwater for the last twenty minutes.

"I'm the one who goes back for people," she said. "That's the whole inheritance. That's all of it."

The *Mariposa* turned downriver toward San Remedios, riding the ebb of the sixth tide, and behind them the current kept turning over what the mountain had taken—though whether the river was burying the past or finally, after three hundred years, setting it free, Zephyrine knew she'd never be the one to say.

But ahead, in the dark, the village transmitter was already warming up.
