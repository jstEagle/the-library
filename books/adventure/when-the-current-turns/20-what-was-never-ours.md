## 20. What Was Never Ours

The rain had stopped by the time they carried the codex case up from the Mariposa, but San Remedios still dripped. Water fell from the church eaves in a slow percussion, and the flooded street reflected the gray morning like a second sky lying on its back.

Zephyrine walked beside her mother, and neither of them spoke. Imelda Sandoval leaned on a driftwood cane Tomás had cut for her, her left leg bound in a splint of scavenged aluminum and river cloth, and she refused—absolutely refused—the arm Zephyrine kept offering. Pride, Zephyrine thought. Seven years I ran from it, and it walks beside me now, limping.

The village had come out to meet them. Not a crowd, exactly. A gathering. Old men in plastic ponchos, women with children balanced on their hips, teenagers perched on the sagging balcony of the customs house where the waterline still stained the plaster at shoulder height—a mark from the year the dam closed and the river came into their homes and stayed.

Doña Esperanza stood at the head of the stone steps of the flooded church, which rose out of the water like the ribcage of some patient animal. She was smaller than Zephyrine remembered from three nights ago, when she'd spat at the Sandoval name. Or perhaps Zephyrine was simply seeing her clearly now.

"You brought it back," Esperanza said. Not a question. Her voice was dry as old rope.

"We brought it back," Imelda said.

"Say it plainer."

Imelda set the case down on the lowest step, where the water lapped at the stone. She knelt—bad leg and all, lowering herself with the care of a woman twice her age—and opened it. The codex lay inside, wrapped in oilcloth, its bark-paper pages dark with forty years of river damp but intact. Intact because Imelda had kept it that way. Intact because she had hidden it well.

"The Codex of San Remedios," Imelda said, loud enough to carry across the water. "Recovered from this church in the flood year. Removed without consent. Kept under my name for thirty-one years. It was never mine. It was never ours." She looked up at Esperanza, and Zephyrine watched her mother's face do something it had never done in any photograph, any lecture hall, any award ceremony: it emptied itself of performance. "I am signing over all claim to it, and to everything recovered from the galleon. The papers go to the notary in Puerto Cobre on tomorrow's mail boat. I'm told my signature is worth something. Let it be worth this."

Silence, except the dripping eaves.

Then an old man near the back—the one who had guided them through the cavern mouth—began, quietly, to sing. Others joined. It wasn't a hymn Zephyrine knew; the words were in the old river tongue, half-drowned like the village itself, and the melody moved the way water moves, finding every low place.

Esperanza lifted the codex from its case herself and held it against her chest. "Thirty-one years," she said. "My mother died not knowing where it went. She thought the river had taken it back."

"It nearly did," Imelda said. "It nearly took me instead."

That drew something that wasn't quite laughter from the gathering—a sound between grief and relief, the sound people make when a wound is acknowledged after so long that acknowledging it almost hurts worse than carrying it.

---

They argued that afternoon, in the room above the customs house that Cass had declared headquarters—though arguing was perhaps too generous a word for what happened when two Sandovals were given four walls and a shared grievance.

"You could have told me," Zephyrine said. "One letter. One radio call. *Return*, you said—on a frequency only I would monitor. You could have said *come help me*."

"And you would have come running to save the legend," Imelda said. "Not me. The legend. I needed you to come save the truth, and you couldn't have done that while you still believed in the legend."

"That's a hell of a way to test it."

"I don't test people, Zephyrine. I run out of time." Imelda lowered herself onto the cot, wincing at the leg. "Vance's lawyers were already circling the foundation. If I'd broadcast the truth about the codex, he'd have buried it—legally, financially, whatever it took—and the village would have gotten nothing but a lawsuit. So I disappeared. I let him chase a ghost upriver while I sat in an air pocket with a broken leg and thirty years of guilt, waiting for someone stubborn enough to find me." She looked up. "I knew you were stubborn. I hoped you were angry enough."

"You *hoped* I'd be angry?"

"Anger gets people upriver faster than love does. Love makes them careful."

Zephyrine turned away, hands braced on the windowsill, looking out at the water-stained town. "Seven years, Mamá. Seven years I measured everything in miles put between us. And the whole time you were down here drowning in your own conscience."

"Yes."

"That's it? Just yes?"

"What would you like me to say? That I'm sorry? I am. That I was wrong? Sometimes. That I'd do it differently?" Imelda paused, and Zephyrine heard her mother choosing honesty over comfort, the same way she'd always chosen it in her field notes, line after merciless line. "No. I'd do most of it the same. But I would have written to you. That part I'd change. That part was cowardice dressed up as strategy."

Zephyrine laughed. She hadn't meant to—it came out cracked and surprised, halfway to a sob. "You're impossible."

"I'm your mother. Same thing."

And then Imelda laughed too, a real one, rusty and unguarded, and for a moment the room above the customs house held nothing but two women laughing at the sheer absurdity of blood—how it binds, how it blinds, how it waits thirty-one years and seven thousand miles to be honest with itself.

Afterward they sat in silence. Not the old silence, the weaponized one, the silence Zephyrine had used like a door slammed between them across a thousand declined calls. This silence had furniture in it. Room to move. Zephyrine found her mother's hand resting on the cot and didn't take it, exactly. Just let hers settle beside it, close enough that the space between their fingers was a choice rather than a wall.

Outside, the river went on turning, indifferent and generous, carrying the late light toward the sea.

---

Cass found them at dusk. He knocked, which was unnecessary—the door didn't latch—but characteristically polite, and stepped in holding his license, the physical paper, creased and water-spotted, restored by wire order from the inquiry board in Puerto Cobre.

"They reinstated me," he said. "Formally. Something about 'distinguished service in aid of a public inquiry.'" His mouth twitched. "I've been called many things. Distinguished is new."

"You earned it," Zephyrine said.

"I threaded a gorge on a tide table that shouldn't have worked, following a surveyor who reads current lines like scripture." He nodded at her. "We earned it. Both of us. That's how boats work."

Imelda studied him with the frank appraisal she usually reserved for core samples. "So you're the pilot who outran my salvage fleet."

"Your daughter outran it. I just kept us pointed upstream."

"Hm." Imelda's eyes flicked to Zephyrine, and there was something in them Zephyrine had spent seven years convincing herself she'd never see again: approval, unarmored, offered plainly. "She reads water better than I ever did. I want that noted somewhere official."

"Mamá."

"Noted," Cass said solemnly, deadpan, and Zephyrine threw a rolled-up chart at his head, and he caught it without looking, and Imelda laughed again—that same new laugh, still surprising all three of them.

---

Evening came down over San Remedios the way it must have before the dam: slowly, gold going to violet, the flooded streets filling with reflected sky. From the church steps, Esperanza's voice carried across the water, reading aloud from the codex—not translating, just reading, letting the old words stand in the old tongue while the village listened the way people listen to rain after drought.

Zephyrine stood at the railing of the customs house balcony and felt the weight in her chest shift. Not gone. Weight like this didn't leave; it changed shape. Thirty-one years of a stolen thing, seven years of chosen exile, six tides of fear and current and luck—and none of it erased, none of it undone. But redistributed. Set down where it belonged.

Her mother had built a name on something that was never theirs. Fine. Then the name would carry the repair, too. Inheritance wasn't the gold in the hold or the codex in its case or even the famous surname. It was this: standing in a drowned town at dusk, knowing what you owed and to whom, and staying long enough to pay it.

Below, Cass was arguing cheerfully with Tomás about engine parts. Imelda sat on the steps beside Esperanza, two old women comparing scars, and when Esperanza handed her the codex to hold—one last time, just to feel it—Imelda's hands shook, and she didn't hide it.

Zephyrine watched, and did not look away, and understood at last that she was not her mother's echo, nor her mother's opposite, nor her mother's redemption.

She was the next entry in the log. And tomorrow, she would write it herself.
