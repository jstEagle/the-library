## 21. Broadcast

The transmitter in San Remedios had not spoken in nineteen years.

It sat in the back room of the church that wasn't underwater—the new church, the one built on the hill when the river took the old one—under a canvas tarp gone stiff with salt and mold. Abuela Rosa's grandson, a boy of maybe fourteen named Elio who fixed outboard motors for pocket money, spent two days on it while the village watched with the wary patience of people who had learned not to hope too loudly. He cleaned the tube sockets with vinegar and a toothbrush. He rewound a frayed power lead. On the third morning he flipped the switch and the set coughed, crackled, and settled into a low hum like a sleeping animal.

"Still has voice in it," Elio said, satisfied.

Zephyrine stood in the doorway with her mother's field journal open to the last written page and did not trust herself to answer.

* * *

They had argued about it first, of course. She and Imelda, in the shade of Rosa's porch, three days after the tide took Vance and nearly took everything else.

"You can't just say it," Imelda had said. Her arm was in a sling, her ribs strapped, her face still carrying the yellow-green shadow of the air pocket she'd guarded for eleven days. "Not the way you're planning to. There are procedures. Foundations. A press conference with—"

"With what? With your foundation's lawyers deciding which parts survive contact with the record?" Zephyrine had kept her voice level, mostly. "You taught me this, Mamá. Every site report you ever wrote—you said the record belongs to whoever can hear it first. You said radio beats paperwork every time."

"I said that about survey coordinates."

"I'm saying it about the truth."

Imelda had looked at her for a long moment—that measuring look Zephyrine had spent seven years trying to escape, the look that weighed and catalogued and found most things wanting. But something in it had shifted since the cavern, since the moment they'd surfaced into Cass's floodlight gasping and alive and changed. The look now was less appraisal than recognition.

"You'll name me," Imelda said finally. Not a question.

"The codex came out of their church. You signed it into the family collection under a false provenance. I'll name you, and I'll name me, because I benefited from it for twenty-six years without ever asking where it came from." Zephyrine folded her arms. "That's the whole point. If I leave us out, it's just another story someone else gets to edit."

Her mother had been quiet for a long time. Down the hill, the river ran brown and swollen with the last of the surge tide, carrying silt from the collapsed gallery, carrying—Rosa insisted—pieces of the old world back to the banks where they belonged.

"When I was your age," Imelda said at last, "I would have called this naive."

"And now?"

"Now I think I spent forty years calling things by the wrong names." She'd looked out at the water. "Use my frequency. It's the only thing I ever built that was actually clean."

* * *

The frequency was 4186 kilohertz. Zephyrine had known it before she'd known her own birthday—had fallen asleep as a child to the sound of her mother's evening check-ins drifting up the stairs of whatever field house they were living in that season. *Sandoval expedition, position confirmed, all hands accounted for.* It had sounded like safety. It had sounded like the shape of a life she'd eventually decided she wanted nothing to do with.

Now she sat in front of the resurrected transmitter with a handwritten script on the table beside her, and her hands were steadier than she expected them to be.

Cass leaned in the doorway, arms crossed, freshly shaved for the first time since Puerto Cobre. Tomás sat on an upturned crate near the window, hat in his lap, looking older than he had on the deck of the Aurelia. Rosa occupied the one chair, spine straight, as if attending a mass. Elio hovered over the tubes with a screwdriver, ready to thump the set if it faltered.

Outside, half the village had gathered in the churchyard. Nobody had told them to come. Word simply moved through San Remedios the way water moves through limestone—slowly, invisibly, arriving everywhere at once.

"You don't have to read it word for word," Tomás said quietly. "In case you lose the thread."

"I won't lose the thread." She looked down at the pages. Forty-one names. She'd gotten them from Rosa and the elders over two nights of interviews, cross-checked against the parish ledger Tomás had helped fish out of the flooded nave, against the deed records Cass had photographed in the provincial archive in Puerto Cobre six years ago when he still believed documents could save him. Forty-one families displaced in 1961 when the dam went in and the river swallowed the lower town. Forty-one names that appeared nowhere in the Sandoval Codex acquisition file except as a single line item: *local labor, miscellaneous.*

"It's 0700," Elio said. "Best propagation. My tío used to say the morning band carries farthest before the heat comes up."

Zephyrine keyed the microphone. The hum deepened. Somewhere in the hills, in the river towns, in the port offices and salvage yards and government ministries within range of a hundred-watt signal riding the morning air, receivers were waking up or already awake.

She pressed transmit.

"This is 4186 kilohertz. This is the frequency of the Sandoval expedition, and for the past month it has carried silence and false signals both. My name is Zephyrine Sandoval. I am the daughter of Dr. Imelda Sandoval, and I am speaking today to return something that was taken."

Her voice came out clear. Steady. She'd rehearsed it against the river, but the river hadn't needed the rehearsal; it had heard worse confessions and carried them all the same.

"On the fourth of March, 1961, the community of San Remedios was displaced by the Cobre Gorge hydroelectric project, majority-owned at the time by Corvane Holdings, predecessor company of Vance Salvage and Exploration. Forty-one families lost their homes, their fields, and their church, which remains submerged beneath the reservoir to this day. In 1989, Dr. Imelda Sandoval recovered a manuscript from that church—a colonial-era codex containing parish records, land titles, and the testimony of the town's founding families. She did not disclose its origin. She entered it into the Sandoval family collection under an attribution to a site four hundred kilometers south. That attribution was false. The codex built our reputation on a theft we did not commit but absolutely profited from."

She paused. In the doorway, Cass gave her a small nod: *keep going.*

"Three weeks ago, Aurelio Vance located a colonial galleon in the Cobre Gorge and moved a salvage fleet in to strip it. Among the cargo he sought was that same codex, which my mother had returned to the gorge in an attempt to keep it from him. Vance coerced expedition personnel, falsified salvage permits, and employed armed patrols against civilians. His dredge operations caused the collapse of the lower gallery of the wreck site on tide six. Mr. Vance was lost in that collapse. One of his divers was not, and lives today because my mother chose to go back for him." She let that sit for a second. "I want that on the record too. Mercy isn't a footnote."

* * *

She read the forty-one names aloud.

It took eleven minutes. She did not rush them. Each name got its full weight—*Alvarez, Benavides, Cabral, Delgado*—and as she spoke them, Zephyrine understood something she hadn't when she'd drafted the script at Rosa's kitchen table at two in the morning. The names weren't evidence. Evidence could be filed, misfiled, buried in an inquiry that met twice and dissolved. The names were a restoration. For nineteen years the river had held this town's dead and drowned records, and no one had said any of it out loud, and there is a kind of drowning that happens on dry land too, the kind that comes from being unnamed.

When she finished the list, several voices outside the window had joined the last few names softly, the way congregations finish prayers they know by heart.

Then she named her own.

"The codex was displayed in the Sandoval collection for thirty years. I grew up beneath it. I studied from endowments it funded. I never asked where it came from, because asking might have cost me the version of my family I preferred. That is also part of the theft, and I am accountable for it. As of yesterday, the codex has been formally returned to the community of San Remedios, witnessed by the provincial registrar, and Dr. Imelda Sandoval has signed over all residual claim to the Cobre Gorge site and its contents. The wreck of the galleon—and whatever else the river chooses to give back—belongs to the descendants of the forty-one."

She set down the script. She didn't need it anymore.

"To Aurelio Vance's partners, creditors, and former employees: every permit, contract, and falsified survey associated with the Cobre operation has been copied and transmitted to the provincial antiquities office, the harbor authority, and three newspapers that still employ actual reporters. To the inquiry that will inevitably follow: come to San Remedios. Hold your hearings here, in the church on the hill, where the people affected can attend without buying a ticket. And to anyone listening on this frequency who has ever been told their history belongs to someone else—" her throat tightened, and she let it, and said it anyway— "it doesn't. Say it out loud. The band is open."

She keyed off.

For a moment the room held only the hum of the transmitter and the sound of the river below. Then Elio reached over and, with the solemnity of a surgeon closing, switched the set to receive.

Static. Then, faintly, from somewhere upriver—a fishing cooperative's morning traffic report. Then another voice, farther, garbled, then clearer: a schoolteacher in a town Zephyrine had never heard of, reading the same broadcast back from notes she'd apparently taken live, her voice shaking slightly on the forty-one names.

"She's repeating it," Elio whispered, delighted. "She's rebroadcasting. It'll hop the whole valley by noon."

Tomás put his hat on, which was how Zephyrine had learned he hid emotion, and said gruffly, "Your mother used to do that. Read other stations' traffic and pass it along. Said a frequency was a road—you keep it open for whoever needs it." He cleared his throat. "She'll be insufferable about this. About you. Fair warning."

* * *

The consequences arrived faster than any of them predicted, which was the nature of radio: it outran lawyers.

By afternoon, a launch from the harbor authority anchored off San Remedios with a registrar aboard who interviewed Rosa for four hours and left with photographs of the parish ledger and a formal incident report. By evening, the provincial radio news led with the story—not the galleon, not the gold, but the codex and the forty-one names, read out over the air by anchors who had clearly been handed Zephyrine's transcript and didn't quite know what to do with the part where she'd indicted herself.

Cass's rehabilitation came third, and fastest, because bureaucracy moves quickest when it can hide behind someone else's scandal. The harbor authority's acting director—an appointment made two days earlier, after the previous director resigned citing health, a word everyone understood to mean *Vance*—arrived personally with a document folder and an expression of elaborate neutrality.

"Pilot license, reinstated, retroactive clearance of the suspension," he said on Rosa's porch, holding it out. "The prior action was found to be, ah, commercially motivated rather than safety-based. We regret the error."

Cass took the folder, looked at it for a long moment, and did not thank him. "My boat needs a new hull seam," he said instead. "And the Mariposa's registration lists a home port of Puerto Cobre. Change it to San Remedios. In writing. Then we're square."

The director blinked, wrote it down, and left.

Zephyrine found Cass at the dock afterward, coiling line that didn't need coiling. "You could have made him grovel more."

"Nah." He tested a knot. "Groveling's cheap. Home port's forever." He glanced up at her, and there was something in his face she'd learned to read over six tides and one impossible night passage—the look he wore when a current shifted under him and he'd already adjusted before admitting it. "You know what you did this morning, right? You didn't just tell the truth. You made the frequency yours. Everyone downstream of here is going to think of 4186 as *your* channel now. Your mother's going to notice. She notices everything."

"I'm counting on it."

Down the dock, Imelda stood at the rail of the Mariposa, sling and all, supervising Elio's repair work with the tactless intensity of a woman who could not physically watch someone else work without commentary. She caught Zephyrine looking and raised one eyebrow—the old challenge, the old measurement—but then she smiled, brief and crooked, an expression Zephyrine realized she'd never once seen on her mother's face in any photograph from any dig.

The transmitter hummed behind them in the church on the hill, receiving. All evening, station after station passed the broadcast along, name after name after name, until the whole valley seemed to be saying San Remedios out loud, over and over, like a tide coming in.

At dusk, a new signal broke through the relay chatter. Weak, coded in the old expedition shorthand Zephyrine knew from childhood—a message from a receiver far upriver, past the gorge, past the maps.

She copied it down twice to be sure. Then she walked slowly to the churchyard, where her mother stood watching the water, and held out the flimsy paper without a word.

Imelda read it. Read it again. And went very still.

"That's not possible," she said. "That call sign belonged to someone who died in '61."
