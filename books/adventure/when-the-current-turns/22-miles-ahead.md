## 22. Miles Ahead

The Mariposa sat on the slipway at San Remedios for eleven days, and for eleven days Cass Ibarra treated her repair like a surgery he was performing on a relative.

He'd stripped her down to the ribs, replaced the cracked seam plank with hardwood cut from a tree that had drowned when the dam went in—wood that had spent forty years underwater and come out denser than anything milled above the surface. "She's riverwood now," Cass said, running his palm along the new strake. "Been down there long enough to learn patience. She'll hold."

Zephyrine held the other end of the plank and said nothing, because she'd learned over six weeks that Cass talked to the boat the way other men talked to God, and interrupting was a form of blasphemy.

The village worked around them. That was the thing about San Remedios that she still hadn't gotten used to—work happened in the open, in the street, in the shallows, everyone's hands visible, everyone's business shared. A boy named Elio who had appointed himself Cass's apprentice hauled caulking irons and mallets and asked questions that Cass answered with more patience than he'd ever shown a client. Old Marta brought them coffee at hours that had no relation to meals. The codex sat in the church—not the flooded one, but the small dry chapel up the hill, under a cloth, under guard that nobody had organized and everybody provided—until the ministry's archivists came to receive it formally, and even then the villagers stood in the doorway with their arms folded, watching, making sure.

Her mother had left on the fourth day.

That was the thing Zephyrine kept turning over, the stone she couldn't stop picking up. Imelda Sandoval had surfaced from an air pocket after five days underwater with a broken wrist and a lung half-full of silt, had let her daughter haul her into a skiff, had let Tomás splint the wrist with a boat hook handle and a strip of sailcloth, and then—once she could hold a pen—had signed over her claim to the wreck, the codex, and the family name on the salvage registry, in front of witnesses, without a single word of negotiation.

She'd stayed another week. They'd argued—of course they'd argued, they were Sandovals, arguing was their native language—but it was a different kind of arguing than before. Not the old kind, where every sentence was a move in a chess game seven years deep. The new kind was shorter. Fiercer. Over faster. And afterward, once, they'd sat on the seawall in the evening and said nothing at all, and the silence hadn't hurt. That was the miracle Zephyrine kept examining for cracks and not finding.

Then Imelda had boarded the mail steamer south, to face the inquiry, to testify, to answer for the theft she'd committed at twenty-six and the restitution she'd finally made at sixty-one. She'd gripped Zephyrine's shoulder at the gangway—hard, with her good hand, hard enough to leave a bruise that Zephyrine didn't wash for two days—and said, "You read water better than I ever did."

That was it. That was the whole inheritance, handed over in nine words. Zephyrine had stood on the dock until the steamer was a smudge, and then she'd walked back to the slipway and picked up a caulking iron, and neither she nor Cass had said anything about it, because there was nothing to say that the river hadn't already said.

* * *

The contract came on the ninth day, and it came to her.

Not to Sandoval. Not to the foundation. Not through her mother's old contacts or her mother's name or the legend of the codex that had, for one feverish month, been in every paper from the coast to the capital. It came through Cass—a river man knew a river man, and the surveyor who'd mapped the gorge narrows from a leaking boat at night, in the dark, against the tide, was a name that traveled the way names traveled on the water: quietly, and fast.

The Delta Irrigation Cooperative wanted a channel survey. Forty kilometers of distributaries, silted and shifting, that they needed mapped before the wet season. Small money, honest work, the kind of contract that would have been beneath the Sandoval name and was exactly right for hers. Zephyrine Sandoval, surveyor. No title. No institute. No mother.

She signed it at Marta's kitchen table with a pen borrowed from Elio, and when she wrote her name she wrote it slowly, like she was carving it into something, and Marta watched her do it and nodded once, the way you nod at a boat leaving harbor.

* * *

They went downriver on a morning tide.

Not the dawn tide—Cass had been specific about this, and Zephyrine had learned enough to stop arguing. "Morning tide," he'd said, "not first light. First light the water's still deciding what it wants to be. You go when it's decided." So they'd waited, drinking Marta's coffee on the seawall while the village woke around them, until the current settled into its long green pull toward the sea, and then they'd loaded the Mariposa: survey instruments, sounding lines, the new echograph Zephyrine had bought with the contract's advance, crates of food, two bedrolls, and a small tin box that Zephyrine carried aboard herself and stowed under her bunk where nobody would see it.

In the tin box: the torn journal page she'd found in her mother's field kit, the one with the half-finished sketch of a church nave and a note in Imelda's hand that said *ask them, don't take*. And beside it, a photograph Zephyrine had found in the base camp ruins, of her mother at thirty, standing on the deck of a research vessel, laughing at something outside the frame—before the codex, before the legend, before the weight. A woman Zephyrine had never met. She was bringing her along anyway. Somebody had to keep an eye on her.

Cass cast off the lines himself, waved off Elio's offer of help with a jerk of his chin that Elio correctly interpreted as *you've done enough, kid, and I'm proud of you, and if I say either of those things out loud I'll have to jump in the river.* The village turned out on the seawall anyway—Marta, the elders, a scatter of children, Tomás, who'd stayed to shepherd the restitution through the ministry's paperwork and had the look of a man who had finally set down something very heavy and was still surprised by the lightness.

No one waved. That was the San Remedios way, Zephyrine had learned. They watched you go, and the watching was the blessing.

The Mariposa took the current, and San Remedios slid astern, and Zephyrine stood at the bow with the wind pushing her hair back and felt—nothing she'd expected. She'd braced for grief, for the ache of leaving, for the old familiar itch in her feet that had kept her moving for seven years. What she felt instead was something closer to balance. The village behind her wasn't a place she was fleeing. It was a place she'd helped carry something home to. That changed the arithmetic of leaving entirely.

"Course?" Cass called from the helm.

"Downriver," she said. "Then east fork at the delta mouth. First sounding station's at the old ferry crossing."

He grinned—that rare, whole-face grin she'd seen maybe four times in six weeks—and gave the wheel a quarter turn, and the Mariposa leaned into the current like a dog leaning into a collar it had finally stopped fighting.

* * *

They fell into the rhythm of the river the way they'd fallen into everything else—without discussing it, without deciding, just finding themselves already there.

Cass ran the boat. Zephyrine ran the water. She'd stand at the bow with the sounding pole or the echograph's readout and call the channel: *deep water left, sandbar building off the point, current's eating the east bank, we'll want the far side at the bend.* And Cass would take her readings and do the thing he did—turn them into the boat, make the Mariposa move through the river like she was reading the same page. They'd worked out this language in the gorge under fire, and now, with no one chasing them, it had room to breathe, and Zephyrine discovered that the language had more words than she'd known.

By the third day she'd stopped keeping the log the way she'd always kept logs—mile by mile, distance by distance, the ledger she'd maintained since she was nineteen, as if she could measure her way out of her mother's shadow by outrunning it. She noticed its absence the way you notice a headache leaving. The log now started each day with the date, the weather, the station number, and then—new, unbidden, in her own handwriting—something else. *Morning: heron working the reeds at the ferry crossing. Morning: fog so thick the echograph was useless and Cass navigated by the sound of the current against the cutbank, and he was right. Morning: coffee burned because I was watching the light come up instead of the pot.*

Mornings. She was counting mornings now. She wasn't sure when it had happened. Somewhere between the slipway and the delta mouth, the unit of measurement had changed, and she suspected—strongly—that it was permanent.

* * *

The old ferry crossing was exactly where the cooperative's file said it would be, and nothing like what the file described. The file said *degraded channel, silt accumulation, recommend dredging.* The river said: a drowned ferry landing with its pilings still standing, a cut that had migrated two hundred meters east in the years since anyone surveyed, and—when Zephyrine dove the station with a hand line and a slate—a submerged settlement foundation, square-cut stones, colonial work, sitting in four meters of water exactly where the cooperative wanted to dig.

She came up the ladder with the slate dripping and Cass read her face before she said anything.

"No," he said.

"I don't know yet."

"You've got the look. The one your mother had, probably."

"I'm not my mother."

"No," Cass agreed, taking the slate from her hand and studying the sketch of the foundation. "Your mother would've flagged it, filed it, and dug it in secret. You're going to flag it, file it, and make the cooperative change their route." He looked up. "Aren't you."

Zephyrine sat down on the gunwale, dripping, and looked at the water that had swallowed someone's home the way the dam had swallowed San Remedios, and thought about the word her mother had sent down a dead radio band: *return.* One word, seven years of silence, and it had meant all of it—return the codex, return the truth, return to the places you took things from. And maybe it meant one more thing that Zephyrine was only now growing into: return, over and over, to the work itself, to the water, to the question of what belongs to whom.

"Yeah," she said. "I am."

She wrote it up that evening at the chart table while Cass cooked—badly, with too much pepper, a fact she'd stopped mentioning because his face when he served it was so hopeful. The amendment to the survey ran three pages. She attached her sounding data, her photographs of the stonework, a recommendation to reroute the channel north of the foundation, and a final paragraph she rewrote four times before she got it right:

*The undersigned recommends protection of the submerged structure at Station 3 pending archaeological assessment. The river country is full of what the water took. It is not ours to dredge.*

She signed it: *Zephyrine Sandoval, Surveyor.* Then she looked at it for a long moment, crossed out *Sandoval*, and left it standing alone. *Zephyrine.* First name only, the way river pilots and dredge captains and the people of San Remedios had come to say it. It looked small on the page. It looked like it could hold.

* * *

On the sixth morning, the fog came down thick again, and they anchored in a backwater to wait it out, and Zephyrine climbed to the roof of the cabin with the tin box and the photograph and let the river talk to itself around the hull.

The inquiry was starting in the capital that week. Vance was in custody pending charges that would take years and probably wouldn't stick to the man himself—such men rarely did—but the fleet was impounded, the dredges were idle, and the codex was under glass in the dry chapel with a ministry seal on it and a village elder sitting beside it every day, reading to it in a language the archivists couldn't translate, which Zephyrine suspected was exactly the right way to keep an artifact. Her name was in the testimony. Her mother's name. Her family's. She'd broadcast all of it on the old frequency, into static and whatever might have been listening, and the world had heard it secondhand within a week. Some doors didn't reopen after that. Some names didn't either.

She found she didn't miss hers.

The fog began to lift—not clearing, just rising, pulling up off the water in long ropes—and through it came the sound of the east fork running fast somewhere ahead, the channel they'd spend the next week mapping, the work that was hers, under her name, for a client who had hired a surveyor and gotten one.

Cass's head appeared through the cabin hatch. "Visibility's coming. We moving?"

"We're moving."

He started to duck back down, then stopped. "Log entry for today?" he asked. He'd started asking, a few days ago. She'd never figured out if it was teasing or something else.

Zephyrine looked at the water, at the fog ropes lifting, at the green country opening up ahead of them like a page.

"Morning," she said. "Fog lifting. River decided what it wants to be. So did I."

She wrote it down, closed the log, and went to take the bow.

The Mariposa took the current, and the east fork opened ahead of them, unmapped, and for the first time in her life Zephyrine Sandoval—Zephyrine, just Zephyrine—was not running toward a distance or away from one. She was simply going, in the company of the current, and the current, which had turned for everyone in the end, was turning for her now, and it was taking her exactly where she'd chosen to be.

The heron lifted off the reeds ahead. She counted that too.
