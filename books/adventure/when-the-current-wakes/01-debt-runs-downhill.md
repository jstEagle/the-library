## 1. Debt Runs Downhill

The thing about debt is that it never sleeps, never eats, never needs fuel. It just sits there in the dark of your ledger growing like mold in a wet hull, and you can hear it if you're quiet enough—this soft, patient sound, like water finding the seam in a boat that hasn't sprung a leak yet but will.

I was on my back under the *Prospect*'s engine housing with a wrench in one hand and my other arm elbow-deep in salt-corroded machinery when Denny Vane's shadow fell across the deck boards.

"Hazel Sandoval," he said, in the voice of a man greeting an old friend at a wedding. "You know what I love about you? You always look busy."

I didn't come out right away. That was a mistake and I knew it was a mistake, because Denny Vane interpreted silence as an invitation to get comfortable. His shoes appeared at the edge of my vision—good shoes, too good for a working dock, polished to the color of dark honey.

"Third time this month," he said. "A man could take that personally."

"A man could take up a hobby," I said, and kept turning the wrench. The coupling bolt was fused with corrosion. Everything on this boat was fused with something. "I'd suggest fishing, but you strike me as a man who likes his fish already dead and on a plate."

He laughed. That was the worst part about the Vane brothers—they laughed easily, they meant most of it, and none of it changed a single number in their books. Denny was the younger one, round-faced, cheerful the way weather is cheerful right before it turns. His brother Marcus did the actual arithmetic. Denny just delivered it, like a singing telegram.

"Your father's loan," he said, settling onto my supply crate like he owned it, which, given the circumstances, he arguably did. "Do you know what it's at now?"

"I know what it was at last Tuesday."

"It moves." He said it gently, almost tenderly, the way you'd tell someone a tide comes in. "That's the beautiful thing about compound interest, Hazel. It doesn't need your permission. It doesn't need you to show up. It works while you sleep. Honestly, it's the hardest-working member of your family."

I slid out from under the housing and sat up, wiping my hands on a rag that had stopped being blue sometime last year. The *Prospect* sat on blocks in the boatyard at Port Calloway, stripped to her bones, and I'll be honest—she looked bad. Storm chasers always do between seasons. We chase the worst weather on the coast for money that barely covers the chasing, and then we limp home and spend everything we don't have making the boat seaworthy again so we can go lose more money doing it. My father had run the same equation for thirty years and died owing the Vanes forty thousand. The interest had been eating the principal's corpse ever since.

"How much," I said.

Denny beamed and produced a folded paper from inside his jacket, unfolding it with the ceremony of a man revealing a winning ticket. "Sixty-one thousand, four hundred, and change. The change grows hourly. Isn't that something? Your father borrowed forty and you owe sixty-one. If you live long enough, you'll owe him a hundred. There's a kind of immortality in it."

"You came all the way down here to tell me a number I can't pay."

"I came down here because Marcus worries." He looked around the boatyard—the rusted crane, the tarpaulins snapping in the harbor wind, the pelican on the piling judging everyone equally—and his cheerfulness dimmed by exactly one degree, which for Denny was a funeral. "And because I like you, Hazel. Genuinely. You remind me of our early clients. Hungry ones. And I want to give you something before the arithmetic does what arithmetic does."

"Which is?"

"Options." He said the word like it tasted sweet. "Marcus has been looking at the *Prospect*. At you. At the whole picture. And the picture says you're not going to make sixty-one thousand chasing storms. No offense. But storms are a young person's game and a poor person's trap, and you're running out of both youth and margin. So either you find something bigger—" he spread his hands, "—or we find something of yours. The boat's worth maybe eight. Your father's charts and records, maybe two. There's the house upriver, though I understand that's complicated."

The house wasn't complicated. The house was gone, sold three years ago to cover a season of no storms and one season of storms that found us instead. But I let him think I still had things left to strip, because a creditor who thinks there's more to take is a creditor who waits.

"I'm listening," I said, which was a lie. I was calculating. There's a difference, and Denny Vane has never understood it, which is why he's cheerful.

* * *

The salvage broker worked out of a converted shipping container near the fish market, and his name was Osgood, and he had the eyes of a man who read people the way actuaries read death tables. I'd done two contracts through him before—recovery jobs, insurance work, nothing glamorous. He paid late and full, which in my experience is the best combination available in this economy.

He listened to my pitch without blinking, which I appreciated, and then he slid a folder across the desk instead of answering, which I didn't.

"Meridian Scientific Consortium," he said. "Research vessel and shore expedition, operating upriver in the monsoon country. Eleven days silent."

"So they're dead or their radio is."

"Or both. Or neither. Meridian carries insurance through a syndicate that wants answers before it wants to pay out. They want confirmation of status, recovery of scientific materials if the party is lost, and—this is the part they emphasized—discretion. No press. No port gossip. You find them, you assess, you report back through me. Salvage rights on anything recoverable are yours, minus my cut."

I opened the folder. Coordinates, a river chart with the upper reaches marked in that particular cartographer's hatching that means *nobody who drew this has actually been here*. A photograph of the expedition leader—a woman, fifties, the kind of face that had decided things—captioned *Dr. Amara Osei*. A crew manifest. A last known position that sat deep in territory where rivers braid and un-braid themselves like something alive deciding what it wants to be.

"The monsoon's coming in early this year," I said. "Every pilot on this coast knows it. The upper river closes in—what, three weeks? Four?"

"Three," Osgood said. "Maybe less. Which is why the fee is what it is."

I looked at the fee. Then I looked at it again, because the first time I assumed I'd misread a digit.

"That's half my debt."

"That's half your debt," Osgood agreed. "And there's a completion bonus that covers most of the rest, contingent on recovering specific materials. Documents. Field records. The insurance syndicate is very interested in Dr. Osei's paperwork."

Everything in me that had survived thirty years of this life raised its hand and said *no*. Upriver work in monsoon country is where boats go to die and crews go to disappear. The river up there isn't water, it's weather with a current. Radios fail. Charts lie. And eleven days of silence from a well-funded expedition means whatever happened to them was either fast enough to leave no distress call or slow enough that nobody wanted to make one.

But sixty-one thousand four hundred and change was sitting in Denny Vane's ledger growing hourly, and the *Prospect* needed a new coupling, new planking below the waterline, and roughly four hundred dollars of parts I didn't have, and stopping—just stopping, standing still, letting the account rest—even for a month, cost more than moving. That's the trap nobody warns you about with debt. Running is expensive. Standing still is fatal. The only cheap direction is forward, and forward is always toward something with teeth.

"What materials?" I asked.

Osgood shrugged with professional vagueness. "Field journals. Survey plates. Correspondence. The syndicate's adjusters were vague. 'Scientific documentation of record.' You know how insurers are."

I did know how insurers were, and how brokers were, and how men like Denny Vane were, and the whole apparatus of paper that sits on top of the world's real work and skims it. But knowing the game is rigged doesn't get you out of playing; it just means you stop being surprised when the dice come up snake eyes.

"I'll need an advance," I said. "Enough to make the boat right. Fuel, parts, a crewman."

"Ten percent, standard."

"Fifteen, and I keep full salvage rights on the vessel itself if she's abandoned. Any cargo of record goes to your syndicate."

Osgood considered me for a long moment, and I watched him revise his estimate of me upward, which is the only currency of respect I've ever cared about. "Twelve percent, and you have a deal."

"Fourteen, and you throw in the satellite phone rental."

"Thirteen, and the phone, and I never want to hear your name attached to a press inquiry."

We shook on it. His hand was dry and cool and closed around mine like a contract signing itself.

* * *

Denny found me at dusk, sitting on the *Prospect*'s bow with the contract papers spread out beside me and a beer I wasn't drinking, watching the harbor lights come on across water the color of old pewter.

"Well?" he said, dropping onto the dock beside my boots. "Marcus sent me to check whether you've decided to be sensible."

"I took a job."

His face lit up like a carnival. "Hazel! I knew it. What kind of job? Please tell me it's not storm chasing. Marcus says storm chasing is a rounding error with a hull."

"Salvage. Upriver."

Something flickered behind the cheerfulness—not quite a flinch, but the ghost of one. "Upriver," he repeated. "Monsoon country?"

"Is there another upriver?"

"No. No, of course not." He laughed, but it was a smaller laugh than usual, and I filed that away, because Denny Vane flinching at a geography was information. "Well. Good. Work is work. And when you're paid, you'll come see Marcus, yes? Settle some of the principal? He does so love a payment plan honored."

"Denny." I picked up the contract and held it out. "Who's the client?"

He glanced at the letterhead and his smile stayed exactly where it was while everything behind it went very still, the way water goes still over something big moving underneath. "Insurance syndicate," he read. "Anonymous holding company. Very common structure. Very legal."

"Uh-huh."

"Hazel." He stood, brushed off his trousers, and gave me the full wattage, the real smile, the one that had charmed my father into a loan thirty years of interest ago. "Whatever's up that river—find it, tag it, bring back the paper, and come home. Don't ask questions the fee doesn't cover. Questions are expensive. You of all people know that."

He walked away down the dock whistling, and I sat there until the beer went warm, looking at the letterhead, at the anonymous holding company, at the coordinates in the hatched-out country where the river braided and lied.

Eleven days of silence. Three weeks of river. Sixty-one thousand dollars of gravity pulling me upstream toward something that had already scared off better-funded people than me.

I should have said no. I want that on the record, somewhere, in case anyone ever asks: Hazel Sandoval, at dusk, in Port Calloway, knew.

But knowing costs nothing and stopping costs everything, so at first light I bought the coupling bolt, the planking, and four hundred liters of diesel, and started loading the boat that would carry me up into the teeth of the monsoon after a dead woman's paperwork.

Her name was Amara Osei. I didn't know yet that I'd carry that name a lot farther than any river goes.
