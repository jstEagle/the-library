## 2. The Contract and the Current

The salvage broker's office smelled like mildew and printer toner, which in my experience are the two smells most common to places where people sign things they shouldn't.

His name was Osgood—Mr. Osgood to me, though he called me "Ms. Sandoval" with the particular warmth of a man who has already read your file and knows exactly how much rope you can afford. He had a desk the size of a coffin lid and a window that looked out on the harbor, where my boat sat at anchor looking smaller than I remembered her being when I'd bought her with money I hadn't had either.

"Standard terms," he said, sliding a folder across the desk like it was nothing, like paper was weightless, like ink didn't have mass. "Find the Meridian expedition vessel or its survivors. Confirm status. Recover any company property you encounter along the way."

"Company property," I repeated. "Meaning what, exactly?"

"Whatever's marked." He smiled. He had good teeth, which is its own kind of warning. "You'd be surprised what floats down from the upper river. Crates. Documents. Occasionally people." He said *people* the way you'd say *driftwood*, and I decided not to ask him to elaborate, because elaboration costs time and time was accruing interest.

I flipped through the contract. I want to be honest here: I read maybe forty percent of it. There was a clause about liability waivers that ran three paragraphs and used the word *foreseeable* four times, which in contract language means *we foresaw it and we're not paying for it*. There was a section on salvage rights that gave me sixty percent of assessed value, minus expenses, minus a "logistics fee" that wasn't defined anywhere but appeared twice. There was a page about radio silence protocols that made no sense to me at all—why would a rescue contract require the rescuer to go quiet?—and when I asked, Osgood said it was standard for operations in contested waters.

"Contested by whom?"

"The river," he said pleasantly, "is contested by everyone. That's rather the point of it."

Here's the thing about debt. It doesn't make you stupid. It makes you fast. You start reading contracts the way you read weather—you're not looking for the whole picture, you're looking for the part that kills you, and if you don't see it in the first pass you tell yourself it probably isn't there. Denny Vane had taught me this without meaning to. Denny Vane taught everyone things without meaning to; it was his gift, along with an unshakeable cheerfulness and a ledger book he kept in a leather case that matched his shoes.

Three years ago my father had needed a boat engine and then a second boat engine and then, after the stroke, a hospital that took payments in advance. The Vane brothers had been delighted to help. That was the thing about them—they were genuinely delightful. Denny would sit across from you at his kitchen table, pour you coffee you didn't want, laugh at your jokes before you finished them, and write numbers in his book while maintaining eye contact. His brother Marcus handled collections, and Marcus never raised his voice, and somehow that was worse.

The interest compounded monthly. My father had died owing them more than he'd borrowed, which is arithmetic as a form of inheritance, and now the debt was mine, and every month it grew like something alive in the bilge.

So I signed. I signed the liability waiver and the salvage split and the radio protocol and a page near the end that I'm fairly sure assigned Osgood's firm power of attorney over anything I found, including, possibly, my soul—the print was small and my eyes were on the signature line because the signature line meant fuel drums and fuel drums meant upriver and upriver meant money, and money meant months off the Vane brothers' books, and months off their books meant I could sleep without doing the math against the ceiling.

Osgood countersigned and shook my hand with both of his, which felt like being caught in a net made of warm skin.

"Pleasure, Ms. Sandoval. Fuel drums are at Pier Six. Your crewman will meet you there."

"I don't need a crewman."

"You have one," he said, still smiling. "It's in the contract. Page nine."

Page nine. Of course it was. Somewhere between *foreseeable* and *logistics fee* I had acquired a human being, and I hadn't even noticed, which told me something about how carefully I was reading and how much I wanted to believe that not noticing was the same as it not mattering.

* * *

Pier Six smelled like diesel and fish guts, the two great perfumes of my entire adult life. The fuel drums were stacked in a pyramid that some dockhand had clearly built for his own amusement—six drums high, no straps, one bad shove from becoming a rolling catastrophe. I checked each drum's seal myself because I'd once bought fuel upriver that turned out to be half water, and I'd spent two days drifting and learning humility.

My boat sat low in the water beside the pier. She was a converted storm chaser, thirty-one feet of stubborn aluminum that I'd named the *Undertow* back when naming things after your own bad decisions seemed funny. She had a reinforced hull, a shallow draft for running flooded country, a radar dome that worked about seventy percent of the time, and a bilge pump that worked about sixty. Together they averaged out to optimism.

"You Sandoval?"

He came around the drum pyramid carrying two duffel bags and a hard case, and he moved like a man who'd learned to walk on decks that were trying to kill him—knees soft, weight centered, ready for the world to lurch. He was maybe fifty, gray going on white, with a face like a topographic map of somewhere unpleasant and hands that looked like they'd been repaired rather than maintained.

"Tuck," he said, before I asked. "They'll have told you nothing about me, so let's save time. Twenty-two years running boats on this river system. Four of those for companies I won't name and you've already heard of. I quit the last one because they asked me to do something I'd have had to stop liking myself to do." He set the duffels down. "I cook okay, I fix engines better than I cook, and I don't talk much. Which of those three is selling you?"

"The third one, honestly."

"Good. Most people pick the cooking." He looked past me at the *Undertow* and did the thing all old boatmen do—ran his eyes along her waterline, her rub rail, her radar dome, taking inventory of her sins. "She leak?"

"She weeps."

"Weeping's fine. Weeping you can manage." He picked his bags back up. "One thing before we cast off, and then I'll shut up like promised."

"Go ahead."

He nodded upriver, toward the north, where the valley narrowed into the monsoon country and the maps got vague and the rivers braided into a hundred arguments with themselves. "Upper river eats radios first."

"What does that mean?"

"It means what it says. You get above the confluence and the atmosphere turns thick as soup. Static eats your signal, storms eat your static, and sometimes—and I've seen this, so I know how it sounds—sometimes you call a station that answered fine yesterday and today there's just nothing, and later you find out the station's fine, the antenna's fine, everything's fine except your voice never arrived anywhere." He shrugged. "Old-timers say the river keeps what it catches. Scientists say ionospheric interference. I've stopped caring which one's true. Point is, whatever happens up there, nobody's coming to check on you, because nobody will hear you say you need checking on."

"That's cheerful."

"I'm not paid to be cheerful. Cheerful costs extra." He stepped aboard, and the *Undertow* took his weight without complaint, which was more than I could say for most people. "Cast us off whenever you're ready, Captain. Weather window's closing."

I looked north. The sky over the valley had gone the color of a bruise deciding whether to bloom, and the wind had that particular wet heaviness that monsoon sailors learn to feel in their fillings. The season was early yet—the real deluge wouldn't land for another week or two—but the leading edge was already reaching down the valley like fingers testing a lock.

Somewhere up there, eleven days silent, was a scientific expedition full of people who had also signed papers they probably hadn't read.

I cast off.

* * *

We made good time for six hours, which should have been my first clue that the river was setting me up.

The lower reaches were wide and brown and lazy, the banks stitched with fishing villages and the occasional rusting hulk of somebody's abandoned ambition. Tuck proved his worth inside the first hour by finding a rattle in the engine that I'd been ignoring for a month and fixing it with a wrench, a zip tie, and what appeared to be pure contempt. He cooked rice with dried fish and chili that was better than it had any right to be, and he talked exactly as much as advertised, which is to say he pointed at things worth pointing at and otherwise let the river do the narrating.

"Current's faster than the charts say," he remarked around midafternoon, watching the bank slide by. "Rain upcountry already. Season's starting early."

"Early's good. Early means fewer pleasure boats."

"Early means the river's awake before you are." He said it mildly, the way you'd mention the tide, but it stuck in me anyway. There's a particular kind of unease that comes from hearing someone state a fact you already felt but hadn't admitted, and I'd been feeling watched since the pier—not by anyone, just by the water itself, the whole brown length of it flowing south past me while I pushed north against its grain, like walking the wrong way through a crowd that knows something you don't.

At four o'clock the sky finally made up its mind.

I'd chased storms for eleven years. I knew their grammar—the way a squall line announces itself in the light before the wind, the greenish tint that means hail, the sudden dead calm that isn't calm at all but an inhalation. This one came off the western hills like a freight train with the brakes cut, a wall of rain moving sideways, and I got the *Undertow* turned into it with about ninety seconds to spare.

Then the world went white and loud.

Monsoon rain doesn't fall so much as arrive. It hit the deck like thrown gravel, filled the air to a consistency closer to soup than weather, and reduced my visibility to roughly the length of my own arms. Tuck appeared beside me in the cockpit without my hearing him come, handed me a strap for the wheel, and shouted observations directly into my ear, which is how boat people flirt, I think, or at least how they communicate.

"Bank's off our port! Feel it?"

I felt it—a change in the chop, the water going confused where current met shallows. I eased us away from the sound, trusting my ears over my eyes, riding the bow wave of a storm that wanted very much to introduce me to a mudbank.

Lightning walked down the western sky in long vertical strokes, close enough that I could smell the ozone mixing with the rain. Thunder arrived not as sound but as pressure, thumping in my chest. And through all of it, absurdly, the radio crackled to life behind us in the cabin—the automated weather buoy off Cape Renner, broadcasting its loop, its little recorded voice reciting wind speeds and barometric readings to an audience of nobody.

Tuck glanced back at it, then at me, and I saw him thinking the same thing I was: *enjoy it while it works.*

The squall passed in forty minutes, because squalls do, because the monsoon sends scouts ahead of the army. When it cleared, the air was scrubbed and steaming, the sun came through in broken gold columns, and the river lay flat and gleaming and innocent as a liar after church. A rainbow stood over the northern hills, arcing down toward the upper valley, toward the place we were going, like a road marker painted by something with a sense of humor.

Tuck wrung out his shirt and studied the north for a while.

"Charts show a checkpoint at Kilometer One-Forty," I said. "Magistrate post. We should raise them tonight, confirm transit permission."

"Sure," he said. "If they answer."

"They'll answer."

He didn't argue. He just gave me a look I'd seen on old pilots and old doctors and other people whose jobs consist of knowing how things end—patient, faintly sorry, entirely unsurprised—and went below to check the engine.

I stood my watch alone as the light died. Behind us, the weather buoy kept chirping its loop, faithful and far away. Ahead, the valley narrowed by degrees, the banks drawing closer, the current quickening, the first lightning of the evening flickering deep in the hills like a signal lamp sending a message in a code I couldn't read yet.

The river knew something. I couldn't have told you what. But I've spent my life around large moving bodies of water, and I'll tell you what they never do: they never lie about what's coming. They just don't explain it in words.

That night, anchored in the lee of a bluff, I tried raising the Kilometer One-Forty checkpoint on schedule. I got static. I tried again. More static, threaded through with something that might have been a voice or might have been the atmosphere chewing on itself.

Tuck stood in the cabin doorway, drying his hands on a rag.

"Eats radios first," he said.

"Yeah," I said. "So you mentioned."

I keyed the mic one more time, and this time—just for a second, just long enough to doubt my own ears—the static shaped itself into something almost like a word before it collapsed back into noise.

Neither of us slept well after that, and in the morning we went north anyway, because stopping cost more.
