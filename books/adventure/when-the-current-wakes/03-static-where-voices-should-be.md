## 3. Static Where Voices Should Be

The river stopped pretending to be a river somewhere past the second bend and started acting like a rumor—wide, brown, indecisive about which direction it actually wanted to go. I stood at the wheel of the *Sparrowhawk* with one hand on the throttle and the other on the radio's volume knob, because in my experience those are the two controls that decide whether you live to spend your money or die having saved some.

Tuck was up on the bow with a cup of coffee he'd been nursing since dawn, mostly as ballast for his opinions.

"Third time they've come through garbled," he said, not turning around.

"I heard."

"You want to hear what I think?"

"Not especially."

"They're being jammed." He said it the way other men say *nice weather*, like it was nothing you could do anything about anyway. "Or their set's dying. Or both. Radios go first up here. River eats them. I told you that at the dock."

"You told me a lot of things at the dock. Most of them were about your ex-wife."

"She's also upstream," Tuck said. "In a manner of speaking."

I let him have that one. It was cheaper than arguing, and arguing with Tuck was like arm-wrestling a barge—you could win, technically, but nobody would be impressed by the effort.

The Meridian expedition had gone silent eleven days ago, according to the salvage broker whose contract was currently folded in my breast pocket, getting soft with sweat. Eleven days of nothing from a scientific outfit with three boats, forty-odd personnel, and a satellite uplink good enough to file papers from the middle of nowhere. The broker had used words like *anomalous* and *insurance event* and *generous completion bonus*, which in my experience are the three phrases most likely to precede a body count. But the contract paid enough to make Denny Vane stop smiling at me for a fiscal quarter, maybe two, and there is no price I wouldn't pay for that particular silence.

So here we were, chasing static upriver into monsoon country, because stopping costs more than going. That's not courage. That's arithmetic wearing a life vest.

* * *

The coordinates had come through in fragments over the last day and a half, clipped and compressed, like someone reading them while running. Latitude, longitude, a bearing correction, then the carrier would wobble and drop and we'd get thirty seconds of that wet-hiss static that means either distance or damage. The last clean transmission—the last almost-clean one—had been at 0400 this morning.

"Meridian base, this is salvage vessel *Sparrowhawk*, inbound on your last position, ETA fourteen hours, how copy?" I'd said it four times into the gray dark before dawn, watching the needle twitch.

And then, instead of coordinates, a voice. A woman's voice, thin through the compression, stripped down to wire and breath. Not panicked. Worse than panicked—*deliberate*. Someone choosing her words the way you choose footing on rotten ice.

She said: "If anyone receives this. Do not—" and the signal folded, and when it unfolded she was saying something else, something that didn't fit any protocol I knew, any distress script, any mayday template. She said:

"Inheritance."

One word. Then static, long and flat and total, like a door closing on a room where the lights had just gone out.

Tuck had come aft and stood next to me while I tried twice more to raise them. Nothing. He hadn't said anything for a full minute, which for Tuck was practically a eulogy.

"Inheritance," he repeated finally. "That's an odd word for a mayday."

"It's not a mayday." I keyed off the mic. "Maydays have structure. Position, nature of emergency, souls aboard. That was a message. Somebody picked that word on purpose."

"Meaning what?"

"Meaning somebody wanted whoever heard it to know what this was about." I looked at the chart spread under the plexiglass, the river running north like a vein, the little penciled X where the Meridian's last confirmed camp sat in a valley the mapmaker had annotated with a single dismissive word: *remote*. "You don't say 'inheritance' if you're drowning. You say it if someone's taking something that isn't theirs."

Tuck drank his coffee. "Could be looters."

"Could be." Looters don't take out forty people and a satellite uplink. Looters don't leave a woman calm enough to choose her vocabulary. But I kept that in the drawer where I keep the thoughts that cost money to think out loud.

We ran all morning. The squall from our departure had blown itself out overnight, leaving behind that green, bruised light monsoon country does between fits, everything dripping, the air thick enough to chew. The banks closed in—mangrove giving way to taller stuff, buttressed trees standing in the shallows like a crowd waiting for something. Birds went quiet in stretches, whole miles of them, and started again all at once, as if someone upstream had a switch.

Around noon we came around a bluff and saw the checkpoint.

* * *

You can tell an abandoned post from a destroyed one, and the difference matters. Destroyed means fight. Abandoned means the people who were supposed to stay decided, collectively and quickly, that elsewhere was better. This one was abandoned the way you abandon a house when the fire is still two streets over but coming.

It sat on a cut bank above a narrows—a timber platform, sandbag emplacement, a long shed with a tin roof, a patrol skiff tied up at the pilings riding so high on the water it was nearly out of its paint line. Nobody on the platform. Nobody in the shed. And on the flagpole above it all, where the magistrate's colors should have been flying—blue field, white fish, the emblem every official boat on this river answered to—there was nothing but the halyard slapping the pole in the wind, and below it, snagged in the mangroves at the waterline, the flag itself. Torn down. Not lowered. Ripped off its clips and thrown into the brush like something dead.

Tuck brought the binoculars up. "Skiff's got fuel cans on deck," he reported. "Two of them. Full, by the sit of them."

"So they left in a hurry but not empty-handed."

"Rifles racked inside the shed. I can see three, maybe four, through the window." He lowered the glasses. "Armed men don't leave rifles, Hazel. Armed men leave rifles when they're running from something worse than unarmed men."

I brought us in slow, against every instinct that keeps a person alive in my line of work, because I needed to see it close. The *Sparrowhawk* kissed the pilings and Tuck made fast while I walked the platform with my boots loud on purpose, announcing myself to whatever wasn't there.

Inside the shed it smelled of cold ash and insect spray. A card game sat mid-hand on a crate—someone had been winning, judging by the little hoard of cigarette butts used as stakes. A kettle on a cold burner. A logbook open on the desk, and this was the part that put the cold in my spine, because the logbook wasn't abandoned. It was *finished*. The last entry was dated nine days ago, written in a steady hand, and it read:

*Relieved of post per verbal order. No relief party arrived. Flag struck. Recommend downstream stations do same.*

No signature. Just a rank abbreviation scratched out, like the man writing it had decided halfway through that he no longer wished to be identifiable as having been here.

Below that, in different ink, fresher, someone else had added a single line:

*They bought the magistrate. Who's left to buy us?*

I stood there a while. Somewhere outside, Tuck was saying my name, and then saying it louder.

"Hazel. You want to see this."

He was at the edge of the platform, pointing down at the mud under the pilings. Footprints. A lot of them, pressed deep by men carrying weight, all heading the same direction—not toward the skiff, not toward the river, but inland, up a game trail toward the high ground. And mixed among the boot prints, smaller ones. Bare feet. Children's feet, some of them, moving in the same direction, hurried, the toes dug in like the small owners had been pulled along faster than they liked.

Armed men fleeing inland, taking villagers with them. Or villagers fleeing inland, escorted by armed men who'd abandoned their own post and flag rather than stay and be part of whatever came next.

"Whatever scared them off their own post," Tuck said quietly, "is upstream."

"Or downstream," I said. "Behind them. Chasing them up."

"That's worse."

"Yeah." I took the logbook. It wasn't mine, and taking it was probably theft from a government installation, but the government in question appeared to have resigned, and I've never met a rule that survived contact with a drowned jurisdiction. "Let's move. We're fourteen hours out from those coordinates and I want to be closer than that before dark."

* * *

We ran the narrows with the throttle open and the rain starting again, fat drops that hit the water like fingers testing a drum. I kept the radio on scan, volume up, letting the frequencies wash through the cabin—fishing bands, weather, the magistrate's channel, all of it ordinary, all of it wrong in the way a room is wrong after someone's taken the furniture. Too quiet. Too much space where voices should be.

At 1500 the Meridian frequency coughed back to life.

Not the woman. A man this time, or the same woman pitched low, hard to tell through the interference. Clipped coordinates again—the same camp position, confirmed—and then, tacked onto the end like a smuggled note, three more words before the carrier collapsed:

*"—plates. Bell tower."*

Then static. Real static this time, the deep kind, and underneath it, faint as a heartbeat, something rhythmic that might have been engines. More than one. Big ones.

Tuck appeared in the doorway, streaming rain. "Tell me you got that."

"Bell tower." I marked the coordinates, then marked the phrase beside them, because some messages you write down even when you don't know why yet. "There's a mission upriver. Old one. Pre-dates everybody. If there's a bell tower, it'll be the tallest thing in the valley."

"Tallest thing in a valley is where you go to be seen." He wiped his face. "Or to hide something."

"Or both." I looked at the chart, at the penciled X, at the distance we still had to cover with a leaking fuel line, a crewman who quoted his ex-wife like scripture, and a radio that was starting to sound like it was drowning ahead of schedule. Somewhere ahead, a woman had chosen the word *inheritance* out of every word in the language, and armed men had torn down their own flag rather than stand under it, and children's footprints ran for the hills between the boots of men with rifles.

Eleven days of silence, and now three fragments: *inheritance. Plates. Bell tower.*

Whatever the Meridian had found in this valley, they hadn't lost it. They'd hidden it. And somebody upstream was willing to drown forty scientists to keep it buried.

I opened the throttle. The *Sparrowhawk* climbed onto plane and ran north into the rain, and the river ran with us, wide and brown and patient, like it already knew how this ended and was just curious whether we'd figured it out yet.

Behind us, faint through the static, the engines on the dead frequency kept counting.
