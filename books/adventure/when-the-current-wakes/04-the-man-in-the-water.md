## 4. The Man in the Water

The river gave him up on the third morning, the way rivers give up everything eventually—reluctantly, and in pieces.

Tuck spotted him first. He was up on the bow with his binoculars doing his usual sweep of the banks, cataloguing every crocodile and floating log with the weary professionalism of a man who'd decided long ago that the world was mostly things trying to kill you, when he went rigid in that particular way that meant he'd found something that wasn't a crocodile or a log.

"Person," he said. "Dead ahead. Or what's left of one."

I throttled down and we drifted toward it, and I'll be honest about what I was thinking, because I've never seen the point of lying to myself: I was calculating. A body meant a report, a report meant officials, officials meant questions about why a salvage boat with no registration worth mentioning was poking around a stretch of river where armed men had abandoned their own checkpoint. A dead man cost money. That's the arithmetic I run by default—my father taught me to read water before he taught me to read people, but the Vane brothers finished my education in valuation. Everything is an asset or a liability, and grief doesn't appear on either column of a ledger.

Then the body moved.

Not much. One arm lifted out of the brown water, fingers opening and closing like something testing whether the air was still breathable, and Tuck said a word he usually saved for engine failures, and I threw the boat into a turn hard enough that the fuel drums shifted and clanged against each other below decks like church bells announcing a very bad wedding.

Getting him aboard was ugly. The current had wedged him against a mat of hyacinth and storm debris—a tangle of branches, a child's plastic chair, half of someone's roof—and every time we got a hand on him the flotsam fought us for the privilege of keeping him. Tuck lay flat on the bow and grabbed a fistful of shirt while I held the throttle at a crawl, and for about ninety seconds the three of us played the world's most miserable game of tug-of-war, with the river as the third team and the only one playing for keeps.

He came over the gunwale in a rush of brown water and looked, briefly, like cargo. Which, I suppose, is what he was. My cargo. My liability.

He was maybe thirty-five, lean in the way of men who work outdoors and eat irregularly, dark-haired, brown-skinned, wearing the remains of what had once been decent field clothes—a khaki shirt gone gray with river water, trousers that had started life as part of a uniform set I didn't recognize. One boot. The other foot was bare except for a sock that had given up all pretense of being a sock.

He coughed up half the river onto my deck, rolled onto his side, and said, in accented but functional English:

"Don't radio anyone."

Not *thank you*. Not *where am I*. His first coherent words, spat out between retches, were instructions about communications security. I crouched next to him and did my own inventory, because that's what I do instead of sympathy.

His lips were blue-gray. His pulse, when I got two fingers on his throat, was fast and thready but present. His skin had been in the water long enough to prune white at the fingertips but not long enough for the sun to start cooking him, so under an hour, probably less. He was shivering in great racking waves, which was good—shivering meant the body still believed in its own future.

And his hands.

I took one of them to check for injuries and he flinched—not from the touch itself but from where I touched. Across both palms, running up over the heels of his hands and wrapping around toward the wrists, were rope burns. Not the kind you get from a single slip. These were layered, healed-over-then-reopened, the kind of marks that come from rope moving across skin repeatedly, under load, over hours. Days, maybe. Someone had tied this man up, and he had worked against the ropes until his own flesh paid the invoice.

I turned his hand over in mine and watched him watch me do it. His eyes were clear, which surprised me. Drowning usually scrambles people; his gaze was steady and appraising, and I got the distinct impression I was being inventoried right back.

"You fell off a supply barge," I said. It wasn't a question so much as a placeholder, a line item waiting for a real figure.

"Yes." No hesitation. Too little hesitation.

"Falling barges are dangerous."

"They can be."

I let go of his hand and nodded at the bare foot. "And the other boot?"

"Lost it in the water."

"Mm." I picked up the socked foot, gently enough, and turned it toward the light. The sock was torn, and beneath the tear, around the ankle, there was a mark I recognized from a lifetime of reading damage on bodies and hulls alike: a clean, straight abrasion, the signature of a blade. Something sharp had been drawn around that ankle, deliberately, in a circle. And then I looked closer at the remaining boot—the one he'd kept—and saw that its heel was missing. Not worn away. Cut away, sheared off cleanly at an angle, the leather showing knife marks under the mud.

People who fall off barges don't cut their own boots apart. People who escape do. You shear the heel off a boot to slip shackles, or to keep a tether from catching, or because someone tied you to something and you needed a edge to work free. Combined with the wrists, the story wrote itself, and it wasn't a story about clumsiness on a barge deck.

I set the foot down and sat back on my heels.

"Tuck," I said. "Get the blanket out of the dry box. And the kettle."

He looked from me to the man on my deck and back, and whatever he saw in my face made him go without argument, which was wise, because I wasn't entirely sure yet what my face was saying.

* * *

We got him into the cabin, wrapped in the blanket, and I put a mug of sweet tea in his hands and made him drink it in sips, which he did with the obedience of a man who understood that he was in no position to negotiate beverage policy. The rain had started again outside—that thin, persistent monsoon drizzle that isn't dramatic so much as patient, like it knows it has weeks to wear the whole world down—and the cabin drummed with it, close and dim and smelling of diesel and wet wool.

He drank. He shivered. He watched me.

"So," I said, settling across from him on the bench by the chart table. "Supply barge."

"That's right."

"Which supply barge?"

A pause. Small, but I've spent years listening to radios degrade, learning exactly how long a signal takes to decay, and I know what a pause costs. This one cost him something.

"The *Corvane Meridian*, out of—" He stopped. Recalculated. "It doesn't matter. It's gone, or it will be."

"*Corvane*," I repeated. The name meant nothing to me then. Remember that. There was a time, three days ago, when Corvane Extraction was just a word I hadn't learned yet, and I want credit for the innocence.

"It's a company," he said. "They move freight on the upper river. Supplies, equipment." His eyes came up to mine. "You're going upstream."

It wasn't a question, and I didn't confirm it, and we both pretended that wasn't an answer.

"I'm Hazel Sandoval," I said. "This is Tuck. We're under contract to locate a scientific expedition that stopped answering its radio eleven days ago. The *Meridian* expedition, as it happens. So if your supply barge was supplying them, you might be the most useful thing this river's floated past since the last bridge washed out."

Something moved behind his eyes at that—something quick and complicated, guilt or fear or their unholy offspring—and he looked down into his tea like it might contain instructions.

"I don't know anything about an expedition," he said.

"Sure."

"I don't."

"You said the name of the company that owns their supply contract without hesitating, you're wearing clothes cut for fieldwork rather than dockwork, and your hands look like you spent a week tied to something you didn't appreciate. But sure. You don't know anything."

He was quiet for a moment. Rain ticked against the cabin roof. Down below, Tuck was making a show of checking the bilge pump, loudly enough to indicate he was listening to every word, which I appreciated. An audience keeps everyone honest. Or at least keeps everyone aware of the witnesses.

"What happened to your wrists?" I asked.

He flexed his fingers against the mug. "Rope."

"What happened to your heel?"

"My boot caught on something."

"On a knife, from the look of it. Repeatedly. In a circle. Around your ankle." I leaned forward. "Here's where we are, friend. I pulled you out of the river because leaving you there would have haunted me, and I can't afford haunting any more than I can afford anything else. But I don't rescue strangers. I acquire liabilities. And right now you are a liability with an unknown interest rate. So you can tell me the truth now, cheaply, or I can find it out later, expensively, and neither of us will enjoy the second version. Your call. I'm told I'm reasonable, but the people who say that have usually already gotten what they wanted from me."

He looked at me for a long moment. Outside, the rain thickened, and somewhere upstream thunder muttered like a creditor clearing his throat.

"You should turn around," he said finally. "Whatever they're paying you, it isn't enough. Turn around, go downriver, forget the expedition, forget the name Corvane. There are men up there who make the water look merciful."

"Men generally do," I said. "I've met several. They all had paperwork."

"This one has deeds." He said it flatly, and then seemed to hear himself, and closed his mouth like a man who'd just stepped off a ledge and was hoping nobody noticed the drop.

But I'd noticed. Deeds. Paperwork. A surveyor's hands. I'd spent two days reading the contract that sent me up here, skimming clauses written by lawyers who billed by the syllable, and somewhere in my gut a connection was forming—the kind of connection I usually ignore right up until it becomes unavoidable.

"You're a surveyor," I said.

His silence lasted four seconds. I counted.

"Was," he said.

"And someone tied you up because of maps."

Another silence, longer. Then he set the mug down on the bench with exaggerated care, the way a man does when he wants his hands to have a job that isn't shaking.

"If I tell you," he said slowly, "you can't unknow it. And if you keep going upstream after knowing it, they won't treat you like a salvage crew anymore. They'll treat you like a problem. And Ms. Sandoval—I've seen what they do with problems. I'm what they do with problems. I'm what's left of one, floating downstream."

"Good news," I said. "I float too."

That almost got a smile out of him. Almost. It died halfway, the way smiles do on men carrying more than luggage.

"Your expedition," he said. "The scientists. What names do you have?"

"Dr. Amara Osei. Expedition lead. Eleven days of silence, then coordinates, then one word on the radio before the static took it."

His face changed. I watched it happen—the color draining not gradually but in stages, like a tide going out, and his hand found the edge of the bench and gripped it.

"What word?" he asked, and his voice had gone quiet in a way that had nothing to do with politeness.

"Inheritance."

He closed his eyes. For a moment he looked less like a rescued man and more like a man attending a funeral he'd helped arrange, and I thought: *there it is.* Whatever this was, whatever he knew, it lived behind that word, and he'd been carrying it alone in the water for however many hours, and the river hadn't managed to wash it out of him.

"Ms. Sandoval," he said, opening his eyes. "My name is Ilya Reyes. I was the senior land surveyor for Corvane Extraction's valley operations. And everything I'm about to tell you is going to make your contract worth less than the paper it's printed on, and possibly worth less than you are."

"Impossible," I said. "I'm heavily leveraged."

He almost laughed. It came out as a cough. And then he looked at the rain hammering the cabin, and upstream, where the river bent north into country none of the charts bothered to detail, and when he spoke again his voice had the flatness of a man reading figures he wished were wrong.

"Before I say anything else—you need to understand that I drew the maps. Whatever you decide to do with what I tell you, remember that the lines on the paper came from my hands first. I'm not a witness, Ms. Sandoval. I'm evidence."

Outside, the rain kept falling, patient as arithmetic. And I sat there across from a half-drowned stranger with rope-burned hands and a cut-open boot, doing the thing I always do, running the numbers: the fuel we'd burn turning back, the payment we'd lose pressing on, the debt waiting downriver with its cheerful face and its open ledger.

Every number said turn around.

So naturally, I asked him to start talking.
