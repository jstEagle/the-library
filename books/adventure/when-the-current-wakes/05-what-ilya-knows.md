## 5. What Ilya Knows

There are three ways a man tells the truth: willingly, under duress, or by accident while trying to lie about something smaller. I managed all three before the sun cleared the treeline, which I consider efficient, if not dignified.

It started with the boot.

Hazel Sandoval had my missing boot heel sitting on the deck of her boat like an exhibit in a very small, very hostile museum. She'd fished it out of the flotsam along with me, and she'd been turning it over in her fingers for the better part of an hour while Tuck steered and I sat wrapped in a tarp, shivering, pretending to be grateful instead of terrified.

"Cut clean," she said. Not to me, exactly. To the heel. "Saw marks. Somebody didn't lose this. Somebody *removed* it."

"Rot," I offered. "River rot. Very common in—"

"In synthetic heels." She looked up at me then, and her eyes were the color of the water before a squall decides what it wants to be. "In three days."

I want it on the record that I am, professionally speaking, an excellent liar. Surveyors have to be. We spend our lives drawing lines that say *this is yours* when both parties know full well the river has never once agreed with us. You learn to make fiction look like measurement. But Hazel Sandoval had the specific talent of a woman who has been lied to by professionals and has stopped being impressed by it, and lying to her felt less like deception and more like handing her ammunition one bullet at a time.

So I told her something true, as a kind of down payment.

"My name is Ilya Reyes," I said. "And I wasn't fishing."

Tuck spat over the side, which seemed to be his general commentary on most statements.

"No kidding," Hazel said.

* * *

Let me back up, because if I'm going to confess, I'd rather do it properly, in order, the way you'd stake a boundary line — from the corner post forward.

For six years I worked for Corvane Extraction. Not in the mines themselves; I never went near the mines. I was a land surveyor, which sounds respectable because it is designed to sound respectable. I drew maps. I established boundaries. I took instruments worth more than most of the houses in the valley and used them to determine, with centimeter precision, exactly how much of other people's lives could be reclassified as ours.

That's the sentence I couldn't say out loud for six years, so let me say it now, here, on this leaking boat in the rain: I drew the lines that took their land. That was the job. That was always the job. The job description said *geodetic services*, and what it meant was theft with coordinates.

The valley assignment came eleven months ago. Upper river, monsoon country, the kind of place where the maps changed every wet season and nobody upstream cared enough to update them. Corvane wanted a comprehensive resurvey. Rare-earth deposits, they said. Preliminary assessments. Standard procedure.

What they actually wanted — what Ms. Ashgrave's office actually wanted, though I didn't know her name yet — was a legal architecture. A claim. And claims need provenance, and provenance needs paper, and paper needs a dead woman's signature.

Dr. Amara Osei. Meridian Scientific Expedition. I knew the name before I ever saw the valley, because her expedition's mineral surveys were the reason Corvane came at all. She'd found something extraordinary in those hills — I've seen the assay numbers since, and extraordinary doesn't cover it — and she'd done the unforgivable thing with it.

She'd refused to sell.

"They offered her a partnership," I told Hazel. The rain had eased to a fine gray drizzle, the kind that gets inside your thoughts. "Equity participation, infrastructure development, community benefit framework — all the words. She turned it down. In writing. Politely, even, from what I understand. Which made it worse for them, because a polite refusal is *documented*. You can't pretend it didn't happen."

"So they killed her," Tuck said. He said it the way he'd report weather. Flat, factual, already moving on.

"The official record says her boat capsized at Kessel Bend during a storm surge." I heard my own voice do the thing it does — the smooth administrative register, the voice of reports and filings. I hated it and kept using it anyway, because the alternative was shaking. "Eleven days ago. Body recovered downstream. Signed off by the magistrate's office within forty-eight hours, which, for context, is a jurisdiction where I once waited nine weeks for a permit to build a *toilet*."

Hazel hadn't moved. She sat with the boot heel in her palm, watching me the way you watch a gauge needle climb toward red.

"And the deed?" she said.

Here is the part I will carry until I die, so I may as well set it down carefully. Three days after Dr. Osei's reported death, a document surfaced. An inheritance claim. It named Corvane Extraction as sole beneficiary of the Osei estate — including, critically, all mineral rights, survey data, and territorial interests within the upper valley watershed. It bore her signature. It bore her seal. It was witnessed, notarized, filed, and stamped, and every single thing about it was true except its entire substance.

"I authenticated the signature," I said. "That was my contribution. My actual hands-on contribution. I pulled her prior signatures from expedition filings and did a graphical overlay, and I confirmed the match, and I wrote a report saying the signature was genuine, and I knew — I *knew* —"

"You knew what?"

"That a woman who refused to sell her findings wouldn't leave them to the company that killed—" I stopped. Corrected. "To the company whose offer she declined. That's not evidence. That's just sense. But sense isn't admissible, and my overlay was, and so the deed stood."

The rain ticked against the tarp. Somewhere ahead, thunder muttered like a man reading terms and conditions aloud.

"But that's not why I ran," I said.

* * *

Why I ran. Yes. Let's do that part too, since we're apparently doing all of it.

Two weeks ago I was sent upriver to finalize the boundary demarcation — the physical work, the stakes in the ground, the lines painted on trees. I went with a relocation team. That's what the paperwork called them. Relocation. As if the villages were furniture.

The first village was called San Isidro, or had been, on older maps. Forty-one households. They'd fished that bend of the river for longer than anyone had written anything down. The relocation team arrived at dawn with the magistrate's men, and there was a document, and there was a deadline, and when the elders asked to see the deed — asked politely, these people are unfailingly polite, it's how they survive everything — the team lead showed them a copy with the Corvane letterhead and said the original was held in trust.

Held in trust. By the party claiming to own your home. You have to admire the elegance. I did, briefly, in the way you admire a snare for its engineering.

Then they started loading people onto barges.

Not all of them. Just the ones who asked questions. The rest got a compensation schedule — figures so small they functioned as insults, denominated in a currency the nearest bank was four days' travel from. And the ones who loaded onto the barges weren't taken anywhere with housing. I followed the route on my own charts, because that was my job, confirming access corridors, and the corridor terminated at a clearing with no settlement, no infrastructure, nothing but a stockade going up and guards going around it.

I asked the team lead what the facility designation was. He checked his sheet and said, cheerfully, "Transitional."

Transitional. To what? Nobody would tell me, and the not-telling was the answer. I stood there with my theodolite over my shoulder, the instrument I'd used to draw the lines that put those people on that barge, and I understood that I was not a witness to the crime. I was a component of it. A load-bearing one.

That night I cut the heel off my boot — the heel had a hollow core, and in the hollow core I'd kept the thing I'd been carrying for a month without letting myself know I was carrying it: a microfilm strip of the boundary survey, the real one, the pre-forgery baseline, plus a copy of my authentication report with my private annotations still on it. The annotations where I'd written, in the margin, in my own hand: *signature consistent, provenance impossible.* I'd written it thinking no one would ever read margins. Margins are where honesty goes to hide.

Then I waited two more days, because I'm a coward with excellent planning skills, and then the barge stopped at a fueling point and I went into the water.

"Hence the flotsam," Hazel said.

"Hence the flotsam."

"And hence the rope burns." She nodded at my wrists, where the marks had scabbed over into brown bracelets. "You tied yourself to something. Debris raft?"

"A fuel drum. Empty ones float beautifully. It's their one virtue."

Tuck laughed, one syllable, like a door slamming. Hazel didn't laugh. She set the boot heel down on the deck between us, deliberate as a judge setting down a gavel.

"Here's my problem, Reyes," she said. "Everything you just told me makes you either the most useful man on this river or the best-prepared plant I've ever fished out of it. A syndicate that forges deeds and disappears villages would absolutely float a sympathetic defector downstream to land in the boat of whoever comes looking for the Meridian expedition. Confession included. Guilt included. Rope burns —" she glanced at my wrists again — "rope burns are cheap."

The terrible thing, the thing I want noted in whatever margin this confession ends up in, is that I admired the theory. It was a good theory. If I'd been running the operation, I'd have floated a man exactly like me.

"So prove it," I said. "Or don't. But ask yourself why a plant would volunteer the location of the forged deed, the magistrate's involvement, and the fact that Corvane's claim dies if my authentication report is discredited. A plant gives you a trail that leads somewhere useful *to Corvane*. Everything I've given you leads somewhere useful to a lawyer with a conscience. Ask yourself who benefits."

"Nobody benefits," Hazel said. "That's usually how you know it's true."

* * *

We anchored that night in the lee of a drowned island — a sandbar that the rising water had reduced to a fringe of reeds and one stubborn acacia. Tuck cooked rice with the economy of a man who considers flavor a luxury item, and we ate in the boat's small cabin while the rain worked through its repertoire outside.

Hazel spread her charts on the table and weighted the corners with fuel caps.

"If half of what you said is true," she said, "the survivors are upstream. Meridian had four boats and nineteen people. Eleven days silent. Either they're dead, hiding, or held."

"Held," I said, before I could stop myself.

Both of them looked at me.

"There's a transit camp," I said. "Stockade on the east bank, past the second confluence. When I ran, it was holding maybe thirty people. Fishermen, two families from San Isidro, some boat crew who asked too many questions. If any Meridian people survived Kessel Bend, that's where Corvane puts people it can't afford to have talking and can't quite afford to have drown." I tapped the chart, a bend of green marked with nothing at all. "Here. My survey put it at kilometer 214. I staked that line myself. I know exactly where it is, because I measured the ground they built the cage on."

The cabin got very quiet. Rain filled the silence, patient, professional.

"Why tell us this?" Tuck said. First full sentence he'd aimed at me all day. "You could take that knowledge downriver. Sell it. Papers pay for papers."

"Because I drew the map," I said. "All of it. Every line on every chart in this boat's locker that says where Corvane's claim begins and ends — versions of those lines came off my instruments. I keep thinking there's a version of me that's still useful to them, and I keep realizing the only way to not be that man is to be useful in the opposite direction. Completely. All the way." I looked at Hazel. "You want to find the truth of what happened to Dr. Osei? I'm the man who helped bury it. I know where the bodies are buried because I surveyed the cemetery."

Hazel studied me for a long moment. Outside, lightning walked along the far hills, counting something.

"Your authentication report," she said finally. "The annotations. Where is it?"

"Waterproof sleeve. Inside jacket." I hesitated. "Along with the microfilm. Baseline survey coordinates. Pre-forgery. If anyone ever asks whether the deed's boundaries match the original field data, the answer is no, by roughly forty kilometers, and I can show them exactly where and how."

She held out her hand. Not grabbing. Offering, which from this woman I suspected was rarer than mercy.

I gave her the sleeve. Watching her open it and read my marginal handwriting — *provenance impossible* — felt like watching someone read a diary entry I'd written drunk. True, but naked.

"This is worth money," she said. "Real money. To lawyers, to newspapers, to whoever ends up holding Corvane accountable, if anyone does."

"I know."

"It's also the only thing keeping you alive. The moment Corvane believes you've lost it, you're a rounding error."

"I know that too." I almost smiled. "I've read their internal memos, Ms. Sandoval. I know precisely what size error I am."

Something shifted in her face — not softness exactly, more like recalibration. A new number entered into whatever ledger she kept behind those storm-colored eyes.

Tuck leaned in the doorway, chewing, and delivered his verdict to the rain: "He talks like a man reading his own eulogy. I say we keep him. Dead weight floats anyway."

* * *

I took the middle watch that night, because I couldn't sleep, because guilt is a stimulant if you take enough of it. I sat in the stern under the tarp with the river sliding black past the hull, and I did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding for eleven months.

Forty-one households in San Isidro. Thirty-odd in the transit stockade, last count. Nineteen on the Meridian expedition, status unknown. Add the fishing families, the boat crews, the elders taken "for meetings." Call it two hundred people whose lives I had redrawn, with centimeter precision, into the category of *in the way.*

A good surveyor checks his work. That night, alone on the water, I finally checked mine.

The current pushed us gently upriver against our anchor, the way it does before a flood, restless, rehearsing. And I thought about what Hazel had said on the deck — *nobody benefits, that's how you know it's true* — and I thought about Dr. Osei, whom I never met, whose signature I authenticated into a forgery, who had looked at the richest offer on the table and said no on behalf of people she'd known for a season, because that was what the data told her was right. Data. I trusted data my whole life. Her data said protect the valley. Mine said draw the line.

Somewhere upstream, past the second confluence, past kilometer 214, there was a stockade full of witnesses and possibly survivors, and a deed resting on my fraudulent overlay, and a company that had bought a debt today or would tomorrow, because companies like Corvane buy everything that floats, including, briefly, me.

I woke Hazel at dawn, because the light was wrong.

Not the sky — the sky was doing its usual monsoon impression, gray on gray. The light on the water. There was a slick of it coming downriver, rainbow-skinned, oily, spreading from the northern channel in long fingers.

"Tuck," I called. "Kill the pump intake. Now."

Hazel was up in seconds, binoculars up. Downstream of the slick, the river had begun to carry things that weren't leaves. Plastic sheeting. A company-stenciled drum. A plank with paint too fresh to be old wreckage.

"What is that?" she said. "Where's it coming from?"

I took the binoculars and looked north, toward the high country, toward the mine works I had mapped and mapped and mapped, and my stomach dropped the way it does when a benchmark doesn't close and you realize the whole grid is wrong.

"Tailings dam," I said. "Corvane's containment dam, upstream. That's seepage. That much seepage means the structure's already compromised." I lowered the glasses. My hands, I noticed, were steady, which felt like a betrayal of how I felt. "Ms. Sandoval — when that dam lets go, it won't just drown the evidence. It'll drown everything downstream of kilometer 100. Every village. The stockade. Anyone Corvane is holding, anyone who's hiding, everyone."

Hazel took the binoculars back and stared north for a long time. Then she looked at me, and I watched her arrive at the same arithmetic I had, the one where every hour we spent deciding was an hour the water spent deciding for us.

"How long?" she asked.

I thought about the seepage rate, the rainfall forecast, the geometry of the valley. I thought about how many times in my career I'd given a precise figure and been believed.

"Days," I said. "Maybe less. And the next heavy rain—" I nodded at the sky, which chose that exact moment to split open, as if it had been waiting for its cue. "The next heavy rain answers the question for us."
