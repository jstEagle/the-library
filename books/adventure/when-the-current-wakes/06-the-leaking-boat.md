## 6. The Leaking Boat

The river hit us at dawn, or rather we hit the river's opinion of us, which amounted to the same thing.

I'd been running the *Vela* upriver for three days by then, and I want to be clear about what that means: not cruising, not navigating—running. The monsoon had turned the water into something between a highway and a grudge. Brown, muscular, moving faster than any river has a right to move when it's carrying half a mountain range of runoff toward the sea. Tuck called it "the current waking up," which was either poetry or a warning, and with Tuck it was usually both.

We were threading the narrows above the second bend when the log came for us.

You don't see submerged timber in flood water. You see the boil on the surface where it's pinned, maybe, if you're lucky and paying attention, and I was both—but luck is a currency that devalues fast in monsoon country, and my attention was split between a fuel gauge I didn't trust and a radio hiss that had gotten worse overnight. The log was a whole tree, actually, root ball down like an anchor, trunk angled into the current just below the surface. It caught the *Vela*'s port bow and opened her like a letter nobody wanted to read.

The sound is hard to describe. Not a crash. More of a long, wet tearing, the river reaching inside the boat and taking something. I killed the engine before it swallowed water through the intake, and Tuck was already moving—he's a quiet man but he moves like he's rehearsed for exactly this, which, knowing him, he probably has.

"Portside," he said. "Below the waterline. She's drinking."

"How much?"

"Enough."

That's Tuck's entire vocabulary distilled: enough, plenty, maybe, don't. He handed me the bilge pump handle and started hauling us toward the bank with the sweep oar while I pumped like the handle owed me money. Which, in fairness, everything did.

We made the shallows on the north side, where the jungle came down to the water in a green wall, and grounded her on a shelf of mud with about four inches of freeboard left. I sat there a moment with my hands still on the pump, listening to the rain start again on the canopy—that sound like applause from an audience that wants to see you fail—and did the arithmetic every boat owner does in that moment: what she cost, what she owes, what she's worth as scrap.

The *Vela* wasn't scrap yet. But she was leaking, and the nearest marine yard was two hundred miles downstream, and the nearest anything else was—

"Smoke," Tuck said.

I looked. Through the rain-haze, maybe half a kilometer downstream, a thin gray thread rising off the treeline. Woodsmoke, cooking smoke. People.

"Great," I said. "Strangers."

"You could stay here," Tuck offered, "and drown politely."

Tuck's humor works like that—dry as kindling, and you only notice it's there when it catches fire.

* * *

The village didn't have a name on any chart I owned, which these days means it doesn't officially exist, which these days means somebody official decided it shouldn't. Maybe forty houses on stilts along a mudbank, nets drying on racks, a dozen boats pulled up under palm-thatch shelters. Fishing people. River people. The kind of community that survives the monsoon every year because they've learned what the rest of us keep forgetting: the water always wins, so you build for losing.

They saw us coming—of course they did; we were a mud-caked stranger boat limping out of the fog with a hole in her—and instead of meeting us with machetes or indifference, an old woman waved us toward a gap in their moorings like we were expected.

Her name was Maren. She was perhaps seventy, built like the mangrove roots she walked among, and she looked at the *Vela*'s torn bow the way a doctor looks at a wound: professionally, without flinching.

"Log," she said. Not a question. Upstream of her village, apparently, the river delivered trees the way other places deliver mail.

"Log," I confirmed.

"You'll need tar," she said. "And canvas. And help." Then, before I could perform whatever ritual of payment or refusal I'd prepared: "Eat first. Patching goes better fed."

Here's the thing about me: I don't do free. Free is a hook with no visible line. My father taught me that lesson with his life, more or less—the Vane brothers' cheerful loans started as somebody's generosity, and look how that compounded. So when Maren set rice and smoked fish in front of me in a house that smelled of woodsmoke and river, I started calculating what I could trade. Fuel, maybe—I had drums to spare now that the *Vela* couldn't burn them fast enough. Tools. Labor. Something. A transaction, because transactions are safe. Transactions end.

But nobody in that village asked me for anything.

Not Maren, who fed us. Not the men who helped haul the *Vela* onto rollers of stripped bamboo so they could work on her hull without fighting the tide. Not the kids, who hung around the boatyard-in-progress asking questions in that universal dialect of children—pointing, miming, laughing at Tuck's face until Tuck, who I'm fairly sure has never laughed in his adult life, almost smiled.

It was the kids who told us about the company men.

A boy named Pilip, maybe ten, missing a front tooth and shy about it, drew it for us in the mud with a stick, because some things are easier drawn than said. Men in orange. Orange shirts, orange band on the patrol skiffs—Corvane colors, though I wouldn't learn the name until later. The stick-figure men had come in three boats, weeks ago. They'd taken the fishing boats—the good ones, the ones with engines—and loaded them with crates. And they'd taken people too. Elders. The headman. Maren's brother, though Pilip didn't know that part; Maren told me later, quietly, the way you tell someone bad news you've already finished grieving.

"For meetings," Pilip said, and made a face that ten-year-olds make when adults use words that taste wrong. "Meetings." He dragged the word around in the mud like something dead. "They come back sometimes. Without the elders."

Nobody in the village said much about it beyond that. That was the worst part—not fear exactly, but a practiced quiet, the silence of people who've learned that questions are expensive. They'd paid already. They knew the exchange rate.

I stood there in the rain looking at a stick drawing in the mud, and I felt something I didn't have a name for yet. It took me until evening to name it, and the name was shame.

Because my first thought—my honest, reflexive first thought, the one I'd never say out loud—was: *good.* Good, because a valley with trouble in it meant a salvage contract worth more. Good, because scared people pay better than comfortable ones. I've spent years thinking like that. Debt teaches you to think like that. When everything you own is collateral, you start seeing everything else as inventory, including other people's catastrophes.

And then Maren brought me a blanket that night, dry, because mine had soaked through, and asked nothing for it, and I lay awake under it feeling like a thief who'd broken into an empty house.

* * *

The patch took most of two days.

The hull damage was worse below the waterline than I'd hoped—a gash maybe eighteen inches long, planks sprung, one frame cracked. Nothing a proper yard couldn't fix in a week. We had no yard, no week, and a contract clock ticking somewhere upstream where eleven days of silence had become twelve, then thirteen.

Maren's son-in-law, a broad silent man called Dario, turned out to have patched river boats since before I was born. Between his hands and my tools and Tuck's stubbornness, we worked out a system: pull the sprung planks tight with ratchet straps, seal the gash with layers of tarpaulin and marine epoxy and—this was Dario's contribution—melted tree resin mixed with pitch from the palms, a recipe older than epoxy and possibly better. Three layers, each one pressed and heated and pressed again, until the patch was less a repair than a scab the river would have to argue with.

"It will hold," Dario said, "until it doesn't."

"I can work with that," I said. It's the same warranty the river gives everything.

While we worked, the village worked around us, and that's what shamed me most, I think. Nobody stopped their lives for our emergency—they folded us into theirs. Women mended nets and kept half an eye on the boat. Kids hauled fresh water. An old man whose name I never got sat on Maren's porch sharpening a fish knife with the patience of geology and occasionally shouted advice about caulking that was, annoyingly, correct.

On the second day, Maren sat beside me while I waited for a layer of pitch to cure, and asked me, in the way of people who already know the answer, why I was going upstream.

"Salvage contract," I said. "An expedition went missing. Meridian Scientific. Eleven days ago."

"Mm." She watched the river. "The doctor woman. Amara Osei."

I turned. "You knew her?"

"She came through." Maren's voice stayed level, but her hands, working a net needle, slowed. "Twice. She tested the water. Asked about the fish, the floods, where the old channels ran. She listened." The needle paused. "The others"—and by *others*, I understood, she meant the orange shirts—"do not listen. They measure."

There's a difference, if you're wondering. Listening is what you do when you think a place might have something to teach you. Measuring is what you do when you've already decided what it's worth. I've been measured my whole life—by banks, by brokers, by two cheerful brothers with a ledger full of my father's blood—and I recognized the instrument immediately, the way you recognize a familiar weapon.

"The elders they took," I said carefully. "For meetings. What happens at the meetings?"

Maren tied off a knot before answering. "They bring papers. They ask the old ones to press a thumb into wax. They say it is only to record who lives where. And the old ones, who cannot read, press." She looked at me then, and her eyes were the flat brown of the river itself, holding everything, giving back nothing. "Paper is how they take what they cannot carry."

I thought about the salvage broker's contract I'd signed in the port town, the stack of pages I'd skimmed because stopping to read them would have cost me the advance. Paper is how they take what they cannot carry. I'd carried my own leash out of that office and called it opportunity.

"We'll be gone tomorrow," I said, "when the patch cures."

"Yes," Maren said. "The river is high. You should go while going is possible." And then, because she was Maren and not me: "Take rice. Take the resin pot. If you find the doctor's people—" she stopped, untied the knot, retied it. "If you find them, tell them the villages remember who listened."

* * *

We launched at first light on the third morning, into a river that had risen another foot overnight and was running the color of strong tea.

The patch held. Of course it held—Dario's resin-and-prayer composite was honestly better engineering than the original planking, and I told him so, and he shrugged the way craftsmen shrug when complimented on miracles. The village turned out on the bank to watch us go, and I did something I'm not sure I've ever done on departure: I looked back. At Maren standing straight as a piling. At Pilip waving with the whole arm, the uninhibited wave of a kid who hasn't yet learned that waving costs something.

I had fuel drums to spare, so I left two on the bank, and when Maren tried to refuse them I told her it was ballast adjustment, which was even true. She let me have the lie. That's hospitality, really—letting your guest preserve their dignity while they rob themselves for you.

Tuck took the tiller and I took the bow watch, and we ran the flooded channel downstream of the village for an hour before the country changed—narrower, rockier, the karst hills starting to shoulder up out of the jungle like the backs of sleeping animals. Somewhere ahead was a checkpoint that had gone silent, an expedition that had gone silent, and a word that had come through the static right before it died:

*Inheritance.*

At the time, sitting in the port town signing papers, I'd taken it for a corrupted transmission, a fragment, noise wearing meaning like a mask. But after Maren and her thumbprints in wax, after Pilip dragging the word *meetings* through the mud like a corpse, the word sounded different. Less like static. More like a message that had cost somebody everything to send.

"Radio's worse," Tuck said, nodding at the set behind me, where the hiss had developed a rhythmic pulse, almost like breathing.

"Everything's worse. That's the monsoon."

"No." Tuck shook his head slowly. "Radios die first upriver. Before the boats. Before the lights." He glanced at me with those flat gray eyes. "Somebody's killing them on purpose."

I looked at the pulse in the static, and the pulse looked back, and for the first time since I'd signed the contract I considered the possibility that the eleven days of silence weren't an accident, an act of God, or a scientific expedition getting lost in the weather.

Somebody upstream didn't want voices leaving the valley.

And I was sailing straight into the mouth of the thing, on a patched hull, with a debt on my back and a resin pot Maren had pressed into my hands like a blessing—or a bet.

The river bent north. We followed it.
