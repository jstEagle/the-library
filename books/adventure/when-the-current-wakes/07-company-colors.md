## 7. Company Colors

I've been boarded twice in my life before this river, and both times the men doing the boarding were drunk, loud, and stupid enough to announce themselves from fifty meters out. The third time was Corvane Extraction, and they did it so quietly I almost missed it happening.

We'd come down out of the narrows that morning with the sky the color of wet slate and Hazel at the wheel doing her storm-chaser thing, which is to say reading water like other people read weather reports. Tarpaulin patch holding on the hull. Ilya sitting in the stern like a man waiting for a verdict he already knows is bad. Me on the radio, which was mostly me listening to static and pretending it was conversation.

"Fuel stop at the next town," Hazel said. "We're burning more than we should."

"We're always burning more than we should," I said.

"That's what makes us honest people. Thieves plan their fuel."

I liked Tuck's Law of Rivers, which is: everything upstream comes down eventually, and most of it you don't want. I'd been saying it for years. That morning the law came down in the shape of two gray skiffs sliding out of a side channel with their engines idled so low they made less noise than the rain.

"Hazel," I said. "Company."

She didn't swear, which told me how bad she thought it was. Swearing means surprise. No swearing means she'd been expecting them since the checkpoint with the torn-down flag, maybe since the contract itself.

The skiffs came alongside without hurry. Four men in each, rain slickers over gray uniforms, no insignia except a small stitched mark on the shoulder — a stylized C wrapped around a bar of ore, if you cared to look, and I looked, because that's my job on this boat. Hazel drives, Ilya regrets, and I watch things. Somebody has to count the guns.

Six long arms visible. Two of the men had the loose-shouldered ease of actual soldiers. The rest had the grip-too-tight posture of hired security who'd signed up for gate duty and gotten a war instead.

A young officer stepped across onto our deck without being invited, which takes a certain kind of nerve or a certain kind of orders, and I couldn't tell yet which. He was maybe twenty-four, twenty-five. Rain running off the brim of his cap. He had the kind of face that hadn't decided yet what it was going to be when it grew up, and I mean that as an observation, not an insult — half the dangerous men I've met never did decide, and that's exactly why they stayed dangerous.

"Morning," he said. Polite. Almost cheerful, in the way of a man performing cheerfulness for an audience of one — himself. "Corporal Ennis, Corvane River Security. I'll need to see your papers and your cargo."

"You'll need to see our papers," Hazel repeated, flat.

"Yes, ma'am. Standard procedure." He said *ma'am* like he'd been trained in it recently and wasn't sure it applied here. His eyes went past her to Ilya and stopped there for half a second too long. Half a second doesn't sound like much. On a boat, in the rain, between two armed parties, half a second is a headline.

Hazel handed over the salvage contract and her registration. Ennis read them under a plastic sleeve, lips moving slightly, and I watched his thumb press against the corner of the contract page hard enough to whiten. He knew what salvage meant. Salvage meant someone was looking for the Meridian. He'd known we were coming, or at least coming-shaped.

"Cargo?" he said.

"Fuel drums, repair supplies, food," Hazel said. "You want to count it, count it. We're not hiding anything."

That was a lie, technically. We were hiding Ilya, but only in the sense that he was standing in plain sight trying to look like ballast. Ennis glanced at him again. Ilya gave him the blank stare of a man who has practiced being furniture.

Ennis didn't ask. That interested me. A man confident in his authority asks. A man following orders he doesn't understand doesn't ask, because asking produces answers, and answers have to go in reports, and reports have to be defended. I filed that away.

* * *

Here's what I noticed while Ennis's men walked our deck, and I want to be fair to myself here, because noticing things is the one skill I've got that Hazel can't replace and Ilya is too guilty to use:

Their skiffs were riding high.

Not empty-high. Loaded-high, the way a boat sits when the weight's all above the waterline — gear, weapons, men — but light below. Light in the place where fuel lives. I've spent enough years around engines to read a waterline the way Hazel reads clouds. Those boats had come upriver fast and far, and whatever fuel depot they'd drawn from, they'd drawn the last of it, or close to it.

One of the soldiers opened a drum, sniffed it, closed it. Another checked our flares and put them back crooked. Nobody wrote anything down. That was the part that really got my attention. You board a vessel, you log the boarding — registration numbers, cargo estimate, time, position. It's not just procedure, it's the whole point. Paper is how companies own things. These men were going through the motions without producing the paper, like actors who'd lost the script and were improvising off the poster.

"Your papers check out," Ennis said finally, handing them back. And then he did the strange thing. He stepped closer to Hazel, under the awning, out of the rain, and lowered his voice — not to a whisper, nothing so dramatic, just to the register you use when you don't want your own men hearing you say what you're about to say.

"I'm required to advise you that the upper valley is closed to civilian traffic. Active extraction operations. Insurance liability." He paused. "For insurance reasons, you should return downriver."

"For insurance reasons," Hazel said.

"Yes, ma'am."

"Whose insurance?"

He blinked. It was a good question and he clearly wished she hadn't asked it, because there was only one honest answer and the honest answer was *ours* — Corvane insuring itself against witnesses — and nobody puts that in a brochure. "The company maintains comprehensive coverage for all operational zones," he recited, and it came out sounding exactly like what it was, a sentence written by a lawyer and swallowed by a soldier.

"Corporal." Hazel leaned on the wheel, casual as anything, though I could see her knuckles. "How far up does the closure run?"

"All the way to the headwaters."

"And the towns?"

"The towns are being assisted with relocation."

"Relocation," Ilya said, very quietly, from the stern. Just the word. But the word came out of him like a tooth coming out of a jaw, and Ennis heard it, and for a moment the two of them looked at each other across our little leaking boat with something passing between them that I didn't have a name for yet. Recognition, maybe. Two men who'd both seen the same thing and were both pretending they hadn't.

Then Ennis straightened up, became a corporal again, and touched his cap brim. "You're advised to turn back. Weather's deteriorating. Have a safe trip downriver, ma'am."

They pulled away the way they'd come, quiet, engines low, and slid into the rain going upstream. Toward the headwaters. Toward a closure zone they barely had the fuel to patrol.

"Well," I said, when they were gone. "That was friendly."

"They didn't board us properly," Hazel said. She was still watching the gray shapes fade. "No log. No manifest copy. No report number. You know what that means?"

"It means their paperwork's as leaky as our hull."

"It means they don't want a record of us existing." She turned and looked at Ilya, who had gone the color of old dishwater. "And it means they recognized you."

"He's young," Ilya said. "He hasn't learned to lie with his eyes yet."

"No," Hazel said. "He's learned to lie with his mouth, which is worse. Eyes are instinct. Mouth is training."

* * *

We ran another hour before Hazel called the fuel stop, and by then the rain had settled in for the long haul, that monsoon patience where it isn't a storm anymore, it's a climate. We tied up at a town called Sarn's Landing, which consisted of a fuel dock, a church, forty houses on stilts, and a magistrate's office flying a flag so new it still had its creases — company colors, gray and ore-red, hung right where the regional flag used to be. Somebody had taken the old one down recently enough that the mounting holes didn't match. Two extra screw holes, rusted, empty. I counted those too.

The fuel dock man was named Berto, or said he was, and he pumped diesel with the unhurried rhythm of a man who'd decided long ago that hurry was for tourists. While the drums filled, I did what I do. I leaned on the rail, smoked half a cigarette I wasn't supposed to have, and listened.

Berto talked the way dock men talk, sideways, everything in metaphor. The price of diesel had gone up "since the new neighbors arrived." The new neighbors paid cash, which sounded nice until you understood the cash was for things like silence and boat rentals and "meetings." The elders had been to several meetings. The elders came back from the meetings tired. There was a word he used for the meetings — *consultations*, delivered with a small bitter twist — and I understood that consultations was what a company called it when it explained to you, patiently, with documents, why your grandfather's fishing grounds were now an asset class.

"How many boats they got?" I asked him, casual as asking about the weather, which in a way I was.

"Enough," Berto said. Then, after a moment, because dock men are honest in the ways that matter: "Not enough fuel, though. They buy from me too. Last week they took four hundred liters on credit. Company credit." He smiled without any joy in it. "Their own paper. They sign and I'm supposed to wait for a office in the capital to reimburse me. I am still waiting for reimbursement from the last government, so."

There it was, confirmed, third source. The great gray machine of Corvane Extraction, with its lawyers and its forged deeds and its polite terrifying corporals, was running its river empire on fumes and IOUs. The patrol boats rode high because the tanks ran low. The soldiers didn't log boardings because logging requires forms and forms require a functioning office and somewhere upstream the office was either drowning in relocation paperwork or had never been more than a desk and a stamp.

People think power looks like guns. Guns are just the loud part. The real machinery is slower and quieter — a deed dated three days after a death, a flag changed overnight, a credit line at a fuel dock, a young man saying *for insurance reasons* with sweat under his cap in the rain. You don't fight that with a gun. You fight it with patience, or you don't fight it at all, and most people, sensibly, don't.

I finished my cigarette and went to find Hazel, who was having a conversation with a woman selling dried fish, and by the set of Hazel's shoulders I could tell the conversation had gone somewhere useful, which for Hazel usually meant somewhere painful.

* * *

That night we anchored mid-channel, away from the town lights, because Hazel didn't trust the dock and I didn't trust the flag. Rain on the canopy, drumming. Ilya sat up late, and I sat up with him, because somebody had to and because I wanted to hear what he'd say when he finally said anything.

"You knew that corporal," I said.

"I didn't know him." Ilya turned his cup in his hands. "I briefed him. Eight months ago. Orientation session for new river security — I gave the boundary presentation. Where the new lines ran. Which villages fell inside." He laughed, short and awful. "I had slides. I remember thinking I made the maps clear. Very legible. Good contrast."

"And?"

"And he asked questions. Good ones. About whether the compensation schedules accounted for fishing seasons." Ilya stared into the rain. "Nobody ever asked about fishing seasons. So I remembered him. And today he stood on this deck and said *relocation* like it was a neutral word, and I watched him hate the word while he said it, and I thought — that's what I looked like. Eight months ago. Hating it and saying it anyway."

I didn't have anything comforting to offer, so I offered the truth, which is my version of comfort. "He warned us back downriver."

"He did."

"Quietly. Off the record. With his own men ten feet away."

Ilya looked at me.

"A man fully owned by the company doesn't break script," I said. "Even a little. Even kindly. Script-breakers get reported by their own crews. Either he's sloppy — and he didn't strike me as sloppy — or he wanted us to keep going and needed plausible deniability to survive giving us the nudge." I stubbed out nothing, since I'd already smoked the cigarette, but the gesture felt right. "Either way, Corporal Ennis is a crack in the wall. Small one. Hairline. But I've patched hulls, I know what hairline cracks become when the pressure builds."

"You think the pressure will build?"

I nodded upstream, toward the headwaters, toward the closure zone, toward whatever had scared armed men off their own checkpoint and emptied a magistrate's flagpole. "Everything upstream comes down eventually, Ilya. That's the law of rivers. The only question is what shape it's in when it arrives."

He went to sleep eventually. I stayed up and kept the anchor watch, listening to the rain and the channel, and around two in the morning I heard engines — distant, upstream, more than one boat moving fast in weather they shouldn't be moving fast in. Running something. Or chasing something. Or hauling something heavy toward the mine, burning fuel they didn't have, spending money they hadn't got, holding a stolen valley together with paper and patience while the paper got wetter and the patience wore thinner.

In the morning Hazel spread the chart on the cabin table, tapped a point well inside the closure zone, and said, "Osei's last coordinates are here. Eleven days of silence started here."

"Corporal Insurance Reasons says we should turn back," I reminded her.

"Corporal Insurance Reasons also tipped his hand, ran out of fuel money, and forgot to write us down." She rolled up the chart. "Tuck, how do you feel about making a company's paperwork problem worse?"

I thought about the high-riding skiffs, the empty screw holes on the flagpole, Berto's unreimbursed four hundred liters, and a young man hating a word while he said it.

"Tuck's Law of Rivers," I said. "Everything upstream comes down. Might as well help it along."

Hazel almost smiled. Somewhere upstream, thunder rolled across the water like a door closing — or opening, depending on how you read weather, and reading weather was, after all, the family business.
