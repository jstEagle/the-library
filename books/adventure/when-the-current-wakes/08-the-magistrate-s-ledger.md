## 8. The Magistrate's Ledger

The town was called Serrano's Landing, which was optimistic, since the landing had washed out two floods ago and nobody named Serrano had lived there in living memory. What remained was a spine of stilt houses along the muddy bank, a fuel depot that sold diesel by the rumor, a cantina with a roof that leaked in the philosophical sense—it let the rain in but charged it rent—and, wedged between the cantina and a shrine to a saint whose specialty was apparently drowned things, the district registry office.

The registry office was where the law kept its paperwork. The law, in my experience, was mostly paperwork with a uniform on.

"We need to be quick," Tuck said, tying off at the remains of a public jetty. He'd been quiet since the checkpoint with the torn-down flag, quiet in the way Tuck got quiet, which was to say he'd said four things in two days and three of them had been about fuel.

"We are quick," I said. "I'm a woman with a leaking boat and a schedule."

"You're a woman with a leaking boat and a plan I haven't heard yet."

"The plan is I walk into that office and read the magistrate's ledger."

Tuck looked at the registry office, then at me, then at the two uniformed constables playing cards under an awning across the street, their rifles propped against a post like umbrellas. "And the magistrate's going to let you."

"The magistrate isn't here. That's the point. Magistrates in towns like this keep offices in the provincial capital, where the air conditioning is. What's here is a clerk, and clerks can be bribed."

"Can this one?"

I'd spent the morning taking inventory of my remaining cash, which was the kind of arithmetic that made Denny Vane's interest rates look sentimental. "This one's going to find out that I'm charming."

* * *

The clerk's name was Ferro, and he was maybe twenty-five, with the pale, indoor complexion of a man whose entire relationship with the river was secondhand. He sat behind a desk in a room that smelled of mildew and stamp ink, and when I walked in he looked up with the expression of someone who'd been interrupted mid-daydream and was deciding whether to be annoyed.

"Registry's closed for the holiday," he said.

I looked out the window at the rain, which was not falling so much as hanging in the air like the whole valley had been submerged in gray water without anyone noticing. "What holiday?"

"The one I declared."

Okay. Points for honesty. I put a folded bill on the desk, not a big one, the kind of note that says *I'm serious but not serious enough to be a problem.* "I'm looking for a shipping record. Salvage contract, upriver. I need to know if any vessel registered to the Meridian expedition came through here, and I need to know what the district office has on file about land titles in the upper valley."

Ferro looked at the bill the way a man looks at a fish that might be spoiled. "Land titles."

"Land titles."

"That's not shipping records."

"No," I agreed. "It's better."

He didn't touch the money. That was the first thing I noticed, and the first thing that told me this wasn't going to be the easy version. A clerk who won't take small money either wants big money or is scared, and Ferro didn't have the posture of a man negotiating. He had the posture of a man listening for footsteps.

"Who's asking?" he said.

"Hazel Sandoval. Independent salvage. Contracted to locate the Meridian expedition, which has been silent eleven days, which means somewhere upstream there are people who might be dead, and I'd like to know if your district office noticed."

"They noticed." It came out flat, automatic, and then he heard himself and his eyes did a quick, panicky flick toward the door. "I mean—the district office processes what it's given. We process what we're given."

"And what were you given?"

He was quiet long enough that the rain filled the silence for him. Then he said, in a voice pitched for a confessional, "You should talk to the magistrate."

"The magistrate's in the capital."

"The magistrate's *deputy* is in the capital. The magistrate is at the Corvane guest house up the hill. He goes up on Thursdays." Ferro swallowed. "He came back last Thursday with a new boat."

I let that sit. A new boat, in a town where the landing had washed out and the constables played cards under a leaking awning, was a sentence with a lot of words in it, and none of them were *justice.*

"How much for the ledger?" I said.

"Hazel Sandoval." He said my name like he was checking it against a list. Maybe he was. "You should go. You should take your boat and go downriver. There's nothing up there for you."

"There's a salvage fee."

"There's a river that eats radios," he said, and I felt that one in my back teeth, because Tuck had said nearly the same thing a week and a lifetime ago, "and there are men in company colors who don't file reports, and there's a deed in that ledger that I stamped, and I stamped it because I was told to stamp it, and I've been telling myself for two weeks that stamps don't mean anything. That's the holiday I declared. The holiday from what stamps mean."

I put a second bill on top of the first. Then a third. It was ugly, watching my escape fund flatten itself out on a stranger's desk, but Denny Vane had taught me that money isn't money until it's moving, and right now I needed it to move toward the truth.

"The ledger," I said. "Fifteen minutes. I was never here, you never saw me, and if anyone asks, I tried to bribe you and you threw me out."

Ferro looked at the money for a long time. Then he got up, went to the door, looked both ways down the veranda with the thoroughness of a man rehearsing for his own trial, and closed it.

* * *

The ledger was a real ledger, the old-fashioned kind, a fat bound volume with a spine cracked from use, because out here the law ran on paper and diesel and not much else. Ferro laid it open on the desk and stood back like a man who'd opened a cage.

"Land transactions, upper valley, this quarter," he said. "Start at the ribbon."

The ribbon was red and it marked a page dated—my eyes went to the date and my stomach went cold in a way I wasn't expecting, because I'm a pragmatist, I deal in dates and tide tables and fuel burn rates, and this date was wrong in a way I could *prove.*

Corvane Extraction. Deed of absolute title, upper river valley, some forty kilometers of shoreline and every tributary feeding it. Consideration: nominal. Basis of conveyance: inheritance. Executed by the district magistrate under power of attorney, on behalf of the estate of Dr. Amara Osei, expedition leader, Meridian Scientific Survey, reported deceased.

Dated three days after her reported death.

I read it twice. Then a third time, because the first two times I was reading the words and the third time I was reading what the words were doing, which was wearing a suit and tie and carrying a briefcase full of nothing.

"Reported deceased," I said. "Reported by whom?"

"Corvane filed the death notification," Ferro said. His voice had gone very quiet, the voice of a man walking on ice he'd already heard crack. "An affidavit from their regional office. No body. No coroner. The magistrate accepted it—expedited acceptance, given the, ah, circumstances of the river."

"The circumstances of the river."

"Monsoon season. Things get lost." He shrugged, and the shrug was the saddest thing in the room. "Bodies, boats, jurisdiction. Everything floats downstream eventually, and by then the paper's dry."

I turned back a page, then two, looking for the death notification itself, and found it: filed, witnessed, stamped. Filed on a date. And I'm not a lawyer, I'm a storm chaser with a boat held together by tarpaulin and prayer, but I have spent my whole life reading water, and water keeps records. Water is the most honest bookkeeper there is.

"Ferro," I said. "The tide tables. Do you have them? This quarter's?"

He frowned. "The—what?"

"Tide tables. Current tables. The river gauge readings. Whatever your office uses for scheduling barge traffic." I kept my voice level, because I'd learned long ago that if you sound like a question is routine, people answer it like it's routine. "The Meridian expedition would have needed them too. Anybody moving heavy equipment on this river in monsoon season lives and dies by them."

He dug in a cabinet and produced a slim pamphlet, official issue, water-stained. I laid it open on the desk beside the ledger and did the thing I'm actually good at, the thing that has kept me alive through eleven years of chasing weather that wanted me dead: I read the numbers.

The deed was executed three days after the death notification. Fine. Grief moves fast when lawyers are involved. But the death notification was dated a day when—according to the gauge readings, according to the current tables, according to the river itself—the upper valley was in the teeth of a surge that had closed every navigable channel for six days running. I knew because I'd been sitting in a port town at the tail end of that same system, watching the barometers, listening to the same storm walk up the coast. Nothing had moved on that river for six days. No barge, no skiff, no patrol boat, no courier. The river had been a closed door.

And yet a death affidavit had traveled down it, and a power of attorney had traveled up it, and a deed had been executed in the middle, all inside a window when the only thing moving on that water was the water.

"It doesn't fit," I said.

"What doesn't fit?"

"The dates." I tapped the ledger, tapped the tide table. "You can't file papers on a river that's closed. You can't execute a deed when nobody can reach the office to execute it. Either your magistrate was doing business during a flood that shut down every boat on this river, or—" I stopped, because the end of that sentence was so clean it almost sounded naive. "Or the papers were made up first and the dates were written in after, by somebody who didn't check the weather."

Ferro stared at the two documents side by side. I watched him do the same arithmetic I'd just done, watched it land on him in stages—the way a man realizes the floor he's standing on is a ceiling over something else.

"I stamped it," he said. "I stamped all of it."

"You stamped what you were told to stamp."

"I *checked* the dates." His voice cracked on it. "That's my job. I check dates, I check signatures, I check the chain of— I checked, and everything fit, because the dates said what the dates said, and I didn't think to ask whether the *river* agreed with the dates." He laughed, one dry note with no humor in it at all. "Nobody ever taught me to check the river. The river's not a party to the transaction."

"The river's the only party that doesn't lie."

He sat down slowly, like his legs had gotten the news before the rest of him. Outside, the rain kept up its patient work on the town, on the landing, on the whole soft valley, and I stood there over the ledger feeling something I hadn't felt since I'd signed Denny Vane's papers: clarity. Cold, clean, bottom-of-the-winter-lake clarity.

This wasn't law. I'd half suspected it since the checkpoint, since the torn flag, since Ilya's rope-burned hands—but suspicion is fog and this was a shoreline. This was forgery wearing law's clothes. Somebody had killed Dr. Amara Osei—or was willing to let the river take the credit—and then dressed her corpse in a deed, and every stamp between here and the capital had pressed the costume down flat. And the beautiful, filthy part, the part that made my hands want to close around something, was that it would work. It would have worked. In a month the monsoon would have scoured the valley, the flood would have scattered the survivors and the witnesses and the evidence, and Corvane would have inherited forty kilometers of river on the strength of a dead woman's signature and a magistrate's new boat.

The only flaw in the whole machine was a tide table. The river keeps records, and nobody thought to bribe the river.

"Ferro." I kept my voice low. "I need a copy. The deed page, the death notification, and the gauge readings for those dates. Hand copies if you have to."

He looked at me like I'd asked him to hand me his own liver. "You know what they do to clerks."

"I know what they do to expeditions," I said. "I've seen the flotsam. I pulled a man out of it. He had rope burns on his wrists and half a boot, and the boot was cut, Ferro—cut clean, on purpose, by a man who decided drowning was better than going where the barge was going. That's the company your magistrate keeps. That's whose stamps you've been licking."

He was quiet for a long time. Rain on the tin roof. The constables' laughter drifting faint across the street. Somewhere a dog complaining about the weather on everyone's behalf.

"If I copy it," he said finally, "it goes in the book. There's a register. Every document that leaves this office gets entered, and if the register's checked—"

"Then don't take it out of the office." I leaned on the desk. "I read it here. You witnessed my reading. That's all. I'll write down what I need, and your register stays clean, and if anyone ever asks, a salvage captain came in asking about shipping records and you threw her out for trying to bribe you. Which is true. I did try. You threw me out. We're practically alibis for each other."

"That's not how alibis work."

"I've never had one that worked. I'm improvising."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth—not a smile, more like the memory of where a smile used to be. He pulled a sheet of paper toward himself and uncapped his pen, and for ten minutes the only sounds in the district registry office were rain and the scratch of a clerk transcribing his own complicity in careful, registry-grade handwriting. Deed of absolute title, Corvane Extraction, dated. Death notification, filed by Corvane regional office, dated. Gauge readings, current tables, closure of navigable channels, dated. Three columns of dates that could not all be true, copied out in ink that would outlast everyone who tried to pretend they were.

When he finished, he blotted the page, folded it, and held it without giving it to me.

"The magistrate goes up the hill on Thursdays," he said. "Tomorrow's Thursday. If you're still in this town tomorrow, you'll be in the ledger too. The real one. The one he keeps in his head."

"Then I'll be gone by Thursday."

"Where?"

"Upriver." I took the paper. It was warm from his hand. "There's a salvage contract, a leaking boat, a defected surveyor, and a dead woman who apparently wrote a will. I'm starting to think the salvage was never the cargo."

Ferro looked at me for a moment, and then, very quietly, like a man paying into an account he didn't expect to live to withdraw from, he said: "The mission. Up past the confluence. The old bell tower." He saw my face change and nodded. "The elders ring it when the flood's coming. Everyone in the valley knows the sound. If there's anything in this river worth saving, that's where it would be—because that's the one place the water can't take without everyone knowing it's been taken."

I folded the copy into my jacket, next to my skin, where the important things go.

"Thursday," I said. "You declared a holiday. Take it."

I walked out into the rain, and the rain didn't care about deeds or dates or the difference between law and the thing wearing its clothes, and that, I was beginning to understand, was the whole point. The river had already voted. Now I just had to get upstream before the counting was done.

Tuck was waiting under the jetty awning, and one look at my face and he said, "We're going up, aren't we."

"We're going up."

"Into the teeth of it."

"Into the teeth of it." I stepped into the boat, and my hand found the copy of the ledger through my jacket, and I thought about a dead woman's signature traveling down a river that was closed, and I said the thing I'd been not-saying since the checkpoint. "Tuck—when the current wakes, I want to be the one who told it why."
