## 9. Monsoon Season Is a Character

I have a professional relationship with water. I've spent eleven years measuring it, mapping it, predicting where it will go and selling that knowledge to people who wanted to move it out of the way of their money. Water, in my experience, is the most honest client I've ever had. It tells you exactly what it intends to do. The trouble is that nobody pays me to listen anymore.

The monsoon arrived at four in the morning, and it did not knock.

One moment we were tied up under a stand of kapok trees, Hazel sleeping in the boat with her boots on — she sleeps like a woman expecting to be robbed — and Tuck snoring against the fuel drums, and the next the sky simply came down all at once, a single sheet of it, as if someone upstream had pulled a plug the size of God's bathtub. The rain didn't fall so much as relocate. It went from cloud to river with no interest in the space between, and the sound was not rain-sound at all but a kind of pressure, a white roar that made conversation impossible and thought difficult and prayer, I imagine, very popular.

We ran for the church because Tuck had seen it on the way in — a stone mission half a kilometer back from the bank, built by somebody a century ago who understood, unlike everyone since, that rivers have moods. We carried what we could: the patched tarpaulin, two fuel drums, the radio wrapped in oilcloth, Hazel's case of documents, which she carried herself and would not let either of us touch. Nadia Okonkwo had given it back into her keeping two days ago, and I had watched Hazel hold it the way other people hold infants or grenades.

By the time we reached the church door, the path was ankle-deep and moving.

* * *

The church was flooded to the third pew. Not catastrophically — the floor sloped toward the old baptistery, and the water sat there brown and patient, reflecting the one intact window, a blue saint whose face had been worn off by weather into something anonymous and forgiving. Bats had colonized the rafters. They objected to us in small squeaking voices and then settled, deciding we were a temporary problem, which was more optimism than I could manage.

Hazel set the case on the altar rail, looked around once — exits, roof height, sightlines; the woman inventories every room like a burglar doing an appraisal — and said, "How long?"

"How long what?"

"Don't do that. Don't be coy, it doesn't suit you." She wrung out her shirt with the brisk violence of someone punishing it. "You've been looking at this rain like it owes you money. You know something about the water. So. How long?"

Tuck, dripping in the doorway, said nothing, but he turned his head slightly toward me, and I understood that in the economy of this little crew I had been quietly reclassified. Two days ago I was cargo. Now I was a source. It is remarkable how quickly a man can go from being rescued to being debriefed.

"Sit down," I said. "Both of you. This takes some explaining, and you're going to want to be sitting."

They sat on a dry pew near the back, close together, the way people sit when they've decided the person talking to them might need watching. I stood in the aisle with the floodwater lapping at my remaining boot — my left boot, the right one having been cut free of my foot by my own hand nine days ago, a story I'd already told them and did not intend to embellish — and I told them about the dam.

* * *

Here is what a surveyor knows that a soldier doesn't, and what a soldier knows that a surveyor doesn't, and why Corvane Extraction employs both and listens mostly to neither.

The upper valley narrows. That's the first fact. The river comes down through a gorge called, on my own maps, Sector 7, and on no map at all a place the locals call the Throat, and at the narrowest point of the Throat, three years ago, Corvane built a tailings dam. A tailings dam is not a real dam. It is not engineered concrete holding back a reservoir for a city to drink. It is a wall of compacted mine waste — crushed rock, silt, chemical residue — stacked up behind an earthen embankment, and its only purpose is to let a mining operation keep operating without paying to process its own garbage properly. Tailings dams are the cheapest structure in heavy industry and the deadliest. When they fail — and they fail, somewhere in the world, every year or two, always suddenly, always at night or in flood season when the warning signs are drowned out — they don't release water. They release a slurry: water, mud, and poison, moving at the speed of a freight train, and everything downstream of one is downstream of nothing afterward.

I surveyed the Throat. My instruments told me what my instruments always tell me, which is the truth: the valley walls were schist, fractured, shedding blocks every wet season; the drainage basin above the dam site collected monsoon runoff from forty square kilometers of mountainside; the spillway Corvane approved was sized for a fifty-year flood event, and the rainfall records for this valley showed a hundred-year event roughly every eleven years, because climate does not read engineering specifications.

I wrote a memo. I want that on the record, whatever else is on it. I wrote a memo saying the spillway was undersized, recommending either reconstruction or abandonment of the site, and my memo went up through channels, and channels are how corporations digest bad news: each level of management chews it a little finer until what reaches the top is flavorless and harmless. What came back down was a revised projection. The revised projection assumed lower peak rainfall. The assumption was labeled conservative. Nobody asked who had made the assumption or on what data, because the answer would have been: on the data required to make the project profitable.

That was two years ago. Since then the tailings pile behind the embankment has grown to the height of a fourteen-story building. I know because I measured it in March, on my last survey before I ran, and while I was measuring it, a section of the downstream face slumped — not failed, slumped, a soft settling sigh of maybe a meter — and the two engineers on site looked at each other, and then at me, and then at the sky, and one of them said, "Rain season'll pack it back down," and the other one laughed, and neither laugh was the laugh of a man who believed it.

"Say the number," Hazel said.

I had been circling. She has a gift for cutting circles into chords. "If the dam fails during a monsoon of this magnitude — and this is a monsoon of this magnitude, I've watched the gauge readings come off the radio band for three days —"

"You've been listening to the weather band. At night. When you think we're asleep."

"I sleep four hours a year. Yes. If it fails now, the surge reaches the confluence in roughly ninety minutes. It reaches the first villages — San Isidro, the ferry crossing, the mission itself — inside of two hours. Peak depth over this floor—" I looked at the brown water in the baptistery, serene as a held breath— "somewhere between four and six meters. It won't be clean water. It'll carry the tailings. Arsenic, cadmium, the leachate from the heap-leach pads. Everything it touches will be poisoned past recovery for a generation. The fishery dies. The wells die. The soil dies."

"And the evidence," Tuck said quietly.

I looked at him. He was staring at Hazel's case on the altar rail — Osei's true will, the unaltered surveys, the correspondence proving the forgery — and I saw him arrive at the same arithmetic I'd been carrying around in my chest for nine days like a swallowed stone.

"The evidence," I agreed. "Corvane doesn't have to burn the documents. They don't have to find us, chase us, shoot us. All they have to do is wait. The river is going to do their shredding for them. Every copy we carry drowns with us, every witness in every village downstream drowns or scatters, and when the water goes down, Corvane arrives with relief supplies and clipboards and rebuilds the valley on paper exactly the way they drew it — theirs. Ashgrave doesn't need to win. She needs the weather to hold."

* * *

The rain kept up its white roar against the roof, and for a while none of us said anything, and the bats rustled, and the saint with no face looked down at the rising water with his worn-away expression, which I chose to interpret as patience.

It was Hazel who broke it, and she broke it the way she breaks everything, directly, with the safety off.

"Ninety minutes from failure to here. How much warning do we get before failure? There must be signs."

"There are signs. Seepage on the downstream face — muddy springs where there shouldn't be any. Cracking along the crest. Instrument readings, if anyone were reading them, which —" I shrugged. "The piezometers were last calibrated eighteen months ago, and the technician who calibrated them was reassigned to the road crew because the road crew was short-handed and the dam was, quote, stable. But yes. A big failure usually announces itself. Groaning. The ground humming. Sometimes a smell — wet earth, sulfur. Sometimes animals leave beforehand. Dogs especially. Dogs are better engineers than the men who signed off on that spillway."

"So we'd hear it coming."

"You'd hear it coming. Whether you'd have time to do anything with the hearing is another question. Ninety minutes sounds generous. It isn't. Ninety minutes assumes the failure is gradual, a breach that widens over an hour. Some tailings failures are liquefaction events — the whole mass turns to fluid at once, and then the ninety minutes becomes twenty, and the twenty becomes a rumor."

Tuck rubbed his jaw. "And the villages downstream. Do they know?"

"Do they know that the mountain of poison above them was built too cheaply on ground that moves?" I heard my own voice come out flat and bitter and stopped, took a breath, started again. "They know there's a mine. They know the trucks. They know the fish got strange two years ago and the reeds below the outfall died in a straight line, which is not how nature kills things. Old Manolo at the ferry crossing told me the river 'tasted of coins.' That's iron. That's the tailings seeping already, slow, the way a wound weeps before it opens. Do they know the dam might fail? No. Nobody told them. Telling them would have been admitting the memo I wrote existed."

Hazel was quiet a moment. Then: "You wrote a memo."

"A memo."

"That said the dam was going to kill these people."

"That said the dam *might* fail. Corporations distinguish carefully between *will* and *might*. *Might* is a rounding error. *Will* is a liability."

"And then you redrew their maps anyway."

The question landed where she'd aimed it, which is her other gift. I sat down on the flooded front pew, because standing felt suddenly dishonest, and the cold water soaked through my trousers and I deserved the cold water.

"Yes," I said. "After the memo died, they offered me the inheritance project. Redraw the cadastral boundaries of the whole lower valley to match a claim document they'd obtained. I told myself it was just paper. Lines on maps. Nobody bleeds from lines on maps. And then I stood in a meeting where they discussed 'relocation logistics' for villages that were suddenly, per my beautiful new boundaries, sitting on Corvane land, and the word *logistics* was being used about grandmothers, and I understood what I'd done. Maps are not paper. Maps are instructions. Every line I drew was an instruction to men with boats and rifles about where the people were who could now be moved. Or drowned. Whatever the paperwork required."

The rain hammered. Somewhere up in the dark the river was gathering itself, and I thought about the Throat, and the fourteen stories of tailings, and the two engineers laughing their unbelieving laughs, and I said the rest of it, the part I'd been not-saying since the day they pulled me out of the water.

"I didn't run because of the forgery. I ran because of the dam. The forgery stole a valley. The dam is going to erase it. And I helped build both — the claim with my lines, the dam with my silence. So when I tell you the clock is ticking, understand that I'm not describing your problem. I'm describing my sentence."

* * *

Hazel stood, walked to the altar rail, and put her hand flat on Osei's case. Not opening it. Just touching it, the way you touch a table when you need the world to be solid.

"Then here's the arithmetic," she said. "Ashgrave wins if the dam fails before we broadcast. We win if we broadcast before the dam fails. Which means the race isn't us against her gunboats. It's everybody against the water." She turned. "How long do we have? Best guess. Surveyor's guess."

I closed my eyes and did what I do, the thing I'm actually for, the thing underneath all the compromises: I read water. Three days of gauge reports off the radio band. The rainfall curve for this basin, which I could draw from memory. The saturation state of the catchment — every slope in those mountains has been drinking for a week. The seepage I'd seen in March, extrapolated forward through two wet seasons of neglect. The slump on the face, and what slumps mean, which is that the material has begun to negotiate with gravity and negotiations of that kind only end one way.

"Days," I said. "Not weeks. The basin is saturated. Every hour of this rain adds pressure behind the embankment. If this system sits on us for another thirty-six hours the way it's sitting now, I'd put the probability of structural failure north of even. If it rains like this for five days —" I opened my eyes. "Five days isn't a scenario. Five days is a schedule."

"Then we move tomorrow," Hazel said. "First light, whatever the river's doing. Back to the mission tower, get on the radio, and read that woman's will to the entire valley before the water reads its own version."

"The river will be high," Tuck said. "High and fast and full of logs."

"The river is always full of logs," Hazel said. "I've made a career of it."

I almost smiled. Almost. Because there was one more thing, and I had been saving it the way you save the worst news for after the plan is made, so that the plan gets made at all.

"There's a complication," I said.

Hazel looked at me with an expression I was beginning to know well, the expression of a woman confirming that the universe remained on brand. "Of course there is."

"The dam failing doesn't just drown the evidence. It drowns the *witnesses*. Four thousand people live between the Throat and the sea. If we spend our ninety minutes broadcasting from a tower, we're not spending them getting people to high ground. And most of the high ground—" I gestured upward, at the rain, at the whole drowning sky— "is upstream. Toward the dam. Toward the thing they don't know is hanging over them. Somebody has to warn them, Hazel. And somebody has to broadcast. And we are four people and one leaking boat."

The bats resettled overhead. The water in the baptistery had risen another finger's width since we arrived; I noticed it the way I notice everything about water now, compulsively, the way a gambler counts cards he wishes he'd never learned.

Hazel looked at the case, and at the window, and at the brown rising water wearing the saint's reflection like a mask.

"Then we'd better hope," she said, "that the valley's been paying attention longer than we have."

Outside, beneath the rain, far upriver in the dark, something groaned. It was a long, low sound, felt more than heard, rolling down the current like a knuckle dragged along the spine of the valley — and all three of us went still, and the dogs of the nearest village began, one by one, to howl.
