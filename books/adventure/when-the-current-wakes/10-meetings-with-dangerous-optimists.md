## 10. Meetings with Dangerous Optimists

The satellite phone found me at the worst possible moment, which is the only time satellite phones ever call.

We'd tied up in a flooded copse of nipa palms about two hours past the fishing village, Tuck rigging a tarp over the fuel drums while Ilya stood hip-deep in brown water pretending to check the hull patch. The patch was holding. The patch was always holding, right up until it wasn't. I was eating cold rice out of a tin and doing arithmetic I didn't like—fuel burn against river miles against the number of days since anyone had paid me for anything—when the phone buzzed against my chest pocket like a hornet with a business degree.

Nobody calls that phone. That's the whole point of it. It's for emergencies, contracts, and creditors, which is to say it's for three flavors of the same animal.

I looked at the screen. I knew the number. You know your dentist's number. You know your mother's number. And if you owe the Vane brothers money, you know their number too, because Denny Vane calls every month on the first like clockwork, cheerful as a man reading lottery numbers, to tell you how much deeper you've dug.

It wasn't the first of the month.

That's what made my hands go cold before I even answered. Denny Vane calling off-schedule meant something had changed in the ledger. In my experience, when something changes in a ledger, it changes in your favor exactly never.

"Hey there, Hazel." Warm as bathwater, bright as a coin fresh from the mint. "You're coming through clear. Love that. Love good signal quality."

"Denny." I put him on speaker so Tuck could hear, because whatever this was, I wasn't carrying it alone. "You're off your calendar."

"I am! I am off my calendar." A laugh, easy, unhurried. Somewhere behind his voice I could hear the port—the gulls, the diesel, the forever sound of money changing hands at a discount. "You know how it is. Things move. Opportunities arise. Somebody makes an offer on an asset, and suddenly you're not a lender anymore, you're a partner. I love being a partner, Hazel. Partnerships are where the real warmth is."

I set down the rice tin. "What asset."

"Oh, come on." He said it the way you'd chide a friend for false modesty. "Don't make me say it. It feels rude when I have to say it. Your paper, Hazel. Your father's loan. All eleven thousand and change of it, plus accrued interest, plus the fees we've been so patient about." A pause, savoring. "Somebody bought it. Whole thing. This morning. Wire transfer cleared before lunch."

Tuck stopped working on the tarp. Ilya went very still in the water, which for a man who claimed to be checking a hull was a hell of a tell.

"Who," I said.

"And there she goes, straight to business. That's why I like you, Hazel. No small talk, no feelings, just the numbers." He let the silence stretch long enough that I could hear him smiling through it. "Corvane Extraction. Big outfit. Mining, mostly, some logistics, some—well, let's call it community relations. They've got interests upriver. Which, hey, funny thing, is where you are right now. Small world. Rivers make it smaller."

I looked at Ilya. Ilya looked back at me, and I watched a man do math he'd already done once and gotten an answer he hated.

"When did they buy it," I said. Not who. When. Because I'd pulled Ilya Reyes out of the water four days ago, and I wanted to know how long Corvane had known exactly where I'd be.

"Day before yesterday, give or take the time zones." Denny said it lightly, but he heard the question under the question—he always did; that was the whole Vane gift, hearing the debt inside the words. "Now, before you get paranoid—and I say this with love, Hazel, genuine love—nobody bought your paper because of anything you've done. They bought it because of what you might do. That's just smart business. You're a known quantity. You find things. You're currently finding something for a broker who, between you and me, has been subcontracting for Corvane longer than either of us would find comfortable. So really, this is a compliment. You're an appreciating asset."

"You don't own me, Denny."

"No, no. God, no. Nobody owns anybody, that's—" another laugh, delighted, genuinely delighted, which was the worst part, "—that's barbaric thinking, Hazel. We're way past ownership. What I have is a *relationship* with your obligations. And now Corvane has a relationship with them too. We're all connected. Like family. Like a river."

Ilya waded toward the boat. Tuck mouthed something at me that I chose to interpret as *keep him talking*.

"What do they want, Denny."

"There it is. See, this is why partnerships work." Papers rustled on his end—I could picture his office, the model ships, the framed photo of him and his brother Marty shaking hands with somebody whose face was always turned just out of frame. "Simple ask. Beautifully simple. You've got a man aboard. Ilya Reyes. Former Corvane surveyor, current—well, current whatever he is. Defector, I guess, if we're being dramatic, and I love being dramatic. He walked off with property that isn't his. Original survey plates. Field documentation. Very valuable paper, Hazel. You of all people understand valuable paper."

My eyes went to Ilya's pack, sitting in the bilge where it had sat since the day I fished him out. I'd never gone through it. Not thoroughly. Some part of me had decided that a man's bag was a man's bag, and that line had felt like the last clean one I owned.

"He's a person, Denny."

"He's a rounding error with legs," Denny said cheerfully, and then, catching himself, "okay, that came out wrong. He's a person. Absolutely. But here's the beautiful part, and I want you to really sit with this: everybody wins. You bring him in—alive, comfortable, unharmed, we're not monsters—you hand over the plates, and Corvane retires your debt. All of it. Every cent. Eleven thousand, interest, fees, the works. Gone. Zeroed. You walk away free, Hazel. Free. Do you know how long it's been since you were free? Since before your dad died, if I'm doing my math, and I always do my math."

I did the math too. It took half a second. My freedom, priced against one wet surveyor and a satchel of maps. It wasn't even a hard number. That was the obscene part—that it was easy. That somewhere in a glass office, someone had run the calculation and found that erasing Ilya Reyes cost less than the interest I'd accrue by Christmas.

"That's not a partnership," I said. "That's a bounty with extra steps."

"It's *arithmetic*, Hazel." And there it was—the real voice, underneath the cheer, the one the Vanes kept folded up in a drawer for moments like this. Still friendly. Still warm. But flat as a flooded plain. "And arithmetic doesn't care what we call it. Look. I'm going to be straight with you, because I respect you and because Marty says I'm too soft, which, ha. If you bring him in, great. Wonderful. Everybody's happy, I buy the drinks. But if you *don't*—if you decide to get sentimental, or heroic, or whatever it is you storm-chaser types do instead of making good decisions—then nothing bad happens to you today. Nothing violent. We're not that kind of operation, and neither are they, mostly. What happens is the interest keeps running. Daily. Compounding. And Corvane holds your paper now, and Corvane is very patient, and very thorough, and they have lawyers in four countries who can make a woman's life into a very long, very slow form-filling exercise. Boats impounded at every port. Licenses suspended. Accounts frozen pending review. You'd drown in paperwork before you ever drowned in water, and believe me, that's the worse way to go."

He paused, and let the kindness come back into his voice like a shopkeeper dusting off the display.

"But you're not going to do that. Are you? Because you're smart. Smartest client I've got, and I've got clients who own actual islands. You'll do the sensible thing. You always do. That stopping-costs-more instinct of yours? It's the best thing about you, Hazel. It's why you're still alive, and it's why your old man trusted us with his signature." A beat. "Give me a day. Call me back tomorrow, same number. Just say yes or say no. No pressure. Well. Some pressure. There's always some pressure, that's physics."

"Denny."

"Yeah, hon?"

"If I say yes, what happens to him?"

Another pause. Longer. Long enough that I heard him decide whether to lie, and then decide lying wasn't worth the effort, because I'd hear it anyway.

"Hazel. Come on. Don't ask questions you already know the answers to. It's bad for both of us."

He hung up before I could. Of course he did. Denny Vane never ended a conversation you could win.

* * *

For a while nobody said anything. The rain came down soft and steady on the tarp, drumming the kind of rhythm that makes you sleepy if you let it, and the river moved past us in the dark like it had somewhere better to be.

Ilya climbed aboard without being asked. He sat down across from me, dripping, and didn't reach for his pack. That was its own kind of answer. He'd heard enough, standing in the water, to know exactly what was in the balance, and he sat there anyway, hands open on his knees, waiting to see what kind of person I'd turn out to be.

"So," he said finally. "Eleven thousand."

"Eleven thousand and change."

"My life is worth eleven thousand dollars." He said it without self-pity, which somehow made it worse. He turned it over like a coin he was testing for counterfeit. "I drew the maps that moved three villages. I redrew boundaries so that a graveyard became 'unencumbered industrial land.' I knew the names of every family on those sheets, Hazel. I wrote the names myself, in the margin notes. And I am worth less than your boat."

"The boat's worth maybe six."

"Then I'm worth half a boat." He almost smiled. "Ashgrave would pay more. That's the difference between us and them. They price things honestly."

Tuck crouched at the gunwale, spat thoughtfully into the water. "Could sell the plates ourselves," he offered. "Broker downstream. Cut out the middlemen."

"They'd know within a week," Ilya said. "The plates are traceable. Serialized. And the moment Corvane knows they're loose, every fixer on this river starts hunting us, and we stop being people and start being cargo." He looked at me. "She won't sell. She's already decided. I can see her deciding."

He was wrong. I hadn't decided anything. That was the truth of it, the ugly little truth I kept turning over: I hadn't decided, because not deciding felt like it kept my hands clean, and keeping my hands clean was the one luxury I'd been telling myself I couldn't afford for eleven years.

Here's what nobody tells you about debt. Everyone talks about the money. The money's the least of it. The money is just the hook. What debt actually does is rent out your future decisions, one at a time, to whoever holds the paper. Every choice you make, you make twice—once for yourself, and once for the ledger. And after enough years of that, you stop being able to tell which want is yours. I'd told myself I took the salvage contract because stopping cost more. Sitting in that flooded grove, listening to the rain, I finally asked whether that had ever been true, or whether it was just the sentence Denny Vane had taught me to say so I could keep calling my cage a heading.

"Say I hand you over," I said to Ilya. "Debt dies. I'm free. Clean slate, new season, whole horizon." I watched his face while I said it, and he didn't flinch, didn't plead, just received it like a man reading his own obituary to check the spelling. "Say I don't. Then I spend the rest of my life paying eleven thousand dollars at whatever rate the Vanes feel like charging, and Corvane spends the rest of *your* life doing whatever Ashgrave does to surveyors who grow consciences. Those are the options. That's the menu."

"Not quite," Ilya said quietly.

"Enlighten me."

"There's a third option. It's the one Osei picked." He nodded upstream, toward the dark, toward the valley, toward whatever was left of the Meridian expedition and the woman who'd refused to sign. "She had money. She had a company that would have made her rich. All she had to do was put her name on their paper and everything she owed—her funding, her career, her expedition—would have been forgiven. One signature. She wouldn't do it. She hid the real documents instead, and it killed her, and everyone's been treating that like a tragedy ever since." His jaw tightened. "Maybe it wasn't a tragedy. Maybe it was a quote. A price list. Here's what a clean name costs, and here's what it buys."

"She's dead," Tuck said. "Forgive me, but dead doesn't spend well."

"Dead spends fine," I said, "if you spend it right."

I picked up the satellite phone. Its weight in my hand was familiar as a tool, hateful as a chain. Tomorrow, same number. Yes or no. Denny would be waiting with his cheerful voice and his clean arithmetic, and whichever word I gave him, I'd be giving it for the rest of my life—one way, I'd spend it looking over my shoulder at a ledger; the other way, I'd spend it knowing I'd sold a man for the price of my own reflection.

Except.

Except there was a third entry on the menu, and it was sitting in the bilge in a canvas pack, and it was heavier than survey plates had any right to be. Ilya saw me looking at it. He didn't move to protect it. He just reached over, unclipped the buckle, and turned the pack toward me like a man laying cards face-up.

"Before you decide," he said, "you should see what she actually surveyed. What the forgery was covering up. Why Ashgrave needs these back so badly she'd buy a stranger's debt to get them." He pulled the first plate free of its oilcloth, and even in the dark, even by the single lantern, I could see the lines on it were wrong—not wrong like mistakes. Wrong like secrets. "Because it isn't rare earths, Hazel. It was never rare earths. Dr. Osei found something else under this valley, and Corvane's entire claim—the deed, the magistrate, all of it—is built on making sure nobody reads what's on this sheet."

Rain on the tarp. River in the dark. Somewhere upstream, faint as a heartbeat through water, I swear I felt the dam shift.

"Well," I said, and reached for the plate. "Show me what I'm deciding about."
