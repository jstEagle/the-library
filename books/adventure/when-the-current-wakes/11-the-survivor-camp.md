## 11. The Survivor Camp

The coordinates Dr. Amara Osei had broadcast eleven days ago put her last known position at a limestone wall rising out of the flooded forest like the spine of something drowned and unfinished. Karst country. The river here didn't flow so much as seep sideways through a thousand green throats, channels splitting and rejoining until a chart was more suggestion than fact.

"Nobody lives out here," Tuck said, cutting the engine so we drifted on current alone. "Too much rock, not enough bank."

"People live everywhere there's water," I said. "That's the whole problem with people."

Ilya stood at the bow with Osei's last transmission printed out and folded into quarters he'd worried soft as cloth. He'd been quiet since the church, since his lecture on tailings dams and hydrology and the particular way Corvane's arithmetic treated human beings as rounding errors. Quiet in the way of a man who has said his piece and is now waiting to find out whether anyone believed him.

"You're sure about this?" I asked him.

"I'm sure about the coordinates." He didn't turn around. "I'm not sure about anything else. That's why I'm still alive."

We poled the last half mile. The boat drew too much water for the channels, and every few minutes the hull gave that soft wet cough it had developed since the patch job—tarpaulin and prayer, exactly as advertised—and I found myself talking to it under my breath the way you talk to an old dog you're not ready to lose yet.

Tuck noticed. "You know it can't hear you."

"It hears everything," I said. "It just doesn't care. Like most of my creditors."

* * *

We found the cave by accident, which is how you find most things worth finding. A channel narrowed between two walls of vine-hung stone, and where the vines thinned there was a seam of darkness at the waterline, maybe four meters wide, and above it—faint enough that I'd have missed it if I hadn't been looking—a hand-painted mark. White paint, three horizontal strokes.

"That's Meridian's survey code," Ilya said sharply. "Checkpoint marker. It means *verified safe harbor.*"

"It means somebody wanted to be found by the right people," Tuck said, "and eaten by the wrong ones. You sure we're the right ones?"

"No," I said. "But we're here."

I went in alone. Not because I'm brave—I've met brave, brave dies young and leaves a beautiful corpse and no estate—but because if there were guns behind that seam of dark, I'd rather they belonged to one debt-ridden salvage captain than to all three of us. Tuck called me an idiot in the affectionate register he saves for occasions when I'm being an idiot correctly.

The cave swallowed the daylight in about ten meters. Then my eyes adjusted, and I stopped breathing for a second, because forty meters in, lit by a single camp lantern turned down to a coal-glow, four people were pointing rifles at me.

Three men and a woman. The woman stood slightly forward, rifle held low and steady, the way you hold a weapon when you've decided not to use it but want everyone to know the decision could be revised. She was maybe thirty, thin in the specific way of someone who'd been rationing herself for weeks, with a surgical scar of white light across her cheekbone where something had opened her face and healed crooked.

"Hazel Sandoval," I said. "Salvage contract out of Port Anselm. Looking for the Meridian expedition."

"We're what's left of it," she said. "Nadia Okonkwo. Put your hands where I can see them and answer one question."

"Ask."

"What did Dr. Osei call the karst formations north of the third confluence?"

I blinked. "How would I—"

"She sent a message to a broker in Port Anselm. The broker hired you. If she trusted the broker, the broker passed along a verification phrase. If he didn't—" Nadia's rifle came up six centimeters, which is a whole conversation at gunpoint. "Then you're Corvane's, and this cave stays a secret."

"I don't have any phrase," I said. "Marek—the broker—he gave me coordinates, a fee schedule, and a speech about how the upper river eats radios. That was the whole briefing. I got more detail from the fuel receipt."

Behind me, from the mouth of the cave, Ilya's voice carried in off the water: "Nadia. It's Reyes. Ilya Reyes. Tell them the phrase yourself—you'll see if I remember it."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight and temperature.

"*Sable heron,*" Ilya called. "*Resting on one leg.*"

One of the men made a sound that might have been a laugh or might have been the beginning of crying. The lantern swung up. Nadia looked past me toward the entrance for a long moment, and whatever she saw in Ilya's face—or remembered about it—lowered her rifle three centimeters, which at that range was practically an embrace.

"Get him inside," she said. "And then, Captain Sandoval, you're going to tell me exactly what Marek told you, because if Corvane knows those coordinates too, we have about a day before this cave stops being a secret."

* * *

The camp was four people, two tents, a rain-catchment tarp, a cook fire burning driftwood so smokeless it was nearly theoretical, and a wall of the cave covered in charcoal notes arranged with the obsessive neatness of a mind that organizes even its terror. Dates. Patrol sightings. Water levels, marked twice daily in a column that climbed like a fever chart.

The four survivors were Nadia; a geologist named Bram whose left arm hung in a sling improvised from a shirt; a hydrologist named Okoye who hadn't spoken since I arrived and communicated through nods and a notebook; and a kid—twenty at most—called Fen, who'd been the expedition's boat mechanic and now spent his days keeping their one hidden skiff alive with parts cannibalized from nothing.

They'd been hiding for nineteen days. Eleven since the last radio transmission. Before that, they'd been running.

"Tell me what happened," I said. "From the beginning. All of it."

Nadia sat across the fire from me, and I watched her decide how much truth I could carry. People do that. They weigh you like cargo.

"You know who Dr. Osei was?"

"Expedition lead. Hydrogeologist. Famous enough that even I've heard of her, and I don't read science unless it's about weather."

"She was famous for refusing things." Nadia almost smiled. "She refused funding from three companies before Meridian. She refused to publish preliminary findings early. And when Corvane Extraction offered her—conservatively—more money than the rest of the expedition would earn in our lifetimes, to sign off on a mineral survey of this valley, she refused that too. Politely. In writing. She kept the correspondence." Nadia's jaw tightened. "She kept everything. She was a scientist. Documentation was her religion."

"And Corvane wanted the valley."

"Corvane wanted the rare-earth deposits under the valley. There's a body of ore through these karst systems that would make them a player in every supply chain that matters for the next thirty years. But the valley isn't empty. Fishing villages, farming communities, a mission settlement that's been here a hundred and sixty years. You can't strip-mine a valley full of people without either moving them or making them disappear into statistics. So Corvane needed legal cover. They needed the land to belong to them on paper before anyone asked questions."

"And Osei's survey was the obstacle."

"Osei's survey was the *map.*" Nadia leaned forward. "Her mineral surveys were the only complete ones in existence. Her boundary documentation established that the communities here hold de facto tenure going back generations—she'd spent two years building that case for a land-trust proposal. Without her surveys, Corvane's claim is a guess. With them, it's indefensible. So they needed her signature, her data, or her silence."

"They got her death instead."

"They got a forgery wearing her death." Nadia's voice didn't rise. That was the frightening thing about it—it stayed level, flat as still water, while everything underneath moved. "Eleven days ago we picked up a syndicate transmission relayed through the magistrate's office. A deed of transfer, dated three days after a reported boating accident. Signed *A. Osei.* Dated after her death, Captain. Transferring her 'personal holdings' in the valley—which she never had—to Corvane as 'inherited extraction rights.' They built a fake inheritance around a dead woman and used her own name as the signature."

I thought of the ledger in the magistrate's office, the dates that didn't fit the tide tables. Cold clarity is supposed to feel clarifying. Mostly it felt like swallowing ice water.

"The accident," I said. "Was there an accident?"

"There was a night," Nadia said, "when Dr. Osei took the survey launch upriver to move the documentation, and a patrol skiff with its running lights off came out of the rain at ramming speed, and Corporal Ennis—who I am told by a very reliable source recently grew a conscience—was on that skiff." She looked at me. "Your mechanic friend hasn't stopped staring at me since you walked in. He has Ennis's patrol schedule memorized. So yes. I know your defector is aboard. We'll discuss what that's worth later."

Fen, the kid, spoke for the first time. "Dr. Osei didn't drown."

Everyone looked at him. He was turning a wrench over in his hands like a worry stone.

"She washed ashore near the fishing village. Third bend. The villagers pulled her out. She was alive for two days." His voice cracked and he pushed through it. "I know because I was there. I'd gone to trade for carburetor parts when the patrol hit our camp. I ran downstream instead of upstream. Wrong instinct, right luck. The villagers hid me too. She knew she wasn't going to make it—her lungs were full of river. So she talked. For two days she talked, and I wrote down everything, and the village elders helped us move the documentation to the mission bell tower because it's the highest ground and the strongest walls and—" He stopped. Looked at Nadia. "I haven't told you all of it. I'm sorry. I was waiting until—"

"Until what?" Nadia said, but gently.

"Until someone came who could actually *do* something."

* * *

What Osei had done, in her last two days of breath, was rewrite her will.

Not the forged one—she never signed anything for Corvane, dead or alive. The real one. Dictated to Fen, witnessed by three village elders, sealed with the mission's old wax stamp that hadn't been used since the colonial administration left. In it, she did three things.

First, she formally released her entire body of research—every survey, every map, every boundary study—into public record, to be held in trust by the valley's communities. Not sold. Not licensed. Given.

Second, she named the forgery. She dictated a point-by-point refutation of the deed Corvane would eventually produce, including the tide-table discrepancy, the impossible date sequence, and the fact that her actual signature had a tremor in the downstroke of the *O* from an old nerve injury—which the forged version, executed by someone with a steady hand, conspicuously lacked.

Third, she stated—in language Fen recited from memory, word for word, because he'd heard it enough times—that the valley's mineral wealth was not hers to give and never had been, and that any legitimate claim to it belonged to the people who lived on top of it, and that she hoped they'd be smarter about it than the rest of the world had been.

"She willed the valley to itself," Nadia said quietly, when Fen finished. "And she hid the physical documents—the will, the true surveys, the correspondence proving the forgery—in the bell tower. Which is also the flood refuge for four villages, which means when the dam goes, the evidence drowns with everything else unless someone moves it first."

I sat with that. Around me the cave dripped its slow limestone clock. Outside, the monsoon worked the surface of the world like a man kneading dough.

"You understand what you're asking," I said finally. "The bell tower is forty kilometers back the way we came. Through patrol water. With a patched hull and a crew of five and a dam upstream that's groaning like my knees."

"I'm not asking you to do anything, Captain." Nadia's eyes were dry and very bright. "I'm telling you what exists. What you do with it is your ledger, not mine."

"That's the problem with ledgers," I said. "Everything gets written in them eventually."

Okoye, the silent hydrologist, slid his notebook across to me. On the page was a hand-drawn cross-section of the tailings dam, annotated in a cramped engineer's hand, with a date circled. Nine days out. His estimate of structural failure, given current rainfall.

Nine days. Maybe less. I'd been listening to water my whole life, and the river outside had started saying something new in the last day—a deeper note underneath the rain, patient and enormous, like a debt accruing.

* * *

Later, while Tuck argued engine theology with Fen and Ilya sat apart from everyone, being carefully forgiven in small doses, Nadia brought me the field journal.

Osei's actual journal. Waterproof casing swollen with river water, pages warped but legible, handwriting that started precise and ended shaky. Nadia held it out and I took it the way you take something that might be hot.

"Read the last entry," she said.

I read it. It was short.

*If you are reading this, I did not get to choose the manner of my leaving, but I chose everything else. The valley keeps its own accounts. Do not let anyone—not Corvane, not the magistrate, not well-meaning strangers with boats—decide for the people here what they inherit. Put the truth where they can reach it. Then get out of the way.*

There was a postscript, added in different ink, in Fen's younger hand:

*She made me promise to write that down exactly. She said, "Someone will come up this river eventually. Someone who reads water. Make sure they understand the assignment."*

I closed the journal. My hands weren't entirely steady, which annoyed me.

"For the record," I said, "I came up this river because stopping costs more than going."

"I know," Nadia said. "She'd have liked you anyway. She had a weakness for people who pretend their motives are worse than they are."

Outside, thunder rolled down the valley from the direction of the dam, long and low, and nobody in the cave flinched, because we'd all been hearing it for hours and pretending it was weather.

Nadia started rolling up her maps. "Captain. How fast can that patched hull of yours run at night?"

I looked at the water climbing the gauge marks on the cave wall, and I did the arithmetic I was already going to do anyway.

"Faster than the river wants," I said. "Which is the only speed that matters."
