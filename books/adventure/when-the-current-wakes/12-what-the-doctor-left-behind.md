## 12. What the Doctor Left Behind

The cave smelled like bat guano and wet limestone and, faintly, like Dr. Amara Osei's insect repellent—that citronella-and-chemistry smell I'd spent four months breathing on the *Meridian*, and which had outlived her the way a perfume outlives its wearer, clinging to the canvas of her old field tent where Nadia Okonkwo now slept and rationed her grief into portions small enough to swallow.

I was sitting cross-legged on a crate of dried fish, holding a dead man's compass, when Hazel Sandoval asked me the question.

"Where is it?" she said. "The will. The real surveys. Whatever she left that Corvane wants badly enough to hunt four scientists through monsoon country."

I could have answered immediately. I'd known for eleven days—known since the night on the ridge when we watched Osei's boat get swallowed by searchlights and engine noise, and I understood, with the particular clarity of someone watching their last exit close, that everything Amara had done in her final week had been done for an audience that hadn't arrived yet.

But I made them wait. Not for drama. For arithmetic.

There were four of us survivors, and now five strangers with a leaking boat and a defected syndicate surveyor, and every piece of information I gave away was a piece I couldn't take back. Tuck had told me about the river, about how the upper water ate radios first. Information worked the same way. Once you broadcast it, you didn't own it anymore.

So I looked at Hazel Sandoval—storm chaser, debtor, woman who'd pulled Ilya Reyes out of the river because a liability floating was worse than a liability aboard—and I said:

"Before I tell you, I want to know what you'll do with it."

Hazel's mouth did something complicated. "That depends on what it is."

"No," I said. "It doesn't. You already know what it is. A forged deed, a dead scientist, a valley full of people being relocated off land they've fished for six generations. The only question is whether you're the kind of person who takes the documents and sells them to the highest bidder, or the kind who reads them out loud." I nodded at Ilya, hunched by the cave mouth with his rope-burned wrists wrapped in gauze. "He drew the maps. He can tell you what Corvane does with people who read things out loud."

"I know what they do," Ilya said quietly. "I did some of it."

"Then you understand why I ask."

* * *

Here is what I have learned about inheritance, in the four months since I signed onto the Meridian expedition as Dr. Osei's field assistant: it is not what you're given. It's what you're trusted to carry.

Amara Osei carried things the way rivers carry silt—continuously, invisibly, depositing them exactly where they'd do the most good over decades nobody would live to see. She was sixty-one years old and built like a question mark from thirty years of leaning over core samples, and she had a habit I found maddening until I understood it: she never explained herself in advance. She'd hand you a task—a soil assay here, a water sample there, go interview the elders at Kilometre Nine about flood seasons—and you'd do it, and only weeks later would the purpose assemble itself behind your eyes like a photograph developing.

"Dr. Osei," I said once, three weeks before she died, "why are we mapping the tailings seepage if our contract is rare-earth prospecting?"

We were on the aft deck of the *Meridian*, rain hammering the canopy, and she was eating rice out of a tin plate with the concentration other people reserved for defusing bombs.

"Because contracts," she said, "are the first draft. Somebody else writes the second one. Your job is to make sure the second draft can't erase you."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only kind worth giving." She scraped her plate clean and looked at me with those eyes that had catalogued forty years of landscapes going wrong. "Nadia. What do you think happens to this valley when Corvane finishes its feasibility study?"

"They mine it. The rare earths are—" I'd run the numbers myself; they were extraordinary. "The deposits are world-class. They'll strip the east bank."

"And the people?"

"Relocated. Compensated. It's in the standard framework—"

"The standard framework," Amara said, "was written by people who have never once been relocated themselves. Write that down somewhere you won't lose it."

I wrote it in my field journal, between a pH reading and a sketch of a kingfisher. I still have the journal. It's in my jacket right now, wrapped in plastic, and it has survived drowning, a machete, and Corporal Ennis's patrol searching our camp twice. Some sentences refuse to be lost.

* * *

Three weeks before she died—the same conversation, later that night, under the awning while the crew slept—Amara told me about the offer.

Not all of it. She gave me the shape of it, the way she gave me everything: enough to act on, never enough to make me complicit before I chose to be.

"Corvane's regional director came upriver on the barge," she said. "A woman named Ashgrave. Very reasonable. Excellent wine, which she drank very little of, which frightened me more than the armed men on deck."

"What did she offer?"

"A chair. Endowed research position, my name on a building, full funding for ten years. And authorship credit on the environmental impact statement—which would have been my signature on their relocation plan, dressed up as science." Amara smiled, and it was the saddest smile I have ever seen on a living face. "She'd done her homework. She knew I was tired. Sixty-one, bad knees, a career of watching good data get buried under better politics. She offered me a comfortable way to stop fighting."

"And you said no."

"I said no slowly, so she'd believe it cost me something. People trust refusals that look expensive." She turned her tin cup in her hands. "Then she made a second offer, and that one wasn't reasonable at all. That one had a date attached. My death, effectively, scheduled in triplicate."

"You're joking."

"I am many things, Nadia, but a joker in matters of corporate scheduling is not one of them." She stood, knees cracking, and looked upriver toward the dark bulk of the tailings dam, invisible beyond the bend but present the way a tumor is present—by implication. "Ashgrave's problem is that I've already finished the work. The surveys are done. The assays are done. And I've found something in the hydrology that makes their entire operation a mass-casualty event wearing a hard hat. That dam upstream is failing. Not eventually—soon. The seepage rates tripled between March and June. When it goes, it takes the tailings pond with it, and everything downstream drowns in arsenic slurry, including every village Corvane hasn't yet managed to relocate. Including, incidentally, most of the evidence."

"So they need you dead before you publish."

"They need me dead before the dam fails, whichever comes first. I intend to win that race." She patted my shoulder, once, briskly. "Go sleep. Tomorrow we go to the mission, and you're going to help me hide my life's work inside a bell tower."

* * *

Present tense. The cave. Rain doing its monsoon impression against the karst, which swallowed the sound and gave back a low drumming, like the whole valley was a skin being played by an enormous patient drummer.

"—the mission bell tower," I finished. "Sainte-Anne-de-la-Rivière. Built 1911, abandoned as a parish in the seventies, but the villagers kept it up because it sits on high ground above the flood line. Every family in the valley has a memory of sheltering there during high water. It's the refuge. Nobody builds anything else near it, nobody quarries near it, and the tower foundation is older than any deed Corvane could forge."

Hazel had stopped pretending to check her boat supplies twenty minutes ago. "She hid the documents in a flood shelter."

"In the single most protected building in the valley, guarded not by guns but by six generations of habit." I allowed myself the smallest smile. "Amara thought in systems, Ms. Sandoval. Guns get bought. Habits don't."

Tuck, from his seat against the wall: "How'd she get access? Village like that, strangers don't just climb bell towers."

"She earned it." I said it flatly, because it was simply true. "Four months of showing up. She treated the malaria cases at Kilometre Nine when the government clinic ran dry. She mapped the fishing grounds for free—not for Corvane, for the co-op, so they'd have documentation when the syndicate started claiming traditional waters as 'survey zones.' She sat through two funerals and a wedding and learned enough of the river dialect to be dangerous at cards." I shrugged. "By July, Père Anselme—he keeps the keys, he's eighty-three and sharp as a machete—would have given her the tower if she'd asked. But she didn't ask. She traded for it."

"Traded what?"

"A promise. That whatever she left there would belong to the valley. Not to Meridian, not to any university, not to her estate. To the people who lived downstream of that dam." I looked around the cave—at Hazel, at Tuck, at Ilya with his ruined wrists, at our other two survivors, young Okoro shivering through a fever he was too stubborn to admit to. "You understand what that means? She wasn't hiding evidence. She was writing a will. The legal kind came later—there's a sealed document, her actual testamentary will, drawn up with a notary in the port town, witnessed by two elders. But the real will was the arrangement itself. The tower. The promise. The choice she handed forward."

Ilya spoke without lifting his head. "What choice?"

"Whether to use it." I let that sit. "Corvane thinks inheritance is a thing you seize. Ashgrave bought a magistrate, forged a deed dated three days after Amara's reported death—I've seen the timeline, it doesn't survive contact with the tide tables—and she believes that paper plus patience equals ownership. Amara's counter-move was to make ownership impossible. The surveys prove the mineral claims were discovered on customary land under expedition permits that named the valley communities as stakeholders. The correspondence proves the forgery. And the will—" my voice caught, just slightly, and I hated it, and continued, "—the will assigns her personal rights in the discovery to the valley's inhabitants collectively. Not as compensation. As inheritance. Her words: 'to be stewarded, not sold.'"

The rain filled the silence.

Hazel was quiet for a long moment. Then, wryly, in that voice of hers that treated catastrophe as a logistics problem: "So the woman hid a legal time bomb in a church, set it to go off whenever somebody brave enough climbed the stairs, and then got herself murdered before she could press the button."

"She wasn't murdered in the tower," I said. "And she didn't get murdered. She was hunted down on open water by men in company colors while her radio called coordinates that matched the mission's approach channel to within two hundred meters." I met Hazel's eyes. "Her last transmission wasn't a distress call. It was directions. She died steering us toward the safe."

Nobody said anything for a while. Down in the flooded dark below the cave ledge, the river moved with the sound of something enormous turning over in its sleep.

* * *

Later—when the others had taken what rest the bats permitted—I sat beside Hazel at the cave mouth and gave her the operational details, because she'd earned them by not asking twice.

"The case is sealed. Lead-lined document tube inside a steel ammunition box, welded shut—Amara had the mission's old generator and a welding kit, and Père Anselme's nephew did the work. Inside: the original unaltered survey plates, the correspondence file, the notarized will, and a copy of the hydrology report on the dam with Amara's margin notes. The notes alone would end Ashgrave's career—she signed off on the seepage assessments knowing the numbers were falsified."

"Where in the tower?"

"Bell chamber. Under the floor plating, in the cavity where the old clock mechanism used to mount. Accessible from inside, invisible from outside, and the bell still rings—Père Anselme rings it when the river passes the third marker stone. Flood warning. Which means when the dam finally lets go, half the valley will be standing in that tower, directly above the documents that could save them, and not one of them will know."

Hazel chewed on that. "So we're not just retrieving papers. We're on a clock."

"We're always on a clock. That's the monsoon's whole personality." I pulled the field journal from my jacket and opened it to the page with the tide tables and the forged deed dates, the arithmetic I'd done eleven times hoping it would come out differently. "But yes. Two clocks, actually. The dam, and Ashgrave. She knows the documents exist—Ilya's defection told her that much—and she knows where they are, because Amara's last known course pointed at the mission. She'll have gunboats on that bend within a day of the weather lifting."

"Then we move tomorrow. First light, such as it is." Hazel said it lightly, but her hands were checking the boat's patch seams again, the way she'd been doing every hour since the tarpaulin repair, and I recognized the gesture. It wasn't anxiety. It was a person making peace with a machine she intended to bet her life on.

"One more thing," I said. "When we get there. When you hold that case. I need to hear you say what you'll do with it."

Hazel looked out at the black water. Somewhere downriver, a patrol engine coughed and died, coughed again, gave up—Corvane running low on fuel, Tuck had noticed, running low on everything except certainty.

"I'm a debt collector's problem," she said finally, "with a leaking boat and no plan past Tuesday. But I've read enough ledgers lately to know a forged entry when I see one." She turned back to me, and there was nothing wry in her face at all. "I'll read it out loud. All of it. On every frequency that tower's radio can reach. And then I'll hand copies to whoever in this valley wants to carry them, and I'll get out of the way, because it was never mine and it was never yours either. It's theirs."

I believed her. That surprised me more than anything else that had happened in eleven days.

"Good," I said. "Amara would have liked you. She collected people the way she collected samples—only kept the ones that tested true."

"And what did I test as?"

"Unpriced," I said. "It's rarer than the rare earths. Don't spend it."

Below us, far up the black valley, the dam groaned—once, deep, structural, a sound I felt in my sternum before I heard it with my ears. Hazel and I looked at each other in the dark, and neither of us said what we were both thinking:

The second clock had started ticking.
