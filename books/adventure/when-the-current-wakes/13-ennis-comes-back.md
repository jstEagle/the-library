## 13. Ennis Comes Back

I've been keeping watch off and on since the mangroves, which is how I know the skiff comes back before anyone else does. It comes back wrong.

A patrol skiff is supposed to announce itself—engine note, running lights, that particular arrogance of a boat that knows it's the biggest thing on the water. This one comes in dark, engine at a murmur, riding low like it's ashamed of itself. I count the seconds between its bow wake and the sound of the motor, and I don't like the math, so I go find Hazel without waking anybody else.

She's sleeping sitting up against the tarpaulin patch on the hull, one hand resting on the case we pulled out of the bell tower like it's a dog that might wander. She wakes the way she does everything—fast, quiet, and already annoyed about it.

"Skiff," I say. "Company colors. One man. He's not trying to hide, exactly. More like he's given up on hiding mattering."

Hazel looks at the water for a long moment. "Ennis?"

"Could be a trap wearing Ennis."

"Could be," she agrees, and starts checking the shotgun's shells with her thumb, not looking at me. "Trap or not, he ran us down once already. Let's see if he runs himself back."

That's Hazel for you. She doesn't forgive people. She audits them.

* * *

We let him beach the skiff and wade the last ten meters, hands up, because the water's too shallow to bring a patrol boat in quiet and he knows it. He's soaked to the chest, and when he gets close enough for the moon to find his face, I see what I missed the first time we met him—the kid under the corporal's stripes. He's maybe twenty-three. Somebody gave this boy a gun, a flag, and a set of orders, and he's been carrying all three like they weigh more every day.

"Ms. Sandoval." His voice cracks on her name and he has to start over. "Corporal Ennis. We met."

"I remember," Hazel says. "You told us to turn around. Insurance reasons."

"Yes, ma'am."

"You're a long way from your patrol route."

"That's—" He swallows. "There is no patrol route anymore. Not after tonight. Ms. Ashgrave ordered the river locked down above the confluence. Every boat recalled to the barge. She said there'd been a theft of company property." His eyes flick to the case between Hazel's feet, then away fast, like looking at it might make him complicit. Which, fair. "She's pulling everyone in before the dam goes. She knows it's going. They all know. They're just deciding what to save first."

"And you decided to save yourself," Hazel says. Flat. Not cruel. Just an entry in a ledger.

"No, ma'am." The kid stands up straighter, and for a second I see the soldier somebody trained him to be. "I decided to stop being what I was saving myself *for*."

He unslings a waterproof satchel from his shoulder and holds it out. Doesn't step forward. Just holds it there, dripping, like an offering at a shrine he's not sure will take it.

"Patrol schedules through the end of the week. Radio frequencies, call signs, the rotation for the barge watches. Fuel manifests—Corvane's running dry faster than they'll admit, that's why Ashgrave recalled everything, she can't chase us and run the pumps both." He takes a breath. "And the names of every man on the night shift who'd rather be fishing than shooting. There are more of them than she thinks."

Nobody moves. Rain ticks on the tarpaulin over our heads. Somewhere out in the dark the dam makes a sound I've been pretending not to hear for two days—a long structural groan, like a giant shifting in its sleep—and Ennis flinches at it same as I do.

Hazel doesn't reach for the satchel.

"Why?" she says.

Ennis's jaw works. And here's the thing—I've known Hazel Sandoval long enough now to read her silences, and this one isn't *convince me*. It's *say it out loud, because saying it is part of the price*.

"Because three months ago," Ennis says, "I was on night patrol below the mission bend, and Dr. Amara Osei's boat came downriver with no lights, and the *Vigilant*—the director's launch—it came out of the side channel and ran her down. Deliberately. I watched it happen. I had a spotlight in my hand and orders not to use it, and I didn't use it." His voice doesn't crack this time. It just goes thin and even, the way voices do when a man has rehearsed something until the rehearsal wore a groove. "She was in the water. I saw her in the water. And I held my light down and I counted to sixty and I reported a navigational incident, cause unknown."

The rain fills the silence for him.

"The next morning my sergeant shook my hand," Ennis says. "Told me I had a future with the company. And I've been shaking hands ever since, and every handshake's been worse than the last, and I keep thinking—she was right there. In the water. Right there."

He's crying now, quietly, the way young men cry when they've been taught crying is a supply problem—something to be managed, rationed, kept off the books. He doesn't wipe his face. He holds the satchel out further.

"So that's why, ma'am. Not because I'm brave. Because I'm tired of being the man who counts to sixty."

* * *

Later, when Ennis is asleep under a tarp at the far edge of camp—Hazel put him there, close enough to watch, far enough to think about—I sit with her on the hull and we have the conversation I knew we were going to have.

"It's a trap," I say, because somebody has to say it. "It's a beautiful trap. Guilty kid, sad story, satchel full of schedules that march us straight into Ashgrave's guns. It's the best trap I've ever seen, and I've seen some good ones."

"It might be," Hazel says.

"It probably is."

"Probably." She turns the word over like a coin she's checking for shaving. "You know what my whole life is, Tuck? Arithmetic. Denny Vane smiling at me across a desk while he calculates what my father's signature costs per month. Corvane buying my debt like it's a pallet of ore. Everybody I've ever met doing sums where people are the rounding error." She looks out at the black water. "And here's this kid who did the worst arithmetic of his life—counted to sixty instead of turning on a light—and now he's walked into our camp carrying the only numbers that could get us all killed, and he wants nothing except to stop being that."

"He wants absolution."

"Sure. Everybody wants something. Ilya wanted to fix his maps. Nadia wants her doctor back. You want your engines." A ghost of a smile. "Doesn't mean they're lying."

"You believe him."

"I believe his hands were shaking," Hazel says. "Traps don't shake. Traps are steady. Traps are *patient*. That kid couldn't hold that satchel still because he's been holding it for three months inside himself and it weighs what it weighs." She's quiet a moment. "Here's what I keep coming back to. If I write him off because of the worst sixty seconds of his life, then I'm doing exactly what Vane does. Reading a person by their balance due."

"That's a dangerous way to bank," I say.

"Everything worth anything is." She stands, joints cracking, and looks down at me. "Set a watch on him anyway. Two watches. I'm pragmatic, Tuck, not stupid. But tomorrow we plan the barge run using his schedules, and if it's a trap, we die knowing we died as the kind of people who give a guilty man a hearing." She pauses at the edge of the tarp. "And if it's not a trap—if it's really him—then he's more than his worst entry. Same as the rest of us."

She says it like she's reading it off a ledger she's only just learned to keep. New arithmetic. People entered at their full value, losses and all.

I lie back and listen to the rain and the dam groaning, and I think about the kid counting to sixty in the dark with a dead woman in the water, and I think about Hazel handing him a second chance like a line thrown to a drowning man—which is funny, coming from her, because she told me once she doesn't rescue strangers, she acquires liabilities.

Maybe that's changing too. Maybe we all are.

Because here's what I didn't say to her, out there on the hull: I checked his satchel while he slept. Schedules, frequencies, manifests—all real, all current, all matching what Ilya told us before Ilya went and got himself taken. And tucked in the back pocket, folded small, a photograph. Ennis, younger, in a cadet uniform, standing next to a woman with gray braids and a field journal under her arm.

Dr. Osei. At some company function, years ago, before any of it. Both of them half-smiling, like two people who didn't yet know what they'd owe each other.

On the back, in pencil, in a young man's careful handwriting: *She taught the hydrology seminar. Best teacher I ever had.*

So no. I don't think it's a trap.

I think it's a debt. And tomorrow, when Hazel reads those patrol schedules and plans whatever crazy thing comes next, I'm going to tell her what I found—because if we're doing new arithmetic, the kid deserves to be entered at his full value too.

Out in the dark, the dam groans again, longer this time, and doesn't quite stop.

Tomorrow, one way or another, the river settles it.
