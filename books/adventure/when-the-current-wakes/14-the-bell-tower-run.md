## 14. The Bell Tower Run

The river had been rising all night, and by dawn it had stopped pretending to be a river at all. It was a rumor of the sea, moving inland with intent.

I stood at the wheel of my boat—still unnamed, still leaking in two places I'd rather not discuss in polite company—and watched brown water climb the mission's retaining wall like it was checking the locks on a house it planned to rob.

"That's another meter since midnight," Tuck said beside me. He had his binoculars up, sweeping the tree line. "Maybe more."

"Then we don't dawdle."

"I never dawdle. I loiter with purpose."

Nadia Okonkwo came forward from the cabin, Osei's field journal clutched against her chest the way other people hold infants or grenades. She'd barely slept. None of us had, but she wore it differently—the rest of us wore exhaustion like old coats; she wore hers like a wound dressing that needed changing and couldn't be touched.

"The bell tower," she said for the fourth time since the cave. "There's a false floor under the bell platform. Amara showed me once, years ago, when we surveyed the mission for the heritage registry. She said—" Nadia's voice caught, steadied. "She said every valley needs one place the water can't argue with."

"Stone walls and high ground," I said. "Your doctor thought like an engineer."

"She thought like someone who expected to be betrayed." Nadia looked at me. "She just didn't know by whom, until the end."

That landed where it landed. I turned back to the wheel.

Ennis stood at the stern with his hands folded behind him, out of uniform, wearing a fisherman's shirt two sizes too big that made him look like a teenager playing dress-up in his father's clothes. The patrol schedules he'd brought us were already soaked through with river spray and ink smears, but we'd memorized what mattered: the Corvane gunboats ran the upper bend on a loop, forty minutes out, forty minutes back, give or take the weather. And the weather, this morning, was generous to no one.

"They'll have moved the loop up," Ennis said quietly. "Ashgrave knows the survivors are loose. She'll tighten everything."

"Then we're racing two clocks," Tuck said. "The river and the loop."

"And losing to both," I said cheerfully, because somebody on this boat had to keep morale artificially inflated. "Full throttle. If God wanted us to arrive late, He shouldn't have made the current so cooperative."

* * *

The flooded church appeared around a bend of dead kapok trees, and I understood immediately why Osei had chosen it.

It sat on a knoll of older stone, some colonial mason's stubbornness from a century ago, and the floodwaters broke around its foundations like a crowd parting around a man who won't move. The nave's roof had lost half its tiles, but the bell tower rose intact above everything—a square stone finger pointing at a sky the color of wet slate, its arches open to the wind, the bell itself hanging dark and patient inside them.

Villagers were already there. Of course they were. Dozens of them, maybe a hundred, spread across the knoll and the church steps with their goats and their chickens and their rolled bedding, the whole quiet machinery of people who had done this before and would do it again. Children ran the perimeter like sentries. An old woman directed traffic with a stick and the authority of a four-star general.

"We can't land at the steps," Tuck said. "Too shallow, too many people. There's a cut on the north side, behind the cemetery wall."

"You've been here before?"

"No. But I can read water, same as you."

We came in through the cut slow, engine idling, and grounded on a submerged garden path with a crunch that I chose not to think about financially. Ilya jumped into thigh-deep water and held the bow while Nadia climbed down, then me, then Tuck, then Ennis, who hesitated on the gunwale like a man stepping off a cliff into a new life, which is exactly what he was doing.

The villagers watched us come. Nobody spoke. A boy of maybe ten looked at Ennis's face, then at the faded ghost of a Corvane insignia the too-big shirt didn't quite hide, and his eyes went flat and old.

"He's with us," I said to the boy, in my terrible river pidgin. "He's paying a debt."

The boy considered this with the gravity of a loan officer, then nodded once and went back to chasing a chicken. In this valley, apparently, debt was a language everyone spoke fluently.

Inside the church, the air smelled of candle smoke and wet stone and a hundred people's lives compressed into temporary space. Nadia led us past the altar, past families sleeping in pews, to a narrow stair spiraling up into the tower. It was darker than sin and steeper than regret, and about halfway up I started hearing the bell hum—a low, felt-more-than-heard vibration as the wind moved the great bronze mouth above us.

"She never rang it," Nadia said, climbing ahead of me, her voice echoing off stone. "Amara. Not once in three years. She said a bell is a promise you make to everyone who can hear it, so you'd better be sure of what you're saying before you pull the rope."

"What did she want to say?"

"I don't think she knew yet." Nadia paused on the stairs. "I think she was waiting to be sure."

* * *

The bell platform was a wooden deck beneath the bell's yoke, and the false floor was exactly where Nadia remembered: three boards near the eastern arch, cut slightly prouder than their neighbors, fastened with brass screws gone green with age.

Tuck produced a knife, pried, and the boards came up like a lid.

Beneath: a sealed case, oilcloth-wrapped, tarred at the seams, the kind of waterproofing you do when you expect your work to wait out a monsoon or two without you. Stenciled on the top in careful block letters:

A. OSEI — FOR THE VALLEY — NOT FOR SALE

My chest did something complicated. I covered it by hauling the case up onto the deck and going at the tar seams with Tuck's knife.

"Careful," Nadia breathed.

"It's been underwater-adjacent for months. A little more abuse won't kill it." I worked the hasp free and lifted the lid.

Inside, packed in waxed paper: documents. A thick envelope tied with legal tape, marked LAST WILL AND TESTAMENT — DR. AMARA OSEI, countersigned by two names I didn't recognize and stamped by a notary office in the provincial capital. Beneath it, survey plates—actual linen-backed plates, the originals, with Osei's own field annotations in the margins, dates running years deep. Beneath those, a correspondence file: letters between Osei and Corvane's regional office, offers and refusals, escalating in tone from courteous to glacial. And at the bottom, wrapped separately, a single sheet that made Nadia make a sound like she'd been struck.

It was a draft deed. Corvane letterhead. The inheritance claim—the very claim Ashgrave's lawyers were waving across the valley like a hunting license—except this copy was unsigned, undated, and covered in Osei's red ink. She'd gotten her hands on their forgery while she was still alive. She'd annotated it line by line. *Fabricated signature — compare stroke order, see plate 7.* *Date impossible — tide tables attached.* *This document requires my death to function.*

"She knew," Nadia whispered. "She knew exactly what they'd do, and she built the proof before they finished building the lie."

Ilya, who had come up the stairs behind us and stood now in the archway with rain blowing past his shoulders, made a noise I'd learned to read over the past week. It was the sound a man makes when the ledger he's been carrying finally balances against something worse than zero.

"She asked me for the boundary drafts," he said. "Three days before they killed her. I told her I hadn't finished them. That was even true. But she looked at me like she could see the finished map through my skull, and I—" His jaw worked. "I gave her nothing. And she found it anyway. She was always ten meters upstream of everyone."

"Then let's finish her run," I said, and started repacking the case.

And that was when Tuck, who had drifted to the western arch with the binoculars, said the words I'd been listening for all morning and dreading anyway.

"Hazel. Bend. Two boats. Company colors."

* * *

Here is a thing nobody tells you about triumph: it has a shelf life of approximately ninety seconds, and the countdown starts the moment you touch the prize.

I crossed the bell platform in three strides and took the binoculars. Through the rain-haze, two shapes were coming around the far bend of the river, riding low and mean, bow wakes cutting white lines through the brown. Gunboats. Not patrol skiffs—these were the heavy ones, the ones with mounted hardware amidships and room for a boarding party of eight. They weren't running the loop. They were running straight at the mission, which meant either Ennis's schedules were worthless or Ashgrave had stopped needing schedules.

"They saw us beach," Ennis said. He'd come up beside me, and his voice had gone flat and professional, the fisherman's shirt suddenly irrelevant. "Or someone downstream sold our position. Either way, they'll be here in six minutes. Maybe five."

"How long to get the case down the tower?"

"Two minutes if we run. Four if we're careful. We are not being careful."

"Tuck—"

"Already moving." He had the case strapped across his back with rope, which meant he'd been preparing for this moment since the cave, which meant Tuck contained multitudes and most of them were paranoid. He took the stairs first, Nadia behind him, hands white on the rail.

I lingered one second longer at the arch. Below, the village was reacting—not panicking; these people didn't panic, they *mobilized*. The old woman with the stick was moving families away from the landing side. Men were pulling boats up behind the cemetery wall. A boy was ringing a handbell somewhere, a small bright sound against the big dark silence of the tower bell above me.

For one stupid second I looked at that bell. Bronze, huge, hung ready, rope coiled and waiting. A promise you make to everyone who can hear it.

"Not today," I told it. "Today we run. But I'm keeping your number."

Then I took the stairs three at a time after the others, and the tower swallowed my footsteps behind me.

* * *

We came out of the church into organized chaos, which is the only kind of chaos worth having. The villagers had already decided what we were—messengers, runners, trouble of the useful variety—and the old woman with the stick intercepted us at the door, took one look at the case on Tuck's back, and pointed north with the stick like Moses parting a much wetter sea.

"Back channel," she said, in accented but functional trade-tongue. "Behind the cemetery. Water's deep enough for your boat. Gunboats cannot follow—too many drowned trees. Go."

"Do you know who we are?" Nadia asked her.

The old woman looked at her for a long moment, rain streaming off her chin.

"You are the ones carrying the doctor's box," she said. "Everyone on this knoll knows what Dr. Osei kept in the tower. We have been waiting eleven days for someone brave enough or stupid enough to go get it." Her eyes flicked to me. "Sometimes both qualities live in one person. Go. Argue later."

I have been insulted by professionals—Denny Vane could insult your ancestry, your boat, and your interest rate in one cheerful sentence—and that old woman outclassed all of them in nine words.

We ran.

The back channel behind the cemetery was exactly as promised: deep, narrow, choked with the crowns of drowned trees poking up like the knuckles of a submerged giant. My boat sat where we'd left her, riding higher than I liked on the swollen path, and Ilya was already waist-deep pushing her stern toward open water when the first gunshot cracked across the knoll.

Not aimed at us. Aimed at the sky—a warning shot from the lead gunboat, now visible through the trees, swinging wide toward the mission steps where a hundred villagers stood between it and us. A loudhailer crackled to life, a voice smooth as bank lube: *"This is Corvane security operations. This area is closed for insurance reasons. Remain where you are."*

Insurance reasons. Somewhere, a lawyer was earning his bonus.

"They won't shoot into the crowd," Ennis said, wading beside the hull, rifle slung, face gray. "They need the valley calm. But they'll board us the second we clear the trees."

"Then we don't clear the trees," I said, climbing aboard and throwing the throttle forward. "We go through them. Tuck, bow watch, call the trunks. Ilya, weight aft. Nadia—hold onto something and hold onto the case. If we separate from that case, everything we just did was a funeral procession."

The channel opened ahead of us, green-dark and littered with floating debris, and I drove us into it at a speed that would have made my insurance adjuster—we don't have one, Denny Vane doesn't believe in insurance—resign on principle.

The first trunk came out of nowhere, a black snag sliding past my port side close enough to comb the paint. Tuck called the next one—"Hard starboard!"—and I swung us through a gap between two drowned kapoks with maybe a handspan on either side, the hull shrieking along a submerged branch, and then we were through, into a wider stretch where the channel braided around a long island of bamboo.

Behind us, the gunboats' engines changed pitch. They'd reached the mission steps. They'd found a hundred witnesses and no target. The loudhailer voice was getting less smooth.

"They'll split up," Ennis said. "One boat down each side of the island. We've got maybe two minutes of tree cover and then open water all the way to the confluence."

The confluence. Where the valley's two rivers married into one, wide and exposed, the fastest line downriver and the most visible piece of real estate in the entire flood zone. Every instinct I owned said avoid it. Every mile of river between us and anywhere safe said we had no choice.

"Hazel," Nadia said quietly, behind me. She had the case open on her lap despite everything, despite the branches whipping past, and she was holding the will envelope like a compass. "Whatever happens—if they catch us, if the boat goes down—this doesn't sink. Promise me. Paper floats, but paper also burns, and Ashgrave owns a lot of matches."

I looked at her in the rearview mirror of my own priorities.

"Nadia. I have spent seven years losing things to rivers. Boats, money, my father's good name, one unfortunate engagement ring. I am not losing a dead woman's last word to anybody." I fed the throttle everything the engine had left. "Besides, I owe her. She's the first client who ever paid in truth instead of cash. Cash I can get anywhere."

Ahead, the bamboo island ended. Ahead, the confluence opened up like a held breath—wide, silver-brown, rain-pocked—and on its far shore, small with distance but unmistakable, a third gunboat sat anchored with its engine smoking, waiting, as if Ms. Ashgrave had drawn the shape of our day on a map and simply refused to be surprised by it.

"Well," said Tuck, raising the binoculars one last time. "That's inconvenient."

The current took our bow and swung us out into the open, and the race became a chase, and the chase became arithmetic: two boats behind gaining, one boat ahead waiting, one leaking hull between them carrying a will, a lie's autopsy, and five people who had run out of clever options somewhere back at the bell tower.

I put the nose downriver and started looking for a miracle in the flood lines.

They say the river provides. Today it was going to have to provide fast.
