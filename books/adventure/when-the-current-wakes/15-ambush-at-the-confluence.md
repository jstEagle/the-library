## 15. Ambush at the Confluence

I have a theory about rivers that I never shared with my employers, because it would have made me useless to them: a river is the only honest mapmaker in the world. Everything I ever drew for Corvane Extraction was a lie with contour lines. The river doesn't lie. It just moves.

The confluence where the Sere meets the main channel was one of my favorite lies. On my survey plates—the ones I drew, the ones Dr. Osei's forged signature supposedly blessed—I'd marked it as a clean Y-junction, navigable on both arms at all seasonal stages. That's what the contract wanted: two wide blue lines meeting like a promise. What the water actually does there is uglier and more interesting. The Sere comes down out of the karst carrying half a mountain of silt, and where it hits the main channel it builds a shifting bar that no chart survives for more than a month. In monsoon stage, the bar goes under and becomes something worse than a shoal: a seam of confused current, water arguing with itself about which way to go.

Hazel read that seam from two hundred meters out without slowing down, which is when I understood she was better at this than any of us deserved.

"Backchannel," she said. "Portside, behind the bar."

"You can't see the entrance," Tuck said.

"I can see where it has to be."

"It's not on your own maps," he added, jerking his chin at me, and there it was—the small cold weight that had been settling into the boat since the bell tower, since Ennis's confession, since Nadia opened Osei's case and we all saw what a dead woman had trusted us to carry. My maps. My lines. Every meter of this valley, I had measured and drawn and signed, and every meter of it was now a corridor someone could use to hunt us, because I was very good at my job.

"The entrance is real," I said. "It was real when I surveyed it. Whether it still exists after three days of this rain is a question I can't answer."

"Comforting," Hazel said, and turned the wheel.

That was the moment the gunboats came around the bend.

* * *

There were two of them, maybe three—hard to count through the rain, which by then wasn't falling so much as arriving sideways. They came out of the main channel with the flat authority of things that had never once been told no: gray hulls, company colors bleeding gray into gray, a mounted gun on the lead boat's bow covered by a tarp that somebody hadn't bothered to secure because nobody expected to need it. Behind them, upstream, I could see the shape of the command barge, Ashgrave's floating office, sitting in the flood like a cathedral that had given up on land.

"They've been waiting," Nadia said. She had the case strapped across her chest like a child, both arms around it, and she hadn't let go of it since the mission. "They knew the confluence was the only way through."

"They didn't know," I said. "They *read*. My access notes are in their system. Approach vectors, fuel stops, choke points. I wrote a guidebook to catching anyone moving up or down this valley." I heard my own voice doing the thing it did under pressure—going light, going dry, a man narrating his own autopsy. "Congratulations. You're being caught according to specifications I personally drafted."

"Then you know how they think," Hazel said.

"I know how they *buy*," I said. "Thinking costs extra."

The lead gunboat's loudspeaker crackled through the rain, a voice trained to sound reasonable the way Ashgrave's whole operation was reasonable, everything in good grammar and bad faith. It named our boat. It named Hazel by name—which meant Denny Vane's paperwork had traveled faster than we had—and then it named me, and the voice softened on my name the way you'd soften telling a dog it was going home.

*Ilya Reyes. You are recalled to Company service. Your vessel is instructed to heave to. Resistance will be logged as breach of contract.*

Breach of contract. Of course. Even now, even here, it came down to paper. I had spent six years learning that the syndicate's true weapon was never the gunboat; it was the clause. The gunboats were just the clause with an engine.

Tuck was already moving. He'd been moving since the loudspeaker started, low and quick along the starboard rail, dragging the fuel drum, and I watched him work and thought—not for the first time—that Hazel collected strange liabilities and somehow they kept paying out. He wedged the drum against the transom, checked the patch line on the hull with his palm, and said, without looking up:

"How deep is that backchannel?"

"Two meters in dry season," I said. "Now? Nobody knows. The bar's moved. The flood's rearranged everything."

"So it's either there or it isn't."

"That's a very complete summary of hydrology, yes."

He grinned at me. Tuck had exactly one expression for imminent death and it was that grin, and I have thought about that grin more times since than I'd like to admit, because it was the face of a man who had decided the arithmetic didn't scare him, and arithmetic was supposed to be my department.

Hazel brought us around the tail of the bar and the world went sideways. The seam took the hull and shook it—a lurching, muscular confusion, the Sere's brown water shoving against the main channel's green, the boat slewing fifteen degrees off her heading while Hazel fed the wheel in small corrections, patient as a surgeon, saying nothing, which from Hazel was the loudest possible statement of concentration. The gunboats held back at the mouth of the confluence. They didn't need to chase. The backchannel was a throat, and throats only go one direction, and somewhere downstream of the throat they would simply be waiting, patient as interest accruing.

That was when I did the calculation I'd been avoiding since the church.

* * *

Let me tell you how I was recruited, since we're being honest with each other and there may not be many chances left. It wasn't men in a room. It was a woman with a folder, very much like Ashgrave, who sat across from me in a government office in the capital and explained, kindly, that my survey license had a discrepancy in it—a real one, an honest mistake I'd made at twenty-six—and that the fine, plus the review period, plus the suspension, would end my career. And then she explained that Corvane had a position open, and that the discrepancy would develop amnesia.

Six years later I could redraw a valley's inheritance around a dead woman's forged signature and sleep fine, most nights. That's what a clause does. It doesn't ask you to be evil. It asks you to be *consistent*, one small consistency at a time, until the sum of your consistencies is a valley full of people whose boats have been taken for meetings.

In the flooded church, Ilya-the-surveyor had laid out the hydrology of the tailings dam for people who were about to bet their lives on his numbers, and I had watched Hazel listen, and I had understood that she trusted my figures the way you trust a bridge built by the engineer who burned the last one. She trusted me anyway. That trust had a balance outstanding. I'd been carrying it since the village, since the children described the company colors, since I stood in that doorway and realized that every relocation order, every boundary line, every neat polygon on my plates had a face, and the faces were wet and small and watching us repair a boat.

So: the calculation. Two gunboats at the confluence mouth. One backchannel, unknown depth, unknown exit. Four people in a patched hull, one of them carrying a dead woman's testament, one of them a defector worth more to Corvane alive than any of us were worth dead, because Ilya Reyes, recalled to Company service, was a story Ashgrave could sell—*see, even the traitor came home*—and a story like that keeps the other frightened employees frightened in the correct direction.

And one more variable, the one that settled it: the dam. We'd heard it groan that morning, a sound I felt in my teeth before my ears, a structural complaint rolling across the flooded forest like the earth clearing its throat before it speaks. When that dam finished its sentence, everything downstream drowned—including, conveniently for Ashgrave, every witness, every elder, every copy of anything. The clock wasn't ticking toward our escape. It was ticking toward erasure, and erasure doesn't leave survivors to testify.

They couldn't catch all of us if they were busy catching one of us.

I'm not going to dress it up. I've read enough of other men's memoirs to know how this passage is supposed to go—the sudden nobility, the swelling strings. There were no strings. There was a man in a leaking boat doing arithmetic against his own ledger, and the ledger said he owed more than a boat could pay, and the only currency he had left was himself.

"Ilya." Hazel's voice, over the engine noise. She'd seen me looking at the gunboats too long. Hazel missed nothing; it was her least charming quality. "Whatever you're calculating, stop it."

"I'm a liability," I said. "You said it yourself, day one. You don't rescue strangers, you acquire liabilities. Well. Time to divest."

"Don't—" Nadia started, arms tight around the case.

"They want me alive. That's the one advantage we have that they can't buy back." I was already pulling off my pack, already stripping it down to nothing, leaving the waterproof pouch with my old survey field notes—my *real* ones, the pre-forgery originals, the ones that proved my own plates had been altered—wedged under the thwart where Hazel would find it. "Those go to whoever ends up holding the pen when this is over. Consider it a correction notice. Surveyors file those."

"You're not making sense," Hazel said, and then she made the mistake of looking at me, and I watched her understand, and I watched her hate understanding, and I thought: there it is. That's the payment. Not the gunboats, not Ashgrave's reasonable voice. Her face, right now. That's the exchange rate.

"Tuck," I said. "Get them through the channel. The exit should be past the second karst spine—if the flood hasn't eaten it. If it has, pray, loudly, Hazel's good at transactional prayer."

"And you?" Tuck said.

"I'm going to stand up on a boat in the rain and wave at some very tired young men with guns," I said, "and remind them that Corporal Ennis found a better employer. Morale is a weapon. I read that in a Company manual. Chapter four."

Hazel cut the engine. In the sudden relative quiet—all that remained was rain and water and the far-off mutter of the dam—I heard her say, quietly, not a command, not a plea, just a fact set down on the record:

"You come back. That's an order. I don't care that I don't own you."

"Nobody owns me," I said. "That's rather the point."

* * *

Here is what surrender looks like from the inside, in case you're collecting details for your own eventual ledger: it looks like standing up slowly in a small boat with your hands visible, in rain so heavy the gunboats are ghosts, and shouting a name across the water until the loudspeaker tells you to stop shouting. It looks like the lead gunboat coming alongside with its young crew gripping rails, and a corporal—young, rain-soaked, terrified in the specific way of a man who has recently learned his officers listen to radio broadcasts now—looking at you with something that isn't quite hatred and isn't quite relief.

It looks like hands on your collar, and a hood that smells of diesel, and the engine note changing under you as the boat turns back toward the barge.

And it sounds—this is the part I'll keep—it sounds like Hazel Sandoval's engine opening up behind you, running the seam of the bar hard and fast, the patched hull slapping the confused water, getting smaller, getting away, carrying the case and the journal and the correction notice and the dead woman's will toward whatever throat of the valley was still open.

I gave them a show, because morale is a weapon and because theater was free. I told the corporal I'd reconsidered my position. I told him Ms. Ashgrave's offer had always been fair. I told him the others had abandoned me at the confluence, which was even true, if you squinted. He wrote it all down. Somewhere in the Company's system, a file updated itself: *Reyes, I. Recalled. Cooperative.* A small consistency. I'm told that's how it starts.

On the barge, they took the hood off in a cabin that smelled of coffee and toner, and Ms. Ashgrave looked up from her desk with the expression of a woman whose spreadsheet has balanced, and she said, "Mr. Reyes. We have so much to discuss," and outside, through the porthole, the rain kept falling on the valley I had mapped, and far upstream, faint but unmistakable, the dam groaned again—longer this time, lower, a sound with a deadline in it.

I smiled at her, because I owed her a smile from six years ago, with interest.

And I hoped to God Hazel had found the field notes.
