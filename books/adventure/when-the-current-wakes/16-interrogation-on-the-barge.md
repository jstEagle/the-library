## 16. Interrogation on the Barge

They took my boots before they took my statement.

I want to note that for the record, if there ever is a record: Corvane Extraction's regional command barge, the *Prosperity Meridian*—and yes, someone in their naming department has a sense of humor, or a grudge—collected me from the water at the confluence with the efficiency of a vacuum cleaner and immediately stripped me of my footwear. Not my weapons; I didn't have any. Not my survey instruments; those had gone down with Tuck's blood in the backchannel. My boots. Because, I suppose, a man without boots is a man who isn't going anywhere on foot, and everything else about me they intended to negotiate.

The deck was wet under my socks. The monsoon had settled into its long afternoon argument with the valley, rain coming down in gray sheets that turned the river into something churning and brown and alive, like a muscle flexing. Somewhere upstream, the tailings dam was groaning its way toward failure, though at that moment I didn't know that yet. At that moment I thought the worst thing in the world was the coffee they gave me.

It was good coffee. That was the worst thing about it.

"You look terrible," said Ms. Ashgrave.

She sat across a folding table in a cabin that smelled of diesel and lilies—the lilies real, in a vase, which told me more about her than any dossier could have. Fresh flowers on a working barge in monsoon country means either madness or absolute confidence, and Ashgrave did not strike me as mad. She was maybe fifty-five, silver hair cut short enough to be practical and styled enough to be deliberate, wearing a field jacket over what I'd have bet my remaining boot was an expensive blouse. She had the kind of face that made you feel you'd been introduced somewhere before and had disappointed her then too.

"I've been having a week," I said.

"So I understand." She opened a folder. An actual paper folder, manila, with a brass clasp. "Ilya Reyes. Thirty-four. Born in Cebu, raised in three countries, degree from Delft in hydrographic surveying. Recruited by our land division four years ago after your previous employer—" she checked, though we both knew she hadn't needed to— "let you go for, and I quote their internal memo, 'an unwillingness to round numbers in the company's favor.' You were hired specifically because you were honest. Do you find that ironic?"

"Every day," I said. "Usually around breakfast."

"Good. Irony is a sign of intelligence." She slid a photograph across the table. It was me, younger, at a conference in Rotterdam, holding a paper award and smiling like a man who believed maps could make things better. "This is who we hired. This is who drew the Osei boundary revisions."

"That's not—"

"It is exactly what happened," she said, pleasantly. "You surveyed. You drafted. You signed. The fact that the boundaries you drew enclosed eleven villages and two thousand people inside a mineral claim is a matter of administrative consequence, not moral one. Lines on paper, Mr. Reyes. You of all people know lines are just decisions."

"And whose decision was it?"

"Mine." She said it the way other people say *the weather*. "Now. Let's talk about your week."

And here is the thing I need you to understand, whoever ends up reading this, if anyone does: I knew how this worked. I'd been on the recruiting side of this table once, four years ago, sitting where Ashgrave sat, across from a frightened hydrologist in Guayaquil, explaining reasonably why his data mattered more than his scruples. I remembered exactly what I'd said. I remembered that it had worked. So when Ashgrave began her pitch, I listened with the double attention of a man hearing both the argument and the echo of his own voice in it.

"The situation," she said, "is as follows. Dr. Amara Osei is dead. Her expedition is scattered. Her documentation—which I believe you and your associates recovered from the mission bell tower—is currently in the possession of a storm-chaser with criminal debts, a defected corporal, and a grieving graduate student. None of these people can hold it. The dam upstream will fail within"—she glanced at a tablet, not the window, which told me she already knew and didn't want to watch—"seventy-two hours, possibly less. When it fails, the floodplain floods. Every village downstream evacuates or doesn't; either way, in six months there will be no functioning local authority to contest a claim, no intact survey markers, and a great deal of very convenient silt. We rebuild. We refile. The valley becomes ours on paper, which is the only place valleys have ever belonged to anyone."

"You're describing drowning two thousand people."

"I'm describing a flood," she corrected. "Floods are natural. I'm describing our response to a flood, which is logistical." She folded her hands. "Here is what I'm offering you, Ilya. Your old life. Your name cleared of the defection charge—which, incidentally, would otherwise follow you through every licensing body on three continents. A salary with a number of zeros that will embarrass you. And a role in the reconstruction survey: chief hydrographer, valley district. You'd be drawing the new maps. The honest ones, this time, if that matters to you. Accurate to the centimeter."

"And the villages?"

"What about them?"

That was the trick of it. That was always the trick. Not cruelty—cruelty is loud and makes people flinch. *Reasonableness.* The slow, patient arithmetic that turns human beings into line items so gradually that by the time you notice, you're the one doing the dividing. I had done it myself. In Guayaquil I had told a frightened man that his scruples were a rounding error against the electrification of forty thousand households, and I had believed it, the way you believe a tide chart, because the numbers were right there and numbers don't lie, only the people choosing which numbers to count.

"How many people live downstream of that dam?" I asked.

"Approximately two thousand three hundred permanent residents, plus seasonal fishing families." She had it instantly. Of course she did. "Which sounds like a large number until you set it against the rare-earth supply chain for the next thirty years. Set it against the batteries in the devices you're using to read this someday. Set it against—" she smiled, thin and genuine, which was worse— "the fact that most of them will survive. Floods kill careless people, Ilya, and we are issuing warnings. Through channels. Channels that may or may not function. But we will have issued them. On paper, we will be blameless, and paper is where blame lives now. You taught me that, actually. Your boundary work was impeccable."

"My boundary work put a fence around people."

"My dear boy, every fence does. That's what fences are *for*." She leaned forward. "Let me tell you what happens instead, if you refuse. You go back in the water, or you sit in a hold until the dam goes, and afterward you are a footnote. Hazel Sandoval rows out of here with her debt unpaid and her conscience intact and absolutely nothing else, because conscience doesn't clear interest with the Vane brothers—I've read their file too, they're delightful. Nadia Okonkwo publishes nothing, because journals require institutions and institutions require funding and funding requires not being at war with us. The valley inherits a mudflat and a memory. And in ten years, some bright young surveyor—you, perhaps, rehabilitated—draws the same lines you drew before, and nobody will even remember there was a choice."

The rain hammered the cabin roof. Somewhere below decks, an engine turned over, patient as digestion.

"You're very good at this," I said.

"I'm adequate," she said. "The argument is good. That's different. Good arguments are cheap, Ilya. They're manufactured in bulk. What's expensive is the person refusing to buy one." She pushed a pen across the table. It was a nice pen. Everything about her was nice; that was the whole design. "Sign the reinstatement. Tell me where Sandoval's boat will run when the surge comes downriver—that's all I need, one heading—and walk out of here dry."

I looked at the pen. I looked at my hands, at the rope burns still scabbed along the palms from the night I'd cut myself loose from the supply barge and let the river decide whether I was cargo or swimmer. The river had decided swimmer, barely. And I understood something then, with the clarity that only exhaustion and good coffee can provide: Ashgrave wasn't wrong about the arithmetic. Two thousand people *were* small against thirty years of supply chains. The flood *would* bury the evidence. The paper *would* win, eventually, unless—

Unless someone stopped counting the way she counted.

Because here's what I'd learned since I jumped off that barge, since a woman with leaking hulls and a debt-shaped shadow pulled me out of the water without asking my price: the rounding error is never actually a rounding error. It's a door. Once you agree that two thousand people can be rounded down, you've agreed that anyone can be, including yourself, including the version of you that signed the Osei revisions, including the young idiot in the Rotterdam photograph. Ashgrave wasn't offering me my old life back. She was offering me the life I'd already been living, with better lighting.

"I know how you recruited me," I said.

She tilted her head, genuinely curious.

"In Guayaquil. I was the one across the table once. I said almost exactly what you just said. The rounding error speech. The *paper is where blame lives* speech." I turned the pen around and slid it back to her. "You don't recruit people with money, Ms. Ashgrave. You recruit them with permission. You let them stop being responsible for the numbers. That's the product. That's what I bought four years ago, and I've been paying it off ever since, and I am *done* making payments."

For the first time, something moved behind her eyes—not anger. Recalculation. The sound a spreadsheet makes when a cell returns an error.

"They'll pull you out of the flood anyway," she said quietly. "My boats. For the optics. And then we'll have this conversation again, with colder coffee."

"Then bring better flowers."

She stood, buttoned her field jacket, and paused at the door with her hand on the frame. "The dam has maybe sixty hours," she said, not turning around. "Your friends are hiding in mangrove shallows north of the confluence with a patched hull and no fuel to speak of. I mention this not as a threat but as a courtesy, because unlike you, I keep accurate records." She glanced back, and for one unguarded second she looked tired, the way executives look when the spreadsheet balances but the ledger doesn't. "You were the best surveyor I ever hired, Ilya. That's not flattery. It's inventory."

The door closed. The lock didn't click—they hadn't bothered, or they wanted me to hear it not bother—and I sat alone in the diesel-and-lily smell with a full cup of excellent coffee going cold, listening to the rain and the engines and, beneath both, faint and rising, a sound I would spend the rest of my life hearing in my sleep.

A groan. Deep, structural, rolling down the river from upstream like the earth clearing its throat.

The dam was waking up early.

And sixty miles of flooded valley away, in the mangroves, a woman who read water better than any surveyor I'd ever trained was about to hear it too—and I had roughly one locked-door, bootless, sock-footed chance to make sure that when she did, she wasn't the only one running toward the sound.

I picked up the cold coffee, poured it slowly into the vase with the lilies—it seemed only fair that something in this cabin drown—and went looking for whatever passed for a door on the *Prosperity Meridian* that a man without boots could open.
