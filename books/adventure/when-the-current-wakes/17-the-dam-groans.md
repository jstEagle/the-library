## 17. The Dam Groans

The mangroves swallowed us whole around noon, and I let them.

I'd spent my whole life reading water—storm water, flood water, the green-black glass that means a twister's coming—and mangrove water was its own dialect. Shallow, brackish, tangled at the roots with a century of patience. The boat slid in on a falling tide with the engine off and Tuck poling us through channels no wider than the hull, and every meter we went deeper felt like the river closing a door behind us.

"Corvane's gunboats won't follow in here," Tuck said, low. "Draft's too deep for them."

"They'll wait at the mouth," I said. "They've got time. They've got paperwork."

Ilya was gone. That fact sat in my chest like ballast I couldn't jettison. Twelve hours ago he'd stood up on the deck of a stolen skiff, raised his hands to a patrol boat full of men who'd once been his colleagues, and said, "You want the surveyor? Here." And then he'd looked at me—just once, over his shoulder, with an expression I'd file under *accounting*—and stepped across.

He'd bought our escape with himself. I hated him for it. I hated myself more for letting him.

Nadia hadn't spoken since the confluence. She sat in the bow with Osei's sealed case strapped across her lap like a child, staring at nothing, running her thumb along the brass clasps over and over. Ennis had taken the tiller without being asked; defectors make themselves useful or they make themselves suspects, and he knew which one kept him aboard.

We'd run the flooded backchannels south all night, past drowned paddies and the tops of submerged fences, until the mangroves rose up out of the mist like a coastline that had decided to grow teeth. Then we stopped. You stop when you're hidden. You stop when you can't tell if you're hiding from Corvane or from your own decisions.

The case held everything. The true will. The unaltered surveys. Correspondence proving the deed was forged, dated against tide tables that didn't lie. A dead woman's voice, sealed in wax and canvas, waiting for someone with a working radio and the nerve to use it.

The mission tower was two hours downstream by water, maybe less if the current ran hard. Ashgrave's command barge was anchored mid-channel below it, fat and lit up at night like a riverboat casino, because Ms. Ashgrave believed—reasonably, from where she sat—that she'd already won. Every plan I'd made since the bell tower run pointed at that barge. Get to the radio. Read the will. Burn the claim to the waterline while the whole valley listened.

That was the plan. The river had other plans. The river always does.

* * *

It started as a sound I almost dismissed.

A groan. Deep, structural, the kind of note you feel in your sternum before your ears sort it out. I'd heard buildings moan in tornado winds. I'd heard ice shear off lake edges. This was neither. This was stone and earth and a hundred thousand tons of wet tailings shifting their weight and not liking what they found underneath.

Tuck froze mid-stroke. "Tell me that was thunder."

"It wasn't thunder."

Nadia lifted her head. "The dam," she whispered, and the word came out of her like something she'd been holding behind her teeth for days.

Osei's journal had described it—I'd read the pages by flashlight in the church while the monsoon hammered the roof. The tailings dam upstream, built by Corvane's predecessor company to hold back the slurry from twenty years of rare-earth processing. Built cheap, inspected rarely, patched with promises. Osei had flagged it in her reports: *structural integrity questionable; failure would inundate the entire lower valley.* Corvane had inherited the dam along with everything else they'd stolen, and like everything else, they'd taken it without taking responsibility for it.

Now the valley itself was telling us what the reports said.

The second groan came longer than the first, rolling down the channel through the water as much as the air, and I felt it come up through the hull and into the soles of my boots. Somewhere upstream, behind hills we couldn't see, a wall of grey sludge and monsoon water was learning how much it weighed.

"How long?" Ennis asked. He'd gone very still at the tiller, the way men do when the military part of their brain starts doing math their stomach doesn't want finished.

"I don't know," Nadia said. "Hours. Maybe less. The journal said the seepage had tripled last season. If the core is already saturated—" She stopped. Swallowed. "When it goes, it goes all at once. There's no slow version of this."

I did the arithmetic I didn't want to do. The surge would come down the main channel at whatever speed a valley full of water could travel—fast, faster than any boat we had, faster than anything with an engine. It would hit the fishing village first. The one with the children who'd told us about the men in company colors. Then the river towns. Then the lowlands where the elders lived, the ones Nadia had grown up listening to, the ones Osei had willed a valley to.

And between us and them, anchored fat and confident in the main channel: Ashgrave's barge. And beyond it, the mission tower. The radio. The will.

Every version of justice I'd been carrying since the confluence suddenly required the same thing: time. Time to reach the tower. Time to broadcast. Time to make sure the people who were supposed to inherit this valley survived long enough to inherit anything.

Time was exactly what the dam was about to take away.

* * *

"We go after Ashgrave first," Ennis said.

He said it carefully, the way you set down something heavy. He'd been quiet most of the day, and I understood why. A man who'd watched a boat get run down and said nothing doesn't get to be casual about proposals involving more boats.

"The barge is between us and the tower," he went on. "If we broadcast while she's still operational, she cuts the transmission halfway through. We've seen her do it. But if we disable the barge—if we put her radios down first—the will gets read clean."

"And how do we disable a gunboat barge?" Tuck asked. "With what? Good intentions?"

"With the patrol schedules," Ennis said. "Her night watch rotates at two. There's a forty-minute window where the aft deck is thin. I know the boarding points. I know where the fuel lines run."

"You know how to die," Tuck said. "That's different."

They argued. I let them, because some part of me wanted them to argue, wanted someone else to carry the plan for a few minutes while I sat with the sound still rolling around in my skull. *Groan.* Another one came while they talked, fainter now, muffled by distance and mangrove roots, but there. Patient. The dam wasn't in a hurry. Dams never are, right up until they are.

Nadia touched my arm. "Hazel."

"Yeah."

"If we spend tonight on Ashgrave, we lose the villages."

"I know."

"Do you?" Her voice cracked, and I let her have the crack, because she'd earned it. "There are three hundred people in San Isidro alone. Fishermen. Children. They can't outrun a slurry surge in dugout canoes. If we don't warn them—if we don't start moving them *now*—" She pressed her palm flat against the case on her lap. "Amara didn't write this will for corpses. She wrote it for people who are alive tomorrow morning."

"And if Ashgrave's still floating tomorrow morning," Ennis said quietly, "the will doesn't matter. Paper burns. Barges don't. We get one shot at the radio, and if we waste it on evacuation warnings before we've secured the transmitter, she jams us, finds us, and takes the case. Then everyone dies anyway, just slower."

"And if we board that barge," Nadia fired back, "and it goes wrong, then nobody warns anybody, and the surge does Corvane's work for free. That's what the dam is, Hazel. Don't you see it? It's not an accident waiting to happen. It's an eraser. Everything downstream drowns, the evidence drowns, the witnesses drown, and Corvane files an insurance claim. Ilya told us that in the church. He told us exactly what they were waiting for."

Ilya. His name landed in the middle of the argument like a stone in still water. Nobody picked it up. Nobody had to.

Here's the thing about me, and I'm not proud of it: my whole life, I've priced things. Debt teaches you that. Every choice has a number attached, and you learn to do the math fast and cold, because the Vanes of the world collect on hesitation. So I sat in the mangroves with a failing dam upstream and a tyrant downstream and a dead woman's will in a waterproof case, and I ran the numbers.

Option one: the barge. Disable Ashgrave, secure the radio, read the will. Cost: hours we might not have. Risk: everything. Upside: the claim dies forever, and the valley inherits itself.

Option two: the villages. Warn them, move them, spend ourselves on evacuation. Cost: Ashgrave stays afloat, maybe forever. Risk: the case, the will, the truth—all of it stays buried under a functioning dictatorship. Upside: three hundred people who'd otherwise be statistics.

Justice that costs the innocent isn't justice. The thought arrived without drama, the way real thoughts do—not a revelation, just a door swinging open onto a room I'd apparently been standing outside of my whole life. I'd spent weeks telling myself the will was the point. The paper. The proof. The legal kill-shot. But Osei hadn't hidden her documents in a bell tower so lawyers could admire them. She'd hidden them so the people in the valley could decide what to do with their own inheritance. People. Alive ones. Breathing ones, preferably, with their houses not underwater.

If I chose the barge and the dam broke early, I'd be trading San Isidro's children for a radio broadcast. And no matter how good the broadcast was, no matter how completely it burned Corvane's claim to ash, it would be read over a drowned valley, to survivors who'd lost everything while I settled scores.

But if I chose the villages and left Ashgrave intact—

No. That wasn't actually the choice. I kept catching myself framing it as either/or, and either/or is how brokers and bankers think. Either/or is Denny Vane's arithmetic: one asset, one price, pick your loss. The river doesn't do either/or. The river does *sequence*.

"Hazel," Tuck said. "You're doing the face."

"What face?"

"The one where you've already decided but you're making us wait for it."

I looked at each of them. Nadia with her thumb on the brass clasps. Ennis with his patrol schedules and his guilty ledger. Tuck, who'd warned me on day one that the upper river eats radios first, and who'd stayed anyway, poling a leaking boat through a drowning world for wages I couldn't pay.

"Both," I said. "Same night. Sequence, not choice."

Ennis frowned. "Explain."

"The surge gives us a clock, and the clock gives us cover. Ashgrave knows the dam's failing too—her own hydrologists will have told her, and she wants it to break, remember? It's her eraser. Which means tonight, while every other Corvane eye is upstream watching the water rise, her barge crew is going to be nervous, distracted, and looking the wrong direction." I tapped the chart spread across the thwart. "We split. Ennis takes the skiff and two of the villagers' boats—we borrow them, we leave payment, we're not animals—and runs downstream to San Isidro and the lowland settlements. Not to evacuate them single-handedly. To ring the church bells, fire flares, bang drums, whatever it takes to get them moving to high ground *before* the surge, using the flood-refuge routes Osei mapped. Nadia goes with him, because those routes are in her head and the elders will move for her when they won't move for a Corvane uniform."

"And you?" Nadia asked.

"Tuck and I take the case up the backchannel to the mission tower. We don't fight the barge. We don't even look at it. We get the will on the air while Ennis is emptying the valley." I met Ennis's eyes. "Your forty-minute window stays in your pocket. We only need it if the broadcast fails. One contingency, not a plan."

"And if the dam breaks while we're split up?"

"Then the current settles it," I said. "All of it. Every account. But we'll have done both things first, or died trying, and there's a difference between losing and forfeiting. I've forfeited before. I'm not doing it twice in one lifetime."

Nobody spoke for a moment. Out on the main channel, distant but distinct, the river made a sound I'd never heard before and hope never to hear again—a long, grinding exhalation, like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

Nadia unstrapped the case and held it out to me. Her hands shook. Her eyes didn't.

"She trusted strangers," Nadia said. "Right up until the end. Make it count."

I took the case. It weighed less than I expected and more than I could say.

* * *

We moved at dusk, when the mangroves went from green to black and the rain settled into a steady, businesslike drizzle. At the channel fork, we divided the fuel, the flares, the food. Ennis shook my hand like a man settling a debt of his own, and Nadia hugged me quick and fierce, and then their boat was gone downstream into the murk, engine throttled low, running toward three hundred people who didn't know yet that the sky was about to fall sideways.

Tuck poled us north up the narrow channel, toward the mission, toward the tower, toward the radio. Behind us, upstream, the dam groaned again—and this time the groan didn't stop. It stretched out, deepened, became a continuous subterranean note, the sound of a decision being made by geology.

Tuck glanced back over his shoulder. "How long you figure we've got?"

I looked at the black water ahead, at the tower light I couldn't see yet but knew was there, at the case sitting between my feet holding a dead woman's last word on the whole valley.

"Less than we need," I said. "More than zero. Row."
