## 18. Broadcasting the Dead Woman's Will

The mission tower smelled like bat guano and old incense, which I figured was as honest a combination as any. Somewhere below us, the river was climbing the mission steps two at a time, and somewhere upstream, a dam made of greed and bad concrete was groaning out its death rattle. And here I was, sitting in a sweltering bell loft with a dead woman's papers spread across my knees, about to do the one thing I'd spent my whole life avoiding.

I was going to say something that mattered.

"You don't have to read it," Nadia said. She'd climbed up with me, along with Ennis, who kept glancing at the door like his old employers might come through it at any second. Which, to be fair, they might. "I can do it. I knew her voice."

"That's exactly why it should be me." I smoothed the field journal's last pages flat against the radio housing. "Nobody in this valley knows my voice. Which means nobody can accuse me of reading my own script. You're her protégé, Nadia. If you broadcast this, Ashgrave's people will call it grief talking. If I do it, I'm just a stranger with a contract and nothing left to lose."

Ennis snorted. "That's not reassuring."

"It's not supposed to be. It's supposed to be true." I keyed the mic twice, testing. The old shortwave set had been maintained by some long-dead missionary with a sense of duty, and Tuck—God, Tuck, shot and feverish but stable in the village below with the elders' wives fussing over him—had coaxed it back to life that morning with wire, solder, and profanity. It crackled to life under my hand like something waking from a long sleep.

"Every frequency?" Nadia asked.

"Every frequency she listed." Osei had been thorough. Of course she had. A woman who hid her will inside a bell tower during a monsoon didn't leave anything to chance. She'd left instructions for this too—a broadcast schedule, frequencies, even suggested phrasing, written in that same precise hand I was coming to know like a friend's. *If you are reading this, the current has carried you further than I could go. Finish it.*

"Testing," I said into the mic, and my voice came out steadier than I felt. "This is an emergency transmission from the upper Serrano valley. All stations, all vessels, all patrols. Please stand by."

I let the static breathe for a moment. Then I picked up the first page.

"My name is Hazel Sandoval. I'm a salvage contractor, and eleven days ago I took a paid job to find out what happened to the Meridian expedition. Here is what happened: Dr. Amara Osei discovered rare-earth deposits in this valley worth more than most countries' annual budgets. Corvane Extraction offered to buy her silence. When she refused, they killed her, forged her signature on an inheritance deed, and hired surveyors—including a man named Ilya Reyes, who is now paying for that mistake in ways you'll hear about—to redraw your valley around their theft."

Nadia flinched at Ilya's name. I kept going.

"I am now going to read you Dr. Osei's actual will. Not the forgery. The real one. Dated, signed, witnessed, and hidden where only someone who loved this valley would think to look."

* * *

Reading a will aloud on a dying radio while a flood climbs toward you is a strange way to spend an afternoon. My voice went hoarse by the third page. Osei wrote the way she must have spoken—clear, unsentimental, furious in the quietest possible register. She named the villages. She named the elders. She named the schoolteacher in San Rafaél who'd taught half the valley to read, and the fisherman whose family had worked the confluence for six generations. She willed them the valley. Not the minerals—the valley. The water, the banks, the floodplains, the karst caves, the fishing grounds. The mineral rights she assigned to a trust, administered jointly by the valley council and whatever government authority eventually crawled upriver to take a look, with proceeds earmarked for schools, clinics, and flood defenses.

"And to any party claiming otherwise," she wrote, and I read it verbatim, letting the words land, "let them produce documentation predating this will, witnessed by hands other than their own, or accept that they are thieves quoting scripture they wrote themselves."

Then came the evidence. The tide tables. The deed dated three days after her reported death—but signed with ink batch numbers that matched a shipment Corvane received six weeks later. The correspondence between Ashgrave and the magistrate, cheerful as Christmas letters, discussing 'relocation logistics' for villages that hadn't agreed to relocate. The survey plates, original and unaltered, showing what the valley actually looked like before Ilya's red pen got to it.

When I finished, I sat back. The tower creaked. Below us, through the louvers, I could see the village green turning into a lake, and boats—dozens of them, more than I'd known existed—gathered in the flooded streets. People had been listening. On radios, on battery sets, on the patrol skiffs' own equipment. Word travels faster than water when the water's giving you time to prepare.

"Now the copies," Nadia said.

We'd spent the morning making them. Osei's documents, photographed page by page on Ennis's field camera, printed on the mission's ancient mimeograph and copied again by hand by three schoolteachers who showed up asking how they could help. We had maybe forty sets. It wasn't enough. It would have to be enough.

* * *

Going down the tower stairs into the crowd was like walking into weather. Not hostile weather—worse. Attentive weather. Two hundred people watching a stranger carry their future down a staircase in a waterproof satchel.

An elder named Mateo—silver-haired, river-weathered, with the kind of face that had been saying no to powerful men since before I was born—stepped forward first. I handed him a copy with both hands, the way you hand someone something breakable.

"Dr. Osei wanted you to have this," I said. "Not me. Her."

He weighed it in his palms like he was checking the depth of a river by feel. "And what does she want us to do with it?"

"That's not my call." I meant it. "She willed it to you. What you inherit, you decide. That's the whole point of a will."

Behind him, I could hear the murmur moving through the crowd—people passing word of what they'd heard on the broadcast, someone reading passages aloud from a battery radio replay, a woman weeping quietly near the church doors. Nobody was rioting. Nobody needed to. Something worse for Ashgrave was happening: people were *deciding*.

Ennis appeared at my elbow, looking like a man who'd jumped off a cliff and just now noticed there was no water below. "Three of my officers came in overnight," he said, low. "From Station Two and the fuel depot. They heard the broadcast on the patrol band." He swallowed. "They brought the station logbook. It has orders in it, signed by Ashgrave, dated before Osei died. Preparations. For everything."

"Your people are listening to the dead woman's will on company equipment," I said.

"Yes." He almost smiled. It was a terrible smile, brave and doomed and relieved all at once. "And Corporal Vasquez asked me to tell you that if Ms. Ashgrave orders a raid on this village, four patrol boats will develop engine trouble simultaneously. Very sudden. Very mysterious."

"Tell Vasquez she's got a gift."

* * *

Ashgrave's answer came within the hour, because of course it did. Power like hers doesn't argue; it issues statements.

The command barge came around the bend at dusk, running lights blazing, loudspeakers crackling across the water. Her voice rolled over the flooded village, smooth and reasonable as ever—the voice of a woman explaining why the fire is actually good for you.

"This is a corporate security zone. Unauthorized broadcasts constitute sabotage. All residents are advised that emergency relocation protocols remain in effect. Order will be restored."

The loudspeaker cut. Silence, except for rain on water.

Then, from the darkness downstream, a patrol skiff's radio squawked—and instead of static, it played back my recording of Osei's will. Someone aboard had taped it. Another skiff answered with the forgery evidence, the tide-table discrepancy, read aloud in a young officer's careful voice. A third simply played the sound of the dam groaning, recorded that morning, over and over, like a bell tolling.

Ashgrave's loudspeakers stayed silent after that. There wasn't much left to say that her own people weren't already broadcasting back at her.

Mateo stood beside me at the water's edge, watching the barge sit there, huge and lit up and suddenly very alone. "She'll try something tonight," he said. "Before the law arrives. Whatever she tries, it'll be desperate."

"The law's late," I said.

"The law is always late." He tucked the document copy inside his shirt, against his skin. "But look—it came."

Upstream, so faint I might have imagined it, the dam groaned again. Longer this time. Deeper. Like something enormous shifting its weight, getting ready to stand up.

I looked at the black water climbing the mission steps, at the barge hanging on the bend like a held breath, at the villagers loading their boats with the calm of people who had finally been told the truth and found they could carry it.

"Hold onto something," I told Mateo. "Tonight the current wakes."

And far upstream, the mountain answered.
