## 19. When the Current Wakes

### Hazel

The dam announced itself the way old men announce their opinions—loudly, and whether anyone asked or not.

I'd been holding position in the mangroves for two hours, engine idled to a mutter, watching the mission tower shrink behind us in the rain. Nadia was below with the case strapped to her chest like a life vest made of paper. Ennis had the wheel watch. Tuck lay on the bench seat under a tarp, gray as river clay but breathing, complaining about the quality of my bandages, which was how I knew he'd live. Complaining was his vital sign.

Then the sound came up the valley.

Not an explosion. Nothing so honest as that. It was a groan—deep, structural, the kind of noise you feel in your back teeth before your ears sort it out—rolling down the water like a rumor with weight behind it. The mangroves shivered. Fish jumped in silver sheets, all at once, going the wrong direction.

"There it is," I said, to nobody, because there was nobody left to say it to who didn't already know.

Ennis looked at me. "How long?"

"Depends on how it fails." I throttled up. "If it goes all at once, we're debris. If it goes slow, we have maybe forty minutes to get every living soul off the low banks between here and the bend."

"And if it goes slow first," Ennis said, "and then all at once?"

"Then we'll know exactly how much time we wasted talking about it."

I swung the boat out of the mangroves and pointed her nose downstream, toward the fishing village where children had once described men in company colors taking boats and elders for meetings. The same village whose hospitality had shamed me so thoroughly I still hadn't figured out what to do with the feeling. Behind us, upstream, the groan came again, longer this time, and something in it cracked—a report like a rifle shot underwater—and I stopped doing arithmetic about time and started doing arithmetic about people.

Forty minutes. Maybe three hundred souls on the low bank. A boat that held twelve comfortably and twenty if nobody minded intimacy.

You do the math. Then you do it again, hoping it comes out different. It never does.

* * *

We hit the village landing at full throttle and I stood on the bow and shouted over the rain, and here is the thing I will remember until the river takes me: they didn't panic. An old woman—the one who'd fed us fish stew and refused payment, the one whose name I never learned because shame kept me from asking twice—looked at the sky, then at the water, then at me, and started moving people inland with the calm of a woman who had survived six monsoons and considered this one rude but not unprecedented.

Except the low bank wasn't the only problem. The tailings were the problem. When that dam let go, it wouldn't release clean water—it would release the mine's whole biography, years of slag and slurry and whatever Corvane had been too cheap to process properly, and everything it touched would wear it like a coat for a generation.

"Inland!" I shouted. "High ground! Not just out of the water—uphill!"

They went. Mothers, kids, elders, dogs. Some of them carried things that made no sense—a sewing machine, a framed photograph of a man in a uniform from a war nobody remembered correctly—and I understood that too, because when the world ends you don't carry what's valuable, you carry what's yours.

I loaded eleven of them who couldn't make the climb—bad hips, bad lungs, one woman nine months pregnant who informed me, with terrifying serenity, that the baby would come when it came and the flood could wait its turn.

"You can't take them all," Ennis said quietly beside me.

"I know."

"The boat won't handle the surge weighted down—"

"I *know*, Corporal." I wiped rain out of my eyes. "Get the rest uphill. Take the case off my boat and give it to the elders. Whatever happens to me, whatever happens to this boat, that paper reaches the bend and gets read out loud. That's not negotiable. That's the only thing today that isn't."

He hesitated. Then he saluted—actually saluted, this kid who'd watched a doctor's boat get run down and said nothing, paying against his own ledger now the only way he knew how—and took the case, and ran.

I put the wheel over and turned upstream.

Because the surge would come down the channel, yes. But the confluence where Ilya had bought our escape with himself—that was upstream. And Ashgrave's barge, heavy with seized ore, riding low and slow and arrogant, was anchored at the confluence.

And I am not a good person. I've made peace with that. But I am a person who reads water, and the water was telling me something very simple: everything heavy drowns first.

* * *

### Ilya

They kept me in the hold of the command barge, which told me everything about Ms. Ashgrave's priorities. Not chained in a cabin among officers—stowed, like cargo, like the ore stacked in the next compartment. I had measured that ore. I had drawn the maps that told them where to dig it. There was a symmetry to being stored alongside it that I suppose a better man would have found instructive sooner.

When the groan came through the hull, I laughed.

I couldn't help it. It came out of me like something escaping a cage, and the young guard outside my door banged on it and shouted at me to stop, and I said, "Do you feel that? Do you hear it? That's the sound of your employer's arithmetic failing," and he looked at me with the particular terror of a man who has heard the water change and knows his bosses will not authorize evacuation until a document says he may.

That was the thing about Corvane that I had spent four years refusing to see: it wasn't evil in any way you could shoot at. It was delay. It was *pending approval*. It was a regional director weighing the cost of lives against the cost of ore and finding the equation comfortable, and everyone downstream of her comfort drowning in it.

The barge began to move. I felt it in the deck plates—engines reversing hard, the whole structure shuddering as Ashgrave tried to turn two hundred tons of seized mineral wealth in a channel that was about to become a waterfall's argument.

She was going to run for it. Leave the valley, leave the villages, leave me stowed in the hold, and ride her cargo downriver ahead of the surge, and file the insurance claims from a city with dry carpets.

I did the only thing I could think of. I screamed. I screamed fire, I screamed flooding, I screamed in three languages, and when the guard opened the door to shut me up, I knocked him past caring—not badly, I want that noted, not badly, I'm done adding injuries to accounts—and got into the passageway.

The hold was open. The ore was there. And I am a surveyor, and a surveyor knows what everyone else forgets: weight has opinions. Two hundred tons of ballast on one side of a barge in moving water is not a ship. It is a decision the river gets to make.

I found the seacocks. Old ships have them; new barges have valves, painted over, maintained on paper only. This one turned easier than it should have, which told me the maintenance schedule was as fictional as the deed, and cold river water came up into the hold like it had been waiting for an invitation.

I'm not going to pretend I thought it through. I didn't sabotage the barge to save the valley. I sabotaged it because it was full, and overloaded things sink, and I wanted the sinking to happen *here*, in shallow-ish water near the bend, near people with boats, instead of mid-channel where the surge would swallow the wreckage and every man on it without leaving anything for the current to be merciful about.

Or maybe I wanted revenge. I've had four days to examine myself and I still can't tell you honestly. Both things were true. Both things are probably always true.

The water reached my knees when the second groan came, and this one wasn't the dam anymore. This one was the barge, answering.

* * *

### Hazel

I saw the barge listing before I understood why.

It sat at the confluence canted hard to port, ore sliding somewhere inside her with a sound like gravel poured into a coffin, and men were in the water around her already—Corvane men, patrol skiffs swamped, guys who two days ago would have boarded me and asked for papers now swimming for their lives in company colors that weighed them down.

And tied at the barge's stern, absurdly, half-swamped, was a longboat with Ilya in it. Alive. Rowing away from a sinking ship with the unhurried strokes of a man who has decided that if the river wants him, it can work for it.

I came alongside him in the teeth of the first outrider of the surge—the leading edge of it, a brown wall maybe a meter high and rising, carrying trees and a drowned fence and somebody's roof—and hauled him over the gunwale by his collar.

"You sank their barge," I said.

"I opened a valve," he said, dripping. "History will decide what I sank."

"The history's downstream and it's coming fast. Grab a line."

Because the low bank wasn't clear yet. The old woman's people had gone uphill, but there was a hamlet further down—six houses, a drying rack, a family I'd traded rope to in chapter one of this disaster—and the surge would reach them in minutes, and their boats were gone, taken by patrols weeks ago.

So we did what you do. Which is everything, fast, badly, and anyway.

Ilya and I pulled four people off a roof. Ennis, who had come back downstream in a commandeered patrol skiff because apparently defection is a habit once it starts, pulled two off a floating log. Tuck, who should have been horizontal, stood upright in my boat's bow throwing lines with his shot arm strapped down and his good arm doing the work of two, swearing steadily in a dialect of profanity I intend to learn someday.

The salvage cargo went first. I want that recorded accurately, because Denny Vane will ask: the moment the first family came aboard, I cut loose the fuel drums and the sealed crates and everything Corvane was paying me to recover, and watched it all go under the brown water without a single feeling I could name. Payment. My debt. My father's loan accruing interest under Denny's cheerful eye. All of it slid off the deck and the river closed over it like a ledger balancing itself.

And do you know what I felt? Nothing. Relief, maybe. The specific lightness of a woman who has finally thrown the anchor she's been dragging for ten years.

The surge proper hit us at the bend.

I have chased storms for eleven years. I have driven into weather that made grown meteorologists pray to gods they didn't believe in. None of it was this, because storms are wind and this was the earth changing its mind. The river rose a meter in a breath. My boat—Osei's boat now, though she doesn't have her new name yet—stood up on the front of it like a thing born to it, and I will confess the only honest moment of that entire day: for about four seconds, surfing the leading edge of a tailings flood with seven strangers and a bleeding crewman aboard, I was happy. Purely, stupidly happy. The current woke up and I was awake with it.

Then the happiness passed, because happiness does, and there was work.

Ashgrave's barge went down at the confluence while we rode the surge past it. Slowly, the way big things die—settling, groaning, her decks going under by degrees while her crew abandoned her in disorder. Ms. Ashgrave herself I saw only briefly: standing on the tilting superstructure in a rain jacket worth more than my boat, being handed into a launch by men who were, notably, saving themselves rather than her, which is the final audit of every powerful person. Her launch made the downriver channel. Whether it made the downriver *surge* is another question, and the river wasn't taking questions.

Ennis's skiff was everywhere that morning. I lost sight of him and found him again, always pulling people out—Corvane troopers, villagers, a magistrate's clerk clinging to his own ledgers like they'd float, which they didn't, and Ennis took the man and let the ledgers go, and I swear the clerk cried harder about the paper than he ever had about anything done to the valley. You spend long enough serving documents and you start thinking you are one.

By afternoon the water had done its worst and settled into sullen, spreading flatness, and the rain kept on, indifferent, professional. We ferried people to high ground until my arms stopped negotiating and simply obeyed. Ilya rowed. Tuck swore. Somewhere in the middle of it the pregnant woman's baby decided the flood could indeed wait no longer, and was born in the wheelhouse of a storm-chasing boat during a tailings flood, and screamed at the rain with a fury I found extremely promising.

I named the boat that evening, at anchor in flooded timber, while the valley dripped and steamed and counted itself.

*Amara*. Paint was a problem—I used bilge paint and a rag and my own handwriting, which looks like a ransom note—but names matter more than fonts.

Tuck looked at it and said, "Who's Amara?"

"A woman who wrote a will," I said. "Best document on this river."

* * *

### Ennis

I pulled thirty-one people out of the water that day. I know the number because I counted, because counting was the only penance available and I intended to serve every unit of it.

Some of them wore my colors. Some of them spat at me while I hauled them in, and I deserved it, and I held on anyway. One of them was Sergeant Okafor, who had stood next to me on the dock the night Dr. Osei's boat went down, and who looked at me across the gunwale with the water streaming off him and said, "You too?" and I said, "Me too," and neither of us said anything else, because there wasn't anything else that fit in a boat.

Ms. Ashgrave's launch was seen heading downriver, riding low, refusing to jettison the strongbox she'd gotten out of the barge. Refusing to lighten. The men with her would have told her to drop it. She wouldn't have. I'd served under her long enough to know that she believed, truly believed, that what she carried was worth more than what it cost—and the river, which settles every account its own way, was already reading her books.

Whether she learned otherwise before the end, I don't know. I hope so. I hope it for her specifically, because I know exactly how long it takes to learn it, and I wouldn't wish the lesson on anyone, even her.

At dusk I tied up next to Hazel Sandoval's battered boat, newly renamed, and the valley lay around us in the rain—flooded, scarred, alive. On the hill above the mission, hundreds of people who should have been dead sat around fires, and someone had begun to sing, and others joined in, badly, in parts, the way people sing when they are counting themselves and finding the number sufficient.

Nadia Okonkwo stood at the fire with a sealed case in her hands, and elders around her, and she was saying something I couldn't hear over the water and the singing. But I saw the elders' faces. They weren't faces of people receiving orders.

They were faces of people deciding.

And I understood that whatever came next—investigations, arrests, companies regrouping in cleaner offices, lawyers arriving on the next relief boat like gulls—it would all arrive late, and find the decision already made. The law would come upriver eventually. It always does. But tonight the law was a fire on a hill and a dead woman's will being read aloud, and the current, having settled what it came to settle, moved on toward the sea with the rest of us in its pockets.

Hazel Sandoval leaned on her rail beside me and watched the fires.

"They'll send someone for the debt," I said. "Vane."

"Oh, I know." She smiled at the rain. "Let him come upriver. Let him see what his new employers' name is worth here now."

Downstream, far off, thunder stacked along the horizon—and beneath it, faint but unmistakable, the sound of an engine working hard against the current, coming toward us.
