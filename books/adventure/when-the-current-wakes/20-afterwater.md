## 20. Afterwater

The dead do not need boats. That was my first thought, standing on the mission steps on the fourth morning after the dam broke, watching the relief craft come up the drowned valley like patient beetles—flat-bottomed, government-stenciled, one of them flying a flag I didn't recognize and probably shouldn't have trusted. The living needed them desperately. The dead, Dr. Amara Osei most of all, had already gone wherever water goes when it finishes its work.

I had slept maybe six hours in four days, and most of those upright, in the bell tower, with her case under my legs like a dog that might bolt.

"Nadia." Elder Sefa's hand on my shoulder. She had a way of touching people that felt less like comfort than like inventory—checking that you were still whole, still present, still worth counting. "You should eat."

"I should catalog," I said.

"You should eat *while* you catalog. The dead are not going anywhere. That is the entire point of them."

It was the closest thing to a joke she'd made since the flood, and I took it the way you take bread from a stranger: gratefully, without asking what it cost her. Her village had lost eleven people. Eleven names, and she could recite all of them, and did, every morning, out loud, before anything else got done. I had started doing it with her. It seemed like the minimum rent on being alive.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about the aftermath of a catastrophe that also happens to be a crime scene: the paperwork arrives before the grief counselors, and the paperwork is worse.

The relief boats brought soldiers first—not Corvane's rented guns but actual government ones, which in this country was a distinction with a real difference, though I'd stopped betting on it years ago. They brought tarps, chlorine tablets, rice in sacks stamped with an aid agency's logo and, underneath, faintly, another logo where someone had been sloppy with the paint roller. They brought a magistrate's commission from the provincial capital, three investigators, and a warrant.

They arrested Magistrate Oduya on his own veranda, at breakfast, with his ledger open in front of him.

I know because Hazel Sandoval made sure we were all there to see it. She'd timed it. She'd found out which morning the investigators would come upriver and she'd arranged—through Ennis, through the elders, through whatever alchemy of leverage and stubbornness she carried in that scarred canvas bag of hers—for Oduya to be sitting down to his eggs when they arrived. Not dragged from a hiding place. Not caught burning documents. Sitting in the open, in his official chair, with the ledger that recorded every bribe in his own tidy handwriting, because Hazel wanted the valley to watch him taken from the seat where he'd sold it.

"He'll get eight years," she said beside me. We were on the mission steps again. She ate nothing; she never seemed to eat; I'd begun to suspect she photosynthesized grievance. "Maybe ten. He'll write a memoir in prison claiming he was protecting the valley from worse men."

"Will he be wrong?"

"Some of him will be. That's the part that keeps me up at night—the true parts of terrible men's memoirs."

She said it lightly, but I'd learned by then that everything Hazel said lightly was load-bearing.

* * *

The claim itself died faster than anyone expected, which is to say it died the way ice dies—slowly, invisibly, and then all at once with a sound like a shot.

Corvane Extraction's inheritance deed, dated three days after Dr. Osei's reported death, signed in a hand that wasn't hers on paper bought from a clerk who'd already confessed to selling two other valleys' worth of fiction—the deed went to the provincial registrar under seal, along with Ilya Reyes's original survey plates and the tide-table analysis showing the dates couldn't fit any water that had ever touched this river. The registrar voided it pending investigation. Pending investigation. Those four words, which in my experience usually meant a grave so deep nothing ever climbed out of it, here meant something else entirely, because the investigation would be conducted with the valley's witnesses standing in line to testify, and half of Corvane's own patrol officers had already volunteered.

Ennis among them. He testified first, actually. He stood up in front of the investigators in the flooded church—it was the only building big enough, and there was something right about that, about confession happening under a roof the river had already judged—and he told them about the night Dr. Osei's boat was run down. He named the barge. He named the order-giver. He named himself, standing on the bank, watching, saying nothing.

Hazel had vouched for him. I still hadn't decided how I felt about that. I was working on it. Grief makes you a slow accountant.

* * *

The bell tower smelled of river mud and pigeon and, underneath both, of her. I don't mean that mystically. I mean she'd spent nine days up here in June of last year—I knew because her field journal logged it, hour by hour, in that handwriting that slanted forward like it was late for something—and the tower remembered her the way old buildings remember anyone who sat still long enough: in the wax stubs, the coffee ring on the sill, the pencil marks on the beam where she'd measured something twice because once wasn't honest enough.

Elder Sefa climbed the stairs behind me, slower now, one hand on the wall.

"You've been up here three days," she said.

"Someone has to sort it."

"Someone has to eat. Someone has to sleep. Someone has to let the sorting take longer than a week, because the sorting is going to take years." She lowered herself onto the sill, which creaked a protest she ignored. "Show me what you have."

So I showed her. This was the thing I'd been dreading and wanting in equal measure: laying it all out for the valley's own eyes instead of keeping it as mine, as the expedition's, as the dead woman's secret. The true will. The unaltered mineral surveys. The correspondence proving the forgery. And the journal—her journal—which was the part I hadn't shown anyone yet, because it wasn't evidence, it was flesh.

Sefa read the will first. Slowly. Her lips moved over the legal language, and when she reached the clause that transferred everything—all documentation, all claims, all rights of discovery—to "the peoples of the valley, jointly and without hierarchy, for stewardship in perpetuity," she read it aloud, twice, in her own language and then in English, as if testing whether it survived translation.

"She did not leave it to us," Sefa said finally. "She left it *with* us. There is a difference."

"I know."

"Do you? You have been holding this case like it will hatch. A steward sets things down where others can reach them." She tapped the surveys. "These numbers. What do they say?"

"They say the valley sits on one of the largest rare-earth deposits in the hemisphere. They say it can be mined carefully or mined fast, and that fast pays ten times more and kills the river."

"And who decides?"

"That's—" I stopped. Because the honest answer was that I didn't know, that no document decides that, that a will is a door and not a room. "That's what has to be worked out. By everyone. For years. Probably badly, sometimes."

Sefa smiled, and it was the first smile I'd seen from her that had weight behind it. "Good. If it were easy, they would send men like Ashgrave's to make it easy."

* * *

Ms. Ashgrave herself was pulled from the river two days after the barge went down, alive, soaked, and—according to the soldiers—already drafting a statement blaming a rogue regional security contractor. They fished her out near the confluence where Tuck had been shot, which felt like the river making a point it didn't bother to explain. She'd survive. People like her always survive; they're built low to the ground, emotionally speaking, hard to topple, easy to relocate. There would be inquiries. There would be hearings in rooms with better air conditioning than this valley has ever seen. And somewhere in those hearings, a transcript would include the words *pending investigation*, and someone would sign something, and the machine would grind on.

But not here. Here, the ledger was public. Here, the patrols had mutinied politely, by listening. Here, a dead woman's voice had gone out over every frequency for six hours while Hazel read until her voice cracked, and you cannot un-hear a thing like that, not if you're a corporal with a family two bends downriver, not if you're a clerk who sold one signature too many.

Tuck was recovering in the fishing village, third house past the drying racks, propped up on mats and fed constantly by women who had decided he belonged to them now. He'd lost blood and gained about forty new grandmothers. When I visited, he complained about the soup. It was the happiest complaining I've ever heard.

And Ilya—

Ilya came back. Walked out of the mangroves on the second day, thin, rope-scarred, carrying his survey kit like a penitent carries candles. Nobody shot him. Hazel looked at him for a long moment and said, "Your ledger's not balanced. But it's open. Sit down." He'd been sitting at the edge of every gathering since, saying little, drawing maps—new maps, honest ones, flood lines and safe ground and where the tailings had spread and how long the river would need to flush itself clean. His maps used to erase people. Now they were the tool everyone used to find them.

* * *

On the sixth day, Sefa brought the other elders up into the tower, and we began.

Not deciding—we began *cataloguing*. That was the word we kept using, deliberately, because catalogue is a humble word, a librarian's word, and what we were doing felt like it should be either sacred or criminal and humility kept it human. We laid out Dr. Osei's legacy across the tower floor: the will, the surveys, the correspondence, the journal, and then the things the elders added from their side—boundary songs, fishing rights memorized across generations, a map stitched into a wedding cloth that matched Ilya's hydrology within a hundred meters, which made Ilya laugh for the first time since I'd known him, a short startled sound like a man finding money in an old coat.

Item by item. Number, description, condition, custodian. My handwriting, because I was the protégé and that was my job now, the job she'd actually left me, though the will didn't mention it. The job of writing things down so they can't be quietly rewritten.

At dusk we reached the journal. I hadn't opened it in front of anyone. I opened it now, to the last written page, dated the day before her boat went down.

She'd known. That's the thing I keep returning to, the thing that sits in my chest like a swallowed stone. She'd known they were coming for her, and she'd spent her last evening not fleeing but writing—a final entry that wasn't fear, wasn't farewell, but instructions. Notes on the deposit's extraction rates. Warnings about the tailings dam, observed and measured, flagged in red months before it failed. And at the bottom, a single line addressed to whoever read next:

*"If you are reading this, the valley held. Tell them the land was never mine to give—I only refused to let it be taken. The rest is their work."*

Nobody spoke for a while. Down below, the river ran brown and busy, hauling the last of the tailings toward the sea, cleaning itself the slow way, the only way rivers know.

Then Sefa said, "Read it again," and I did, and we wrote it into the catalogue as item one.

* * *

The relief boats kept coming. The investigators kept writing. Somewhere downriver, Denny Vane was discovering that his cheerful arithmetic had developed a hole in the hull—but that was Hazel's account to settle, not mine, and she'd gone down to meet his boat that morning with the expression of a woman who has finally found a negotiation she enjoys.

I stayed in the tower. There was so much of her, still—boxes in the mission cellar, samples, letters, a second journal nobody had known about. Years of work. Decades of stewardship ahead, and it would be done badly sometimes, and argued over endlessly, and that was fine. That was the inheritance: not the rare earths, not the paper, but the argument itself, conducted in the open, by people who intended to still be here when the lawyers' grandchildren retired.

On the seventh evening, I catalogued the last box and wrote the final entry, and then I sat with the ledger of the dead—eleven names from Sefa's village, plus the ones from upstream, plus hers, plus Tuck's wounded, plus everyone the water had taken without leaving a name to file—and I understood at last why she'd written what she'd written.

The land was never hers to give. It was never anyone's to give. It could only be kept.

I closed the book. Below, lamps were coming on along the banks, one by one, village by village, and far downriver—so far it was almost imagination—a storm cell was stacking itself against the horizon, and a small repainted boat with a new name on its stern was pointing straight at it.

I hoped the name on the bow brought her luck.

I suspected she'd bring the storm luck instead.
