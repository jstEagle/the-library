## 21. The Vane Brothers' Arithmetic

Denny Vane arrived on the ninth day after the flood, which told me two things: the river was open again, and somebody upstream had finally paid him enough to make the trip worth his while.

I watched his boat come around the last bend of the channel below the mission, riding high and empty in the way that collection boats always ride—no cargo, no crew beyond muscle, just a man and a ledger and the cheerful certainty that arithmetic always wins. He'd repainted it. The old hull had said *VANE BROS. MARINE FINANCE* in letters you could read from a kilometer off; now it said *VANE & ASSOCIATES*, which meant either expansion or bankruptcy, and with Denny it was usually both at once.

Tuck stood beside me on the bank with his arm still in a sling and his opinion already loaded. "That's a man who smiles before he shoots," he said.

"That's a man who invoices before he shoots," I said. "The smiling is free."

Nadia was up at the mission with the elders, cataloguing Dr. Osei's papers into something the relief investigators could actually use, so it was going to be me on the dock when Denny stepped ashore. Fine. This was always going to be my conversation. You don't send a protégé to settle your debts; that's how protégés become collateral.

He came down the gangplank with his hands in his pockets like a tourist, and God help me, he looked *good*. Tan, rested, a linen shirt that had never once been rained on. The monsoon had chewed this valley down to its ribs for three weeks and Denny Vane had apparently spent it somewhere with umbrellas.

"Hazel Sandoval." He spread his arms like we were family. "You look terrible."

"You look expensive."

"Success agrees with me." He glanced past me at the boat—the *Osei*, renamed and repainted, patched hull and all—and his eyebrows did a little dance of professional appraisal. "New name. Sentimental. That's a lien-able asset, you know. Renaming doesn't confuse the paperwork."

"Nothing confuses your paperwork, Denny. That's your whole personality."

He laughed, because Denny laughed at everything, and that was the thing people never understood about the Vane brothers until it was too late. Everyone expected loan sharks to be wolves. Nobody prepared for retrievers. Denny would fetch your boat, your house, your father's engine shop, wagging the whole way, and he'd genuinely feel bad about it afterward, over dinner, somewhere nice.

"Can we walk?" he said. "I find conversations about money go better in motion. Standing still, people feel cornered. Moving, they feel like they're going somewhere."

"We're not going anywhere, Denny."

"See, that's the spirit of honesty I've always liked in you." He fell into step beside me anyway, and we walked the muddy length of the dock while behind us his two crewmen stayed on the boat, pretending to inspect an outboard motor with the focused boredom of men being paid to be visible.

* * *

Here's what I knew by then, because Ennis's defected officers had kept me current: Corvane Extraction was finished in this valley, at least in the form that had bought magistrates and run down scientists' boats in the dark. Ms. Ashgrave's barge was on the bottom of the confluence with most of her seized ore and several tons of her credibility. The regional office had gone quiet in the way offices go quiet when lawyers start arriving in numbers. And the name Corvane was on every tongue from the fishing villages to the magistrate's own holding cell, spoken with the flat disgust people usually reserve for weather.

Which meant Denny Vane's new employers were, as of roughly nine days ago, radioactive.

He didn't know that yet. Or—he knew it and hadn't done the arithmetic. That was the flaw in the Vane brothers' whole operation, the crack running through all that cheerful competence: they understood interest rates perfectly and reputations not at all. They thought debt was physics. It's not. Debt is weather. It moves with the conditions.

"So," Denny said, hands still pocketed, stepping carefully over a rope some fisherman had left coiled like a trap. "Let's review. You went upriver on a salvage contract. Contract value, forty thousand against your balance. You found no salvage. You returned no surveyor—Ilya Reyes remains unaccounted for, and my new friends were very specific that Reyes and the original plates were the deliverables." He clicked his tongue. "And the contract itself? Issued through a broker whose surety was underwritten by—" he tapped his own chest, delighted, "—us. So technically, Hazel, you took a job from me, failed it, and now I'm here to discuss the difference."

"The difference is four hundred thousand dollars," I said. "Give or take the eleven years of interest you've been compounding on my father's loan like it was a religion."

"Forty-one-two, as of Tuesday." He said it warmly, the way other men say their daughter's name. "But who's counting?"

"I am. Constantly. It's the soundtrack of my life."

"And here's where I have good news." He stopped walking and turned to face me, and I got the full Denny—the open face, the hurt sincerity, the eyes of a man who wants so badly for you to understand that he's on your side. "Corvane acquired your paper. All of it. Your father's loan, the interest, the works. Which means I'm not here as your creditor anymore, Hazel. I'm here as your *partner*. You bring me Ilya Reyes and those survey plates, and the whole balance goes to zero. Zero! Forty-one thousand dollars, forgiven, for one fat little surveyor and a tube of maps. That's not a debt collection, that's a lottery ticket with legs."

I looked at him for a long moment.

"Denny," I said, "do you know what happened to Ashgrave?"

His smile flickered—one frame of static across the retriever's face. "There were... reports. Flooding. Tragic."

"There were reports, yeah. Here's mine. I was there. I read Amara Osei's will out loud on every frequency this valley can receive, and I handed copies to elders, to police officers, to a corporal who watched her die and decided to stop pretending otherwise. Every person in this valley knows the name Corvane. Not the company—the *name*. The way you know the name of the dog that bit you." I let that sit. "Your new partners aren't buying anything upriver ever again. Their claim is void pending investigation. Their director is explaining herself to people with clipboards. And you're standing on their dock wearing their colors, asking me to hand them the one witness who can bury them completely."

* * *

He recovered well. I'll give him that. Denny's recovery time was the best thing about him.

"Corporations are resilient," he said. "Names get rehabilitated. There'll be a rebrand, a settlement fund, a documentary where a former executive cries tastefully. In five years nobody remembers."

"In five years nobody remembers," I agreed. "But I remember now. And so do about nine thousand people who live between here and the headwaters, and who just spent three weeks learning exactly what a company deed looks like when it's used as a weapon. You want to be the man who came to drag their witness back to them? Walk up to the mission right now. Introduce yourself. Tell Nadia Okonkwo what you're collecting. She'll give you tea. She'll be very polite. And by nightfall every kid on this river will know the *Vane & Associates* paint job the way they know crocodile slides."

"That's a threat, Hazel."

"It's a forecast. You of all people should respect those. You made your living predicting what people would do when squeezed." I turned and started walking back toward the boat, because I wanted him following me, not facing me. Small thing. Matters more than you'd think. "Come on. Let me show you my books."

"My favorite genre."

We reached the *Osei* and I sat on the gunwale and he stood in the mud, which felt correct. Tuck drifted over and leaned on a piling within easy earshot, radiating the specific menace of a man with one working arm and no patience left.

"Here's the salvage report," I said. "Item one: no Ilya. He traded himself to Ashgrave's people as bait so the rest of us could live. Last I saw him he was being walked onto a barge that's now part of the riverbed. If he's alive, he's in a legal system that has his testimony and doesn't need yours. Item two: no plates. The originals went into a sealed case that's currently sitting in front of three separate investigators and a village council. Item three: no payment, because the salvage cargo went into the flood along with everything else I was supposed to sell." I shrugged. "So the contract failed. You're right. Bill me."

"I intend to."

"But here's the asset I *do* have, Denny. The only one." I hopped down and stood close, close enough that he had to decide whether to step back, and he didn't, because Denny never conceded ground even when it was flooding. "My testimony. Everything I saw. The forged deed dated three days after a death that the tide tables say couldn't have happened that way. The patrol boats. The run-down skiff. Ashgrave offering Ilya his old life back over coffee while her dam groaned like a dying animal upstream. Names, dates, coordinates. I kept a log the whole way up—not because I'm thorough, though I am, but because I grew up with people like you and I learned early that paper is the only knife that stays sharp."

Denny's smile was still there but it had gone thin, stretched over something working underneath. "Testimony against whom?"

"Against whoever's still standing when the investigations finish. Right now that's a short list, and you're on it. You're the local instrument, Denny. You're how Corvane touched this valley without dirtying its own gloves. Somebody's going to ask who financed the brokers, who held the paper on the chasers, who kept the pressure on. And when they ask, I can answer one of two ways." I let the pause do its work. "I can stand up in whatever room they put me in and describe, in detail, eleven years of the Vane brothers' arithmetic—including the part where you bought my father's loan knowing he was dying, because distressed assets appreciate. Or I can say the words I'm about to say next, which are: *Hazel Sandoval settled her accounts privately and has nothing further to add.*"

The river moved under the dock. Somewhere downstream a kid was yelling about a fish. Denny Vane stood in the mud of a valley he'd helped bleed and did the math, and I watched him do it, and it was the most honest I'd ever seen him—no smile, no warmth, just the bare machinery ticking over.

"You're asking me to eat forty-one thousand dollars," he said finally.

"I'm asking you to price your reputation against my silence. You're the one who taught me everything has a number. Run it."

"And if I say no? If I take the boat instead?" His chin tipped toward the *Osei*. "Two of my boys are right there. It would be regrettable, but assets get repossessed every day."

"Then you'd be doing it in front of witnesses, in a valley where your employers' name is a curse word, three days after their barge sank, with a police detachment up at the mission that includes a former Corvane corporal who'd love nothing more than a reason to be useful. And I'd testify anyway, from wherever they put you, and my log would testify too, and logs don't drown." I smiled at him, and it wasn't a nice smile, and I didn't pretend otherwise. "Also, Tuck would be upset, and he's been looking forward to being upset at someone all week."

Tuck cracked his knuckles on his good hand. It sounded like a door closing.

* * *

Denny Vane left an hour later, riding low for the first time all day, weighted down with a signed statement of account that read, in full: *Balance discharged by mutual agreement. No further obligations exist between the parties.* He'd written it himself, in his beautiful bookkeeper's hand, because that was the one language he trusted, and I'd watched him write it, and I'd signed it, and then I'd given him a copy of my log anyway.

Not the real one. A copy. But he didn't know that, and he never would, and the not-knowing was the point. Leverage isn't the knife. It's the sound of the sheath.

At the gangplank he paused, and for a second—just a second—the retriever looked out from behind the accountant, tired and almost human.

"Your dad would've hated this," he said. "All of it. The river, the risk, the—" he gestured vaguely at the burned valley, the flooded fields, the whole wet ruin of it. "He was a keep-your-head-down man."

"He was a keep-your-head-down man who died owing money to people like you," I said. "I think he'd have understood the arithmetic eventually."

Denny laughed at that—a real one, short and surprised—and then he was down the plank and into his boat and gone around the bend, and the wake rocked the *Osei* against her lines like a slow heartbeat.

Tuck came and stood beside me. We watched the water close over the channel.

"Feel different?" he asked.

I checked, the way you check a wound. The number was still there—forty-one thousand dollars doesn't evaporate, it just changes custody, and somewhere in a filing cabinet a piece of paper still says I owe. But the weight of it had shifted. For eleven years that debt had been a hand on the back of my neck, steering. Now it was just a fact, filed among facts, and the hand was gone, and what remained was mine: the boat, the log, the name, the long gray water running south.

"No," I said. "Feels the same. Just lighter than I remembered."

Tuck snorted. "That's what lighter feels like. You forget."

Downstream, past the last of the flood debris, the sky over the delta had gone that bruised afternoon color I knew better than my own handwriting, and the wind had picked up the smell of a storm cell stacking itself somewhere out past the horizon—big, organized, going somewhere. The kind of sky that used to mean *run* and now just meant *weather*.

I untied the first line.

"Tuck," I said. "How do you feel about the coast?"
