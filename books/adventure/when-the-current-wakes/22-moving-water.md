## 22. Moving Water

The boatyard at Puerto Cielo smelled like turpentine and burnt sugar, which is what happens when a town tries to be a port and a festival ground at the same time and doesn't fully commit to either.

I'd been there nine days. Nine days of scraping, sanding, patching, repainting—work I'd done a hundred times before on boats that didn't have names yet, work that usually felt like penance and this time felt like something closer to a conversation. The hull had taken the surge better than I deserved. Tuck's patch job in the mangroves had held through the worst water I've ever put a boat into, and the yard crew here kept shaking their heads over it like it was a religious artifact.

"Whoever did this," the old shipwright said, running his thumb along the tarpaulin-and-resin seam he was replacing with actual fiberglass, "they were thinking about the person who'd have to fix it later."

"He was," I said. "That was most of what he thought about."

Tuck was alive. That still required saying to myself some mornings, the way you check a pocket for keys you're sure are there. The bullet had gone through the meat of his shoulder and out the other side, missing everything that mattered by margins that would make an actuary weep with joy. He'd spent two weeks in a clinic in the valley with Nadia Okonkwo's people fussing over him, and now he was upriver helping catalog Dr. Osei's legacy, sending me messages that read like a man discovering that work could mean something: *Found another crate of her soil samples today. The elders argue about where to build the school. Arguing is good. Arguing means they think they'll be here.*

He was right. Arguing is good. I'd spent years in places where nobody argued because arguing meant you believed tomorrow was negotiable.

* * *

The renaming happened on day six, mostly because I ran out of excuses.

The old name had been the *Cassandra*—my father's choice, back when he still named things after women who warned people and weren't believed. It had been a good joke and then it hadn't been, the way jokes about debt tend to go. When the Vane brothers took their lien on the boat, Denny had suggested, cheerfully, that I paint *VANE ASSETS #4* on the transom, and I'd told him I'd paint it when he pried the brush from my cold hand, and he'd laughed like that was the best offer anyone had made him all year.

Now the lien was frozen—not forgiven, not paid, just frozen, a glacier of arithmetic sitting on my ledger that neither side wanted to thaw first. My testimony sat quiet in exchange for the debt staying quiet. It wasn't freedom. But it was the shape freedom makes from a distance, seen through fog, and I'd learned to take shapes.

So I painted over *CASSANDRA* in white, let it dry, and then painted the new name in letters tall enough to read across a harbor:

*AMARA OSEI.*

The shipwright watched me do it, sucking his teeth. "A scientist? You naming a storm boat after a scientist?"

"A woman who read water," I said. "Same trade, different instruments."

He considered that, then nodded like I'd passed some local exam. "She'll run lighter with that name. Boats always know."

Boats always know. I used to think that was superstition. Then I spent a monsoon season learning that the river has opinions, that dams groan before they fail, that men in company colors run low on fuel before they run low on nerve, and that every system—natural or human—telegraphs its collapse to anyone patient enough to watch. Reading water turned out to be the same skill as reading people. Both of them lie constantly, but they lie in patterns.

* * *

Denny Vane came on day eight.

I heard his outboard before I saw him—that particular cheerful sputter of an engine maintained exactly well enough—and by the time he tied off at my slip I already had two coffees going, because if you can't beat dangerous optimists you might as well caffeinate them.

"Hazel Sandoval." He stepped onto the dock with his arms open like we were family reunion material. He looked the same as always: sunburned, silver-toothed, dressed like a man who'd been told about yachts secondhand. "You look terrible. Monsoon agree with you?"

"It nearly killed me, so yes, extremely agreeable."

"That's the spirit." He took the coffee, examined the fresh paint on the transom, and whistled low. "*Amara Osei.* Huh. Named after a dead woman whose valley your testimony helped un-steal. There's poetry in that. There's also, and I want to be clear this is not a threat, merely weather reporting—a lot of very unhappy people upstream who remember your name."

"I counted on that."

"Course you did. That's why you're you." He leaned against the rail, and for a moment the cheerfulness thinned and I saw the accountant underneath, the one who'd inherited his father's ledger along with his smile. "Status report, since you'll ask anyway. Corvane's under investigation in three jurisdictions. Ashgrave's lawyers are earning their keep, which means she'll walk, but the claim's void pending review and the review's being handled by people who've actually read tide tables. Your debt's frozen. Not dead. Frozen. There's a difference, and the difference is that someday somebody buys that paper again, and someday somebody comes collecting."

"I know how paper works, Denny."

"You do. Better than most." He drank his coffee. "Here's the thing I came to say, and I'll only say it once, so enjoy it. My brother thinks we should sell your debt to one of the unhappy people upstream. Let them come at you sideways, through courts or worse, and wash our hands. I told him no."

I waited. With Denny you always waited; the real sentence came after the pause, like thunder after lightning.

"Because," he said finally, "you're worth more quiet than collected. A woman who burned down a mining syndicate with a radio broadcast and walked away owing us money—that woman is a story. Stories appreciate. Dead women or jailed women, they depreciate. So the debt stays frozen as long as you stay interesting and alive, and the moment you stop being either, all bets resume." He smiled his silver smile and set down the cup. "Congratulations, Hazel. You've achieved job security."

"That's the least flattering compliment I've ever received."

"And yet accurate, which puts it ahead of most compliments." He untied his line, paused, and added, almost gently, which was somehow worse: "For what it's worth—I read the transcript of her will. The doctor's. Whole thing, front to back, twice. 'I leave the river to the people who live beside it, because they are the only ones who will miss it.'" He shook his head. "Nobody's ever left me anything but collateral. Anyway. Fair winds, Sandoval. Stay interesting."

His outboard sputtered away down the channel, and I stood there holding my own coffee and feeling something I couldn't immediately name. It took me until the cup went cold to figure it out.

It was the absence of dread. For six years, every knock, every engine, every envelope had been the Vane brothers arriving in my chest. And now a Vane brother had literally arrived, delivered his threat wrapped in a compliment, motored off—and nothing in me had clenched. The fear was still there somewhere, the way scar tissue is still there. But it wasn't running the show anymore.

Moving, I realized, finally cost nothing. That was the whole revelation, and it had taken a flooded church, a failing dam, a shot friend, and a drowned syndicate to teach it to me. Stopping had always been the expensive thing. Stopping was where the interest accrued—in money, in guilt, in the stories you tell yourself about why you can't turn around.

* * *

On day nine, the horizon started stacking.

I was up the mast at dawn checking the rigging when I saw them: storm cells building over the eastern sea, tower after tower, flat-bottomed and hard-edged, organized in a way that disorganized weather never bothers to be. Not monsoon squalls. Something with a plan. By noon the barometer had fallen twelve points and the fishing fleet was coming in early, which fishermen only do when the sky has said something rude to them personally.

The harbormaster found me studying it. "You're the chaser," she said. It wasn't a question; word travels down rivers faster than water does. "That system's going to make landfall north of here inside three days. Coastal towns, the low barrier islands. They've got maybe forty hours of sensible warning time and a mayor who doesn't believe forecasts that come from women."

"Charming. Sounds familiar."

"There's a relief coordinator passing through on the afternoon packet boat. She's been asking if anybody with equipment and a death wish is available."

I looked at the cells stacking on the horizon, beautiful and terrible, doing their slow arithmetic of wind and water. Somewhere under them were towns full of people who'd been told the sea was far away and permanent, the same lie everyone tells themselves about everything until the current wakes.

I thought about stopping. Genuinely, for a whole minute, I tried. I had a repaired boat, a frozen debt, a name that belonged to me again, and a legitimate excuse to sit in a sugary port town drinking rum while other people did the worrying. Rest was available. Rest was, for the first time in six years, affordable.

But rest, it turns out, is a place, and I'd never been any good at places.

* * *

I provisioned that afternoon like a woman possessed, because I was, mildly, by the old familiar demon—the one that whispers *someone has to read this water and it may as well be you*. Fuel drums. Water. The good medical kit, restocked. Charts of the northern coast that I spread across the cockpit table and annotated by lantern light while the first outer bands of cloud slid overhead like a hand testing a door.

Tuck messaged from upriver: *Heard there's weather coming. Heard you're probably already pointing at it. Don't die. The elders want to name a reading room after you and it would be embarrassing.*

*I'm not dying,* I typed back. *I'm employed.*

Nadia sent a longer message, the kind she wrote when she was really writing to Dr. Osei through me: *Amara used to say that the opposite of inheritance isn't loss—it's stewardship. That whatever you carry, you carry forward, not just for yourself. Go be someone's warning. Come back and tell us about it.*

I painted nothing else on the hull. The name was enough. *Amara Osei*, heading into weather, carrying forward.

At dusk I cast off. The packet boat was due at dawn with the relief coordinator aboard, and I intended to meet it at the channel mouth, because forty hours is not a lot of warning and I have never once in my life arrived anywhere early except for disasters.

The harbor lights fell behind. The swells came long and low out of the east, the sea clearing its throat. On the horizon the storm cells stacked higher, lit from within by their own private lightnings, like a city deciding whether to burn.

I put the bow into it and opened the throttle, and the boat—her, the *Osei*, patched and renamed and finally mine—rose to meet the swell the way she was built to, the way I was built to, without hesitation, without debt, without anything weighing her down but fuel and purpose.

Somewhere out there was a coastline that needed a chaser who reads water.

And moving, at last, cost nothing at all.
