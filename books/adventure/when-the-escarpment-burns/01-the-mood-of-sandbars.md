## 1. The Mood of Sandbars

The Alder River was lying to him again.

Silas Voss stood at the wheel of the *Marten*, reading the water the way other men read faces, and the water was doing its best to look innocent. A long, glassy sheet of brown-green stretching from bank to bank, smooth as poured tea. Any fool would take it for deep water. Silas knew better. Smooth like that, in late summer, in a dry season this mean, meant one thing: sandbar, broad and shallow and patient as a debt collector.

"Left side's lying," he called down to the deck. "Right side's worse."

Wren Halloway looked up from where she was coiling rope with the aggressive efficiency of someone who thought coiling rope should be done faster than God intended. She was nineteen, all elbows and opinions, with a smudge of tar on her cheek that had been there since Tuesday and no apparent plans to leave.

"How can both sides be lying?"

"Because it's a river," Silas said. "Lying is the whole job."

He eased the wheel a hair starboard, then corrected, threading the needle between two lies. The *Marten* drew four feet loaded, and the channel through the Narrows—everyone called it the Narrows, though it was really just the river pinching itself between old gravel shoals—ran closer to five on a good year. This was not a good year. The rains had quit in June and never come back, and the Alder had been shrinking all summer like a man pulling in his belt before asking for credit.

"Sound!" he barked.

Tomas wasn't aboard yet—that came later, though Silas didn't know it yet—but Wren swung the sounding pole off its brackets and drove it down off the bow. The pole went in, and kept going, and stopped at three and a half feet.

"Three and a half!" she shouted back.

"Where?"

"Everywhere I can reach!"

Which meant the bar had moved. Bars did that. A sandbar was not a thing; it was an argument the river was having with itself, and every freshet and every drought changed the terms. The charts in Silas's cabin were three years old and worth exactly what he'd paid for them, which was nothing, because he'd won them at cards from a man who'd then tried to win them back and lost his boots instead.

Silas cut the engine to half and let the current carry them, listening now more than steering. You could hear a bar if you were quiet enough—a soft hushing under the hull, like the river whispering *not here, not here*—and you could feel it in the wheel, a faint deadness when the water went thin beneath the rudder. He'd learned that trick somewhere he didn't talk about, on water bigger and colder and considerably less friendly than the Alder, and it had kept him alive in places where charts were a rumor and soundings were a luxury.

"Hush on the left," he murmured. "Feel it?"

Wren cocked her head. "I feel a boat about to run aground."

"That's the same thing." He nudged the wheel. "There. Feel how she goes light? That's the bar saying hello. We say hello back by going around."

The *Marten* slid past the shoal with six inches to spare, close enough that Wren could have leaned over the rail and trailed her fingers in the shallows, and then the channel opened up and the river exhaled and so did Wren.

"You know," she said, coming aft, "most people would dredge a channel. Mark it. Put up stakes."

"Stakes move."

"So do we, apparently. Forever."

Silas let that one go by the way he let most things go by, which was to pretend he hadn't heard them. He was forty-one years old and built like the boats he favored: plain, sturdy, designed for work rather than admiration, with a face that invited strangers to tell him their troubles and a habit of forgetting those troubles by morning. His beard was going gray at the edges in a way he suspected made him look distinguished and feared actually made him look tired. His eyes, gray as river fog, missed very little and admitted even less.

They came around the bend below Harrow's Landing, and there it was: the town, such as it was. A grain elevator leaning like a drunk against a lamppost. A dock of gray planks worn soft as soap. Six buildings, seven if you counted the church, which Silas generally didn't, on the grounds that it counted on him often enough during flood season.

"Debts," Wren said, in the tone of someone continuing a conversation he'd refused to have.

"What about them?"

"Yours. You've got the cooper's invoice from Millbrook, the feed bill from Two Rivers, and whatever you owe the woman at the Harrow's Landing chandlery, whose name, I'd remind you, is Mrs. Okafor, and who terrifies me."

"She doesn't terrify you."

"She absolutely does. She once made me return a coil of rope because it was 'insultingly coiled.' I was fourteen."

"And yet your coils are beautiful now." Silas brought the *Marten* alongside the dock with a gentleness that seemed impossible for a hundred-ton boat, kissing the planks so lightly the fenders barely sighed. "We pay Mrs. Okafor first."

"We always pay everyone first. That's the strange part." Wren dropped the mooring lines over the bollards and fixed him with a look. "You settle up everywhere we stop. Every debt, every time, down to the penny. And then we leave before the ink's dry. Most captains run tabs. You run away from tabs you've already paid."

"I don't like owing people."

"Nobody likes owing people. But most people manage to be owed *and* stay for supper." She crossed her arms. "You know what I think?"

"I suspect I'm about to find out."

"I think you're not running from debts. Debts are just the excuse. I think you're running from something else, and paying everybody off is how you make sure nobody has a reason to come looking for you."

The silence that followed lasted exactly long enough to be an answer, and Silas filled it by pretending to check the engine telegraph, which did not need checking, and had not needed checking since 1908.

"Mrs. Okafor," he said. "Then the cooper's man. Then feed. Then we take on grain for Calloway Bend and are gone by dawn."

Wren held his gaze a moment longer, then shrugged and let it drop, which was her way. She pushed hard only when pushing might work, and after six years on the *Marten* she knew which doors in Silas Voss were painted on.

"There's a passenger waiting on the dock," she said instead. "Been there since before we tied up. Woman and a boy. They've been watching us like we owe *them* something."

"People always watch us like that."

"Not like this. Like they've been watching for a long time." Wren squinted down the dock. "And the woman's got a bag packed like she means to be on this boat whether you say yes or no."

Silas followed her gaze. At the far end of the landing, half in the shade of the elevator, stood two figures. A woman, mid-thirties maybe, dressed in travel-worn clothes that had been good once and were determined to be good again. Beside her a boy of fifteen or so, tall for his age and trying to hide it by slouching, with the particular watchfulness of a child who had learned that adults were weather—changeable, occasionally violent, best tracked from a distance.

They weren't looking at the boat. They were looking at him. Both of them, at once, the way you'd look at a door you'd been knocking on for years.

Something cold moved through Silas then, brief and specific, like a fish brushing his ankle in dark water. He'd spent eleven years building a life with no handles on it—no history anyone could grab, no debts unpaid, no name worth remembering—and he had gotten very good at not being found. It was, in fact, the only thing he'd ever been truly great at besides piloting.

Nobody looked at Silas Voss like that. Nobody looked at him at all. That was the arrangement.

"Probably want passage to Calloway Bend," he said.

"Probably," Wren agreed, in a voice that said *probably not*.

* * *

Mrs. Okafor's chandlery smelled of hemp, pitch, and the particular authority of its owner, who stood behind her counter like a lighthouse behind its lamp and surveyed Silas with the expression of a woman who had already located his soul and found it wanting but serviceable.

"Voss." She said it the way she said everything, flat and final, a stamp on an envelope. "You owe me for the deck paint, the lamp oil, and the coffee, which I notice you buy the cheap kind and drink like it's the expensive kind."

"The cheap kind tastes better if you believe in it."

"It doesn't. That's why I sell the expensive kind." She totaled the ledger without looking at it, because Mrs. Okafor carried her ledgers in her head, where no one could steal them. "Eleven dollars and forty cents."

Silas counted out the money, counting it twice, laying each coin down flat so she could see it was honest. She swept it into the drawer and then, instead of dismissing him, folded her hands and looked at him over the counter with something almost like curiosity, which from Mrs. Okafor was roughly equivalent to a hug from anyone else.

"You paid the cooper's man this morning," she said. "Before your boat was even cool. And the feed store. And I hear you left money with the widow Pram for the window your deckhand broke last spring, which she never asked for and which you never mentioned."

"Wren throws like a girl who grew up throwing."

"You settle everything." Her eyes narrowed. "Every port, every time. Men who intend to come back leave things unsettled. It gives them a reason. You leave nothing anywhere, Voss. Not a tab, not a favor, not a toothbrush. You know what that tells a woman who's watched this dock for thirty years?"

"That I'm tidy."

"It tells me you're running." She said it without drama, the way she'd report the price of nails. "I don't know from what. Don't want to. But running men pay fast and sleep light, and I've buried enough of both to recognize the breed." She slid a paper sack across the counter—the expensive coffee, unasked for, unpriced. "For the river. Buy the good kind next time. Life's short, and yours looks shorter than most."

Silas took the sack because refusing Mrs. Okafor was not among the available options. "Thank you," he said, and meant it, and hated that she'd seen through him anyway, and hated more that part of him was already composing the goodbye.

That was the trick of it, the ugly little machinery of his life: he arrived, he worked, he paid, he charmed just enough to be remembered fondly and not enough to be remembered specifically, and then some morning—not any particular morning, just the next one—he cast off and didn't come back. Millbrook. Two Rivers. Granger's Slough. He could chart his whole eleven years on the Alder by the towns he'd been decent to and then abandoned, like a man leaving small gifts at a series of doors he couldn't bring himself to knock on twice.

It wasn't cruelty. He genuinely liked these people. That was the problem. Every town had someone like Mrs. Okafor, someone like the cooper's laughing daughters, someone who made him think *maybe this one, maybe here*—and every time, the thought arrived hand in hand with its twin, the older, colder thought, the one with teeth: *they'll find out what you were. Stay long enough and they always find out.*

So he paid his debts and kept his bags mentally packed and told himself it was prudence, and the river carried him on, and the moving felt, most days, almost like freedom.

Almost.

* * *

The woman and the boy were still on the dock when he came back, and they did not approach, which interested him. People who wanted something usually approached. These two waited like anglers who understood that the fish comes to you or not at all.

The boy saw him first. Silas watched it happen—the kid's chin lifting, the nudge of an elbow, the way both of them straightened slightly, the way you do when the person you've traveled a long way to find finally walks toward you and turns out to be real.

Up close, the woman had eyes the color of strong tea and a face that had been handsome before hardship got its hands on it, and hardship had not been gentle. There was a scar through her left eyebrow, old and clean. She held herself like someone who had practiced standing straight in places where standing straight could get you killed.

"Captain Voss?" Her accent was faintly foreign, vowels rounded a little wrong, music from a country Silas had spent eleven years not thinking about.

"That's me."

"Ilsa Brandt." She didn't offer a hand; she offered her attention, full and level, which was somehow more disarming. "This is my brother, Tomas."

The boy nodded once, wary as a cat at a dog show.

"We need passage," Ilsa said. "Upstream. As far as you'll go."

"Passage costs money."

"We have some."

"Passage also requires a destination, and 'as far as you'll go' isn't one."

Something flickered across her face—not anger. Recognition, almost. As if she'd expected the wall and was simply confirming its dimensions. Behind her, Tomas shifted his weight, and Silas caught the boy's eyes flicking—not to his face, but lower, to his hands, his build, the set of his shoulders, cataloguing him the way soldiers catalogue. The cold fish brushed Silas's ankle again.

"Where are you headed?" Ilsa asked.

"Calloway Bend, with grain. After that—" He shrugged. "After that depends on the river."

"And after that?"

"After that isn't your business, ma'am."

"No," she agreed quietly. "But it may become so." She reached into her bag, and Silas's hand drifted—without his quite deciding it—to the wheelhouse, to the shotgun racked there, and Ilsa noticed the drift and paused, and slowly, deliberately, drew out not a weapon but a folded square of oilcloth, worn soft at the creases.

She didn't open it. She just held it, and looked at him, and said, "We're looking for something that came off the bottom of this river. Something old. Something with a seal on it that hasn't existed for eleven years." Her voice stayed gentle, almost apologetic, the way you speak to a man you're about to hurt. "And Captain Voss, I'm given to understand there was a storm last week above Harrow's Bend. Bad one. Tore the channel up. Dredged things loose that have been down a long while."

The river whispered under the dock planks. *Not here, not here.*

"I wouldn't know anything about that," Silas said.

"No," Ilsa Brandt said, and her tea-colored eyes did not blink. "I imagine you wouldn't."

Behind her, the boy Tomas watched Silas's hands, and Silas realized, with a sensation like the deck tilting a degree off true, that they had been watching his hands the whole time—and that somewhere upstream, in the flooded dark below a drowned village, a chest sat in the hold of the *Marten* under a tarp and a lie, wearing a seal from a country that no longer existed, waiting for him to decide what kind of man he was going to be this time.

He smiled his blandest river smile, the one that had charmed a dozen towns and told them nothing.

"Safe travels, ma'am," he said, and walked up his gangplank, and began making ready to leave Harrow's Landing by dawn.
