## 2. What the Dredge Brings Up

The storm had spent itself against the hills by dawn, and the Alder River came down brown and swollen with everything it had stolen in the night.

Silas Voss stood at the wheel of the *Merry Adeline* and read the water the way other men read letters—slowly, and with a certain suspicion. The river was high and fast, carrying fence posts, roof shingles, half a chicken coop, and one truly enormous hog that paddled past with an air of grim determination, as though it had somewhere to be and no intention of discussing it.

"Hog," Wren reported from the bow, because reporting things was her job and she took it seriously.

"I see it."

"Think it'll make the bend?"

"River hogs usually do." He eased the wheel a spoke to port. "It's the fences you have to watch for. Fences give up."

Wren laughed—that short, barking laugh of hers that always sounded slightly surprised at itself—and went forward to coil lines that did not need coiling. She was nineteen, all elbows and opinions, with hair the color of wet rope and a face that could not hold a lie longer than a heartbeat. He'd hired her two seasons back out of a dockside argument she'd been losing loudly and honorably, and she had never once asked him where he'd come from or why he didn't stay anywhere longer than a season. For that alone he would have carried her on his back up the Escarpment itself, if it came to it.

The *Merry Adeline* was a shallow-draft freight boat built for exactly this kind of nonsense: sixty feet of patched oak and stubbornness, hauling grain and salt pork and the occasional coffin between the landings nobody else bothered with. The storm had shut down traffic for two days, which meant every farmer between here and the forks was now sitting on produce that wouldn't keep, which meant money, which meant Silas intended to be the first hull through.

Provided the river let him.

The sandbars were the trouble. Low-water season had already begun pulling the Alder's teeth, exposing bars that charts marked as channels and channels that charts marked as bars, and now the flood had rearranged even that. Somewhere below Miller's Bend there was a stretch the old pilots called the Mood—a mile of shifting sand where the river couldn't decide what it wanted to be—and Silas had run it eleven times without touching bottom, mostly by refusing to believe anything he remembered about it.

"Sound," he called.

Wren swung the pole and drove it down. "Six feet. Five. Five." A pause, the pole shuddering as the current grabbed it. "Four and mud!"

He turned the wheel and felt the boat lean into the turn, the current shouldering them sideways like a drunk friend who loved you too much. The *Adeline* slid across the bar's shoulder with six inches to spare, and Wren whooped, and Silas allowed himself the small luxury of not dying.

* * *

They found the chest that afternoon, after the water began to fall.

That was the thing about floods: they gave back almost as much as they took, if you knew where to look. Driftwood piled in eddies worth its weight in stove-wood. Livestock standing bewildered on islands. Once, near Tarrow's Landing, a piano, upright and nearly playable, which Wren had wanted desperately and Silas had refused on the grounds that a piano took up cargo space needed for things that paid.

The chest sat wedged in the roots of a fallen cottonwood on the downstream point of a sandbar, half-buried in fresh silt, with the receding water still licking at it like something ashamed.

"What do you figure?" Wren said, hip-deep and grinning, hauling at it while Silas held the boat steady. "Some farmer's strongbox? Flood takes the house, chest floats off?"

"Floods don't usually deliver furniture upstream," Silas said. "Water goes down. Things float down with it. That came from above us."

"So somebody upstream lost it." She got a better grip. "So somebody upstream is having a worse day than us. So it's ours by river law."

"There is no river law."

"There is absolutely river law and I'm the magistrate."

The chest came free with a sucking sound and nearly took Wren with it. It was iron-banded oak, heavy as sin, and when they wrestled it onto the deck and turned it over, Silas stopped breathing for a moment.

Stamped into the lid, beneath a crust of dried mud, was a seal.

A tower with seven stars around it.

He knew that seal. He had known it before he could write his own name—or the name he used now. It had been on flags and coins and the shoulders of uniforms, and then it had been nowhere at all, because Vhalen had been wiped off the maps the way you wipe a spill, thoroughly and with official satisfaction. Twenty years gone. There were men in taverns who would argue it had never existed at all.

"Vhalen," Wren said, tracing the stars with one finger. "The drowned country. My gran used to talk about it. Said their cheese was criminal." She looked up. "What's it doing in our river?"

"No idea," said Silas, who had three ideas, all of them terrible.

* * *

They opened it on deck, because Wren insisted and because Silas discovered he lacked the will to stop her, which was itself a warning sign he chose not to examine.

The lock had rusted through; the crowbar made short work of it. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth that had done its job heroically, lay paper. Reams of it. Ledgers with swollen covers. Bundles of letters tied with wire. And at the bottom, in a leather dispatch case, a stack of military orders, the kind that went from a commander's hand to a subordinate's and changed what happened to villages.

Wren pulled out a ledger and opened it, sneezing at the smell of river and age. "Numbers," she said. "Grain requisitions. Livestock counts. Somebody was very organized about—" She squinted. "About confiscations, looks like. Whole farms, by the look of it. 'From the Holdens, forty head, two wagons.'" She flipped pages. "This is a whole country's worth of stealing, Captain."

"War does that."

"You sound like my uncle, and my uncle was a horse thief."

She set the ledger down and picked up the dispatch case, and Silas watched her hands move toward it and thought, distantly, *don't*, though he said nothing, because saying nothing was the oldest skill he had.

She unfolded the top sheet. Her lips moved as she read, which she'd have denied under torture.

"'By order of Commander E. Ashe,'" she read aloud, "'the settlement of Kolvary is to be cleared of all persons deemed hostile. No quarter is authorized. Command responsibility for compliance rests with—'" She stopped. Looked up. "Silas."

"Mm."

"This says no quarter. That means—"

"It means what it says."

She read on, silently now, faster, her face doing the thing faces did when they met something too big for them. Then she looked at him again, and whatever she saw in his face made her go still.

Because he had gone white. He knew it the way you know a floor has tilted—the blood leaving his head so fast and completely that the afternoon seemed to dim. His hands, braced on the gunwale, had gone the color of the silt on the sandbar.

Kolvary. He had stood outside Kolvary. He had—

No. He had stood outside Kolvary and he had refused, and the refusal had cost him everything except his life, and he had spent twenty years making sure no living soul knew either half of that sentence. And here it all was, dredged up out of the mud like a drowned body, coming ashore on *his* boat, in *his* hands, addressed in a hand he recognized the way you recognize a voice in a dream.

"Captain." Wren's voice had gone careful, the way voices go around spooked animals. "You know this name. Commander Ashe. You've gone gray as the deck."

"I haven't."

"You absolutely have. You look like you've seen a ghost wearing your own coat."

"The sun's in my eyes."

"It's behind you."

Silas straightened. He put on the face he wore for customs men and suspicious widows and anyone who asked questions about Silas Voss, riverboat captain, born—so far as any record said—in a town that had burned down along with its records, convenient that.

"It's salvage," he said. "Paper salvage. Worth maybe nothing, maybe a little to collectors. We'll dry it out and ask at the next landing. If nobody claims it, we sell it and split it, per river law."

"That's not river law."

"It is today." He began rewrapping the papers, briskly, too briskly, and heard how it sounded. "Put the lid on, Wren."

"You're lying."

"I'm being private. It's different."

"It's really not." But she put the lid on, and helped him lash it under the pilothouse awning, out of the weather, and she didn't say anything else about it, which from Wren was louder than shouting.

* * *

That night they moored at Corben's Flats, a landing of four warehouses and a saloon, and Silas could not sleep.

He sat in the pilothouse with a lamp turned low and the chest at his feet, and he told himself every sensible thing there was to tell. Paper rots. Nobody cares anymore. Twenty years is long enough that the men who signed those orders are dead or fat, and the men who received them are dead or scattered, and the country they did it to is a footnote and a folk song. Burn it. Burn the whole chest, feed it to the boiler, and by morning it's steam over the Flats and you're Silas Voss again, honest as a riverman needs to be.

His hands didn't move.

Instead he found himself lifting the dispatch case again, unfolding the orders, reading names. Kolvary. Brast. The valley villages whose names he had spent two decades not saying, and here they all were in ink, in that tidy, patient handwriting he would have known blindfolded, because he had once taken dictation in it, standing at attention, believing every word was necessary.

Commander E. Ashe. Elias Ashe. The man who had taught him to read terrain and to read men, who had once shared his coffee and his blanket on a frozen picket line and told him, *Vosk, you're the only officer I trust to tell me when I'm wrong.* And then, at Kolvary, when Silas had told him exactly that—had stood in front of the column and said *no, sir, not civilians, not like this*—Ashe had looked at him with something worse than anger. Disappointment. As if Silas had failed some final examination.

The order in Silas's hands named him. Not by the name he wore now. By the old one. *Compliance to be verified by Lt. S. Vosk.*

He hadn't complied. That was the whole point. That was the single clean fact he had built a life on, the keel under everything. He had dropped his sword in the mud at Kolvary and walked south through the marshes with nothing, and the man who came out the other side of those marshes had been Silas Voss, a man with no history, a man who paid his debts in cash and left towns before anyone could ask a second question.

And yet here was the paper. Here was proof that he had been there, been *that*, been close enough to the machine to be named in its orders. To anyone who didn't know—to Wren, to any soul on this river—he wasn't the man who refused. He was the man the refusal was assigned to. The paper didn't care about the difference. Paper never did.

Down on the main deck, a board creaked.

Silas closed the dispatch case without letting himself hurry. Wren appeared at the pilothouse stairs with two mugs of chicory substitute, which was the closest thing the *Adeline* carried to coffee, and handed him one without asking.

"Couldn't sleep either," she said.

"I was sleeping fine."

"You were holding that folder like it owed you money." She leaned on the rail beside him, looking out at the black water sliding past the hull. For a while neither of them drank. Then she said, quietly, "My gran came from Vhalen. Before it burned. She used to sing songs in the language and pretend she'd forgotten the words halfway through so we wouldn't ask for more." A pause. "There's a lot of them scattered around, you know. Vhalenis. All up and down these rivers. They'd want to know this chest exists."

"I expect they would."

"And you're going to tell me it's just salvage worth a few coins to collectors."

"I am."

"And you expect me to believe you."

Silas looked at her. Nineteen years old, elbows and opinions, loyal as a dog and twice as hard to fool, and the only person in twenty years who had ever come close enough to see him flinch.

"Wren," he said, and found that the lie, when it came, was the worst kind—the kind that was true and incomplete at once. "Some things are safer sunk. This is one of them. Take my word and don't ask why."

She studied him for a long moment. The lamp threw his shadow huge against the pilothouse glass.

"Okay," she said finally, and he felt relief start to rise—

"But I'm keeping one of the letters," she added, patting her jacket pocket, entirely unrepentant. "Magistrate's fee. River law."

Before he could answer, she was down the stairs and gone, barefoot and quick, and Silas stood alone with the chest and his cooling chicory and the certain knowledge that the river had not finished with him.

Because far downstream, at the mouth of a side channel where the floodwater was still draining off the fields, he had seen it that morning and filed it away and told himself it was nothing: a boat, dark-hulled, riding light, moving upstream against the current without sails or smoke.

Nobody poled upstream after a flood unless they were looking for something the flood had moved.

And it had been gaining on them all day.
