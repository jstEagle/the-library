## 3. Passengers Who Don't Pay

Wren Halloway had a theory about people who came down to the docks at dusk: they were either looking for something or running from something, and either way they were trouble with shoes on.

So when the two of them appeared at the foot of the *Merry Adeline*'s gangplank — a woman in a coat too thin for the season and a boy who walked like he was trying to take up less space in the world — Wren set down the coil of rope she was mending and watched them the way she'd watch weather coming off the water.

They stood at the bottom of the plank for a long moment. The woman said something to the boy, low. The boy shook his head. Then the woman squared her shoulders like a soldier reporting for duty she didn't want, and started up.

"Captain's below," Wren said before she'd reached the top. "And we're not taking passengers."

"I haven't asked yet." The woman had an accent Wren couldn't place — vowels rounded wrong, consonants soft at the edges, like words that had been carried a long way and gotten scuffed. "Ilsa Brandt. This is my brother, Tomas."

The boy lifted two fingers off his side in something that wasn't quite a wave. He had river-gray eyes and the kind of face that made you want to feed him whether he asked or not. Wren resisted the urge.

"Wren," she said. "Deckhand, cook, and official turner-away-of-people-who-look-like-trouble."

"And what do we look like?"

"Hungry," Wren said honestly. "Which is worse."

Something flickered across Ilsa Brandt's face — not embarrassment. Recognition, maybe. The look of someone being seen accurately for the first time in weeks.

"We can pay," she said.

"Everybody says that."

Ilsa reached into her coat and produced a coin purse that clinked with the heavy, satisfying sound of actual money. Not dock coppers. Silver, by the weight of it. She held it out on her open palm like an offering at a shrine.

Wren looked at it. Then past it, at the woman holding it, then at the boy behind her, whose eyes had gone to the purse with an expression Wren knew intimately from her own mirror: the careful blankness of someone pretending not to need a thing.

"It's not about the money," Wren heard herself say, which was a lie, because it was always partly about the money, and both of them knew it. "It's about the captain. He doesn't carry passengers."

"Why not?"

"Ask him."

"I intend to." Ilsa closed her fist around the purse. "Where?"

Wren jerked a thumb toward the deck hatch. "But if you wake him from a nap, you're on your own. He bites."

* * *

Silas Voss did not bite, as it turned out. He did something worse. He listened.

Wren hung back by the galley door, ostensibly scrubbing a pot that was already clean, while the captain sat across the chart table from the Brandts with his big hands flat on the wood and his face doing absolutely nothing. That was the thing about Silas that unsettled people who hadn't worked with him long: he could sit through anything — insults, sob stories, offers of money — without his expression moving so much as a plank in a storm. Wren had once watched him hear out a customs man's twenty-minute speech about tariffs without blinking. The customs man had left convinced Silas agreed with him. Silas had then done exactly what he'd planned to do all along.

He was doing his listening face now.

"We need passage upriver," Ilsa said. "As far as you'll go. Corren's Ford, if you'll take us that far."

"That's eleven days at low water," Silas said. "Sandbars like teeth the whole way."

"We know the river's low."

"Do you." It wasn't a question. "Most folk asking for passage don't know the state of the channel."

"We've been asking at every landing since Marrow Bend." Ilsa's voice stayed level, but Wren caught the fraying underneath it, thread by thread. "Every captain says the same thing. No passengers. No cargo. Nothing going upstream this season."

"They're right. Low water's no season for passengers."

"Then we'll work. Tomas can haul line. I can cook—"

"He cooks," Wren said from the doorway, before she could stop herself.

Ilsa turned. For a heartbeat something raw crossed her face — the look of a person who has been told no so many times that even a joke lands like a blow. Then she mastered it. "I can learn."

Silas still hadn't moved. His eyes went from Ilsa to the boy — Tomas had planted himself near the stern windows, where he could see the dock, and kept glancing at it, quick little flicks, like a man checking for pursuers — and then back.

"You've been following something," Silas said. "Since Marrow Bend. Maybe since before. What is it you're tracking?"

The silence stretched. Wren scrubbed her clean pot harder.

"A boat," Ilsa said finally. "A salvage boat working the shallows below Harrow Shoal. We heard it pulled something up after the storm. Something old."

Wren felt the temperature of the room change. It was subtle — Silas didn't so much as shift in his chair — but she'd spent two years reading this man the way she read water, and under all that stillness, something had gone taut. A rope with weight on the other end.

"What kind of something?" Silas asked.

"A chest," Tomas said.

It was the first time the boy had spoken. His voice cracked in the middle of it, half-boy, half-something-coming, and he flushed and pushed on anyway. "Cedar and iron bands. About so big—" he sketched it with his hands, "—with a seal stamped in the lid. A tower with seven stars over it."

Nobody breathed.

Wren knew the chest. Of course she knew the chest; she'd helped haul it aboard three days ago, dripping and stinking of river mud, and she'd watched Silas Voss — unshakeable, unblinkable Silas Voss — go the color of old dishwater the moment he pried the lid. She'd been dying to ask him about it ever since. He'd claimed it was nothing. He'd claimed it was ballast. He'd claimed, with a straight face, that a sealed cedar chest full of ledgers and letters was *ballast*, and Wren had let it go because pressing Silas was like pressing a cat into a bath: you'd get scratched and the cat would remember.

Now these two strangers were describing it in detail, seal and all, and Silas's listening face had developed its first crack.

"Vhalen," Ilsa said quietly. "The seal of Vhalen. You know it."

"I know a lot of seals," Silas said.

"Not anymore, you don't." There was no bitterness in it — that was the strange part. Just a tired certainty, worn smooth like a stone. "There's no Vhalen to put seals on things. Eleven years gone. Off the maps, off the treaties, off the gravestones. They burned the records in the capital and called it administrative reorganization." Her mouth twisted around the phrase like it tasted of ash. "My father used to say that was the cruelest part. Not the war. The paperwork after."

Tomas had come away from the window. He stood beside his sister now, close enough that their shoulders touched, and Wren saw that his hands were shaking and that he was gripping them still with visible effort.

"The chest has our family's name in it," he said. "Land grants. Records. Proof. We've been looking for it for six years."

"Six years," Silas repeated.

"Since I was ten." The boy's chin came up, defiant, daring anyone to laugh. "Our uncle hid it during the evacuation. Put it on a barge headed downriver and never came back for it. Everyone said it sank. Everyone said forget it. But Ilsa found a bill of lading in a port archive, and the barge didn't sink, it grounded, and the cargo got sold off, and the chest got sold again, and again—" He stopped, swallowed. "We've followed it through nine owners. Nine. And now it's on this boat, and every other boat on this river has already said no, and if it moves downstream tomorrow we might lose it for another six years, or forever, and I—" His voice gave out entirely. He stood there breathing hard, fifteen years old and furious at himself for it.

Ilsa put a hand on his shoulder. It was the only time she'd touched him in Wren's sight, and the gesture had the practiced weight of something done ten thousand times.

"We're not asking you to understand," Ilsa said. "We're asking you to carry us eleven days upriver. We'll pay double your rate. We'll work. We'll stay out of the way. Please."

* * *

Later — much later, after the Brandts had gone back down the gangplank to whatever cheap room they were renting, after the lamps were trimmed and the river muttered against the hull — Wren found Silas at the stern rail with a pipe he wasn't smoking.

She leaned on the rail beside him. For a while neither of them said anything. This was one of Wren's favorite things about the *Adeline*: that silence aboard her was allowed to be comfortable. On land, silence meant somebody had run out of words. Aboard, it just meant the river was talking instead.

"So," she said eventually. "Ballast."

"Don't."

"I'm just saying, that's some very talkative ballast. Ballast with a family crest. Ballast that two people tracked across nine owners and half a country." She turned her head to look at him. In the lamplight from the cabin his face was half gold, half shadow, and unreadable in both halves. "You went white as a drowned man when you opened that thing. I've seen you read your own eulogy without flinching — well, all right, I've seen you do things nearly that bad. But I've never seen you scared. Until that chest came over the rail."

Silas knocked the dead pipe against the rail, once, twice, like punctuation he couldn't find words for.

"They'll follow us," he said at last. "People like that. They've given six years to it. They won't stop because a riverboat captain said no."

"So say yes."

"No."

"Silas."

"Low water, Wren. Sandbars the whole reach. I'm not risking the boat."

"The boat." Wren laughed, short and sharp. "The boat. Right. Because it's the boat you're worried about. Not the seal on the chest. Not whatever's written in those ledgers that made you look like you'd seen your own ghost. Not the fact that those agents at Marrow Bend — the ones asking after salvaged cargo, real polite, matching boots — gave you a look like they recognized your walk."

That landed. She saw it land, in the small tightening at his jaw.

"Those men weren't customs," she went on, softer now. "I've been around docks my whole life, Silas. I know hired muscle when I see it, and I know who they were asking about. Salvage. Upstream cargo. A cedar chest." She paused. "Funny how everybody wants your ballast."

For a long moment Silas said nothing. Out on the black water, a night bird made its lonely complaint, and somewhere up the bank a dog answered it, and the river kept on doing what rivers do, which is leave.

"If I take them," he said finally, "it ends badly. One way or another. People carrying that kind of history — it doesn't ride light. It sinks ships."

"People carrying secrets sink them faster," Wren said. "And you're carrying enough of those to ballast a fleet."

He almost smiled. Almost. It flickered at the corner of his mouth and died there, which was more than she usually got.

"Go to bed, Wren."

"Aye, Captain." She pushed off the rail and took three steps toward the hatch, then stopped. "For what it's worth — the girl offered double rate. Double. And the boy hauled line like he was born to it, you could see it in how he stood. And they've eaten maybe one real meal this week between them, and they still held that coin purse out like it was nothing." She shrugged. "I'm just saying. Some passengers are worth the sandbars."

She went below. At the foot of the ladder she paused, listening, and heard Silas stay at the rail a long, long time, the boards creaking under his weight as he shifted, shifted again, a man arguing with himself in the only language he spoke.

* * *

Wren woke before dawn to the smell of smoke.

Not the good kind — not the galley kind, not bacon-and-coffee kind. Woodsmoke, thick and wrong, with a chemical edge underneath, and beneath that, faintly, shouting.

She was up and out of her bunk before her head finished waking, bare feet hitting cold boards, and she burst onto the deck into air that tasted like a burnt match.

Downriver, maybe half a mile, the warehouse district of the landing glowed orange against the last of the night. Flames stood up off the roofline of the big grain warehouse like banners. Even at this distance she could hear timber cracking, and bells starting up in the town, ragged and late.

Silas was already at the rail. He'd slept in his clothes — he always slept in his clothes — and he stood utterly still, watching the fire, and Wren saw his hands grip the rail hard enough that the knuckles went pale.

"Silas," she said. "The warehouse. That's—"

"I know whose it is." His voice was flat and terrible. "Kessler's. Kessler bought cargo off salvage boats. Kessler bought cargo off *our* boat." He turned, and in the firelight his eyes were the coldest Wren had ever seen them. "Get below. Wake the others. We're casting off. Now."

"Cast off? The fire's downriver, it's not coming here—"

"It's not the fire." He was already moving, hauling in the fenders, throwing off the mooring lines with fast, economical violence. "It's what the fire's for. Burn the places it might be stored, question the people who might have moved it, and follow the ones who run." He glanced back at her once, and the look on his face was one Wren would carry a long time afterward — the look of a man watching a language he'd sworn he'd forgotten come out of his own mouth fluently. "Trust me. I know how this works. I taught half of them."

Out on the dark dock, small in the distance, two figures were running along the planks toward the *Adeline* — a woman in a thin coat, and a boy beside her, and behind them, unhurried, three men walking in step like they had all the time in the world and all of it belonged to them.

Wren grabbed the mooring line and started pulling.
