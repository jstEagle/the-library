## 4. Smoke on the Water

Silas smelled it before he saw it—the way he always did, the way a man learns to when fire has been part of his education. Woodsmoke was common enough on the Alder; every landing town burned its scrap and its slops and its bad decisions. But this smoke had a chemical edge to it, a sweetness underneath like burnt sugar, and that meant pitch or oil or something bought specifically to eat through timber.

He set down the crate of nails he'd been carrying up the gangplank and stood very still in the middle of Marlow's Landing's main dock.

The warehouse belonged to a man named Dossel, who traded in hemp rope and salt pork and had never once asked Silas a question he didn't want answered. It sat forty yards down the waterfront, squat and gray-weathered, with its loading doors open to the afternoon because Dossel believed—loudly and often—that a man who hid his goods had something to hide.

Smoke was curling out of those open doors now. Not pouring. Curling. The way smoke behaves when someone has taken the time to bank a fire properly, to let it build slow and hungry inside a structure before anyone notices.

"Captain?" Wren came up beside him, a coil of line over her shoulder. She followed his gaze. "Huh. That's Dossel's."

"That's Dossel's," Silas agreed.

"You suppose he finally smoked one of his own hams too long?"

"Dossel doesn't make mistakes with fire." Silas watched two figures detach themselves from the warehouse's shadowed side and walk—not run, *walk*—toward the far end of the dock. They moved the way soldiers move when they're off duty but not really: weight forward, eyes doing a constant quiet sweep. One of them carried a canvas satchel. The other had his hands loose at his sides in a way that said there was a knife or a pistol under his coat and he wanted an excuse to use it.

They paused at the end of the pier. One of them looked back—at the *Marrow Bell*, moored amidships, her paint peeling, her name lettered crooked by a sign painter who'd been paid in whiskey.

The look lasted three seconds. Silas counted them.

Then the two men stepped off the dock into the crowd and were gone, the way good ones always were.

"Wren." His voice came out flatter than he intended. "Get aboard. Now."

She looked at him—she had a talent for looking at him like he was a locked drawer she intended to pick—and for once she didn't argue. She dropped the coil of line and went up the gangplank at a jog.

Silas made himself walk, not run, toward Dossel's warehouse. There was no saving it and he knew it; whatever was inside had been soaked in accelerant and lit from three points, and by the time the first flame showed itself the whole frame would go up like a festival lantern. He walked anyway, because a man who ran from fires drew attention, and a man who strolled toward one merely looked like a fool, and fools were invisible.

Inside the open loading door, the darkness was breathing. Heat rolled out against his face, thick and sweet. Somewhere in the depths, a stack of rope bales caught with a sound like a long exhale, and the light changed from ember-orange to true orange, and Silas understood that in about four minutes this building would be a torch visible from the bluffs.

He also understood, looking at the burn pattern already climbing the interior wall—three separate columns of flame, evenly spaced, started low and fed from below—that whoever had done this had learned their trade somewhere specific.

He knew that trade. He had, in another life, under another name, been considered *good* at it.

*Three points of origin,* the old instructor used to say, pacing the barracks at Vhalmoor while young Silas Vosk sat with his squad and memorized everything because forgetting anything could get you shot. *Two is suspicion. Three is certainty. You want the building gone and the question gone with it.*

The question, in this case, being: where is the chest?

Silas backed out of the doorway as the first real flames licked up through the floorboards. Behind him, someone shouted, and then the whole waterfront was shouting, buckets appearing as if conjured, and Dossel himself came barreling down the street in his shirtsleeves howling a word that would have gotten his mouth washed out in any church.

Silas walked back to the *Marrow Bell* through all of it, untouched and unnoticed, which was exactly what the two men on the dock would have expected of a riverboat captain watching a neighbor's misfortune. Grief and curiosity were public property. Terror was private, and terror was what he carried up his own gangplank like contraband.

* * *

"We're leaving tonight."

Wren looked up from the galley stove, where she'd been coaxing life out of damp kindling. "Tide's wrong for night departure. Current'll be against us till past midnight, and the channel above the Narrows isn't lit."

"I know the channel."

"You know the channel *in daylight*. Mostly." She straightened, wiping her hands on her apron—a garment she wore purely out of spite, in Silas's opinion, since she'd never once cooked anything that required protecting clothing from splatter. "What happened? And don't say 'nothing,' because you've got your river face on, and your river face is worse than any other face you own."

"My river face is my best face."

"It's the face you wear when you're lying, is what it is." She pointed the poker at him. "Dossel's warehouse is burning. Two men walked off that dock like they owned it. And now we're leaving at night against the current, which costs money we don't have, for reasons you won't share. So either you tell me something, Captain, or I start telling myself things, and I promise you mine will be more interesting."

Silas crouched by the stove and adjusted a stick of kindling that needed no adjusting. "Fire wasn't an accident. It was a search. Somebody wanted something they thought was in that warehouse, and they burned it to be sure, and to send a message."

"What message?"

"*We can reach you.*"

"And do we have the thing they want?"

The honest answer was yes, and it was sitting thirty feet below them in the bilge space beneath the cargo deck, wrapped in oilcloth, smelling of river mud and old paper. Silas said nothing, which Wren correctly interpreted as everything.

"Huh," she said, and went back to the stove, and said nothing else for a full minute, which from Wren was louder than shouting.

"The passengers," Silas said finally. "The brother and sister. They boarded at Tallow Bend without papers, and I let it slide because the girl paid double and the boy looked half-starved. I want them off at the next landing."

"They haven't left their cabin since yesterday."

"Good. Easier."

"Captain." Wren turned, and her voice dropped into the register she saved for things she actually meant. "I went down to check on them this morning. Brought tea, because I'm a saint. The boy—Tomas—he's got a sketchbook full of drawings of our boat. Our *boat*, Silas. Drawn from memory, every angle, including the bilge hatch. And the sister asked me, very politely, whether we'd salvaged anything after the storm last week. Anything unusual."

The stove crackled. Somewhere above them, the shouts at the burning warehouse continued, fainter now, the drama moving into its second act.

"So they know," Silas said quietly.

"Oh, they've known since Tallow Bend. Question is what they know it *is*, and who else knows they know." Wren folded her arms. "You want to put them ashore, fine. Put them ashore. But if those two are running from the same people who burned Dossel's warehouse, then putting them ashore is just delivering them to the fire a day early. And I'd like to note, for the record, that I've gotten fond of the boy. He fixed my winch brake without being asked and he laughs at my jokes, and only one of those things is a miracle."

Silas stared into the stove. The kindling had caught at last, and the little fire threw wobbling light across the galley's low ceiling, and he thought about how a boat was just a box that floated, and how a family was just a crew that stayed, and how he had spent eleven years making sure nothing ever stayed.

"Midnight," he said. "We cast off at midnight. Nobody goes ashore between now and then. Nobody comes aboard. And Wren—if anyone asks, we're running grain for a miller upriver and we left early because of the weather."

"What weather?"

"Any weather. Pick one."

* * *

The exiles—Ilsa and Tomas, though Silas hadn't asked their names and resented knowing them—came up on deck at dusk as if summoned by the smell of Wren's stew, which was possible; Wren's stew had a range of approximately half a mile.

Ilsa was maybe thirty, with the kind of stillness that Silas associated with people who had learned to wait out sieges. Tomas was sixteen and all elbows, with ink stains on his fingers and the watchful hunger of a boy who'd grown up counting other people's bread. They ate in silence at first, and then Tomas, unable to help himself, said:

"Is it true you're leaving at midnight?"

"It's true we're leaving at midnight."

"Because of the fire."

"Because of the weather," Silas said. "Pick one."

Ilsa set down her spoon. She had her brother's dark eyes but none of his restlessness; looking at her was like looking at deep water. "Captain Voss," she said, and the name landed on him like a hand on the shoulder. "We heard the men on the dock speaking this afternoon. Before the fire. We were on deck."

Silas kept his face pleasant and empty, the river face. "And what did they say?"

"They spoke Vhaleni." Her voice didn't change at all. "Badly. Like men who learned it from prisoners." She let that sit. "They were asking dockhands whether a boat had pulled salvage from the shallows below Tallow Bend after the storm. Whether the captain had brought anything unusual aboard."

The stew steamed. The lamp swung gently with the current pressing against the hull.

"If they were asking about salvage," Silas said, "then they weren't asking about us. We're a freight boat. We haul grain and rope and other people's furniture."

"Yes," Ilsa agreed. "That's exactly what a man says when he has something in his hold worth killing for."

Tomas had stopped eating entirely. He was watching Silas with an expression that Silas recognized from the mirror, years ago, in another country: the look of a young person recalculating whether an adult was safe.

"Here's what I know," Silas said, and heard his own voice go low and even, the voice he used in fog. "A warehouse burned today. Two professionals did it, and they did it the way I was taught to do it, which means whoever sent them pays well and doesn't send them twice. If they come back, they won't ask questions at the dock. They'll come aboard quiet, and they'll take what they want, and they'll leave nobody to describe them." He met Ilsa's eyes. "So here's my offer, and it's the only one. At midnight we go upstream, fast and dark, and I put you off at Corren's Ford, three days from now, and after that you're your own problem. In exchange, you keep your knowledge to yourselves and your hands off my boat. Do we understand each other?"

Ilsa held his gaze for a long moment. Then, softly, in the language of a drowned country—a language Silas had sworn he'd forgotten, and whose words rose in him unbidden, complete, treacherous—she said something brief.

Tomas flinched. Wren, who understood nothing, looked between them like a spectator at a duel fought with invisible swords.

"What did she say?" Wren demanded.

"She said," Silas translated, his mouth dry, "*you still speak it in your sleep.*"

"I don't sleep on runs," he said, and left the galley.

* * *

They cast off at 12:40, because at midnight a drunk had fallen off the pier downstream and half the landing's population had turned out with lanterns to fish him out, and Silas refused to depart with fifty witnesses memorizing his wake. By 12:40 the crowd had gone home, the drunk had been deposited on a bench to reconsider his life, and the *Marrow Bell* slipped her lines into black water with only the moon for company.

Silas took the wheel himself. The channel above the Narrows was a rumor in the dark—sandbars that migrated with every flood, snags that migrated with every storm—but he'd run it twice by day in the last year, and a river, whatever else it was, was honest. It told you where it was deep by the way the current smoothed, where it was shallow by the way it wrinkled and muttered. He read the water the way other men read letters, and the water, tonight, told him: *left of the willow snag, hard left, then trust the middle and pray.*

Behind him, the boat settled into its night rhythm. Wren had the engine. The exiles had retreated to their cabin, though Silas noted that Tomas had posted himself at the stern rail, facing backward, watching the landing lights shrink behind them with an expression Silas couldn't read and didn't try to.

An hour upriver, Wren climbed to the pilothouse with coffee that tasted like boiled rope and stood beside him in silence for a while, which was her version of a conversation opener.

"They're asleep," she said finally. "Or pretending. The girl sleeps like a cat—one eye, I'd bet money. The boy actually sleeps, poor creature. Kids can sleep anywhere. It's the last thing they lose."

"Mm."

"You want to tell me why you knew those arsonists' work on sight? Because I watched your face when you came back from that warehouse, Captain, and I've seen you hit a sandbar, lose a towline, and get swindled by a horse trader, and none of those faces were that face."

Silas turned the wheel a spoke. The *Marrow Bell* leaned obediently around the willow snag, her hull whispering against the current seam.

"Wren," he said, "there are things I know the way you know your mother's voice. Not because I studied them. Because they were poured into me before I had any say about it."

"That's not an answer."

"No," he agreed. "It's not."

She drank her terrible coffee and glared at the river on his behalf. "One of these days," she said, "you're going to trust me with the whole truth, and I'm going to be so shocked I'll drop something valuable overboard, and it'll serve us both right."

"One of these days," Silas said, and neither of them believed it, and both of them pretended to.

It was nearly three in the morning when he saw the lantern.

Not on the water—on the bluff above the east bank, where no honest person had business standing at three in the morning. A single shuttered light, blinking. Long, short-short, long. Then dark. Then again.

Signal code. Old signal code. Army signal code, the pattern drilled into recruits until they could tap it in their sleep—which, apparently, some part of Silas still could, because his mind translated it before he could stop it:

*Position confirmed. Moving upstream. Hold at the ford.*

Corren's Ford. The place he'd promised to put the exiles ashore.

His hands went cold on the wheel, colder than the night justified. Ahead the river bent north into hills he could feel more than see, and somewhere up that dark corridor, men who had learned fire the same classroom he had were already moving, patient as winter, certain as current.

Running, he understood suddenly, had stopped being a choice somewhere back at Dossel's warehouse. It had become simply the direction he was already pointed.

"More steam, Wren," he said quietly. "All she has."

She looked at him once—long, level, filing everything away—and went below, and the *Marrow Bell* groaned and gathered herself and drove north into the dark, carrying a captain who couldn't sleep, a secret that wouldn't drown, and two passengers who had never paid a cent for passage and were, Silas was beginning to suspect, about to cost him everything else.
