## 5. Wren's Bargain

Wren had learned to read Silas Voss the way she'd learned to read water: by watching what the surface tried to hide.

The river taught her that a smooth stretch of current could be lying. A green tongue of water sliding over what looked like nothing might be covering a snag that could tear the bottom out of a boat. The trick was never to look at where the water pointed you—it was to look at where it flinched.

Silas was flinching everywhere.

Three days since they'd burned away from Corben's Landing in the dark, three days of running the Alder upstream through fog and dead water, and the captain hadn't once touched the chest. It sat in the hold under an oilskin tarp like a sleeping dog nobody wanted to wake, and every time Wren came down the companionway and found him near it, he found somewhere else to be. He'd re-caulked a seam that didn't need caulking. He'd sharpened every knife on the boat twice. This morning she'd caught him alphabetizing the spice tins, which was a thing Silas Voss had never done in four years aboard the *Marta June*, because Silas Voss did not believe cinnamon and cloves needed governing.

So when he called down at midday—*"Wren, take us wide of that point, there's a wing dam under the surface"*—in a voice too casual by half, she decided the conversation was happening whether he liked it or not.

She took them wide of the point first. She wasn't a fool, and she wasn't going to prove her point by putting a hole in the hull. But once the *Marta June* settled into the long slow channel behind the island, she set the wheel pin, climbed down from the pilothouse, and planted herself on the deck between Silas and the hatch to the hold.

He stopped mid-stride, a coil of rope in his arms, and looked at her with the expression of a man who has just realized the sandbar is not where his chart said it would be.

"Something on your mind?"

"As a matter of fact." She leaned against the rail, arms crossed, and gave him her best dockside smile—the one that had gotten her free meals in six river towns and thrown out of two. "Funny thing about that chest."

"Is it."

"It's been sitting down there for three days, and you haven't opened it again. Not once. A man finds a strongbox full of somebody's papers, first thing he does is count it twice." She tilted her head. "Unless counting it twice is the last thing he wants to do."

"You've been talking to Ilsa."

"Ilsa talks to me plenty. That's not the same as me listening." Which was a lie—Ilsa had talked to her plenty and Wren had listened to every word, because Ilsa talked about a country that didn't exist anymore the way other people talked about a mother who'd died young, soft and careful and furious underneath—but Wren wasn't ready to show all her cards. "I'm talking to you. Captain to deckhand. Or whatever we are."

"We're crew," Silas said.

"That's the word I'd have used," Wren agreed. "Four years, that's the word I'd have used. Crew means you split the work and you split the pay and you don't lie to each other about cargo." She nodded toward the hatch. "So. What's in the box, Silas?"

For a moment he just stood there with the rope in his arms, and she watched him do the thing he always did, the thing she'd catalogued over four years without ever naming it: he measured the distance to the nearest exit. She'd seen him do it in taverns, at landings, whenever a stranger asked him a question with more than two words in it. Where's the door. How fast can I be gone.

There was no door on a boat in the middle of a river. She'd picked this spot on purpose.

"They're old papers," he said finally. "Dead man's paperwork. Worth nothing but kindling."

"Mm." She pushed off the rail and walked past him, close enough that he had to turn to keep her in view, and rapped her knuckles on the hatch cover. "See, here's my problem. I helped haul this worthless kindling out of the mud. My back did the lifting. And Ilsa and the boy upstairs are so scared of somebody finding it that they sleep in shifts, which tells me it's worth a great deal to *somebody*. So either everybody on this boat knows what it's worth except me—" she turned, smiled again, less warmly this time, "—or you know, and you're not saying."

"Wren."

"And before you say 'it's complicated,' let me save us both the speech." She held up her fingers and counted. "One: I've run contraband tobacco with you, so it takes a lot to complicate me. Two: men burned down a warehouse in Corben's Landing looking for this box, and one of them looked at you across the fire like he knew your face, and you looked back like you knew his, and neither of you blinked, which scared me worse than the fire did. Three—" she dropped the hand, and the humor went out of her voice entirely, because this part wasn't a performance, "—four years, Silas. Four years you've pulled me out of rivers and I've pulled you out of taverns, and I have never once asked you where you came from, because it seemed rude, and because I figured you'd tell me when you were ready. So I'm asking now. Politely. Once."

The river slapped against the hull. Up on deck above them, Tomas's footsteps crossed back and forth, restless as a cat in a sack. Somewhere ahead a kingfisher rattled off its branch.

"I can't tell you," Silas said quietly.

"Can't or won't?"

"Is there a difference you'd accept?"

"No," said Wren. "That's rather the point."

He set the rope coil down on a crate, slowly, the way a man puts down a weapon when he doesn't want the other person to think he's reaching for one. "You want a share," he said. "Of what, exactly? There's no money in that chest. I told you—"

"You told me a lot of things." She kept her voice level. She'd learned that from him, ironically—the flat river voice, the one that doesn't rise no matter how deep the water gets. "You told me you were from downriver. You told me you had no family worth the name. You told me you'd never seen that seal before, the day we pulled the chest up, and Silas, I was standing right next to you, and your hands went white on the winch bar. White as bone. You don't go white over dead men's kindling."

His jaw worked. For a second—one real, unguarded second—she thought he was going to break. She thought: *here it comes, four years of it, all at once.* She braced herself the way you brace for a wave.

Then the shutters came down. She watched them come, plank by plank, the same way she'd watched them come a hundred times in a hundred ports, and something in her chest went cold and quiet, colder than the river in March.

"There's nothing in that chest for you," he said. "And the less you know, the safer you are. Take that for what it's worth."

"Take it for what it's worth," Wren repeated. "Right."

She turned and walked to the rail and looked at the water for a while, because if she looked at him she was going to say something she couldn't take back, and some part of her—the part that still remembered him hauling her half-drowned out of the flood current at Miller's Bend, coughing up half the Alder while he held her hair back and told her she was an idiot and never let go of her arm—that part wanted very badly not to end things.

But another part of her had grown up watching her father count coins behind closed doors and hand out smiles at the table, and she knew exactly what it meant when a man decided protecting you was easier than trusting you.

"Here's my offer," she said at last, turning around. "Final offer, one time only, no refunds. You give me either a share of whatever that chest turns out to be worth—and don't tell me it's worth nothing, because Ilsa's brother sleeps with a knife under his pillow and people who guard nothing don't do that—or you give me the truth. All of it. Who you were, why those men knew your face, why a drowned nation's seal makes you look like you've seen a ghost. Either one. I'm not greedy. Money or honesty. Pick."

Silas looked at her for a long moment. And the worst part, the part that lodged under her ribs like a fishhook, was that he looked *sad*. Not guilty. Sad. Like a man watching a bridge he liked burn down and knowing he was the one holding the torch.

"I pick neither," he said.

* * *

She went up to the pilothouse and took the wheel and steered, because steering was the one job where you were allowed to look busy forever, and because if her hands weren't on the wheel they were going to do something regrettable.

The afternoon went long and gold. The Alder narrowed as they climbed upstream, the banks closing in, cottonwoods giving way to scrub pine, the current quickening under the keel the way it always did this far from the delta. In another week, maybe two, the boat couldn't go any farther. Everyone knew it. Nobody said it.

Tomas appeared at the top of the stairs around sunset, moving the way he always moved now, light on his feet, checking corners. Fourteen years old and already built like a boy who'd learned that walls have ears. He slid into the pilothouse and leaned on the rail beside her, and for a while they just watched the water together.

"He won't tell you anything either, huh," Wren said.

"He told me to stop asking." Tomas shrugged, elaborately casual, fourteen years old doing twenty-five. "Ilsa says patience. Ilsa's been patient for six years. Patience is easy when you're not the one who has to be patient."

Wren snorted despite herself. "Your sister sounds exhausting."

"She is. She's also right, which makes it worse." He was quiet a moment, then, not looking at her: "Do you think he's one of them? The ones who did it?"

The question landed harder than the boy probably intended. Wren kept her eyes on the channel. "I don't know what he is," she said honestly. "That's the whole problem."

"My sister says the chest will buy Vhalen back. Papers, grants, proof. She says when the council sees it, the maps get redrawn." He picked at a flake of paint on the rail. "She says a lot of things. But she's never been wrong about the important ones."

"And you? What do you say?"

Tomas considered this with the gravity of a judge sentencing a man. "I say I'm tired of running," he said. "We ran from Vhalen. We ran across the border. We ran through three towns whose names I'm not supposed to remember. Now we're running up a river on a boat with a captain who goes white when he sees our seal." He looked up at her, and his eyes were older than the rest of him. "Somebody on this boat knows how this ends. I'd like it to be more than one somebody."

He left. Wren steered into the dusk.

* * *

She found Ilsa on the foredeck after dark, wrapped in a blanket, watching the stars come out over the ridge line to the east. The Escarpment. Even from here, even fifty miles off, it stood up against the sky like a wall somebody had built and then abandoned, blacker than the night around it.

"Your brother's been recruiting," Wren said, sitting down beside her.

"Tomas recruits everyone. It's cheaper than an army." Ilsa smiled faintly, then let it fade. She studied Wren with those patient, terrible eyes. "He spoke to you. And now you're speaking to me. So the captain refused."

"How'd you know?"

"Because you're angry, and you only get angry when you've been refused, not when you've been deceived. Deceived, you'd be quiet." Ilsa pulled the blanket tighter. "What did he offer you?"

"Nothing. That was the offer." Wren tipped her head back against the deckhouse. "Six years you've chased this chest. You must know what's in it better than he does. Tell me I'm wrong to want a cut."

"You're not wrong to want a cut." Ilsa said it simply, without judgment, which somehow made it worse. "You hauled it up. You're feeding the people who need it. If Vhalen lives again—even on paper, even as lines on someone else's map—you'll have earned a piece of that. I'd sign it myself, if there were anyone left to sign to." She paused. "But that's not really what you asked him for, was it."

Wren didn't answer.

"You asked him for the truth," Ilsa said softly. "Because you thought you were owed it. And he wouldn't give it. And now you're sitting out here deciding whether four years was a lie." She turned, and in the dark her face was mostly shadow and starlight. "I'll tell you what I know, Wren, since he won't. Whatever Silas Voss was before he was a riverboat captain—he was there. When Vhalen burned. He was on the wrong side of it. Ilsa and Tomas Veyl don't track a chest across six years and three borders without learning the faces attached to it, and the face I saw the day we came aboard belongs to a name in the documents. I haven't said it aloud yet. I'm waiting to see if he does."

The river ran dark beneath them. Somewhere far downstream, behind them, Wren imagined she could almost hear the smoke from Corben's Landing still rising.

"So what do we do?" Wren asked. "Throw him overboard?"

"No." Ilsa's answer came fast and certain, faster than Wren expected. "No. Because whoever he was, he ran *upstream* when the agents came. He put his boat and his crew between us and the fire without being asked. Men who did what was done at Vhalen don't do that." She looked east, at the wall of the Escarpment, black against the stars. "But men who refuse to say what they were—they'll do something else, eventually. Something they think is for our own good. And that's what frightens me."

* * *

Wren took the middle watch, alone in the pilothouse with a lantern turned low and the river talking to itself in the dark.

Around midnight, Silas came up. He didn't ask her to leave, and she didn't offer. They stood at opposite rails like two countries sharing a disputed border, and the *Marta June* ran on through the black water, engine muttering, bow light swinging gently with the current.

"Tomorrow," Silas said at last, not looking at her, "we make the last navigable landing. Old ferry slip below the Narrows. After that it's mules and mountain tracks." A pause. "I've been thinking I should sell the boat there."

Wren's head came around. "Sell the *June*?"

"She's worth enough to outfit us for the climb. Guides, gear, passage for four." He said it evenly, reasonably, the way he said everything, and that evenness made her want to shake him. "It's just a boat, Wren."

"It is not just a boat." Her voice cracked on it and she hated that it cracked. "It's the only thing on this river that ever stayed put. You think I don't know what you're doing? You've been selling things your whole life, Silas. Towns. Names. Whatever you were before. And now you're going to sell the one thing that was ours, and call it practical, and keep not telling me why." She turned back to the wheel, knuckles white. "Get off my watch."

He stood there a moment longer. She felt him wanting to say something—felt it the way you feel weather coming—and then she heard his boots on the stairs, going down, and the pilothouse was hers again, and the river, and the dark.

Below her feet, under the oilskin tarp, the chest sat silent in the hold, full of a dead country's paper. Full, though she didn't know it yet, of a document that would change everything—though not tonight. Tonight it was just weight. Just ballast keeping a boat honest.

Ahead, past the last bend, the Narrows waited, and beyond the Narrows the foothills, and beyond the foothills a town where, unknown to any of them, fresh bills of sale were already nailed to the post office wall, bearing a name that Silas Voss had buried so deep even he sometimes forgot where the grave was.

Wren steered into the dark and did not look back at the cabin light where her captain sat awake, alone, not sleeping, not telling, not staying.
