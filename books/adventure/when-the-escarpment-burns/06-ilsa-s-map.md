## 6. Ilsa's Map

Ilsa had learned to read men the way her mother taught her to read weather: watch the edges first, because the middle always lies.

Captain Silas Voss was all edges. A jaw like a river stone, hands that never quite finished a task before starting another, and eyes that went to the horizon every few minutes as if checking whether it had moved. Three days aboard the *Marrow*, and she had catalogued his habits the way she'd once catalogued the patrol schedules of Vhaleni border guards—carefully, hungrily, because lives depended on noticing.

He avoided her. That was the interesting part.

Wren didn't avoid her; Wren watched her with the frank arithmetic of someone calculating what a person might be worth. Tomas charmed her in the way of teenage boys everywhere, which is to say he asked endless questions about the boat's engine and then pretended not to be impressed by the answers. But Silas gave her the wide berth a man gives a hole in the ice he suspects will hold his weight anyway.

So when he came down into the cargo hold on the third evening, where she'd been sleeping among crates of salt pork and pretending otherwise, she knew something had broken loose in him.

"You've been living down here," he said. Not a question. An accusation wearing a question's coat.

"I've been *staying* down here," Ilsa said. "Living implies comfort."

He looked at the pallet she'd made from flour sacks, at the oilcloth she'd rigged against drips, at the small tin cup she'd turned into a candle holder with a nail and stubbornness. Something in his face almost softened. Almost. He was very good at stopping things halfway—that much she'd already learned.

"Wren says you haven't eaten since noon," he said.

"Wren says many things. Wren also told me your boat is named after a fish that eats its own young, which I have chosen to find charming rather than ominous."

"The marrow eel doesn't eat its young." He paused. "It eats everything else."

"Ah. So it's named for honesty."

For a moment—one flickering moment—he laughed. It was a rusty sound, like a gate opening after years of rain, and it closed again just as fast. But Ilsa had spent six years learning to live on flickers. She stood up, brushed flour dust off her skirt, and said:

"Captain Voss. I need to show you something, and you're going to want to be sitting down, and you're going to refuse to sit down, so let's skip that part."

* * *

She led him to the stern deck, where the evening light came low and gold across the water, and where Wren sat coiling rope with the studied casualness of someone who intended to overhear everything. Tomas appeared within a minute, drawn by whatever invisible wire connected him to his sister's moods. Silas noticed them gathering and did not object, which Ilsa chose to read as permission.

The chest sat between them, padlocked, water-stained, its iron bands furred with rust. The seal of Vhalen—the twin birches bent by wind, roots joined beneath the soil—was still legible on the lid, though the storm had tried hard to erase it.

"Open it," Ilsa said.

"It's your chest."

"It's my nation's chest, and my nation has been dead for six years, which makes ownership a complicated legal matter. Open it."

Silas worked the lock with a marlinspike and a patience that suggested he'd done this kind of thing before, and Ilsa filed that away too. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth that had done its job heroically, lay the documents: ledgers swollen with damp, letters sealed with wax gone soft as candle flesh, and at the bottom—a leather folio, stiff with age but dry, because whoever had packed it had understood exactly what mattered most.

Ilsa lifted the folio out with both hands, the way you lift a sleeping child.

"These are land grants," she said. "Issued under the old crown, countersigned by the provincial governors, sealed with the full authority of the Vhaleni state. They grant title—to farms, mills, whole valleys—to the families who held them. My family's valley is here." She touched a page without quite touching the ink. "The Aldervale. Four hundred hectares of orchard country. My grandmother pressed cider there every autumn and complained about it every winter, and I used to think complaining about good fortune was the most Vhaleni thing a person could do."

Tomas made a small sound. She didn't look at him. If she looked at him she would stop talking, and she could not stop talking, not now, not when the words had waited six years behind her teeth.

"When Vhalen burned, these grants didn't burn. Paper survives fire better than people do—it's one of paper's few virtues. The exiles carried them out. Copies, mostly. Some originals. And for six years they've been passed hand to hand through every exile camp from here to the coast, gathering endorsements, gathering signatures, gathering *proof*. Because here is what nobody wanted to say out loud—" She looked up at Silas, and made herself hold his gaze. "Vhalen isn't dead. Vhalen is *displaced*. There's a difference, and the difference is a document."

Wren had stopped coiling rope. "What kind of difference?"

"The kind that gets recognized." Ilsa opened the folio fully and turned it so they could see: columns of names, seals, dates, and at the head of the final page, an endorsement in fresh ink, barely two months old. "There is a winter council. Beyond the Escarpment, in the high valleys where the exiles who kept faith gathered after the war. Every community sends delegates. Every claim gets weighed. And this year—for reasons I'll come to—the council has agreed to formally petition the border powers for recognition of Vhaleni territorial rights. Using these grants as the legal foundation."

"And Ashe's patrons claim that same territory," Silas said quietly.

"Ashe's patrons *occupy* it," Ilsa corrected. "Occupation is not ownership, no matter how many fences they build. But they know that. Which is why Ashe wants this chest so badly he's burning warehouses over it. If the grants reach the council, the petition goes forward with unbroken documentation—six years of endorsements, ending in these original instruments. If the grants don't reach the council..." She closed the folio gently. "Then the petition dies, the occupation becomes permanent in law as well as fact, and everyone who died carrying these papers died for nothing."

The river slapped against the hull. Somewhere upstream a night bird started its idiotic lovely racket.

"How long," Silas said.

Ilsa took a breath. This was the part she'd rehearsed, and rehearsal hadn't helped; the numbers were just numbers until you said them aloud, and then they became a wall rushing toward you.

"The council convenes at the turn of the season. When the dry winds stop and the snows begin. The passes beyond the Escarpment close within days of the first real snowfall—not weeks, days, the high trails go from passable to suicide between one morning and the next. And the dry winds stop in—" She counted backward from the almanac pages Tomas had memorized, because Tomas memorized everything and forgot nothing except to wash behind his ears. "Five weeks. Perhaps six, if we're lucky, and I stopped believing in luck around the time our town stopped existing."

Silence. Wren broke it, because Wren always broke silence eventually, the way water breaks through a bad dam.

"So let me make sure I have the shape of this," she said. "We're sitting on a box of paper worth—what, legally? Whole valleys? Towns? And it has to travel over mountains that kill people in good weather, pursued by a commander with government money and a grudge, in five weeks."

"That's the shape of it," Ilsa said.

"And you want us to carry it."

"I want the captain to carry it. You I'm less sure about, since you've been pricing my brother like livestock since the day we boarded."

"He's a growing boy," Wren said. "I was estimating his caloric needs. For provisioning purposes."

"For *provisioning*—"

"*Enough.*" Silas said it quietly, and both women shut their mouths, which Ilsa suspected was the only command he ever gave that worked. He was staring at the folio—at the Aldervale entry, specifically—with an expression she couldn't parse. Not greed. Not pity. Something older and more tired than either.

"You knew the Aldervale," she said. It wasn't a guess. She'd watched his face too long not to know.

"I passed through it once." His voice was flat as slate. "Before the war."

"What was it like?"

"Orchards," he said. "And a woman who sold cider and complained about it."

Ilsa felt the ground tilt slightly, the way it does when a floorboard turns out to be a trapdoor. "That would be my grandmother."

Something crossed Silas Voss's face then—fast, buried, but not fast enough, not for someone who'd spent six years reading edges. He looked at the river instead of at her, and said, "Small world," in a voice that meant the opposite.

* * *

They argued until dark, which Ilsa considered a form of progress, because arguing meant he hadn't said no.

Wren argued for money, fluently and shamelessly, laying out figures for freight rates, hazard pay, and the resale value of a boat abandoned mid-route, until Silas pointed out that abandoning the boat meant having no income at all, and she recalculated with visible annoyance. Tomas argued for the sheer romance of it, which Ilsa appreciated even while recognizing it as strategically useless. And Silas argued against—argued with the weary fluency of a man reciting a list he'd written years ago and kept in his pocket.

"I run a shallow-draft freight boat on sixty miles of river," he said. "I don't do mountains. I don't do politics. I don't do causes."

"You did once," Ilsa said.

"You don't know what I did once."

"No," she agreed. "But I know what you did three nights ago, when the warehouse burned. You could have slipped your moorings and been gone before the smoke cleared. Instead you went *toward* it. You pulled two strangers off a burning dock because they were strangers and they were burning. Men who don't do causes don't do that."

"I did it because Wren was on the dock."

"I was getting clear fine on my own," Wren said.

"You were running *toward* the fire to see what was in it."

"...Also fine on my own."

Silas pinched the bridge of his nose, and Ilsa saw it—the crack, the hairline fracture in the wall he'd built, widened not by her arguments but by his own crew refusing to let him stand alone behind it. She pressed, gently, the way you press on ice to test it.

"Captain. I won't pretend to know what you're carrying. Everyone out of Vhalen carries something; it's practically a national export. But I know what *I'm* carrying, and I'm telling you it weighs less when four people hold it. Five weeks. One crossing. After that, the chest leaves your hands forever and you can go back to sandbars and debts and towns you abandon before anyone learns your middle name."

"I don't have a middle name."

"Everyone has a middle name. Yours is probably something terrible, like Bartholomew."

"It's not Bartholomew."

"So there *is* one." Tomas grinned. "Progress."

Silas looked at each of them in turn—Wren with her arms crossed and her eyes doing arithmetic, Tomas with his hopeless open face, Ilsa with the folio held against her chest like a shield that cut both ways. Then he looked at the river, running dark and patient toward places he'd never have to go if he simply stayed on it.

"If I say yes," he said slowly, "it's not for Vhalen. It's not for the council, or the grants, or the cause. Understand that."

"Then what is it for?" Ilsa asked.

He didn't answer right away. When he did, his voice was so quiet she nearly missed it under the water.

"Because somebody packed that folio at the bottom of the chest and waterproofed it first. Somebody decided those papers mattered more than anything else on that boat—including the boat. People like that deserve to finish what they started." He straightened. "We moor at Harlow's Bend tomorrow. I'll sell the *Marrow* there—she's worth more in pieces than running, this season—and we buy mules, ropes, cold-weather gear. Five weeks is tight. We leave the day after tomorrow."

Wren whistled. "You're selling the boat. Your boat. The one you sleep facing, like other men sleep facing their wives."

"The *Marrow* eats everything else," Silas said. "Including, apparently, my plans."

Ilsa laughed before she could stop herself—a real laugh, the first in longer than she cared to count—and for a moment the stern deck felt less like a negotiation and more like a kitchen, which was the highest compliment her heart knew how to pay.

* * *

Later, past midnight, Ilsa sat alone with the folio open on her knees, memorizing the route she'd traced a hundred times in her head: upriver to Harlow's Bend, then east on the drover's road, then the long climb into the Escarpment's lower canyons, the switchbacks, the saddle, the pass. Five weeks. Weather holding. Luck optional.

Tomas found her there, the way he always did, appearing with the soundless certainty of a cat who owns the house.

"You saw the posters," she said quietly.

His silence lasted three seconds too long. "What posters?"

"Tomas."

"There were posters in Corren's Ford," he admitted. "On the notice board by the ferry landing. I saw them when we came through. I didn't—I wasn't sure what they meant. They described a man. A soldier. An officer." He swallowed. "The name was different. But the face..."

Ilsa closed the folio. Outside, the river ran on, indifferent and enormous, carrying them north toward mountains and whatever waited in them.

"Show me the poster," she said, "when we reach Corren's Ford. Don't tell the captain. Not yet." She touched her brother's cheek, feeling how fast his heart beat under the boyish bravado. "First we get him to commit to the mountain. Then we learn who we've committed to."

Tomas nodded, unhappy and obedient, and slipped away into the dark.

Ilsa stayed a while longer, watching moonlight move on the water, and thought about orchards, and cider, and the faces of men who look at horizons as if checking whether the past has caught up yet.

Hers, she suspected, was closer than any of them knew.
