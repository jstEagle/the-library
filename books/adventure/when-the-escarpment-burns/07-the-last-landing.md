## 7. The Last Landing

Silas had moored the *Marten* in four hundred different places over eleven years, and he could count on one hand the times he'd tied her off with any intention of staying past morning.

This was not one of those times. Not yet. He told himself that as he looped the bowline around the pilings at Tallow Bend—a landing so small it didn't deserve the word *bend*, just a shelf of mud and gravel where the Alder slowed to think and a general store leaned against a cottonwood like an old man against a fence. He told himself they were stopping for supplies only. Rope, flour, lamp oil, salt pork. Two days, maybe three, while the river dropped another foot and made the upper channels honest again.

Wren didn't believe him. He could tell by the way she coiled the stern line without being asked, slow and deliberate, watching him over her shoulder like a woman counting a man's lies the way she counted coins.

"You're doing the thing," she said.

"What thing."

"The thing where you look at the boat like you're apologizing to her."

"I'm looking at the water level."

"Sure." She flipped the coil over the bollard with a snap. "And I'm looking at your hairline."

Silas let that go. It was easier than admitting she was right, which she was, which was becoming an uncomfortable pattern in his life ever since a drowned chest had come up out of the Alder with a dead nation's seal on its lid.

Behind him, on the deck, Tomas sat cross-legged with Ilsa's map spread across his knees, holding down its corners with river stones because the wind kept trying to take it back. The boy had barely spoken in two days—not since the warehouse fire, not since Silas had cut the *Marten* loose at midnight and run her upstream through channels he'd only dared in daylight before—but Silas had caught him studying the map the way other boys studied wanted posters, memorizing distances, whispering place names under his breath like prayers.

*Corren's Ford.* That was the next name on the map, the last town before the foothills took over and the river stopped mattering. And beyond Corren's Ford, the Escarpment, drawn on Ilsa's map in faded ink with a line of Vhaleni script beneath it that Silas refused to translate even though he could.

He went below to check the stores and found Ilsa already there, sleeves rolled, taking inventory in a small neat hand in the back of one of Ashe's ledgers—which struck him as a fitting use for the man's paper, though he'd have died before saying so aloud.

"We need rope," she said, not looking up. "Real rope, not the splice Wren keeps swearing will hold. We need boots for Tomas, his are held together with hope. We need—"

"I know what we need."

"And we need a guide," she said, and now she did look up, and her eyes were the flat gray of the river in November. "Overland. Past Corren's Ford. Someone who knows the old drover trails, because the maps don't, and winter doesn't care whether we do."

Silas crouched by the stores barrel and pretended to count tins. "I'm a riverman."

"You're a liar who lives on a river," Ilsa said. "There's a difference, and the difference is that liars eventually run out of river."

That was the trouble with Ilsa Brandt: she'd spent six years tracking a chest across three borders, which meant she'd spent six years learning exactly how men like Silas hid things, and she had no patience left over for the hiding. He respected it the way he respected a snag in a channel—deeply, and from a distance.

"I'll ask at the store," he said. "Someone there will know the trails."

"Someone there will know the trails for coin we don't have, or someone there will sell us to Ashe's people for coin we also don't have." She closed the ledger. "Captain. I've watched you read this river for two hundred miles. You read it like scripture. Don't insult both of us by pretending you can't read dirt too."

He didn't answer. There wasn't an answer that wasn't yes, and he wasn't ready to say yes, because yes meant selling the *Marten*, and selling the *Marten* meant—

Well. It meant a great many things he wasn't ready to say either.

* * *

The store at Tallow Bend was called Ostler's, though no one named Ostler had owned it in a generation, and it smelled of kerosene, cured ham, and the particular dust that accumulates around a cash box. The proprietor was a wide woman named Dree with forearms like dock rope and the unblinking appraisal of someone who'd priced every kind of customer from pilgrim to fugitive.

Silas bought rope first, because rope was simple. Then flour, oil, salt pork, a sack of dried apples that Wren would pretend not to love. Boots for Tomas—he held them up against his own hand to guess the size, feeling like a fool and something worse than a fool, something dangerously close to a father—and when Dree rang them up she looked at him over the counter with her chin tipped.

"Heard about the fire at Marlow's warehouse," she said. "Downriver. Night before last."

"Did you."

"Whole dock quarter's talking. Say the men who set it wore gray coats and asked questions after, quiet-like. Questions about a boat heading upstream." She wrote the total on a slate without hurry. "Say the questions had a shape to them. Like questions that get asked until somebody answers."

Silas laid coins on the counter, one at a time, making each one mean something. "I wouldn't know anything about that."

"No," Dree agreed. "You wouldn't." She swept the coins into her apron and then, instead of turning away, rested both forearms on the counter and lowered her voice to the register women like her used for information rather than goods. "Man comes through here twice a year buying surveyor's chalk and complaining about the price of everything. Name of Hobb. Knows the drover trails over the Escarpment better than the goats do. Also knows which side of the mountain pays best for knowing things, if you follow me."

"I follow you."

"He's drinking at the Ferry House. Has been since Tuesday. Which means by Friday he'll be broke enough to guide anybody anywhere, and desperate enough to do it badly." She straightened. "Or—" and here she paused, and Silas understood she was deciding how much trouble to make herself, "—or you've got a boat sitting at my landing that I've admired for eleven years, and a face that says you're about to stop running, and maybe you don't need Hobb at all."

The bell over the door jingled. Wren came in with Tomas behind her, the boy wearing new boots that he hadn't broken in yet and was therefore walking in like a stork on a frozen pond, and whatever else Dree might have said got folded away into the professional smile of a woman who knew when a conversation had grown ears.

But the words stayed in Silas's chest all the way back down to the landing, heavy as ballast.

*A face that says you're about to stop running.*

He stood on the bank and looked at the *Marten*—really looked, the way you look at a person—at the patched rail where he'd replaced planking after the flood year, at the wheelhouse glass gone cloudy at the corners, at the name repainted four times because he kept choosing paint that couldn't hold up to sun. Eleven years. She'd been older than that when he bought her with money he still didn't entirely trust himself to have earned honestly, and she'd carried him out of more trouble than he'd ever admit, and she had never once asked him his real name.

Boats were good that way. Boats didn't care who you'd been.

People, it turned out, were starting to.

* * *

He didn't decide at the landing. Deciding was a thing he'd learned to avoid the way other men avoided debt, and for the same reason: it always came due bigger than you'd planned.

Instead he cooked. There was a galley stove aboard the *Marten* that he'd rebuilt himself, and he made the flatbread with crackling fat and the bean stew with too much pepper that Wren liked, and he fed the four of them on the aft deck while the light went long and gold across the water, and nobody talked about mountains or chests or men in gray coats, and for one hour it was almost the life he'd built, the small good floating life, intact.

It was Tomas who broke it, because it was always going to be Tomas.

"How much is she worth?" the boy asked suddenly, staring at the deck boards under his new boots. "The boat. If you sold her."

"Tomas," Ilsa said, sharp.

"It's a fair question."

"It's a rude question."

"It's the question everybody's thinking and nobody's asking, which makes it the most honest thing anyone's said in two days." The boy lifted his chin, and Silas saw Ilsa in the set of it, the family stubbornness coming through like a vein of ore. "We've got nothing. We've been spending his supplies and sleeping under his roof and eating his food, and we've got nothing to pay with except a map and a story. So. How much?"

Silas turned his tin cup slowly on his knee. Downstream, a heron stood in the shallows with the patience of a creature that had never once been in a hurry and had never once been wrong about it.

"Eighty crowns," he said. "Maybe ninety, if I find a buyer who doesn't haggle, which I won't, because every buyer haggles. Sixty if I sell fast to a man like Hobb, who knows I'm selling fast." He took a drink. "She's worth more than that to me. But that's not what you're asking."

"No," Tomas admitted.

"And the answer to what you're actually asking is that I haven't decided."

"You have," Wren said quietly. She was leaning against the rail with her arms crossed, and she didn't look at him when she said it, which somehow made it worse. "You decided at the store. I watched your face. You've been walking around with it all evening like a stone in your boot."

Silas set down the cup. He looked at the river, because it was easier than looking at them—the three of them sitting on his deck in the last of the light, eating his food, waiting on him with the terrible patience of people who had lost a country and therefore could not be rushed by anything smaller.

Here was the arithmetic he'd been refusing to do. Running cost everything, always, forever, and bought nothing but distance. Stopping cost once—one boat, one name, one clean break with the current—and bought, possibly, an ending. The chest had to go over the Escarpment before the winds turned. The exiles couldn't do it alone; they knew it, and worse, they were polite about knowing it. And somewhere behind them, patient as winter, came the men in gray coats with their questions shaped like knives.

Stopping cost less than running. It was the first honest sum he'd done in years, and he hated the answer.

"There's a man at the Ferry House," Silas said. "Hobb. Knows the drover trails. Dree says he drinks until he's cheap." He paused. "But Dree also says a guide who's drunk and desperate guides badly, and bad guiding above the treeline gets people killed. So here's what I know about the old drover roads, which is more than Hobb knows and less than I wish I did." He heard himself say the next part from a strange distance, as though some other man were borrowing his voice. "I can get you to Corren's Ford. From Corren's Ford, I can get you up the switchbacks as far as the tree line. After that—" he shrugged, and the shrug cost him more than the sentence had, "—after that we learn together, or we turn around. Those are the terms."

Ilsa's breath went out of her, slow and controlled, the sound of a woman refusing to hope out loud until the paperwork was signed.

"The boat?" she asked gently.

"Selling the boat." He said it to the river. "Tonight, probably. Haggling included."

Nobody cheered, which he was grateful for. Wren pushed off the rail and put her hand briefly on his shoulder—two seconds, no more, but her hands were calloused and warm and it undid something in him that he'd have denied under torture—and then she started gathering the bowls, because in Wren's language, tenderness was expressed almost exclusively through dishes.

Tomas opened his mouth—to thank him, Silas supposed, or to apologize for the question—and Silas pointed the ladle at him.

"Break in those boots tomorrow," he said. "Walk the landing until your heels blister and then keep walking. We leave the day after, and I'm not carrying you."

The boy grinned, and it transformed his whole face into something young and unguarded, and Silas had to turn away and look at the water again because apparently he was going soft in his old age and there was nothing whatsoever to be done about it.

* * *

He sold the *Marten* to a barge captain named Pell at half past ten by lantern light, and it went the way he'd known it would: Pell circled her twice, found the patched rail, found the cloudy glass, found the four layers of name-paint, and offered fifty. Silas countered at eighty-five. They settled at seventy-two and a bottle of rye that Silas accepted mostly so he'd have something to smash later in private.

Pell was a decent man, as buyers went. He ran a hand along the rail and said, "She's a good boat. Who'd you steal her from?"

"Myself," Silas said, "eleven years ago," and Pell laughed, and the deal was done, and that was that.

He walked back down to the landing alone and sat on the mooring post with the bottle unopened beside him and watched the *Marten* ride at her lines in the dark, riding lighter now, already belonging to someone else in some legal sense that hadn't reached her timbers yet. A night bird called somewhere up the bank. The current spoke its one long sentence against the pilings, the same sentence it had been speaking since before he was born and would speak after everyone he'd ever met was gone.

Eleven years ago he'd come down out of the hills with ash still in his boot seams and a name he couldn't use, and he'd seen this boat at a war-surplus dock, ugly and sound, and he'd thought: *a thing that floats can't be buried.* He'd been right. She'd floated him clear of everything—every border, every inquiry, every memory with teeth. She'd been the longest conversation he'd ever managed to sustain with another living thing, and he'd never once had to tell her the truth.

Tomorrow she'd carry barge freight. Barley, maybe, or timber. Some other man's hands on her wheel, calling her by some other name.

"Sentimental," he said aloud, disgusted with himself, and reached for the bottle, and then didn't drink, because the last time he'd drunk alone on a mooring post he'd been twenty-six years old and it had gone very badly indeed.

Footsteps on the gravel. He didn't reach for the knife, which told him something about how far gone he was; a year ago he'd have had it out by the second step.

Wren sat down on the next post over, close enough to share warmth, far enough to pretend otherwise. For a while neither of them said anything, which was the particular gift of Wren—that she could be silent *with* a person instead of merely near them.

"Pell give you a fair price?" she asked finally.

"Pell gave me a Pell price."

"Mm." She picked up the bottle, considered it, and set it back down untouched, which was its own kind of solidarity. "Ilsa's drawing the route. She's got Tomas copying the map so there's two. She's already arguing with him about his lettering."

"He writes like a spider fell in ink."

"She says the same about yours."

"She would."

Another silence, comfortable, the river filling it. Then Wren said, not looking at him, in the tone she saved for things that mattered: "You could still run. Sell short, take the sixty, be three locks upstream by Thursday under a new paint job. I'd help you do it. I'm good at helping people disappear; it's most of my skill set."

"I know you are."

"So why aren't we?"

Silas looked at the dark water, at the boat that was no longer his, at the lamplit window of the store where Ilsa's shadow bent over her maps. He thought about giving the true answer—the whole answer, the one with the massacre in it, the commander, the name he'd buried—and found that his throat wouldn't open that far yet. Maybe on the trail. Maybe at high altitude, where the air was thin enough that secrets leaked out on their own.

"Because running costs everything," he said instead, "and stops costing nothing. And this—" he gestured at the landing, the store, the window with the shadows in it, "—this only costs once."

Wren nodded slowly, as if he'd said something wise, though privately he suspected he'd only said something tired. Then she stood, brushed off her trousers, and paused.

"For what it's worth," she said, "she was a good boat."

"She was the best thing I ever stole."

And Wren laughed—short and surprised and real—and walked up the bank toward the lamplight, and Silas sat alone on the mooring post with a bottle he wasn't going to open and a grief he was going to carry, and above the black shoulder of the Escarpment the stars were coming out hard and cold, and the wind, when it came down the valley, had a thin edge to it that hadn't been there last week.

Dry season's last winds, Ilsa had called them. Weeks left. Maybe fewer.

Up in the Ferry House, a drunk guide named Hobb was telling stories to strangers about the drover roads, and one of the strangers, Silas would learn soon enough, was listening a little too carefully and asking a little too quietly, and wore a gray coat folded away in his pack.

But that was tomorrow's water. Tonight there was only the river, letting go of him by inches, and a man on a mooring post learning—for the second time in his life, and this time on purpose—how to stop.
