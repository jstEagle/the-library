## 8. Foothill Town, Unfriendly

Tomas smelled Corren's Ford before he saw it—woodsmoke and tanneries and the sour-sweet reek of a town that had too many horses and not enough patience. He stood at the rail of the mule train's last cart, wedged between a crate of dried apples and Wren's bedroll, and tried to look like a boy who had never been afraid of anything in his life.

He was fifteen. He was very good at trying.

"Stop fidgeting," Ilsa said without looking up from her map. She'd been studying it since dawn, tracing the road from the river landing where they'd sold the boat—the boat, which had been Silas's whole life, gone in an afternoon for coin and supplies—to this town at the foot of the Escarpment. "You fidget when you're nervous."

"I don't fidget."

"You're doing it right now."

"That's strategic movement."

Isla looked at him then, one eyebrow raised in the exact way their mother used to do it, and Tomas felt the familiar ache under his ribs that had nothing to do with fear. Their mother would have liked this. The cart, the mountains, the ridiculous man driving them.

Silas Voss drove the lead mule with the same unhurried competence he'd brought to everything since the river. He hadn't talked much on the trail. Three days of switchbacks out of the lowlands, and mostly what he said was "watch that footing" and "water here" and once, memorably, to Wren: "If you eat my last strip of smoked fish again I will leave you for the bears." Wren had eaten it anyway. She said she wanted to see if he'd follow through.

That was the thing about Silas that Tomas couldn't stop turning over, like a stone with something underneath it: everyone joked around him, and he let them, but he never quite joined in. He was a wall you could lean against. Walls didn't laugh.

* * *

Corren's Ford was built the way frontier towns are built—quickly, defensively, and with the assumption that everyone passing through was either buying something or lying about something. The main street ran along a brown creek spanned by a bridge old enough to have opinions. Buildings leaned against each other like tired drunks. A smithy hammered away somewhere, keeping the town's heartbeat.

"We need flour," Ilsa said, counting coins into her palm. "Rope. Another blanket for Wren, because hers smells like a wet dog."

"It smells like character," Wren said.

"And lamp oil. And we should ask about the pass road—whether the upper switchbacks are open yet or if there's been rockfall."

"I'll ask about the road," Silas said. "You two take the stores. Wren—" He paused. "Don't sell anyone anything."

"You wound me."

"You once sold a man his own hat back."

"He'd forgotten it was his. That's called a service industry."

Silas almost smiled. It was a small thing, that almost-smile, and Tomas caught it and held onto it, because he had a bad feeling sitting in his stomach like a swallowed hook, and he needed something true to hold onto.

The bad feeling had started an hour ago, actually, at the last bend in the road, when the town came into view and Tomas saw the notice board by the bridge. Even at distance, he'd seen the shape of paper on it. White rectangles. Official corners.

Towns like this had notice boards. Notice boards had notices. Most of them were about lost mules and tax levies and which days the tanner's wife sold pies.

But some of them weren't.

* * *

They split up the way sensible people do, which is to say they split up the way people do right before things go wrong in stories, and Tomas told himself he was being a coward and a fool and maybe both, and then he did it anyway.

While Ilsa argued with a grocer about the price of flour—a price that climbed visibly whenever she opened her mouth, because the grocer could hear the old-country vowels in it, and towns like Corren's Ford charged extra for vowels—Tomas drifted toward the bridge.

The notice board stood where the road met the planks, nailed and tarred and layered like sedimentary rock. Mule notices. Tax notices. A reward poster for a horse thief with a magnificent mustache that clearly belonged to no horse thief anywhere.

And three sheets of good clean paper, freshly pinned, ink still sharp.

WANTED

SILAS VOSK

Formerly an officer of the Vhalen Provisional Command. Wanted for questioning regarding stolen property of significant value. Last seen on the Alder River. Answers to the name VOSS. Grey at the temples. Scar through left eyebrow. Carries himself like a soldier even when pretending otherwise.

REWARD: 400 crowns. Additional sum for information as to the whereabouts of a strongbox bearing the seal of Vhalen. Persons assisting may be compensated regardless of outcome. Apply to the office of Commander D. Ashe, or to any agent of the Meridian Charter Company.

Below the words was no likeness—just a written description, which was somehow worse, because a picture you could argue with, but a description was a mirror held up and tilted until you saw what they saw.

*Carries himself like a soldier even when pretending otherwise.*

Tomas read it four times. His hands were cold. Not mountain-cold. Other cold.

He had known—Ilsa had known, they'd both known—that Silas was from over there, from the war, from the part of the world their parents had died leaving. You didn't get riverboat captains with eyes like that from anywhere else. But knowing a man had come from a fire was different from learning he might have carried the torch.

*Formerly an officer.*

An officer. Not a conscript dragged from a field, not a boy handed a gun and pointed wrong. An officer of the Provisional Command, which every exile child learned about the way children learn about wolves: by name, by habit, by the sound of the word alone.

Tomas thought of Silas teaching him to read river water on the third night out, crouched at the rail, tapping the dark surface. "See how it wrinkles there? That's a sandbar thinking about becoming an island. Everything that looks calm is deciding what it wants to be." He'd said it like a joke. Had it been a joke?

He thought of Silas standing between them and the men at the warehouse fire, arms spread, voice flat as a blade laid on a table: *You'll go through me.* And the agents had hesitated. Because they knew him. Because he was one of theirs—or had been—and men hesitate before cutting their own.

Was that protection? Or was it authority?

Tomas didn't know. That was the whole problem. He stood at the notice board with the wind pulling at the papers and he did not know, and the not-knowing sat in him like a stone he couldn't cough up.

"Tomas!" Ilsa's voice, sharp with grocery-triumph. "Come carry things. You exist to carry things."

He pulled his hood up, turned from the board, and went to carry things.

He said nothing.

* * *

He said nothing through the afternoon, which was its own kind of work, harder than carrying flour.

They took rooms above a tavern called the Split Antler, two beds and a cot, because Silas insisted on separate rooms for himself—"I snore like a landslide"—and paid the innkeeper, a woman named Berta with forearms like ship rope, in exact coin and no conversation. Tomas noticed that. He noticed everything now. Silas paid exact. Silas kept his face angled away from the lamplight. Silas asked Berta about the pass road in a flat accent that had no vowels in it at all, a voice sanded down to nothing, and Berta answered without interest because she'd decided he was boring, and Tomas understood suddenly that boring was a costume Silas wore the way other men wore coats.

"The upper switchbacks are open," Silas reported, spreading a hand-drawn map on the table that evening. "Berta says the last rockfall got cleared a week ago, but there's weather coming down off the Escarpment within the fortnight. First snows. We move tomorrow at first light, hard, and we don't stop except to breathe."

"And after the pass?" Wren asked, feet up on the cot, peeling an apple in one unbroken ribbon. "What's past it? Besides the council and all your glorious legal vindication?"

"Valley settlements. Old Vhaleni grant land." Ilsa smoothed the map's edge with both hands, and her voice did the thing it always did when she spoke about home—the thing where it got careful, like walking on river ice. "Orchards. Terraced fields. My grandmother wrote about them. Before."

Before. One word containing a whole country.

Silas watched Ilsa say it. Tomas watched Silas watching, and tried to read the man's face the way Silas read water, and got nothing. Calm surface. Something underneath, deciding what it wanted to be.

"Eat," Silas said. "Sleep. Tomorrow's long."

Tomas ate. Tomas lay on the cot and listened to Wren's apple-ribbon snap and Ilsa's breathing slow into sleep, and across the hall, through the wall, he heard Silas's floorboards creak twice and go still. A man checking the window. A man sleeping light, the way soldiers sleep.

*Carries himself like a soldier even when pretending otherwise.*

He said nothing.

* * *

He lasted until midnight, which for a fifteen-year-old carrying a secret shaped like a boulder is roughly heroic.

The problem was that secrets don't stay still. This one shifted every time Tomas closed his eyes. If he slept, he dreamed of the notice board, and in the dream the description grew new lines: *Known to smile rarely. Known to teach boys to read rivers. Known to stand between danger and small children, purpose unknown.* And in the morning Ilsa would walk into some checkpoint or ferry crossing or mountain tollhouse and see the same white rectangle, and she would turn around slowly and look at Silas, and Tomas would have to watch her face do whatever it did, and he would have done nothing to prepare her. That was the part he couldn't swallow. Not his own fear—fear was just weather—but letting Ilsa be ambushed by it. She'd been ambushed enough. Everyone they loved had been ambushed enough.

So he got up. He crossed the hall in his socks. The door to Silas's room wasn't locked, which told Tomas something too—either the man trusted this town, or he trusted nothing enough to know locks wouldn't matter.

Silas was awake. Of course he was awake. He sat in the chair by the window with the shutter cracked an inch, moonlight laying a grey stripe across his knees, and he looked at Tomas the way he looked at sudden obstacles in the current: assessing, unhurried.

"Bad dreams?" he asked quietly.

"No." Tomas's mouth was dry. "Yes. I mean—I need to ask you something."

Silas waited. He was good at waiting. It was one of the frightening things about him, Tomas realized; patient men with soldier posture were frightening in a way loud men never managed.

"The notice board," Tomas said. "By the bridge."

Something moved behind Silas's eyes—not surprise. Something older and wearier than surprise. Recognition, maybe, arriving like a letter he'd been expecting for years.

"How much did you read?"

"All of it."

Silas nodded slowly. He didn't deny it. Didn't reach for a lie, and Tomas understood with a lurch that this was itself an answer—the man had lied badly about the chest back on the boat, had gone white and stammered, but here, cornered by a boy in sock feet at midnight, he didn't insult either of them with a story.

"Say it," Silas said. "Whatever you came to say. Say it plain."

"Are you him?" Tomas's voice cracked on *him*, and he hated that. "Are you Vosk? Are you—were you really an officer? Did you—" He couldn't finish it. The massacre didn't have a name in their house. It had a silence, and the silence had a shape, and he'd grown up inside the shape.

For a long moment Silas looked out the crack in the shutter, at the moonlit street, at the notice board invisible in the dark but present anyway, the way drowned things are present under calm water.

"Yes," he said. "I'm Vosk. I was an officer. Ashe's." He said the name like spitting out a bone. "And no, I didn't—what you're asking. I need you to hear the rest, and I need you to decide whether Ilsa hears it from me or from a poster, because the poster version is worse. The poster version is theirs."

"Then tell me the real version."

"I deserted." Silas's voice stayed level, but his hands, folded in his lap, had gone tight enough to whiten. "Three days before. I refused an order, and I ran, and I've been running along rivers ever since because rivers only go one direction and I thought that counted as going forward." He looked at Tomas then, fully, and there was no costume on his face at all. "I won't swear to you that I'm innocent. Innocent men don't hide for ten years. But I'll swear to this: I never fired. And I've spent every day since wishing I'd been braver sooner, instead of just brave late."

Tomas stood in the doorway with his heart hammering and his secret finally out of his chest, and found that the space where it had been didn't feel empty. It felt raw. Like a tooth pulled. Better, eventually, but not yet.

"Why didn't you tell us?" he whispered. "On the boat. Any time."

"Because telling you made it real for you. As long as I was just a captain, you were safe from knowing me." A ghost of the almost-smile. "Turns out hiding is just another way of deciding for other people. I'm told that's a flaw."

Downstairs, the tavern door banged. Boots on boards—more boots than a midnight deserved. Voices, low and official. And then Berta's voice, pitched carefully neutral, saying words that turned Tomas's blood to creek-water:

"Didn't know you had business this late. There's travelers upstairs, if that's what you're—"

"There's a man upstairs," said another voice, smooth and unhurried, a voice used to being obeyed in rooms like this. "Grey at the temples. Scar through the eyebrow. Carries himself like a soldier."

Beside the window, Silas was already moving—snuffing the candle stub, gathering the map, waking Wren through the wall with two soft knocks that meant *now* in whatever language he and she had built out of three days of shared danger. Ilsa sat up in the next room, eyes instantly wide and clear, because exile children sleep the way cats do.

"Tomas." Silas's hand landed on his shoulder, brief and warm and steady. "Whatever you decide about me—decide it later. Right now, back door. Go."

And Tomas, whose loyalty had been cracking all day like spring ice, found that his feet had already chosen. He ran for the back stairs.
