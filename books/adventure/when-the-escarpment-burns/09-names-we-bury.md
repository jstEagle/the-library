## 9. Names We Bury

The trail out of Corren's Ford climbed through pine country that smelled like a church and a lumberyard having an argument. Silas liked it better than the town below. Towns had eyes. Trees only had opinions about shade and water, and he could live with both.

He'd bought the mule in Corren's Ford from a woman who'd haggled him down to a price that was frankly insulting to the animal, then thrown in a sack of oats because, she said, "You've got the look of a man who'll need the luck." He hadn't asked what that look was. He suspected it was the same one he'd been wearing since the dockside warehouse burned, and since Tomas had come back from the notice board outside the ferry office with his face arranged into something very careful and very wrong.

Three days now. The boy hadn't said a word about it. He'd been polite—*pass the bread, Silas; is the fire high enough, Silas*—in a way that made Silas's back teeth ache. Wren had noticed too, because Wren noticed everything except when to keep her mouth shut, but she'd kept her theories to herself for once, which meant she was saving them up like a squirrel hoarding grievances for winter.

Ilsa walked ahead with the map rolled in her coat, reading the country the way Silas read water. She was good at it. Better than him at land, maybe. The thought sat strangely in him—not jealousy exactly, more like watching someone else's hands do a trick you thought only yours could do.

They made camp that night in a hollow where a creek came down off the high country, cold enough to make your fillings sing. Wren cooked beans. Wren always cooked beans. There was a theory among certain river people that she knew four recipes and three of them were lies, but nobody said so to her face, because the fourth recipe involved a knife.

Tomas volunteered for first watch, which he never did. Silas let him have it, and lay on his bedroll staring up through the pines at stars doing their slow, indifferent bookkeeping, and waited.

It didn't take long. The boy's boots crunched over within the hour, stopping just short of the firelight's edge, the way you'd approach a dog you weren't sure had finished being angry.

"Silas."

"Mm."

"Can I ask you something?"

"You can ask me anything. Whether I answer is a separate negotiation."

Tomas came around into the light and sat on the far side of the fire, knees drawn up, arms wrapped around them. In the flames' stutter he looked younger than fifteen and older than thirty, sometimes in the same second. "At Corren's Ford," he said. "The board by the ferry office. There were papers nailed up."

Silas fed a stick to the fire. "Were there."

"They're offering money. A lot of money." Tomas's voice stayed level, which cost him—you could see what it cost, in the set of his jaw. "For information leading to a man called Silas Vosk. Formerly a lieutenant in the Ninth Column of the Vhaleni Expeditionary Force. Wanted for—" he stopped. Swallowed. Started again, quieter. "Wanted for crimes at Kestrel's Ford."

The name went through Silas like a fishhook going through a wet sock: no resistance at all going in, and then everything after that was pulling.

"Kestrel's Ford," Silas repeated.

"That's what it says."

"That's not what it was called." He heard his own voice come out flat and strange. "That's what *they* called it afterward. When they needed it to be a place instead of a thing that happened."

Tomas stared at him across the coals. "So it's true. You're him."

"I'm him." Silas turned the stick in his fingers until the burning end broke off and dropped. "Lieutenant Silas Vosk, Ninth Column, attached to the staff of Commander Edran Ashe. At your service. Or disservice. The paperwork's ambiguous."

"Why didn't you say anything?"

"Because saying things is how they find you." He looked up. "Because I spent eleven years building a man named Voss out of driftwood and river mud, and he doesn't have a war in him anywhere. I liked him. I was planning to keep him."

"But the posters—"

"The posters are old, Tomas. Older than you think. Ashe has been circulating those since before you could shave, which granted isn't a high bar. He keeps them fresh the way other men keep a grudge fresh. It's the only crop he grows reliably."

Tomas wasn't listening to the clever part. Fifteen-year-olds have a genius for skipping past the clever part straight to the wound. "Kestrel's Ford," he said again. "My grandmother's village was near there. My mother talks about it like it's weather. Something that came and took everyone." His voice cracked on the last word and he hated it, you could see him hate it. "Were you there?"

And here was the moment. Silas had rehearsed this conversation for eleven years in taverns and wheelhouses and the bottomless hour before dawn, and every rehearsal ended differently, and none of the endings had ever included a boy of fifteen asking it with his grandmother's weather in his mouth.

"Yes," Silas said. "I was there."

Tomas flinched as if struck.

"But not for what you think." Silas set the stick down and made himself sit still, made himself meet the boy's eyes, because anything less would be another kind of running and he was so tired of running he could taste it, like copper at the back of the throat. "I was there because Ashe ordered the column into the valley, and I refused the order. I told him I wouldn't put my company against civilians. He had me flogged for insubordination—that part's true, the poster undersells it—and then he stripped me of command and gave the order to Captain Merle instead, who did not refuse. And while they were deciding what to do with me, I ran. I deserted in the middle of the night before it happened. Before. Do you understand? Before, not after. I wasn't there when it—"

His voice did something he hadn't authorized. He stopped, breathed, started again.

"I wasn't there when it happened. I've spent eleven years telling myself that matters. Some nights it does. Most nights it doesn't, because Merle's company was mine once, and those were my soldiers, and I trained them, and I taught half of them everything they knew about shooting, and knowing that I left doesn't unteach any of it."

The fire popped. Somewhere up the slope an owl asked its one question and got no answer, which Silas found relatable.

"So you ran," Tomas said slowly.

"I ran."

"And you've been running ever since."

"It's the one thing I'm genuinely excellent at. Ask anyone. Ask the mule."

But the joke landed in snow, no sound, no tracks. Tomas was looking at him with an expression Silas recognized from the mirror on bad mornings—the arithmetic of a person trying to decide whether a truth makes a man safe or merely explains him.

"My mother says the officer who refused," Tomas said. "She tells the story. Everyone tells it, in the camps. That one man said no, and they whipped him for it, and some people say it proves the soldiers were monsters, and some people say it proves they weren't, and nobody agrees, and everybody remembers his name." He paused. "Nobody remembers it right. They call him the Lieutenant of Kestrel's Ford. Like a title. Like a ghost story."

Silas laughed—a short, broken sound with nothing funny in it. "Eleven years of ghost stories. I hope I'm at least frightening in them."

"You're not." Tomas's voice was fierce suddenly, and young. "You're just *gone*. That's the whole story. One man said no and then he was gone, and nobody knows if it mattered."

"It didn't matter," Silas said. "That's the honest ending. Merle fired anyway. The village burned anyway. Saying no is worth exactly nothing unless someone's standing behind you when you say it." He gestured vaguely at the dark trees, the whole indifferent world. "There was no one behind me. So I became a man who never stands anywhere long enough to be stood behind. You see the engineering of it. Quite elegant. Completely gutless."

"That's not—"

"Tomas." Silas leaned forward. "I'm not asking you to forgive me. I'm not even sure there's anything to forgive, and I'm certainly not the one who gets to decide that. I'm telling you the truth because you saw the poster and you've been carrying it for three days like a stone in your boot, and that's no way for a boy your age to hike. That's all this is. Truth, offered freely, no refunds."

Tomas opened his mouth—

And that was when Silas saw her.

Ilsa stood at the edge of the camp, where the trail came up out of the dark, and she must have been there for some time, because her bedroll was under her arm and her boots were in her hand, and she was standing very still in the way of a person who has just learned that the floor of their house is not where they thought it was.

How much had she heard? From the angle of her, from the stillness—from the way her eyes went from Silas to her brother and back again, cataloguing—he guessed: most of it. Maybe all of it. Maybe the part where he'd said *I was there*, which was the part that would stay.

"Ilsa," Silas began.

"Don't." Her voice was quiet. That was the worst of it. He'd braced for fury; fury he knew how to survive. This was quiet the way deep water is quiet. "Don't explain it to me. I heard you explain it to him."

"I wasn't explaining. I was—"

"You were confessing." She set her boots down, one and then the other, with terrible care, like a woman putting away dishes in a house she was leaving. "Do you know what I did for six years, Silas Vosk? I sat in a rented room in Marrow's Landing with our mother's ledger books and I copied names. Four hundred names. Every Vhaleni family that lost someone at Kestrel's Ford, cross-referenced against the garrison rosters we smuggled out, trying to learn which officers, which companies, which *men*. And do you know what I used to pray?" She almost smiled. It was the coldest thing Silas had ever seen. "I used to pray that somewhere, one of them had said no. That one of them had been human. That the story my mother told—the lieutenant who refused—I used to think, *if that man exists, then the world isn't entirely rotten.*"

The fire cracked. Nobody moved.

"And here you are," Ilsa said. "Alive. Comfortable. Sailing a boat. Telling jokes to a mule." Her breath came out white in the cold. "You got to be alive. Do you understand that? You got the one thing none of them got. You got to leave."

"Ilsa—" Tomas started.

"Did you know," she said to Silas, ignoring him, "that my father's name is in the ledgers? Third page. He drove a grain cart. He was forty-one years old and he drove a grain cart, and your commander decided that was a military target, and the man who might have stopped it was busy deserting." She picked her bedroll back up. "I'm glad you told Tomas. He needed to hear it. But don't mistake what tonight was. You confessed. Confession is not absolution. Absolution isn't yours to give yourself, and it isn't mine either—it belongs to four hundred dead people who can't be reached at this address."

She turned and walked to the far side of the camp, laid out her roll with her back to the fire, and was still.

Silas sat looking at the coals for a long time. Tomas hadn't moved either. Finally the boy said, quietly, "She didn't mean all of it."

"She meant all of it," Silas said. "That's what makes it useful." He rubbed his face with both hands. "Go get some sleep. Second watch is mine."

Tomas hesitated. Then, awkwardly, fiercely, he reached over and squeezed Silas's shoulder once, hard, the way boys do when they can't yet build sentences big enough for what they feel, and went to his bedroll.

Silas took the watch alone. The creek talked to itself in the dark. Up beyond the Escarpment's black shoulder, clouds were assembling out of the west, fat and low, the first real weather of the season, and the wind that came ahead of them carried a smell he hadn't smelled since he was a young man in a different uniform: snow coming, days early, weeks ahead of any schedule Ilsa's map allowed.

He sat with his back against a pine and did the arithmetic he'd been avoiding since Corren's Ford. Snow in the high passes inside four days, maybe five. The winter council convening at the far side. A chest full of paper that four hundred ghosts were counting on. And somewhere behind them on the trail, patient as debt, the men who burned warehouses for a living.

From the darkness across the fire, Ilsa's voice came once, flat and awake:

"If the weather turns, we move at first light. Don't be slow, Lieutenant."

* * *

They moved at first light.

The sky over the Escarpment had gone the color of dirty pewter, and the wind had teeth in it now, and when Silas looked back down the trail toward Corren's Ford, small and gray in the valley below, he saw what he'd been afraid he'd see: riders. Three of them, maybe four, coming up the switchbacks at a pace that had nothing casual in it.

Wren spotted them a heartbeat later and swore with impressive range. "Tell me those are pilgrims."

"Pilgrims travel slower and pray louder," Silas said, and turned uphill.
