## 10. The Switchback Road

The Escarpment did not announce itself. It simply arrived one morning as a wall of gray and green filling the northern sky, and Wren understood why Silas had gone quiet the night before.

They'd left the *Marrow* at Corren's Ford—or what was left of her after Silas sold her to a barge man with kind eyes and a bad handshake—and taken the switchback road out of town before the wanted posters could do their work twice. Now, three days in, the road had given up pretending to be a road. It clung to the canyon wall like a drunk to a lamppost, doubling back on itself every few hundred yards, each turn revealing more of the drop below: a river of white water so far down it looked spilled milk.

Wren had grown up on decks. She knew heights the way she knew rope—by trusting them not to kill her. But this was different. This was the mountain reminding everyone who worked for whom.

"Cheerful place," she said, mostly to hear her own voice over the wind.

Tomas, walking ahead with Ilsa's map case slung across his chest like a bandolier, glanced back. "The Vhaleni pilgrims used to call this stretch the Ladder of Sighs."

"Why?"

"Because by the third switchback you're sighing, and by the seventh you're praying, and by the ninth you've made peace with whatever god will have you."

"I don't make peace," Wren said. "I negotiate."

Silas, bringing up the rear with the pack frame that held the chest wrapped in oilcloth, said nothing. He'd said very little since Corren's Ford, but he'd changed how he walked. On the river he'd been loose, economical, a man conserving motion the way he conserved words. Up here he moved like something coiled—always reading the slope above them, always placing his boots where the stone was oldest. Watching him, Wren kept catching herself thinking about the posters in Corren's Ford, the inked face that was almost his face, the name *Vosk* printed beneath it. Tomas had seen them first and said nothing for two days, which told Wren the boy had decided something, though she couldn't tell what.

She hadn't decided anything herself. That was the trouble. She'd spent the whole climb telling herself she was still weighing her options—the rival faction's scouts had been spotted on the far side of the canyon two mornings ago, three figures moving fast, and where there were scouts there was money, and where there was money there was a bargain to be struck—but the truth was uglier and simpler. She didn't know what she'd do yet, and not knowing felt like standing on ice she could hear cracking.

"You're limping," Silas said, materializing beside her without seeming to have crossed the distance between them.

"I'm favoring my good ankle. There's a difference."

"There is. One of them means we stop at noon."

"We stopped an hour ago."

"And your ankle has had an hour to get worse." He nodded up the trail. "There's a cut in the rock ahead. Old mining adit, if the maps are honest. We'll take water there."

"The maps are honest," Tomas called back. "Ilsa checked them against three sources."

"Three sources can agree on a lie," Silas said, but mildly, and Wren saw the boy grin rather than bristle. Whatever had passed between them on the trail two nights back—the confrontation Wren had pretended to sleep through—it had ended with Tomas still walking beside Silas instead of away from him. That counted for something. Wren just hadn't figured out how much.

* * *

The adit was real: a black mouth cut into the canyon wall, its timber lintel silvered with age, a trickle of snowmelt running out across the trail. Inside it went back thirty feet and stopped at a fall of rubble. Someone had camped here before them—a ring of stones, char gone soft with damp, a tin cup gone to rust.

Ilsa knelt at the fire ring and went very still.

"What?" Wren asked.

Ilsa lifted the cup, turned it. Stamped into the tin's base, half worn away, was a mark Wren didn't recognize—a stylized mountain over three lines.

"That's the Kestrel mark," Tomas said quietly. "The other faction."

"How long?"

Ilsa touched the ash again, rubbed it between finger and thumb, and Wren watched her become, briefly, someone else—not the tired woman who'd begged passage on a riverboat, but somebody harder, somebody who had done this kind of reading before, in worse places. "Two days. Maybe three. They're not hurrying."

"They know something we don't," Silas said. He'd set down the pack frame and was studying the trickle of meltwater, the way it ran, the angle of the rubble pile at the adit's end. "Or they're not racing us at all. They're waiting for us to clear the hard part and then following our route through it."

"That's not comforting," Wren said.

"No," Silas agreed. "It isn't." And then, because he was apparently incapable of leaving well enough alone: "Wren. Your ankle. Off the boot."

"It's fine."

"It's swollen to the size of a plum and you've been compensating since the second switchback, which means your right knee is doing work it wasn't built for, which means tomorrow you'll be limping on both legs. Boot off."

There was a tone in his voice Wren recognized from the river—the captain's voice, the one that brooked no argument about lines in a storm. She hated how much she didn't hate it. She sat on a flat stone, pulled off the boot, and tried not to wince when the air hit the swelling.

Silas crouched, turned the ankle gently with hands that were careful in a way she hadn't expected from a man who'd been a soldier. He bound it with a strip of linen and a splint of green alder, quick and neat, and when he was done he said, "You walk behind me from now on. Not because you're slow. Because if the slope goes, I want to be between you and it."

"That's a strange way to say you care."

"It's the only way I know how to say it."

Tomas snorted. Ilsa hid a smile in the map case. And Wren, who had spent four chapters' worth of miles building a wall of grievances against this man—*he lied to me, he doesn't trust me, he knows things about himself he won't say*—felt a crack run through that wall and resented it thoroughly.

* * *

They climbed through the afternoon, and the road got worse, and the sighs got shorter.

By the sixth switchback they were traversing a shelf barely wide enough for two abreast, the drop on their left falling away in tiers of talus and pine, the wall rising sheer on their right. The wind came up the canyon in long cold breaths that smelled of stone and coming winter. Somewhere below, a hawk rode the thermals, indifferent.

It was Tomas who heard it first. He stopped, hand raised, head cocked—the same listening posture Wren had seen him use on the river at night, hearing engines before anyone else.

"Rockfall," he said. "Above us. Listen."

Wren listened. At first: nothing but wind. Then, threaded inside the wind, a sound like distant applause—small stones shifting somewhere high on the wall, hundreds of feet up, where the freeze-thaw had been prying at the cliff all season.

"Move," Silas said. Not loudly. That was the frightening thing. He said it the way a man says *dinner's ready*, and somehow it was worse than shouting. "Off the shelf. To the next bend—there's a hollow under the overhang, fifty yards. Go now, go quiet, don't run."

They went. Fifty yards had never been so long. Wren, hampered by her bound ankle, fell behind even Silas's deliberately unhurried pace, and Ilsa dropped back to match her, and that was when the mountain cleared its throat.

The sound was enormous—not the applause anymore but an avalanche's older cousin, a deep grinding roar, and Wren looked up in time to see the face of the cliff come apart. It didn't look dangerous. That was the obscene thing about rockslides; they looked almost gentle, a slow bloom of dust and tumbling stone, beautiful, like watching a waterfall decide to fall sideways.

"Down!" Silas's hand closed on her collar and hauled her against the wall, into the lee of a granite rib that jutted from the slope, and he folded himself around her—arm over her head, body between her and the open air—and the world became noise.

Stones came down like weather. Big ones boomed off the shelf and bounded past close enough that Wren felt the wind of them; small ones rattled off the granite rib above their heads like hail on a cabin roof. Dust filled everything, thick and choking, turning noon to twilight. Something struck Silas's shoulder hard enough that she heard him grunt, felt him flinch, felt him *not move*, holding his position over her while the mountain spent itself.

And then it was over, the way storms are over—one moment roar, the next a ringing silence full of settling dust and Wren's own heartbeat hammering in her ears.

"Everyone," Silas said into the haze, voice rough with grit. "Sound off."

"Tomas. Fine."

"Ilsa. Fine. A scratch."

"Wren." Her voice came out smaller than she intended. "Fine. You're bleeding."

"My shoulder argued with a rock. The rock won." He eased off her, and she saw him properly for the first time: gray with dust, a tear in his jacket weeping red at the shoulder, dust caked in his eyebrows so thickly he looked suddenly old. Then he coughed, spat grit, and asked, with genuine concern, "How's the ankle?"

Wren stared at him. "You took a rock to the shoulder and you're asking about my ankle."

"The rock's done with me. Your ankle's still a problem."

Behind them, where the trail had been, there was no trail. The shelf was buried under a fan of fresh talus twenty feet high, blocks the size of barrels tumbled together with gravel and shattered pine. The road forward was gone. The road back was gone. They stood on a broken tooth of the mountain with the sky above and the gorge below and nowhere in particular to be.

"Well," Wren said, after a long moment. "That's one way to settle whether we were being followed."

* * *

Silas found the way through, because of course he did.

He spent an hour reading the slide the way he read water—walking its edges, testing stones, muttering to himself about angles and load and what holds and what lets go. "Rivers and scree are the same animal," he explained, when Tomas asked. "Both of them are always trying to get downhill. Both of them will carry you along if you respect the current and bury you if you fight it. You cross a slide the way you ferry a rapid—pick your line, commit, and never stop moving."

They crossed in single file, Silas first, then Wren (because he insisted, and because her ankle had settled the argument), then Ilsa, then Tomas with the map case hugged to his chest. Halfway across, a slab shifted under Wren's boot and slid a foot, and her stomach dropped, and Silas's hand shot back and caught her wrist without his even looking, the way he'd once caught her slipping on wet deck plating a hundred miles and a lifetime ago.

"Line," he reminded her. "Commit."

"I'm committing," she muttered. "I'm committing extremely reluctantly."

"Reluctant commitment is still commitment."

They gained the far side as the light went long and copper, and found the trail resuming beyond the slide as if nothing had happened—as if the mountain had merely sneezed and smoothed its sleeve. Below them, far down the canyon, Wren spotted movement: three figures on the opposite rim, tiny with distance, stopped at the edge of the dust cloud still hanging over the slide. Watching. Measuring.

One of them raised an arm—not waving. Pointing. Then all three turned and began moving upstream along the rim, fast, toward some crossing only they knew about.

"They'll be behind us by morning," Ilsa said quietly. "The Kestrels know these canyons. Their grandmothers smuggled grain through them."

"Then we don't sleep long," Silas said. He looked at Wren—at her ankle, at the dust still in her hair—and something crossed his face that he didn't quite manage to hide. Concern, unarmored. "We camp at the next water. Real rest, both watches set. Tomorrow we climb."

* * *

The camp was a hollow among boulders with a spring seeping from a crack in the rock, cold enough to make teeth ache. Ilsa brewed tea from needles. Tomas cleaned the grit out of everyone's boots with the solemn thoroughness of a boy who needed useful things to do with his hands. And Wren sat beside Silas while he re-dressed his shoulder, because he was making a hash of it one-handed and his pride would never ask.

"Hold still," she said, taking the bandage.

"I can manage."

"You can manage to bleed on your shirt. Hold still."

He held still. In the last light she cleaned the gash—a long bruised furrow where the rock had grazed him—dressed it, bound it. His skin was warm under the grime. Up close he smelled of woodsmoke and stone dust and, faintly, of the river, as if the Alder had gotten into him permanently and would never wash out.

"You didn't hesitate," she said. She meant the slide. She meant all of it.

"Neither would you."

"I might have hesitated a little. For effect."

That earned her the ghost of a smile. Then it faded, and he said, quietly, "I owe you honesty, Wren. More than I've given. When this is over—if we get over—there are things I need to tell you. About who I was. Some of it you may already suspect."

"I saw the posters, Silas. So did Tomas. We've all been very politely not mentioning it for two days, which is the most exhausting politeness of my life."

"I know." He looked at his hands. "Knowing and understanding aren't the same thing. What I was—what I did and didn't do—I want you to hear it from me, not from ink on paper. But not tonight. Tonight the truth can wait until we're somewhere it can't be interrupted by rockfall or Kestrels."

Wren tied off the bandage and sat back. She thought about the bargain she'd been nursing for weeks—the share, the sale, the clean exit—and found, examining it honestly for the first time, that it had gone stale on her somewhere back on the river, maybe the moment he'd stepped between her and the mountain without a second's calculation. You couldn't sell out a man who'd use his own body as a roof over yours. It didn't balance. The arithmetic refused.

"Tomorrow, then," she said. "Your truth. My attention. Fair trade."

"Fair trade," he agreed.

Across the fire, Ilsa was showing Tomas the map by needle-light, tracing the switchbacks they'd climbed and the ones still to come—a long ascending scribble ending at a saddle marked with a tiny symbol Wren couldn't read. Ilsa's finger stopped on the saddle and pressed there, hard, as if she could hold the whole future in place by pinning it to the page.

And that was when Tomas, scanning the dark canyon below out of pure habit, went rigid.

"Lanterns," he whispered. "On the rim. Two—no, four. Moving fast."

Silas was on his feet before the boy finished speaking, dousing the fire with a sweep of his boot, and the night snapped shut around them like a trap. Far below and across the gorge, four points of light swung along the opposite rim, closing on the crossing the Kestrel scouts had marked—closing on their side of the canyon, on the trail ahead, on the only road over the Escarpment.

"They're not following us anymore," Ilsa breathed.

"No," Silas said grimly, shouldering the chest. "Now they're ahead of us."
