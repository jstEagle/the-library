## 11. Ashe's Voice

The agents had been tied to a lodgepole pine since sundown, and by morning one of them had worked his wrists raw enough to complain about it.

"Loosen the rope," the taller one said. He was sitting with his back against the bark like a man waiting for a coach that was late, which Silas had to admit took a certain kind of nerve. "You've got us trussed up like hams in July heat. A man could lose fingers this way."

"A man could," Silas agreed. He crouched at the edge of the fire pit, turning a strip of dried venison over the coals without much interest. "I've seen it happen."

"You were army. You know how this goes."

Silas did not look up. That was the trick he'd learned early — the less of your face you gave a man, the more of his own face he gave you. "I was never army."

"Vosk."

The name landed on the camp like a stone dropped into still water. Wren's knife stopped mid-whetstone. Tomas, who had been pretending to sleep under the wagon tarp they'd bought in Corren's Ford, went very still in the way of boys who have learned that stillness is a kind of armor. Only Ilsa moved, and she moved toward the fire, arms crossed, watching Silas the way she'd watched him since the posters in town — not with fear, exactly, but with the particular attention of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like.

"That's not my name," Silas said.

"It's the name Commander Ashe knew you by." The agent shifted, wincing as the rope bit. "It's the name on the pardon he's offering you."

Silas turned the venison again. The fat hissed and spat. "We don't want your pardon."

"You haven't heard what comes with it."

"I've heard what comes with everything Ashe offers. It comes with a bill."

The second agent laughed — a short, ugly bark — then stopped when the first one glared at him. This one was younger, maybe twenty-five, with a soldier's bad haircut growing out into something shaggier, and he'd spent the whole night shivering in a way that told Silas he'd been garrison-raised, soft in the ways that mattered. The tall one was different. The tall one had river mud dried in the creases of his knuckles and a scar through his eyebrow that Silas half-recognized, and that half-recognition was the most unpleasant thing about the whole morning.

They'd caught the pair two days back, at the mouth of Miller's Draw, where the trail narrowed between rock walls wide enough for one mule and its opinions. The agents had come up the draw quiet, professional, three of them — and gone out of it considerably less quiet, because Tomas had heard the third one's boot scuff on shale and Ilsa had dropped a deadfall log across the path behind them, and after that it had been short work. Short, and uglier than Silas would have liked. The third agent had run. These two they'd marched back to camp, tied to the tree, and left to think about their choices while the mountains thought about theirs.

Now the tall one leaned forward as far as the rope allowed, and his voice changed — dropped the bluster, found something almost reasonable underneath it.

"Captain Voss. Lieutenant Vosk. Whichever name you're wearing this season." He smiled, thin and knowing. "You should hear the offer before you decide it's beneath you. Men have died for less than what he's offering you for free."

"I'm listening," Silas said, mostly because refusing outright would only make the man talk longer.

"Ashe wants the chest. You know that. What you may not know is what he's willing to pay." The agent ticked items off on his bound hands, absurdly formal. "Five thousand crowns. Hard coin, not script. A full pardon — your name struck from every list in every garrison from here to the coast. And reinstatement. Your commission restored. Your rank restored. Your *history* restored, Captain. Everything that happened at—" he paused, delicately, choosing his ground, "—at the end, expunged. You'd be able to walk into any city in the Protectorate and be greeted as an officer of good standing instead of hunted like a dog."

The fire popped. Somewhere above them a jay made its harsh opinion known and flew off.

Wren spoke first, because Wren always spoke first when silence got expensive. "Five thousand crowns." She said it flatly, testing the weight of it. "That's real money."

"It's real money," the agent agreed warmly.

"For a box of papers."

"For a box of papers that five nations would burn a province to hold."

"And all he has to do," Wren went on, and here her voice did something complicated, something that wasn't quite mockery and wasn't quite not, "is hand it over to the man who burned the country it came from. Walk away from us. Go back to being whoever he was before he was nobody." She looked at Silas across the fire. Her eyes were unreadable, which from Wren was itself a message. "Sounds like a bargain to me."

"Wren," Silas said quietly.

"What? I'm doing the arithmetic. Somebody around here ought to." She went back to her whetstone, but slower now, and Silas saw that her hand wasn't steady. Five thousand crowns was more than either of them had seen in a lifetime of river work. He filed that away, the way he filed away everything about Wren these days — carefully, and with a low-grade dread he couldn't quite put a name to.

"The boy," the younger agent said suddenly, and his voice cracked on it, which was interesting. "Tomas. Ashe knows about him too. Knows he's Vhaleni. Knows he's on the lists." He swallowed. "There's no pardon for the boy, understand. But if the chest comes back, Ashe said — he said the boy wouldn't be *pursued*. There's a difference. There's a big difference."

Tomas sat up under the tarp. His face was pale and set, and he looked at Silas with an expression Silas couldn't read at all — some new thing, some thing that had been building since Corren's Ford, since the posters, since whatever the boy had seen and hadn't said.

"There's a difference," Ilsa repeated softly. She hadn't taken her eyes off Silas. "Yes. There's always a difference, with men like Ashe. The difference between the knife at your throat and the knife an inch away. Generous of him."

"Ma'am—"

"Don't *ma'am* me." She stepped closer to the tree, close enough that the tall agent had to tilt his head back to keep her in view. "You want to know what your commander's pardon is worth? Ask him what happened at Kestrel Ford. Ask him who gave the order. Ask him why the chest exists at all — why a man spends years burying ledgers in a riverboat's salvage line unless the paper says something he can't afford to have read aloud." Her voice didn't rise. That was the frightening part of it. It stayed level and cold as well-water. "Your commander isn't offering a pardon. He's offering to buy back his own confession."

The agents exchanged a look. It was quick, but Silas had spent eleven years reading faces for a living — first as a smuggler's pilot dodging customs cutters, later as whatever he'd been before, and longest of all as a man trying not to be recognized — and he caught it. It wasn't guilt, exactly. It was the look of two men who had heard something like this before, late at night, in garrison bunks, and had agreed without agreeing to never speak of it.

"We follow orders," the tall one said finally. "That's the trade we're in."

"I know the trade," Silas said. "I held a share of it once."

He stood, brushed off his knees, and walked away from the fire before anyone could see what his face was doing.

* * *

They kept the agents tied through the day, fed them venison and water because Silas refused — loudly, and with more heat than the situation strictly required — to treat prisoners worse than he'd treat mules. Wren raised an eyebrow at that. Ilsa said nothing at all, which was worse.

In the afternoon Silas climbed the ridge behind camp alone, the way he did most days now, and looked north.

The Escarpment filled half the sky from up here, a wall of gray and white going up into cloud, its canyons stitched with the thin silver threads of frozen waterfalls. Somewhere up there was the pass. Somewhere beyond it, the winter council, and Ilsa's land grants, and whatever was left of a nation that existed now mostly in ledgers and in the memories of people like her. Three days' hard climbing, if the weather held. Two, if it broke against them and they were fools.

Behind him, downhill, the camp smoke rose straight in the windless cold. Four people and two prisoners and a chest wrapped in oilcloth, and every one of those elements pulling in a direction Silas didn't fully trust.

*He knows about the boy.*

He turned that over. The younger agent had said it too easily, or not easily enough — Silas couldn't decide which bothered him more. If Ashe truly knew Tomas was on the lists, then the offer wasn't just bait for Silas. It was bait with a hook shaped like a sixteen-year-old boy. And the worst part, the part that sat in Silas's chest like swallowed river stone, was that it was *good* bait. Not pursued. Not pardoned, not saved, just — not hunted. For a kid whose whole life had been running, that was nearly mercy. Nearly.

Ashe had always understood the difference between what men wanted and what they'd admit wanting. That had been his genius, back when Silas had called him sir. Other commanders motivated with fear. Ashe motivated with *almost*. Almost enough pay. Almost enough leave. Almost enough forgiveness that you kept believing the rest was coming, one more campaign, one more order followed, one more village—

Silas pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes.

Don't. Not yet. Not until dark, anyway, when it came whether he wanted it or not.

* * *

It came at dark.

He slept apart from the others, in the lee of the wagon, as he had every night since Corren's Ford — partly habit, partly because he snored, mostly because he didn't trust what he might say in his sleep. The fire had burned down to a red eye. Wind had come up from the north, carrying the smell of snow that hadn't fallen yet but was thinking about it.

And in the dream, for the first time in eleven years, he was there.

Kestrel Ford. He knew it was Kestrel Ford even though the dream rendered it wrong — the river too wide, the willows too green for the season, the church steeple standing where no steeple had stood. Dreams did that. They dressed the dead in borrowed clothes so you could stand to look at them.

He was on the near bank in the dream, in a uniform that fit him perfectly, which no uniform ever had, and Ashe was beside him on a gray horse, and the sun was coming up the way it had come up that morning, gold and indifferent, lighting the mist off the water so the far shore looked like somewhere worth visiting.

"The orders are clear, Lieutenant," Ashe said, and his voice in the dream was the voice from the waking world, the reasonable voice, the almost-voice. "The ford must be denied to them. All of it. You understand me. *All.*"

"Sir, there are families on that bank. There are children—"

"There are supplies on that bank, and supplies in enemy hands are ammunition." Ashe turned in the saddle, and his face was calm, and that was the horror of it, that was the thing Silas had never been able to explain to anyone who hadn't seen it: there was no cruelty in it at all. Just bookkeeping. "Give the order, Vosk."

And in the dream — this was the part that was new, this was the part that had never happened in any of the small grey nightmares that usually served him instead — Silas opened his mouth to refuse. He felt the word *no* build in his chest like a wave. He felt his hand come up, felt himself turn to the firing line drawn up along the bank, eighty men with loaded rifles and faces like wet paper, and he heard himself say, clear as a bell across the water:

"No, sir. I won't. Find another officer."

And Ashe smiled — smiled, in the dream, kindly, the way he'd smiled at Christmas dinners and promotions — and said, "Very well," and gave the order himself, and the volley went off, and Silas stood there untouched and unblamed and *clean*, and the smoke drifted across the river, and through the smoke he could see them falling, and no one, no one in the whole golden morning, looked at him at all.

Because that was the dream's cruelest invention. In the dream, his refusal had cost nothing. In the dream, he had said no and the world had simply absorbed it and done its work without him, and he had walked away with clean hands and a clear conscience and nothing to run from, nothing to bury, no invented name, no river, no eleven years of sandbars and debts paid in towns he abandoned before anyone could ask him a direct question.

He woke with his own hand clamped over his mouth.

The fire was ash. The wind had strengthened; the wagon cover snapped and muttered. For a long moment he lay still, heart slamming, tasting woodsmoke and the copper ghost of panic, and tried to remember which world he was in. Then memory sorted itself: the ridge, the agents, the chest, the pass. The offer.

*Everything that happened at the end, expunged.*

That was the poison in it, he understood now, lying in the dark with frost forming on the wagon canvas above him. Not the money. Not even the pardon. It was the *expunging*. Ashe was offering to erase the massacre — and Silas knew, with the terrible clarity that only comes at three in the morning, that some exhausted, hollowed-out corner of himself wanted to take it. Wanted to wake up tomorrow as a man with no Kestrel Ford in him at all. The dream had shown him the shape of the temptation perfectly: not forgiveness, but *forgetting*. A self with no scars, purchased at the price of everyone else's.

But the dream had gotten it wrong, and getting it wrong was the only mercy in it. Because that wasn't how it had happened. He *had* refused. He had stood on that bank and said no, and it had cost him everything — the brand, the flight, the name traded for an alias like a worn coat — and the volley had fired anyway, with or without him, and the world had not absorbed it, and neither had he. That was the whole point. That was why he was here, freezing on a mountainside, guarding a box of other people's grief: because some things don't get expunged. Some things you carry. The only question left was whether you carried them running or carried them standing still.

A floorboard of the night creaked — boots, moving careful among the rocks. Silas's hand found his knife before his mind caught up.

"Tomas." The whisper came out of the dark, barely a breath. The boy crouched at the edge of the wagon's shadow, eyes huge in the firelight's last glow. Behind him, at the tree line, the two tied agents were visible as darker shapes against darker shapes — and one of the shapes was sitting up straight, and one of the shapes was gone.

"They cut loose," Tomas whispered. "The young one. He's been working the rope since moonset. And there's—" The boy swallowed, and Silas watched him fight down fear the way you'd swallow a stone. "There's lights in the draw below. More than two. A lot more."

Silas was already reaching for his boots.

Below them, faint on the wind, someone downstream blew a single low note on a signal whistle — the old army pattern, two-and-one, *assemble on me* — and the note hung in the frozen air like the first crack of ice on a spring river.

The dry season was ending. And the mountain, it seemed, intended to make them earn the pass.
