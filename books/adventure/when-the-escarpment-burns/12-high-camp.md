## 12. High Camp

The storm found them an hour above the treeline and did not let go.

It came down the Escarpment like something with intent—first a wind that made the canvas guy-lines sing, then sleet that ticked against the tarp like a thousand small fingernails, then snow, real snow, fat and businesslike. By the time they'd wrestled the tent up behind a shelf of granite, Ilsa's fingers had stopped negotiating with her and simply refused. She sat on her bedroll inside the shelter, hands tucked in her armpits, watching Tomas and Wren argue over the stove while Silas stood at the tent flap with his back to them all, reading the weather the way other men read letters from home.

"Bad?" Tomas asked.

"Two days," Silas said. "Maybe three."

"Three days of *this*?" Wren gestured at the flap, where the wind was doing something unspeakable to the tarp corner she'd just staked twice.

"Three days of worse than this," Silas said. "This is it introducing itself."

Ilsa almost smiled. It was the first joke he'd made since the couloir, since Wren came back to them with frostbitten cheeks and the chest strapped to her back and a confession none of them had asked for but all of them, Ilsa thought, had needed to hear. Things were not mended. But they were holding, the way a patched hull holds—not pretty, not trusted, but floating.

The stove finally caught, and the small space filled with the smell of burning pine scrub and melting snow. They had food for five days if they ate like monks, four if they ate like Tomas, who was already eyeing the ration bag the way he'd once eyed the riverboat's galley. Ilsa doled out hardtack and dried fish and a thumb's width of dark sausage each, and when Tomas opened his mouth to protest the portion, she said, "Eat it or wear it," which was their mother's phrase, and he shut his mouth, and for a moment the tent was very quiet because they were both somewhere else entirely.

* * *

The storm thickened as the light failed. There was nothing to do but be inside it together, four people and a chest wrapped in oilcloth, waiting for the mountain to finish its tantrum. Wren produced a tin of tea leaves she'd been hoarding "for emergencies," and when Ilsa pointed out that this qualified, Wren said, "I meant *my* emergencies," but brewed it anyway.

They drank tea that tasted mostly of tin and faintly of pine, and it was the best thing Ilsa had tasted in weeks.

It was Tomas who broke first. He always did. Sitting still was a form of torture the boy hadn't learned to endure, and by the second hour of wind-noise he was fidgeting like a man with ants in his boots.

"Tell the story," he said to Ilsa. "The one about the fountain."

"There is no story about the fountain."

"You *told* me there was a story about the fountain."

"I told you not to put soap in the fountain."

"And then there was a story." He grinned, gap-toothed, fifteen years old and insufferable, and Ilsa felt the old ache and the old warmth arrive together, twins, as they always were.

Wren leaned forward, elbows on knees. "Soap in a fountain. Now you have to tell it."

"It's nothing. It's a children's story."

"We're trapped in a tent on a mountain," Wren said. "We are exactly as mature as children right now. Tell it."

So Ilsa looked into her tin cup and told it.

"The Fountain of the Nine Spouts stood in the Weaver's Quarter, in Vhalev, the capital. Nine spouts, one for each of the old guilds—weavers, dyers, boatwrights, glassmakers, all of them. The water came down off the mountains through clay pipes two hundred years old, and in summer it ran so cold it hurt your teeth. Every festival the guilds would race little boats down the channels from the aqueduct to the fountain, and the whole quarter turned out to bet on it, even though betting was officially forbidden, which everyone ignored, including the magistrates, who bet worst of all."

"Tomas put soap in it," Wren prompted.

"Tomas was six. Our mother sold dye at the market two streets over, and one festival day he got hold of a brick of indigo soap and decided the fountain needed washing. All nine spouts ran blue for three days. Blue water in the weavers' quarter—the dyers thought it was a miracle. The glassmakers thought it was sabotage. Two guild elders nearly came to blows over whether it was an omen of prosperity or ruin." She paused. "Our mother paid a fine we couldn't afford, and Tomas washed floors for a month, and the fountain ran blue anyway, and people still talk about it. The Blue Festival, they call it now. They named it after my brother being a menace."

"They named it after *genius*," Tomas said.

Silas had gone still.

Ilsa noticed it the way you notice a change in current—a small wrongness in the pattern, a held breath. He was sitting with his cup forgotten in his hands, and he wasn't looking at Tomas or Wren. He was looking at the middle distance, at something a long way away and a long time ago.

"You know it," she said. Not a question.

Silas didn't answer right away. The wind filled the silence, pressing against the canvas like something trying to get in.

"The Fountain of the Nine Spouts," he said finally. "By the chandlery with the crooked sign. There was a woman who sold roasted chestnuts on the corner every evening, winter and summer, and swore the same oath at every customer who haggled." His voice was flat, careful, a man walking on ice he didn't trust. "She used to give extras to soldiers. Free. We weren't supposed to accept."

"You were stationed there." Ilsa kept her voice level too. Two people on the same thin ice, testing it together.

"Two years. Garrison duty in Vhalev before—" He stopped. Started again. "Before everything. My barracks was in the old customs house by the river gate. You could see the aqueduct channel from the wall."

"The boat races," Tomas said eagerly, leaning in. "Did you ever see the races?"

"I judged them once." Something moved across Silas's face—almost a smile, quickly gone, like a fish under ice. "The boatwrights' entry sank at the first bend. Sabotage, they claimed. The dyers denied everything with great dignity while standing next to a barrel of the soap they'd definitely used."

Tomas barked a laugh. Even Wren laughed, a short surprised sound, and Ilsa laughed too, and for a moment the tent was full of it—four people laughing about a soap-soaked fountain in a country that no longer appeared on maps—and then the laughter ended all at once, the way laughter does when everyone remembers at the same time what it was attached to.

The wind howled. Nobody spoke.

It was Ilsa who reached across the cold air between them. Not with her hand—with words, carefully chosen, laid down like planks over a gap.

"The chestnut woman," she said. "Small, gray braid, apron always singed?"

"Marta," Silas said. "Her name was Marta."

"She gave Tomas free chestnuts too. Called him *little sparrow* because he was skinny and loud." Ilsa wrapped both hands around her cup. "Do you know what happened to the Weaver's Quarter? In the end?"

"I know what happened to all of it." His jaw tightened. "I read the reports afterward. That's not the same as knowing."

"No," Ilsa said. "It isn't."

And here was the strange thing, the thing she would turn over in her mind for years afterward: she found she wanted to tell him. Not to wound him—he'd been wounded enough, and the documents in the chest had done their own terrible work proving what he'd claimed, that he'd refused the order, that Commander Ashe had branded him traitor for it. She wanted to tell him because he was the only person in this tent, on this mountain, maybe in the world, who had *stood where she had stood*. Everyone else could mourn Vhalen. Only Silas Voss could mourn it from both sides.

"The quarter burned on the third day," she said quietly. "After the garrison fell. The fires jumped the canal—you couldn't believe it, water everywhere, and still the fire crossed. Marta's stall was on the corner by the fountain. The fountain itself cracked in the heat. All nine spouts. When the rains came that autumn, the water just poured out through the cracks and pooled in the square, and for a few weeks it looked almost like it used to. People came and sat around it. Like pilgrims." She swallowed. "Then the patrons' crews came through and demolished the square for their new depot. Paved it. You can't tell anything was ever there."

Silas set his cup down very slowly, as though his hands needed a task.

"I helped build that depot," he said.

Tomas's head came up sharply. Wren went rigid.

"Not the demolition," Silas said quickly, and his voice cracked on the second word, and he steadied it. "Not that. The construction. After. I'd deserted by then—I was gone, I was running, but the men I trained, the engineers I'd taught to survey—they built it. My work was in those walls. Every road I graded, every foundation I laid in that country, they used. I built things for them for six years, and then I built the thing they paved your fountain with." He looked at his hands. "I've spent eleven years trying to outrun that. Turns out the river doesn't go far enough."

The stove popped. Snow ticked against the canvas. Ilsa found that her eyes were wet and did not hide it, because hiding it would have been a lie, and tonight of all nights she was tired of lies.

"My mother died in the third day," she said. "You should know that. Since we're telling the truth. She went back for the dye stock—all that indigo, it was worth a season's income—and the street went up while she was inside." Her voice caught; she let it. "Tomas doesn't remember her face without help. I'm the help. So I tell him the fountain story, and the market story, and the story about how she sang off-key on purpose during festivals because she said the gods hated a perfect note. And now you're sitting here having bought chestnuts from the same corner she sold dye two streets away from, and I don't know what to do with that. I don't know whether it makes you closer or farther."

"Both," Silas said simply. "It makes me both."

Wren cleared her throat. "For what it's worth," she said gruffly, "the man who pulled me out from under that rockslide yesterday wasn't building anybody's depot. Just saying."

"You were under a rockslide because you took the chest and ran," Tomas said.

"One crime at a time, kid."

Despite everything, despite the grief sitting in the middle of the tent like a fifth passenger, Ilsa felt her mouth twitch. Wren had a gift for that—breaking tension the way you break ice, bluntly, with a tool, before it can crush the hull.

* * *

They banked the stove and settled into bedrolls as the storm screamed itself hoarse outside. Sleep came slowly. Ilsa lay listening to the wind and to the sounds of the others: Wren's occasional muttering (she talked in her sleep, always about cargo), Tomas breathing deep and even, already gone, and Silas—Silas awake, she was sure, staring at the canvas the way he stared at rivers, reading something in it.

"Vosk," she whispered, into the dark. His old name. She said it gently, testing.

A pause. "That's not me anymore."

"No," she agreed. "I'm beginning to understand that. But the boy who judged the boat race, who took chestnuts from Marta—that boy was real. Whatever name he wore." She rolled toward him. In the dim glow of the banked stove she could just make out his profile. "My mother believed the gods hated a perfect note. I think she'd have liked you. You're very far from perfect, Captain."

"That," Silas said, "is the kindest thing anyone's said to me in eleven years."

She slept then, really slept, and dreamed of blue water running in nine stone spouts, and woke once in the black hours to hear Silas humming—very low, barely a breath—some half-remembered festival tune, off-key on purpose, and pretended to still be asleep so he wouldn't stop.

* * *

Morning came gray and white and silent. The storm had spent itself; the wind dropped to a moan, and through the tent flap Ilsa saw the world remade—snow to the knees, the sky a lid of pearl, the Escarpment's upper reaches hidden in cloud.

They ate the last of the sausage and half the remaining hardtack. Wren repacked the chest with exaggerated care. Tomas melted snow for water and complained about it, because complaining about chores was how he said *I'm still here, we're still us*.

They were striking the camp, Ilsa shaking frost from the tarp, when Silas—who had climbed the granite shelf at first light to read the trail ahead—came back down faster than he'd gone up.

His face was wrong.

"What is it?" Ilsa said.

"Smoke," he said. "Downslope, in the trees we passed yesterday. Fresh. More than one fire." He was already tightening his pack straps, movements quick and economical. "Someone spent the night below us. Someone moving the same direction we are."

"The rival faction," Wren said, all the humor gone out of her.

"Or Ashe's men." Silas looked at each of them in turn, and Ilsa saw that whatever softness the night had opened in him was still there—but underneath it now was the other Silas, the river-reading Silas, the one who survived. "Either way, they're between us and the pass, and the snow won't hold our tracks much longer than it holds theirs. Pack everything. We move in ten minutes."

Above the treeline, under the pearl sky, the mountain waited—and somewhere below, so did whoever owned that smoke.
