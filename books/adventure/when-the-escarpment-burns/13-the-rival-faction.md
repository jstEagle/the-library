## 13. The Rival Faction

Tomas had learned to sleep in layers of listening. On the river, in Corren's Ford, on the trail — everywhere, he slept the way a rabbit did, with one ear turned to the world and the other turned to his own heartbeat. It was a skill he'd learned the year Vhalen ended, when he was seven and the sounds outside a cellar door decided whether he and Ilsa lived or didn't.

So when the camp changed its breathing in the hour before dawn, Tomas woke before the alarm could be given.

It wasn't a sound, exactly. It was the absence of one. The night birds that had been muttering in the krummholz below the treeline had stopped. The wind had shifted, carrying a different cold down from the heights. And somewhere in the gray murk beyond the fire's last coals, something that had been still was no longer still.

He sat up in his bedroll, quiet as spilled flour, and counted the shapes around the dead fire. Silas lay nearest the trail, on his back, hands folded on his chest like a man in a coffin who had climbed in voluntarily and was only waiting for the lid. Wren was a lump at the far edge of camp, closer to the packs. Ilsa slept curled around her pack the way she always did, arms around it like it was a child, or a country.

Tomas reached for the knife under his rolled coat.

The first thing he saw was not a man. It was a rope.

It came over the rocks at the camp's uphill edge, thin and dark and deliberate, and it was followed by a hand, and the hand was followed by a face — a lean, weathered face with a strip of gray cloth tied across the brow, and eyes that caught the last of the coals' light and gave nothing back.

Tomas did the arithmetic of terror very fast. Four of them coming down the rope line. Two more moving along the streambed, low, making less noise than the stream itself. One holding back at the rocks with a crossbow, its quarrel glinting like a fishhook.

Seven. Against four. Against four who were asleep, except for a fifteen-year-old boy and a knife.

He thought about shouting. He thought about it the way you think about jumping a river — measuring the far bank and knowing you might not reach it. If he shouted, Silas woke, and Silas was fast, but fast was not the same as awake, and a crossbow bolt did not care about the difference. If he shouted, Ilsa woke, and Ilsa would fight, and Ilsa fighting seven men with crossbows was a short story with a bad ending.

So Tomas did something braver and stupider. He got up, and he walked toward the fire, and he coughed.

He coughed the way a boy coughs when he's cold, which was true, and when he's half-awake, which was also true, and he shuffled toward the coals and knelt and began, with trembling hands that were only half pretending, to feed twigs onto them. A fire. A campfire. A boy tending a fire because he was cold.

The rope men froze. He could feel them freezing, seven statues of indecision, because their plan had assumed sleep and here was a boy awake and building a fire, and plans hate surprises even more than soldiers do.

The twigs caught. The flame stood up, small and yellow, and in its light Tomas saw the nearest gray-browed man's face clearly — young, younger than Ilsa, with a scar through one eyebrow and an expression that was not cruel, which was somehow worse. These were not bandits. These were people doing a thing they hated because they believed it was necessary.

Tomas knew that expression. He'd seen it in his own reflection often enough.

"Silas," he said, conversationally, as if remarking on the weather. "Company."

Silas came awake the way water comes to a boil — no visible transition, just suddenly a different state of matter. One moment a coffin-shaped lump; the next he was on his feet with a boat hook in his hand, because Silas Voss traveled overland with a boat hook, and Tomas had long ago stopped asking why.

"Down," Silas said, and Tomas went down, and the world came apart.

* * *

The crossbow bolt took the boat hook out of Silas's hands, which Tomas would think about later — the fact that the shooter had aimed to disarm, not to kill, and what that meant about who these people were. Then the gray-brows came in from the rocks and the streambed all at once, and the camp became a place of shouting and grappling shadows and one very indignant voice.

"That's my *pan*," Wren was bellowing, from somewhere in the melee. "That is my *pan*, you absolute — give it *back*—"

Tomas, flat in the dirt as ordered, watched the fight the way he watched everything: closely, hungrily, cataloguing. Silas moved like the river in flood — not graceful, nothing graceful about it, but irresistible, everything in the way simply *moved*. He put a gray-brow into the fire ring with the boat hook's butt. He caught a second one's wrist and folded it with a sound like a green branch. A third got an arm around his throat from behind, and Silas went down with the man, and for a long, horrible moment they were a single thrashing animal.

Ilsa had not stayed down. Of course she hadn't. She'd come up with the short blade she kept under her pack and put herself between the melee and Tomas, and her face in the firelight was the face from the cellar year, the face that had fed him and lied to soldiers for him and carried him on her back through a burning orchard, and Tomas's chest hurt with love and fear in equal measure.

"Two down," Silas grunted, from under his attacker. "Three — *running* —"

They were. Two of the gray-brows had broken off and were sprinting uphill, and the crossbow man had vanished into the rocks, and the one under Silas went suddenly limp in the way of someone deciding discretion was the better organ to preserve. He scrambled free and ran, and just like that it was over, the whole thing lasting less time than it took to brew tea.

Silas knelt in the wreckage of the fire, breathing hard. Ilsa stood over Tomas. Wren stood over her pan, which she had recovered, and which she held by the handle like a war trophy.

"Everyone," Silas said, "is —"

He stopped.

Tomas saw it happen. Saw Silas's eyes go to the packs. Saw the arithmetic there — the same arithmetic Tomas had done at the rope, except with a different answer, a worse answer.

The chest was gone.

* * *

They found the trail in the growing light, and it was not much of a trail, because Wren had not wanted one. But Wren had spent three years learning tracking from Silas and had learned maybe two of the lessons, and the one she'd missed was the one about doubling back, and so her prints went up the streambed, bold as a signature, and then stopped pretending.

"She went up," Silas said. He crouched over a scuffed stone, and his voice was very flat, and Tomas had heard that flatness before, in Corren's Ford, in the doorway of the posting house, and had not known then what it cost. "Toward the couloir. Alone."

"Why alone?" Ilsa said. Her voice was not flat. Her voice had a crack down the middle of it. "She wouldn't go alone. She can't know these mountains. She'd —"

"She'd have a head start," Silas said, "and she'd have the one thing everyone in this valley wants, and she'd have decided, sometime in the night, that the rest of us were the price of it."

Tomas looked at the fire ring. At the scattered bedrolls. At the place where Wren's bedroll had been, at the edge of camp, closest to the packs, and remembered — he *remembered* it, from last night, from every night — how she always bedded down there. How she always volunteered for last watch and then, somehow, was never quite awake when relieved. How she'd asked Silas, back on the river, with that crooked grin, what a thing like the chest might be worth to the right buyer, and how Silas had looked at her and said nothing, and how the silence between them had been a door quietly closing and how none of them, none of them, had thought to wonder what Wren did behind closed doors.

He had liked her. That was the thing sitting in his stomach like a swallowed stone. He had *liked* her. She'd taught him dice games on the boat deck and cheated at all of them and let him win exactly often enough to keep him playing. She'd given him half her rations on the hard days and lied about not being hungry. She'd called him "sprout" in a way that had made him feel, for the first time since the orchard burned, like a little brother instead of a burden.

And she'd waited until they were three days from the pass, in the middle of nowhere, with the enemy behind them and the winter ahead, and she'd taken the country Ilsa had carried across a continent, and she'd left.

"Tomas." Ilsa's hand on his shoulder. "Tomas, look at me."

He looked at her. Her face was gray. Not with cold.

"We'll get it back," she said. "That's all this is. A delay. She's one person with a heavy chest and no idea where she's going, and we have Silas."

Silas had risen and was standing at the edge of camp, looking up the mountain, and he did not say anything, and his not-saying was louder than Wren's pan.

"Silas," Ilsa said. "Tell me we can catch her."

Tomas watched the old man's back. He watched it for a long time. He thought about wanted posters and the name Vosk and a boy's crisis of loyalty, and then he thought about something else, something smaller and colder: the way Wren had looked at Silas two nights ago over the fire, when she'd thought no one was watching. Not angry. *Counting.* The way you count when you're deciding whether a debt is worth collecting.

"We can catch her," Silas said at last, and turned, and his face was the flat gray face of a man reading his own obituary in a stranger's handwriting. "If she wants to be caught. If she hasn't already made other arrangements." He began rolling his bedroll with hands that did not hurry and did not shake. "Eat. Pack. We move in ten minutes."

"Other arrangements," Ilsa repeated. "What other arrangements? Who —"

"The gray-brows didn't come for us," Tomas said.

They both looked at him. He hadn't meant to say it out loud. But it had been sitting in him since the rope, since the young face with the not-cruel eyes, since the bolt that had taken the boat hook instead of the man.

"They came for the chest. Same as us. Same as her." He heard his own voice, steady and strange. "She didn't rob us. She *outbid* us. She's been negotiating with them for days — that's why they knew exactly where we'd camp. She sold us the location and bought herself a way over the pass."

The silence that followed had a sound to it, like ice settling on a lake.

"No," Ilsa said. "No, she wouldn't — she pulled you off that rockslide, she —"

"People do both," Silas said quietly. He shouldered his pack. "That's the whole lesson of my life, Ilsa. People do both."

* * *

They climbed.

The trail Wren had left went up through the krummholz and into the first bare scree, and it was good trail, honest trail, and that was somehow the worst part — that she wasn't even trying to hide, that she trusted her head start and her bargain and the mountain's indifference to everything except altitude. Silas read her prints the way Tomas had seen him read water: the weight on the uphill foot, the rest-stop at the boulder, the place where she'd paused and looked back down at the camp she'd left, because there was a scuff there, a hesitation written in gravel, and Silas had knelt over it for a long moment and said nothing at all.

They climbed past the treeline. The air went thin and bright. Ilsa set a pace that would have killed a mule, and Tomas kept it, because if he fell behind he would have to be alone with his thoughts, and his thoughts were a fifteen-year-old boy sitting by a fire playing dice with a woman who was already gone.

Near midday they came over a shoulder of rock and saw the couloir — a great frozen gash running up the mountain's face, white and blue and terrible — and at its mouth, small with distance, a single figure with a chest on her back, climbing.

"There!" Ilsa's voice cracked across the scree.

The figure stopped. Turned. For one long moment, across half a mile of cold bright air, Wren looked down at them, and Tomas would swear to his grave that she raised one hand — not in mockery, not in triumph. In something that looked horribly like farewell.

Then she turned into the couloir, and the mountain took her.

Silas stood at the edge of the drop, and the wind pulled at his coat, and when he spoke, his voice was so quiet Tomas had to lean in to catch it.

"She's heading for the high route. Alone. In this season." A pause. "She'll die up there."

"Then we go get her," Ilsa said, "and the chest."

Silas looked at the couloir a long time. And Tomas, watching him, saw something move behind the old man's eyes that he'd never seen there before — not the flat gray calm, not the river-flood competence, but something rawer. Something that looked, God help them all, like a father whose daughter had run.

"Then we go," Silas said. "All of it. Her *and* the chest. I'm not losing both."

He started down toward the couloir's mouth, and Ilsa followed, and Tomas stood one moment longer on the shoulder of rock, looking at the place where the mountain had swallowed the woman who'd taught him dice.

*Sprout,* she'd said, dealing the cards. *Never bet what you can't afford to lose.*

He'd laughed, then. He didn't laugh now. He turned and followed the others down, toward the ice, carrying a debt he intended to collect.
