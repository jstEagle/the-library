## 14. Cold Trail

The snow told Wren's story the way the river told mine.

I'd spent twenty years reading water—the way a current braided around a snag, the way a sandbar's shadow deepened before it shifted, the lie a smooth surface could tell over a hole that would swallow a man whole. Snow was just another current. It moved, it pooled, it betrayed what passed through it. And Wren had left me a chart.

She'd gone up. Not along the ridge line where the rival exiles' tracks fanned out like spilled grain, but up—a steep line through the pines above our shattered camp, straight as a towrope under strain. Her stride was long. She hadn't looked back. That hurt more than I expected it to, and I filed the hurt away where I kept everything else that didn't help.

"She went toward the couloir," I said.

Ilsa crouched beside me at the edge of camp, breath steaming, Tomas's blood still on her sleeve from the melee—his, not anyone else's; he'd taken a boot to the face and wore it like a medal he didn't want. Behind us the camp was wreckage: a torn tent, scattered rations, one of the rival faction lying groaning against a stone with a broken collarbone and my boot print on his chest, which I wasn't proud of but wasn't going to apologize for either.

"The frozen waterfall," Tomas said. He'd found his voice somewhere in the last hour, after the shaking stopped. "Wren asked about it yesterday. At the last landing. Whether it froze solid enough to climb."

Of course she'd asked. Wren never asked a question without already knowing what she'd do with the answer. I'd taught her half of what she knew about rivers and she'd taught herself the other half, which was mostly how to make money off people who thought they knew better than her. Including me. *Especially* me, apparently.

"Then we go up the couloir," I said.

"They'll see us," Ilsa said. "They split into three parties. We watched them from the rocks."

"Good," I said. "Let them watch. Watching costs them time, and time is the only thing they can't buy back."

* * *

We climbed for most of the morning, and I will not pretend it was anything but miserable.

The cold above the treeline was a different animal from river cold. River cold got into your bones and made them ache; mountain cold got into your bones and made them *leave*, quietly, without telling you, so that your fingers kept reporting for duty long after they'd stopped actually working. I'd learned this lesson once before, in a war I don't name, in a pass whose name I've also surrendered, and I'd sworn I'd never learn it again. The mountain, as usual, didn't care about my oaths.

Tomas climbed like a boy who'd grown up in hill country, which apparently he had—Vhalen's eastern provinces, he'd told me once, all limestone and goat paths. He moved ahead of us setting the pace, and every hundred yards or so he'd stop and look back with an expression I couldn't read anymore. Since Corren's Ford, since the wanted posters, there'd been a wall in the boy's eyes with me on the wrong side of it. I understood. I'd have built the same wall. But understanding didn't keep me from feeling its shadow every time he turned away.

Ilsa climbed like a woman doing arithmetic. Every step measured, every handhold tested twice, no motion wasted. Twice she stopped entirely, closed her eyes, and stood breathing until the thin air agreed to cooperate.

"You should have been a boat pilot," I told her during one of those pauses.

"I get seasick," she said.

"That explains why you're so good on land. All that practice suffering elsewhere."

Something twitched at the corner of her mouth—not quite a smile, but the place where a smile used to live before Vhalen burned down. I counted it a victory anyway. You take what the mountain gives you.

Below us, the rival faction's searchers were black specks moving through the white, three lines of them combing the lower slopes. They were good. Disciplined. They moved the way soldiers move when someone's paying them by the result, and I recognized the spacing between them because I'd invented half of it—or rather, Ashe had, and I'd drilled men into it until it lived in their spines. That was the thing about your past: you couldn't burn it, drown it, or rename it. It just waited downstream, patient as silt, until one day the dredge brought it up.

But being good at searching isn't the same as being good at tracking a woman who grew up stealing cargo off riverboats. Wren had done something clever—I saw it when we reached the base of the couloir and I nearly laughed out loud, which at eleven thousand feet comes out as a wheeze.

She'd walked the scree field below the couloir mouth in both directions, laying false trails east and west, then doubled back and gone up the ice itself. The false trails were good. On any other day, with any other quarry, they'd have cost her pursuers hours. But she'd made one mistake, bless her mercenary heart: she'd stepped in her own coming-back prints going out, and the doubled impressions sat deeper in the snow, and if you knew to look—and I knew to look, because I'd spent twenty years reading exactly this kind of lie—it pointed at her like a signpost.

"She's smart," Tomas said, studying the scree.

"She's smart," I agreed. "And she thinks I'm dumber than her, which has been true exactly once, and it involved a card game and a bottle of plum brandy, and I've never let her forget it."

"Why does that matter?"

"Because she left these trails for people who'd believe them." I looked up the couloir—a narrow throat of blue ice and gray rock, maybe six hundred feet of climbing, wind screaming through it like a kettle with ideas. "She didn't leave them for me."

* * *

The couloir was the worst kind of climb: easy enough to attempt, hard enough to kill you for getting casual about it.

The ice was old and rotten near the walls, solid only in the center channel, and the wind came down it in gusts that felt personal. I went first, cutting steps with the hatchet I'd carried since the Alder, driving each one twice before trusting it. Ilsa second. Tomas third, paying out the rope I'd rigged from mooring line—river line, good line, the last piece of the *Marrow* I owned besides the clothes I stood in, and I tried not to think about that either. There was a lot I was trying not to think about. The mountain was narrowing the list hourly.

Halfway up, the wind dropped without warning—that dead hush you get in high country, worse than the gale because you can hear yourself think—and in the hush I heard voices below. Echoing up the rock. The searchers had found the real trail.

"They're coming," Tomas said.

"How many?"

"Can't tell. Two voices. Maybe more behind."

Two voices meant two climbers, and two climbers on rotten ice behind us meant we had perhaps an hour's lead, shrinking. I put my head down and cut steps faster, and I won't claim I was thinking noble thoughts. I was thinking about Wren's hands on the rope the day the snag nearly took her off the bow, about her laughing afterward with blood in her hair, about the way she'd said, *You're stuck with me, Captain, I signed nothing but I'm here anyway.* People say things like that and you believe them, and then they take your chest and vanish into a snowfield, and you find out belief was just ballast—something to keep you steady until the storm proves otherwise.

Except I was climbing six hundred feet of ice to get her back, so maybe it wasn't ballast after all. Maybe it was keel. I didn't have time to work out the metaphor properly. My fingers were going.

Near the top, Ilsa's boot punched through a shelf of ice to the knee. She didn't scream, which frightened me more than screaming would have. I anchored, took her weight on the line, and held while Tomas talked her through pulling free—calm, steady, the boy sounding suddenly twenty years older, and I thought: *this is what war does, it ages children in fast-forward,* and hated that I knew it firsthand.

Her leg came free. Below the knee, her boot was soaked, and we both knew what that meant at altitude with the sun dropping.

"We rest at the top," I said. "Fire, dry socks, ten minutes. No more."

"Ten minutes," she repeated, like a woman agreeing to terms she intended to renegotiate later.

* * *

We topped out onto a shoulder of wind-scoured rock as the light went copper, and there—half a mile across a bowl of snow, tucked under an overhang I could just make out—was smoke. A thread of it, thin and careful, the smoke of someone who knew fires could be seen for miles and had built the smallest one she could.

Wren.

"There," Tomas said, pointing, and then his arm swung further right, and my stomach dropped.

Three figures on the far rim of the bowl. Descending toward the cave. Moving fast, unhurried in the way of people who know their quarry is cornered.

They'd found her false trails, dismissed them, and come around the high route—the route I'd have chosen myself, if I were hunting me. Which, in a sense, they were.

"No fire," I said. "No time. We cross now, in the open, and pray the light's in our eyes and not theirs."

"And her leg?" Ilsa asked quietly.

"Your leg," I said, "is about to become the fastest leg on this mountain, because the alternative is watching them reach that cave, and I have already lost one person to this mountain today and I am not losing the second."

It wasn't entirely true—I hadn't lost Wren, not yet, not finally—but grim determination runs on small lies, and I fed it what it needed.

We crossed the bowl low and fast, bent double, using a fold of ground I hoped would hide us. The cold bit through everything. My right hand had stopped reporting pain somewhere on the couloir, which I noted the way you note a bilge pump running quiet: with gratitude and suspicion. Tomas stayed glued to my shoulder, matching me step for step, and when I glanced back at him he was watching the three figures on the rim with an expression I recognized from mirrors in another life—the look of someone counting distances and weapons and odds, and not liking any column of the sum.

Halfway across, a shot cracked off the rocks above us. Then a second, closer, kicking up a plume of powder twenty feet to our left.

"They've seen us!" Ilsa shouted.

"Then stop sneaking!" I grabbed them both and ran, upright, exposed, the stupidest and only remaining tactic, and the mountain rang with more shots that all missed because shooting downhill at running targets in failing light is a fool's arithmetic even for professionals.

We reached the overhang as full dark settled, and I threw myself around the rock wall into the cave mouth—

—and stopped, because there was Wren, backed against the far wall with the chest under one arm and my own boat hatchet raised in her fist, wild-eyed, frostbitten, and absolutely ready to open my skull with it.

"Evening, Captain," she said, and her voice shook only slightly. "You're a hard man to lose."

"Wren—" I started.

"Don't." The hatchet didn't lower. Her eyes went past me to Ilsa and Tomas, then back, and something in them was wet and terrified and furious all at once. "Don't be kind to me yet. There's something in here you need to read first. Something I found. About you." She thrust the chest forward, lid open, a single page held out in her ruined fingers. "About Ashe. About what he wrote down the night Vhalen burned. And Silas—" her voice cracked clean in half, "—you need to sit down for this one."

Outside, boots crunched on snow. Three sets, closing slow, in no hurry at all.

I took the page.
