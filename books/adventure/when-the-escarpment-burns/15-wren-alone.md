## 15. Wren Alone

The cave smelled of wet stone and old smoke, which was better than it smelled outside. Outside it smelled of snow coming down the couloir like a debt collector.

Wren had run for two hours on legs that had stopped negotiating with her somewhere around the second switchback. She'd taken the chest off the camp while Tomas was shouting and Ilsa was bleeding and Silas was swinging a boat hook at a man with a knife, and she'd run because running was the one thing she'd never been bad at. Running and counting. She'd counted her share of the chest's worth eleven times on the way up, and every time the number came out different, and every time it came out bigger.

Now she sat in the dark with her back against rock cold enough to bite through wool, and the chest sat in front of her like a dog that knew what she'd done.

"Shut up," she told it.

It didn't shut up. Chests never did. That was the trouble with them—they just sat there being full of whatever they were full of, and you did all the talking, and everything you said sounded worse the longer the silence went on.

She'd meant to go through it hours ago. That had been the plan, or part of the plan, the part she'd rehearsed on the trail while pretending to check the mule's hooves: get clear, open it, find out exactly what she was holding before she handed it over to whoever paid best. A girl who grew up dredging other people's losses off river bottoms learned early that you never sell a thing sealed. Sealed things are worth what buyers imagine. Opened things are worth what they are. And imagination, in Wren's experience, always bid higher.

Her hands were shaking. She told herself it was the cold. It was a good lie—she'd told better ones—but even she didn't quite buy it, so she held her fingers over the little spirit lamp until feeling came back into them, and then she got to work.

The lock was Vhaleni army issue, forty years old at least, and the storm water hadn't been kind to it. But storms are honest workers; they do their job thoroughly. The keyhole was half silt, the tumblers rusted to suggestions of themselves. Wren picked it in the time it took her teeth to stop chattering, which was longer than usual, and then the lid came up with a groan like something exhaling after decades underwater.

Ledgers. She'd expected ledgers—Ilsa had talked about them enough, land grants and council seals and legal ownership of ground that other people now farmed. Wren understood paper worth. Paper worth was the best kind: light to carry, heavy to argue with.

She lifted the top ledger out and set it aside. Another beneath it, swollen and warped, its ink bled into gray ghosts. Letters bundled with tarred string. A leather folio, stiff as board. And at the very bottom, wrapped in oilcloth tied with wire, a single document that felt different from the rest when her fingers found it—heavier, somehow, though paper doesn't weigh more for what's written on it. Except it does. She'd known that since she was nine years old, the day a man from the salvage office put a bill of sale in her mother's hands and her mother's whole face changed weight.

Wren unwrapped the wire. Her fingers were clumsy again, and this time she didn't bother lying to herself about why.

The oilcloth fell open.

It was an order. Military hand, formal phrasing, the kind of document that gets copied three times and filed in two places and remembered by nobody except everyone it touches. She read the header first—the date, which was older than she'd guessed, older than the massacre by weeks—and then the body of it, and then she stopped reading, because reading requires breath and she wasn't doing that anymore.

*Field Order, 3rd Brigade, Special Attachment.*

*Regarding: Lieutenant Silas Vosk.*

*The undersigned records that Lt. Vosk, having been ordered to execute prisoners under standing directive, refused the order at [location redacted by later hand] and did further attempt to prevent its execution by others. Lt. Vosk is hereby stripped of commission and declared a traitor to the command. He is to be shot on sight should he be encountered. His name is to be struck from all rolls.*

*By order of Commander E. Ashe.*

There was more—a signature, a wax seal cracked down the middle, a second paragraph about disciplinary measures taken against the prisoners' guards—but Wren read none of it twice. She read the middle part once, and then again without meaning to, the way your eye goes back to a wound.

*Refused the order.*

*Attempted to prevent its execution.*

*Shot on sight.*

She sat there in the lamplight with the paper trembling in her hands, and the cave got very quiet, the way caves do, the way the world does when it decides to let you hear yourself think.

Because here was the arithmetic she'd been doing since the camp, the sums she'd run eleven times on the trail, and every sum had come back bigger: *the chest is worth gold, and Silas is in the way of the gold, and Silas is a liar anyway.* That last part had been the load-bearing beam of the whole structure. She'd built her betrayal on it the way you build a dock on pilings. He *was* a liar. She'd seen him go white over that seal in the salvage mud and lie about it, badly, the way men lie when the truth would cost them something they can't afford. She'd watched him refuse Ilsa passage, refuse Wren answers, refuse everything except his own smooth competence and his own closed mouth. A man with no past, he said. A man who pays debts in towns he leaves before dawn. She'd known him two years and he'd never once told her where he slept as a boy, whether he had a mother, what the scar on his forearm came from. Nothing. A man made entirely of surface.

So when the moment came at the camp—when the rival exiles came out of the dawn fog and the melee broke loose—it had taken her perhaps four seconds to decide. Four seconds. She'd grabbed the chest and gone, and she hadn't looked back, because looking back is how runners die, and also because she hadn't wanted to see his face.

And underneath it all, under the pilings, under the dock, under the black water: *he deserves it. Men like that always deserve it. Whatever he did over there, whatever made him white at the sight of a seal, it was bad enough to run from for twenty years, and bad men don't get to be the ones holding the map home.*

That was the beam. That was what she'd stood on.

*Refused the order.*

Wren put the document down on her knee very carefully, the way you put down something that might go off.

He hadn't lied about the massacre. He'd lied about *being in it*—no. He'd lied about nothing, actually, if you wanted to be precise, and precision was supposed to be her religion. He'd simply never said. He'd carried a paper with his own death warrant folded inside a chest at the bottom of a river, and he'd hauled that chest aboard his own boat with his own hands, and he'd gone white not because his past had surfaced but because his *sentence* had, and he'd said nothing. Not to Ilsa, whose nation he'd tried to save. Not to Tomas, who needed someone to trust. Not to her. Twenty years of silence, and the silence hadn't been guilt. It had been a man sitting on the one fact that would have gotten him killed by half the people hunting him and hated by the other half, and keeping it, because telling it wouldn't have saved anyone and might have doomed everyone.

Or—her mind, ever the merchant, ever the appraiser—because he was clever, and clever men curate their truths.

No. She tested that thought the way she tested deck plating, putting her weight on it, and it went through like rotten wood. She'd watched him take a rockslide's worth of canyon on himself to pull her back from the edge two days ago. She'd watched him give Ilsa the last of the coffee without making a show of it. She'd watched him look at Tomas the way—she'd watched him look at Tomas the way she'd once wished somebody would look at her, steady and unafraid of the looking.

And she'd sold him. Not for much, either. That was the part that started the heat behind her eyes. The rival faction's offer hadn't even been generous. Passage east, a purse, "a fair share when the matter resolves." Fair share. She'd heard that phrase her whole life from men whose shares were always fairer than hers, and she'd sworn at fifteen she'd never be the one saying it, and here she was in a cave saying it to herself in another woman's voice.

"I didn't know," she said out loud, to the chest, to the cave, to nobody.

The cave, being a cave, declined to accept the excuse. And the truth was the truth didn't want the excuse either, because she *had* known something. Not this—not the order, not the words in Ashe's hand—but she'd known Silas. Two years of knowing. She'd known a man who flinched at nothing on the river and flinched at everything on land, who checked the horizon the way other men checked their watches, who never slept with his back to a door. You don't learn those habits collecting debts. You learn them owing one.

She thought about the night he'd fled the burning warehouse, how he'd recognized the agents' tactics before the fire was even lit—*that's how my old unit clears a building*—except he hadn't said that, he'd just moved like a man following instructions written on the inside of his skull. She'd noticed. She'd noticed and she'd filed it under *trouble* instead of under *grief*, because filing it under grief would have meant asking him, and asking him would have meant he might answer, and an answered question is a door you can't pretend isn't there.

She'd chosen the sealed box. She'd chosen imagination's price over the thing itself. And the thing itself had been sitting in the bottom of the chest the entire voyage, wrapped in oilcloth, waiting for her with the patience of drowned paper.

Wren laughed. It came out wrong, half a sob wearing a laugh's coat, and she pressed the heel of her hand against her mouth to keep the rest of it in, and that didn't work either.

She wept the way she did everything—efficiently at first, angrily, furious at her own eyes for wasting water she'd need later at this altitude. Then less efficiently. Then not at all efficiently, hunched over the open chest with the order crumpling slightly in her grip, crying for a man she'd betrayed four seconds' worth, crying for the version of herself who'd done it, crying maybe a little for Vhalen, a country she'd never seen, whose dead had waited forty years in a river for someone to carry their paper home, and the someone they'd gotten was her.

"He refused," she said into her knees. "You stupid, stubborn, silent river-rat. You *refused*, and you let us all think—" 

And that was the worst of it, the part that kept the tears coming after she'd decided to stop: he'd let them think it. He'd walked up the Escarpment beside a brother and sister from the country he'd tried to save, carrying their homeland in his boat, knowing they'd have shot him themselves if they'd read this page, and he'd said nothing, because the truth served them no better than the lie. He'd taken their suspicion like freight. He'd loaded it and carried it and never once asked for help with the weight.

Ilsa had gone cold on the trail after overhearing his confession. *Deserter,* Ilsa had said, like a diagnosis. And Silas had stood there and taken it, and Wren had stood there too, and she'd thought: *good, now I know what he is.* When all along the document in her lap was the only testimony that mattered, signed by the devil himself, and it said the opposite.

She wiped her face with both sleeves, which accomplished nothing, and then she did the practical thing, because grief is no excuse for sloppy handling of evidence. She smoothed the order flat on her knee. She rewrapped it in its oilcloth—not to hide it, but because paper survives winters better dry, and this paper had a job left to do. There was a winter council beyond the pass. There were elders who would want to see Ashe's own hand declaring a traitor the one man who wouldn't kill for him. This wasn't just Silas's pardon. It was a weapon. It was, possibly, the sharpest thing in the whole chest, sharper than the land grants, because grants win ground and testimony wins *people*, and nations are rebuilt out of people or not at all.

Which meant she couldn't keep it. Which meant she couldn't sell it. Which meant the four seconds at the camp had bought her a purse she could no longer spend and a debt she couldn't drown.

"That's what you get," she told herself thickly. "That's what you get for selling sealed boxes."

Outside, the wind changed its mind mid-gust and came down the couloir carrying grit and the first dry whisper of snow. Wren snuffed the lamp to a bead and listened, because listening was the other thing she'd never been bad at, and what she heard made the cold in her chest relocate to her spine.

Voices. More than one. Below the cave, on the switchback trail, moving with the unhurried discipline of people who are not lost. And threaded through the wind, faint but unmistakable to a river ear tuned to sound over water: the click of a boot sole on stone, in rhythm. Soldiers' rhythm.

They'd followed her. Of course they'd followed her—she'd taken the chest and run, and a lone runner with a heavy box leaves a trail a child could read, and Wren had spent the last two hours crying instead of erasing it.

She looked at the chest. She looked at the dark mouth of the cave, and past it, down through the falling haze, toward the switchbacks where the footsteps were coming from.

Then she looked at the oilcloth bundle in her hands, and thought about a man halfway up a frozen mountain who believed, right now, with every reason in the world to believe it, that she'd sold him for pocket money.

"Well," Wren said, and her voice cracked, and she let it. "Come on, then."

She kicked snow across the lamp, dragged the chest deeper into the shadow at the cave's rear, and took up the iron pry bar she'd carried from the boat—the one piece of the *Marrow* she'd refused to leave behind—and positioned herself at the cave mouth with her back straight and her eyes stinging and her heart going like a paddle wheel in flood.

If it was the rival faction, she'd hold them as long as a girl with a pry bar holds four soldiers, and then she'd die having at least read the paper.

But if—if—one set of those footsteps below climbed the way Silas Voss climbed, reading stone like current, patient and relentless and utterly unwilling to quit—

Then she was going to hand him his own death warrant and say the only word she had left that was worth anything.

Footsteps rose on the trail. The snow began, fine and gray, sifting down into the couloir like ash from a country that had already burned.

Wren gripped the bar and waited to find out who she was.
