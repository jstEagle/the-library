## 16. The Couloir

Wren had been in caves before. She'd slept in them, hidden contraband in them, once spent a memorable week in one hiding from a customs man in Corren's Ford who had very firm opinions about her paperwork. But she had never been in a cave with a chest full of a dead country's sins and a confession sitting in her stomach like a swallowed stone.

The cave was shallow — a bite taken out of the couloir's east wall, maybe twelve feet deep, floored with gravel and old snowmelt. The chest sat against the back wall where she'd dragged it, and she sat beside it, and the wind came up the couloir in long white breaths that made the opening flicker like a bad lamp.

She'd read the last document three times. She could have recited it.

*By order of Commander Edran Ashe: the officer Silas Vosk, having refused the lawful command at Halden's Ford, is hereby stripped of rank and declared traitor to the Crown of Vhalen. He is to be shot on sight.*

Refused. He'd *refused*. Before the massacre, before the burning, before any of it — Silas Vosk had stood in front of the man and said no, and for that he'd been branded a traitor by the very government whose papers he was now hauling up a mountain for free.

And she had taken the chest and run.

Wren pulled her knees up and put her chin on them. Out past the cave mouth the couloir dropped away in a long white throat, and somewhere below it, she knew, Silas was climbing. She'd left his tracks crossing hers — she'd *seen* him reading them, that morning on the ridge, the way he'd gone still and then started moving like a man following a rope through fog. He was coming. She didn't know if that made her feel better or worse.

Both. It made her feel both, which was the whole trouble with Silas Voss. He was a man you could steal from and he'd still come find you, and she couldn't decide whether that was love or a trap.

"Stupid," she told the chest. "Stupid, stupid, stupid."

The chest, being a chest, declined to argue.

She heard them before she saw them — not Silas. The other ones. Voices carried up the couloir the way voices did in cold air, flattened and strange, and then three figures came around the bend below, moving fast, crampons biting. The rival exiles. The ones with the hard faces and the hard arithmetic, the ones Ilsa had warned them about back when everything had still seemed like a problem you could solve with a boat.

Wren snuffed her candle and slid back against the wall, hand finding the boat hook she'd been carrying since the foothills. It wasn't a weapon. It was a boat hook. But a man with a boat hook and bad intentions could ruin a lot of good intentions.

They stopped below the cave. One of them — a broad man with a scar splitting his beard — shaded his eyes and looked up the couloir, then at the walls, reading it the way a riverman read water.

"She's up here somewhere," he said. "The deckhand. Tracks end."

"Tracks don't end," said a woman beside him. "They get smart."

Wren's heart did something complicated. She pressed deeper into the dark.

"Vosk's behind us," the scarred man said. "Half a day, maybe less. We take the chest now, we don't have to deal with him at all."

"He's the one who refused to fire," the woman said, and something in her voice made Wren go cold all over again. "Half the council wants to shake his hand. The other half wants the same thing Ashe wanted. Either way, he doesn't get to decide where these papers go. We do."

They started up.

Wren counted her options, which took very little time, because she had one: hold the cave mouth until Silas arrived, which was either a plan or a prayer, depending on how honest she was willing to be with herself. She'd never been very honest with herself. It was one of her best qualities.

She moved to the cave mouth, boat hook low, and waited for the first face to come around the bend of rock.

* * *

It came around the bend, and Wren hit it.

Not with the hook — with her shoulder, a full-body shove that sent the scarred man staggering back into the woman behind him, boots skidding on the ice-glazed rock. The hook came around after, catching his ankle, and he went down hard with a sound like a dropped crate.

"You picked a bad cave," Wren told him.

The woman came at her with a knife, which Wren thought was unsporting, and the next thirty seconds were the worst thirty seconds of Wren's life, which was saying something, because Wren's life had contained a customs man, two floods, and a ferret. She caught the knife arm on the hook's crook, twisted, lost her footing on the gravel, went down with the woman on top of her, and did the only thing left in her repertoire, which was bite.

The woman swore in Vhaleni — a word Wren didn't know but could guess the meaning of, because some words were universal — and rolled off, and Wren scrambled back into the cave, hook up, bleeding from a shallow line along her forearm, breathing like a bellows.

Three against one. Two of them upright. The scarred man getting up.

And then, from down the couloir, a voice — hoarse, wind-scoured, absolutely furious:

"Wren!"

Silas came up the couloir like weather. He wasn't running, exactly — nobody ran at that altitude on that grade — but he was moving with the terrible steady economy of a river in flood, and behind him, smaller and lighter and faster, came Ilsa, and behind *her*, Tomas, whose face Wren had never seen look like that. Not scared. *Certain.*

The rival exiles turned. The arithmetic in the scarred man's face visibly changed.

"Four of us," Ilsa said, coming up beside Silas, her voice carrying that cold clear note Wren had heard her use exactly twice before, both times right before something broke. "And this is our chest, and our mountain, and you are standing between a Vhaleni family and their homeland, Davor. Your mother would be ashamed."

The scarred man — Davor — hesitated. Wren watched him hesitate. Watched the knife in the woman's hand lower an inch, two inches, watched the whole hard arithmetic of the morning wobble.

Then the woman lunged for the chest.

She didn't get it. She got Silas, who had put himself between the cave and the couloir the way he put himself between a boat and a rock — without discussion, without drama, the way other men breathed. The knife caught him across the shoulder and he grunted and took her wrist and turned her, not cruelly, just completely, the way you turned a boat out of a current, and put her down on the ice, and put his boot on her blade.

"Enough," Silas said. "All of you. Enough."

And the strange thing — the thing Wren would tell people about for years, when she told this story in warm rooms with drinks in hand — was that they stopped. All of them. Davor with his split beard heaving for breath, the woman pinned under Silas's boot, Ilsa with snow in her hair and fury in her face, Tomas with a rock in his fist the size of a small loaf. They stopped, because Silas Voss had one of those voices that sounded like it had already considered every argument and found them all heavy.

"The council decides where the chest goes," Silas said. "That's the whole point of the chest. That's what it *is*. If we take it from each other on this mountain, Ashe wins without sending a single man past the foothills. He's counting on this. He *built* us like this — every one of us, exiles and deserters and deckhands. People who run." His eyes found Wren in the cave mouth. "People who run," he said again, softer.

Wren's chest hurt.

Davor looked at Ilsa. Ilsa looked at Davor. Something passed between them in Vhaleni, a whole paragraph in two words, and then Davor spat, sheathed nothing because he carried no knife, and said, "The council decides," in the tone of a man agreeing to a thing he intended to be angry about for the rest of his natural life.

They went. Down the couloir, single file, the woman rubbing her wrist, Davor not looking back. Wren watched them go and thought: *that's not over.* But it was over for today, and today was the only day she'd ever been any good at.

Then it was just the four of them and the wind, and Silas bleeding quietly through his shirt, and the chest sitting in the dark of the cave like a witness.

Wren came out of the cave. She stood in front of Silas, and she had a whole speech prepared, she'd been drafting it for two days, and what came out was:

"I bit someone."

"I saw," Silas said.

"It was very effective."

"I don't doubt it." He was watching her with those river-gray eyes, patient as water. Waiting.

Wren looked at the ground. Then at the chest. Then at him.

"There's a paper," she said. "In the chest. The last one. It's — Silas, it's an order. Ashe's order. It says you *refused*. It says you refused to fire at Halden's Ford and he stripped your rank and called you a traitor and ordered you shot on sight. It says—" Her voice cracked, which was mortifying, and she pushed through it the way she pushed through rapids, fast and graceless. "It says you were the only one who said no. And I read it, and I took the chest anyway, and I ran, because I'd already made the bargain in my head and I was too much of a coward to unmake it, and I told myself you'd understand, because you never explain anything, so how would I ever *know*—"

"Wren."

"—and you came after me anyway, which is the worst part, because you *shouldn't have*, I stole from you, I sold you out to people with knives—"

"*Wren.*"

She stopped.

Silas was holding out his hand. Not for the chest. For her arm — the cut one. He turned it over gently, frowning at the shallow line the knife had left, in the exact way he frowned at hull damage, and Wren realized with a lurch that he was more concerned about the cut than the confession.

"You held the cave mouth," he said. "Alone. Against three."

"I bit someone," Wren said again, because it seemed important that this be on the record.

"I know." He looked up at her. "You could have kept running. The chest, the money, the pass — you were ahead of us, the weather was covering your tracks. Nobody would have blamed you. I wouldn't have."

"That's the problem," Wren said, and her eyes were doing something embarrassing, and she let them. "That's the whole entire problem, Silas. You wouldn't have blamed me, and I couldn't stand it. I've spent my whole life being not-blamed by people who should have been furious at me, and it's fine, it's *easy*, you just keep moving. But you—" She gestured at him, at all of him, the bleeding shoulder and the frost-burned face and the terrible steadiness. "You came up a frozen couloir for me. You don't get to not-blame me. You have to actually do it. Say I did wrong."

"You did wrong," Silas said, without a flicker of hesitation.

"Thank you."

"You stole from people who trusted you, and you did it because you were scared, and being scared explains it and doesn't excuse it, and I've done worse for better reasons, so I'm not the judge. But you came back. You held the door." He almost smiled — that rare, rusted thing, like a hinge that hadn't moved in years. "On this crew, that's how we settle accounts. We hold the door."

Wren laughed, wetly, horribly, in a way she'd deny forever. "That's a terrible accounting system."

"It's a river system," Silas said. "It's how we do things."

Ilsa had gone into the cave and come back out with the chest, and Tomas was wrapping Silas's shoulder with a strip of shirt that had probably been Tomas's shirt, complaining the entire time in the tone of a boy who had discovered that worrying about people was a full-time job with no pay. The wind came up the couloir, white and long.

And then it stopped being wind and started being snow.

Not the dry season's dust. Not the high-country flurries they'd been outrunning for a week. Real snow, fat and slow and silent, falling straight down, the kind that ended arguments and closed passes and buried the year.

"The dry season's done," Ilsa said quietly. She was looking up, letting it land on her face, and Wren saw that she was crying and smiling at the same time, which seemed to be contagious on this mountain. "The council convenes at moonrise. We have until then."

"How far?" Tomas asked.

"Half a day. Downhill."

"Then we walk," Silas said. He picked up one end of the chest. Wren took the other without being asked, because some things didn't need asking anymore, and together they swung it up between them, and the four of them started down the couloir into the falling snow.

Behind them, far below at the mouth of the valley, Wren caught one last glimpse through the white — a line of dark figures on the saddle ridge, arranged like a sentence waiting to be finished. Riders. Too many. Too still.

One of them wore a commander's coat.

Silas saw them too. Wren felt it go through him, through the chest, through her hands on the other end — a single long tremor, like a hull hitting something under the water.

"Silas," she said. "Who is that?"

"You know who it is," he said.

The snow came down harder, and the man in the commander's coat began to ride down to meet them.
