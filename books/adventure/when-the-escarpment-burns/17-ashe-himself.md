## 17. Ashe Himself

The saddle came into view an hour past noon, and Silas knew before he saw them that it would not be empty.

He'd been reading the mountain the way he read the river—the way the crows lifted off the scree in a loose, unsettled spiral instead of streaming downwind; the way the wind changed its mind at the notch and carried back to him a smell that didn't belong up here. Woodsmoke. Coffee. The particular chemical bite of gun oil kept warm near a body.

"Company," he said quietly.

Ilsa stopped mid-stride. Tomas's hand went to the hatchet at his belt—Wren had finally taught him to carry it low, where it could actually be drawn—and Wren herself just sighed, the sigh of a woman who had been expecting this since the couloir and was mostly annoyed it had taken so long.

"How many?" she asked.

"Enough." Silas crouched behind a granite shoulder and studied the saddle through the gap between two boulders. "There."

They were arranged well, he had to give them that. Six figures spread across the notch like they'd grown there, two high on the flanking rocks with rifles slung but hands free, four below in the lee of a drift fence some shepherd had abandoned a century ago. And standing apart from all of them, at the exact center of the saddle where the light fell best, a man in a grey officer's coat that had no business being this clean at nine thousand feet.

Commander Edran Ashe had always understood theater. It was, Silas thought, the thing he'd mistaken for greatness.

* * *

"There's no way around," Ilsa said. She'd come up beside him without a sound, which still unnerved him after three weeks on the trail. "I've seen the maps. The eastern face is a cliff. The western is worse."

"I know. I helped survey these passes." He said it flatly, and watched her jaw tighten. Twenty years of knowing things about him she wished she didn't. "We walk through. They want to talk first. If they wanted us dead we'd already be dead—he wouldn't waste six men on corpses he could get for two."

"You're sure of that?"

"No," Silas said honestly. "But it's the best bet we have, and I've survived twenty years on best bets."

Wren snorted. "You've survived twenty years on running. Different thing entirely."

"That too." He stood, brushed the scree dust from his knees, and found that his hands were steady, which surprised him. He'd imagined this meeting for two decades—in fever dreams, in the gray hour before dawn on the river when the fog made shapes of everything—and in every version of it his hands shook. Apparently the real thing was easier than the rehearsal. Or apparently he'd changed more than he'd noticed. Both possibilities sat strangely in his chest.

Tomas fell in beside him as they started up the last stretch. The boy said nothing, but he shifted position so that he walked half a step ahead, between Silas and the open ground, and Silas felt the gesture land somewhere under his ribs and stay there.

* * *

Ashe had aged well, which seemed unfair. The war had taken Silas's hair and given him a riverman's squint and a scar he didn't like discussing; it had apparently merely given Ashe distinguished silver at the temples and a coat tailored to hide whatever else it had done. He looked like a man who advised kings. He looked, Silas thought, exactly like what he was: the kind of officer who signed orders from rooms where the smoke never reached.

"Vosk." Ashe said the name gently, almost fondly, the way you'd greet a dog that had run off years ago and turned up at the neighbor's farm looking thin. "You look terrible. Mountain air disagrees with you."

"It's the company," Silas said. "It usually is."

Behind him, Wren made a small sound that might have been a laugh strangled for politeness.

Ashe smiled. It was a good smile. Silas remembered recruits trusting it. "Twenty years, Vosk. Twenty years I let you be dead, because dead men are comfortable, and because—" the smile warmed another degree, "—because I hoped you'd find your way back eventually. And look at you. You did."

"I found my way onto a boat," Silas said. "Different thing."

"A boat. Yes." Ashe's gaze slid past him, cataloguing—Ilsa's set face, Tomas's hand on the hatchet, Wren's expression of a woman calculating resale value on everyone present. "And now you're hauling a chest across the Escarpment for people who can't pay you. That doesn't sound like you, Vosk. The man I trained took payment. The man I trained understood that sentiment is a luxury for people who've never had to hold a line."

"The man you trained held a line at Kestrel Hollow," Silas said, "and then read the reports afterward and discovered the line had been holding civilians in place. So no. The man you trained drowned. Took me a while to notice, but the river's thorough."

Something flickered across Ashe's face—not guilt, Silas noted, nothing so useful as guilt. Recalculation. "You were never suited to this," Ashe said, softer now, the voice he'd used in tents before bad orders, the voice that made men feel chosen rather than used. "That was always your problem, Vosk. Too much conscience for the work, not enough for the grave. But times change. Nations change. What's erased can be un-erased, if a man has friends in the right rooms." He gestured, and one of the agents below produced a leather folio and held it out at arm's length, like an offering. "Your name restored. Your rank restored. A commission signed by the Provisional Authority—countersigned by patrons whose names would make your exiles weep. A house in the capital. A pension. And amnesty for everyone standing behind you, including the girl with the sticky fingers and the boy holding that hatchet like it's a personal insult."

"He does hold it like that," Wren murmured. "I keep telling him."

"Wren," Tomas hissed.

"Silence, please," said Ashe, without heat, the way a man quiets dogs. His eyes came back to Silas and stayed there, and this was the performance's true center, Silas realized—the folio was scenery; this was the scene. "Everything you lost, returned. Everything you ran from, forgiven. All I ask—" and here he spread his hands, reasonable, wounded, magnanimous, "—is the chest. Give me the chest, Vosk, and go home. *Home*, Silas. Not a boat. Not a sandbar. Home."

The word did its work. Silas felt it do its work, and hated that. Home. He'd spent twenty years building a life out of the absence of the word, and here was a man offering to hand it back pre-assembled, like furniture, like a coffin.

He looked at the folio. Then, deliberately, he looked at his crew.

Ilsa stood with her arms crossed and her chin up, and her eyes were dry, and he understood suddenly that she expected him to take it. Had perhaps always expected it. A Vhaleni exile learned early that other people's promises were weather.

Tomas wasn't looking at the folio at all. He was watching Silas's face with an expression the boy probably thought was neutral and wasn't—a boy on a dock, weighing whether the water was cold.

And Wren. Wren had gone very still, in the way she went still before doing something catastrophic or brilliant, and she caught him looking and raised one eyebrow, and mouthed, with perfect clarity: *your call, Captain.*

His call. When had that happened? When had these three decided his judgment was worth anything more than the wages he paid?

Silas turned back to Ashe.

"You know what's funny," he said conversationally, walking forward a few steps into the open saddle, close enough to speak without shouting, far enough that the riflemen above would have to think twice. "For twenty years I thought the thing I was running from was you. Or the war. Or the name. I built a whole new man out of not-thinking-about-it—new name, new trade, new towns every season. Paid my debts and left before anyone could learn my middle initial. My deckhand thought I was allergic to permanence." Behind him Wren said, "I *did* think that," and he pressed on. "And then a chest floated up out of the Alder River with your seal on it, and I learned what I was actually running from."

"Do tell," said Ashe.

"It wasn't you. You were never worth running from. It was the arithmetic." Silas held his old commander's gaze. "The arithmetic you taught me. That a village is a number. That an order is cleaner than a question. I deserted before Kestrel Hollow, Ashe—you know that, your own files say it—but I'd already done the math your way for six years, and the massacre didn't shock me, it just confirmed the sum. That's what I couldn't live with. Not the crime. The arithmetic."

"And the chest changes the sum?" Ashe's voice cooled by degrees. "Paper, Vosk. Old paper. Land grants for a country that exists only in the memories of sentimental refugees."

"It changes who owns the number," Silas said. "That's all it has to do."

He heard Ilsa shift behind him. Heard Tomas breathe.

"So," Silas said. "Here's my answer, and I'll say it once, and I'll say it plainly, because you always hated plain speech—you thought it was a peasant habit." He took a breath, and found, again, that his hands were steady, and this time he knew why. "My name isn't Vosk. It hasn't been for twenty years. I didn't pick 'Voss' out of a hat—it's not clever, it's barely different, a riverman's name, a working name. But I earned it. Every mile of shallow water, every debt paid in full, every night I stayed put when staying put scared me. That name is the only thing I own outright, and I am not selling it back to you at any price, and I am certainly not paying with a nation's last will and testament." He paused. "My name is Voss now. Say it however you like. Write it on whatever posters you want. But I'm not your lieutenant, Commander. I'm the captain of a forty-foot freight boat with a cracked boiler and the finest crew on the Alder River, and we are crossing this pass, and you are going to watch us do it."

The silence lasted four seconds. Silas counted them, the way you count seconds between lightning and thunder.

Then Ashe laughed. It was a genuine laugh, which was somehow worse than anything else he might have done. "Oh, Vosk. *Voss.* You always did mistake stubbornness for principle." He nodded once, and the nod was the most frightening thing yet, because it wasn't angry. It was administrative. "I offered you the civilized option. I want that noted. Whatever happens next, I offered."

"You'll want a lot of things noted," Silas said. "History's short-handed."

"Take them," Ashe said—to his men, not to Silas, already turning away, already done with the theater because the theater had failed and the second act required no audience. "The chest. Alive if convenient."

The riflemen on the flanks started moving downhill, unhurried, professional. Below, the four by the drift fence unslung their rifles.

"Well," said Wren, drawing both pistols in one motion, sounding genuinely delighted. "I *did* pack extra powder."

"Tomas!" Silas was already moving, shoving the boy toward the boulder field on the south side of the saddle. "High ground, now! Ilsa—stay behind me and *stay low!*"

Ashe's voice followed them up the slope, calm as a tide table: "Lieutenant. Last chance."

"My name," Silas called back over the crack of the first rifle shot, "is *Voss*—"

And then the ridge erupted, and there was no more time for names at all.
