## 18. Ridge Fight

Tomas had learned to read Silas Voss the way he'd once read weather over the Alder: by small signs, by what a man's hands did when his mouth said nothing else.

So when they came up out of the couloir that morning and Silas stopped at the lip of the ridge saddle, went very still, and then—deliberately, almost casually—shifted Ilsa behind him with one arm, Tomas knew before he saw them.

Agents. Four of them, fanned across the saddle like crows on a fence rail. And behind them, wrapped in a grey officer's cloak that the wind kept trying to steal, stood Commander Ashe himself.

Tomas's heart did something strange. Not fear exactly—or not only fear. It was more like the moment on the dock in Corren's Ford, except reversed. There, he had looked at a poster and seen the enemy wearing his protector's face. Here, he looked at the man in the grey cloak and understood, with a clarity that felt almost holy, which of them was actually the enemy. It had always been this one. The posters had just gotten the faces backwards.

"You're late," Ashe called, and his voice carried strangely in the thin air, flat and clear as a struck bell. "The council convenes in two days. You could have saved us all this climb."

"We took the scenic route," Wren said. She was breathing hard from the couloir, her split lip crusted dark, but her grin was intact. "You should try it sometime. Very restful. Lots of falling rocks."

Ashe ignored her. His eyes found Silas and stayed there, and something passed between the two men that Tomas couldn't read—a whole conversation conducted in silence, years long, conducted in a language Tomas didn't speak but recognized anyway. The language of people who had once belonged to each other and now belonged to nothing shared at all.

"Vosk." Ashe said the name gently, the way you'd say it to a drunk friend you were trying to talk off a bridge. "Look at yourself. Look at where you are. Half-frozen, hunted, carrying a dead nation's paperwork for strangers who will forget your name the moment the grants are signed." He spread his hands. "Come back. Rank, pardon, a name that isn't borrowed. Your commission is still valid. I checked."

Tomas watched Silas's face do something complicated. Grief and disgust and—this was the part that frightened him—temptation, real temptation, flickering through like lightning under cloud.

And then Silas laughed. It wasn't a big laugh. It was tired and a little broken, and it came out white in the cold air, but it was a laugh.

"You checked," Silas said. "You climbed a mountain to check whether my commission was still valid."

"I climbed a mountain because you have something of mine."

"No." Silas shook his head slowly. "You climbed a mountain because I have something of *yours*. Proof. That's the difference between us, sir. I've spent eleven years trying to give my past away. You've spent them trying to buy yours back."

Something changed in Ashe's face then. The gentleness went out of it the way warmth goes out of a room when a door opens onto winter, and Tomas saw, for the first time, the man underneath the commander—the man who had signed orders in ledgers with a steady hand while villages burned.

"Last chance," Ashe said quietly.

"My name is Voss now," Silas said. "I built it myself. It fits better."

* * *

It started, as these things always seemed to, with Wren.

Later—and Tomas would think about this later, lying awake in the council tents, turning it over like a stone with a surprise inside—later he would realize that Wren had been edging left along the ridgeline for a full minute before anyone noticed, talking the whole time, loud and funny and completely irrelevant, a running commentary about the weather and Ashe's cloak and the general indignity of being ambushed before breakfast. She was buying space. She was putting herself between the agents and Ilsa without anyone asking her to.

The first agent moved, and everything happened at once.

Tomas had never been in a real fight. He'd thrown punches behind the fish market in Vhalen's last free port, scrappy boy things, over insults and stolen bread. This was different. This was four trained men on a knife-edge of rock with a gorge on either side and snow beginning to fall—not drifting down so much as arriving sideways, fast and dry and hissing against the stone.

He grabbed Ilsa's arm and pulled her down behind a spur of granite as the first crossbow bolt cracked off the rock where her head had been.

"Ilsa—" he started.

"I know." Her voice was shaking but her hands weren't; she was already pulling the flare-pistol from her coat, the one they'd bought in Corren's Ford from a man who swore it worked. "Go help them. Go."

"But—"

"*Go*, Tomas. I'm not a child."

She wasn't. That was the terrible thing he was learning about his sister on this mountain: she hadn't been a child for years, and he'd been too busy being the younger brother to notice. He squeezed her shoulder once and scrambled along the spur toward the fight.

What he saw when he got there rearranged something in him permanently.

Silas fought like water. That was the only way Tomas could describe it. He didn't seem to attack so much as redirect—an agent lunged and found himself stumbling into empty air, off-balance, and then Silas's boot caught his knee and the man went down hard on the rock. Another came from behind and Silas turned inside the swing, close and economical, no wasted motion, no anger in it at all. Eleven years of river work, Tomas realized. Eleven years of poling barges through sandbars and reading currents. All of it had been practice for this without Silas ever knowing it.

But there were four of them, and one of them was very good.

That one circled wide, patient, letting the others spend Silas's attention, and when Silas drove the second agent back with a flurry that would have ended any normal fight, the patient one simply stepped in and hit him.

Tomas heard the impact from ten yards away. Silas folded around it, staggered—and the ridge tilted away beneath his heel, scree sliding, and suddenly he was down on one knee at the very edge, the gorge yawning below him, all that grey depth full of falling snow.

The patient agent raised his blade.

Everything after that, Tomas remembered in pieces.

He remembered moving. He didn't decide to move; his body decided, some old animal part of him that had watched Silas save Wren from the rockslide and pull him out of the river current outside Corren's Ford and carry him, feverish, for two days through the foothills when the bad water got him. Debts, Silas had said once, were the only religion Tomas had ever seen him practice. Well. Tomas had been keeping count.

He remembered the sound of his own voice, screaming something wordless and enormous.

He remembered the flare-pistol—he'd taken it from Ilsa's pack that morning without telling her, because he'd woken before dawn with a feeling in his chest like a held breath, and he'd wanted, needed, to be carrying something. He remembered firing it from six feet away directly into the patient agent's chest, and the world going red and roaring, and the agent shrieking and staggering backward with his sleeve alight, slapping at himself, blundering away along the ridge and down into the rocks beyond.

He remembered reaching Silas at the edge and hauling on his collar with both hands, his boots skidding on scree, his arms burning, whispering—actually whispering, absurdly, in the middle of everything—"Not like this, not like this, *not like this*"—until Silas's weight came back up the slope and into him and they both went down together on solid stone, gasping.

Silas stared up at him. Snow was settling in his hair and on his eyelashes and he looked, for a moment, astonishingly old and astonishingly young at the same time.

"Tomas," he said. "You—"

"Shut up," Tomas said, and was horrified to find he was crying, hot tears freezing on his cheeks. "Just—shut up and get up."

* * *

Across the saddle, the fight was ending badly for everyone.

Wren had disarmed one agent with a boat hook—she'd carried it the entire expedition, over every objection, on the grounds that "a person who doesn't bring a boat hook up a mountain deserves what happens to them"—and now stood over him breathing hard while he clutched his wrist and moaned. The second agent had fled. The first lay groaning where Silas had dropped him.

Which left Ashe.

The commander had not joined the fight. He had watched it, Tomas realized, the way a man watches a business transaction go wrong—with cold assessment, already calculating losses. And now, seeing his agents broken and scattered, seeing Silas rise to his feet with a sixteen-year-old boy planted at his side like a second keel, Ashe did the thing that finally made Tomas understand him completely.

He reached for the chest.

Ilsa had it strapped to her back, and she twisted away from his grab, but she was tired and the strap was iced and his fingers caught the buckle. For one horrible moment the two of them were locked together at the saddle's center, Ilsa snarling, wrenching, Ashe pulling with the flat strength of a man who had never once doubted his right to take what he wanted—

And then Wren hit him with the boat hook.

Not hard. Just enough. A tap, really, against the backs of his knees, delivered with a deckhand's perfect timing and a deckhand's complete lack of ceremony. Ashe's legs went out from under him. He sat down hard on the ridge, and the world—which had been tilting toward violence all morning—seemed to hold its breath.

For a moment nobody moved. Snow fell into the silence, fat flakes now, the dry season's last winds finally giving way.

Ashe looked around at them. At Silas standing straight despite the blow, blood at the corner of his mouth. At Wren with her ridiculous boat hook. At Ilsa clutching the chest, and at Tomas, still trembling, still crying, standing where a lieutenant should stand.

"You're nothing," Ashe said to Silas, and there was something raw underneath the contempt now, something almost bewildered. "You were nothing when I found you. I made you. Everything you are, I—"

"That's true," Silas said quietly.

Tomas blinked.

"Everything I was, you made," Silas said. "And I spent eleven years rowing away from it. But this—" he touched his own chest, briefly, clumsily, like a man unsure of the gesture, "—this crew, this name, this stubborn girl and this boy who just pulled me out of a gorge—none of that is yours. None of it was ever for sale. You can't pardon me into it and you can't pay me into it. It's the only thing I've ever owned outright."

Ashe got to his feet. Slowly. He was looking at Silas, and whatever he saw there seemed to settle something in him, the way a verdict settles a case. He stepped back—one step, two—and his heel came down on the ridge's spine, on the knife-edge itself, on ice that the first true snow had glazed in the last few minutes.

Tomas saw it happen. Saw the exact moment the commander's certainty met the mountain's indifference.

Ashe's arms windmilled. In the last instant his hand shot out—not toward Silas, not toward the chest, not toward anything that could have saved him—but toward his own breast pocket, where his commission papers sat in their oilskin wrap, the rank and the name and the signature that had ordered a nation burned. He clutched them as he went over, clutching them the way a drowning man clutches driftwood, and the papers fluttered free even as he fell, a scatter of pale rectangles tumbling into the gorge after him, turning over and over in the new snow like a flock of small white birds.

Then the gorge had him, and the snow closed over the place where he'd been, and the wind filled the silence with its long grey voice.

Nobody spoke for a long time.

Wren sat down abruptly on a rock, boat hook across her knees, and started laughing—a shaky, half-hysterical sound that was mostly relief. Ilsa slid down beside the chest and put her forehead against its lid and stayed there. The wounded agents, sensing the arithmetic of the moment had changed, began the slow, humiliating business of surrendering to a teenage boy and a woman with a boat hook.

Silas walked to the edge. Tomas watched him look down into all that depth, and braced himself for—what? Satisfaction? Horror?

What he saw instead was Silas take the scattered commission pages that had landed short of the drop, gather them up one by one from the snow, consider them for a moment—and then let them go, one at a time, into the wind. He watched them until the last white page vanished into the white air.

"He was holding them when he fell," Silas said, not turning around. "In the end, that's all he had. I keep thinking I should feel something bigger than this."

"What do you feel?" Tomas asked.

Silas considered. Snow gathered on his shoulders and he didn't brush it off.

"Cold," he said. "And like we should keep moving. The pass closes tonight if this keeps up."

* * *

They buried the dead agent under a cairn, because Silas insisted, because "we don't leave people for the crows, even his people." They bound the surrendered ones' wrists with boat-hook line and left them with food and a marked map showing the way down to Corren's Ford, because Ilsa insisted, because "we are not them, and today proved it better than any document in this chest."

And then they climbed.

The snow thickened as they rose, the dry season dying above them flake by flake, and the pass showed itself at last as a pale notch in the Escarpment's shoulder, maybe three hours distant, maybe less if the wind was kind. Nobody had the breath to talk. But somewhere in the second hour, without discussion or decision, Tomas found himself walking beside Silas, and Silas put a hand briefly on the back of his neck—rough, warm, brief as a struck match—and neither of them said anything, because everything worth saying had already been said at the edge of a gorge, in the wordless grammar of a rope of an arm and a boy who refused to let go.

Tomas thought about debts. About how Silas paid his in towns he then abandoned, and how Tomas had once believed that was cowardice, and how he knew better now. Some men run from what they owe. Some men run toward it. And some men, given a mountain and a storm and a family that chose them anyway, finally stop running altogether.

The notch in the ridge grew closer. Below it, faint through the snowfall, Tomas could see lights—orange points in the grey, campfires, tents, banners he didn't recognize but Ilsa clearly did, because she made a sound he'd never heard from her before, small and cracked and hopeful, and pressed both hands to the chest on her back.

"The council," she whispered. "We made it. We actually—"

The wind gusted, the snow whirled, and through it, from the fires below, figures were already coming up toward them through the drifts—dozens of them, hooded and lantern-lit, come out into the first snow of winter to meet whatever the mountain sent down.

One of the lanterns lifted higher, and a voice called up through the wind—a voice that made Ilsa stumble and grab Tomas's arm, a voice from another country, from a country that no longer existed:

"Who comes to the pass?"

Silas glanced at Ilsa. Ilsa glanced at the chest. And Tomas, sixteen years old, frozen, exhausted, happier than he had ever been in his life, watched his sister draw herself up to her full height in the falling snow and open her mouth to answer for all of them.
