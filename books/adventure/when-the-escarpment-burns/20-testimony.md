## 20. Testimony

The council hall had been a granary once, back when Vhalen still existed to fill it. Silas could tell by the smell—old grain sweetening the timber, decades of it soaked into the beams like a memory the wood refused to give up. Now the great bins were gone and benches stood in their place, rows of them, filled with people who had crossed a mountain to hear him speak.

He counted them without meaning to. A riverman's habit: count your cargo, know your load. Two hundred, maybe more. Old men with medals they'd sewn onto coats that no longer meant anything. Women with the particular straightness of spine that came from carrying grief upright for eleven years. Children too young to remember Vhalen at all, swinging their feet beneath benches too tall for them, waiting for a story about a country that was, to them, a bedtime tale.

Silas stood at the front of the hall in a borrowed coat that didn't fit through the shoulders, and he understood suddenly why condemned men asked for a blindfold.

* * *

They had given him the night to prepare. He hadn't slept.

Instead he'd sat in the room they'd lent him—a narrow cell of a thing, formerly a storage closet by the smell of tallow and turnips—and written on the back of Ilsa's map, the one that had started all this, until there was no blank paper left. Names. Dates. Orders. The ledger of a massacre, kept in the only place it had never been recorded: his own skull.

Wren had found him there past midnight, sitting in the dark because he'd run out of candle and couldn't be bothered to find more.

"You look terrible," she'd said from the doorway.

"I've been told I clean up well."

"By who? Liars?" She'd come in without invitation, the way she did everything, and dropped onto the floor across from him with her back against the wall. For a while neither of them spoke. Somewhere above them, the mountain wind worked at the eaves like a patient carpenter.

"When my da died," Wren said eventually, "I was twelve. And everybody in the boarding house kept telling me how sorry they were, and how he'd gone to a better place, and all of it—kind words, every one. And none of them helped. You know what helped?"

"What?"

"The one woman who said, 'Your father drank, and he owed me money, and he once threw a boot at my cat.' That's it. That's what helped. Because it was true, and true things you can hold." She'd looked at him then, in the dark, with those flat river-gray eyes that had seen him salvage a chest and lie about it and sell her out for nothing and come back anyway. "Tomorrow, don't give them kind words, Silas. Give them the boot."

* * *

The council convened at midmorning. It was not a government—the exiles were quick to say so, quick to insist that Vhalen's assemblies had been elected and these were merely elders, keepers of records, custodians of a nation reduced to paperwork—but it had the gravity of one. Seven elders sat at a long table draped in cloth the color of undyed wool. At its center sat an ancient woman named Maren Ostrevik, whom Ilsa had described on the climb as "the last person alive who held office before the burning," and who looked, to Silas, like a sparrow that had survived four winters too many out of pure spite.

Ilsa stood beside him as witness. Tomas sat in the second row, front and center, arms folded, wearing the expression of a boy determined not to be impressed by anything ever again. Wren leaned against the far wall with the studied boredom of someone who had personally fought three men on a frozen ridge two days ago and found the whole business tedious.

Maren Ostrevik opened the proceedings in Vhaleni. The language rolled through the hall like water over stones, and Silas understood perhaps one word in ten—he'd heard enough of it during the war to know the cadence of prayers and the cadence of orders, and precious little else. Then she turned to him and repeated herself in trade-tongue, her accent precise and cold as a ledger line.

"Silas Vosk. You stand accused by no one and accused by everything. The council has read the documents you carried over the Escarpment. We have wept over them in private, which is not your concern. Today we ask you to speak. Not as a penitent—we have no rite for that, and no power to absolve. Speak as a witness. Tell us what you know. What you did. What you failed to do. Begin wherever the truth begins."

Silas looked down at his notes and found, as he'd suspected he would, that he didn't need them. The truth began in a place he'd spent eleven years not looking at, and it had been waiting patiently, the way drowned things wait.

"My name is Silas Vosk," he said. "I was born in Kettering, in the border provinces. I was twenty-two years old when I took a commission in the Ninth Column, under Commander Edran Ashe. I want to say at the outset that I volunteered. Nobody conscripted me. I believed in the cause—or I believed I believed in it, which at twenty-two is the same thing."

A murmur moved through the benches. He let it pass.

"The Ninth Column was a punishment formation. We were sent where the regular army wouldn't dirty itself. Ashe built us out of men like me—border folk, mixed blood, men with something to prove and nothing to lose. He was very good at finding us. That was his real talent, if you want to know. Not tactics. Not strategy. He could look at a broken man and see exactly which piece of him was missing, and offer to replace it with a rank."

He told them about the campaign up the Vessa valley. About the villages that emptied ahead of them like water fleeing a hot iron. About the orders that changed, slowly, in language so incremental that a man could follow each step and deny he'd walked anywhere at all. First: secure the population. Then: relocate the population. Then: the population is a resource of the enemy. Then, on a gray morning in late autumn, with frost on the ground and smoke standing perfectly still over the hills:

"Then the order was a number," Silas said. "Just a number. Eleven thousand. That's what the valley held. Ashe wrote it down like freight tonnage. I know because I countersigned the supply requisitions that supported it. Extra ammunition. Fuel. Shovels." His voice caught on *shovels*, and he hated himself for the catch, and made himself continue. "I knew what the shovels were for. Every man in the column knew. And on the morning of the eleventh of Frostmonth, when the order came down to move on the town of Vel Azhar, I—"

He stopped. The hall was absolutely silent. Even the children had stopped swinging their feet.

"I refused," he said. "I stood in front of my own company and refused a lawful order, and I have spent eleven years telling myself that this makes me different from the men who didn't. It doesn't. Refusing one order is not the same as having served three years of them. I burned villages before Vel Azhar. Smaller ones. Ones nobody counts, because nobody was left to do the counting. I was a good officer. I was promoted twice. The fact that I broke at the end instead of the beginning is not virtue. It is arithmetic—I simply ran out of whatever it is that lets a man keep walking forward."

An old man in the third row stood up. He wore a faded uniform jacket with the collar torn off, and his hands shook, and his voice did not.

"My family was in the Vessa valley," he said. "My wife. My daughters. Which column?"

Silas met his eyes. "The Ninth."

"Then you burned Serevin."

It wasn't a question, but Silas answered it. "Yes. I did. Third month of the fourth year. I led the detachment myself."

The old man looked at him for a long moment. Then he sat down—not gently, but deliberately, the way a man puts down something heavy—and turned his face toward the wall, and did not look at Silas again for the rest of the day.

Silas went on.

* * *

He gave them names. That was the hardest part and the part he'd sworn to do completely, so he did it: Lieutenant Harl Brekke, who had ordered the fuel detail at Serevin and later hanged himself in a garrison outside Marost. Captain Ondine Vael, who had signed the transport manifests and who, as far as Silas knew, was alive and prosperous and running a shipping concern in Corve under a married name. Sergeant-Major Doss, who had fired the first shot at Vel Azhar against no order at all, just eagerness, and who had died of fever two winters later, mourned by his men. He gave them dates, grid references, supply figures. He described the mass graves' locations as precisely as memory allowed, and watched clerks at the side tables write it all down with hands that trembled, and understood that for some people in that hall, his testimony was a map to their dead.

Ashe he saved for last.

"Commander Edran Ashe is in these mountains right now," Silas said. "Or he was, four days ago, before the ridge. Some of you saw him fall. I'll say plainly: I believe he's dead. He fell into the gorge at the Kettle Saddle clutching his commission papers, and I take no satisfaction in describing it, because the man I want you to understand is not the man who fell. The man you should understand is the one who wrote the orders in that chest. He was not a monster. Monsters are simple, and Ashe was never simple. He was a patriot who decided his country's survival required the erasure of another one, and he was intelligent enough to build a machine that let ten thousand ordinary men participate in that erasure while each of them told himself he was only following orders, only doing his job, only keeping his head down." He paused. "I was one of those ten thousand. I am not asking you to forgive the machine. I am asking you to record it accurately, so that the next one cannot pretend it has never been built before."

When he finished, the silence lasted long enough that he could hear the wind at the eaves again, working away, patient as ever.

Maren Ostrevik rose. "The council will deliberate," she said. "You will wait."

"How long?" Silas asked.

"As long as it takes. Truth is heavy. We carry it slowly."

* * *

They put him in the storage closet again, and the hours crawled. Through the wall he could hear the council's voices, rising and falling, sometimes sharp, sometimes low, always in Vhaleni—a language being used, he realized, exactly as it was meant to be used: to decide things together, in the old way, in a granary that smelled like bread that would never be baked.

Near dusk, footsteps stopped outside his door. He expected a guard. Instead the door opened on Tomas, holding two bowls of stew and the expression of a boy conducting a diplomatic mission.

"Ilsa says you haven't eaten," Tomas said, handing over a bowl. "I said that was your problem. She said I had to bring it anyway. So." He shrugged, elaborately casual, and then ruined it by sitting down on the floor across from Silas, exactly where Wren had sat the night before, as if the spot had been designated for difficult conversations.

For a while, the boy ate. Then, staring into his bowl: "The old man. The one whose family was at Serevin. He's my mother's uncle."

Silas set down his spoon. "Tomas—"

"I'm not saying anything. Ilsa told me not to say anything. I'm just eating my stew." He took another aggressive bite. "But I saw the posters in Corren's Ford before anybody else did, and I didn't say anything then either, and I've been thinking about why. And I think—" He stopped, jaw working. "I think it's because I already knew. Back on the boat, when you went white at the seal. I knew you recognized it. And I got on the trail with you anyway, because you carried me over a scree field when I twisted my ankle, and you taught me to read water, and you never once lied to me about small things. So when the big lie came, I figured—it must be different. Somehow. I figured there was a version of it I could live with." He looked up. "Was there?"

"No," Silas said quietly. "There wasn't a version you could live with. There was just the truth, and it's bad, and I'm sorry."

Tomas nodded slowly, as if a sum had finally come out even. "Okay," he said. "That's the first honest answer anybody's given me since Corren's Ford." He stood, collected the empty bowl, and paused at the door. "Uncle Davorin turned his chair around. He's facing the wall. But he stayed in the hall. Ilsa says that means something. She says the ones who hate you stayed, and the ones who can't stand to hear your voice are the ones who left."

After he was gone, Silas sat in the dark and tried to work out whether that was comfort or condemnation, and decided it was probably both, and that this was what accountability actually tasted like—not grace, not yet, maybe not ever, but the plain bread of being seen whole.

* * *

They summoned him at full dark.

The council chamber was emptier now. Perhaps forty remained of the two hundred; the rest had departed through the evening, some in silence, some not—one woman had spat at his feet in the corridor, and a young man had shouted a name, *Serevin*, like a curse, and been led away by his friends. But the benches that remained were full, and the faces in them were the faces of people who had chosen, deliberately, to stay and finish listening.

Maren Ostrevik addressed him one final time.

"Silas Vosk. The council has deliberated. Hear our judgment." She drew breath, and the old sparrow eyes fixed on him without mercy and without malice. "We have no prison for you. No court has jurisdiction here, and no gallows would bring back a single soul from the Vessa valley. We have considered sending you away, and considered worse, and found that both would cost us more than they cost you. So instead we render this: you will carry what you have confessed. All of it. Every year, on the eleventh of Frostmonth, you will speak the names you spoke today, aloud, somewhere that others can hear. You will do this until you die. That is your sentence. Memory, without end, in company."

Silas bowed his head. "I accept it."

"We are not finished." She lifted a single sheet of paper. "The council also records this: the officer Silas Vosk refused the order at Vel Azhar. The document recovered from the chest confirms it—Commander Ashe's own hand, naming you a traitor to the column, ordering your execution. You were branded a traitor by the man who destroyed us. This council does not confuse the brands of monsters with the brands of men. It is entered in the record beside your crimes, and there it will remain, forever, next to them. Both are true. That is the Vhaleni way. We do not round off."

She sat. The hall waited. And Silas stood there in his ill-fitting coat, sentenced to remember and recorded whole, feeling something loosen in his chest that he did not entirely trust—because it felt dangerously like peace, and peace was a thing he had never been permitted to keep.

Ilsa came to him through the quiet benches. She stopped an arm's length away, close enough that he could see she'd been crying earlier and had decided no one needed to know.

"My grandmother's street was in Vel Azhar," she said. "Number fourteen. I've known that since I was six years old. It's the only address our family has left." Her chin came up. "You refused there. At the end. I need you to understand that I heard everything today, and I am not forgiving you, because forgiveness isn't mine to give—it belongs to the dead, and they're not talking. But I heard everything, and I'm still standing here. Make of that what you can."

"Ilsa—"

"Don't." But her mouth twitched, almost despite itself. "Wren's waiting outside. She says there's a matter of a freight route to discuss, and she says—" Ilsa glanced toward the door, and her voice softened in spite of every vow she'd clearly made to herself, "—she says the family that runs it should probably vote on the name. Tomas wants to call it something ridiculous. Come and stop him."

Silas followed her out into the cold, past the old man Davorin, who still faced the wall—and who, as Silas passed, said quietly, without turning, "Frostmonth. Don't forget the date."

"I won't," Silas said. "Eleventh. Every year."

Davorin grunted. It was not forgiveness. It might, someday, become the ground forgiveness could be built on.

Outside, the first snow of the season was coming down soft and thick over the pass, and Wren was arguing loudly with Tomas about the name of a boat that did not yet exist, and Silas Voss—sentenced, recorded, remembered, and, somehow, still chosen—walked toward them through the falling white.
