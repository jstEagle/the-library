## 21. First Snow

Wren had never trusted quiet. Quiet on a boat meant sandbar ahead. Quiet in a town meant the constable was coming. Quiet in her own head meant she was about to do something stupid, which, historically, was most of the time.

So when the council chamber went silent after Silas finished testifying—when forty-odd Vhaleni exiles sat in that cold stone hall above the pass and said absolutely nothing—Wren's hands started itching for something to hold.

She settled for holding her breath.

Silas stood at the front of the room like a man at his own trial, which, she supposed, he technically wasn't. He'd given them everything. Names. Dates. The order of march on the day of the burning. The number of boats on the river, the color of the smoke, the way Commander Ashe had stood on the far bank with his hands clasped behind his back like a man watching fireworks. Wren had heard it all twice now—once in the cave, once here—and it still made her stomach turn over like bilge water.

An old woman rose from the council bench. She wore a shawl the color of rust and a face like a river stone, worn smooth by things Wren couldn't imagine. She looked at Silas for a long moment.

"You were there," she said. Not a question.

"I was there," Silas said.

"And you did not fire."

"I did not fire."

The old woman nodded slowly, as if he'd confirmed the price of flour. "Then you will carry it," she said. "Not as a sentence. We have no jail up here, and no rope, and no use for either. You will carry what you saw, every day, in your own name—the new one, the one you earned. That is the whole of our judgment."

"That's—" Silas stopped. Cleared his throat. "That's acceptable."

"It is not negotiable," the old woman said, and then, to everyone's visible surprise, including apparently her own, the corner of her mouth twitched upward. "It is also not so terrible. Memory is heavy, but it is honest cargo. I am told you know how to handle cargo."

A few people laughed. It was a small sound, careful, like ice cracking on a pond in early morning—but it spread. And just like that the silence broke, and the hall filled with the murmur of a people who had spent nineteen years waiting for this chest and were only now realizing they didn't quite know what to do with their hands.

Wren let out her breath and elbowed Tomas, who was sitting beside her looking like a boy who'd aged ten years on the ridge and hadn't decided yet whether that was a good thing.

"Your sister's crying," Wren whispered.

"I'm not," said Ilsa, who was, furiously and silently, wiping her eyes with the heel of her hand while pretending to read a land grant.

* * *

They came out of the council hall into light so bright it hurt.

Snow. Real snow, thick and unhurried, falling straight down through windless air—the first snow of the season, arriving exactly when the dry season's last winds gave out, as if the mountain itself kept a ledger and had finally balanced the books. The pass below the hall was already white. The pines wore it. The roofs of the exile settlement—two dozen low stone houses clustered against the mountainside like sheep against weather—wore it.

Tomas caught a flake on his tongue. Then another. Then he abandoned dignity entirely, tipped his head back, and opened his mouth like a fish.

"You'll catch a fever," Ilsa said automatically.

"He'll catch snowflakes," Wren said. "Different thing entirely."

Silas came out last. He stood in the doorway a moment, blinking at the white, and Wren watched him do the thing he always did—check the sky, check the water, check the exits. Except there were no exits up here. Just the pass, closed behind them until spring, and the valley beyond, opening wide and white and new.

"No sentence but memory," Wren said, coming to stand beside him. "You got off easy."

"I got off exactly right." He said it quietly, without drama, and she believed him. That was the strange thing about Silas Voss—once he told the truth, he kept telling it, like a man who'd found the current and didn't dare fight it again. "Nineteen years I thought if anyone ever knew, they'd hang me. Turns out they just want me to remember."

"Cheaper than rope," Wren agreed. "Better for business, too. Hard to run freight with a dead captain."

He glanced sideways at her. "Freight."

"Mm." She rocked on her heels, watching her breath fog. "I've been thinking."

"That's dangerous this close to winter."

"Shut up and listen." She turned to face him, and the speech she'd been composing since the ridge—all the way down through the pass, through the council, through the long night of rationed tea and Tomas snoring—came out of her faster than she'd planned, the way everything important did. "The grants open a trade route. You heard them say it. Corren's Ford to the valley settlements, goods both ways—grain going up, wool and iron coming down, and nobody's running it because everybody thinks the Escarpment can't be crossed regular-like. But we crossed it. In November. Half-starved, hunted, with one sled between four people and a boy who navigates by stubbornness."

"We nearly died six times," Silas said.

"Seven, counting the goat incident."

"The goat incident doesn't count."

"It counts. My point is—we know the route. Every switchback, every couloir, every place the rock likes to come down. Nobody else alive knows it like we do. Boat in summer, sled in winter. The Alder when she's high, the ridge when she's not." She spread her hands. "Voss Freight & Passage. Or Reyes-Voss. Or whatever name makes you stop making that face."

Silas was making a face. It was the face he made when someone offered him a future, which, in Wren's experience, was roughly the shape of a man biting into an apple and finding it was an onion.

"Boats are my life," he said slowly. "Sleds are dogs and misery."

"Dogs are excellent."

"Sleds are dogs and misery and frozen rivers and—"

"—and a route nobody else can run, and work enough to keep four people fed for the rest of their natural lives, and a reason to stay in one place that isn't a grave." She softened, just slightly, because under all the bluster she could see him actually listening, actually turning it over, the way he turned a boat in a narrow channel—careful, deliberate, reading the water before committing. "You don't have to run from anything anymore, Captain. So what are you going to run toward?"

For a long moment he didn't answer. Snow gathered on his shoulders and he didn't brush it off. Down in the settlement, somebody was singing—a low Vhaleni melody, half-familiar to Wren now after weeks of hearing it around fires—and children were shrieking happily at the snow, and Tomas was attempting to build something that would eventually be called a snowman and would honestly be more of a snow cairn.

"Reyes-Voss," Silas said at last. "Boat in summer. Sled in winter." He exhaled, long and slow, like a man setting down something he'd carried so long he'd forgotten its weight. "God help us all."

"That's the spirit."

* * *

Ilsa cried again when they told her, which Wren pretended not to notice, and then immediately began drafting schedules on the back of a land grant copy, which Wren also pretended not to notice, mostly because the schedules were good.

"A depot at Corren's Ford," Ilsa said, pencil scratching. "A warehouse at the valley landing. Contracts with the council—they'll want reliable freight, they'll sign tomorrow. Wages. Actual wages. Wren, do you understand we're talking about wages?"

"I've heard rumors."

"Tomas can learn the accounts—"

"I can hear you," Tomas said, from inside the snow cairn, where he was apparently constructing it from within, like a hermit crab.

"—and Silas can train pilots for the lower river, because he knows every sandbar on the Alder better than the river does, and—" Ilsa stopped. Looked up. Her eyes were red-rimmed and fierce and shining. "Do you know what this means? For Vhalen? Not the nation. The nation's gone; I've made my peace with that, or I'm lying my way toward it. But this means there will be a road. A real one. People will come up this route, and some of them will be ours, and some won't, and in twenty years nobody will think it strange that Vhaleni names are on the warehouses. That's how countries come back, Wren. Not with armies. With freight."

"With freight," Wren repeated solemnly. "Say it louder for the council."

Ilsa threw her pencil at her.

Later—much later, after the evening meal in the council hall, after the old woman in the rust shawl had formally signed the freight contracts with a flourish that suggested she'd been waiting nineteen years to sign anything at all—Wren stepped outside alone.

The snow had stopped. The sky had cleared, and the stars up here were ferocious, close, uncountable. The settlement glowed with lamplight through frost-feathered windows. Somewhere a dog barked once and thought better of it.

She found Silas at the edge of the settlement, standing where the ground fell away toward the pass, looking north—the way they'd come. His breath steamed. His hands were in his pockets, and he wasn't checking the exits. She noticed that. For the first time since she'd known him, he wasn't checking the exits.

"My father ran freight," she said, coming up beside him. "Before the war took the river trade apart. Grain barges, mostly. He used to say a loaded boat is the most honest thing in the world—it goes where it says it'll go, carries what it says it'll carry, and everyone eats. Everything else is politics."

"Your father sounds like a wise man."

"He drowned." She said it lightly, the way you say things you decided long ago not to bleed on. "Sandbar shift in flood season. So maybe wise and lucky aren't the same thing."

"No," Silas said quietly. "They're not."

They stood together in the cold. Below them, the pass wound down into darkness, and somewhere down there was the couloir, and the cave, and the knife-edge ridge where Tomas had pulled a grown man back from the gorge by nothing but fury and a rope's end. Somewhere down there, too, was the spot where Ashe had fallen, though the snow had already taken care of that, smoothing it over, keeping what it kept.

"Wren." Silas's voice was odd. Careful. "What you read. In the cave. What the last document said."

"Don't."

"I need to say it once."

She sighed and watched her breath drift. "Fine. Once."

"You sold me out for money, and then you found out I was innocent, and you came back anyway, into gunfire, to give it all back." He turned to look at her, and his face in the starlight was older than she'd ever seen it and somehow lighter, too. "Most people would've kept running. You didn't. Whatever debt you think you owe me—you paid it on that ridge."

"That's not—" She stopped. Swallowed. Started over, because apparently honesty was contagious up here, like a cough. "That's not why I came back. I came back because you're mine. My crew. My idiot captain who pays debts in towns and then vanishes before breakfast. Family isn't a debt, Silas. Debts get paid off. Family just keeps showing up."

Silas looked at her for a long time. Then he did something he almost never did: he smiled with his whole face, no guard on it, and put his arm around her shoulders, and for once neither of them said anything clever.

Above the settlement, fat clouds were rolling back in from the west. More snow coming. Good sledding snow, Ilsa would say tomorrow, pencil already moving.

"So," Wren said, when she trusted her voice again. "First load goes down after thaw. Grain contracts, warehouse leases, and I'm telling you now, I'm negotiating the Corren's Ford lease myself, because you'd pay full price out of guilt."

"I would not."

"You absolutely would. You tipped a ferryman double last month because his dog liked you."

"That dog had excellent judgment."

"* * *"

No—no asterisks yet. Wren grinned and let him steer them back toward the lamplight, arguing cheerfully about dog-based accounting the whole way, and behind them the snow began again, soft and steady, filling in their footprints on the road they'd be traveling for years.

Down in the valley, come spring, a new boat would take water for the first time. Wren had already picked the name. She hadn't told anyone yet.

She was going to call her *The Honest Cargo*.
