## 22. Anchored

The Alder River in spring was a different animal than the one Silas had spent ten years learning to fear and flatter. In the dry season she ran thin and mean, all sandbar and temper, a river you argued with. But in April, when the snowmelt came down off the Escarpment in long brown ropes, she swelled into something generous—wide-shouldered, deep-channeled, forgiving of a man's mistakes.

Silas stood at the wheel of the *Marten* and forgave her everything.

She wasn't much of a boat yet. Six weeks old out of the Corren's Ford yards, flat-bottomed, shallow-drafted, built to the exact specifications of a man who had once told a shipwright, with great patience, that he wanted a vessel that could lie down on a sandbar and get up again without complaint. Her paint was fresh enough to smell. Her engine had a knock on the port side that Wren swore was character and Silas suspected was a loose bearing, and her cargo hold smelled permanently of cedar shavings because Tomas had decided the walls needed lining before anyone asked him to.

But she floated. She answered her helm. And for the first time in longer than Silas could honestly reckon, the man at her wheel was not counting the miles behind him.

"Sandbar coming up," Wren said from beside him, not looking up from the manifest she was annotating with a pencil stub. "Port side. The one."

"I see it."

"You're not looking at it."

"I'm looking at the water around it." He adjusted the wheel two degrees, gentle as turning a sleeping child. "That's how you look at a sandbar. You never look at the thing itself. You look at what it's doing to everything else."

"Mm." Wren made a note. "That's very wise. I'm writing it down so I can ignore it later."

He almost smiled. It still surprised him, how easily the smile came now, how little it cost. For years he'd kept his face like a locked drawer—not because he was cold, but because every expression felt like evidence. A man who laughed too freely got remembered. A man who got remembered got found.

Nobody was looking for Silas Vosk anymore. That was the strange, sunlit truth of this spring. The council had published his testimony in full—the massacre at Kellin's Ford, the orders, the names, his own name among them—and the world had done what the world does with an ugly truth it can no longer use: it had absorbed it, argued about it, and moved on. Some of the exiles would never forgive him. He'd stopped waiting for that. Ilsa had told him once, on the trail, in that flat Vhaleni way of hers that made mercy sound like arithmetic: *"You don't get absolution, Voss. You get work. Take the work."*

So he took the work.

The *Marten* ran freight on the new route the land grants had opened—grain and timber downstream from the resettled valley, salt and iron and books upstream, because the exile council had opinions about books and had budgeted for them accordingly. In summer they ran the river. In winter, when she froze, they ran sleds over the ice road, which Wren called "the same job with worse coffee." It was, by any honest measure, a small life.

Silas had never wanted anything so badly in all his years of wanting nothing.

* * *

"Captain." Ilsa appeared at the top of the companionway with a ledger under her arm and the particular set of her jaw that meant business, or possibly breakfast. With Ilsa the two were often indistinguishable. "We have a discrepancy."

"We have a discrepancy," Silas repeated. "Is it a large discrepancy?"

"It is four sacks of flour that our manifest says we delivered to Dellis Landing and Dellis Landing says we did not."

"Dellis Landing," Silas said, "is run by a man who cannot count past his own grievances."

"And yet the grievance has been counted. Four times."

"Tomas signed for those sacks."

"Tomas signed for three."

Silas looked at her. She looked back with the serene patience of a woman who had crossed a mountain range to reclaim a nation and was not going to be defeated by a bakery ledger.

"He signed for three," Silas said slowly, "because there were three sacks left after—" He stopped. Closed his eyes briefly. "After I gave one to the widow Harkness. Her boy was sick and her mill money hadn't come through and she was standing on the dock looking like winter hadn't ended for her yet."

Ilsa's expression didn't change, but something behind it did—a settling, like silt finding its level. "You gave away cargo."

"I gave away one sack of flour."

"To a woman whose husband," Ilsa said, "was conscripted by Ashe's army in the last year of the war and died carrying ammunition he was too young to lift."

The wheel creaked under Silas's hands. "I didn't know that."

"No," Ilsa agreed. "You didn't. Which is either a coincidence or the kind of thing this river arranges when it decides a man owes a debt." She opened her ledger, wrote four words in it, closed it. "I've marked it as charitable expenditure. The council will grumble. Let them grumble; it keeps them warm." She turned to go, then paused. "Next time, tell me first. Not because I'll say no. Because I keep the books, and a crew that hides its kindnesses ends up hiding worse things."

"Aye," Silas said, and meant it in the marrow-deep way he meant very few things.

She went below. Through the deck planks he heard her say something to Tomas in Vhaleni, heard the boy laugh—a real laugh now, unguarded, the laugh of a kid who had slept through whole nights without checking the door. Somewhere on the trail last autumn, between the couloir and the pass, Tomas had stopped flinching at loud noises. Silas had noticed the exact morning it happened and had said nothing about it, because some repairs are best left unpainted.

* * *

They came around the long bend above Marrow Shoals just past midday, and there it was.

The sandbar.

It didn't look like much. It never had. A pale crescent of gravel and bleached driftwood, maybe forty yards of it, lying in the river's elbow like a dropped coin. The storm that had put the chest there was two years gone. The chest itself was in a council archive in the valley now, behind glass, visited by schoolchildren who were taught to say the word *Vhalen* without lowering their voices. The sandbar was just a sandbar.

Except it wasn't. Not to him.

Wren saw where they were. He knew she did, because she went quiet in the particular way she went quiet when she was giving him room—the same way she'd gone quiet outside the cave in the couloir, when she'd handed the chest back with frostbitten fingers and said, *I read the last page. I'm sorry. I'm sorry. I thought I was selling out a liar,* and he had looked at her shaking in the snow and understood that forgiveness wasn't a feeling you decided to have. It was a load-bearing wall. You built your house on it whether you trusted it or not, and only afterward did you find out it held.

"Want me to take her?" Wren asked quietly.

"No." He eased the throttle back. The *Marten*'s knock settled into a steady rhythm, like a heart deciding to be calm. "No, I'll take her through."

The channel ran close along the bar's upstream tip—that was the trick of it, the trick he'd known even then, half-drowned in dread, hauling a dead nation's secrets out of the shallows while lying to Wren's face about why his hands were shaking. You hugged the bar where the current cut deepest and let the river's own hunger carve your road for you. He brought her in slow, watching the water curl off the gravel lip in long amber ribbons, reading the surface the way other men read faces.

And he looked at it. That was the difference. That was the whole of it, maybe.

Two years ago he had passed this bar with his eyes fixed anywhere else, the way he'd passed every town he'd ever paid a debt in, every dock he'd ever been liked on, every morning of a life built facing forward because backward was a country that burned. He had spent twenty years being a man who did not look away from danger and always looked away from himself.

Now he let the sandbar fill the window. Gravel, driftwood, a heron standing in the shallows with the absurd dignity of a tax collector. Water moving over the place where the dredge line had snagged, where Wren had hauled and cursed and hauled again, where the mud had given up a chest stamped with a seal from a nation that officially had never existed.

"It's smaller than I remember," Wren said beside him.

"Everything drowned looks bigger from shore."

"That's not what people usually say."

"People usually haven't dredged one up." He held the channel true, the bar sliding past the rail, close enough that he could have thrown a biscuit onto it. "Hello," he said to it. Just like that. Out loud.

Wren glanced at him. "Talking to geography now."

"Seemed rude not to. We have history."

The heron lifted off, unhurried, offended, magnificent. The *Marten* cleared the bar by a comfortable fathom and the river opened ahead of them, wide and brown and going somewhere, and Silas Voss—who had once been Silas Vosk, who had once been nobody at all—stood at the wheel of his own boat and felt the strangest thing, which was nothing like the itch. For twenty years the horizon had itched. Every landing had been a door left open behind him. Every kindness received had been a debt accruing interest, and debts were the one cargo he'd never learned to stow.

The itch was gone. He checked for it sometimes, the way a man checks for a tooth he's lost. Still gone.

* * *

They moored that evening at a landing that hadn't existed three years ago—new pilings, new warehouse, a string of lamps somebody had strung optimistically between poles, half of them working. The valley settlements sent their goods down here now instead of hauling overland to the old toll towns, and the toll towns' magistrates had written letters, and the letters had been answered by the council in careful diplomatic Vhaleni that Ilsa described, with visible enjoyment, as "polite in the way a fence is polite."

Tomas met them at the gangplank, sixteen now and suddenly all elbows and appetite, with a crate of books on his shoulder and ink on his fingers.

"There's a man asking for you, Captain," he said.

Silas's hands went still on the mooring line—an old reflex, the body remembering hunted before the mind could veto it. "What kind of man?"

"The harmless kind. Old. Says he knew you before you were you." Tomas grinned. "Says his name is Breck. Says you'll either buy him dinner or throw him in the river, and he'd genuinely enjoy either."

Silas let out a breath. Breck. Quartermaster Breck, third company, the man who had falsified supply manifests for eleven months so that Silas could slip rations to a Vhaleni farming family in the hills and call it spoilage. The man who had shaken his hand at the crossroads the night Silas deserted and said, *Go. And if anyone asks, I never learned your face.* One of perhaps four people alive who had known both names and chosen, decades ago, to keep the secret.

"Dinner," Silas said. "Definitely dinner. Where is he?"

"Warehouse office. He brought cheese. He says it's Vhaleni-style. He says Ilsa will want to interrogate him about it."

"He's right about that," Ilsa's voice called from below decks, immediately, without hesitation, as though she had been lying in wait for the word *cheese.*

* * *

Breck was older—of course he was, they both were—but his eyes were the same quick brown ones that had once counted crates in the dark and lied about the totals. They ate at the trestle table in the warehouse office: hard cheese, dark bread, river fish Tomas had caught that afternoon with the smugness of a boy who had finally found the one skill he was effortlessly best at. Wren told the story of the bearing knock and how she'd fixed it with a leather shim and a threat. Ilsa did indeed interrogate Breck about the cheese, established that his grandmother had been Vhaleni, and wrote down his grandmother's village name in her ledger with the gravity of a woman adding a brick back onto a national wall.

And then, over the last of the bread, Breck looked across the table at Silas and said, casually, terribly:

"You know they're reprinting your testimony in the valley schools next year. Condensed version. There was an argument about whether to include your name in the text or just say 'a river officer.' The council voted to include it." He paused. "Full name. Both names, actually. The record shows the change and the reason: 'so that no child learns that men are only ever one thing.'"

The table went quiet. Wren's hand found Silas's forearm under it—not gripping, just resting there, an anchor that didn't drag.

"What did you vote?" Silas asked Ilsa.

"I voted for both names." She met his eyes steadily. "A man who spends twenty years building an honest self deserves to have it attached to the record. Otherwise the record implies the second name was the real one. It wasn't. It was the wound. You are the scar tissue that grew over it, and scar tissue," she added, with the faintest dry edge that was as close as Ilsa came to tenderness, "is stronger than what it covers."

Silas looked down at his hands. River hands now. Rope-callused, flour-dusted from the widow Harkness's sack, steady on a wheel. He had spent so many years assuming that the day his past caught up with him, it would kill him—would reach up out of the water like the dredge chain and take him under. Instead it had caught up with him at a mountain pass in the first snowfall, and what it had done was introduce him to his family.

"Well," he said, when he could speak evenly. "Then I suppose I'd better make sure the scar tissue keeps good books."

"Four sacks," Ilsa said instantly. "Three delivered, one charitable. It's already entered."

* * *

Later, when Breck had been installed in the guest bunk protesting that he'd sleep on the floor and been firmly overruled by everyone including the boat, Silas climbed to the wheelhouse for the last check of the night. It wasn't necessary. Wren had the watch until midnight, and the *Marten* rode her lines easy, and the river made its soft endless conversation against the hull.

He went anyway. Habit, but a good one now—the kind of habit a man keeps on purpose.

Through the wheelhouse windows the landing lamps glowed, most of them working. Downriver, invisible in the dark, the sandbar lay where it had always lain, holding its gravel and its herons and its history, keeping the spot warm for whatever the river decided to give up next. Maybe nothing. Maybe something. The Alder was generous in spring.

Wren appeared in the doorway with two tin mugs, handed him one, and leaned on the sill beside him. For a while neither of them said anything, which was its own kind of language, and one they'd gotten fluent in.

"Route schedule for summer's drafted," she said eventually. "Valley run twice monthly, Dellis Landing weekly, and I want to try the upper reaches past the ford once the melt settles. There's timber money up there and nobody running it."

"Nobody running it because the upper reaches eat boats."

"Nobody running it because nobody's you." She sipped her coffee and grimaced at it on principle. "Besides. If it eats the boat, we walk home. We're good at walking home. We've practiced."

Silas laughed—out loud, unguarded, the drawer wide open—and the sound seemed to surprise even him, still, after two years, a small miracle he hadn't gotten used to.

Below them, light spilled from the galley hatch where Ilsa was teaching Tomas card games from the old country and losing to him on purpose, or possibly not on purpose; the boy had gotten disturbingly good. From the guest bunk came the rumble of Breck already asleep, mid-sentence, the way old soldiers do. The boat knocked gently at her lines. The river went on talking.

Silas Voss stood at the wheel of a boat at anchor, going nowhere tonight, tomorrow, or any night he didn't choose—and found that nowhere was exactly where he wanted to be, because everywhere he looked, home was already aboard.

He raised his mug slightly, to the dark water, to the buried bar, to the drowned nation and the living one, to the man he'd been and the crew who'd made the man he was.

"Anchored," he said.

"Aye, Captain," said Wren, and drank with him as the first stars came out over the Escarpment, sharp and white and patient, like snow remembering the way down.
