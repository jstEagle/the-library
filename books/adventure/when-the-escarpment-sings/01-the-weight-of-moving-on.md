## 1. The Weight of Moving On

The anchor came up clean, which should have been my first warning that the day was going too well.

I stood at the bow of the *Magpie's Rest* and watched the last of the morning fog peel off the escarpment coast like a bandage coming away from a wound nobody wanted to look at. Below us, forty feet down in the green murk, the fishing trawler *Gladys B* sat on her keel with her holds full of salvage we'd spent nine days prying loose—brass fittings, a bronze propeller worth more than the rest of the boat combined, and a ship's bell that Tomas had insisted on bringing up even though it was pitted to hell and answered to no one.

"Last crate's lashed," Ilsa called up from the deck, her voice carrying that particular flat satisfaction she reserved for machinery that had behaved. "Rigging's stowed. If you're waiting on me, stop waiting."

"I'm never waiting on you," I said. "I'm waiting on Tomas."

Tomas surfaced thirty seconds later, spitting his regulator out like it had insulted him. He grabbed the ladder, climbed aboard, and stood dripping on the deck with the *Gladys B*'s bell cradled under one arm, water streaming off him in ropes.

"She says goodbye," he announced.

"The boat?"

"The bell. I asked."

Nine days underwater will do that to a man. Nine days at forty feet, working a wreck by feel half the time because the silt this far down the escarpment doesn't so much settle as hang around like a relative who won't take the hint. Tomas had spent more of the past week breathing compressed air than talking to us, and somewhere in there he'd started holding conversations with the dead boat's fixtures. We'd all stopped commenting on it around day four.

"Pim," I called toward the wheelhouse. "Weather?"

Pim's head appeared over the rail above me, upside down, hair hanging like seaweed. "Wind's backing southwest. Cloud building over the headland. I'd give us six hours before it turns ugly, eight before it gets honest about it."

"Then we're gone in four." I turned back to the deck. "Ilsa, get us moving as soon as the crane arm's locked. Tomas, dry off or don't, but be useful either way."

"And you?" Ilsa asked, already moving, because Ilsa didn't ask questions she didn't intend to answer herself. "What are you going to do?"

"What I always do," I said. "Count what we've got and figure out how much of it we keep."

* * *

Here is what you need to understand about the escarpment coast, if you've never worked it: it is two hundred miles of cliff and current where the land gives up on being gentle and drops into water cold enough to stop your heart in ten minutes flat. The old charts call it the Singing Coast, because when the wind comes off the open ocean and hits those cliffs just right, the whole escarpment hums—a low, mournful note that sailors used to say was the coast counting its dead.

The sailors were wrong. The coast isn't counting. It's keeping.

Every storm for three hundred years has fed ships into those cliffs, and every wreck on that bottom is a small fortune sitting in the dark, unclaimed, unmapped, and—this is the important part—unwatched by anyone with the authority to ask questions. Salvage law along the escarpment is a rumor. Permits are a fiction sold to tourists. What actually governs who takes what from that water is simple: whoever gets there first, works fastest, and leaves before anyone official develops an interest.

That last part is where I come in. Or rather, that last part is why I run the crew the way I run it, which is to say like a man fleeing a fire that went out years ago but might not have.

We finished loading by noon. By twelve-fifteen the *Magpie's Rest* was standing offshore with the *Gladys B* fading behind us into the fog line, and by one o'clock we'd put enough water between ourselves and the nearest harbor town that even the most dedicated harbormaster would have needed a telescope and a grudge to find us.

This is the part of the job I'm best at. Not the diving—I leave that to Tomas. Not the engineering—Ilsa could rebuild our engine from scrap and spite, and has. Not the spotting—Pim reads weather and coastline better than men with twenty more years on the water. What I'm best at, what I have been best at for eleven years now, is leaving.

There's a craft to it. Anyone can run. Running well is a discipline.

Rule one: never stop longer than the work requires. A job takes nine days, you're gone on the tenth. A job takes two, you're gone before the second sunset. The moment a crew becomes a fixture, someone starts asking what you're doing, and then someone else starts asking who authorized it, and then a third someone arrives with paperwork and a badge and opinions about your future.

Rule two: never settle. No leases. No mooring contracts. No bar tabs that outlive the week. Every port is a doorway, not a room. You sleep aboard, you eat aboard, and when the mood takes you to own something—a chair, a painting, a dog—you lie down until the feeling passes.

Rule three, and this is the one I wrote myself, the one the others learned by watching what happened to people who broke it: never go back. Not to a wreck you've worked, not to a port you've loved, not to a life you lost. The past is a riptide. It feels like it's pulling you toward something familiar, and what it's actually doing is dragging you under.

Eleven years, three crews' worth of turnover, two countries that would arrest me at their respective docks, and I have kept all three rules without exception.

So naturally, that afternoon, with the weather building over the headland and the *Magpie's Rest* running south with a hold full of brass, I did the thing I never do.

I stopped.

* * *

It happened like this: Pim spotted the courier boat first, because Pim spots everything first—that's the whole reason we keep a teenager on payroll whose primary qualifications are eyesight and a complete absence of fear. A single-masted skiff, running lights dark, cutting across our wake line at an angle that meant they'd been aiming for us since before we cleared the *Gladys B*.

"We're being followed," Pim reported, with the enthusiasm most people reserve for finding money. "Small craft. One sail. They've adjusted course twice to match ours."

"Tomas, below. Ilsa, give me everything the engine has that won't shake loose."

But the skiff wasn't chasing. It pulled alongside at fifty yards, held there, and a man in oilskins stood up in it and raised something over his head. Even at that distance I knew the shape: a sealed tube, the kind lawyers and cowards use for documents they don't want to hand over face to face.

"They want to come alongside," Pim said.

"Nobody wants anything," I said. "We're a ghost. Ghosts don't receive mail."

"Ghost's getting mail anyway," Tomas said, coming up from below with a boathook, because Tomas's solution to every problem is to get closer to it.

I could have run. That's the truth of it, and I want it on the record that I considered running—the engine was hot, the wind was fair, and one skiff against the *Magpie's Rest* is not a contest, it's a joke. But here's the thing about running that nobody tells you: after eleven years, you start to notice that you're not running *from* anything anymore. You're just running. The fire went out. The badges stopped looking. Somewhere in the last few years, the discipline became the disease, and I'd started waking up in foreign ports asking myself the question I'd sworn never to ask:

*Why, exactly?*

I don't have an answer. I want to be clear about that. Eleven years, and I still don't have an answer. What I have is a habit wearing the costume of a principle, and a crew that follows me because following me is the only home any of us can afford.

So I hove to. I let the skiff come alongside. And I took the tube from a courier who wouldn't meet my eye, paid him the fee tucked in the cap, and watched him turn back toward shore like a man who'd delivered worse things to worse people and expected to again.

The letter inside smelled of tar.

Not metaphorically. The paper itself had been treated with it—waterproofed, the way survey documents and sea charts are waterproofed, the way a certain kind of maritime correspondence gets treated when the sender expects it to end up wet. I knew that treatment. I'd handled a hundred documents with that same coating, back when I was young and credentialed and worked for institutions instead of against them.

And I knew the handwriting on the address before I read the name.

*M. VOSS.*

I stood there on my own deck, in my own weather, with my own crew watching me with varying degrees of alarm, and I read a name I hadn't seen in eleven years.

"Cyrus?" Ilsa said. "You've got a look."

"I've always got a look."

"You've got a *different* look. Like someone walked over your grave and apologized for it."

I didn't answer. I broke the seal instead, because the alternative was standing there forever, and rule one says never stand still longer than the work requires.

The letter was short. Maren Voss had never wasted words on me when we were friends, and she clearly saw no reason to start now that we were whatever we were instead. It offered a contract. It named a vessel.

The *Kestrel*.

I read the name three times. The letters didn't change. The *Kestrel* still lay at the bottom of the escarpment coast, sixty feet down in a cove that doesn't appear on charts because Odile made sure it never would, and eleven years of tide had done nothing to soften what happened there, because water doesn't heal anything. It just covers it. That's the whole trick of the sea—it keeps things, and it keeps them *down*, and it lets you believe for years at a stretch that drowned means gone.

The letter offered money I didn't need for a job I'd sworn off, from a woman I'd sworn off, to recover a wreck the entire crew had sworn—out loud, at a fire, with salt in our hands—an oath never to return to.

Behind me, the wind shifted. Southwest, backing hard, exactly as Pim had said. Over the headland, the cloud had organized itself into something with intent.

"Cyrus," Pim said quietly, from the rail. "What does it say?"

I looked at the letter. I looked at the coast behind us, where the escarpment was starting, very faintly, in the rising wind, to sing.

"It says," I told them, "that somebody wants us to go back."

* * *

I want to tell you I threw it overboard. I want to tell you I weighed the oath against the offer, found the oath heavier, and burned the letter in the galley stove while the crew applauded. That's the version of me I'd have written, back when I still believed I was the hero of my own story instead of just its most reliable coward.

What I actually did was fold the letter along its original creases, tuck it inside my coat, next to the place where a man keeps the things he tells himself he'll deal with later, and spend the rest of the afternoon pretending to check rigging that didn't need checking.

Ilsa found me at dusk, coiled around a winch handle I'd been polishing for two hours.

"The letter's from Voss," she said. Not a question. She'd seen the handwriting too, or she'd read my face, which amounts to the same thing after eleven years.

"Maren Voss."

"The one who testified."

"The one who testified."

Ilsa leaned on the rail beside me and looked out at the water, which was going dark and restless in the way water does when it's thinking about something. "You're going to show the crew."

"I haven't decided."

"You've decided. You folded it instead of burning it. Cyrus Eriksen has never in his life folded a document he intended to destroy." She turned to look at me, and in the last light her expression was the one she wore when an engine made a sound she recognized. "You're going to show them tonight. At the fire."

"There's no fire. We're underway."

"There'll be a fire. There's always a fire when you've got something to say and no courage to say it standing up." She pushed off the rail and headed aft, then paused. "For what it's worth—and it isn't worth much—I remember the oath too. All of it. Every word. I also remember that Odile taught us the rules so we'd know when to break them."

"She taught us the rules so we'd follow them."

"No," Ilsa said. "She taught us the rules so we'd know *which ones were bought*. There's a difference. You'd know that if you ever talked about her."

She left me alone with the winch handle and the wind and the faint, rising song of the escarpment off our stern, and I stood there until full dark, doing the arithmetic I've been avoiding for eleven years.

A crew of four. An oath sworn over salt. A woman whose testimony ended my career and possibly my life as I'd planned it. A wreck where our mentor died, sixty feet down, in a cove that isn't on any chart. And a letter smelling of tar, offering money for the one job in the world I can never take.

Rule three says never go back.

Rule three has kept me alive for eleven years, and I have never once—not once—been able to explain to myself what I'm staying alive *for*.

Below decks, I could hear the crew gathering. Tomas laughing at something. Pim arguing about it. Ilsa's voice cutting through both, level as a spirit gauge. The sounds of the only family I have, assembled around a stove, waiting, because Ilsa had already told them I'd come down when I was ready, and Ilsa is never wrong about machinery or people.

I took the letter out of my coat. I looked at it for a long time in the dark.

Then I went below to break an oath.
