## 2. A Letter Smelling of Tar

The courier found us in Saltmere, which was the first sign something was wrong.

Saltmere is the kind of town that prides itself on never finding anything—lost sailors, lost cargo, lost causes. It sits at the base of the escarpment like a dog that's given up begging and settled for sleeping in the road. We'd finished a job two days earlier, pulling a drowned grain skiff off a sandbar for an insurance man who paid in cash and didn't look at our faces too long, which is exactly how I like my clients. By rights we should have been gone by now. Ilsa had said so twice. Tomas had said it once, which from Tomas counts as three times.

But Pim had found a tide pool full of sea stars behind the breakwater, and somehow that had become everyone's problem, and somehow we were still there when the courier came up the gangway of the *Marrow* with a parcel under his arm and the particular walk of a man who has been paid to deliver something he'd rather burn.

"Looking for a Cyrus Eriksen," he said.

I looked at him. He looked at me. Behind him, the harbor did its usual gray arithmetic: gulls, rust, the long black teeth of the escarpment running north until the haze ate them.

"No one here by that name," I said.

He sighed the sigh of a professional. "Look, friend, I don't care what you're called. I was told to give this to the man who owns the boat with the patched gunwale and the anchor chain painted green, and to tell him—" he squinted at a card, clearly reading someone else's words, "—'the green paint was always a mistake.'"

I felt that land somewhere under my ribs. Only four people alive knew about the green paint. Three of them were standing within earshot pretending not to listen, and none of them would have wasted money on a courier.

I took the parcel. It was heavy for its size, wrapped in oilcloth, tied with tarred twine, and even before I opened it I knew what I'd find inside, because there's only one person who ever wrapped things that way—tight enough to survive a drowning—and she taught all of us to do it and then stopped writing to us eleven years ago.

The courier touched his cap and left, walking fast, like a man who'd delivered his last message for a client he didn't want to be owed anything by. Smart man.

* * *

I didn't open it on deck. That's not superstition—or it's not only superstition. It's that some things you open where the wind can't take the pieces.

Below, in the cramped cabin that smells permanently of diesel and Ilsa's soldering flux, I cut the twine with my belt knife. Inside: a letter, folded once, and beneath it a chart.

The letter smelled of tar. Not the twine—the paper itself, as if she'd written it in a boatyard, or as if she wanted me to know exactly what kind of day she'd been having when she wrote it. The handwriting hadn't changed. Maren Voss wrote the way she sailed: hard strokes, no hesitation, every letter looking like it was trying to overtake the one before it.

*Cyrus.*

*I'll keep this short because I know you'll stop reading the moment you decide it's a trap, and I want to get the important part in before then.*

*Meridian Salvage has claimed the Kestrel. Their survey crews marked her last month off the escarpment shelf, forty-one fathoms, and their claim papers are already filed in two ports. When their fleet arrives—and it will arrive within the fortnight—they'll raise her, strip her, and everything she went down carrying will belong to them. You know what she carries. Or you don't, which is worse, and which is why I'm writing.*

*I'm offering you the contract. First refusal. Recover her contents before Meridian's fleet makes anchor, and I will pay your crew more than they've made in the last five years combined. I know you don't need the money. I know you need it less than you need what's in those ledgers, though you may not know that yet either.*

*I know what I am to you. I'm not asking forgiveness. I'm asking for six tides of your time.*

*The Kestrel, Cyrus. You swore an oath. I remember. I was there.*

*M.V.*

I read it three times. On the third pass I set it down on the chart table, and my hands were steady, which surprised me, because the rest of me felt like a hull with its seams opened.

Under the letter lay the chart. Hand-drawn. Odile's hand—I'd have known it anywhere, that particular way she had of drawing depth contours like fingerprints, whorls and ridges instead of lines. It showed the escarpment coast north of Saltmere, and there, forty-one fathoms down on the shelf edge, a small inked cross and, beside it, in Odile's cramped notation: *K — she lies quiet. Do not wake her unless you mean it.*

Eleven years dead, and Odile Marchetti was still leaving instructions for people who didn't listen well enough while she lived.

* * *

I went up on deck because staying below felt like drowning in slow motion, and I've done actual drowning and prefer it.

They were all there, arranged around the deck with the studied casualness of people who have absolutely been listening through the cabin overhead. Tomas was coiling rope that was already coiled. Ilsa was cleaning a wrench that didn't need it. Pim sat on the rail swinging one leg, watching me with the frank, unblinking attention of a nineteen-year-old who hasn't yet learned that some questions are better asked silently.

"Well?" Ilsa said.

"It's a job offer," I said.

"From?"

"You know how couriers are. They never leave a card."

"Cyrus." She set the wrench down with a click that meant *we can do this the easy way or the way where I take the letter out of your pocket.* "The man said 'the green paint was always a mistake.' Nobody knows about the green paint except—" and I watched her arrive at it, watched her face do something complicated, "—no."

"Maren Voss," I said.

Tomas stopped coiling the rope that wasn't moving anyway. Pim's leg stopped swinging. For a moment the only sound was the harbor slapping against the hull, patient as ever, keeping its own ledger.

"The Kestrel," I said, because they were going to make me say it, so I might as well say it first. "Meridian's claimed her. Maren wants us to recover what she was carrying before their fleet arrives. Six tides, she says. She's paying more than we've seen in five years."

"And the oath?" Tomas asked quietly.

That was the thing about Tomas. He could go years without saying much of anything, and then he'd ask the one question nobody wanted answered, in the voice of a man defusing something.

"We all took it," I said. "We all remember it."

Pim raised a hand, which is a habit picked up from no crew I've ever known, and said, "For those of us who weren't there—"

"You were there," Ilsa said. "You were fourteen. You stood on the cliff with us and you held the lantern."

"I held the lantern," Pim agreed. "I didn't understand what I was lighting."

Nobody answered that. The truth was none of us fully understood it either, even the ones who'd spoken the words. Oaths are like that. You say them in the white heat of grief and they seem simple, and then ten years pass and you discover they were written in a language you've forgotten, and all you can remember is that breaking them cost something, and keeping them costs something else, and nobody told you the prices would compound.

I looked north along the escarpment. Somewhere past that haze, forty-one fathoms down, the *Kestrel* lay on the shelf edge where she'd gone down with Odile aboard and a hold full of consortium paperwork that had ruined my life without ever introducing itself to me.

"Dread dressed as curiosity," Ilsa said, reading my face the way she read weather. "That's what you're wearing."

"That's my best coat."

"Cyrus." She crossed to me, lowered her voice, and for a moment the engineer fell away and it was just Ilsa, who had pulled two of us out of the water that night and never once talked about it. "She testified against you. Her testimony put you on two blacklists. And now she sends you a letter smelling of tar and a dead woman's chart, and you're standing there doing sums."

"I'm always doing sums."

"These sums end with us going back."

I didn't answer. That was answer enough, and she knew it, and I saw her jaw tighten in the way it did when she was converting fear into inventory lists.

* * *

Here's what I remembered about Maren Voss, in the order I remembered it:

Her hands on a winch, faster than anyone's, laughing at the strain. The way she could read water the way other people read faces—which sounds like a compliment until you realize both skills are mostly about spotting lies. The night in the tavern at Greyharbor when she and Odile argued about salvage ethics until closing, and Odile won, and Maren bought the next round anyway, which was its own kind of surrender.

And then the inquiry. The hearing room in Port Callow with its wood-paneled walls and its consortium men sitting in the front row like furniture that owned the building. Maren in the witness box, dry-eyed, precise, testifying that she'd seen evidence of sabotage on the consortium vessels—that she believed Cyrus Eriksen had taken payment to scuttle them. Every word measured. Every word true-sounding. That was the worst part; I could never even hate the performance, because it was flawless. She gave them exactly what they needed, in the exact tone that made it unanswerable, and when it was done she walked out of the room and out of my life and I spent eleven years building a version of her that fit in a small enough box to carry.

Two countries put me on their blacklists off the strength of that testimony. No guild would license me, no port master would log my work, no insurance house would touch a claim with my name near it. So I became what I am: a man who salvages what the law forgot, for clients who pay cash and don't look at faces. Never stop. Never settle. Never go back. Three rules, worn smooth as river stones from handling.

And here was the thing I'd never told the crew, the thing I'd barely admitted to myself at three in the morning in a dozen different anchorages: I had never learned what the ledgers actually contained. I knew the consortium used the *Kestrel* as a survey vessel, knew she carried their books, knew she went down with Odile and nine others in a storm the consortium's own captains had been warned about and chose to ignore. I knew the ledgers were the reason the inquiry happened at all—the consortium wanted them recovered quietly, wanted them never to surface, and when the recovery crew I was part of failed, the story got rewritten with me holding the knife. But the contents? I'd built eleven years of ruin around a locked box whose contents I'd never seen. Rage needs very little to feed on. A name will do.

If Maren's letter was true—if Meridian was going to raise the *Kestrel* and strip her—then whatever was in that hold would end up in a consortium warehouse, or a furnace, and the last proof of what actually happened to Odile and to me would go down a shredder in a country I can't enter.

Or the letter was bait, and Maren Voss had spent a decade learning exactly which hook fits in my mouth.

Both things could be true. Both things probably were. That's the trouble with old rivals: they know your architecture.

* * *

Evening came down on Saltmere the way it always does, the escarpment going purple-black behind the town like a wave deciding whether to fall. I sat on the foredeck with the letter and the chart and let the crew orbit me, because that's what they do when something's wrong—they orbit, and wait, and pretend they aren't waiting.

Pim broke first, naturally. Pim always breaks first; it's practically a navigational aid. "Read us the middle part again. The part about the oath."

"'I know what I am to you,'" I recited. "'I'm not asking forgiveness. I'm asking for six tides of your time.'"

"She remembers the oath," Tomas said. From the shadows by the mast. "Ten years, and she remembers we took an oath."

"Of course she remembers," Ilsa said bitterly. "It's useful. People who betray you keep excellent records of what they took."

"Tomas," I said. "You were closest to Odile. If anyone gets a vote weighted double, it's you."

He was quiet long enough that the gulls got bored and left. Then he said, "My mother taught me that the sea keeps what it's owed, and gives back what it's ready to. I've spent eleven years thinking the *Kestrel* was the sea's business. Now a woman who helped ruin you says the sea's business is about to become a corporation's business, and I find—" he turned his hands over, studying them like they belonged to a stranger, "—I find I mind that. More than I expected to. More than is convenient."

"That's not a vote," Ilsa said.

"No. It's worse. It's a feeling."

I looked at the chart. Odile's cross, forty-one fathoms down. *Do not wake her unless you mean it.* Eleven years ago I'd stood on a cliff with three grieving people and a fourteen-year-old holding a lantern, and sworn never to return, and I had meant it, and meaning it had kept us alive and free and hollow.

Now the same wreck wore a new flag—a corporate claim number filed in two ports—and the choice wasn't whether to go back anymore. The choice was whether to let Meridian go back for us, and live with whatever they carried up into the light.

Dread dressed as curiosity. Ilsa was right. But dread, I've found, is just curiosity that's been to sea a few seasons and learned caution. And curiosity, in my experience, always wins eventually. It's why I'm still alive. It's also why I'm still running.

I folded Maren's letter along its original crease and tucked it inside my jacket, over the left breast, which is where I keep things I haven't decided to trust.

"Fire on the beach," I said. "Tonight. Full crew. We'll lay it all out—the offer, the odds, the oath—and everyone speaks, or everyone stays silent, and silence counts as what it always counts as."

"And what's that?" Pim asked.

"On this crew?" I stood, and the escarpment loomed behind me, and somewhere north of the haze the *Kestrel* waited with her dead and her secrets and her eleven years of quiet. "Silence counts as yes. It always has. God help us."

Ilsa was already gathering the lantern.
