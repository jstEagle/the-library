## 3. The Crew Votes With Silence

Cyrus read the letter twice by firelight, and I watched his face do something it almost never does: nothing at all.

That's the tell, if you're learning to read him. Most men wear their fear like weather — clouds moving across the face, a squall in the jaw. Cyrus goes flat. He becomes the escarpment itself, that long gray wall of stone running the coast, the one that doesn't care what the sea does to it. When Cyrus went flat, I knew the letter was bad. When he folded it once and fed it to the fire without offering it around, I knew it was worse than bad.

"Job offer," he said.

Ilsa looked up from the winch bearing she was cleaning with a rag and an amount of profanity usually reserved for hull breaches. Pim stopped whittling. The kid always stops whittling when there's news; it's the only time those hands go still.

"We just finished a job," Ilsa said. "We finished it eleven hours ago. There's a rule about this."

"There is."

"You wrote the rule."

"I did." Cyrus turned the letter's ghost over in the fire with a stick, watching the ash curl. "Rule stands. This isn't work. It's a question. And we vote on questions."

"We've never voted on anything," I said.

"No," he agreed. "We haven't."

He laid it out anyway, because of course he did. Maren Voss. The name landed on our little beach like a dropped anchor, and nobody moved to pick it up. She wanted the *Kestrel* recovered before Meridian Salvage got their claws into her. She was paying — well, she said she was paying, which in my experience is a sentence that means either everything or nothing depending on who says it. And the *Kestrel*, in case any of us had managed to forget in nine years, was the survey vessel that went down off the escarpment with Odile aboard, and the place where four people stood on a cliff the week after and swore, out loud, with the wind taking most of the words, that they would never go back.

"I'm not asking you to say yes," Cyrus said. "I'm telling you what's on the table. We vote with silence. Anyone who wants no part of it, stand up now, walk down the beach, and when you come back it'll be decided and you'll never owe me an explanation."

Then he sat down on the driftwood log and put another stick on the fire, and waited.

That's the thing about Cyrus Eriksen. People hear "blacklisted from two countries" and picture a loud man, a man with grievances, a man who talks. He's not. He's the quietest guilty-or-innocent man I ever sailed under. He makes silence do the arguing for him. And so we all sat there in his silence, each of us alone with ours.

I'll tell you mine, since apparently I'm the one keeping the record.

* * *

I was twenty-three the year the *Kestrel* went down, and I wasn't on her. That's the whole wound, right there, in one sentence. Everyone assumes the grief of a survivor is about being there. Mine is about the opposite. Odile trained me. Two years of it — how to read current against tide, how to equalize slow and never rush a descent, how to come up when every part of you wants to stay down because staying down feels like honesty. She used to say the sea keeps what it's given, so give it nothing you can't afford. Then she took a contract surveying the consortium's shipping lanes, and the weather turned, and the *Kestrel* didn't come back, and I was ashore nursing a burst eardrum and hating my own head for it.

They never found her. They found the wreck eventually, or part of it, wedged in deep water along the escarpment base where the currents run like freight trains. But they never found *her*, and for nine years some drowned, stupid, faithful part of me has believed that means she's not done. That somewhere in the dark of that hull there's still a woman calmly equalizing, waiting for conditions to improve, because Odile did not panic and Odile did not quit and the sea keeps what it's given but it has never once kept her.

You see the trap. If I dive that wreck, I find a cabin, a boot, maybe worse, and the faithful part of me dies underwater where I can't even bury it properly. If I don't dive, she stays not-done forever, and I stay twenty-three, ashore, holding a burst eardrum and a debt I can't pay.

Either way the sea wins. It always does. But I wanted — God help me, sitting there at that fire — I wanted to lose on purpose for once. To walk into it with my eyes open instead of spending another decade pretending the ache in my ears was just the old injury.

So I didn't stand up.

* * *

Across the fire, Ilsa was doing the thing where she dismantles a problem by refusing to look at it directly. She'd gone back to the bearing, turning it in her fingers, checking surfaces that were already clean. Ilsa processes grief through machinery. Give her a broken engine and a dead friend and she'll fix the engine, and the friend will get handled somewhere in the torque specifications where nobody has to watch.

But I'd shared a wheelhouse with her nine years, and I knew her reason too, though she'd have gutted me for saying so.

The *Kestrel*'s engines were hers. Not literally — she never touched that ship — but the class of them. Odile ran a rig Ilsa had helped design as an apprentice, back when Ilsa was all elbows and opinions and nobody's crew. When word came that the ship was lost, Ilsa didn't cry. She spent three weeks building the same engine from memory, at half scale, on our deck, and then she lit it, and ran it, and listened to it the way other people listen to a recording of a voice. Then she stripped it for parts and never spoke of it.

She's been rebuilding that engine in various forms ever since. Every rig we own has its heartbeat. And I think — I *think*, because Ilsa would deny it under torture — that going back to the *Kestrel* means hearing whether the real ones are still singing down there. Whether the thing she made held, even at the end. Engineers want to know if their work survived them. It's the closest they come to praying.

She set the bearing down. Looked at Cyrus. Looked at the fire.

"Well," she said, to no one, in the voice she uses when a decision has already been made and she's furious about how easy it was. "Meridian won't wait for us to be comfortable."

Nobody answered her. Nobody needed to.

* * *

Pim was the problem. Pim is always the problem, in the sense that Pim is nineteen and has no scar tissue yet, and the rest of us keep forgetting that our ghosts are, to the kid, mostly stories. The kid never met Odile. Came to us at fifteen, half-starved off a fishing skiff, with eyes good enough to spot a periscope at four miles and a past nobody asked about because Cyrus's rule is you don't ask. In nine years — sorry, six — Pim had learned our trade, our silences, and exactly enough about Odile to know the name was load-bearing. Say it wrong in the wheelhouse and the whole structure creaks.

Now the kid sat very straight, whittling knife closed, and asked the question flat out, because Pim has never once learned to leave things alone.

"Would she have wanted us to go?"

And the fire cracked, and the waves worked the shingle below us, and none of us said anything, because here's the ugly truth: we don't know. Every one of us carries a different Odile, and the three versions don't agree.

My Odile was careful. My Odile would have called this job suicide dressed up as salvage and told us to let Meridian have the rust.

Ilsa's Odile was exacting. Hers would have demanded to know what Maren Voss actually wanted recovered, and why, and would have refused to move until the numbers reconciled — and would have gone anyway once they did, because Odile respected a reconciled ledger more than a safe harbor.

Cyrus's Odile — and I only saw this version in glimpses, in the way he still steers by her habits, in the way he checks the anchor twice because she checked it twice — his Odile was stubborn in the specific way of people who believe the truth is a place you can sail to if you refuse to tack. His Odile might have gone. Might have insisted.

Three women. One name. None of them real anymore, all of them realer than the actual person, because the actual person is nine years of cold water and we've each been carrying our own hand-carved copy ever since, worn smooth by handling.

"Would she?" Pim pressed.

"She'd have wanted us to decide together," Cyrus said finally. "That's the only honest answer. Everything else is us talking to our own ghosts."

Which was, I noted, a very Cyrus answer: technically true, emotionally useless, and delivered in a tone that made you feel scolded for hoping otherwise.

* * *

Here's what happened next, and I want it recorded precisely, because later — after the tides, after the fleet, after whatever this becomes — I'll want to remember that it happened this way and not some braver way.

Nobody stood up.

Ilsa picked up her bearing and put it away in the toolbox, which meant the night's work was done, which meant she was staying. Pim looked at the dark water past the point where the *Kestrel* lay, and then deliberately turned their back on it and faced the fire, which from Pim is a whole speech. And I sat there with my burst-eardrum year lodged in my throat like a fishbone, and did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding since Cyrus unfolded the letter.

Six tides, Ilsa would tell us tomorrow — she'd already be charting it, I could see the monsoon math happening behind her eyes. Six tides before the storm closes the site till spring. Meridian two behind us. A rival who testified against our captain hiring us to do the one thing we swore blind we wouldn't. Every line of it wrong. Every reason to walk down the beach.

And underneath all of it, quiet as ballast: nine years of not-knowing, and one chance to know.

Cyrus watched us not standing up. I watched him watch us, and I saw something cross his face that I've only seen twice before — once when we pulled him out of the water at Corren Point, once the night we buried the oath. It wasn't relief. Relief is simple. It was more like a man watching a door close that he'd been holding open against his own better judgment, and grieving it, and being grateful, all at once.

"All right," he said quietly. "Silence carries it. We go."

"Against the oath," Ilsa said. Not a question. A flag planted, so that later, if it went wrong, everyone would remember she'd named the crime.

"Against the oath," Cyrus agreed. "We'll swear a new one when this is done. Better one."

"You said that last time."

"No," he said. "Last time I said we'd never go back. This time I'm saying we'll come home. Different oaths. Harder one."

Pim snorted — the kid's whole commentary system is snorts and eyebrow positions — and threw a stick on the fire like a casting vote.

I stayed awake after the others rolled into their tarps, because someone had to take first watch and because my ears were ringing with the old injury and the new decision both. The sea worked the shingle. Out past the point, under all that black water, a ship sat broken with our teacher in her, and a strongbox of somebody's sins, and nine years of our unfinished business, and come morning we were going to sail straight at all of it.

I found I was smiling, which worried me. Grief does that sometimes. It gets tired of being carried and starts enjoying the weight.

First light came up gray off the escarpment, and I woke the crew, and nobody asked whose idea this had been, because by then it belonged to all of us equally, the way the worst and best things always do.

We weighed anchor on the morning tide, six of them left until the monsoon, and I caught myself counting them down like a diver counting breath.
