## 4. Six Tides

I've never trusted a calm sea. Calm is just the ocean holding its breath before it says something cruel, and I've spent thirty years learning to read the pause. So when I woke on the third morning out of port and found the water flat as poured pewter and the air thick enough to chew, I didn't think *lucky*. I thought *clock*.

The barometer had been falling since midnight—slow, polite, the way bad news arrives in a family that doesn't want to upset anyone. By dawn it had dropped twelve points and kept going, and the gulls had gone inland in one long gray ribbon, which any fool knows means the birds have read the same letter you have.

Monsoon.

I climbed to the wheelhouse with two mugs of tea because Cyrus takes his bitter enough to strip varnish and I take mine sweet enough to qualify as dessert, and I found him already there, staring south like he could out-stare a weather system if he committed hard enough.

"Barometer's in freefall," I said, handing him his mug. "Pressure's down fourteen since midnight. Wind's backing from northeast to north, which it only does when something big is organizing itself out past the shelf."

He drank. "How long?"

"That's the fun part." I spread my charts on the chart table, weighted the corners with mugs and a wrench, and showed him what I'd been working on since three a.m., because apparently sleep is for people whose pasts don't live at the bottom of the sea. "The system's coming up the coast faster than the seasonal models say it should. Warm water's feeding it. My guess—and it's an educated guess, not a hopeful one—is landfall in four days. Maybe five if we're blessed, and we are not a crew that gets blessed."

"Meaning?"

"Meaning the Kestrel site closes when the storm surge hits the escarpment. Not 'gets dangerous.' Closes. The swell wraps around that headland from three directions and turns the whole dive zone into a washing machine with a gravel bottom. Nobody works it again until the season turns. Six tides, Cyrus. We get six usable tides, and after that the wreck sleeps under forty feet of churning gray until spring."

He was quiet for a while. That was one of the things I'd always respected about him—he didn't waste breath arguing with arithmetic he couldn't beat.

"And Meridian?" he asked finally.

I tapped the second chart, the one with the fleet movements I'd been tracking through harbor gossip, AIS ghosts, and a fisherman or two who owed me favors I intended never to repay. "Their scout ship passed Cape Verren eighteen hours ago, running dark. If the main fleet weighed anchor when the scout did—which they did, because Meridian doesn't send scouts ahead for their health—they're two tides behind us. Two and a half, if their captain is cautious. He isn't. Call it two."

"So we work six tides against a fleet that arrives on tide three."

"Now you're getting it. And here's the part I love—" I said it flatly, because loving it was the only alternative to screaming about it, "—the ledgers, assuming they exist, assuming they're in the hold, assuming the hold isn't silted to the ceiling, are behind everything else. We can't cherry-pick. We have to open the wreck up properly, which means rigging, cutting, lifting, all of it done by people breathing compressed air at depth, in water cold enough to kill a careless man, on a schedule set by a storm that does not care about our feelings."

Cyrus looked at the charts for a long moment. Then he said, "How many tides do we need?"

"Eight," I said honestly. "Maybe nine, working safe."

"We have six."

"I'm aware. I did the math first. That's why I brought you tea instead of good news."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about anxiety: it wants to be motion. Left alone, it sits in your chest like a swallowed stone and grows heavier by the hour, but give it a task—a broken engine, a fouled line, a dive rig that hasn't run in six years—and it becomes fuel. I learned this young, in a machine shop in a port town whose name I've legally forgotten, and I have built an entire life on the principle.

So while Cyrus went aft to brief the others, I went below and opened the locker I'd been avoiding since we took this job.

The old dive rig sat where we'd left it, under canvas, in pieces, the way you leave a thing you swore you'd never need again. Which, come to think of it, describes most of my possessions and all of my relationships.

Six years ago, after Odile, after the oath, Tomas had stripped the rig down himself—every regulator, every manifold, every length of umbilical—and packed it away like a body prepared for burial. I'd helped. I'd tightened the last bolt on the crate myself and said, "There. Now she can't hurt anyone." And Tomas had looked at me with those diver's eyes of his, the ones that see everything at half a fathom's remove, and said nothing, which from Tomas is a full paragraph.

Now I laid the pieces out on the deck plates under the work lamp, and I'll admit it: my hands shook. Not from fear of the machinery. From what the machinery meant. Every valve was a memory; every hose coil was a year I'd spent pretending the sea was just weather and not a place where people we loved stopped existing.

Then I picked up the first regulator, and the shaking stopped, because there was work to do, and Ilsa Rask does not shake while holding a tool. That's not pride. It's architecture.

The rig was old school—surface-supplied, umbilicals down to hard-hat helmets, the kind of setup that lets you talk to your divers, warm them, keep them alive by main force when the sea tries otherwise. Modern scuba would've been lighter, faster to deploy. It also would've put Tomas and Cyrus alone in the dark with no voice, no air but what they carried, and no way for me to haul them back by hand if the current took them. On the Kestrel, at that depth, in that murk? No. If we were going back to that grave, we were going back loud, tethered, and talking. Odile would have insisted on it, and Odile wasn't there to insist, so I insisted for her.

I rebuilt her from memory and spite. Memory for the parts I knew. Spite for the parts I'd forgotten—spite being, in my experience, a more reliable adhesive than any thread compound on the market.

The compressor first. She'd sat with salt fog in her soul for six years, so I pulled the heads, honed the cylinders, lapped the valves, and reassembled her to tolerances tighter than the manufacturer ever asked for, because the manufacturer never had to trust her with the lungs of men I loved. When I spun her up at noon, she ran smooth as a hymn, and I confess I stood there with my hand on her casing like an idiot, feeling her breathe.

Then the helmets. Two of them, brass and steel, dented and patched, each one carrying scratches I could date like tree rings. This one from the Cortes bank job. That gouge from the night off Skerry Light when the current tried to peel Tomas off the hull and the helmet rim caught the ladder and saved his life, and afterward he'd bought me a drink and said "your rig held," and I'd said "my rig always holds," and neither of us had mentioned that the rig was really Odile's design, improved, and that we were both standing inside her shadow every time we dove.

I replaced every gasket. I pressure-tested every hose to twice working load and then, because anxiety likes company, tested them again overnight. I rebuilt the communications box from spare parts and soldered connections until the audio came through clean as a chapel choir. I made new umbilicals—air line, comms line, strength member—three hundred feet each, spliced and whipped by hand, because a knot tied by a machine is a knot you can't interrogate when things go wrong.

Pim came down at some point and watched me work without saying anything for a long time, which is unusual for Pim, who treats silence as a tax levied on other people.

"You haven't slept," Pim said finally.

"I slept in March."

"The rig's beautiful," Pim said, and meant it, and the compliment landed somewhere under my ribs where compliments aren't supposed to reach. "Is it weird? Rebuilding it?"

I torqued a fitting before answering, buying time. "It's honest," I said. "We swore we'd never go back. Fine. But a vow is a promise you make to who you were, and I'm not her anymore. She was scared. I'm just busy."

Pim nodded slowly, the way young people nod when they're filing something away to feel later. Then: "Do you think Odile would've wanted us to come back?"

That one I answered with the truth, because Pim deserved it and because lying to the young is how old crews rot. "Odile wanted us to survive. Everything else was negotiable. Now hand me that spanner and stop asking questions that require answers I'd have to sit down for."

* * *

By evening the rig was whole, gleaming, hungry. I ran the compressor another hour just to hear her sing, and then I went up on deck and did the thing I'd been putting off all day: I sat down with the tide tables and the weather fax and made the schedule official.

Six tides. I wrote them out in grease pencil on the chart house glass, because paper schedules can be lost, but glass has to be faced every single day.

**Tide One:** survey and descent. Find the wreck, find the hold, map the damage.
**Tide Two:** cut access. Open the hull above the cargo space.
**Tide Three:** recover the ledgers.
**Tide Four:** contingency. Anything that goes wrong in the first three gets fixed here.
**Tide Five:** contingency for the contingency.
**Tide Six:** final recovery and withdrawal, with the monsoon's leading edge already chewing the horizon.

And somewhere in there, sliding in behind us like a knife between ribs: Meridian. Two tides back. Their scout running dark, their fleet heavy with divers and lift gear and lawyers, their interest in the Kestrel exactly as innocent as a shark's interest in blood.

I stood looking at the glass until the numbers blurred, and I did the math I'd been refusing to do all day. Six tides of work. Eight needed. A fleet two tides behind. An informant somewhere ashore—I didn't know that yet, wouldn't know for days—but even then I felt it, the way you feel a change in pressure before the instruments confirm it. Something in the pattern was wrong. Our route had been too easy to predict. Our timing, too visible.

You spend enough years running, you develop a sense for being hunted. It sits in the back of the skull like a low-grade headache. Mine had been flaring since we took Maren Voss's money, and I'd been telling myself it was nerves.

Nerves don't track your position. People do.

I wiped my hands on my rag, took up the grease pencil, and added a seventh line to the glass, small, in the corner, where only I would read it:

**Tide Zero: figure out who's selling us.**

Then I capped the marker, went below, and woke the compressor for one more test run—not because she needed it, but because the sound of her working was the only prayer I knew, and tomorrow the real work began, and I intended to arrive at it having already argued with God and won.

Behind us, far to the south, the horizon had gone the color of a bruise deciding whether to bloom.
