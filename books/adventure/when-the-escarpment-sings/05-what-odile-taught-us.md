## 5. What Odile Taught Us

I never met Odile Marchetti. This is a fact I have carried like a stone in my boot for three years now, ever since I joined the crew of the *Salt Lark* and realized that everything we did—the way Cyrus checked a mooring line twice, the way Tomas spat over the rail before every dive, the way Ilsa swore at engines in a language that wasn't quite any language—was liturgy. A church built around a woman who was already dead when I arrived, and nobody handed me the hymnal.

So on the third day of the coastal trek to the Kestrel site, with the escarpment rising red and sheer on our left and the sea doing its gray, patient work on our right, I finally asked.

"Tell me about her."

The question landed in the middle of the trail like a dropped anchor. Tomas stopped walking. Ilsa didn't stop walking, which was its own kind of answer. And Cyrus, who was out front reading the tide pools like scripture, went very still in that way he has, the stillness of a man deciding whether to answer or simply become scenery.

"Which part?" he said at last, not turning around.

"All of it. Any of it. You all talk about her like she's standing right behind us, but nobody's ever actually told me anything. Was she tall? Did she laugh? Could she cook? I've been crew for three years and I know she died on the Kestrel and I know you don't go back there, and that's it. That's the whole biography."

The wind came up off the water and pushed at us, the way it does along this coast, as if the escarpment itself were trying to herd us somewhere. Nobody said anything for a hundred paces. I counted them, because counting is what I do instead of panicking.

Then Tomas laughed—a short, surprised sound, like he'd stepped on something soft.

"She was short," he said. "Shorter than me, and I'm not a tall man. Dark hair going gray at the temples by the time I met her, always tied back with whatever was handy. Once it was a fishhook. Actual fishhook, barb filed down. She said hair ties sink."

"That's not—" Ilsa had stopped after all, twenty paces back, arms crossed. "That's not how I'd start."

"How would you start?"

"I'd start with the fact that she was the best engineer I ever saw, and I include myself, which costs me something to say." Ilsa's jaw did the thing it does when she's admitting something expensive. "She could hear an engine lie. You'd bring her a compressor running sweet as Sunday and she'd put one hand flat on the housing and say, 'Bearing's going, two weeks,' and she'd be right to the day. To the *day*, Pim."

Tomas snorted. "She couldn't navigate worth a damn, though."

"She could navigate fine."

"On water, maybe. Put her on land and she'd argue with a map until the map apologized. We lost half a day outside Port Callas because Odile decided the cartographer had drawn the river wrong. She was right about the river. She was wrong about everything else, including where we were."

I looked from one to the other. This was already not matching up. The Odile in Tomas's mouth was small and practical and slightly ridiculous. The Odile in Ilsa's mouth was a prophet with grease under her nails. Neither of them sounded like the ghost whose name made Cyrus go quiet mid-sentence, and I said so.

"You're both describing different people."

They glanced at each other, and something passed between them—not annoyance, exactly. More like two people who had agreed long ago not to compare notes.

"Everybody gets their own Odile," Tomas said, and started walking again.

* * *

We camped that night in the lee of a collapsed watchtower, one of the old consortium signal stations that dotted the escarpment like rotten teeth. Ilsa got a fire going small and smokeless, the way she does, and Cyrus sat apart with his back to a stone, mending a strap, listening without appearing to listen. He was very good at appearing not to listen. Three years of practice had taught me that Cyrus Eriksen heard everything.

"So," I said, settling next to Tomas, who was cleaning his dive knife with the tenderness other men reserve for lovers or children. "Your Odile. Tell me a story."

He considered the blade. "First time I dove with her, I panicked at forty feet. Mask flooded, silt everywhere, and I shot for the surface like a cork. She caught my ankle at fifteen feet and just—held me there. Didn't drag me down, didn't let me up. Just held on until I stopped fighting. Then she pointed at my gauge, pointed at my mask, cleared it herself, and let go. Didn't say a word till we surfaced. Then she said, 'The water will wait for you. It's the only thing that will.'"

"That's a good story."

"It's true, too. That's the part that gets me." He sheathed the knife. "Everything else about her, I'm not sure anymore. But that—I can feel her hand on my ankle. Fifteen feet down. Cold water, June. That happened."

Across the fire, Ilsa made a noise that might have been agreement or indigestion.

"What?" I asked her.

"Nothing. It's just—" She fed a twig to the flames. "She told me a version of that story once. About catching a panicking diver. Except in her version, the diver was Cyrus. And it wasn't fifteen feet, it was eighty, and it was on the Kestrel, and it was six weeks before she died."

Tomas went still. "That's not possible. My dive was before the Kestrel. Before any of us knew the Kestrel existed."

"Then one of you is misremembering," Ilsa said gently, "or she told the same story twice and changed the details, or—" she shrugged— "or she liked the story enough to keep it."

"And you're sure it was Cyrus?"

"Cyrus confirmed it. Once. Drunk, off the record, in a harbor bar in a country whose name I've agreed to forget." She smiled without humor. "He said she held him at eighty feet for four minutes on his own air because he'd blown through his reserve chasing a survey instrument into the wreck. Four minutes, Pim. She burned through her own tank holding him. When they surfaced she told him the same line. 'The water will wait for you.' And then she fined him a month's pay for wasting salvage gas."

Tomas stared into the fire for a long moment. "Huh," he said, in the voice of a man watching a load-bearing wall develop a crack.

* * *

Cyrus took second watch, which meant I did too, because I sleep badly on land and worse near the Kestrel's longitude. We sat on the tower's fallen lintel with the sea below us making its endless argument against the rocks.

"Ask me," Cyrus said eventually. He didn't look at me. "You've been circling it for three days. Ask."

"What was she to you?"

He was quiet long enough that I thought he wouldn't answer. Below us, something in the dark water boomed against the escarpment base, patient as a clock.

"She found me," he said. "When I was nineteen. Half-starved, working the docks, lying about my age and my certifications and my name—all three, in that order of shamelessness. Everyone else saw a liar. Odile saw a boy who lied well enough to survive, and decided that was a skill worth training instead of punishing." A dry almost-laugh. "She hired me on the spot. Told the harbormaster I was her nephew. I wasn't. I don't think she even had a nephew. I think she invented one the way she invented half her paperwork—because the truth was inconvenient and the lie was useful."

"That doesn't sound like the woman Ilsa describes."

"No. Ilsa got the engineer. Tomas got the diver. You'd have gotten—" he paused, weighing— "the teacher, probably. She gave each of us what we needed. Or maybe we each just took what we could carry, and called it her."

"Is that what memory is? Taking what you can carry?"

Cyrus turned and looked at me then, and in the firelight from below his face had that weathered, unreadable quality it gets when he's deciding how honest to be. He decided on mostly.

"Memory is salvage," he said. "You pull up what survived the water, and you patch it, and you use it, and after enough years you can't tell which parts were original hull and which parts are your repairs. Doesn't make the boat less real. Makes it yours, though. Every person who loved her is sailing a different ship named Odile, and none of them is wrong, and none of them is hers."

"That's bleak."

"That's the trade." He stood, stretched, rolled his shoulders against the wind. "Get some sleep if you can. Tomorrow we cross the scree fields, and the day after, you'll see her grave."

I hadn't thought of it that way. The Kestrel, sitting on the bottom in however many fathoms of cold green dark—that was a grave, and we were walking toward it, and tomorrow night or the night after we'd be diving into it. I said so, more bluntly than I meant to.

Cyrus looked out at the black water.

"The dead don't mind being visited," he said. "It's the living who mind what the visit costs."

* * *

The scree fields took all of the fourth day, and somewhere in the middle of them, limbing down a slope of loose red stone with the sea thundering far below, Ilsa started talking without being asked. That's how I learned the third Odile.

"She saved my life twice," Ilsa said, breath ragged, eyes on her footing. "Once literally—a winch cable parted and she pulled me clear by the collar, tore her shoulder doing it, never mentioned it, I found out from the medic. Second time—" she hopped a gap, landed hard— "second time was the year before the Kestrel. I'd gotten an offer. Legitimate yard work, dry dock, steady pay, no blacklist, no running. Good money. I was going to take it."

"What stopped you?"

"She bought me a drink and told me I'd hate it." Ilsa laughed, short and sharp. "Said, 'You'll be safe, and warm, and bored, and in four years you'll hate yourself so thoroughly you'll come find me and apologize, and I'll charge you for the drink.' So I stayed. And then she died, and I stayed anyway, because leaving after that would have felt like—" She stopped. Scree slid away under her boot and rattled down two hundred feet of nothing. "Like agreeing with the people who killed her."

"Tomas says she drowned saving someone. You say she died on the wreck. Which was it?"

Ilsa was quiet for several steps. The wind pushed at us, insistent, herding.

"Nobody knows," she said finally. "That's the thing nobody says out loud. The Kestrel went down in a squall with eleven aboard. Six bodies came up. Odile wasn't one of them. There are three stories about her last hour and every one of them comes from a survivor who was half-blind with spray and terror, and every one of them makes her die differently. Tomas needs her to have died saving someone. Cyrus needs her to have died knowing something. I need her to have died working—engine room, hands on the problem, right to the end." She wiped her face with her sleeve; sweat or spray, impossible to tell. "Maybe she did all three. Maybe none. The sea kept the receipt, Pim, and the sea doesn't give refunds."

* * *

We made camp that night within sight of the cove. I could see it from the ridge: a gray bite in the coastline, sheltered, unremarkable, except for the way every member of the crew looked at it and then carefully looked away, the way you don't stare at a scar on someone you love.

Tomas sat down beside me at the fire. He'd been quiet since the scree fields, turning something over.

"You want the last piece?" he said. "The one nobody tells you?"

"If you're offering."

"Odile knew the Kestrel was going to be trouble. Before they ever launched her. She read the consortium's survey contracts—she read everything, that woman read ballast manifests for pleasure—and she told Cyrus, 'This ship is going to find something somebody wants lost, and when it does, everyone aboard becomes a loose end.'" He poked the fire. "Cyrus wanted to refuse the contract. Odile talked him into taking it. Said the only way to know what the consortium was hiding was to be inside the hull when they started hiding it."

"Why would she say that? Why walk into it?"

"Because she was going to copy the ledgers herself. Build a dead-drop, hide the evidence, make it survivable for whoever came after." Tomas's voice dropped. "That's the version Cyrus believes. That she died mid-task. That there's something down there she left for us, and that's why he's breaking the oath. Not Maren's money. Not Meridian. He thinks she left instructions."

"Do you believe it?"

Tomas looked at me across the fire, and behind him, at the edge of the light, Cyrus sat awake against his pack, watching the cove, hearing everything, saying nothing.

"I believe she was the smartest person I ever knew," Tomas said. "And I believe the smartest thing she ever taught us was that the water keeps what it's given. Everything else—" he spread his hands— "everything else is just the shape the grief froze into."

I lay back and watched the stars wheel over the escarpment, and I thought about five people carrying five different women named Odile down this coast, and how tomorrow we would set all of them down on the bottom of the sea together, and only one of them could be true.

Behind me, I heard Cyrus shift, and speak—quietly, not quite to me, not quite to himself:

"She also said the ledgers would be in the hold. Under the false floor. Third plank from the frame."

I sat up. "You never told us that."

"I'm telling you now," Cyrus said, and his voice had an edge on it I'd never heard, old rage coming up through the years like a wreck through fog. "Because today I remembered something else. Something I haven't let myself remember since she died." He looked at me across the fire, and his eyes were terrible and bright. "Pim—she told me where the ledgers were *six weeks before the Kestrel ever sank.*"

The fire cracked. Out in the dark, the sea went on singing against the escarpment, patient as ever, keeping what it was given.

"And I have spent ten years," Cyrus said, "not asking myself how she knew."
