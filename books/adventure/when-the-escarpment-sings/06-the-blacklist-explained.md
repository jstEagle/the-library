## 6. The Blacklist Explained

There is a particular kind of silence that lives in a boat when everyone aboard knows where you're going and nobody wants to say it out loud. I'd been listening to it for two days. It sat in the wheelhouse with me like a third crew member, heavy, damp, smelling faintly of old rope.

The Kestrel lay forty fathoms down off the escarpment's eastern shoulder, under six tides' worth of borrowed time. Behind us, somewhere past the horizon, Meridian's fleet was burning fuel to get there. Ahead of us was the water that had taken Odile.

And between us and all of it stretched eleven years of my own name being worth less than bilge water in two countries, which seemed as good a time as any to explain myself — if only to the fog rolling off the cliffs, since my crew had heard most of it before and Pim had learned to stop asking.

"You ever wonder," I said to the fog, "why I don't have a license anymore?"

The fog didn't answer. Fog never does. That's why I like it.

* * *

The consortium — Halvard Shipping Combine, though they preferred "the Combine" the way a knife prefers to be called a letter opener — ran the escarpment coast for thirty years before anyone thought to look at their books. They moved ore, timber, contraband, and occasionally whole ships that were insured for more than they were worth. A vessel with bad cargo and good paperwork could earn more at the bottom of the sea than she ever earned afloat. The trick was making sure the right people signed the survey reports saying the sinking was an accident.

Survey guides did the signing. That was us. Odile trained half the licensed guides on this coast, and her signature carried weight in three port authorities. If Odile Vantol said a hull failed, then the hull had failed, and no adjuster alive wanted to argue with her.

I was twenty-six. I thought a reputation was armor.

The night the *Corvane* went down with a hold full of "grain," I was the junior guide on her final survey. My report said the grain. My report also said the hull plating forward of the number two bulkhead showed stress fracturing consistent with overloading, which was not the kind of thing you wrote about a Combine ship unless you wanted your mail opened for the rest of your life. I wrote it anyway, because Odile taught me that a report was a promise: whoever read it deserved the truth, even the inconvenient parts.

Three weeks later the *Corvane*'s sister ship sank the same way. Then another. And the ledgers — the ones I would spend eleven years not knowing the contents of — began to matter.

Here's what I understood at the time, which was almost nothing: someone inside the Combine kept accounts. Real ones. Not the books they showed the port authorities, but the true ledger — every bribed inspector, every overloaded hull, every captain paid to scuttle, every guide bought or blackmailed into signing fiction. A record so complete it was less a document than a weapon with a spine. Whoever held those ledgers could end the Combine with a single posting. The Combine knew it existed. They hunted it the way you hunt a rat in the walls: poison everywhere, sleep nowhere.

They found a copy. Or part of one. And instead of destroying it, they did something cleverer.

They used it to build a story.

* * *

The tribunal was held in Karst Harbor, in a room that smelled of varnish and wet wool, with a panel of five men who each owned something the Combine wanted or owed the Combine something they couldn't pay. I sat at a table bolted to the floor, which struck me even then as a strange precaution against a man accused of sabotage by sea. Perhaps they feared I'd tip the room over.

The charge was straightforward: that Cyrus Eriksen, guide, had falsified survey records on the *Corvane*, concealing deliberate structural sabotage commissioned by parties unknown, resulting in the loss of the vessel and eleven souls. The evidence was a set of ledger pages — genuine pages, torn from a genuine book, in genuine ink — showing payments made to "C. Eriksen, survey services" across fourteen months. Fourteen entries. Sums large enough to buy a boat outright.

I had never seen the money. But the handwriting was mine.

That was the detail that broke me, sitting there at the bolted table. Not the amounts, not the dates — the *hand*. Someone had forged my script so well that I stared at those pages and felt the ground tilt, because I recognized the way I crossed my sevens, the little hook I put on the tail of a k. Only two people alive knew I wrote my k's that way. One was dead. The other was in the room.

Maren Voss testified on the second day.

She'd been Odile's other protégé, back when we were young enough to think rivalry was a game. She stood at the witness rail in a gray coat, hands folded, voice level, and told the panel she had seen me accept payment from a Combine broker in the back room of the Salt Anchor tavern. She gave a date. She gave a time. She described the broker's coat, his ring, the way he counted the notes twice.

Every word of it was false. I know because I spent the next decade checking — quietly, obsessively, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. The broker she named hadn't been within four hundred miles of Karst Harbor on that date; I found his death notice eventually, which closed that door politely. The Salt Anchor's back room had been sealed for renovations that entire month; I found the renovation permit, yellowed and filed, and sat in a records office holding it like a pardon that wouldn't speak.

But tribunals don't run on permits. They run on testimony, and Maren's was clean, confident, and delivered by a woman whose own record was spotless. When the chairman asked whether she was certain, she said, "I am certain," and looked at me while she said it, and I have replayed that look ten thousand times trying to find in it what wasn't there.

What wasn't there, I believed for eleven years, was doubt.

They took my license. Then Karst revoked my mooring rights. Then the neighboring coast — different country, same Combine money in the same comfortable pockets — flagged my papers, and a flag follows a man like gull after gutting. Within a year I couldn't sign a survey, charter a berth, or buy insurance without a broker suddenly remembering an appointment. Blacklisting isn't a wall. It's weather. It's everywhere and touches everything and you can't punch it.

So I did what the coast taught me. I went around.

Salvage doesn't require licenses if you never file claims. Wrecks don't ask for papers. A crew that works outside the law's memory can work anywhere the law forgot to look, and the escarpment coast has more forgotten places than remembered ones. I gathered Tomas out of a debtors' brig, Ilsa out of a shipyard that fired her for rebuilding engines they'd condemned, and later Pim out of a cliff village that had stopped feeding its spotters. We became excellent at being invisible. Invisible pays badly, but it pays honestly, which was the only currency I had left.

And through all of it, one question sat in my chest like a swallowed anchor: *what else was in those ledgers?*

Because the pages they'd shown the tribunal weren't the whole book. You could tell — the edges were cut unevenly, the pagination jumped from 40-something to 200-something. They'd shown the panel a chapter and burned the rest, or buried it, or locked it away. Either way, the full account of what the Combine had done — every sunk ship, every bought man, every guide framed the way I was framed — existed somewhere, and I had spent eleven years not knowing whether my name appeared in it once or a hundred times.

Turns out I was going to find out. Forty fathoms down. On a clock.

* * *

The escarpment rose out of the morning haze to starboard, red-brown and patient, the way it always does — like a thing that has watched every ship that ever lied about itself and intends to keep watching. Ilsa came up beside me at the wheel, wiping engine grease onto a rag that had given up being a rag years ago.

"You're doing the face," she said.

"What face?"

"The one where you're talking to weather. You do it when you're chewing on the old stuff." She leaned her elbows on the rail. "Tomas does the same thing. Different face. He goes quiet and checks his gear four times."

"He checked it six times yesterday."

"We're all handling it our own way. Pim asked me last night if Odile was real or a story we invented to scare ourselves straight." Ilsa shrugged. "I told her both."

The wind came off the cliffs carrying salt and something green, the smell of the deep water beyond the shelf. Two days of running had put the site half a tide closer, and Meridian's fleet half a tide closer behind us, and none of that arithmetic improved with staring.

"I never told you the whole of it," I said. "The tribunal. What Maren did."

"You told us enough." Ilsa didn't look at me. "You told us a woman you trusted sold you to save herself, and you've hated her cleanly ever since, because hating her cleanly is easier than asking whether she had reasons." She picked a fleck of rust off the rail. "Tomas thinks I'm too soft on you. I think you're too easy on yourself, hiding in the hate. Hate keeps a man warm. It just doesn't feed him."

"That's very philosophical for someone with grease to her elbows."

"I contain multitudes. Most of them are grease." She pushed off the rail. "For what it's worth — I hope the ledgers say what you need them to say. And I hope you're ready for what they actually say. Those are rarely the same document."

She went below. Ilsa has a gift for leaving a room at exactly the moment the room becomes unbearable.

* * *

We raised the marker buoy at dusk — Tomas's buoy, dropped on the first pass, riding the swell like a patient headstone. Beyond it the water went from green to a blue so dark it looked solid, and somewhere under that solidity lay the Kestrel, and under the question of the Kestrel lay the older question, the one I'd carried since Karst Harbor.

Eleven years I'd built my life on a single sentence: *they framed me.* It was a load-bearing sentence. Take it out and the whole structure asks uncomfortable questions — like why I never went back, why I never fought the tribunal's finding publicly, why I let two countries believe a forgery rather than stand in a square and shout the truth until someone listened.

The honest answer, the one I kept below the waterline, was fear. Not of the Combine. Of the possibility that the frame was real. That some tired, broke version of me had taken Combine money and buried it so deep even I couldn't find it. Rage is a comfort that way — it lets you skip the audit of yourself.

Tomorrow I'd dive into the actual answer. Whatever the ledgers held, they held it in ink, in dates, in sums — facts, which are crueler than any tribunal because they can't be coached.

Pim appeared at the wheelhouse door, chart roll under one arm, hair full of spray.

"Cyrus. Tide tables say we've got slack water at first light. Tomas wants to go down on the first one." A pause. "He also says — he says if we see anything down there that looks like it shouldn't be disturbed, we leave it. His words. He was looking at you when he said it."

Meaning: Odile's cabin. Meaning: the door.

"Aye," I said. "Tell him we'll honor it."

Pim lingered. Young enough still to ask the questions the rest of us had learned to swallow. "Is it true what they say? That you lost everything because of one book?"

"Not one book," I said. "Half of one. The half they showed the judges." I turned back to the darkening water. "The other half is down there, Pim. And tomorrow we find out whether I've spent eleven years running from a lie — or from the truth."

Out past the buoy, the first leading edge of the monsoon flickered along the horizon, silent and slow, like a fuse deciding how long to burn.
