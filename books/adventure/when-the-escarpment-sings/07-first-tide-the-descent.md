## 7. First Tide: The Descent

There is a moment on every dive, just before the water takes you, when your body files a complaint with management. The lungs say *no*. The heart says *absolutely not*. The hindbrain, that ancient clerk who has been keeping the books since before we had names, stamps the whole enterprise REJECTED and suggests a nice warm fire instead.

I have learned to treat that moment like an old colleague who complains about everything but never actually quits. I nod at him politely, check my regulator twice, and step off the gunwale.

The Kestrel was waiting for us forty fathoms down, which is to say she wasn't waiting at all, because ships don't wait, they just stop, and the difference between those two things is the whole reason I was shaking by the time I hit the water.

* * *

Let me back up.

We'd anchored over the site at slack water before dawn, in that gray hour when the escarpment coast looks less like geography and more like a rumor of itself. Ilsa had spent the night re-rigging the compressor with the tenderness other people reserve for infants or firearms, and Pim had stood at the bow for three hours straight, watching the depth sounder like it might lie to him if he blinked.

"Forty-one fathoms," he'd said, for the fourth time. "The charts said thirty-six."

"The charts are five years old," Ilsa said. "The sea doesn't file amendments."

Cyrus said nothing. That was the thing about Cyrus — he could fill a room with silence the way other men filled it with shouting. He stood at the rail with his arms folded, looking down at the water like it owed him money, and I thought: *he's counting tides too.* We all were. Six tides, Ilsa had told us, before the monsoon closed the site like a fist. Meridian's fleet two tides behind us. Which meant every dive from here on out was borrowed time, spent at interest.

Tomas checks his gear. Tomas complains about his gear. Tomas dives anyway. That's the job description; I wrote it myself, in the sense that nobody else would have me.

"Bottom time's going to be short," Ilsa warned, running through my harness buckles with her fingers because she didn't trust mine, and she was right not to. "Forty-one fathoms, cold water, current off the point. Twenty minutes of work if you're lucky. Thirty if you're stupid."

"I'm always both," I said.

"That's what worries me."

Cyrus came over and did the thing he does instead of good luck, which is grip your shoulder once, hard, like he's testing whether you'll hold under load. His eyes were doing something complicated. Grief, maybe, wearing a work shirt.

"You remember the layout?" he asked.

"No," I said. It was mostly true. "I remember pieces. The corridor ran fore to aft. The hold was amidships, port side. And—" I stopped.

"And?"

"And there was a door," I said. "At the end of the crew quarters. Port side, last one. I don't want to talk about it yet."

Cyrus looked at me for a long moment, and whatever he saw made him decide not to push. "Twenty minutes," he said instead. "You see anything you don't like, you come up. No heroics. Odile's rules."

Nobody had said her name out loud since the vote at the fire. Saying it now was like dropping a stone into still water — we all watched the ripples go out and pretended they didn't reach us.

"Yeah," I said. "Odile's rules."

Then I stepped off the gunwale, and the sea took me, and the complaining stopped, because below the surface there's no room for complaints. There's only breath and pressure and the slow blue closing-in of the world.

* * *

Descending on the Kestrel's anchor line was like sinking through someone's memory of the ocean rather than the ocean itself. The light went from silver to pewter to a bruised violet, and the temperature dropped so fast my teeth ached behind the mouthpiece. At twenty fathoms the current found me and pushed sideways, patient as a debt collector, and I hooked my elbow around the line and let it argue with itself.

The wreck appeared the way wrecks do — not all at once but in pieces, as though the sea were dealing her out card by card. First the masthead, snapped and lying across the sand like a fallen tree. Then a rail, furred white with plumose anemones, waving gently in a current that hadn't decided where it belonged. Then the hull itself, listing hard to starboard, its plates peeled back along the impact scar where the reef had opened her up.

She was worse than I remembered.

That sounds obvious — five years underwater, of course she's worse — but memory is a liar with a soft heart. In my head the Kestrel was broken but dignified, resting on the bottom like a woman asleep. What lay below me was a carcass. The bow section had collapsed inward, deck plating folded like wet cardboard. Silt had drifted through her in dunes, burying the deck to knee height in places, swallowing whole rooms. A trawl net had wrapped her midships sometime in the intervening years, dragging a curtain of it across the superstructure, and dead fish bones hung in the mesh like terrible decorations.

And she was deeper. Forty-one fathoms against the thirty-six I carried in my bones. The extra five fathoms meant colder, darker, shorter bottom time, and a longer, lonelier ascent. The sea had moved the goalposts while we weren't looking, and it hadn't told anyone.

I leveled off above the wreck and hung there a moment, breathing hard through the regulator, letting the narcosis whisper its usual nonsense at the edge of my mind. At this depth the nitrogen gets into your blood and starts editing your thoughts — makes you sentimental, makes you brave in the wrong ways, makes you trust things you shouldn't. Old divers call it being narked. I call it meeting yourself without supervision.

Below me, through the torn-open section of hull, the interior of the Kestrel waited like a held breath.

I checked my watch. Nineteen minutes of usable bottom time. I gave the signal — thumb down, then flat hand toward the breach — and dropped toward the deck.

* * *

Inside, the world narrowed to a torch beam and the sound of my own breathing, which is the loudest thing in the universe when it's the only thing you can hear.

The corridor ran fore to aft, exactly as I remembered, except that memory had flattered it. The overhead had partially collapsed about ten meters in, and I had to pull myself through a gap barely wider than my shoulders, past a door that had become a wall, past the ghost of a bulkhead number stenciled in paint that the sea was slowly eating. My bubbles went up ahead of me and flattened against the overhead and slid along it like nervous animals looking for a way out.

I know what you're thinking: why do it? Why crawl inside a drowned ship? And the honest answer is that I don't fully know. Part of it is the work — Cyrus needed a survey of what was intact, what was accessible, whether the hold could be reached at all before the monsoon buried everything under six months of stirred-up silt. Part of it is that salvage gets into your blood the way salt does, and once it's there you can't sweat it out.

But part of it — the part I don't say out loud — is that some doors you have to walk through eventually, or you spend the rest of your life standing in front of them.

The crew quarters were amidships, port side. I knew it before my torch confirmed it, knew it by the way the corridor narrowed, by the angle of the light, by something older than knowing. The compartment doors stood open as they always had — ship's custom, doors dogged back during the day — and the silt had drifted in and lain across the deck like snowfall in an abandoned house.

The first cabin was empty in the way that means *emptied*: a bunk frame stripped bare, a locker hanging open, nothing left but the shape of a life pressed into the silt. The second held a desk bolted to the deck, its drawers long since swollen shut, a chair drifting loose and tethered by its own rot. In the third, someone's boot — just one — stood upright in the silt, sole up, patient as a grave marker.

I photographed each one because that was the job, and my hands were steady because that was the training, and neither of those facts had anything to do with what was happening behind my faceplate.

Four cabins. Five. The corridor bent slightly to follow the curve of the hull, and I followed it, and my breathing got louder, and the nitrogen whispered, *you know this place, you've been here before, you never really left.*

The last door was at the end. Port side. Just as I'd told Cyrus.

It was closed.

Every other door on that deck stood open to the silt and the dark, but this one was shut, and shut doors on wrecks mean one of two things: either the sea sealed it, or somebody closed it. And I knew — the way you know things down there, without evidence, without argument — that this one had been closed by hands.

Her hands.

Odile's cabin. Odile, who taught me that the first rule of diving is that the sea doesn't hate you, it just doesn't notice you, and that the trick is to be worth noticing. Odile, who died inside this ship while I was topside arguing about air shares, because I'd insisted we run the survey shallow-first and she'd overruled me, and the collapse came early, and by the time we cut through to the crew quarters the water in her lungs had already gone still.

They never recovered her. The site was condemned, the consortium pulled its flags, and the Kestrel became a name on a list of losses nobody audited. We swore our oath on the beach afterward — never come back, never dig here, let the sea keep what it took — and for five years I'd kept that oath the way you keep a wound clean: carefully, painfully, and without ever once forgetting it was there.

Now I hung in the corridor outside her door, forty-one fathoms down, with nineteen minutes of air and no excuse left in the world.

My torch beam sat on the door like a hand. Steel, gray-green with growth, the latch a simple lever type, the kind you can open with a gloved knuckle. The hinges faced me. Everything about it said *easy*. Everything about it said *just turn the handle, Tomas, it's been five years, she isn't behind it, nothing is behind it, it's a room full of water like every other room.*

My body filed its complaint. The lungs said *no*. The heart said *absolutely not*. The old clerk stamped REJECTED and suggested, very reasonably, that I ascend immediately and report the quarters inaccessible, and nobody would ever know, and I could keep my oath and my sleep and the careful fiction that the past was somewhere else.

But the past isn't somewhere else. That's the thing I understood down there, in the cold and the blue and the loudness of my own breath. The past isn't past. It's just underwater. It sits exactly where you left it, holding its position against the current, waiting with the patience of pressure itself, and sooner or later every diver swims back down to it, because buoyancy always wins in the end.

I reached out.

My glove touched the latch. Cold steel through neoprene, real as a heartbeat. Somewhere behind me my bubbles ticked up along the overhead, counting seconds I couldn't spare, and my watch face glowed its small green accusation: fourteen minutes remaining, and the hold still unsurveyed, and Cyrus expecting numbers, and the tide turning in ninety minutes whether I found my courage or not.

I put my knuckle to the lever.

And stopped.

Because through the steel, faint as a thought, I felt it — a vibration. Not the current. Not the groan of the hull settling, which has a sound like a whale's grief, low and long. This was sharper. Rhythmic. Mechanical. Coming from inside the cabin, or beyond it, or through the hull from somewhere I couldn't name — a knocking, almost, regular as a pump, wrong in every way a thing can be wrong at the bottom of the sea.

Wrecks don't knock.

I hung there with my hand on the lever of a dead woman's door, listening to something knock, and my air gauge said thirteen minutes, and the blue dark behind me said *ascend*, and the door said nothing at all, the way doors do, keeping whatever it kept.

I thought about Odile's voice, calm as ballast: *the sea doesn't hate you, Tomas. It just doesn't notice you. Be worth noticing.*

I turned the lever.

The door swung inward with a groan of seized hinges, spilling my torch beam into the room — and the beam landed on the far bulkhead, where scratched into the growth-crusted steel, fresh enough that the metal beneath still shone, were four words:

**WE KNOW YOU'RE HERE**

Behind me, in the black throat of the corridor, something knocked again.
