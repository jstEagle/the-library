## 8. Meridian's Shadow

I've always said the sea tells you everything if you're willing to listen like a coward. Most people listen like heroes—they hear what they want to hear. Heroes hear opportunity in a change of wind. Cowards hear a funeral. That's why Cyrus keeps me on the *Magpie*: not because I'm the best engineer on this coast, though I am, but because I am professionally unwilling to be comforted.

So when I climbed the escarpment path at dawn with the tide tables under my arm and saw the smudge on the horizon that wasn't weather, I didn't think "fishing boat." Fishing boats have shapes. Fishing boats have rust streaks and laundry strung on rails and the general air of vessels that have given up on dignity. This smudge had none of those things. This smudge had the particular nothing-shape of something trying very hard to be horizon.

I stood there for a full minute, letting my eyes argue with themselves, and then I went and woke Tomas, because waking Tomas is faster than waking Cyrus and Cyrus needs his sleep to maintain his tragic jawline.

"Ship out there," I said. "No lights last night."

Tomas squinted at the water the way he does before a dive, like he's negotiating terms with it. "Could be anything."

"It could," I agreed. "It could also be a Meridian scout running dark, sitting six miles off our site like a gull that's read our mail."

He looked at me. I looked at him. Somewhere below us, Pim was already scrambling up the switchbacks with the binoculars, because Pim hears the word *ship* the way dogs hear the word *walk*.

* * *

By midmorning we had confirmation, or as close to confirmation as the sea ever gives you politely. The vessel—hull unmarked, superstructure low, lines too clean for any honest trade—had closed to about four miles and then stopped. Just stopped. Anchored, probably, though no anchor light showed, which is illegal in these waters and every other water I've ever worked. A ship that doesn't announce itself isn't fishing. It isn't lost. It's watching.

"They know we're here," Pim said from the cliff watch, flat and certain, and the worst part was that he wasn't scared. He sounded almost relieved, the way you do when the thing you've been dreading finally shows its face and you can stop dreading it and start hating it instead.

Cyrus stood at the rail of the *Magpie* with his arms folded, doing his impression of a man considering options, which is actually an impression of a man who made his option three minutes ago and is waiting for us to catch up.

"Ilsa," he said. "How long do we have?"

"If they're waiting for the fleet? Two tides, maybe three. If they're waiting for dark?" I shrugged, and hated how casual I made it sound. "Depends how patient they are. Patience is expensive. Somebody's paying for theirs."

"So we make patience more expensive."

That's the thing about Cyrus Eriksen that people who only know the blacklist don't understand: the man doesn't fight. Fighting is for people with something to prove. Cyrus *inconveniences*. He makes the world slightly more annoying for anyone hunting him until the hunters give up and go home, and by the time they've gone home they usually can't explain why. It's not glamorous. It has kept all of us alive, which is more than glamour ever did.

"Decoys," I said, and went below to build lies.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about salvage work: half of it is metal and rope and the other half is theater. Every rig worth its salt carries transponders, marker buoys, signal lamps, and enough spare electronics to make a customs officer weep. We had all of it, plus the habits of people who've spent years being hunted, which means we had duplicates of things we'd never officially needed.

I started with the simplest lie: sound. Meridian's scouts would be listening on hydrophones for the signature of dive operations—the compressor cycling, the tender engine, the metallic clatter of gear on deck. So I built them a different operation, two miles north along the escarpment shelf. An old buoy hull, gutted, fitted with a timer and a small speaker rig I'd been meaning to cannibalize for three seasons and never had the heart to. Set it to cycle compressor sounds at intervals matching a real dive rotation. Add a second buoy playing back tender-engine noise, timed to arrive and depart like a boat ferrying divers. Crude. But crude works on tired listeners at four miles in choppy water, and Meridian's people would be tired, because watching someone for days without result is the most exhausting job on the ocean and I say that as a woman who once rebuilt a transmission in a flooding bilge.

The second lie was light. At dusk I hung a work lamp on the northern cliff ledge where it would be visible from seaward, rigged to flicker in the arrhythmic way work lamps flicker when someone's actually working under them. Real work lamps flicker because generators are temperamental. Fake ones flicker because Ilsa says so. From four miles out, in bad light, through salt haze? Indistinguishable. I've staked my life on less distinguishable.

The third lie was the one I was proudest of, and it's the one that kept me up that night, staring at the overhead of my bunk, doing arithmetic I didn't want to do.

Because here's the problem with decoys: they only work if the enemy can't tell where you really are. And to know whether they could tell, I needed to know what they knew. And what they knew, when I laid it out on the chart table with grease pencil and cold coffee, was wrong in a specific way.

They were sitting off the site. Not off the decoy site—off the real one. They'd been holding position over the Kestrel since before dawn, before we'd run a single dive light, before the compressor had turned over once that morning. Which meant they hadn't followed our noise. They hadn't tracked our lights. They had come to these coordinates the way you come to coordinates written down for you.

I put the pencil down. I picked it up. I drew a circle around their position, then a line back to shore, then sat there looking at the coastline like it owed me money.

"We've been sold," I said to the chart table, because the chart table doesn't argue.

* * *

Let me tell you about paranoia, since I'm apparently an expert now. Paranoia isn't fear. Fear is clean; fear says *run*. Paranoia is arithmetic. Paranoia sits down with a list of every person who knows your position and starts crossing names off, and every name it crosses off takes a little piece of you with it, because each one is somebody who fed you soup when you were sick, or pulled you out of a current, or laughed at your terrible jokes at two in the morning because they were too tired to pretend they weren't terrible.

Our circle was small. That was the whole point of the crew. Small circles are hard to sell.

Shore-side, the circle got bigger. Charts, fuel, fresh water, the occasional bribe dressed up as a harbor fee—we couldn't run a coastal operation without contacts, and contacts have their own circles, and circles have edges, and edges leak. But most of our shore people were transactional. They knew where we *were*, roughly, the way a shopkeeper knows a regular customer. They didn't know where we were going to be, precisely, to the fathom, ahead of time.

Except one.

I didn't let myself finish the thought. I made tea instead. I checked the decoy timers twice. I re-greased the winch that didn't need greasing, because Ilsa Voss, engineer, forty-one years old, survivor of two blacklists' worth of consequences she never personally earned, will always choose a task over a thought.

But the thought finished itself anyway, the way thoughts do when you leave them alone in a room together.

Somebody ashore knew our destination before we arrived. Somebody had known long enough for Meridian to position a scout before we'd even anchored. And there was exactly one person on this coast who supplied our charts—who had, in fact, drawn the approach routes to this exact stretch of escarpment in his own cramped, infuriatingly precise hand, the same hand that had been feeding us safe water for six years.

Old Sarel. Chart-man. The man whose maps we trusted with our keels and our lives.

I told myself it could be coincidence. I told myself Meridian had simply done good survey work, that the Kestrel's position was public record, that any competent fleet could find a wreck that had been on the charts for a decade. All true. All comforting. All beside the point, because the scout hadn't gone to the wreck's charted position—it had gone to *our* anchorage, fifty meters off, chosen by Cyrus's own habit of tucking into the lee. Nobody reads Cyrus's habits off a survey chart. You learn those by selling them.

* * *

At dusk I took the news topside. Cyrus was at the stern rail watching the scout's patch of empty water, and Pim was in the crow's nest we absolutely did not install legally, and Tomas was cleaning dive knives with the slow thoroughness of a man avoiding conversation.

"Our friends aren't following us," I said. "They're meeting us. Someone gave them our position before we ever dropped anchor."

Cyrus didn't move. That was the frightening part. A lesser man would have sworn, paced, demanded names. Cyrus just watched the water go dark and said, quietly, "Who knows the anchorage?"

And there it was, the question I'd been carrying around all day like a stone in my boot, and now I had to set it down in front of everyone.

"The anchorage is in Sarel's charts," I said. "His hand-drawn insets. He's been updating them for us for six years. He knows every place we've ever felt safe."

Pim came down the rigging fast, face pale in the last light. "Sarel taught me to read currents. He—" He stopped. Recalibrated. Pim's whole face is a bulletin board; you can watch him pin things up and take them down. "He's old," he said finally. "He's got a grandson with debts, maybe, or—he wouldn't. He *wouldn't*."

"He might not have meant to," Tomas said, not looking up from his knives. "Men don't always mean to. They just answer a question one day, and the question turns out to have teeth."

"You'd know," I said, gentler than it sounded. Tomas's history with answering questions is its own sad country.

"I would," he agreed, and kept sharpening.

Cyrus turned around. In the dusk his face was mostly shadow and jaw, and his voice was level in the way that costs him something—I've heard it crack once in nine years, and I still think about it.

"Nobody confronts Sarel," he said. "Not yet. If he's selling us, we need to know what he's selling and to whom, and a frightened seller stops selling and starts hiding. Ilsa—keep the decoys rotating. Make them beautiful. Pim, from tomorrow you live on that cliff with the glasses, and you log every light, every boat, every gull that flies in formation. If there's a signal going shore-to-sea, I want it written down with times."

"And you?" I asked.

"I'm going diving," Cyrus said, "because whatever's in that hold is worth more than whoever's selling us, and because if I stand on this deck one more hour thinking about traitors I'll do something stupid." He paused at the companionway. "One more thing. Nobody says the name again. Not aloud. Names travel."

We nodded like good soldiers, and then we all lay awake in our bunks saying the name silently, over and over, like a prayer said backward.

* * *

I stayed up with the radio watch, because I'm the one who trusts herself least with sleep. Around midnight the scout moved—not toward us, not away, just a slow lateral drift, repositioning, patient as interest accruing. I logged it. At 0140, I caught it.

A light on shore. High up, past the tree line, where no house had any business being. Three flashes, pause, two flashes, pause, three again. Then dark.

I wrote down the time. I wrote down the bearing. My hand was steady, which surprised me, because inside I could feel something tightening, turn by turn, like a winch taking up slack—and a winch, I'll remind you, is a machine for moving heavy things against their will.

Three-two-three. I ran it against the coastwise signal codes Sarel himself had taught Pim, back when teaching us his craft was just an old man's kindness and not, possibly, inventory management. Coordinates shorthand. A position update.

The scout adjusted course within ten minutes. Toward the new bearing. Toward the decoy, as it happened—my beautiful, lying, compressor-singing buoy two miles north—and I allowed myself one small, bitter smile in the dark wheelhouse. Sold or not, they were buying my counterfeit tonight. There's a satisfaction in that which I intend to enjoy for exactly as long as it lasts, which, knowing our luck, is until breakfast.

But the smile didn't survive the arithmetic. Because the signal had come from high ground, from a vantage I knew, because I'd triangulated from it before—and the only structure up there, the only shelter, the only place a man could sit with a shuttered lamp and a clear view of both our anchorage and the sea road south—

Was the old signal hut above Sarel's cottage. His hut. His lamp. His codes.

I sat in the dark and listened to the compressor-buoy sing its fake song across the water, keeping our enemies company, telling them a story about divers who weren't us, and I understood, with the awful clarity that comes at 0300 when even the sea stops pretending, that the ground under this whole operation had been soft for years. That the maps we trusted had been drawn by a man taking dictation. That somewhere behind us, patient as debt, the truth was walking downhill toward our fire with its hand out.

Above me, the escarpment wind changed direction, coming offshore now, carrying the smell of the land out to sea—the smell of woodsmoke, and wet stone, and someone else's lamp burning late.

Two tides until the monsoon. Two tides until Meridian's fleet. And now a third clock, ticking quieter than either: find out who's selling us before they finish the sale.

I lit the work lamp on the northern ledge myself, so it would flicker right, and while I was up there I looked inland at the dark shoulder of the hill where the signal hut stood, and I made it a promise I intended to keep as soon as Cyrus stopped looking:

Winches run both ways.
