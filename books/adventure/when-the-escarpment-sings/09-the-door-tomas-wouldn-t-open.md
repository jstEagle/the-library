## 9. The Door Tomas Wouldn't Open

There's a particular kind of cowardice that only shows up in cold water. On land I can lie to myself with real craftsmanship. Underwater, at forty meters, with the Kestrel's hull groaning around me like a ship-sized throat clearing itself, the lies get thin and the truth gets heavy, and the truth this morning was simple: I had been avoiding a door for eleven years.

Not metaphorically. An actual door. Steel, green with copper rot, stenciled *O. VANCE — CHIEF SURVEYOR* in letters that had probably been crisp the day she died and were now soft as old fruit. It sat at the end of the crew quarters corridor on the Kestrel's port side, and yesterday, on the first dive, I had looked at it for exactly four seconds, told Cyrus my air was running low — it wasn't — and turned around like a man who'd suddenly remembered an urgent appointment elsewhere.

Cyrus hadn't said anything. That was worse than if he had. Cyrus Eriksen's silences come in grades, and that one had been a full honors silence, the kind he saves for things he's decided not to make your problem until later, when it can be maximally your problem.

So here I was again, second tide, hanging in the corridor of a dead woman's ship, telling myself that a professional diver of nineteen years' standing does not get to be afraid of a piece of steel.

The water up here was different from the open coast. The escarpment threw a shadow over the site in the mornings, so the light came down gray-green and grainy, like looking through dirty bottle glass. Our lamps cut cones through it, and the silt we'd disturbed yesterday still hung in the corridors in slow curtains, so that swimming through the crew quarters felt like moving through a room where someone had recently argued and stormed out.

Ilsa's voice crackled in my earpiece, tinny and patient. "Tomas. Your heart rate is doing something I don't love."

"You can hear my heart rate?"

"I can hear everything. It's a curse. Breathe."

"I'm breathing."

"Like a man breathing, not like a man being chased by one." A pause, the sound of her adjusting something on the surface rig. "Door's not going anywhere, you know. It's had eleven years of practice."

"That's exactly what worries me," I said, and kicked forward before the sensible part of me could file another objection.

* * *

Let me tell you about Odile Vance, since apparently the universe has decided I'm going to spend this whole dive thinking about her whether I like it or not.

She hired me when I was twenty-two and had just been fired from a harbor survey crew for — and I maintain this to this day — accurately reporting that the pilings were rotten. Nobody wants accurate. Odile wanted accurate. She interviewed me on a dock in the rain, read my termination letter upside down while holding it toward the light like she was checking for a watermark, and said, "They fired you for telling the truth. Good. That means you're cheap and honest, which is the only combination worth hiring."

Then she taught me to dive properly. Not the harbor-certification way — the Odile way. Slow. Deliberate. "The water is not your enemy, Tomas, it is just very old and very patient, and it resents being rushed." She'd put her hand flat against the sea itself sometimes, palm down, like greeting a horse. The crew used to joke that she was part fish. She let us joke. She never laughed at her own jokes, which somehow made them funnier.

And then the consortium sent the Kestrel into that storm with a cracked hull fitting they knew about, because repairing it would have delayed a shipment, and Odile went down with her survey notes and her stubbornness and thirty-one other people, and the water got to keep her.

That's the version I carry. There are others — Pim collects them the way other people collect stamps — but mine is mine, and it ends with me on a rescue skiff two days too late, staring at an oil slick, swearing an oath with four other soaked and hollowed-out people that we would never come back here.

We broke that oath yesterday. Oaths, it turns out, have a shelf life slightly shorter than grief.

* * *

The corridor narrowed. Her door was ten meters away. Then five.

Here's the thing nobody tells you about wrecks: they're not frozen. People say that — *a moment in time, preserved* — but it's wrong. A wreck is a slow argument between the ship and the sea, and the sea is winning, and every year the rooms change their minds about what they contain. Bulkheads that stood last season fold. Corridors reroute themselves. And doors —

Doors stay shut. Doors are the sea's favorite thing to keep shut.

I stopped at the frame. My lamp beam lay across the stencil, and beneath it, someone had scratched something into the paint years ago — a small mark, a surveyor's check glyph, Odile's own habit, done with a fingernail or a stylus on some long-ago inspection. *All secure,* it said, in the language of people who check things for a living. All secure. Eleven years dead and her handwriting was still vouching for the room behind her.

"Tomas," Ilsa said softly. "You've been hovering for ninety seconds. Even the fish are starting to comment."

"They're very judgy fish."

"Go through or come up. Pick one. Hovering burns air and dignity."

"Dignity's already gone. You heard my heart rate."

I put my glove on the handle.

It's stupid how heavy a door handle can be. It weighed nothing — buoyancy took care of that — and it weighed everything. Because the last time I'd touched this handle, I'd been twenty-two years old and the ship was alive and Odile had been standing right there, saying, "Lock it after you, Tomas, the chart room floods if anyone leaves this open," and I'd locked it, and she'd rapped her knuckles twice on the steel — her little goodbye, always twice, *all's well, all's well* — and walked away down the corridor toward the bridge, and that was the last time anyone knocked on anything in my presence and I didn't think about it for a decade afterward.

Twice. She always knocked twice.

I turned the handle. The mechanism fought me, rusted half-seized, then gave with a grinding complaint that sounded obscenely loud in the water, and the door swung inward into blackness, and the silt of eleven years rolled out to meet me like a held breath finally released.

* * *

Her cabin was small. Chief surveyors don't rate luxury; they rate proximity to the charts, and Odile had rated a bunk, a desk, a shelf, and a porthole that even now, furred with marine growth, caught a little of the gray light and made the room feel less like a grave and more like a room where a person had lived, which was worse, honestly. Graves you can visit. Rooms where people lived you have to imagine.

Her bunk was stripped — she'd been tidy; of course she'd been tidy; the woman alphabetized her tool rolls. The desk drawer hung open, empty except for a pen that had fused to the wood and a brass clip that might once have held papers. Survey notes. Everyone always assumed the survey notes burned with the bridge records, or dissolved, or were salvaged by Meridian's first illegal sweep within the month. The ledgers Cyrus pulled from the hold yesterday were the consortium's books. Odile's own notes — the ones documenting the cracked fitting, the deferred repairs, the whole quiet machinery of negligence — those had gone down with her, somewhere, or nowhere.

I drifted through the room the way you drift through a house after a funeral, touching nothing, cataloguing everything. The shelf held three books, glued into bricks by decay, spines unreadable. A boot. One boot, upright, patient, absurd. I almost laughed, and the laugh would have cost me air, so I swallowed it, and swallowing a laugh underwater is its own special misery.

"All secure," I murmured into the mic, without meaning to.

"What?" Ilsa said.

"Nothing. Old habit."

"It's not nothing. Your voice did a thing."

"My voice doesn't do things."

"Your voice is currently doing several things. What do you see?"

I described the cabin. She was quiet a moment, and then she said, in the careful tone she uses when she's about to be right about something: "Check the desk. Not the drawers. The desk."

"Why?"

"Because Odile taught both of us that people hide things where furniture is boring, and because you're stalling again, and I'd rather you found something than stalled beautifully."

I ran my lamp along the desktop. Plain oak substitute, warped, edge-grown with a fine fuzz of hydroid. Nothing. I checked under it. Nothing. I was about to report failure when my glove snagged — barely, a whisper of resistance — on the rear panel where the desk met the bulkhead. A seam. Too straight. Furniture seams wander; built-in seams lie flush.

Odile's voice, eleven years old and clear as the day it was recorded: *People hide things where furniture is boring.*

My heart was doing the thing Ilsa didn't love again. I worked my pry bar — standard salvage kit, sorry, Odile, you'd have approved of the tool and disapproved of the manners — into the seam, and the panel came away easier than it should have, easier than eleven years of growth should allow, and behind it was a strongbox.

Steel. Flat. About the size of a ship's logbook, bolted to the hull itself, painted the same gray as the bulkhead so that unless you knew — unless she'd shown you, once, laughing, years ago, *the day someone robs this ship, Tomas, they'll tear apart the captain's safe and walk right past my desk* — you'd never find it.

She'd shown me. Drunk on one glass of plum wine, on a dock, in the sun. I'd forgotten. I'd spent eleven years forgetting, and now the memory arrived all at once, complete with the smell of that dock, and I had to hold onto the desk and breathe until the gray water stopped spinning.

The box was here. Odile's box. Which meant her notes might be here — the proof, the truth, the thing the consortium had murdered a ship to bury.

I got the light close. The lock was a simple three-lever, salt-welded, hopeless — normally. You'd need a cutting torch, or months of soaking, or —

Or hands that had been here recently.

Because the lock was scratched. Freshly. Bright metal showed through the corrosion in clean arcs around the keyway, bright as new coin, and there was no brightness anywhere else on that box, none, the sea had taken eleven years to dull every surface and these scratches laughed at it. And the box hung open. Lid loose on its hinge. Empty inside, except for a rectangle of cleaner wood where something flat and substantial had lain long enough to keep the rot off.

Someone had opened it. Recently. Within — I'm no chemist, but Ilsa would tell me later the corrosion patterns suggested weeks, maybe less — someone had beaten us here, into a wreck at forty meters on a stretch of coast everyone swore was un-divable, and they had known exactly where the panel was, exactly what the lock was, and they had taken whatever Odile hid and left the empty shell bolted to the hull like a taunt.

The wreck wasn't a grave anymore. It hadn't been for a while. It was a scene, and I was standing in it, and the violation went through me colder than the water ever could. This was her room. This was the one place left in the world where she had been, and someone had picked it open like a pocket.

"Tomas." Ilsa again. "Talk to me. Your breathing went strange."

"The box," I said. My voice came out flat and far away. "Odile's box. It's here. It's open. It's empty."

Silence on the line. Then: "Empty how? Destroyed-empty or robbed-empty?"

"Robbed. Lock's freshly scratched. Bright metal, Ilsa. Weeks, maybe days. Someone's been here before us."

Longer silence. Above me, faint through the hull, I could hear the Kestrel settling, that endless slow argument with the sea, and for the first time it sounded less like groaning and more like whispering — the whole ship leaning in to ask me the question I was already asking myself.

Who. Who knew about the panel. Who could work this deep alone. Who wanted Odile's notes badly enough to rob a grave for them — and who, having robbed it, had left the ledgers in the hold untouched for us to find, unless the ledgers weren't the prize at all, unless we'd been handed the decoy while someone else walked off with the truth.

"Cyrus needs to know," Ilsa said at last. "Come up. Straight up, no detours, and touch nothing else."

"Too late," I said. "I already touched everything."

"Then come up and tell him exactly what you touched."

I gave the empty box one last look. The rectangle of clean wood where the missing thing had rested looked like a shadow that had outlived its owner. *All secure,* her stencil said out in the corridor, faithful and wrong.

I backed out of her cabin, and I did the thing I'd failed to do eleven years ago at her funeral, which was say goodbye — twice, knuckles on the doorframe, *all's well, all's well*, though nothing was — and swam for the light.

Whatever had been in that box was now ahead of us instead of behind us. And given what we'd already seen Meridian do for a chance at these waters, I had a bad feeling the race wasn't for the ledgers at all.

It was for whatever Odile had thought was worth more.
