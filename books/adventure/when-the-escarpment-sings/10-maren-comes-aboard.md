## 10. Maren Comes Aboard

I was re-lashing the crane cable when Pim's voice came down from the cliff watch, thin and strange over the radio, like a bird that had learned words for the wrong reasons.

"Cyrus. Skiff coming in. Single runner. She's flying white."

White. On this coast, a white flag meant one of three things: surrender, parley, or a sailmaker with more ambition than sense. I climbed the rocks to the watch point and put the glass on it, and there she was — a hard-chine skiff cutting the swell at an angle that said its pilot had grown up in these waters and never bothered to forgive them. At the tiller, a figure in oilskins too good for salvage work.

Maren Voss.

I stood there long enough for the wind to have opinions about my posture. Ten years since I'd seen her last, and that had been across a courtroom in Halvard, her hand raised, her voice steady, swearing before two magistrates and a consortium lawyer that she'd watched me sabotage the *Coriander's* moorings myself. Steady as a tide table. I used to wonder how she slept. Then I stopped wondering about Maren Voss entirely, which took longer and cost more.

"She's alone," Pim said beside me. "Far as I can see."

"Far as you can see is exactly what worries me."

"You want me to signal Tomas up?"

"No." I handed him back the glass. "If she's here to kill me, she's brought the wrong boat. If she's here to buy me, she's brought the right one."

* * *

We met her at the shingle beach below the camp, because I refused — on principle, and principle was about all I had left of the old country — to let Maren Voss set foot on my deck without asking. The skiff grounded with a scrape of keel on stone, and she stepped off into ankle-deep water without waiting for help, which was also exactly like her. Maren had never once waited for anything she could simply take.

She looked older. Not soft-older. Weathered-older, the way driftwood gets: same shape, less give. Her hair was cropped short now, grey coming in at the temples like frost claiming a field one row at a time. She carried a canvas satchel and nothing else, and she stopped three paces away, which told me she remembered the old rule of Odile's crew — three paces, hands visible, no exceptions — even if she'd spent a decade pretending she'd never been part of us.

"Cyrus."

"Maren." I let the silence do some work. It's cheap labor and it works weekends.

"You look terrible," she said.

"You look expensive."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth. Not quite a smile. The ghost of one, come back to haunt the premises. "The offer stands as written. Half the recovery value of the Kestrel's survey instruments and cargo manifest, plus a completion bonus. My terms were in the letter."

"I read the letter. It smelled like tar."

"It always does. I seal them with it so couriers can't steam them open without knowing they've done it." She glanced past me, up the shingle toward where Ilsa's dive rig squatted against the cliff like a faithful dog that had been kicked too many times. "You rebuilt the rig. Odile's mark-four?"

"Odile's mark-three, with Ilsa's corrections. The mark-four killed a man off Sarnholt."

"The mark-four killed a man because the consortium sold Sarnholt Harbor a corroded pressure manifold and called it maintenance." She said it flatly, without heat, the way people say things they've said to themselves ten thousand times. "But you know that. You've always known that. That was rather the problem."

"Tomas is topside," I said. "So is Ilsa. And Pim, who is seventeen and has heard every story about you, none of them flattering, most of them mine. You'll want to choose your next sentence carefully."

"I imagine I will." She looked out at the water, at the gray line where the escarpment dropped into deep, where the Kestrel lay broken on the ledge with everything we'd sworn never to touch. "Cyrus. I need to tell you why I hired you, and it isn't the reason in the letter."

"There it is," I said. "I was starting to worry you'd changed."

* * *

We talked in the cook tent, because I wanted witnesses and walls between us, though not necessarily in that order. Tomas sat by the stove with his arms crossed and his face doing the thing it does when he's decided to be a wall himself. Ilsa perched on the tool chest, a wrench in her lap like a pet she wasn't sure she trusted. Pim hovered at the flap until Ilsa yanked him inside by the sleeve, because if we were going to be lied to, Pim was going to be lied to with the rest of us.

Maren set her satchel on the table but didn't open it. Good instinct. Anything that came out of that bag first would frame everything after it.

"Meridian Salvage has claimed the Kestrel wreck under a consortium recovery license," she began. "Paperwork filed eight months ago. Do you know what that means, legally?"

"It means they can strip it," Ilsa said, "and nobody at any port office will ask what they took."

"It means more than that." Maren's eyes stayed on me. "A recovery license requires a certified site survey within ninety days of filing. Meridian filed theirs in March. The survey vessel they contracted — the *Larkspur*, out of Vane's Reach — went down in a squall with all hands. Officially."

"And unofficially?" Tomas asked.

"Unofficially, the Larkspur's hull was found by a fisherman six weeks later, forty miles south, burned to the waterline. No storm does that. No accident does that." She opened the satchel then, finally, and drew out a folded chart — not a chart of our coast, but of shipping lanes, annotated in a cramped hand. "Meridian doesn't salvage wrecks, Cyrus. Meridian makes them. They've been sinking ships that carry cargo or evidence the consortium wants gone, then recovering whatever survives under legal cover. The Kestrel isn't a job to them. It's a loose end."

"The Kestrel sank eleven years ago," I said. "Before your consortium friends started their hobby."

"The Kestrel sank eleven years ago carrying the consortium's own survey ledgers — records their own chief accountant compiled before he grew a conscience and tried to sell them to the harbor authority at Halvard. The Kestrel was supposed to deliver him and the ledgers to safe anchorage." Her voice didn't waver, but something behind it did, some load-bearing beam shifting. "It never anchored. It went down on the escarpment ledge in calm water, with a hole in its hull that no rock made."

The tent got very quiet. Outside, the tide worked the shingle, patient as arithmetic.

"You're saying the consortium sank their own ship," Pim said, "to drown one man and some paperwork."

"I'm saying," Maren said, "that Odile Rask found that hole when she dove the wreck three days later looking for survivors. I'm saying she reported it. And I'm saying that nine days after her report, Odile Rask was dead in a diving accident that every port record calls routine."

Tomas stood up. He didn't say anything; he just stood, the way a man stands when sitting has become impossible, and Ilsa put her hand on his forearm and he sat back down, slowly, like a door closing on a hinge that needed oiling.

"I was on that recovery crew," Maren continued, quieter now. "With Odile. With you, Cyrus. I held the line while she went down. I heard everything she said on the wire about the hull. And when the consortium came for the report — when they came for *us* — I gave them what they wanted. I testified that you sabotaged the Coriander. I testified that Odile's findings were the fantasies of a woman who'd been down too long. I did it because they showed me a debt ledger with my father's name in it and told me what happens to families of uncooperative witnesses." She laid both hands flat on the table, palms down, as if steadying herself against weather only she could feel. "I have spent ten years buying my way back to this wreck. Every favor, every coin, every compromise. And now Meridian is two tides behind you with a fleet, and I hired the only crew on this coast crazy enough and fast enough to get there first — not to salvage the Kestrel, Cyrus. To be bait. Because Meridian follows you the way sharks follow blood, and I needed them to commit their fleet to this site while I finish what Odile started."

"Bait," I repeated. The word tasted like bilge.

"You were never meant to be in danger. You were meant to be *visible*. There's a difference."

"Not on this coast there isn't."

"Cyrus—" She stopped. Recalibrated. I watched her do it, watched ten years of boardrooms and bargains fail to find the register she needed, and then she just spoke plainly, which was somehow worse. "I believed for years that you were guilty. That was the price of sleeping. Then I saw the Larkspur's hull, and I understood that the people who bought my testimony had never stopped buying, and that everything I told myself about necessity was just the interest on a debt I keep refinancing." She turned the chart around and pushed it toward me. Shipping lanes. Dates. Wrecks marked with small red crosses, dozens of them, a coastline dotted like a pox map. "This is what I've assembled. It's not the ledgers. The ledgers are still down there, in the hold, in a sealed case, because the consortium couldn't recover them without admitting the Kestrel was murdered. But those ledgers name every ship, every bribe, every guide they framed — including you. Including Odile's report, which they buried with her."

"And your plan," Ilsa said, in the tone she uses for engines that are making a new noise, "was to use us as chum and tell us this *when*? After the fleet arrived?"

"After I was sure," Maren said. "After I knew you'd committed. Because if I'd told you at the start, you'd have walked away, and you know it. All of you swore never to go back. I had to make going back look like a job instead of a grave."

"She's not wrong," Tomas said, to the stove.

"That's not the point," I said.

"Then what is the point, Cyrus?" Maren leaned forward. "That I lied? Yes. That I used you? Yes. That I am the reason you lost your name, your licenses, your country — twice? Yes, and I will stand here while you say every word of it, and I will not defend myself, because there is no defense. But Odile is dead because of what's in that hold, and Meridian is coming to make sure it stays dead, and you are the only people alive who can get to it first. So hate me. Hate me on the way down and hate me on the way up. Just don't let me have done all this for nothing."

* * *

I walked her back to the beach myself. The others stayed in the tent; I asked for that much, and for once in my life I think I asked correctly.

The skiff bobbed at the shingle, patient. Maren waded to it, then stopped, water at her knees, and looked back at me with the light going orange behind her the way it does before weather.

"You're wondering whether to believe me," she said.

"I'm wondering why I want to," I said. "That's worse."

"For what it's worth — and I know exactly what it's worth, which is nothing — I didn't kill Odile. I've spent ten years finding out who did. The trail ends at Meridian's founder, a man named Aldous Crane, who signed the order to sink the Kestrel and the order to bury Odile's report in the same week." She gripped the gunwale. "One more thing. When my scout reported your position yesterday, they already knew it. They were waiting for confirmation. Someone on this shore is feeding them, Cyrus, and it isn't me."

"They've known where we are since the letter," I said. "Your letter. Which you tar-sealed so carefully."

Something flickered across her face — not guilt, exactly. Recognition. The look of a chess player realizing the board was set before she sat down.

"Then we're all being played," she said, "and the only question left is who plays best from here." She pushed off. "Six tides, Cyrus. Don't waste them hating me. There'll be time after."

I watched the skiff shrink against the darkening water until Pim's glass lost it, and then I stood on the shingle a while longer, doing sums I didn't like.

Because here was the arithmetic that kept refusing to balance: Maren Voss had lied to me, used me, and confessed to all of it inside an hour — which was either the most honest thing a traitor ever did, or the most sophisticated lie ever built, a confession designed to be disarming precisely because it admitted everything except the one thing that mattered. Ten years ago I'd have known which. Ten years ago I'd have read her like a depth gauge.

Now all I could read was the water, and the water said: two tides, maybe three, before sails on the horizon.

And underneath that, fainter, the other thing the water said — the thing I'd been refusing to hear since the letter arrived smelling of tar. If someone ashore was feeding Meridian our position, then the ground itself was rotten, and every chart, every supply run, every friendly face from Vane's Reach to the salt lagoons belonged to the enemy.

I turned back toward camp, where my crew was waiting to hear what I'd decided about the woman who ruined my life, and found Ilsa already standing at the tent flap with the radio handset in her fist and an expression I'd only seen twice before, both times right before something broke.

"Cyrus," she said. "You need to hear this. Someone's been keying coordinates on the coastal band. Shore-side. And I know the cadence."
