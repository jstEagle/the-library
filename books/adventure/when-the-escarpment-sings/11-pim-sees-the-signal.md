## 11. Pim Sees the Signal

The cliff watch was mine because I was the smallest and because I complained the least about being cold, which Ilsa said was the same qualification the navy used for lookouts, and which Tomas said was a lie, and which Cyrus settled by handing me the thermos and the binoculars and walking away. That was Cyrus's whole management style, really. He didn't assign you a job so much as he made it obvious which job was yours by leaving it where you'd feel guilty about it.

So there I was, third night running, wedged into a fold of granite two hundred feet above the anchorage, wrapped in a tarp that smelled of diesel and Ilsa's engine degreaser, watching a wreck I wasn't allowed to dive and a horizon I was supposed to pretend wasn't full of enemies.

The Kestrel sat below us in her drowned grave, her mast stump breaking the surface on low tide like a finger pointing at the sky, accusing it of something. The crew boat bobbed at her mooring. Somewhere down in the dark, Tomas was probably sleeping badly, which was his hobby now. Somewhere behind me, up the coast trail, the little harbor town of Saltmere was doing what Saltmere did after dark: three lamps, a dog with opinions, and the chandlery where our shore contact kept his accounts.

His name was Halloway. Old Halloway. The man who had sold us charts for six years.

I want to be honest about what that name meant to me before this night, because it matters to what happened after. Halloway was the first adult who ever treated me like a professional. I was fourteen when Cyrus first brought me along on a supply run to Saltmere, and I expected to be patted on the head and pointed at the door. Instead Halloway looked at me over his spectacles, spread three charts across the counter, and said, "Which of these is lying?"

I'd stared at them for a long time. The middle one had a coastline that bent wrong — a headland drawn a half-mile too far east, the kind of error that puts you on rocks if you trust it at night.

"That one," I'd said.

"Because?"

"Because the soundings don't agree with the shape. Whoever drew it never took the soundings himself."

Halloway had smiled like I'd passed something, and Cyrus had bought the two good charts, and from then on Halloway always kept a chart aside for me — marked up in his cramped hand, little notes in the margins: *reef moves after storms, don't trust the 1847 survey, the lighthouse keeper lies about fog.* He taught me that a chart was a rumor about the world, and my job was to catch the world in the act of contradicting it.

He was the reason I was good at this. He was, in the way that mattered to a kid with no family and a crew that was technically a crime in progress, the closest thing I had to a grandfather.

Remember that. It matters to what happened after.

* * *

The first hour of the watch was the good hour, the one where the sea does its breathing and you can almost believe the world is a calm place that occasionally hires salvage crews. The second hour was colder. The third hour was when my brain started doing what it does, which is inventory.

I inventoried the anchorage. Crew boat, riding light burning. Kestrel's mast stump, dark. I inventoried the horizon. Nothing — or rather, nothing where Ilsa had said the Meridian scout would be if the scout was being legal about it, which Ilsa had bet a week's coffee ration it wouldn't be. Running dark, no lights, hull unmarked, sitting out past the shelf like a rumor with an engine.

I inventoried the shore. Saltmere's three lamps. The dog. And then — a fourth light. Small, low, near the old pilot station at the base of the cliff, a quarter mile down the coast from the town. A place where nobody had business being at this hour, because the pilot station had been closed since before I was born and the stairs down to it had rotted through two winters ago. I knew that because Halloway had told me. Halloway told me everything about Saltmere. That was the arrangement. Charts and gossip, gossip and charts.

The light moved. Not like a lantern moving — like a lantern *signaling*. Three long flashes. Pause. A cluster of short ones. Pause. Three long again.

I'd learned signal lamps the way I'd learned everything, which was from whoever on the crew could be bribed into teaching me with food. Ilsa knew the merchant code. She'd drilled me on it one boring week the previous winter, mostly so I could stop asking her what the fishing boats were saying to each other, which she claimed was all complaints about prices and one ongoing feud between two captains about a crab pot.

Three long, cluster of short, three long. I didn't have the code memorized — not all of it, not the number groups — but I knew a pattern when I saw one, and this wasn't a fisherman complaining about crab pots. This was a rhythm. Deliberate. Repeating.

And it was pointed at the water. At the horizon. At the nothing where Ilsa had bet the scout would be.

I got the binoculars on the gap between the lamp and the sea and held them there until my arms shook, and on the fourth repetition of the pattern, the nothing answered.

One flash. Far out. So far I'd have called it a star if stars didn't blink in code.

My stomach did the thing it does when the ground moves under a boat that isn't moving. I'd felt it before, once, standing on a dock in a port we'd been asked to leave quickly. The sensation of the world rearranging itself around a fact you haven't agreed to yet.

I did the arithmetic I didn't want to do. The lamp at the pilot station. The pilot station with the rotted stairs that nobody could reach. Except — and here my brain, traitorous organ, presented the memory unbidden — except that Halloway had a skiff. Halloway had a skiff and a mooring at the old pilot stairs, because he'd told me once, cheerfully, that he liked to fish the deep water off the point and the harbor moorings charged by the foot, and Halloway was a man who watched his pennies the way Ilsa watched pressure gauges.

Halloway, who knew where we were. Halloway, who'd sold us the coastal charts for this job two weeks ago, marked up in his cramped hand, including — I remembered this with a feeling like cold water coming over the gunwale — including the notes on the escarpment's sea caves. *Flooded at half tide. Don't trust the 1847 survey.* The caves we hadn't told anyone about. The caves that existed, as far as I knew, in exactly two sets of knowledge: Odile's old charts, and Halloway's margins.

I sat with that for a while. The lamp kept talking. The far light kept answering. The dog in Saltmere kept having opinions about all of it, unaware.

Here's the thing about being the youngest on a crew of people who've all survived things you can't imagine: you learn to doubt yourself professionally. Tomas freezes at a door and it's trauma, it's history, it's weight. I see something at night through binoculars and it's *Pim being jumpy, Pim wanting to matter.* I have been managed. I have been handed a thermos and a pat and sent back to bed. I knew, with total certainty, that if I ran down to the anchorage shouting *Halloway's a spy*, I'd get a conversation. A gentle one. The kind where Cyrus asks questions in his flat voice and Ilsa checks my facts and Tomas says nothing in a way that's worse than saying something, and by the end of it I'd half believe I'd imagined the whole thing, because that's what being the kid on the crew means. Your evidence is a rumor until an adult confirms it.

So I didn't run. I did what Halloway taught me.

I caught the world in the act of contradicting itself.

* * *

The lamp's pattern changed around midnight. The three-long-cluster-pause gave way to something longer, a sustained transmission, and I did the thing I'd been working up the nerve to do for an hour: I keyed the crew's short-range set and called Ilsa, keeping my voice at the volume you use for not waking Tomas.

"Watch to boat. You awake?"

A pause long enough to be insulting. "I'm awake because you're calling me at midnight, which makes you the reason and not the beneficiary."

"I need you to look at the old pilot station. Don't use a light. Just your eyes."

Another pause. When she answered, all the joke had gone out of her voice, and Ilsa without jokes is a different instrument entirely, like a wrench used as a hammer. "What have you got?"

"Lamp. Signaling seaward. It's been at it for two hours. Something out past the shelf is answering." I made myself say the next part, because Ilsa respected facts and hated hedging. "I think it's Halloway."

I heard her breathing change. "Pim."

"I know what I'm saying."

"You know what you're *suggesting*." But she was already moving; I could hear the boat's deck changing its sound under her, the creak of someone crossing to the seaward rail. "Give me a minute."

I gave her a minute. The lamp gave its long transmission. Out on the water, the answering light blinked twice more and then, for the first time all night, moved — a slow lateral slide, the way a ship changes station. Coming closer, or repositioning to see better. Ilsa would know which. Ilsa knew everything about ships except how to relax on one.

"Okay," she said finally, and her voice had gone quiet in the specific way it went quiet before she said something she'd already decided was true. "There's a light out there. Low. No running lights. It's holding position about four miles out, which is exactly where I'd put a scout if I wanted to watch an anchorage without being watched." A breath. "And the pilot station is signaling it in merchant code, and I can't read the groups from here, but the rhythm is coordinates. Three figures, pause, three figures. That's latitude work, Pim. That's somebody giving away a position."

"Ours."

"Ours." The word sat between us on the radio like a third person who'd come aboard without permission. "Stay on the watch. Don't do anything. Don't signal, don't move, don't — " she stopped, and I heard her decide to say the true thing instead of the captain thing. "Pim. If it's Halloway, Cyrus needs to know before dawn, and Cyrus needs to hear it from you, not from me, because if it comes from me he'll think it's an engineering opinion and if it comes from you he'll know it's a sighting. You're the spotter. It's your call."

That was Ilsa's other management style, which was to hand you a bomb and tell you it was a decision.

* * *

Cyrus came up the cliff path an hour before dawn, moving the way he always moved in the dark — no light, no sound, just a gradual increase of Cyrus until he was there, sitting down next to me on the granite like he'd been there all night. He didn't ask what I had. He waited. That was the thing about Cyrus that people who only knew the blacklist stories never understood: for a man whose whole life was running, he was the most patient person I ever met. Running doesn't take impatience. Running takes the ability to sit still for years.

I gave him the facts in the order Halloway would have wanted them, which is evidence before conclusion. The lamp. The rhythm. The answering light and its station change. The pilot station and its unreachable stairs and the skiff at the old mooring. The charts in the margins. The sea caves.

I made my voice stay flat the whole way through, and when I finished, Cyrus was silent long enough that I started doing the thing I'd promised myself I wouldn't do, which was take it back.

"I could have misread the — "

"You didn't." He said it simply, the way he said true things, and then he took the binoculars and looked at the pilot station for a long time. The lamp was dark now. Whatever the night's business had been, it was concluded; the scout, Ilsa reported by radio, was holding its new station, four miles out, patient as a debt. "The stairs are rotted through," Cyrus said. "He told us that himself. Two years ago. Told us the whole station was a ruin." He lowered the binoculars. "You don't lie about a building unless the building matters."

"Why would he?" It came out of me before I could stop it, and it came out sounding younger than I wanted, so I made myself say the rest of it like a professional. "He's sold us charts for six years. He taught me — " I stopped. Restarted. "He's the reason I can read a chart at all. He kept the good ones aside for me. He never once — "

"I know." Cyrus looked out at the graying water. "I know, Pim."

And the worst part, the part I still can't make sit flat in my chest, is that he did know. That's what none of the stories about Cyrus Eriksen tell you. The blacklisted guide, the man two countries won't have, the runner — he knew exactly what Halloway was to me, because Halloway was that to all of us. Six years of charts. Six years of *the reef moves after storms* and *don't trust the 1847 survey* and *the lighthouse keeper lies about fog.* Six years of the one man on that coast who never asked what we were salvaging, never asked why we never stayed, never once made us feel like what we were. You build a life out of that. You build a whole coastline out of that.

And the coastline had been talking to Meridian all night.

"Does this change the dive?" I asked, because it was the professional question, and asking it kept me from asking the other one.

Cyrus took his time. Down at the anchorage the sky was doing its gray-to-pale trick, and the Kestrel's mast stump stood out of the water, still pointing its accusation at a sky that hadn't answered in eleven years.

"It changes everything and it changes nothing," he said finally. "We still have four tides. We still have ledgers in that hold. What it changes is who knows we're here — and now we know that they know, which Ilsa will tell you is worth more than ignorance, though Ilsa would tell you that about a knife wound." He stood, and put his hand on my shoulder for exactly one second, which from Cyrus was a parade. "You saw it. Nobody else would have. Halloway taught you too well."

"That's not funny."

"No," Cyrus said. "It isn't." And he started down the cliff path toward the boat and the dive and the third tide, and I sat back down on my granite with the binoculars and the thermos and the specific cold that comes from learning that the ground itself has been for sale the whole time.

The sun came up on Saltmere. Three lamps went out, one by one. The dog finally stopped talking. And down at the chandlery, I knew, Halloway would be opening his shutters right about now, putting his charts in order, keeping one aside for me.

I raised the binoculars and watched the pilot station, and I did not look away.
