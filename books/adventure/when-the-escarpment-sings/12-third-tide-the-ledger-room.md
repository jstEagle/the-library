## 12. Third Tide: The Ledger Room

There is a particular kind of silence that lives forty fathoms down, and it is not the absence of sound. It is the presence of everything you've ever tried not to think about, waiting for you with the patience of water.

I was thinking about this as I swam through the throat of the Kestrel's hold, my lamp beam cutting a cone through murk the color of old tea, and I was thinking it because thinking about anything else—the ledgers, my name, what they might say—was a luxury I couldn't afford at depth. Panic kills divers. So does hope, if you let it in uninvited.

Tomas was ten meters behind me on the guide line, his breathing steady in my earpiece, the way it had been steady for eleven years across two hundred dives and one oath we were currently breaking. Above us, the *Sable Wren* rode at anchor over the site, and above her, clouds the color of wet slate were stacking themselves along the escarpment like a debt collector climbing stairs.

"Clear to the bulkhead," I said. "Hold access is where Odile said it would be."

Nobody answered. There was nobody left to answer who'd been here before.

* * *

You have to understand what the Kestrel was, or was supposed to be. A survey vessel, officially. Coastal hydrography, charting the escarpment shelf for the consortium's shipping lanes. But Odile Marchetti didn't run surveys; she ran questions, dressed up as surveys. She'd take consortium money to map a channel and come back with maps of things the consortium hadn't paid to know—which coves held smuggled cargo, which harbormasters took envelopes, which ships went down too neatly to be weather. She called it reading the coast's handwriting. She taught three of us to read it, and then she sailed into a storm that every other captain in the fleet had been warned away from, and she did not sail out.

The consortium's report called it an act of God. The consortium's reports always did. God, apparently, had a very busy decade.

Tomas's voice came through, low. "Cyrus. The hold door."

I saw it. Or rather, I saw what was left of it. The Kestrel had gone down bow-first against the escarpment's base, and eleven years of current had peeled her open like fruit, but the aft hold had been shielded by a rock shelf, and the door—her door, the one Odile had installed herself, extra hinges, extra lock, because she trusted locks more than people—was still seated in its frame. Crusted white with coral now. A garden growing over a secret.

My hands were steady on the pry bar. I want that noted somewhere, by someone, because inside I was fourteen years old and drowning in a different ocean entirely.

The lock gave on the third pull. Coral doesn't argue.

* * *

Inside, the hold was smaller than memory insisted. Memory does that—it inflates the rooms where the important things happened, gives them cathedral ceilings. This was just a room. Shelving along one wall, most of it collapsed into a drift of silt and rust. Survey equipment under tarps that had long since become part of the sea floor. And there, wedged behind the last shelf, exactly where a woman who trusted locks more than people would put something she never intended anyone to find—

A case. Sealed. Steel, marine-grade, the kind of box you build when you want your contents to outlive your enemies. The consortium stamped its crest on everything it owned, and someone had ground that crest off this case with enough fury to leave tool marks. Odile's work, or so I chose to believe. Choosing to believe things about the dead is half of grief and all of salvage.

"Tomas," I said. "I've got it."

He came through the doorway and stopped, and even through the helmet I could hear the change in his breathing. He was looking past me, at the shelving, at the corner where the crew quarters' ladder rose toward the deck above. Toward a cabin door he'd spent two dives refusing to look at.

"Not yet," he said. Which meant later. Which meant he knew, the way we all knew, that the ledgers weren't the only thing down here that needed recovering.

"Lash it to me," I said. "We're burning bottom time."

The case weighed maybe thirty pounds dry. Underwater, weightless—but drag is its own kind of weight, and I clipped it to my chest harness and felt it immediately, a slab of dead certainty pressing against my sternum with every breath. We turned for the line. My lamp swept the hold one last time, and I made myself look at nothing, catalog nothing, feel nothing, because feeling things at forty fathoms is how you end up staying.

Halfway back along the guide line, Tomas touched my arm. Pointed up.

Through the green ceiling of the water, far above, light flickered—not sunlight. Something moving across the surface. A hull shadow, long and wrong, sliding between us and the sky.

Meridian was early.

* * *

Decompression is designed to give you time to think, which is either medicine or punishment depending on the day. We hung at fifteen feet on the line, the Wren's keel a dark shape overhead, and I had nothing to do but hold a steel case against my chest and wait for my blood to forgive me.

So I thought about my name.

It's a strange thing, being erased. You'd think blacklisting would feel like being struck from a book, but it's the opposite—it feels like being written in, permanently, in ink you can't remove, in a hand that isn't yours. *Saboteur*, both registries said. *Cyrus Eriksen, guide, complicit in the foundering of the merchant vessel Coriander's Wake; complicit in the falsification of coastal charts resulting in loss of life.* Two countries. Two official documents. One signature on each that wasn't mine, and one testimony from a woman I'd have followed into a fire, delivered in a courtroom I still sometimes walk through in my sleep.

Maren Voss had stood at that witness bench with her chin up and her voice level and said, *I saw him take the payment.* And I had believed, for years afterward, that she had. That was the worst of it—not the lie itself, but that I'd believed she was capable of telling it truly. That I'd thought, *of course, people are like that, I was a fool to trust anyone,* and built a whole philosophy of running on top of that single rotten plank.

The case pressed against my chest. Whatever was in it, Odile had died carrying the knowledge of it, and I had spent fourteen years carrying the consequence without the knowledge, which is the heavier load. Consequence without explanation is just superstition. You start believing you deserved it.

"Cyrus." Tomas, quiet. "Your breathing's gone shallow. Ease off."

"I'm fine."

"You're thinking."

"Worse than drowning, I know."

He laughed—a small burst of bubbles, silver in my lamplight. Eleven years of diving together teaches you a whole language made of almost nothing. What he said next, in plain words, was: "Whatever's in there, it doesn't get to be heavier than us. We decide what it weighs."

That's Tomas all over. A man who spends his working life under pressure and has therefore developed opinions about it.

* * *

Ilsa met us at the rail with the winch already running and a face like a slammed door. She got the case onto the deck, got our gear stripped, got us into the wheelhouse, and only then let herself look at the thing, standing over it with her arms crossed like it owed her money.

"Well?" she said.

"Sealed," I said. "Lock's intact. Odile-grade steel."

"So open it."

Pim was there too, hovering at the edge of the lamplight, trying very hard to look like crew and not like a kid who'd grown up on stories about this exact box. Ilsa caught me glancing at him and jerked her chin: *well?*

Here's the thing nobody tells you about vindication. You spend years imagining the moment—you rehearse it, honestly, in the shower, on watch, in the gray hour before dawn. In the rehearsal, you open the evidence and the truth pours out clean and the world corrects itself and you feel *light*. Nobody rehearses the part where your hands shake. Nobody rehearses the part where you realize that whatever's in the box, opening it ends the version of your life you've gotten good at surviving.

I cut the seal.

* * *

Ledgers. Fourteen of them, oilcloth-wrapped, salt-stained at the corners but dry inside, because Odile had packed that case like she was preparing a message for archaeologists. Each ledger bore a year. Each year bore columns.

Bribes first. Harbormasters, registry clerks, two port authority commissioners—I recognized one name, a man who'd personally signed my blacklist order, and there he was in the consortium's own accounting, receiving quarterly payments coded as "channel maintenance." The irony was so pure it was almost beautiful. Almost. Mostly it tasted like copper.

Then the ships. Sunk vessels listed not as losses but as *dispositions*, each with a payout figure beside it. Insurance fraud, some of them. Others worse—ships sunk to bury cargo manifests, crews written off as weather. The *Coriander's Wake* was there, page after page, and beside the entry for its foundering, a line item: *Guide interference — remediation — 4,000.* Remediation. Four thousand crowns to make a guide stop asking questions, and when the guide didn't stop, four thousand more to make everyone believe he'd been bought instead.

And then the guides. That was the section I hadn't known existed, and I read it standing up because sitting down felt like surrender. Name after name. Guides, pilots, coastal surveyors—people like Odile, people like me—who'd asked questions about consortium channels and consortium cargoes, and what happened to each of them. Some blacklisted. Some ruined quietly. Three marked with a small notation in the margin, a symbol I didn't recognize until I did, and then I wished I didn't, because Odile had taught us that symbol once, over charts, late at night: the consortium's shorthand for *handled terminally.*

Odile's own name was in there. Last page of the last ledger. No disposition recorded. Just the notation, and a date six days before the Kestrel sailed into a storm every other captain had been warned away from.

The wheelhouse was very quiet. Pim had gone pale. Ilsa had gone very still, which for Ilsa is the loudest thing she does.

"They didn't frame me because I was guilty," I heard myself say. My voice sounded like it belonged to someone standing further away. "They framed me because I was *asking*. Same as her. The blacklist wasn't a punishment for a crime. It was a fence around a question."

"And the ledgers answer it," Ilsa said.

"The ledgers *are* it. Every question I've carried for fourteen years, itemized. With figures." I laughed, and it came out wrong, and I didn't bother fixing it. "Do you know what I thought vindication would feel like? Light. I thought I'd finally put something down. Instead—" I put my hand flat on the top ledger, the one with my name in it, my actual name, spelled correctly, filed under *remediated*. "Instead it turns out the weight was real all along. I just never got to see it. Now I can see it and I still can't set it down, because setting it down means somebody else picks it up. Means deciding what it's *for*."

"Publish it," Pim said. Quick, certain, the way only the young are certain. "All of it. Every port, every registry—"

"And Meridian burns us off the water inside a week," Ilsa said. "You've seen their scout ship, Pim. That was one boat. Their fleet anchors five tides from now, and right now we are four people in a leaky salvage rig holding the only copies of the document that proves their entire empire is a crime scene."

"Then we sell it," Pim shot back. "Maren offered—"

"Maren Voss testified me into a blacklist," I said, "and then hired us as bait, and I haven't decided yet which of those I'm angrier about. Nobody sells anything tonight."

Silence. Rain had started on the wheelhouse roof, soft, tentative, the monsoon sending ahead its calling card. Out past the breakwater line, the escarpment stood black against a blacker sky, and the wind coming down off it had begun, faintly, to make that sound—the one the old coastal people swore was just wind through the sea caves, and Odile swore was the coast talking, and I had spent fourteen years pretending I didn't hear it.

Ilsa broke it, practical as ever. "Four tides before the storm closes the site. Meridian's fleet behind that. And Cyrus—" she nodded at the ledgers. "Whatever you decide, decide fast. Because that scout ship that crossed over us during decompression? It wasn't searching. It was *confirming*. Somebody told them we'd reached the hold."

I looked down at the ledgers, at fourteen years of my own life filed in another man's handwriting, and felt the full weight of them settle where no harness could carry it.

"Somebody always does," I said. "Question is who—and whether they sold us for money, or for something worse."

Outside, the rain thickened, and the escarpment began, properly, to sing.
