## 13. The Argument at Low Water

I have a theory about crews, and it is this: a crew is not held together by trust or money or even the shared fear of drowning. It's held together by the agreement not to say certain things out loud. Every crew has its sealed compartments, like ballast tanks—things you keep flooded and dark because if you pump them out you'll learn what's actually holding up your hull.

We had been running on sealed compartments for eleven years. The ledgers had opened every one of them in a single afternoon.

The case sat on the deck of the *Magpie's Rest* between us, salt-stained and dented, looking for all the world like a toolbox. Inside were ten years of consortium accounting: bribes, scuttled hulls, insurance fraud dressed as weather, and the names of guides who'd asked too many questions and gotten blacklisted for their trouble. My name wasn't in there. Neither was Tomas's, or Pim's. We hadn't mattered enough to ruin. Only Cyrus had earned that kind of attention, and he'd earned it by being right.

Low water was coming, which meant the sandbar off our stern was going to rise out of the sea like an accusation, and we'd all be able to stand on dry ground while we tore each other apart. Cyrus had anchored there deliberately. He said it was tactical—a defensible position with sightlines to both approaches. I think he just wanted solid footing under everyone for what was coming. There are arguments you have on a pitching deck and arguments you have on land, and only fools confuse the two.

I arrived early, because Ilsa Vinter arrives early or she doesn't arrive at all, and I used the time to do what I always do when the world goes sideways: I counted things. One salvage vessel, patched twice this season. One dive rig rebuilt from memory and spite, currently drying on the rail. Three crewmates walking down from the skiff. One rival salvager's skiff already tied alongside ours, Maren Voss sitting in it with the patience of a woman who had learned to wait a decade for things.

And one strongbox-shaped absence where Odile's cabin used to be, freshly scratched open by hands that weren't ours.

That last one was the splinter under everything. We could argue about ledgers until the monsoon came, but none of it would matter if Meridian's divers had already taken what was in that box—and if they had, then someone had told them exactly where to look.

Tomas came down the shingle first, boots in hand, barefoot on the wet sand the way he always was after a dive, like his feet needed to renegotiate their relationship with the earth. Pim followed, carrying the tea kit, because Pim believed—genuinely, stubbornly, against all evidence—that no argument so bad it couldn't be survived with something hot to hold. Cyrus came last, and he did not look at any of us. He looked at the horizon, where the leading edge of the monsoon was stacking itself into a bruise-colored wall, and I watched him do the same math I'd done at dawn.

Six tides. We'd spent three. Meridian's fleet was one tide behind us and closing.

"Right," Cyrus said, when we were all standing on the bar with the water at our ankles and the wreck of the *Kestrel* showing her broken spine offshore at low water, exposed like a secret told in sleep. "You've all read them."

It wasn't a question. We'd each taken turns with the ledgers through the night, passing them around the lamp like communion. I'd read mine twice, once for the numbers and once for the names.

"So," Cyrus said. "Maren's offer stands. She pays us triple rate, takes the ledgers, and we're gone before the storm. New coast, new names, same life." He paused. "Or we don't sell. We publish."

The silence that followed had texture. It had weight and grain, like wet rope.

I'll be honest about my position, since history will presumably be written by whoever survives long enough to write it: I wanted to sell. Not because I'm a coward—I've cut my own guide line at forty meters to free a fouled anchor, so let's retire that word—but because I'm an engineer, and engineers do not fall in love with systems that only work once. Publishing the ledgers worked exactly once. After that single glorious moment, we would be four people with no country, no harbor, and the full attention of a consortium that owned harbors the way other people own hats. Selling them bought us the same outcome for Cyrus—the truth out, Maren's money in our pockets—with the small added benefit of remaining alive to spend it.

"Tomas," Cyrus said. "You've been quiet."

Tomas was crouched, letting the seawater run over his knuckles. When he finally spoke, his voice had that diver's flatness, the one he got below thirty meters, where emotion is a luxury your air budget can't afford.

"You weren't there," he said. "When Odile went down. You weren't there, Ilsa. None of you were except Cyrus, and Cyrus was busy not dying." He stood up. "Meridian put her in that water. The ledgers prove it—not the framing, not the blacklist, the *sinking*. Page sixty-one. They wrote her death down as a line item. 'Survey vessel Kestrel, hull compromised, unsalvageable, write-off approved.' Write-off approved." His jaw worked. "You want to sell that? Sell it to *whom*, exactly? To the woman who helped them do it?"

"Maren didn't sink anyone," I said. "She testified under coercion, if her story holds, and I've read enough forged documents in my life to know coerced testimony when I hear it."

"And if it doesn't hold?" Tomas's eyes were very still. "If she's buying the ledgers to burn them? Ten years of planning, Ilsa. She's had ten years to get sentimental about the past. People do. They get to the end of a long game and decide the board was the point all along."

"That's why we don't hand them over," I said. "That's why we take the money *and* keep copies. She gets her justice, we get our lives, and somewhere in a waterproof tube there's a second set of pages nobody knows about."

"And when Meridian finds out the pages exist anyway?" Tomas said. "Because they will. They always do. Then we haven't sold the ledgers, we've sold ourselves a head start that isn't one."

Pim cleared his throat. Pim clearing his throat is a sound like a hatch opening, and we all turned toward it whether we meant to or not.

"I don't care about the money," Pim said. Which, coming from a nineteen-year-old spotter who had never once in his life had more than forty crowns at a time, landed harder than any speech Tomas or I could make. "I mean—I care about the money, obviously, I'm not an idiot. But that's not—" He stopped, restarted, the way he did when the thing he was saying mattered more than getting it smooth. "I've been on this crew three years. Three years of leaving harbors before dawn. Three years of learning which questions not to ask in port towns. Three years of watching all of you check over your shoulders like the horizon owes you money." He hugged the tea kit to his chest. "I never met Odile. I know that. I know I don't get a vote on her, really. But I know what it's like to be a ghost, because I've been training to be one since I joined. And I'm tired. I'm so tired of being good at not existing. If we sell these and run again, I'll be nineteen years old and already dead in every port between here and the Spur. If we publish—if we *stop*—then maybe I get to be a person with a name that stays written down somewhere."

Nobody said anything for a while. The tide kept going out around our ankles, patient as arithmetic.

"That's a child's answer," I said finally, and immediately hated how it sounded, so I made myself finish it properly. "It's a child's answer and it's also the truest one in this argument, and I hate that, Pim, because I ran the survival numbers twice last night and they don't care how tired we are."

"Then run them again," Cyrus said quietly. "With new inputs."

I looked at him. He was still watching the horizon, but now he was watching the shape of it rather than the weather on it—the escarpment itself, the long black teeth of it running north, and somewhere along those teeth, the sea caves Odile had charted and nobody else had ever found.

"You already have a plan," I said. "Don't you. You've had one since the ledgers came up the line."

"I have half a plan," Cyrus said. "Half a plan and six tides minus three."

"That's not arithmetic, that's poetry, and poetry sinks ships."

Cyrus almost smiled. It was the ghost of a smile, the kind he rationed out the way a sinking man rations air. "Ilsa. Walk me through it. What happens if we sell?"

I took a breath and did what I do best, which is say the ugly true thing in an orderly fashion. "We take Maren's payment. We scatter. Within a year—probably within six months—Meridian finds out the ledgers surfaced and were sold, not destroyed. They can't un-see that. They start asking who else has seen them. They start pulling threads. And every thread ends at us, because we're the only four people alive who were on this sandbar tonight. Sooner or later a scout ship finds the *Magpie's Rest* in some quiet anchorage, and there's no cave chart for that conversation."

"And if we publish?"

"If we publish, we light a fire that burns the consortium down and lights us up like a signal flare while it does. For however long it takes the ports to act—weeks, maybe—we are the most findable people on this coast. Meridian doesn't send scouts for that. They send the fleet."

"So either way they come," Tomas said.

"No," I said. "Either way they *might* come. That's the difference. Selling gives us a chance they never come. Publishing guarantees it." I kicked at the water, sending a fan of spray across the wet sand, because my hands wanted to hit something and the ledgers were right there and I wasn't raised in a barn. "I have spent eleven years keeping this crew alive, Cyrus. Eleven years of patching hulls other people condemned and rigging decoys and knowing which harbors to skip. Survival isn't my preference. It's my *job*. You're asking me to vote against my own job."

"I'm asking you to tell me what the job is *for*," Cyrus said.

And there it was. The compartment he'd just pumped out, in front of everyone, without warning.

Because that was the question, wasn't it. Eleven years of never stopping, never settling, never going back, and for what? I'd told myself it was for the crew—for keeping Tomas breathing and Pim fed and Cyrus one step ahead of a past that hunted him. But Tomas was standing in front of me wanting justice so badly his hands shook, and Pim was standing there wanting a name more than he wanted food, and I realized, standing on that sandbar with the sea retreating from me like a confession, that I didn't actually know what I'd been preserving all this time. A crew is not a thing. A crew is four people agreeing on a direction. Take away the direction and all you've preserved is the agreement, empty, like a hull with no engine.

"I don't know," I said. It cost me something to say it. It cost less than I expected. "I genuinely don't know anymore. Ask me five years ago and I'd have said survival, full stop. Ask me tonight and—" I spread my hands. "Ask me tonight and I look at Pim and I think, what exactly am I keeping him safe *for*? So he can be a well-preserved ghost? I signed on to keep people alive, not to embalm them."

Tomas made a sound that might have been a laugh, if laughs could bleed.

Maren, who had stayed in her skiff through all of this with the discipline of a woman who knew she was the reason for half the anger in the air, finally called across the water. "For whatever it's worth—"

"It isn't," Tomas said.

"—the fleet doesn't need the ledgers to find you anymore," she went on, as if he hadn't spoken. "They've known where you are since the second dive. Someone on this coast has been selling your positions for years. Whatever you decide, the running is finished. The only choice left is what you're standing still *for*."

We all absorbed that. I watched it land on each of them differently—on Tomas like a blow he'd been expecting, on Pim like a door opening, on Cyrus like a weight he'd set down and only now noticed the absence of.

On me it landed like a bearing failure. Because I'd rigged those decoy signals myself, and I'd known, deep in the winch-gutted paranoid core of me, that they'd been theater. You can't spoof a hunter who already has your scent. I'd known and I'd built the decoys anyway, because building them was motion, and motion was the only prayer I knew.

The tide had bottomed out. The sandbar lay broad and pale around us, and the *Kestrel* sat offshore with her whole drowned skeleton bared to the fading light, and I thought about page sixty-one, about a woman written off as a line item by men with clean fingernails, and about a strongbox scratched open by hands that had known exactly where to reach.

"All right," I said. "Here's my engineer's answer, and nobody's going to like it. We don't sell and we don't publish. Not yet. Not first."

Tomas turned on me. "Ilsa—"

"Hear me out, because this is the part where I save our lives again and you can thank me at the funeral. Maren says someone's been feeding our positions to Meridian. Fine. Then the first move isn't the ledgers. The first move is finding out *who*, and what else they've sold. Because if there's a man on this shore handing Meridian our coordinates, then he may have handed them something else too—he may have told them what we pulled from the hold. And if Meridian thinks we might publish, they won't wait for the monsoon to bury the site. They'll come tonight, or tomorrow's first tide, and they won't send divers with knives next time. They'll send the fleet with guns." I pointed north, at the wall of weather eating the horizon. "So: we find the informant, we find out what he's told them, and we use it. Feed him something false. Buy ourselves the gap between what Meridian knows and what we do. That gap is the only dry land left, and I intend to stand on it."

Silence again. But it was a different silence—engineered, load-bearing.

"And then?" Pim asked. "After the gap?"

"Then," Cyrus said, and now he did turn, and he looked at each of us in turn, and I saw that the weary man who'd been running for eleven years had gone somewhere and left someone else standing in his boots, "then we publish. All of it. Every name, every bribe, every write-off. Not to Maren, not for money, not traded away in the dark. Out loud. Where it can't be bought back."

Tomas exhaled like a man surfacing. Pim was grinning, actually grinning, the fool. And me—I was already doing the math on the sea caves, on Odile's charts, on squall cover and tide windows and how much waterproof canvas we had aboard, because somebody on this crew had to turn a suicide into a schedule, and historically that somebody was me.

"One condition," I said.

"Name it," Cyrus said.

"When this is over—when the ledgers are out and Meridian is ash and Cyrus Eriksen is a name ports can say again—you stop. We all stop. No more decamping before dawn. No more sealed compartments. We pick a harbor and we *stay in it*, and the first person who suggests running gets thrown in the water."

Cyrus looked at me for a long moment. Behind him the first spit of the monsoon's leading edge came rattling across the water like gravel thrown at a window.

"Agreed," he said.

Which is when Pim, still watching the cliff road above the beach, went very still and said, in the flat voice he saved for things that mattered:

"There's a lantern on the headland. It's flashing. And Ilsa—it's flashing *our* code."
