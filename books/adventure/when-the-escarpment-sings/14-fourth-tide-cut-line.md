## 14. Fourth Tide: Cut Line

There is a rule in diving that nobody writes down because everybody learns it the hard way: the sea does not care about your schedule. The tide doesn't care about Meridian's fleet, or Ilsa's charts, or the fact that we had exactly two tides left to pull a sealed case of ledgers out of a wreck that had already killed one person I loved and was apparently hungry for seconds.

The fourth tide came in gray and mean, with a chop on the surface like someone shaking a carpet. Ilsa stood at the rail of the *Harrow*, checking my harness straps for the third time, which was two times more than necessary and exactly the right number for how I felt.

"The case goes on your chest," she said. "Not your back. Chest. If you have to cut it loose, you cut it loose."

"I'm not cutting it loose."

"Tomas."

"Ilsa." I pulled the strap tight myself. "Cyrus didn't spend three dives getting his own name back so I could drop it in forty fathoms because the water got rude."

She looked at me the way she looks at an engine making a sound it shouldn't — that flat, measuring look that says she's already decided what she'll do when it fails. Then she thumped my chest plate once, hard, the way you'd knock on a door.

"Come back," she said. "Both of you. That's not a request, it's a specification."

Pim handed me the slate without being asked. On it, in Pim's careful hand: *If the line breaks, go up slow, count to sixty, listen for the bell.* Underneath, smaller: *Odile's rule.*

I hadn't known Odile. That's the thing people forget about me sometimes — I joined after. Everything I know about her is secondhand, worn smooth by retelling, the way a coin gets thin from passing through too many hands. But this rule I'd heard enough times to feel like mine: *count to sixty, listen for the bell.* It was a good rule. Rules made by dead people are the only ones anyone actually follows.

Cyrus was already at the dive ladder, mask pushed up on his forehead, looking out at the water like it owed him money. Which, in a way, it did.

"You ready?" he asked.

"No," I said. "But I've never been ready for anything, and here I am."

That got the ghost of a smile out of him. He went down first. I followed.

* * *

Forty feet down, the world closed like a book.

The Kestrel sat on the bottom the way old wrecks do — not sunken so much as *settled*, as if the sea had finally gotten comfortable with her. The monsoon swell reached us even down there, a slow breathing in the murk, pushing sediment around in lazy curls. Visibility was maybe fifteen feet on a good day. This was not a good day. This was ten feet of green-brown soup with occasional suggestions of steel.

We'd rigged the guide line on the third dive: a shot line from the surface buoy down to the mast, then a traverse line running aft along the hull to the hold, marked every ten meters with knots and tags. Boring work, unglamorous, the kind of thing that keeps you alive. Cyrus had insisted on re-checking every anchor point before this dive, and I'd grumbled about it at the time. I would be thinking about that later. I think about it now, actually. I'll probably think about it forever.

The hold was where we'd found the case yesterday — wedged under a collapsed bulkhead shelf, sealed, heavy, wrapped in oilcloth that some consortium clerk had thought would keep seawater out for thirty years. He'd been mostly right. Inside were ledgers. Inside the ledgers were names. One of them was Cyrus Eriksen, written in a stranger's tidy hand next to numbers that bought him two blacklists and a decade of running.

Today's job was simple: strap the case to me, run the traverse line back, ascend the shot line, get hauled aboard, drink something warm, complain about the coffee. A recovery dive. The easiest kind. Nothing to explore, nothing to prove, just freight.

I've done a hundred recovery dives. I should have known better than to trust one.

Getting the case free took twenty minutes of careful leverage with the pry bar, working blind in the silt we kicked up, feeling more than seeing the bulkhead groan above us. Cyrus held the light steady while I worked, and twice he put a hand on my shoulder — not guiding, just *there*, the way you'd rest a hand on a horse that's about to spook. When the case finally slid free, the whole shelf shifted six inches with a sound like a whale clearing its throat, and both of us froze until the sediment finished falling.

Then the lashing. Chest mount, per Ilsa's specification. Two straps over, two straps under, cinched at the sternum. The case rode against my buoyancy vest like a second heart, square and unyielding. I knocked on it twice. Solid. Thirty years underwater and still drier inside than most of the harbors I've drunk in.

Cyrus gave me the thumbs-up. We turned for home.

I want to be precise about what happened next, because afterward everyone wanted a story with a villain in it, and the truth is that the murk doesn't give you villains. It gives you sound and absence.

First the sound: a snap, somewhere behind us and below, sharp as a knuckle cracking in a quiet room. Not loud. Sound carries strangely down there; a line parting under tension makes less noise than you'd think, just a wet twang swallowed instantly by all that water.

Then the absence: the tag line, which had been running through my gloved fingers knot by knot, went slack. Then it went *gone*.

I stopped. Cyrus stopped a body-length ahead of me and turned. His light swept back along our wake and found nothing — no line, no tags, just the brown-green blur where the Kestrel's hull faded into everything else. Somewhere behind us, in the direction of the hold, the traverse line lay cut on the bottom. Cut clean. Silt doesn't cut lines. Fish don't cut lines. Time doesn't cut lines that fast.

Divers cut lines.

Cyrus's light found my face. Even through the mask I could read him: not panic — calculation, fast and cold, the same look he gets reading weather. He pointed up. The shot line was still our way out; the traverse being cut didn't matter if the vertical line held. We swam for it.

We got maybe halfway before the second line died.

This time I heard it properly — a sawing, off to our left, rhythmic, patient. Someone working a blade through eleven millimeters of rope in zero visibility, taking their time about it, because they knew exactly where we were and exactly where we couldn't go. The shot line, when its slack drifted into my hands, had been cut below us. Cut between us and the surface. Whoever it was had done their homework: they'd let us get deep, let us load up, and then closed the door.

My chest got tight in a way that had nothing to do with the case strapped to it. Forty meters down, murk thick as porridge, no line, no visual reference, and company. Every diver has a private list of ways to die and this one was near the top: lost inside a wreck field, swimming confidently toward a surface that isn't where you think it is, while your air gauge lies to you in a language you can't hear anymore.

Cyrus grabbed my arm. Turned me. In his light, he held up one glove: *sixty*. Count to sixty. Listen for the bell.

Odile's rule. The one Pim had written on my slate four hours ago in the sunshine, when it was just words.

So we counted. I watched Cyrus's lips move behind his mask and matched him, and we hung there in the soup, neutral, barely breathing, while somewhere out in the dark other men breathed harder and moved faster and thought they'd won. Sixty seconds is a long time when you're spending it doing nothing. Your mind fills the space with bad inventory: air remaining, distance to the cliff, current direction, whether the bell on the *Harrow* even works, whether Ilsa is already diving in after us with no plan and worse equipment, because I absolutely believe she would.

At fifty-something, faint as a memory, I heard it. The bell. Ilsa had rigged it off the buoy line — a ship's bell on a lanyard, struck by a weighted line she could jiggle from the deck. One clang, muffled by forty meters of ocean, arriving from the northwest.

Northwest. We'd been swimming southeast.

I will not describe what that did to my insides. Some things a man keeps.

Cyrus heard it too. He took a bearing off the sound the way Odile must have taught him — or maybe taught Maren, who taught him, who knows how that inheritance traveled — and we swam. Slow. Controlled. Away from the cutting sounds, away from the wreck, up at the safest ascent rate two frightened men ever managed, following a bell we couldn't see toward a boat we couldn't find, with a dead man's rules and a live man's ledgers strapped to my ribs.

* * *

The surface, when we broke it, was a betrayal.

I'd been imagining calm water and the *Harrow*'s hull fifty meters away. What we got was the leading edge of the squall — wind-flattened rain, gray on gray, swell running two meters and confused, no boat anywhere in any direction. The bell had gotten us up alive. It had not gotten us *home*. The current had been walking us the whole time, patient as a thief, and the *Harrow* was a smudge on the horizon that might have been a cloud having doubts about itself.

"Three miles," Cyrus said, spitting out seawater. He'd done the math off the cliff line before I'd finished coughing. "Maybe three and a half, and we're set along it, not toward it."

"So we swim."

"We swim." He looked at the case on my chest. "How's your air?"

"Reserve. You?"

"Same." Which meant neither of us had enough to make three miles at the surface, and both of us knew it, and neither of us said it. There's a whole conversation divers have without saying anything, and it's mostly about arithmetic.

We swam anyway. There's not much else. I swam on my back at first, case riding high, kicking, watching the rain hit the water around my face and trying not to think about how big the ocean is relative to a man. Cyrus swam beside me, stroke after stroke, economical, unhurried, like a man crossing a parking lot. I don't know how he did that. I've seen him face down armed salvage crews and consortium lawyers and Ilsa's cooking, and he always wears that same face, and I used to think it was courage. Swimming next to him in open water with the storm coming, I understood it differently. It wasn't courage. It was a man who had spent so long deciding not to drown that the decision had become structural. Load-bearing.

Halfway through the second hour, the cold got serious. Not dramatic cold — the honest kind, the kind that starts negotiating with your muscles individually. My legs were filing complaints. The case, which had felt like armor, started feeling like an anchor with ambitions.

"Talk to me," Cyrus said. He'd noticed. Of course he'd noticed.

"About what?"

"Anything. The first job you ever did. Go."

So I talked. About the trawler off Sennet Point, sixteen years old, my first paid dive, recovering a winch for a captain who paid half upfront and half in whiskey. About how I threw up in my mask and lied about it for years. Cyrus listened and fed me pieces of himself in return — small ones, the way he does. A harbor in a country whose name he still won't say. A winter he spent repairing engines for a monastery because monks feed you and ask nothing. The story of how he learned to navigate by swell-reflection off cliffs, which Odile taught him, which he now taught me, shouting over the wind between strokes: *feel where the waves fold back wrong, that's your wall, walls mean land, land means bearing.*

It occurs to me now that this is what trust is made of. Not oaths. Not votes at fires. Hours of shared arithmetic in cold water, each man keeping the other's count, each man knowing the other could stop swimming and choosing, stroke after stroke, not to.

Somewhere in the third hour my legs quit giving warnings and started giving results, which is to say they stopped working well. The case dragged. I rolled onto my back and my kicks went shallow and stupid, and the rain filled my mouth when I tried to breathe, and for about ninety seconds I conducted a quiet internal negotiation with the ocean about terms.

Then Cyrus's arm hooked across my chest, above the case, and locked.

"Keep kicking," he said. "Badly. Just keep kicking."

"I've got it—"

"You've got the ledgers. I've got you. That's the division of labor today." And he swam, towing me, one-armed, in building seas, toward a coastline we could feel but not see, and he did not once suggest cutting the case loose. I checked. I was watching for it. He never even glanced at the straps.

That's the thing I need the record to show, whoever ends up reading this. Everyone wants to talk about the broadcast, the caves, the moment the escarpment carried Cyrus's voice into every port on the coast. Fine. But I know what the ledgers cost before they cost anything else. They cost three hours of open water and most of two men's reserves, and at no point in those three hours did the man whose name was inside them treat them as worth more than the man carrying them.

* * *

The *Harrow* found us because Ilsa refused to be reasonable.

Later, the official version was that she'd triangulated our last known bearing against the current tables and run a search pattern. The real version, which Pim told me while wrapping me in blankets on the deck, was that Ilsa had put the boat's position on the chart, drawn a circle around every place she'd have been if she were us, and then simply refused to leave any of it unsearched, in that order, for three hours, while the squall tried to take the mast off. Pim had hung off the bow rail the whole time calling bearings, hoarse, soaked, seventeen years old and steadier than half the salvagers I've worked with twice that age.

They spotted us because Pim saw the case flash. My shoulder light, cracked in the wreck, strobing weakly with each wave. A dying flashlight. A boy's eyes. That was the whole margin.

Hands pulled me over the rail. I remember the deck being the most beautiful thing I had ever felt, wood and salt and rust, and Ilsa's face above mine wearing an expression I'd never seen on her — not relief exactly, something rawer, the look of an engineer whose machine came back from a test she'd expected it to fail.

"Specification met," I managed.

"Shut up," she said, and hugged me hard enough to crack a rib I'm fairly sure was already cracked.

Cyrus came over the rail under his own power, which was showing off, and stood dripping on his own deck while Pim attacked the straps on my chest. The case came free. Water poured off it. Inside, dry as a sermon: the ledgers. Names. Numbers. His name.

He crouched next to the case for a long moment, saying nothing, rain coming off him in ropes. Then he looked up — past me, past Ilsa, past Pim — toward the northeast, where the squall was thickening into something with a monsoon's name on it, and beyond it, invisible but absolutely present, the anchorage where Meridian's fleet would be sitting tonight, counting tides, knowing exactly what we'd pulled and exactly where we were, because somebody on shore kept selling us by the hour.

"They cut the line," I said. "Both lines. They wanted the ledgers down there with us."

"I know."

"And they'll know we've got them now. Three miles adrift, half-drowned — they'll have watched the *Harrow* search. They know we lived."

"I know that too." He straightened up. And here's the thing I'll say about Cyrus Eriksen, the truest thing I know: standing there half-frozen on a pitching deck with a storm inbound and a fleet hunting us, he did not look afraid, and he did not look angry, and he did not look like a man planning an escape. He looked like a man who had finally stopped running long enough to turn around.

"Get below, all of you," he said. "Hot everything. Sleep in shifts." He picked up the case himself, and paused at the companionway, and almost smiled. "Tomorrow we have a conversation about what these are worth. All of us. Nobody decides alone."

He went below. I stayed on deck a minute longer, because my legs weren't ready for stairs and because I wanted to see it — the escarpment rising out of the rain to the east, black and enormous, the cliffs that Odile charted and died beneath, humming under the wind the way they say it does before a monsoon. Like the whole coast was tuning up.

Pim appeared beside me, wrapped in the same blanket economy as everyone, and followed my gaze.

"What are they going to sing?" Pim asked.

I didn't have an answer yet. But Ilsa, at the wheel, already turning us toward the caves on Odile's charts, shouted back over the wind like she'd heard the question:

"Something Meridian's going to hate."
