## 15. The Informant's Name

The lamp man's name was Aldous Wren, though for eleven years we'd only ever called him the Chartman, because that was what he sold us—charts, hand-inked, corrected, annotated with soundings no hydrographic office would ever admit to knowing. He kept a cottage above the fishing village of Greyhollow, three switchbacks down from the cliff watch where Pim had stood the night before and watched his windows flash their treacherous arithmetic at Meridian's scout.

I went down alone. That wasn't bravery. It was arithmetic of a different kind. If Wren had been selling us to Meridian for years, then everything we'd bought from him—every safe anchorage, every tide table, every whispered warning about harbor patrols—had been priced with our lives attached, and I wanted to look at the man's face when he explained it. You can't do that with a crew standing behind you. A crew behind you changes what a man will say. Sometimes it changes what you'll do.

Ilsa had wanted to come. Tomas had wanted to come with a knife. I told them the truth, or most of it: that a confrontation goes better one-on-one, that Wren would spook, that we needed him calm and talking. The part I didn't say was that I was afraid of what Tomas would do, and more afraid of what I might not stop him from doing. Eleven years of running makes a certain kind of animal out of you. I didn't trust that animal around an old man in a lamplit cottage.

The village was waking when I came through it—gulls arguing over the fish scrap piles, a woman hosing blood off a cutting table, two boys mending a net with the boneless patience of people who have done it ten thousand times. Nobody looked at me twice. That's the gift of a face like mine, weathered and unremarkable, the face of a man who could be anyone's uncle come to sell rope. I'd spent a decade being grateful for it. That morning I mostly felt like a man wearing a borrowed coat.

Wren's cottage sat at the top of the last switchback, squat and stone, with a lamp room built onto its seaward side—a private little lighthouse for a private little stretch of coast, licensed once and lapsed now, the light still burning out of habit or vanity. Smoke curled from the chimney. I could see his shadow moving behind the salt-hazed glass, bent over his table, doing what he did every morning of his life: inking coastlines.

I knocked.

The shadow went still. A long stillness, the kind that tells you the person inside is deciding whether to answer, and then the bolt drew back and there he was—Aldous Wren, seventy-some years old, small and neat as a hermit crab, ink stains to both elbows, spectacles pushed up into a thatch of white hair.

He looked at me and I watched him understand, all in the space of a breath, exactly who I was and why I'd come. To his credit, he didn't try to close the door. He just stepped back from it.

"Master Eriksen," he said. "I wondered which of you it would be."

"You wondered."

"I've wondered for years." He gestured me inside with a hand that trembled only slightly, and I chose to believe the tremor was age. "Come in. The kettle's on. It's always on. Old men and their kettles."

* * *

The cottage smelled of ink and lamp oil and, underneath it, something older—damp stone, the way everything smells on this coast eventually, the escarpment seeping into the bones of whatever stands on it. Charts covered every wall. Not for sale, these; these were his life's work, decades of coastline rendered in a hand so fine it looked printed. I found the Kestrel's survey grounds among them, marked in faded red, and had to look away.

He poured tea into two cups that didn't match, and set the chipped one in front of me, which was either an insult or an apology, and with Wren I suspected you never got to know which.

"I'll not waste your morning," he said, sitting down across from me with the careful economy of a man whose joints have opinions. "You're here about the lamp."

"I'm here about the lamp."

"Then I'll tell you, and you can decide what to do with an old man afterward." He wrapped both hands around his cup. "But first I'd ask you a question, if you'll allow it. Just one."

I allowed it.

"When did you decide I'd sold you?" he asked. "Not last night, when the boy saw the light. Before that. There was a moment, wasn't there, when you looked at all those years and all those charts and thought: he always knew too much. When was it?"

I hated him a little for the question, because the answer was true and he knew it would be true. "The night Odile died," I said. "We got out of that water because someone had told Meridian exactly where we'd be. And every chart you ever sold us put us exactly where we needed to be, and exactly where they could find us. I decided that night. I just didn't have proof for eleven years."

He nodded slowly, as if I'd confirmed something he'd been carrying even longer than I had. "And did you ever wonder," he said, "why a man with my charts—my contacts, my knowledge of every patrol schedule on this coast—would still be living in a cottage like this? Selling ink to smugglers at his age? A man with what I know should be rich, Master Eriksen. Should be fat and warm in some harbor city. Why do you suppose I'm here?"

"Because you like the coast."

"Because I'm paying," he said. "Have been for eleven years. Every chart I sold you was priced to cover what I owe, and what I owe doesn't fit in a boat, so I've been paying it out in ink and lamplight, one debt at a time, and I am not nearly finished." He took off his spectacles and polished them on his sleeve, a fussy little gesture, and I realized his hands were shaking now in earnest. "You think Meridian bought me. Everyone would think that. It's the tidy story. But nobody bought me, boy. I sold *first*."

* * *

He told it in the flat voice of a man who has rehearsed a confession for so long it has worn smooth, like a stone carried in a pocket.

Eleven years ago, his son—Tobias, a name I'd never heard, a man I'd never met—had signed on as a supercargo with a consortium shipping line out of Vantage. Good wage, respectable work, the kind of berth a father weeps over. And Tobias, being young and being Wren's son, with his father's eye for figures and his father's weakness for certainty, had begun to notice that the manifests didn't match the cargo. That ships listed as lost were sometimes not lost. That the consortium's insurance claims read like fiction.

"He came to me with it," Wren said. "Burst in here at two in the morning, white as gull-belly, with a sheaf of copied pages. Said the numbers didn't lie, and he'd learned that from me, at this very table. Said he was going to take them to the port magistrate." The old man's laugh had no humor in it at all. "I told him not to. God forgive me, I told him the consortium was bigger than magistrates. And he said—this I'll carry to whatever grave I get—he said, *'Then what's the numbers for, Da?'*"

The kettle ticked as it cooled. Outside, a gull screamed at something, or at nothing.

"Three weeks later they had him. Not arrested—nothing so clean. They called in his debts. He'd signed notes when he took the berth, standard practice, and the consortium held all of them, and they called every one at once. A sum no supercargo's wage could touch in ten lifetimes. And they made it clear what the debt was for. It was for the copies. They knew about the copies because he'd shown them to a friend for advice, and the friend had sold him before the ink was dry." Wren's voice didn't rise or fall. It just went on, level as a sounding line. "They gave him a choice. The debt, with interest, or a quiet berth on a quiet ship doing quiet work, and his father's cooperation when it was asked for."

"And what they asked for," I said, because my throat had gone tight, "was the Kestrel."

"They asked where the survey vessel would be, and when, and who'd be aboard." He met my eyes then, and I made myself hold them. "I gave them the position. I gave them the tide window. I told them Odile Charbonneau ran her crew like a family and kept no armed watch, because she trusted the coast and the coast had always provided. And eleven people went to the bottom of the sea because a man in a cottage couldn't bear to watch his son drown in paper."

The tea had gone cold in my hands. I set it down because I couldn't feel it anymore.

"You testified too," I said. "Against me. You were at the inquiry."

"I was at the inquiry," he agreed, "and I said what I was told to say, which was that you'd been seen taking payment from consortium rivals, that your salvage permits were forgeries, that you'd been asking after consortium shipping lanes. All true, in the way a knife is true. And the blacklist they wrote with my words has followed you across two borders and eleven years, and I inked every correction to every chart you ever bought with those same hands." He looked down at them, the stained, steady, murderous hands. "The blacklist wasn't earned, Master Eriksen. It was purchased. I'm the purchase price. I've been the purchase price every day since."

* * *

I want to tell you I stood up and put my fist through his table. I want to tell you I said something with weight to it, some line that would have sounded right in one of Odile's stories, something about justice or the sea keeping its own accounts. What I actually did was sit in a dead man's father's kitchen and feel the hatred drain out of me like water through split planks, and what came in behind it was so much worse.

Pity.

Because I knew the shape of what he'd done. I'd lived inside its cousin for eleven years. A man with one thing in the world he can't lose, and a machine that knows it, and the machine asking, politely, in the language of ledgers and interest: *the boy, or the coast.* Every man on my crew had made his own version of that trade to end up running with me. Ilsa had walked out of a consortium yard with her tools and a forged discharge. Tomas had left a brother behind a debt wall in Vantage that he still sends money to, quietly, hopelessly. Pim—Pim had been sold to the sea by people who should have kept him, and I'd fished him out, and I'd never once asked myself what I'd have done if the machine had come for someone I loved and offered me a stranger's name instead.

I'd have given it. That was the thing sitting in my chest like ballast. Given a son and a choice, I'd have given the name too, and hated myself in a lamplit cottage for the rest of my life, which was, I understood at last, precisely what Aldous Wren had been doing.

"You could have told me," I said. "Any year. Any one of eleven years. You could have told me and I'd have—"

"What?" He almost smiled. "Turned me over to the consortium? To the magistrates? To Maren Voss, who'd have used me for a lever and dropped me when the lever bent?" He shook his head. "You'd have done what was right, boy. That's the trouble with you. It's what everyone says about you, behind the blacklist. Eriksen does the right thing and it costs him everything, and everyone watches, and nobody helps. I couldn't have stood being helped by you. Not after what I'd cost you."

"Odile trusted you," I said. "She bought your charts for six years."

"I know." His voice cracked, finally, one hairline fracture in all that flatness. "Her last letter's in that drawer. I've never had the courage to burn it and never the courage to read it twice. She wrote to thank me for a correction on the Greyhollow shoals and she called me *the honest man of the coast*." He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "Do you know what it is, to be called honest by a woman you drowned?"

The lamp in his little lamp room hissed and flickered. Down in the village, the morning went on—nets mended, blood hosed off tables, boys laughing at something. The escarpment stood over all of it, gray and patient, keeping every secret it had ever been given.

"Meridian doesn't know I've told you," Wren said at last, lowering his hands. "They think I'm still theirs. That's the only thing I have left to give anyone—the fact that they trust me. The scout that's been signaling my lamp thinks it's signaling a man who'll keep it fed with your position until the fleet arrives." He looked at me, and behind the age and the fear there was something else, something that had been waiting eleven years for this morning: a man asking for a job. "So use me, Master Eriksen. Feed me lies and I'll feed them to Meridian, and I'll do it until the storm comes down and buries us all, if that's what it costs to make the ledger balance. It's the only arithmetic I've got left."

I sat there a long moment, looking at the charts on the walls, at the fine and faithful lines of a coastline drawn by a man who'd sold the one true thing he ever charted. And I thought about Odile's letter in the drawer, unread twice, and about the lamp that would burn tonight with lies in its signal, and about my crew up on the cliff waiting to hear what I'd learned and what I'd do about it.

"Then here's what you'll signal them tonight," I said, and leaned across the table, and gave Aldous Wren the first lie of his redemption.

* * *

I climbed back up the switchbacks with the wind rising off the water and the first leading edge of the monsoon smearing the horizon like thumb-smudged charcoal. Six tides, Ilsa had said, and we'd spent three. Somewhere behind me, Meridian's fleet was coming on, and somewhere ahead of us the Kestrel lay in her grave with a strongbox that had been opened by hands we hadn't identified, and every hour the arithmetic got worse.

But it wasn't the arithmetic I was carrying up that cliff. It was Wren's face, and the sound of his voice saying *the honest man of the coast*, and the terrible, inconvenient truth that the man who'd built my ruin had built it out of the same material I'd have used: love, and fear, and a son's name.

Tomas was waiting at the top, arms crossed, reading my face the way he read water before a dive.

"Well?" he said. "What did the old man say?"

I looked out at the darkening sea, at the place where the Kestrel slept, and I told him the truth, all of it, and watched it land on him like cold water. And when I finished, I said the part that mattered—the part that would decide everything for the next three tides:

"Tomas, the ledgers in that hold aren't just the consortium's. Meridian's name is in them too. And there's something else—Wren says the strongbox that was opened down there wasn't opened by Meridian's divers."
