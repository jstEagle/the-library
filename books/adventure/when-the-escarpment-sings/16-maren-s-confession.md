## 16. Maren's Confession

There is a particular kind of silence that comes from a man deciding whether to throw you off his boat.

I had heard it before—once in a tribunal room in Halvard, once across a negotiating table in Port Meridian itself—but never quite like this. Cyrus Eriksen stood at the rail of his own deck with the sea behind him and ten years of my lies between us, and he did not speak. He just watched me the way you watch a tide gauge: waiting to see how far I'd rise, and whether I'd go out again.

I'd rehearsed this speech for a decade. In hotel rooms. In the back of consortium transports. Once, memorably, in a bathhouse in Sarnholt while two of Aldous Wren's accountants argued about shipping tariffs on the other side of a paper wall thin enough to hear a coin drop through. I had delivered it flawlessly in my head perhaps four hundred times. Every version ended with Cyrus forgiving me, because that was the version I liked best, and a person rehearses the ending she wants.

The real version began with me saying, "You're going to want to sit down," and him saying, "No."

Fair enough.

* * *

Let me tell you what coercion looks like, since everyone imagines it as a gun to the temple. It isn't. A gun is almost merciful; a gun gives you a story you can live with afterward—I had no choice, they would have killed me. What the consortium gave me was slower and much more elegant. They gave me a sister with a degenerative lung condition and a clinic bill longer than the Kestrel herself. They gave me a career that existed entirely at their pleasure. And then they gave me a choice dressed up as a kindness: testify that Cyrus Eriksen sabotaged the survey vessel, or watch them let the debt collectors do what debt collectors do.

They didn't even have to threaten her directly. Wren's people simply mentioned, over brandy, that medical care was expensive these days, and wasn't it fortunate Maren had such steady work ahead of her. The testimony was already drafted. All I had to do was read it aloud in a voice that sounded like mine.

That was the part I could never forgive myself for—not the fear, but the draftsmanship. They hadn't needed to write it well. They'd needed only to write it plausibly, and I supplied the rest. My face. My credentials. My history beside him, three seasons running the same coast, so that when I said *he was capable of it*, every ear in the room believed me, because who would know better than his partner?

Nobody asked why a woman who'd spent six years diving beside Cyrus Eriksen would suddenly believe him a saboteur. Nobody asked what I got out of it. That was the genius of it. They made my betrayal look like conscience.

And here is the detail that has kept me company every night since: I knew, even as I read their words, that he hadn't done it. Not suspected. *Knew.* You spend enough hours on a line with someone, hauling them up half-frozen, sharing the last dry shirt, learning which silences mean *hold* and which mean *run*—you know. I knew the way you know your own handwriting. And I stood in front of a tribunal and lied about a man who had once given me his last oxygen bottle without being asked, because they told me my sister would breathe easier if I did.

She didn't, incidentally. She lived another four years, most of them good ones, and she died not knowing what I'd done to keep the clinic paid. I used to think that was mercy. Now I think it was just one more thing I stole from myself—the chance to be forgiven by the only person whose forgiveness was ever about anything.

* * *

Cyrus still hadn't spoken. Tomas had drifted to the far rail with the studied casualness of a man positioning himself between a stranger and his captain. Somewhere below, Ilsa's engine turned over with the patient rhythm of a heart that had decided, provisionally, to keep beating.

"You testified against me," Cyrus said finally. Not a question. He wanted it said back to him, in the open air, where it couldn't hide.

"I did." I kept my hands visible on the rail. An old habit around men I've wronged. "And I'm going to tell you exactly how, and then you can decide what I'm worth, which is probably nothing, but you should price it yourself rather than take Wren's valuation."

So I told him. The brandy. The mention of the clinic. The drafted testimony with its careful phrases—*in my professional judgment*, *his temperament had changed after the third season*, *I observed modifications to the ballast system consistent with deliberate scuttling*. Phrases I had never observed anything of. Phrases written by a lawyer who had never been wet past his ankles.

"The ledgers," I said, "will show all of it. Payments to tribunal members. Payments to me—line item, dated, initialed. Wren kept records of everything, because records were how he owned people. He thought documentation was armor." I almost smiled. "He never imagined someone might use it as ammunition."

"And you've known this for ten years." His voice was flat, but flat the way ice is flat—one misstep from catastrophe. "Ten years, and you're telling me now. On my boat. With Meridian's fleet forty hours out."

"Yes."

"Why?"

Because I owed you an apology no one should have to hear. Because there is no currency in which this debt can be paid and I have stopped looking for one. Because I am fifty-one years old and I have bought ships and bribed harbormasters and spent a decade clawing my way up through the consortium's own machinery toward the one thing I actually wanted, and now that I'm here I find I don't want anything except—

What I said was: "Because I hired you as bait, and bait deserves to know what it's bait for."

"That's not an answer."

"No," I agreed. "It's the truest thing adjacent to one."

* * *

Here is what I did with the decade, since he was going to ask anyway, or worse, wasn't.

I stayed. That was the first cruelty of it—coercion doesn't end when the testimony ends. Wren's people made it clear that a witness who recanted was worth less than a witness who doubled down, so I doubled down. I took the promotions they dangled. I sat on review boards. I learned to smile at men whose ledgers I later read, and I smiled at them knowing what the ledgers held: the bribes, the insurance frauds, the ships deliberately sunk to collect on hull policies and bury survey data that might have opened the coast to independent operators. Ships with crews aboard, sometimes. The consortium called them *attrition events*. I signed memos using that phrase. I want that on the record somewhere, wherever records go when empires scatter: I wrote the words *attrition event* with my own hand.

And all the while I bought my way back. Quietly. A stake here, a favor there. I funded the harbor survey that flagged the Kestrel site for recovery—not because I wanted the wreck raised, but because I wanted to be the one holding the permit when it happened. I cultivated contacts in two port authorities. I learned which of Wren's lieutenants drank, which gambled, which could be flipped. Ten years of patience, and do you know what I discovered about patience? It doesn't heal anything. It just gives the wound time to develop a personality.

Odile. Yes. I'll say her name, since no one else on this boat seems able to.

I have gone back and forth for ten years on whether Odile's death was murder or arithmetic, and I have finally settled on the truth, which is that with the consortium there is no difference. She found the ledgers' existence—maybe not the ledgers themselves, but the pattern they recorded. She started asking questions in ports where questions get answered with hull breaches. The Kestrel went down in weather that other vessels sailed through comfortably that same night. Meridian's fleet was in the area. Wren's office received a report on the sinking before the coast guard did. I have seen the timestamps. I have spent ten years making sure I would be able to see them, so that when the moment came I could stand in front of the one man Odile loved like a son and say: *it was them, it was always them, and I helped them destroy you instead of helping you destroy it.*

I did not kill her. But I held the door for the people who did, and I took their money, and I slept soundly, mostly, because that is the thing about cowardice nobody warns you—it's comfortable. It fits like an old coat. You stop noticing you're wearing it until the day you catch your reflection and see what everyone else has been seeing for a decade.

* * *

"So," Cyrus said, when I ran out of decade. "Maren Voss, repentant. What does the confession cost? Because everything costs. I learned that from watching you."

The crew had gathered without seeming to gather—Tomas at the rail, arms crossed; Pim halfway up the rigging ladder like a cat that hadn't decided about the dog; Ilsa in the companionway with grease on her forearms and an expression suggesting she'd happily rebuild me into an anchor winch. Good. Let them hear it. If I was going to be judged, I preferred the whole jury.

"It costs nothing," I said. "That's the point. I'm not selling you anything. I have money—consortium money, which is to say blood money, and I'd sooner burn it than spend it on myself, though I'll note the ledgers are worth more than any of you can imagine and I will help you steal every page of them. But the confession itself—" I spread my hands. "Free. Cheapest thing I own."

"Then what do you want?"

And there it was. The question I'd been avoiding through four hundred rehearsals, because in the rehearsals I always had an answer ready and none of them were true.

What I wanted was impossible. I wanted the tribunal unheld. I wanted the blacklist unwritten. I wanted Odile alive to tell me I'd been a fool in the way she used to—affectionately, with salt spray in her laugh—or furiously, which was also affectionate, with Odile. I wanted the version of the last ten years where I'd torn up the testimony and sold my boat and taken my sister to a cheaper clinic and come to Cyrus with empty pockets and clean hands and said *help me fight them*, and he'd have said no, because that's what Cyrus says, but he'd have said it while already planning the fight.

None of that was available. So I said the only true thing left.

"I want to be there when it ends. That's all. When the ledgers go out—however they go out, whatever you decide to do with them, sell them, burn them, nail them to Wren's door—I want to be standing next to you when it happens. Not for forgiveness. I've done the math on forgiveness and I know what it costs and I'm not asking you to pay it. I'm asking for something smaller and more selfish. I want to witness it. I spent ten years building the machine that buried all of you. I want to stand in the weather when it comes apart."

Pim spoke from the rigging, young voice cracking slightly on the wind. "Why would we want you there?"

"Pim," Tomas said.

"No, it's fair." I looked up at the kid. "It's a fair question. Here's the answer: because you don't get to choose who shares your consequences. I chose to make your lives my consequence. That's done. The only question left is whether I stand in it honestly or run from it the way I've run from everything else, and I am—" my voice did something I hadn't authorized, thinned, roughened— "I am so tired of running. You'd know something about that, all of you. You've made an art of it. I'm asking to stop."

Silence again. But a different silence. The first one had been a decision pending. This one was heavier, sadder—the silence of people recognizing a disease they all had.

It was Ilsa, of all people, who broke it. She came the rest of the way up from the engine space, wiped her hands on a rag that made them dirtier, and looked at me with the flat appraisal of a woman pricing scrap metal.

"The dive rig I rebuilt," she said. "The old pattern. Odile's pattern. Where did you get the specifications?"

Ah. There it was—the question under the questions. "From her notebooks. I bought them from the coast guard evidence locker after the tribunal. Legally, if you can believe it. Wren wanted them destroyed; I paid the clerk triple to lose them instead. I've kept them for ten years." I reached into my coat—slowly, hands high, because Tomas had gone very still—and drew out a oilcloth packet, swollen with water-stained pages. "I was going to give them to whoever finished what she started. I hoped it would be you. I feared it would be Meridian. Those were the only two outcomes I worked toward for a decade, and I'm aware of how little that's worth. But the charts are hers. Her hand. Her route notes. Including—" I set the packet on the deck between us, gently, like setting down something sleeping— "the sea caves along the escarpment. The ones she charted and never showed anyone. The ones I suspect you're going to need in about thirty-six hours."

Cyrus stared at the packet the way a man stares at a ghost he'd stopped believing in. Then at me.

"You've known about the caves."

"I've known about everything," I said. "Knowing things is the only skill they ever gave me. It seemed time it finally did some good."

Below us, the tide was turning. Above us, the first outriders of the monsoon sat on the horizon like a held breath. And somewhere out in that darkening water, Meridian's fleet was coming for the ledgers, and for the crew, and for whatever was left of the truth—and for the first time in ten years, I was not afraid of what was coming.

I was afraid of something much worse: hoping.
