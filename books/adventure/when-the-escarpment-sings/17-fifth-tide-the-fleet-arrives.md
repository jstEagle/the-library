## 17. Fifth Tide: The Fleet Arrives

I've spent twenty years reading water the way other people read faces, and I'll tell you what the sea said at dawn on the fifth tide: *here they come.*

The barometer had been falling all night—not the gentle slide of ordinary weather but the long, greedy drop of something with an appetite. I'd been up since the third bell anyway, because sleep and I haven't been on speaking terms since Meridian's scout ship first showed its unmarked hull on our horizon. I stood in the wheelhouse of the *Magpie* with a mug of tea going cold in my hand and watched the eastern water change color.

Not storm color. Storm color is green-black, bruised, honest about its intentions. This was worse. This was a flat, oily calm stretching from the escarpment's base to the edge of sight, the kind of stillness that means the atmosphere has drawn back its fist.

And through that stillness, out of the haze where the horizon should have been, came the fleet.

* * *

I counted them twice because the first count made me feel stupid. Six vessels. Not six boats—vessels. Two converted trawlers with crane arms folded like mantis forelimbs, a supply tender riding low and heavy, a fast patrol cutter whose lines I recognized from a port authority auction catalog three years back, and behind them all, sitting fat and smug on the water like it owned the tide itself, the *Corvus*. Meridian's flagship. I'd seen her once before, from a distance, in another country, another life, when Cyrus pointed her out to me across a harbor and said, "That's the company that owns the law you're standing under." I hadn't understood then what he meant. I understood now.

They came in without hurry. That was the thing that turned my stomach—the unhurriedness of it. A desperate fleet runs. This one *strolled*. They took their positions over the Kestrel site with the casual precision of men laying out place settings, anchoring in a wide arc that said, as clearly as any signal lamp: *we know exactly where you are, and we are not worried.*

"They're not even trying to hide," Pim said beside me. The kid had come up silent—I still don't know how someone that gangly moves that quietly—and was squinting east through the long glass. "Why aren't they trying to hide?"

"Because hiding is for people who think they might lose."

Cyrus's voice came from the companionway, and I didn't need to turn around to picture his face. Twenty years of running had carved him into something lean and weathered, but there was a particular set to his jaw when the arithmetic went bad—a flatness, a stillness, like a man doing sums he already knows will come out wrong.

"Six hulls," Tomas said, emerging behind him, hair still wet from the dawn check on the dive gear. "We've got one salvage tug with a bad starter motor and a crew of four."

"Five," Maren said from the stern, where she'd been sleeping aboard since her parley skiff arrived. Nobody had invited her to stay. Nobody had told her to leave either, which in our crew's language amounts to the same thing. She climbed into the wheelhouse with the unhurried confidence of a woman who had bet everything on this hand and couldn't afford to look nervous about it. "You're forgetting my boat."

"Your boat is a survey skiff with a cracked transom," Ilsa—me, that is—said, and then realized I'd done the thing I do when I'm frightened, which is narrate myself in the third person like a eulogy. Fine. Ilsa's voice, then. My voice. "Your boat is a survey skiff with a cracked transom and you know it."

"My boat," Maren said, "is faster than anything Meridian thinks matters."

"That's the problem," I said. "They don't think it matters. Nothing we have matters to them. Look at them down there." I gestured at the arc of anchors taking shape below us—we were holding position on the shelf above the wreck site, close enough to see the fleet's deck lights burning pale against the grey morning. "Six ships. They didn't send six ships for a box of ledgers. They sent six ships to make sure nobody ever asks questions again. We're not competitors to them, Cyrus. We're a mess they've come to clean up."

Nobody argued. That was the worst part. In our crew, silence is a full sentence, and the silence in that wheelhouse was a whole paragraph.

* * *

Here's what you need to understand about me, so the rest of this makes sense: I am not brave. I want that in the record somewhere, in ink, preferably notarized. Tomas dives into black water on a broken line and calls it Tuesday. Pim once spent nine hours alone on a cliff face in sleet to get a bearing and came down whistling. Cyrus walks toward the things that ruined him like they owe him money.

Me, I'm the one who counts the exits. I'm the one who keeps the fuel margins, the weather windows, the bail-out plans. When the crew votes with silence, I'm usually the silence leaning hardest toward *no*. Fear is my native language and I speak it fluently, with excellent grammar.

So when I tell you that the plan I'm about to describe was mine, understand what that cost.

* * *

By mid-morning the monsoon's leading edge was visible—not as cloud, at first, but as a line on the water. I've seen it described in pilot charts as a "squall front," which is like describing an avalanche as "some snow." It was a wall of grey-green violence maybe eight miles wide, crawling west toward us at fifteen knots, and behind it the sky had gone the color of a drowned man's lips.

I spread my charts on the chart table while the others watched the fleet. Old charts, coastal surveys, the hydrographic sheets our shore informant had supplied us for years—sheets I now trusted exactly as far as I could throw the filing cabinet they lived in. And underneath those, wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of the drawer where I keep things I can't bear to look at, the other charts.

Odile's charts.

Hand-drawn. Ink on waxed paper, the handwriting slanted and impatient, annotated in three colors of pencil. Odile Voss—no relation to Maren, whatever anyone tells you, though I once saw Maren flinch when someone assumed otherwise—had surveyed this coast before any of us could spell "escarpment." She'd charted the shipping lanes, the anchorages, the wreck sites. And in the margins, in that slanted hand, she'd charted other things. Things no hydrographic office would ever print.

Sea caves.

"Not caves like tourists pay for," I said, when I finally pulled them out and flattened them under the lamp. The crew gathered around, and I watched their faces as the coastline revealed itself in Odile's pencil—the escarpment rendered in cross-section, layer after layer of limestone, and threaded through it, in blue, a network of flooded galleries. "Caves like arteries. The whole cliff is hollow along here. Karst limestone—it dissolves from underneath, always has. There are channels in there big enough to run a boat through at high water, if you know which channel and which hour. Odile mapped them. Every one."

Tomas touched the paper the way he touches everything from before—carefully, like it might apologize for something. "She never mentioned them."

"She never mentioned half of what she knew. Why do you think Meridian killed her?" I heard my own voice go hard and stopped myself. Correction: why do *we believe* Meridian killed her. Certainty is a luxury. But I let the hard version stand, because some mornings you need your anger sharp more than you need it accurate. "A guide who knows a route through the escarpment that isn't on any chart—that's not a person, that's infrastructure. You either own it or you bury it."

"And the ledgers?" Cyrus asked. He hadn't looked at the charts yet. He was looking at me, which was worse.

"The ledgers travel tonight," I said. "Through the caves."

Silence again. But a different flavor of it.

"You want to take a loaded boat into unmapped—" Pim started.

"*Mapped*," I corrected. "Mapped by the best surveyor this coast ever produced, and by the only member of this crew who ever met her. These aren't guesses, Pim. Look at the soundings. Look at the tide annotations—she recorded water levels inside the galleries across a full lunar cycle. This is a working document. It's just been waiting thirty years for someone crazy enough to use it."

"It's not the mapping that worries me," Tomas said quietly. "It's the squall."

"I know."

"You want to enter a cave system during the leading edge of a monsoon."

"I want to enter a cave system *because* of the leading edge of a monsoon." I turned the chart so they could all see the timing annotations. "Every surface vessel on this coast is about to be blind. Radar goes to soup in that rain. Visual contact drops to zero past two hundred meters. Meridian's fleet is anchored over open water watching six approaches to this site, and in nine hours every one of those approaches becomes a wall of noise. They cannot cover the cliffs and the sea lanes and each other. Not in that."

"And if the caves flood?" Maren asked. Practical question. I gave her credit for asking it like an engineer instead of an owner.

"Some sections will surge. Odile marked the surge zones—here, and here. Timing matters. We go in on the rising tide behind the squall's leading edge, ride the high water through the deep galleries, and come out at the north blowhole before the peak of the storm. If we miss the window, we don't drown dramatically. We just get trapped in an air pocket until the tide turns, which is undignified but survivable." I tapped the chart. "Probably."

"'Probably' is doing a lot of work in that sentence," Pim said.

"Pim, I've been doing 'probably' my entire professional life. 'Probably' is the load-bearing word of the salvage industry. Every dive, every crossing, every engine start—'probably.' The difference between us and Meridian is that we admit it."

* * *

Cyrus hadn't spoken through any of it. He stood at the end of the chart table with his arms crossed, looking at Odile's blue pencil lines the way a man looks at a letter he's afraid to open. Finally he reached out and traced one gallery with one finger—from the southern cave mouth, up through the cliff's interior, to the small circle Odile had drawn at the north blowhole, annotated in her cramped hand: *exit good in all but worst weather.*

"She found this route before the Kestrel sank," he said.

"Years before."

"So she knew. All that time on the water with us, all those runs, and she knew there was a way through the escarpment the whole time, and she never—" His voice did something complicated and stopped.

"She was saving it," Tomas said. "For exactly this. For the day someone needed to move something past Meridian's eyes. She just didn't live to see the day arrive."

"She left it for us," Pim said. Simply. No drama. The kid has a gift for saying enormous things in small packages. "She drew these knowing someday somebody would need them. That's not a chart. That's a letter."

I looked around the wheelhouse—at Tomas with his diver's calm stretched thin over something rawer, at Pim gripping the chart table's edge, at Maren watching Cyrus with an expression I couldn't file under anything, at Cyrus himself, finger still resting on the dead woman's pencil line.

And I felt the fear—the real fear, the six-hulls-and-a-monsoon fear—do something it almost never does. It turned over. It changed state. Somewhere between the chart table and my own chest, the terror of what we were about to do compressed into something denser and hotter, the way coal becomes diamond if you squeeze it long enough and don't mind being squeezed.

Fear says run. Audacity says run *cleverly*, and make them watch.

"Here's the shape of it," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect. "The *Magpie* stays here, anchored, lights burning, making ourselves obvious. Meridian expects us to run seaward or hole up—they'll keep their picket boats on the offshore approaches. We feed them that expectation. Pim's decoy signals continue on schedule, same frequencies, same rhythm, so their intelligence picture doesn't wobble."

"And the ledgers?" Cyrus asked.

"The ledgers go in Maren's skiff. Small, fast, low profile—she's right, it's the only hull we have they'd ignore. Skiff enters the south cave mouth at slack-before-high, rides Odile's deep gallery north through the cliff, and comes out the blowhole on the far side of the headland, beyond Meridian's picket line, inside the squall's rain shadow. From there it's forty miles of foul-weather running to a port where nobody owes Meridian anything."

"Who pilots the skiff?" Maren asked.

"You do. You know her handling better than anyone." I held up a hand before she could object. "But you don't know the caves, and neither does anyone else alive. So the skiff carries a navigator who does—or rather, who carries the woman who did." I put my hand flat on Odile's charts. "Pim navigates. Odile steers."

Pim went very still. "Me?"

"You spotted the informant's signal from a cliff in failing light. You read water like Odile read water—you can't help it, it's in how you look at things. And you're the lightest of us, which matters in a shallow-draft gallery with surge zones." I shrugged, as if the next part were nothing. "Also, you're the only one I trust to follow a dead woman's pencil marks without improvising."

"What about the rest of us?" Tomas asked.

"We stay visible. Cyrus, you and Tomas make one more dive on the site—loud, well-lit, deliberately slow. Give Meridian something to watch. If they think we're still hunting the hold, they'll keep their divers and their cranes focused on the wreck instead of the cliffs. Every eye on the water is an eye not on the cave mouth." I looked at Cyrus. "It also gives you a reason to be in the water when the skiff runs, in case anything goes wrong and we need a rescue that doesn't exist on any manifest."

"And you?" Cyrus asked.

"I'm the decoy-in-chief. The *Magpie* makes herself very interesting at dusk—running lights, generator noise, a nice conspicuous radar signature doing slow circles over the site. When the squall hits, I kill the lights, weigh anchor, and play ghost. By the time they realize the *Magpie*'s radar return has split into two contacts heading opposite directions, the skiff will be inside the cliff and the rain will be eating their instruments."

Maren spoke into the quiet. "And if the galleries surge early? If the storm arrives ahead of schedule?"

"Then Pim brings the skiff back out the way it went in, we burn the charts, and we spend the rest of our lives being very humble fishermen somewhere with no extradition treaty." I met her eyes. "You asked to be here when it ends. This is what 'when it ends' looks like from the engineering side. Still glad you came?"

Something moved across her face—not quite a smile, not quite its opposite. "Ask me tomorrow."

"That's the spirit."

* * *

We spent the afternoon preparing, and I won't lie to you: my hands shook through most of it. Sealing the ledgers in waterproof drums, lashing them amidships in Maren's skiff where they'd sit lowest and safest. Checking the skiff's bilge pump three times, then a fourth, because fear with nowhere to go gets channeled into maintenance. Loading flare cartridges, spare batteries, a backup compass, and—after a moment's thought—one of Odile's actual pencils, which I handed to Pim without explanation and which Pim took without needing any.

At dusk the fleet's deck lights came on all along their arc, bright and orderly as a town. Through the glasses I watched figures moving on the *Corvus*'s deck, unhurried, confident. One of them, I'd have sworn, was laughing.

Let them laugh. I've been afraid my whole life, and I've learned the one true thing about it: fear is just energy that hasn't been given a job yet. Tonight it has one.

Full dark came down. The western sky flickered—silent heat lightning inside the squall wall, like a lamp being switched on and off in a room where something enormous is turning over in its sleep. The barometer needle lay flat against its stop, giving up entirely.

On the chart table, Odile's blue lines waited. At the south cave mouth, the sea had begun to mutter against the limestone, testing the door.

I lit the *Magpie*'s running lights, loud and proud, a decoy burning bright for six ships full of enemies, and keyed the radio for Pim, one last time before the rain swallowed everything.

"Cave mouth in ninety minutes. Ride Odile's line, trust the soundings, and kid—if the numbers ever stop matching the chart, you turn around. Dead heroes don't publish ledgers."

Static, then Pim's voice, thin through the interference but steady as a sounding line:

"Copy, Ilsa. Taking her in."

And the escarpment opened its throat.
