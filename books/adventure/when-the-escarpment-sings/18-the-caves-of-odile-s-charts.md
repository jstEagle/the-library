## 18. The Caves of Odile's Charts

I want to say, first, that I never asked to be the one holding the charts. Ilsa handed them to me at the cave mouth with the solemnity of a priest passing a relic, and Tomas said something like "no backsies," and Cyrus just looked at me the way he looks at weather coming in—like it's bad news but at least it's honest news. And that was that. The dead woman's maps went into my waterproof satchel, and I became, for one night, the most important person on the escarpment coast.

Which is a sentence I'd like to have carved on my tombstone if this goes wrong: *He held the charts.*

The squall hit while we were still rigging the entry line. Not the monsoon itself—that was still an hour out, a black wall sitting on the horizon like something the sea had decided to keep—but its advance guard, the leading edge of wind and spit that Ilsa had promised us would cover our run and then, presumably, kill us all. The rain came sideways off the water and hit the cliff face so hard it bounced back up again, which meant we were being rained on twice, which felt like the escarpment's idea of a joke.

"Talk to me, Pim." Cyrus's voice through the mask of his hood, close enough that I could feel it more than hear it.

I unrolled the first chart against the rock, pinning the corners with my knees. Odile drew them in pencil on waxed paper, and a decade of salt air had softened her lines until they looked less like navigation and more like handwriting—which, I suppose, they were. Her hand. The only hand she'd ever extended to me, and even that posthumously.

"Entry channel runs thirty meters along the base," I said. "She marked a shelf collapse here—" I tapped a smudged X "—so we hug the north wall until the second seam, then up through the blowhole passage."

"And after the blowhole?"

"After the blowhole, she wrote a note." I turned the chart toward him. In faded graphite, slanting letters: *Don't trust the ceiling.*

"She was fun at parties, clearly," Tomas said.

"She was thorough," Cyrus said. "There's a difference."

* * *

The entry channel swallowed us in pieces. I went first, because the person holding the charts goes first or else the charts are just decoration, and behind me came Tomas with the ledgers lashed to his chest in the waterproof case, and behind him Ilsa with the dive rig she'd rebuilt from memory and spite, and Cyrus took the rear because Cyrus always takes the rear, on the theory that whatever eats a crew should eat the captain last.

The water inside was black and moving. Not waves—currents, slow muscular ones, the caves breathing with the tide in a rhythm you could feel against your chest like a second heartbeat. My lamp made a small yellow room around me and nothing else. Everything beyond it was the kind of dark that has weight.

Second seam. I found it by touch, a vertical crack in the rock where the current changed temperature, exactly where Odile's pencil said it would be, and I remember thinking—with a clarity that felt almost rude given the circumstances—*she touched this stone. Her fingers were where mine are.* Ten years dead and her measurements still held true to the width of a hand. There are worse things to be than accurate. There are worse things to be than remembered.

The blowhole passage was the worst part, because Odile had been right about the ceiling. It wasn't a ceiling so much as a suggestion—one that kept revising itself downward as we finned through, and twice I heard Tomas's tank scrape stone above me and flinched hard enough to swallow half the sea. But the passage opened, eventually, into the first dry chamber, and we surfaced in an air pocket that smelled of wet limestone and old bird nests, and for about four seconds nobody said anything, and those four seconds were the finest silence of my life.

"Chart two," Cyrus said.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about navigating for a crew in a collapsing sea cave during the leading edge of a monsoon: it is mostly waiting. You find the next marker, you confirm it against the chart, you point, and then you stand there watching three people you love more than your own pulse haul sixty pounds of consortium crimes through water that wants it back.

Chamber two flooded to the waist. Chamber three had lost its floor sometime in the decade since Odile walked it—a section of the chart simply ended in a scribbled margin note, *floor gone?, floor gone*, and I got to be the one to tell everyone that our dead mentor had also had questions. We crossed it on a ledge the width of my boot, ledgers passed hand to hand over the drop, and below us the tide filled the gap with a sound like grinding teeth.

It was somewhere in chamber four that I heard the boats.

Not Meridian's engines—I'd know those by now, the way you know a dog that's bitten you—but the sound the storm made funneling into the cave mouths behind us, a low moaning note that rose and fell. Except it wasn't the storm. Storms don't repeat themselves. This moan had a pattern. Three short, one long. Three short, one long.

"They're pinging the entrances," Ilsa said. She'd gone very still, the way she does when she's doing math she hates. "Sonar. They're mapping the caves from outside."

"Can they see us?" Tomas asked.

"No. But they can see the caves. And when the monsoon makes landfall and they can't run their fleet anymore, they'll put divers in at every mouth they've found and wait for us to come out." She looked at me. "How many mouths does she show?"

I counted. Counted again, because the answer mattered too much to get wrong the first time. "Seven. But—" I turned the third chart over, and there it was, in the same slanting hand, almost hidden in the corner behind a depth notation. A small drawing. An eighth opening. Not drawn as a cave mouth at all—it was marked as a *blowhole*, high on the cliff face, with a single word beside it:

*Only in a storm.*

"She found it in weather," I said slowly, working it out the way she must have worked it out, alone, years before any of us existed to her. "The surge drives water up the shaft hard enough to vent spray out the top. It's not an entrance in calm seas. It's only a door when the world is ending."

Nobody said anything. Then Cyrus laughed—one short, disbelieving breath—and said, "She left us a door that only opens in a catastrophe."

"Sounds familiar," Tomas said.

* * *

The monsoon made landfall while we were in chamber six.

I know because the whole cave announced it. Every crack and seam in the escarpment began to speak at once—the pressure change driving air through passages that hadn't breathed in years, a rising chord of groans and whistles that Ilsa later swore sounded like whale song and Tomas swore sounded like the escarpment screaming and I privately thought sounded like both, like the mountain singing through its teeth. That's the title of this whole story, I think. What the escarpment sings. It sings in bad weather. It sang for Odile. It was singing for us.

The water in chamber six started to rise. Fast.

"Blowhole shaft," I shouted over it, already moving. "Forty meters up the east wall, per chart four. The surge will push us up it—we ride it, we don't fight it."

"You want us to let the ocean throw us out of a mountain," Ilsa said.

"I want us to do what the chart says."

Cyrus looked at me across the flooding chamber, and what passed between us wasn't words. It was the thing he'd been teaching me since I was sixteen and useless: *the guide's job is to know which risks are real.* He nodded once.

We tied ourselves together—Tomas with the case, Ilsa with the rig stripped to essentials, Cyrus last with the line coiled over his shoulder—and I swam for the shaft with Odile's chart pressed inside my jacket, against my heart, which is where I'd decided it lived now.

The shaft took us the way a throat takes a swallowed thing.

I have no honest way to describe the next ninety seconds except to say that the sea picked us up and shook us. The column of water drove upward with the full weight of the monsoon behind it, and we rose through blackness lit only by lightning filtering down from above, strobing the walls white, and I could hear the others—could feel them through the rope—alive, alive, alive. Then light. Then air. Then we were vomited out onto the clifftop through eight feet of spray, tumbling across wet grass with the ledgers between us, and the storm closed over the cave mouths below like a fist closing over a secret.

Behind us, down the coast, Meridian's boats ran their lights along the cliff line, hunting seven doors that no longer opened. Three short, one long. They pinged the darkness all night, and the darkness, guided by a dead woman's pencil, declined to answer.

* * *

We lay on the clifftop in the rain, five minutes or fifty, I couldn't tell you. Tomas finally sat up, unlashed the case, and checked the ledgers by lightning-flash. All of them. Every page the consortium ever wrote about bribes and sunk ships and the names of guides who asked questions—including, I knew without looking, a page with Cyrus's name on it.

"He drew these ten years ago," I said. I hadn't meant to say it out loud. "Odile. She mapped caves she hoped she'd never need, and marked a door that only opens in disaster, and then she died, and the maps waited." I looked at Cyrus. "She's been guiding us this whole time. We just couldn't hear her."

Cyrus was quiet for a long moment. Rain ran off his chin. When he spoke, his voice had that raw edge it gets when he's feeling something too large to file under competence.

"Then we listen," he said. He stood, and looked down the coast toward the headland where, according to Ilsa's last radio scan, Meridian's captain had put ashore a boarding party to cut off the cave exits—the seven exits that didn't matter anymore. "Because there's one thing left she can't chart for us. Tomorrow, at first light, the whole coast hears what's in that case. And Meridian's captain is going to want to stop us before we get to a transmitter."

He shouldered the case himself this time.

"So we walk. And Pim—" he glanced back at me, and for the first time in the year I'd known him, Cyrus Eriksen smiled like the running was actually over. "You hold the charts."

The escarpment sang around us, and we moved.
