## 19. Sixth Tide: What the Escarpment Sings

The monsoon arrived the way old debts do — not all at once, but with a pressure you could feel in your teeth long before anything broke.

I stood at the mouth of Odile's sea cave and watched the horizon eat itself. The sky over the escarpment had gone the color of wet slate, and the swell was running wrong, stacking against the cliffs in long gray shoulders that broke not on the beach but against the rock itself, throwing spray forty feet up. Somewhere behind me, in the dark of the cave, Tomas and Pim were lashing the ledger case to a salvage sled while Ilsa argued with a generator that had every right to be dead.

And out on the water, riding at anchor in defiance of everything the barometer knew, sat Meridian's fleet. Four hulls. Running lights burning like a small arrogant city. They'd come through the leading squalls because their captain had decided the storm was his cover too, and now we were both trapped between the cliff and the weather, and only one of us had a cave to hide in.

"You see them?" Ilsa called from inside.

"I see them."

"They're launching boats."

"Of course they are." I turned back into the cave's throat. "They didn't chase us three tides across open water to admire our taste in real estate."

* * *

Let me tell you what the last six hours had looked like, because history will be written by whoever survives this, and I want my version in first.

We'd run the caves at fourth tide and fifth tide both, hauling the ledgers through passages so narrow that Pim had to swim ahead sideways, chart pages waterproofed in oilcloth and held in her teeth like a pirate's knife. Odile had drawn those charts twenty years ago in pencil, in a hand that slanted left, annotated with little marginal notes that read like jokes: *squeeze — exhale first*, *do not trust the echo here*, *if the water tastes of iron, turn back*. She'd charted an escape route through the escarpment's bones for a crew she hadn't met yet, and she'd never told a living soul it existed, and I have spent two days trying to decide whether that was foresight or loneliness. Knowing Odile, it was both.

The ledgers themselves rode in a brass-cornered case that weighed about as much as a sin you can't take back. Inside: eleven volumes of consortium accounting. Bribes listed by port authority and amount. Insurance frauds dressed as weather. Three ships scuttled deliberately, crews written off as losses before they ever sailed. And page after page of payments to harbor officials in two countries for one specific service: the destruction of one Cyrus Eriksen, mountain guide and coastal salvager, blacklisted for sabotage he did not commit, testified against by a woman who'd been coerced into it and had spent ten years buying her way back to undo it.

My name appeared forty-one times. Ilsa counted. She said it the way you'd report a depth sounding — flat, professional, useful. Forty-one entries. It cost them less than a new winch to end my life. That's the part I keep turning over. Not the malice. The price.

We'd made it to the sixth-tide staging point — a chamber Ilsa called the mud room, where the cave floor dipped below the waterline and the air tasted of salt and stone — when Pim came back down the passage at a dead sprint, which is a thing you cannot actually do in a flooded cave, and did anyway.

"Boats," she said. "Two of them. In the outer pool. They've got lights."

So much for the storm being ours.

* * *

Now, standing at the cave mouth with the monsoon making landfall behind me like a closing fist, I had perhaps four minutes to solve a problem that ten years hadn't touched.

Meridian's captain came across the water in a rigid inflatable that handled the swell better than any boat has a right to. He stood in the bow — didn't crouch, didn't brace, just stood, one hand on the console, like the ocean was a formality he'd already dismissed. When the boat ground up onto the shingle inside the cave's outer chamber, he stepped off into six inches of water without looking down.

He was younger than I expected. That annoyed me. Villains should earn their faces.

"Cyrus Eriksen." He said it like he was confirming a delivery address. "Captain Aldous Wren. Meridian Salvage and Recovery."

"Recovery," I said. "That's a generous word for a company that cuts divers' guide lines."

Something flickered across his face — not guilt, exactly. More like a man hearing a noise in his house and deciding it was the wind. "That wasn't ordered by me."

"No," I agreed. "It wouldn't have been. Your orders would have been cleaner. Cut the line, let the sea do the paperwork. That's consortium arithmetic. I've been reading it all week."

Behind him, his two boat crew stayed in the inflatable, hands visible, which told me they'd been briefed on us and hadn't liked the briefing. Smart men. Tomas had come up out of the inner passage and planted himself at my left shoulder like a load-bearing wall, and Ilsa stood at my right with her arms crossed and the expression of a woman calculating how many of Meridian's teeth she could remove with a pry bar if it came to that.

Wren looked past me into the cave. "You have something of ours."

"Yours." I let the word sit there. "Interesting claim. Those ledgers were written by your parent company. Paid for by your parent company. Filed by your parent company. The only thing of yours down here is the shame, and frankly, Captain, you can't afford the storage."

"The case comes with us. You come with us. There's a detention contract —"

"There's always a detention contract." I'd heard that language before, ten years ago, from men in better coats. "Here's my counteroffer. You turn your boats around, you ride out the monsoon at sea like a professional, and in the morning you read the news like everyone else."

"Read what news?"

I smiled at him. It felt strange on my face, like wearing someone else's boots. "You'll see."

He studied me for a long moment. Rain was starting to reach us now, flung in through the cave mouth in horizontal sheets, and the wind had found its voice — a low moan through the upper passages that Ilsa had once described, half-joking, as the escarpment singing. Odile used to say the same thing. On nights like this, she claimed, the whole coast hummed, and if you listened right you could hear every ship she'd ever lost still talking to each other through the stone.

Wren didn't hear any singing. Wren heard a cornered man with a heavy case and nowhere left to run, and he did the arithmetic out loud.

"You're going to publish them." He almost laughed. "That's your play? A broadcast? In this?" He gestured at the storm. "No tower within eighty miles is transmitting. Every band's fouled with squall interference. Even if you had equipment —"

"We have equipment," Ilsa said pleasantly.

"— even if you had equipment, no one's listening."

"That," I said, "is where you're wrong, and I'd love to explain why, but honestly the explanation is going to reach you faster than I can say it."

Because here was the thing Wren hadn't done his own arithmetic on. Meridian's fleet ran illegal lights and unmarked hulls, but they ran legal radios — because a salvage fleet that couldn't call a mayday was a fleet that drowned alone, and even empires buy insurance. Their emergency band was monitored. Not just by their own ships. By every coast station from here to the shipping lanes, because emergency bands are the one frequency the whole world agrees to keep quiet for. And Ilsa — spiteful, brilliant Ilsa, who had rebuilt our dive rig from memory and grudge — had spent the last hour splicing our transmitter into a power feed and tuning it with her eyes shut, and when she'd finished she'd looked at me and said, "Their band, their antenna array, their signal strength. We're going to broadcast on the loudest radio on this coast, and they brought it to us."

Wren followed my glance toward the inner cave, and I watched him understand, and I watched him decide to pretend he hadn't.

"Last chance," he said quietly. "Give up the case. Walk away. You've spent ten years being nobody. I'm offering you ten more."

And there it was. The actual offer. Not the ledgers — the anonymity. The clean, gray, weightless life of a man with no name worth printing. I had guarded that anonymity like a hoarded coin. Never stop, never settle, never go back. Three rules that had kept my crew alive and kept me hollow, and here was a man in a dry jacket offering to sell me back my prison at a fair price.

Tomas shifted beside me. He didn't say anything. He'd stopped saying things about the ledgers after low water, after the argument where Ilsa wanted to sell and he wanted justice and Pim — young Pim, who'd never met Odile, who'd grown up on a crew of ghosts — had said, in a voice none of us were prepared for: "I don't want to spend my whole life being a rumor. I want to be somebody somewhere." After that, the argument was over, though nobody had officially conceded. Some votes are cast in silence and counted in silence, and this crew had been voting that way since the night Odile died.

I thought about Odile's cabin door underwater, and the strongbox someone had scratched open looking for these same ledgers, and how a grave becomes a crime scene becomes, if you're stubborn enough, evidence.

"Captain Wren," I said, "do you know what the worst part of being framed is?"

"I can't imagine I'll care."

"It's not the blacklist. It's not losing the licenses, the work, the name. It's that you start doing the math yourself. Ten years of running, and somewhere in year six or seven you catch yourself thinking — maybe they were right to bury me. Maybe a man who can't prove his innocence doesn't deserve one. That's the real product they sell. Not the frame. The doubt." I took a step forward, into the rain coming off the swell. "You're offering me more of the same. So no. Keep your offer. I've got something better."

I turned and walked into the cave, and — this is the detail I'll want remembered — Wren let me. Because he still believed I was bluffing, and because his boats were pinned by the swell, and because a man who has spent his career recovering other people's property has a hard time believing anyone would destroy their own leverage.

Ilsa had the rig set up on a crate above the waterline, jury-rigged to the generator, the antenna wire run up through a fissure in the cave roof that Odile's chart marked, in that slanted left-leaning hand, with a single word: *whistle*. The wind through the fissure screamed like a kettle, and the wire sang with it.

"Band's hot," Ilsa said. "Emergency channel, wideband relay. The moment you key it, every coast station on two hundred miles of water hears us, and Meridian's own array boosts us clear to the shipping lanes." She handed me the microphone like handing someone a live grenade. "Say something worth hearing."

I keyed the transmit switch.

What do you say? I'd had six tides to think about it and I still hadn't drafted anything, because I am a salvager, not a speechmaker. So I did what Odile taught us to do with wrecks: I opened the case and read what was actually there.

"This is a transmission from the escarpment coast," I said. "My name is Cyrus Eriksen, and for ten years your harbor masters have told you I'm a saboteur. Here is who told them to say it." And I read. Entry by entry. Port by port. The bribes, itemized. The scuttled ships, named, with their crews' names written off in a column labeled *projected loss*. The payments for my own ruin, dated, signed, initialed. I read until my voice went rough, and then Ilsa took the mic and read in her flat surveyor's voice, which somehow made it worse and truer, and then Pim read, and her voice cracked on the names of the scuttled crews because she was young and hadn't learned yet to sound numbers instead of feeling them, and I hope she never learns.

Behind me, in the outer chamber, I heard Wren shouting orders, and boots in water, and then nothing — because his own crew had heard the broadcast too, on their own set, and men who hear their employer's books read aloud tend to develop sudden, profound deafness to further instructions.

The wind rose through the whistle-fissure and the antenna sang with it, and I swear to you — I know how it sounds, I know exactly how it sounds — the signal carried clean. Squall interference everywhere on the dial except the one band the world keeps clear, and our voices riding out of a cave in the escarpment on Meridian's own power, reaching every port station, every watch officer, every insomniac harbormaster with a cup of coffee and a logbook. The escarpment sings on nights like this. Odile always said so. Tonight it sang eleven volumes of the truth, and the whole coast was listening.

When we finished, I keyed off and stood there in the generator's stuttering light, and I waited for the feeling. Vindication, maybe. Triumph. Something.

What came instead was quieter and stranger. It was the absence of a weight I'd carried so long I'd mistaken it for a spine. Ten years of running, and the running didn't stop with a gunshot or a courtroom. It stopped with a man reading numbers into a storm. Never stop, never settle, never go back — and here I stood, back where it all began, settled, stopped, named aloud on an open channel, and alive. All three rules broken in the space of one broadcast, and the world hadn't ended. It had, in fact, gotten slightly more honest.

Wren stood at the cave mouth, drenched, his boats wallowing in the rising surge, and he looked at me with something that wasn't hatred. It was the look of a man watching the tide come in over a sandcastle he'd been paid well to defend.

"You understand what you've done," he said. "Every ship in that consortium's fleet is going to run for international water tonight. Every account freezes by morning. There'll be chaos — ports, insurers, crews —"

"Yes," I said. "There will. That's what the truth costs. We checked the price. We decided it was cheaper than the lie."

He stared at me a moment longer, and then — and I want this recorded too, because it matters — he gave a short nod. Not respect, exactly. Recognition. One professional acknowledging that another had, against all sense, completed the job. Then he turned and shouted his boats back into the storm, and Meridian's captain rode out the monsoon at sea like I'd originally suggested, because even empires, mid-collapse, remember their seamanship.

* * *

The storm hit full force an hour later, and we spent it the way sailors spend all storms: badly, loudly, and together. Ilsa lashed everything twice. Tomas cooked, because Tomas believes no apocalypse should be faced hungry. Pim sat with the ledger case between her knees like a drum she was guarding, and at some point she asked me, over the roar, "Did you mean it? What you said about the doubt?"

"Every word."

"So it's over? The running?"

I thought about it. Outside, the escarpment howled its long note through a hundred fissures, and somewhere under all that sound, twenty fathoms down, a survey vessel named Kestrel lay broken around a cabin door I still hadn't fully forgiven myself for.

"It's over," I said. "Whatever comes tomorrow, we're not running from it."

She nodded slowly, absorbing that, the way you'd absorb a new depth reading. Then she frowned. "Cyrus. If Meridian's accounts freeze by morning — and Maren turns herself in like she said — whose testimony actually clears your name? The ledgers prove the frame. But a pardon needs a witness."

And that was when I remembered, with a lurch that had nothing to do with the swell, that Maren Voss had gone ashore at fifth tide with a copy of the ledgers and a decade of confession in her throat, headed straight for the one port authority in two countries with the power to unmake me — and that the monsoon had made landfall directly across her route, and that the last thing she'd said to me was *don't wait for me*.

I looked at Ilsa. Ilsa looked at the barometer, which had stopped pretending to be ambiguous.

"Tide turns in four hours," she said. "After that, nobody sails anywhere for two days."

Four hours. The storm of the decade between us and the woman who had betrayed me, saved me, and was at this moment walking into the worst weather on the coast carrying my absolution in a satchel.

Tomas was already pulling on his oilskins.
