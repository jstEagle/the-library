## 20. Aftermath of a Storm

The storm ended the way a fistfight ends—not with a decision but with everyone too tired to keep swinging.

I knew it was over before I opened my eyes. The sound changed. For three days the world had been a drum, the escarpment booming like something enormous walking on it, and then, sometime in the grey hours, the drum became a murmur, and the murmur became rain, ordinary rain, the polite kind that just wanted to wet your laundry. I lay in the boathouse loft where we'd dragged ourselves after the caves, listening to it, and I thought: I have survived worse storms than this one. I have survived quieter ones, which is harder.

Cyrus was already up. Of course he was. That man treats sleep like a debt he's ashamed of.

He was at the window with a mug of something he'd made himself, which was always a bad sign, because Cyrus only cooks when his hands need an excuse not to do what they actually want. He looked like a man who had been hollowed out and refilled with someone else's opinions.

"Port of Greywater just went dark," he said, without turning around. "Every Meridian hull in the anchorage is running for open water."

I sat up. My ribs reminded me they had opinions about the last four days—the cut line, the three-mile swim, the ledgers lashed to my chest like a second heart. "Running from the broadcast?"

"Running from the authorities." He finally turned, and there was something in his face I hadn't seen in eleven years. It took me a moment to name it, because I'd only ever seen it on other men. It was relief. Actual, unguarded, standing-in-the-open relief. "Greywater's harbor office seized two ships at dawn. Corvo Port issued arrest warrants by noon. They're reading the ledgers, Tomas. Out loud. In public offices. With witnesses."

I got dressed slowly, because some news you want to arrive gradually. Eleven years we'd run. Eleven years of never stopping, never settling, never going back—Cyrus's three commandments, carved into all of us so deep I'd stopped hearing them as words and started hearing them as my own pulse. And now port authorities were acting on a signal we'd sent, and nobody was coming for us, and I didn't know what to do with my hands either.

* * *

By midday the rain quit entirely and the sky did that thing coastal skies do after a monsoon—scrubbed blue, apologetic, like a man who'd said something unforgivable and meant to make it up to you with weather.

We gathered on the boathouse steps: me, Cyrus, Ilsa with her charts spread across her knees even though there was nothing left to chart, Pim perched on the rail with the restless energy of a nineteen-year-old who has outrun a fleet and needs somewhere to put it. Maren Voss stood apart, as she had since the cave mouth. She hadn't slept either. She looked like a woman who had carried a confession for a decade and set it down only to find her arms still shaped around its weight.

"The fleet's scattering," Ilsa said. Her voice had the flat calm she reserved for calculations she didn't trust. "Meridian's scout, the two corvettes, the salvage barges—all gone different directions. When a company's officers start arresting each other, the crews stop taking orders." She looked up. "It's unraveling. Actually unraveling."

"It'll take weeks," Cyrus said. "Months, maybe. Consortiums don't die in a day; they rot in stages. There'll be men in fine coats denying everything on every dock from here to the Spur."

"And us?" Pim asked. "What happens to us?"

Nobody answered right away, which was itself an answer. We were so practiced at not being asked that question that when it finally arrived, none of us had equipment for it.

* * *

Maren turned herself in on the third day after the storm.

She told us at breakfast, quietly, the way you tell people you've decided to sell a boat. "There's a harbor magistrate at Corvo Port collecting testimony on the consortium. Real testimony, sworn, witnessed. Everything I know about how they operate—how they bought judges, how they sank ships and blamed the salvagers, how they—" She stopped. Looked at Cyrus. "How they used me."

"You could just leave," Ilsa said. "The ledgers did the work. Nobody would blame you for vanishing."

"That's precisely why I have to go," Maren said. "Vanishing is what I'm good at. It's what they trained me for. If I vanish now, I spend the rest of my life being the woman who testified against Cyrus Eriksen and then disappeared when it was convenient." She folded her hands on the table. "I spent ten years buying my way back to that wreck. I'm not spending the rest of my life underwater too."

Cyrus said nothing through any of it. He had that stillness he gets, the kind that makes you check whether he's breathing. When she finished, he stood, walked around the table, and did something I will remember until I'm done remembering things: he shook her hand. Not warmly. Not coldly. Like a man closing a ledger of his own.

"Tell them everything," he said. "Even the parts about me."

"Especially those," she said.

* * *

We heard the outcome in pieces, the way you hear anything important on the coast—by rumor, by courier, by a fisherman who talks too much.

First piece: Maren Voss walked into the Corvo magistrate's office under her own name, first time she'd used it in public in six years, and gave testimony for nine hours. Nine hours. The magistrate reportedly called for water twice.

Second piece: among the documents she surrendered was the original testimony she'd given against Cyrus eleven years ago—and, more damning, the consortium letter that had coerced it, signed by a director whose name was now appearing in the ledgers next to payments for "guide suppression." The phrase made my skin crawl. Guide suppression. As if we were weather to be managed.

Third piece arrived on a courier skiff flying no colors, which made us all reach for knives until the man aboard held up both hands and a sealed document. Cyrus read it standing in the shallows. He read it twice. Then he handed it to me without a word, and I understood why he couldn't speak.

It was a writ of exoneration. Both countries. Both blacklists. Stamped, witnessed, official—the whole machinery of legitimacy deployed at last in our favor instead of against us. Cyrus Eriksen, cleared. The saboteur label, revoked. The bounty, withdrawn.

Eleven years. I watched him hold that paper and I swear his hands were less steady than they'd been hauling ledgers through a collapsing sea cave.

"Well," Ilsa said finally, into the silence. "That's that."

"That's not *that*," Pim said. "That's the opposite of that. That's everything else starting."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about the end of a long flight: it doesn't feel like landing. It feels like the strange weightlessness after you surface, when your body still expects the pressure and keeps bracing for a depth that isn't there anymore.

For days afterward, I caught us all doing it. Cyrus checking sightlines out of habit, then catching himself. Ilsa burning our old charts in the stove one at a time, then stopping halfway through the stack, unable to finish, holding a half-burned chart of the escarpment like a love letter she wasn't ready to part with. Pim asking questions—endless questions, about licenses, about harbors, about whether a person could simply *apply* for things—and flinching every time a stranger looked at them too long.

And me, I kept waking at dawn reaching for the panic. The old engine of get-up-get-moving-don't-let-them-find-you-here. It took a full minute each morning to remember there was nothing to run from. A full minute of freedom, every day, that felt exactly like dread.

On the fifth evening, Cyrus found me mending net on the boathouse steps. He sat down without asking, which was new. Cyrus Eriksen had spent eleven years never sitting anywhere long enough to be asked anything.

"I keep waiting," he said, "to feel different."

"Do you?"

"No." He turned his mug in his hands. "I feel the same. Just... without the reason."

I nodded, because I knew exactly what he meant. Grief works that way too. So does fear. You carry a thing so long it stops being luggage and becomes a limb, and when it's taken you don't feel light—you feel amputated.

"Odile would've laughed at us," I said. "Two grown men, miserable because nobody's chasing them."

"She wouldn't have laughed." He looked out at the water, where the Kestrel's grave lay quiet under forty fathoms, its ledgers empty, its secrets spent. "She'd have said we earned the right to be bad at this. Resting is a skill, Tomas. Nobody taught us."

* * *

The strangest moment came a week later, when a delegation from the Greywater harbor office arrived at our cove in a proper launch, with paperwork.

They wanted statements. Witnesses. Our account of the wreck site, the dive, the cut line, the divers Meridian sent into the murk with knives. Official inquiry, they said. Compensation available, they said. And would Mr. Eriksen be willing to appear before the tribunal in person?

I watched Cyrus receive this request the way you'd watch a man be offered food after years of starvation—he wanted it so badly it frightened him. To stand in a lit room, in front of strangers, under his own name, and say what happened. To have it believed.

"We'll come," he said. "All of us."

After they left, Pim stared at him. "You hate officials."

"I hated being their criminal," Cyrus said. "Different animal entirely."

* * *

The tribunal hearing was held in Greywater's customs hall, a high-ceilinged room that smelled of ink and tar and old arguments. We testified over two days. Me on the cut line, Ilsa on the decoy signals and the informant, Pim on the shore lamp and the cave passage, Cyrus on all of it, in a voice that started rough and grew steadier with every hour, as if the room itself were setting a bone.

When it ended, the magistrate dismissed us with a formality that nearly undid me: "Mr. Eriksen. On behalf of both governments, welcome back to lawful waters."

Welcome back. As if we'd merely been away.

Outside, evening was coming down gold over the harbor. Fishing boats were in. Children ran the seawall. Somewhere a bell rang the change of tide, indifferent and perfect. Cyrus stood on the customs house steps a long time, looking at all of it—the ordinary, unremarkable, unwatched life of a working coast—and when he finally spoke, his voice came out strange.

"I don't remember how to do this," he said. "Any of it. Settling. Staying. Waking up in the same bed twice."

"Neither do I," I admitted. "But Ilsa does. Watch."

Across the square, Ilsa had stopped at a chandler's window and was studying a derelict boathouse listing in a notice—*structure for lease, escarpment cove, negotiable terms*—with the expression she usually saved for engines worth rebuilding.

Pim saw it too. Saw her looking. And grinned like sunrise.

"Cyrus," Pim said. "I think the crew's about to vote again."

He followed our eyes to the notice, to the cove, to the future sitting there in plain sight like a boat that only needed bailing. And Cyrus Eriksen—who taught us never to stop, never to settle, never to go back—took a long breath, let it out slow, and began, very carefully, to unlearn every rule he ever gave us.

"What are the terms?" he called to Ilsa.

And that, I understood, was the sound of a man coming home.
