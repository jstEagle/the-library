## 21. The Crew That Stopped

The strangest thing about the morning after the storm was not the wreckage, or the smell of brine and diesel that clung to everything like a second skin, or even the fact that Meridian's fleet had scattered like startled gulls. The strangest thing was the silence in my head where the escape plan used to live.

For eleven years I had kept a running tally in my skull: fuel reserves, tide windows, border distances, the number of hours between a job finishing and the moment we needed to be gone. It ran constantly, like a bilge pump you never switch off because you're afraid of what happens when it stops. And now, standing on the escarpment road with salt drying white on my forearms and no one chasing us, the pump had gone quiet, and what it left behind was a hollow so large I could hear my own pulse in it.

Cyrus stood beside me looking at the same horizon I was, and he wasn't scanning it for sails. He was just *looking* at it. I found this so unsettling I nearly checked him for fever.

"You're not watching the water," I said.

"No."

"That's how they get you. Complacency. One day you're admiring a sunrise, next day there's a revenue cutter over the bar and you're swimming for it with your trousers full of charts."

"Ilsa." He said it gently, which from Cyrus is roughly equivalent to shouting. "Nobody's coming."

I knew that. Intellectually, I knew that. The ledgers were public. Every port authority from here to the capital had spent the last week reading consortium bribes aloud in courtrooms while Meridian's captains burned their own papers and pointed their bows at whatever horizon didn't have lawyers on it. Maren Voss had walked into the magistrate's office with her testimony under her arm like a woman delivering a wedding gift. Cyrus's name was clean in two countries — scrubbed, certified, stamped. There was even talk of a commendation, which made him physically ill whenever anyone mentioned it.

So yes. Nobody was coming.

And I had absolutely no idea what to do with that.

* * *

We'd anchored in the lee below the escarpment for three days after the broadcast, mostly because Tomas needed them — his lungs had taken a beating in the cut-line incident, and Pim hovered over him with the fierce, terrified devotion of someone who'd nearly lost the only family that had ever bothered to claim him. On the fourth day, Cyrus climbed up onto the deck, looked at the three of us assembled around the galley table like a tribunal, and said the sentence I will remember until I die:

"So. What now?"

Just like that. No plan. No heading. No list of ports ranked by the laxity of their customs officials. He asked it the way a man asks *what's for dinner*, and then he stood there waiting, as if any of us might actually know.

Tomas spoke first, because Tomas always speaks first when the question frightens him. "I could dive anywhere now," he said. "Licensed. Legitimate. There are wrecks off the southern islands nobody's touched in forty years."

"Could," said Cyrus. "Or we could sell the boat, split the salvage fees, and never see each other again. That's an option too. I want it on the table. All options on the table."

I hated him a little for saying it, and loved him a little more for meaning it. Because that's the thing about running together for a decade: half the glue is fear, and if you take the fear away, you find out fast whether anything else was holding you.

Pim looked at his hands. "I don't want to split up," he said, very quietly, the way you admit something shameful. "I know that's stupid. I know we only ever stuck together because—"

"It's not stupid," Tomas said.

"It's the least stupid thing you've ever said," I said, "and I once heard you describe a compass as 'a needle with opinions.'"

Cyrus looked at each of us in turn, and something moved behind his eyes — that slow tectonic shift I'd been watching since the cave mouth, since the moment he keyed the fleet's own emergency band and let the escarpment wind carry our voices into every port on the coast. "Then we don't split up," he said. "But we also don't run. So somebody tell me what's left in between."

I'll confess something here that I've told no one: I already knew. I'd known for two days, since the morning I rowed ashore to check the freshwater seep at the base of the cliffs and found myself standing in front of the old boathouse.

It sat in the crook of the cove below the escarpment trail, half-swallowed by ivy and salt rot, its roofline sagging like a tired shoulder. Odile had used it once — before my time, before any of ours except Cyrus — as a staging shed for her survey gear. The Kestrel's tender had wintered in it. There was a slipway, still sound, and a winch house, and behind it a spring that ran sweet even in drought years. It was derelict, unclaimed, and sitting on land that belonged to no consortium, no fleet, and no blacklist.

I hadn't said anything. I'd just stood there in the doorway with the gulls screaming overhead and felt the strangest sensation of my adult life, which was wanting to stay somewhere.

So when Cyrus asked *what now*, I pulled the chart tube from under my bunk, unrolled it on the galley table, and put my finger on the cove.

"There," I said. "That's what's left in between."

* * *

The boathouse, upon closer inspection, was less a building than a rumor of one.

The east wall had given up entirely sometime during the last decade and lay in the grass like a felled animal. The roof leaked in nine places I counted and probably four I didn't. The winch house contained a winch, a nest of something with teeth, and a smell I chose not to investigate. When Tomas pried open the trapdoor to the under-floor crawl space, he found it occupied by a family of otters who regarded us with the flat affront of prior tenants being shown a lease.

"We're fixing *this*?" Pim said, standing in the doorway with a crowbar and the expression of a man who has been promised treasure and handed a shovel.

"We're fixing this," Cyrus said, and there was something in his voice I'd never heard before. It took me a moment to name it, because I'd never had occasion to. It was *settling*. Not the defeated kind — the deliberate kind. The kind where a man puts down roots on purpose instead of by accident of exhaustion.

He worked differently, too. For eleven years every repair Cyrus made was temporary by design — lashings meant to hold until the next port, patches meant to last until the next escape. Now he scarfed new timber into the wall studs and pegged them proper, and when I caught him checking his work twice, he didn't pretend he hadn't.

"What?" he said, catching me watching.

"Nothing. Just noting that you built that joint to outlive us all."

"Somebody should build things that outlive us," he said, and went back to work, and I decided not to make it strange by agreeing out loud, because some sentences are too heavy to say while holding a hammer.

Ilsa's rules of engineering, for the record: measure twice, cut once, and never fall in love with a structure, because structures sink, burn, or get confiscated. I broke rule three within the first week, quietly, without ceremony, the way you break most important rules.

* * *

Rebuilding became its own tide chart. High water for hauling timber up the slip; low water for digging out the silted foundations; the hours between for the thousand small negotiations a structure demands. Ilsa rebuilt the rigging loft. Tomas re-caulked the slipway boards until they gleamed. Pim — who turned out to have an unexpected genius for joinery, or at least an unexpected tolerance for sanding — refit every window in the place, then built shutters for them, then carved a small kestrel into the lintel over the main door before anyone could stop him.

"Too much?" he asked, when Cyrus came down the path and saw it.

Cyrus looked at the little bird for a long time. Wind came off the water and moved his hair, and for a moment he looked ten years younger and ten years older at once, which I would not have thought arithmetically possible.

"No," he said. "It's exactly enough."

Maren came once, in the second week. She arrived by skiff, as she always did, and stood at the edge of the property line like a woman visiting a grave she wasn't sure she had the right to tend. She'd sold her own vessel — the *Corvid*, sleek and fast and built entirely for leaving — and bought passage on a coastal packet instead, which struck me as either penance or poetry, possibly both.

"The magistrate's office released the final findings yesterday," she said, not quite looking at any of us. "Your names are cleared. Formally. There's a document."

"We saw the document," Cyrus said.

"Yes. Well." She turned her hat in her hands. She looked smaller out of the courtroom, out of the intrigue, stripped of purpose the way a rig is stripped after a voyage. "I wanted to see what you built. With the time I cost you."

I expected Cyrus to give her the cold arithmetic she deserved — eleven years, two blacklists, a mentor at the bottom of the sea. Instead he picked up a spare hammer and held it out to her, handle first.

"East wall needs sheathing," he said. "You owe the wood, not me."

Maren stared at the hammer. Then she took off her coat, rolled her sleeves, and worked beside us until dark. Nobody spoke to her much. Nobody sent her away. Some debts, I've learned, aren't settled in words; they're settled in sweat, board by board, and everyone present understood the terms without a contract being drawn.

She came back the following week. And the week after that. By the third visit Pim had started saving her the straight nails, which in Pim's economy is roughly a knighthood.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about stopping: your hands don't know what to do.

Mine kept reaching for checklists that no longer existed. Fuel caches to rotate. Escape routes to re-survey. I caught myself, three separate times, sketching contingency plans for a life that no longer required them — a bolt-hole here, a false registry there — before laughing alone in the rigging loft like a woman who'd forgotten how to be sane. Eleven years of vigilance doesn't drain out of you the moment the danger ends. It pools. It looks for something to guard.

So I gave it something to guard.

I rebuilt the winch from the frame out, machining parts on the little lathe I'd salvaged from the *Kestrel*'s sister wreck years ago and never had a reason to use. I wired the boathouse for light — proper light, steady light, not the guttering oil lamps we'd lived by for a decade. I built a signal mast on the cliff top above the cove, and then, standing in front of it with the wire in my hands, realized I had no idea who we'd signal, because for the first time in our lives we weren't hiding from anyone.

In the end I strung the wire anyway, and hung a lamp on it, and left it lit.

"For whom?" Cyrus asked, the first night it burned.

"Anyone," I said. "That's rather the point."

He considered that. Down in the cove, the boathouse windows glowed yellow against the darkening water, and Tomas's laugh carried up the cliff path, and Pim was arguing with the otters about territorial rights, and the escarpment wind moved through the grass with that low singing note it makes when it finds the right gap in the stone — the note Odile must have heard a hundred times, standing where I was standing.

"For anyone," Cyrus repeated, testing the words like new rope. "Huh."

* * *

The license came in autumn.

A guide service, properly registered, operating out of the escarpment cove: wreck survey, diver training, coastal salvage under charter. Cyrus signed the paperwork with a hand that shook slightly, though he blamed the cold, and the clerk at the port office — a young man who had grown up on stories of the infamous Eriksen crew — watched him sign with the reverent horror of a boy watching a legend turn out to be a middle-aged man with ink on his fingers.

"Ethics clause," Cyrus added, sliding the paper back. "Put it in writing. We teach recovery, not plunder. We document every site. We report everything we find to the authorities, even when it costs us."

The clerk blinked. "That'll reduce your profit margin considerably."

"It already has," I said, "for eleven years, in ways you can't imagine. We're due a discount."

Word traveled, the way word does. Within a month there were applications — young salvagers, mostly, kids with cheap gear and expensive dreams, the kind we'd once been before the world taught us better. Cyrus interviewed each one personally, and I sat in on every session, and I noticed that he asked none of the questions other operators asked. No *how strong are you*, no *can you hold your breath*. He asked what they'd do if they found a grave on a wreck. He asked what they owed the dead. He asked who taught them the water, and whether they'd thanked them.

Pim took to the teaching like he'd been born to it — patient, funny, merciless about safety lines. Tomas ran the dive program and scared the students half to death with stories he swore were true and I knew for fact were seventy percent embellishment. Maren handled the charting contracts, having discovered in herself a talent for paperwork that bordered on the sinister. And I kept the lights burning and the winch greased and the signal mast lit, guarding nothing, guarding everything, my vigilance finally aimed at a future instead of a past.

Some nights I still wake at odd hours with the bilge-pump silence roaring in my ears, and I walk the property line in the dark out of pure habit, counting the things that could go wrong. The list gets shorter every season. Lately it's mostly just weather, and otters, and the question of whether Pim will ever stop carving kestrels into things.

I can live with that list. I find, to my considerable surprise, that I can live inside it.

Last night I stood at the top of the slipway after everyone had gone in, and I listened to the escarpment sing — that long low note through the stone, the one the whole coast knows but nobody ever stops to hear. Eleven years I heard it as a warning. Standing there, with lamplight in the windows behind me and my name on a license nailed to the door, I finally heard it as what it had been all along.

An invitation.

I was still standing there when Cyrus came out to douse the yard lamp, and he stopped beside me, and neither of us said anything for a while, because we're engineers of a sort, both of us, and we know when a structure doesn't need another word added to bear its load.

"Pim found something today," he said at last. "Down at the tide line, past the point. He wants us to look at it in the morning."

"What kind of something?"

"He wouldn't say. Just kept grinning and saying we'd want to see it at low water." Cyrus squinted toward the dark shape of the headland, and I saw the old alertness flicker back into his face — not fear, not anymore, but curiosity, sharp and bright as a fresh-cut edge. "Low water's at dawn."

I looked at the headland too, and felt the tide turning somewhere out beyond the bar, pulling toward whatever Pim had found.

"Well," I said. "Then we'd best not be late."
