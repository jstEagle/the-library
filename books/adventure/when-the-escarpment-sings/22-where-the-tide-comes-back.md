## 22. Where the Tide Comes Back

The license hangs in the boathouse window now, framed in driftwood Ilsa found herself and refused to let anyone else touch. *Montrose Coast Salvage & Guide Service — Licensed, Bonded, Insured.* Three words that used to make Cyrus laugh like a man reading his own obituary. He doesn't laugh anymore. He just checks the glass twice a day to make sure nobody's scratched it out, which is its own kind of funny, if you ask me. A year ago we'd have paid good money for someone to scratch it out before the port authority saw it.

I'm the one who dusts it. Nobody assigned me the job. It just happened, the way things happen on a crew that's stopped running—somebody starts doing a thing because they want to, and then it's tradition, and then it's law.

One year. Long enough for the escarpment grass to grow back over the scorch mark where Meridian's scout burned its signal oil. Long enough for Pim—the youngest of us, the spotter, the one who never met her—to stop flinching at the name *Kestrel*.

Long enough, apparently, for me to decide I needed one last dive.

* * *

It was Tomas who taught me to dive, back when I was all elbows and bad attitude and a talent for spotting trouble from a cliff top but no idea what to do underwater. "You don't fight the water," he told me on my first descent, his voice crackling through the line like gravel wrapped in patience. "You negotiate with it. You're small, it's big, and the only way through is honesty."

Honesty. That was Odile's word too, according to everyone who tells stories about her. I've collected those stories for three years now, the way other people collect coins—turning them over, checking which ones are worn smooth with telling and which still have edges. Tomas says she was stern. Ilsa says she was funny in a dry way that snuck up on you. Cyrus says almost nothing, and when he does say something, it's usually about knots or navigation, as if grief were a technical problem he could solve with better rigging.

They're all wrong, probably. All of them. And all of them are right, because she was a different woman to each person she saved, and none of those women existed except in the remembering.

But there's one thing every story agrees on: Odile Vasseur believed you leave a place better than the sea left it. Not emptier. Better. You take what's abandoned, you honor what's buried, and you never—*never*—sell what belongs to the dead.

That's the ethics we teach now. Six students this season, kids mostly, some of them runaways from ports where the consortium's collapse left holes shaped exactly like their fathers' jobs. We teach them knots first, then charts, then the oath. Not our old oath—the one sworn in blood and terror on the deck of a boat that smelled of tar and fear. A new one. Shorter. Kinder.

"We salvage what the law forgot," Cyrus tells them, standing at the rail with the wind doing that thing it does off the escarpment, singing through the rocks like a choir tuning up. "And we put back what the living forgot to honor."

The students write it down. One of them, a girl named Sef with salt-bleached hair and hands already calloused from rope work, asked me last week why we bother honoring the dead when the dead can't check our work.

"Because you can," I told her. "That's the whole point. The dead gave us everything we know about diving, about charts, about how to read weather coming off open water. The least we owe them is to be worth teaching."

She thought about that for a long time. Then she nodded once, sharp, like a wave deciding to break, and went back to coiling her line. I think she'll be fine. I think she might be better than fine. I think she might be the reason we stayed.

* * *

The Kestrel sits in forty feet of green-gray water off the escarpment's shoulder, right where she's sat for years, getting more honest by the season. Wrecks do that. Everything false gets eaten; everything true gets crusted over in coral and barnacle until the truth is the only thing strong enough to hold the shape.

We've dived her twice since the storm. Once for survey—Ilsa wanted to map what remained so no other salvager would blunder into her blind—and once for Cyrus, alone, though he told nobody that and I only know because I was on cliff watch and saw him go down without a buddy line, which broke rule four, and which I have agreed to keep quiet about in exchange for him agreeing to never do it again. Negotiation. Honesty. Tomas would be proud.

Today I'm going down with a full rig, a buddy—Sef, because she asked and because someone has to teach the next generation what respect looks like underwater—and a marker I've been building for two months in the boathouse loft where nobody goes.

It's not much. A brass plate, salvaged fair and square from a derelict dinghy, polished until it throws light even down here where light comes down thin and sideways. I engraved it myself, badly, with a jeweler's tool Ilsa lent me and didn't ask questions about, which is its own kind of love.

*ODILE VASSEUR*
*SHE TAUGHT THE WATER TO SPEAK*

Underneath, smaller:

*WE CAME BACK.*

* * *

The descent is easy. Forty feet, good visibility, the wreck lying on her starboard side like a woman asleep with her face turned away from the door. Sef swims point, nervous and precise, checking her gauge every thirty seconds the way I drilled into her. Good kid. Annoyingly good kid.

The crew quarters come into view first—that long low corridor of collapsed bunks and drifting silt, everything softened by years of patient water. This is where Tomas froze, twice, at a door he couldn't bring himself to open. I understand it better now than I did then. There are doors in every life you either go through or spend the rest of your days walking past, and the walking-past gets heavier every year, until one day you realize the door weighs more than whatever's behind it ever could.

Tomas went through his door on dive two. Came out changed. Came out holding an empty strongbox and a face like a man who'd finally set down luggage he'd forgotten he was carrying.

My door is different. My door isn't in the wreck at all—it's the fact that I never knew her. Everyone else on this crew carries Odile inside them like a splinter worked deep into the grain, and I carry only the shape the splinter left behind. For three years I've been grieving a woman secondhand, through other people's memories, through contradictions, through Cyrus's silences and Ilsa's spite-built rigs and Tomas's patient hands.

So today I'm not leaving a marker for her. Not exactly. I'm leaving it for the version of her that lives in this crew—in the ethics we teach, in the charts Pim navigated through flooded caves while a monsoon tried to eat us alive, in the oath we swore new and better. She's alive in all of it. Dead women don't get to choose what they become in the people who remember them, but if she could have chosen, I think she'd have chosen this: a licensed guide service on a coast that used to hunt us, teaching children to be honest with the water.

Sef waits at the corridor mouth while I swim alone to the cabin.

Her door is intact. That's the strange mercy of it—while the rest of the Kestrel has slumped and settled, her cabin holds its shape, the door still on its hinges, the little round porthole still glazed with a skin of algae that turns the water beyond it into emerald glass. I've seen drawings of her, from Ilsa's memory. Small woman. Big hands. Eyes that made you want to have done your homework.

I press the plate flat against the door, low, where a hand would rest to push it open. The brass catches what light remains and throws it back in a slow, wobbling coin across the ceiling. Somewhere above, the monsoon season is still weeks out, and the surface is calm, and the escarpment is singing its low stone song the way it always does when the wind finds the caves.

I hold position there longer than my air plan says I should. Not freezing, the way Tomas froze. Just... staying. Letting the moment be as long as it needs to be, because I've learned that from this crew too—that grief isn't a thing you finish, it's a thing you visit, and the visiting is the point.

Then I tap the plate twice with my knuckle, the way you'd knock on a friend's door, and I turn for home.

* * *

Sef surfaces beside me, spits her regulator, and immediately asks the question I knew was coming. "Who was she? Really?"

We're in the skiff, running back toward the boathouse with the evening sun laying gold across the water, and behind us the Kestrel keeps her silence under forty feet of forgiving sea. Ilsa's on the dock already, waving us in with the particular impatience she reserves for people she loves. Cyrus is at the rail of the boathouse porch, and even from here I can see him watching the water, watching us, watching the place where the wreck lies hidden.

"Nobody really knows," I tell Sef. "That's the best part."

"That's the best part?"

"The best part." I kill the engine and let the skiff drift the last few yards. "Because it means she's still becoming something. Every person who tells a story about her makes a new piece of her, and none of us can be wrong, and none of us can be right, and she just keeps growing." I look back at the water. "Most people stop changing when they die. She didn't. She got bigger."

Sef chews on that. Then: "Will you tell me a story about her sometime? Even if it's wrong?"

"Especially if it's wrong," I say. "Wrong stories are the honest ones. They show you who's doing the telling."

On the dock, Ilsa shouts something about dinner and about how if we let the catch sit another ten minutes she's feeding it to the gulls, and Cyrus laughs—actually laughs, full and unguarded, a sound I heard maybe twice in my first year aboard and hear weekly now. The escarpment rises behind the boathouse, green and gold in the last light, its caves dark mouths that once swallowed us whole and spat us out into a different life.

A year ago I stood on a cliff top and watched a lamp flash coordinates to men who wanted us dead, and I learned that the ground itself could betray you. Today the ground holds a boathouse, a license in the window, six students learning knots, and a crew that chose to stay—not because they were trapped, but because for the first time in any of our lives, staying was the braver thing.

The tide is coming back in. It does that here twice a day, patient as anything, erasing our footprints off the dock boards and bringing the water up close beneath the pilings like a dog returning to a door it's decided is home.

Cyrus meets me at the top of the dock steps. He glances back at the water, then at me, and there's something in his face I've learned to read—the look he gets when he's about to say a thing he's been carrying since morning.

"You left it?" he asks.

"I left it."

He nods slowly. Looks out at the sea a long moment. "Good," he says. "She'd have knocked back."

And that's when Sef comes running up the dock with a scrap of paper in her fist, breathless, eyes wide—the particular wide of a spotter who's spotted something. "There's a boat," she says. "Out past the point. Running dark, no lights, no flag. Been sitting there since noon." She thrusts the paper at Cyrus—a harbor notice, fresh ink. "And the port authority flagged a request. Someone's asking for us by name."

Cyrus takes the paper. Reads it. His jaw works once, the old habit, the one he wore like armor back when names were dangerous things to carry.

But he doesn't fold the paper away. Doesn't burn it. Doesn't glance at the treeline or start calculating tides.

Instead he smiles—small, tired, real—and hands it back to the girl.

"Well," he says, to her, to me, to the escarpment singing its evening song behind us. "Let's go see who's knocking."
