## 1. The Last Timetable

The rain came down on Vell's Crossing the way it always did in the last week of the dry season — not as weather but as a decision, a gray verdict handed down from the escarpment and carried on wind that smelled of wet iron and distant rot. It fell through the gaps in the station awning in long silver threads, pooled in the wheel-ruts outside the freight office, and turned the town's one paved street into a river of dull mercury that reflected nothing.

Tamsin Marchetti — though for eleven days now he had been Tomas Reiner, surveyor, third class, licensed out of Corvane Province with papers that had cost him more than his boots and coat together — stood under that awning and watched the water run, because watching water was safer than watching people, and because after ten years he still could not stand under an awning without checking the sightlines to the street.

Old habits. Old habits were all he'd kept.

The junction was dying the way junctions die: slowly, then all at once. The mainline had been rerouted south twenty years ago, before his time, when the Kessarin gorge floods ate the low trestle and nobody thought the mountain traffic worth rebuilding. What remained was a freight shed with a sagging roof, a hotel whose sign read *VELL'S CROSSING HOTEL* with the L's gone dark so it said VE 'S CROSSING HOTEL, which Tamsin privately thought was more honest, and a timetable board bolted to the platform wall behind cracked glass. Someone had penciled across it, years ago by the fading of the graphite: **CLOSED — SEE AUTHORITY**. Nobody ever had. The authority had forgotten this place existed, and the place had returned the favor.

He liked towns like this. They asked nothing. A man could be a name on a ledger and nothing else, and the ledger would be forgotten by spring.

Inside the freight office, the air was thick with kerosene and damp paper. The district agent — a narrow man named Ostrow with ink-stained cuffs and the permanent squint of someone who had spent thirty years reading carbon copies by bad light — sat behind a counter scarred by decades of crate corners, and slid the contract across it with two fingers, as if it might be hot.

"Survey contract," Ostrow said. "Closed lines. The upper branches."

Tamsin didn't reach for it immediately. He had learned that too. Eager men got remembered; eager men got described later, to the wrong people, in the wrong offices. He let his eyes move over the document first, unhurried, the way a man reads something routine.

"The mountain spurs," he said. "Above the Kessarin?"

"Above and along." Ostrow's squint deepened. "You know them?"

"I know what the maps say about them." Which was true, and carefully incomplete. He knew what every map said about them, and he knew what the maps didn't say, which was considerably more: where the cuttings had slumped, where the culverts would be choked with a decade of wash, where the tunnels ran through rock that sweated in the wet season and wept rust-orange water that stained the ballast like old blood. He had surveyed worse country for less money. He had surveyed better country for worse reasons.

"The client wants grade reports, bridge assessments, tunnel conditions," Ostrow went on. "Full inventory. Everything north of the Third Bend, both banks of the river where the old alignments run. There's talk of reopening."

"There's always talk of reopening."

"This time there's money behind the talk." Ostrow said it flatly, without enthusiasm, the way he might have reported a death. Money in the mountains meant men in the mountains, and men in the mountains meant the quiet years were over. "Salvage concern. Vantrease interests."

Something moved at the base of Tamsin's skull, faint and cold, like water finding a crack in a foundation. *Vantrease.* He turned the name over and found nothing attached to it — no file, no memory, no face. That should have been reassuring. In his experience the names that meant nothing were the ones you hadn't learned to fear yet.

"They want it done before the monsoon," Ostrow said. "Six weeks, they say. Possibly less. The river closes the gorges and everything above the Fifth Bend becomes unreachable until the dry season. So it's now or next year, and the client does not strike me as a woman who tolerates next year."

"The client is a woman."

"The client is a *Vantrease*." Ostrow pronounced it the way other men pronounced a diagnosis. He pushed the contract the last inch across the counter. "Triple standard rates. Half up front. You'd travel upriver by boat from Kessarin Landing — the lines themselves are dead track, no locomotive's run them since the closure. You'd need your own instruments, your own nerve, and your own arrangements for coming back, because I'll tell you plainly, surveyor, the boats don't always come back either."

Tamsin picked up the contract. Good paper. Too good — laid stock, watermarked, the kind of paper that cost more per sheet than Ostrow made in a day. For a survey of dead branch lines in a forgotten gorge, someone was spending money like it was ammunition.

He read the terms twice. The rate was triple, as promised. The duration was open-ended, which he disliked. The clause about confidentiality was unusually thorough — the client's identity sealed, the survey results delivered to her representatives only, no copies retained by the surveyor. That clause he read three times.

"No copies retained," he said.

"Client's requirement."

"It's an odd requirement for a grade survey."

Ostrow shrugged with one shoulder, a motion like a door settling in its frame. "Everything about this commission is odd. The advance is real, if that's your next question. Cash, drawn this afternoon. And the credential check came back clean yesterday, Mr. Reiner, so you can stop looking at me like I'm going to ask you something difficult."

Tamsin kept his face still, but inside, behind the stillness, a small cold alarm rang — the same alarm that had woken him at dawn in a dozen rented rooms across ten years, the one that said *they've looked, they've found the papers clean, and clean papers are only clean until someone looks harder.* His credentials were forged by a man in Corvane City who charged accordingly and delivered work that had survived two provincial audits and one very bored customs officer. But survived was not the same as true. Somewhere in a records office, a Tomas Reiner existed who had never held a theodolite, and if anyone ever put the two of them in the same room —

"Mr. Reiner?"

"Yes." He signed. His false signature flowed out of his hand smooth as water, better than his real one ever had. That was the trick of it, he'd learned: the lie you practice becomes more natural than the truth you abandoned. "When do I start?"

"Boat leaves Kessarin Landing on the morning tide, four days from now. There's a packet steamer down the valley tomorrow that will get you there." Ostrow stamped the contract, blew on the ink out of habit rather than necessity, and produced a heavy envelope from a drawer. He set it on the counter between them and did not let go of it right away. "One more thing. The client's representative will meet you at the landing. A nephew, I'm told. Callum Vantrease." A pause, delicate as a hairline crack. "I'm told he drinks. I'm telling you because a surveyor alone in the mountains with a drunk employer's kin — that's a story I'd rather not hear later."

"Noted," Tamsin said, and took the envelope. It was heavier than half pay ought to be. He counted none of it in front of Ostrow. Another lesson, bought at full price years ago: never show a man what you carry.

* * *

That night the rain settled in for the duration, drumming on the hotel's tin roof with a persistence that was almost conversational, and Tamsin sat in his room — second floor, corner, two exits counting the window — and did what he did in every new town on every first night. He unpacked nothing. He laid out instead the small kit of his running life: instruments wrapped in oilcloth, spare shirt, the forged papers in their oilskin wallet, a folding knife, and a canvas roll containing, folded small at the bottom beneath a spare chamois, a photograph he allowed himself to look at perhaps four times a year.

He did not look at it tonight. Tonight he sat by the window with the shutter cracked and watched the street below, and told himself the familiar arithmetic.

Every job is temporary. Every attachment is a liability. Every town is a room you have not yet left.

Ten years since the Meridian Span came down. Ten years since forty-one names went into the Kessarin in nine seconds of screaming steel, and the inquiry board, meeting in comfortable rooms far from the river, had needed someone to blame and found him: the junior engineer whose stress calculations were on file, whose signature was on the load certification, whose report — he had never been able to prove this, never, not once, though he had spent five years and everything he owned trying — had been altered before it reached the board. Altered, resubmitted, laundered through channels he was too poor and too disgraced to trace. By the time he understood the shape of what had been done to him, he was a name on warrant lists in three provinces, and the men who had done it were men with salvage contracts and government friends, and Tamsin Marchetti was a ghost with a theodolite.

So he ran. Not dramatically — ghosts don't get drama. He ran the way water runs, finding the low places, the forgotten towns, the jobs paid in cash by clients who didn't look closely. He became excellent at being no one. He became so good at it that some mornings, shaving in some inn's spotted mirror, he had to pause and search the face there for the man he used to be, and lately the search was taking longer.

And yet.

He turned the envelope of money over in his hands. Triple rates. Watermarked paper. Confidentiality clauses like a lockbox. A salvage concern buying surveys of closed mountain lines above the Kessarin — *his* Kessarin, the river that had taken the span and taken his name and given him back a life made of leaving.

Coincidence, he told himself. The world was full of rivers and full of salvage men, and the mountains above any of them were full of dead track worth assessing. Coincidence was the story he told himself the way other men told themselves prayers: not because it was true, but because the alternative required action, and action required exposure, and exposure was the one debt he could never again afford to pay.

But the cold thing at the base of his skull did not subside. It had a voice, that cold thing, worn smooth by repetition, and tonight it spoke clearly:

*You are walking up the same river, into the same mountains, toward the same men, and calling it a job.*

Below, the street lay empty and shining. A dog trotted through the puddles with the self-importance of the unemployed. Somewhere a shutter banged, once, twice, and went still. The rain kept up its patient percussion on the tin, and Tamsin sat with the money in his lap and the photograph in its canvas grave and the forged name sitting on his tongue like a coin he was tired of biting.

He thought about refusing. He even got as far as composing the refusal — he would return the advance in the morning, take the packet steamer south instead of north, find some coastal survey, some estuary, some place where the water was slow and honest and no one had ever built a bridge worth blowing. He had composed such refusals before. He had a whole shelf of them, dusted and unused.

What stopped him, as it always stopped him, was simpler than fear or greed. It was the clause. *No copies retained.* No man spends watermarked-paper money to keep a grade survey secret unless the grades matter less than the secrecy — unless what she really wanted was a man up there, alone, unaccountable, asking questions of dead tunnels. And a woman who wanted that was a woman who already suspected the answer, and a woman who suspected the answer to questions buried in those mountains was a woman whose expedition might, just possibly, be digging toward the same altered report that had eaten his life.

Or it was nothing. Or it was salvage rights and timber estimates and a rich woman's whim.

He would go up, he decided, and he would keep his eyes open and his name sewn shut, and the moment the country started to feel familiar in the wrong way — the moment the river showed him a bend he recognized from the worst year of his life — he would walk away. That was the bargain he made with himself, the same bargain, renewed nightly for a decade: *one more job, one more season, one more river, and then I stop.*

He had never once stopped. He suspected, in his honest hours, that he did not know how.

The rain eased near midnight, as if satisfied, and in the sudden relative quiet Tamsin heard the freight yard: couplings clanking, a shunting engine working the sidings with the weary patience of old animals. He listened the way other men listened to music. Track, at least, had never lied to him. Steel under load sang true or it didn't, and it told you which, and it told you in time to do something about it, and in ten years of listening he had come to believe that everything else in the world — boards, inquiries, signatures, names — sang false.

Sleep came late and thin. He dreamed, as he sometimes did, of the span: white concrete piers rising out of green water, the truss arching overhead like a rib of some god-sized animal, and himself young on the deck with a clipboard, checking figures he no longer remembered against calculations he could no longer trust. In the dream the river was high and brown and moving fast, faster than it should, faster than the season allowed, and he was shouting something — a warning, a number, a name — and no one heard him, and the dream, mercifully, always ended before the ninth second.

He woke before dawn with his hand already packed, the way a soldier wakes reaching for a rifle.

* * *

Kessarin Landing was two days' travel north and eight hundred feet lower than the escarpment's shoulder, and it smelled of diesel, fish, and river mud — a working smell, honest as sweat. The packet steamer *Gannet* put him ashore on the morning of the fourth day onto a pontoon dock that flexed underfoot like a living thing, and Tamsin stood a moment getting his land-legs back and taking the measure of the place.

It was bigger than Vell's Crossing and uglier, all corrugated iron and stacked cargo and barges moored bow to stern along the bank. Upriver, past the last warehouses, the Kessarin narrowed into the first gorge and vanished between walls of black-green forest so steep the trees seemed to lean out over the water to watch it pass. The river itself ran high and coffee-dark, carrying whole trees on its surface like matchsticks, and even standing on the dock Tamsin could feel it through the planks — that deep muscular pull, patient and enormous, the pull of water that intended to reach the sea and did not care what had been built in its way.

His hand, without any instruction from him, had gone cold.

He knew this stretch. Not this exact bend — the lower gorge had changed, silted, grown its new jungle — but the *kind* of it. The light on the water. The way sound came off the cliff walls a half-beat late. Somewhere upstream, beyond the bends, beyond the gorges, the Meridian Span's replacement crossed at the only point in eighty miles where the banks came close enough to bridge, and its piers stood on foundations his own drawings had specified, and he had not seen it in ten years and did not intend to see it now.

"Tomas Reiner?" said a voice behind him, pitched with the particular cheerfulness of someone managing a hangover. "Reiner, the surveyor? I'm Callum Vantrease. We're expected, apparently. We're always apparently expected."

Tamsin turned and put on the face he wore for employers' relatives: competent, unremarkable, slightly dull. The young man offering his hand was maybe twenty-five, dressed for a river he clearly did not respect in clothes that had cost more than Tamsin's instruments, handsome in a dissolving way, with the reddened eyes and careful balance of a man who had measured his morning brandy to the half-ounce and gotten it wrong.

"Callum." Tamsin shook the hand briefly. Firm grip, damp palm. "Your aunt's party — how many boats?"

"One boat. Ours." Callum glanced upriver and something flickered across his face that was not quite fear and not quite reluctance, but lived in the neighborhood of both. "She's called the *Coriander*. Pilot's a mountain of gristle named Brannoc who doesn't talk, and a deckhand named Sefa who talks enough for both of them, and me, and you. Five souls bound for the upper gorges to poke around in dead railway tunnels before the monsoon eats the river." He smiled, and the smile didn't reach anywhere near his eyes. "Aunt Odile sends her regards and her instructions, in that order. She says — and I quote, because I've memorized it, she makes me memorize things — *the survey begins today, the tide waits for no one, and Mr. Reiner is to understand that triple pay purchases triple discretion.*"

The cold thing at the base of Tamsin's skull rang once, clear as a struck rail.

"Triple discretion," he repeated mildly.

"That's what she said." Callum was already turning toward the boat, waving at someone on deck. "Come on, Reiner. Tide's turning."

Tamsin stood one moment longer on the pontoon, feeling the river's pull through the planks, looking up at the gorge where the water went dark between the walls, and told himself the nightly bargain one more time — *one more job, and then I stop* — knowing even as he said it that the words had begun to sound like a timetable for a train that was never coming.

Then he shouldered his instruments and followed the nephew down the dock, and behind him the Kessarin ran on toward the sea, high and brown and early, carrying things on its surface that had no business being loose in the water yet.

He noticed that. He noticed the trees moving too fast, the current too strong for the season, the river running like something released rather than something merely full.

He filed it away with everything else he noticed and never said aloud, and stepped aboard.
