## 2. Six Tides

The rain had followed Tamsin Marchetti from the junction, as it followed him everywhere, patient as a debt.

He'd spent the morning in the survey office above the chandlery, correcting the false lines of his own handwriting. The forged credential said *T. Marlowe*, licensed engineer, third class, and he had practiced the signature until it flowed without thought — that was the trick of it, the small daily treasons of a man living under a name not his own. You didn't forge a document once. You forged it every morning, in every ledger you signed, every manifest, every time a stranger asked who you were and you answered smoothly, *Marlowe, yes, that's me*, until the lie wore grooves in you like water wearing stone.

The contract for the closed mountain lines was legitimate enough. Survey the abandoned spurs above the Kessarin gorge, assess what forty years of rot had done to the trestles, mark what could be rebuilt. Honest work, anonymous work. He'd taken it precisely because it was dull — because dull meant safe, and safe meant one more season of being no one in particular, which was all he'd ever wanted since the bridge came down and took ninety-one people and his good name with it.

He was still telling himself that when the summons came.

It arrived by boy, not wire — a cream-colored envelope, heavy stock, sealed with wax though wax seals had gone out with steam paddleboats, which told Tamsin everything about the sender before he broke it. Inside, a single card:

*Miss Vantrease requests Mr. Marlowe at the Kessarin House, this evening, seven o'clock. She will not request twice.*

No reason given. No "regarding your contract." Just the imperative, and the implicit promise behind it: she knew where he was, she knew his working name, and she had decided that his time was hers to command. Tamsin read the card twice, set it on the desk beside his lamp, and listened to the rain hammer the tin roof like a thousand small hammers testing a thousand small rivets.

He could refuse. That was always the arithmetic, wasn't it — you could always refuse, right up until refusing cost more than complying. A man with a clean name could tell Miss Vantrease to post her requests where the sun didn't reach. A man named Marlowe, whose credential would bear perhaps three weeks of close inspection, whose real name sat in an inquiry archive two thousand miles downriver attached to the word *negligent* in red ink — that man did not refuse women who sent wax-sealed cards through boys instead of wires.

Because wires left records. And records were how they'd found him last time.

He dressed in his good coat, the one with the collar that still held the shape of a different climate, and walked down through the junction town as the light went copper and died. The Kessarin House stood at the town's wet heart, a former river customs station converted to something between a hotel and a fortress — deep verandas, shutters like half-closed eyes, lanterns burning yellow against the gray. Odile Vantrease had taken the entire top floor. Of course she had.

* * *

She received him in a room that had been stripped of its hotel furniture and refitted with her own: charts pinned floor to ceiling, a drafting table, a campaign chest, and a woman standing at the window with her back to him as if he were a train arriving on her schedule rather than a person walking into her room.

"Mr. Marlowe." She didn't turn. Her voice was low and precise, each word laid down like rail on graded bed. "You're punctual. Good. Punctual men can be trusted with clocks. Sit."

There was exactly one chair positioned for a guest. Tamsin didn't take it. He stood by the door instead, hands loose, and let the silence stretch until she glanced over her shoulder and found him unmoved. Something flickered across her face — not annoyance. Recalculation. He'd seen that look before, from foremen assessing whether a bridge would hold.

"You don't sit when you're being managed," she said.

"I don't sit when I don't know why I'm here."

"Fair." She turned fully then, and he got his first true look at Odile Vantrease. She was perhaps fifty, tall without softness, dressed in expedition khaki that had been tailored so well it looked like armor rather than clothing. Silver threaded her dark hair at the temples in two hard wings. Her eyes were the pale gray of river fog, and they did not blink when they should have. "Then let's establish why you're here. You signed a contract eleven days ago to survey the closed mountain lines. You've been in this town nine days. In that time you've visited the records office twice, the chandlery once, and you've asked the harbor master — very casually, you thought — about water levels above the gorge." She smiled without warmth. "I have a good memory for details, Mr. Marlowe. It's the family affliction."

Tamsin kept his face still while his pulse climbed. Nine days in town and she'd had him watched from the first. Which meant the survey contract wasn't a survey contract. It never was.

"And?" he said.

"And nothing. Curiosity is a virtue in an engineer. I'm pleased you have it, because you're going to need every virtue you possess." She crossed to the charts, and now he saw them properly: the Kessarin River system rendered in ink and colored pencil, the main channel running up through six successive gorges toward the high country, each narrows marked with tide tables penciled and re-penciled in different hands. "My expedition went silent nine days ago."

"Your expedition."

"I funded it. Outfitting, vessels, crew, instruments — my money, my planning, my cousin's leadership. Thirty-one people went upriver in April to survey salvage rights along the old flood line." Her finger traced the channel upward, past the fifth gorge, to a point near the headwaters where someone had drawn a small, careful circle and then, oddly, crossed it out. "Nine days ago their scheduled transmission failed to arrive. There has been nothing since. Not silence the way rivers are silent — silence the way a cut wire is silent."

Tamsin looked at the chart, at the crossed-out circle, and felt the old cold move through him, the one that lived under his ribs and woke whenever a job began to tilt from work into fate. "What do you want from me?"

"To go up after them."

"That's a river rescue. You want rivermen, divers, a gunboat if your money stretches that far. Not a railroad surveyor."

"The rivermen I hired are already up there. They're part of the thirty-one." She said it flatly, but he heard the fracture underneath, hairline-thin. "What I want is the only man in this town who reads terrain the way you do. I've read your survey notes from the lower lines. You look at a cliff and see the fault lines underneath it. You look at a trestle and know which timber will fail first. I need someone who can get thirty-one people — or whatever remains of them — out through country that wants them dead."

"And if I decline?"

"Then I pay you the balance owed on the survey contract, plus a bonus for your trouble, and you walk back to your rented room and your corrected handwriting, and in approximately four weeks the monsoon comes and closes these gorges until next year, and whatever happened to my people stays buried until the water drops. If it ever does." She let that sit. Then, quieter, with the precision of a surgeon naming the organ: "Or you take triple pay, and we leave on tomorrow's tide."

Triple pay. The number landed in the room like dropped cargo. Tamsin made himself think past it, because the number was meant to be thought past — it was the lure, and the hook was somewhere behind it. He'd been hooked before. He knew the shape of the mouth.

"Why me?" he asked. "Genuinely. This town has pilots who've run the Kessarin twenty years. Why hire a stranger with a third-class credential and nine days' local history?"

For the first time, Odile Vantrease hesitated. It was brief — a half-second, a caught breath — but he'd spent ten years reading people's hesitations the way other men read gauges, and he caught it, catalogued it, filed it under *she knows more than she's saying.*

"Because you were recommended," she said finally. "By a source I trust."

"What source?"

"A source that doesn't concern you." She moved to the drafting table and began rolling a chart with brisk, economical motions, as though the conversation were concluded and only the logistics remained. "We depart on the morning tide. My boat is the *Vespera*, moored at the third berth below the customhouse. Pilot, deckhand, and my nephew make five of us total. Pack for cold nights and wet days and bring whatever instruments you actually trust, because I know better than most that instruments lie when it matters."

Tamsin stood in the doorway of her certainty and felt the trap close around him with the slow, gentle inevitability of a tide coming in. It wasn't the money — though God help him, the money mattered, money was the wall between him and the archive, the wall between *Marlowe* and the inquiry board that still had his real name in a drawer. It wasn't even the threat, though the threat was there, folded into the wax seal and the watched nine days and the phrase *a source I trust*, which meant someone out there knew enough about Tamsin Marchetti to recommend him to a stranger, and that knowledge was a knife pointed at his neck that anyone might pick up.

It was the circle on the chart. The one she'd drawn and then crossed out. He couldn't stop looking at it. Something in the way she'd crossed it out — carefully, deliberately, like a woman erasing a name from a list of the living — told him that whatever waited upriver at that point on the map was the actual reason for all of this, and the missing expedition was either part of it or pretext for it, and either way he was already inside the machine.

"One condition," he heard himself say.

Odile looked up from her chart-rolling, eyebrows raised — imperious, amused, waiting.

"My name stays Marlowe," Tamsin said. "On manifests, crew lists, payroll, everything. You don't ask where I worked before this, and if anyone upriver asks, I'm a surveyor and nothing else. Can you live with that?"

Something moved behind the fog-gray eyes — recognition, maybe, or its twin. As if she'd heard the question before, from her own mouth, in another room, years ago.

"Easily," she said. "Everyone on this expedition is somebody they aren't. It's practically the crew requirement." She extended the rolled chart across the table to him, handle first, like a baton in a relay she'd already started without his consent. "Six tides, Mr. Marlowe. After the sixth, the monsoon seals the gorges, and nothing goes up or comes down until the dry season. We'll be racing the water."

Tamsin took the chart. It was heavier than paper should be, or his hands were.

Outside, the rain kept falling on the junction town, steady and indifferent, and far upriver — beyond the six gorges, beyond the crossed-out circle — something waited in the drowned dark that had already reached into a wax-sealed envelope and chosen his name.

He just didn't know yet whose hand had written it.
