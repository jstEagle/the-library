## 3. Crew of Strangers

The boat was called the *Kestrel's Mercy*, which Tamsin took for a bad omen, since nothing on the Kessarin had ever shown him mercy and he hadn't asked for any.

She was a shallow-draft survey launch, forty feet of riveted iron and rotting teak that smelled of diesel, bilgewater, and the particular sweetness of old rope left out in the rain. She sat low in the brown water at the Vantrease Company dock, riding her mooring lines like something restless in its sleep. Tamsin walked her length twice before he signed anything, one hand trailing the rail, reading her the way he'd learned to read bridges — by what she hid under her paint.

Cracked weld at the stern knee. Repacked stuffing box, recently, competently. A hairline scar along the port chine where she'd kissed rock hard enough to leave a memory but not a wound. Someone loved this boat badly and often, which was the only kind of love that held up out here.

"You'll want to stop doing that."

The voice came from the wheelhouse above him, gravel poured over gravel. Tamsin looked up into a face like a cliff that had been arguing with weather for fifty years and losing slowly: Brannoc, the pilot. He was broad without being tall, built downward rather than outward, with forearms that suggested a life spent wrestling things that did not wish to be wrestled — engines, cables, rivers, perhaps God. His beard was iron-grey and unrepentant. His eyes were pale as river fog and about as warm.

"Doing what?" Tamsin said.

"Reading my boat like she owes you money." Brannoc came down the ladder with the unhurried economy of a man who never wasted a motion because every motion cost him something. He stopped an arm's length away, close enough that Tamsin could smell engine oil and tobacco cut with something herbal and bitter. "She's sound. I keep her sound. That's all you need to know."

"I need to know more than that if we're running the upper gorges in monsoon season."

Something flickered behind the fog — not warmth, exactly, but the recognition of one professional meeting another across a disputed border. "You're the engineer."

"I'm the engineer."

"Odile says you surveyed the Marrow Line before it closed." Brannoc spat over the rail, a habit so ingrained it seemed almost ceremonial. "I ran cargo past the Marrow Line for nine years. Never saw you."

"You wouldn't have," Tamsin said evenly. "I worked the tunnels mostly. Underground men don't get seen."

It was a good lie. He'd had ten years to polish it, and it slid off his tongue now with the smoothness of a stone worn in a current. But Brannoc studied him a moment too long, the way a man studies a knot he suspects is tied wrong, and Tamsin felt the familiar itch between his shoulder blades — the feeling of a story not quite sitting flat against the skin.

"Huh," Brannoc said finally, which could have meant anything, and turned away. "Deck's Sefa's. Don't touch her lines without asking. Galley's yours if you can cook, which I doubt. We cast off at first light."

He climbed back to the wheelhouse and shut the door, and that was the whole of their acquaintance. Tamsin stood on the dock a moment longer, listening to the Kessarin slide past beneath the pilings, carrying silt and secrets down toward the delta, and told himself what he always told himself at the start of a job: *You are here to do a thing. Do the thing. Leave.*

He had gotten very good at leaving. It was, he sometimes thought, the only skill his old life had left him intact.

* * *

Sefa appeared at dusk, materializing at the foot of the gangplank with a woven basket on her hip and no warning whatsoever, so that Tamsin started and nearly dropped the coil of mooring line he was checking.

She was small and quick, maybe thirty, with a face that might have been carved from river clay — broad cheekbones, dark watchful eyes, hair bound up in a red cloth that glowed like a coal in the failing light. She looked at Tamsin the way he imagined the river looked at things that fell into it: assessing whether they would float or sink.

"You're the new one," she said. Not a question. Her accent was lowland, rounded, with the musical lilt of the delta villages far downstream. "The engineer who isn't from anywhere."

Tamsin kept his face still. "Brannoc talk?"

"Sefa listens." She set the basket on the dock and began unpacking it with quick precise movements: candles, a bundle of dried herbs, small cloth-wrapped bundles whose contents clinked. "You checked the mooring lines twice. You walk the boat's edges like the water's going to take her. And when Odile Vantrease said your name this morning, you flinched."

"I didn't flinch."

"You flinched *here*—" she touched the space just below her own collarbone, "—small, inside. I see it. River people see it." She held up one of the cloth bundles. "Salt. For the bow. The Kessarin takes a toll from every boat that goes up past the Escarpment. Old water wants old debts. You pay it small, you pay it early, or it collects big later."

"It's a river," Tamsin said. "Not a creditor."

Sefa smiled, and it transformed her face entirely — sudden, bright, a little wild. "So was the man who told me that, once. He owed the river three days later." She pressed the salt bundle into his hand and closed his fingers around it. "Put it on the bow yourself. First light. It means more if you mean it."

She gathered her basket and went up the plank, humming something tuneless, and Tamsin stood holding the salt like a live coal, aware that he had just been read more thoroughly in four minutes than he'd been in ten years of careful living. He should have found it alarming. What disturbed him was that some tired, buried part of him had found it almost pleasant — to be *seen*, even wrongly, even dangerously.

He put the salt in his coat pocket and told himself he'd throw it overboard when no one was watching.

* * *

Callum Vantrease arrived last, after dark, carried aboard in a manner of speaking — that is, he came down the gangplank in a linen suit that had been white that morning, dragging two leather valises and an expression of aristocratic suffering, and announced to the night in general: "There is no hot water on this vessel."

"There's water," Brannoc's voice rumbled down from the wheelhouse. "Hot's extra."

"That's not funny."

"Wasn't trying to be."

Tamsin watched from the deck as the young man stood at the bottom of the plank, visibly recalculating his life. Callum was twenty-four, maybe twenty-five, with his aunt's dark coloring and none of her armor — a handsome face that seemed perpetually braced for impact, hands too fine for the ropes they'd have to learn, and eyes that kept darting to the black water as though expecting it to produce something. He had the look of a man sent somewhere rather than going: exiled by family, or fleeing something, or both.

"You're the engineer," Callum said, finding him. Up close there was a tremor in him, a fine vibration like a wire strung too tight. "Tamsin Marchetti. Yes. Aunt Odile showed me your credentials." He offered a hand; his grip was damp. "Callum. I'm... expedition support."

"Support," Tamsin repeated.

"Logistics. Documentation. I keep the records." A defensive lift of the chin. "Every serious expedition keeps records."

"Sure," Tamsin said, and let it go, because the young man's knuckles had gone white around his valise handles, and cruelty was a currency Tamsin had stopped spending years ago. "Bunk's aft, second door. Mattress is thin. Sleep on your coat."

Callum blinked, wrong-footed by plainness. "Right. Thank you." He hesitated, then, quieter, almost to himself: "She didn't give me much choice, you know. About coming."

"No," Tamsin said. "She doesn't seem to."

And something passed between them then, brief and unspoken — two conscripts recognizing each other across the gulf of class and age — before Callum caught himself, straightened his spine, and hauled his valises aft with as much dignity as the narrow companionway allowed.

* * *

They cast off at first light, gray-green dawn bleeding through the river mist, and Tamsin put Sefa's salt on the bow with his own hands, mostly because refusing would have required explaining, and explanation was a debt he couldn't afford. Sefa watched from the deck with her arms crossed and her coal-bright headcloth catching the first sun, and when the salt went into the water she nodded once, satisfied, as if a contract had been countersigned.

The first day was easy water. The Kessarin below the Escarpment ran wide and slow, braided around sandbars, fringed with casuarina trees that leaned over the banks like gossips. Brannoc handled the wheel with the lazy precision of long mastery, touching the throttle the way other men touched musical instruments. Sefa worked the deck singing under her breath — sounding depths, coiling line, feeding the firebox — and her songs were all in a language Tamsin didn't know, low and repetitive, like prayers or warnings.

By the third hour he'd stopped pretending he wasn't listening.

"Depth song," Sefa said, appearing beside him without warning again — he would have to train himself out of startling, or she'd kill him eventually by accident. "My grandmother's. You sing the depth and the river sings back what it hides. Snags. Sand shifts. Bad water." She squinted at him. "You don't believe."

"I believe in soundings," Tamsin said. "Lead line and echo. Numbers don't sing."

"Numbers lie quiet," Sefa said. "Songs lie loud, where you can hear them lying." She shrugged and moved off, and Tamsin turned the sentence over for the rest of the afternoon without deciding whether it was wisdom or nonsense, which he suspected was exactly the effect she intended.

Callum, meanwhile, discovered work. It happened to him gradually, like weather. First he complained about the sun; then he stopped complaining because complaining cost breath he needed for hauling; by late afternoon he was coiling line with a competence that surprised everyone including himself, and when Sefa corrected his grip without mockery — just took his hands and moved them, matter-of-fact, the way you'd adjust a tool — he flushed dark and said nothing, and did it right the next time.

Tamsin watched this from the engine hatch, where he'd spent the day learning the *Mercy*'s temper. She was temperamental, the boat: her fuel feed starved on hard turns, her steering had a half-second lag that could kill you in fast water, and her engine had a knock at low revolutions that Brannoc claimed was character and Tamsin knew was a bearing with maybe two hundred hours left in it. He said nothing about the bearing. There would be time, or there wouldn't.

That evening they tied up at a place Brannoc called the Narrows Mouth, where the river drew itself together between the first shoulders of the escarpment and the current picked up a low, continuous mutter, like a crowd before bad news. They ate rice and dried fish and a curry Sefa made from her basket stores, eaten off tin plates on the deck with the lantern hung from the cargo boom, moths orbiting it in slow devotion.

It was, Tamsin realized somewhere in the middle of the meal, the first time in years he had eaten with anyone. He ate alone. That was rule one, unwritten, foundational: eat alone, sleep alone, know the exits. And yet here he was passing the fish to a pilot who hated him on principle, a deckhand who saw through him for sport, and a rich boy who was learning to coil rope, and nobody at the table knew his name, and the food tasted better than it had any right to.

"So," Callum said, around a mouthful, "does anyone actually know what we're looking for? Up there. My aunt bought silence with a smile and a checkbook, and I've learned that's how she says *terrified*."

"Expedition went quiet," Brannoc said. "We go up, we find them, we come back. That's the whole contract."

"Nine days of radio silence," Callum pressed. "In monsoon country. Doesn't that strike anyone as—"

"The river strikes people," Sefa said placidly. "People don't strike the river."

"—as *insufficient*," Callum finished, with the stubbornness of a man determined to be afraid productively. "Aunt Odile doesn't spend triple rates on insufficient. Something's up there. Something she knows and isn't saying."

The lantern hissed. The river muttered. Tamsin found three pairs of eyes on him — the pilot's fog-pale, the deckhand's dark and amused, the nephew's frightened and sharp — waiting, apparently, for the engineer to have opinions.

"I survey closed lines," he said. "What's up there is someone else's secret. I find the route, I keep us off the rocks, and I don't ask questions I'm not paid enough to answer."

"And are you?" Sefa asked. "Paid enough?"

"No," Tamsin said, and was surprised to hear himself smile, and more surprised to see Sefa smile back, quick and complicit, while Brannoc snorted what might have been laughter in a better mood, and even Callum's mouth twitched before he smothered it.

For one moment — one lantern-lit, moth-haunted moment — it felt less like a crew and more like a table. Then Brannoc knocked out his pipe against the rail and said, "First watch, engineer. Middle of the night, the river talks. Listen to what she says and write it down," and the moment folded itself away like a chart.

* * *

Middle watch, and the Kessarin talked.

Tamsin stood at the rail with the lantern doused, letting his eyes go dark-adapted, and listened. Water on the hull. Water on rock, off the port bow, where the escarpment's first buttress shouldered into the channel. Wind in the casuarinas, high and dry. And underneath it all, far upstream, something that took him a long minute to name because it didn't belong: a deep, rhythmic pulse, felt more than heard, regular as a heartbeat.

Machinery. Heavy machinery, working steadily, miles upriver in the dark.

He got out his notebook and wrote it down, because Brannoc had told him to and because writing things down was the closest thing he had to prayer. *Low-frequency percussion, bearing approx. north-northwest, interval ~4 seconds. Pumping? Pile-driving? At this hour?*

Behind him, bare feet on deck planks. Sefa, wrapped in a blanket, a candle stub in a jar in her hand. She followed his gaze upstream and her face did something complicated.

"You hear it," she said.

"Machinery."

"My grandmother would say the river's heart is beating wrong." She set the candle jar on the rail. The flame leaned upstream, toward the sound. "Mine says somebody's holding the water back. Somewhere up there, the river is being made to wait." She looked at him sideways. "You wrote it down. Good. Engineers write things down. Maybe between your numbers and my songs, we figure out what's true."

She left him the candle and went below, and Tamsin stood a long time at the rail with the flame leaning north and the pulse coming up the dark water, four seconds apart, patient as a debt.

He thought about throwing the salt overboard after all. He thought about the last train out of the valley, ten years ago, and how easy leaving used to be.

Then he went below, and slept, and dreamed of bridges.
