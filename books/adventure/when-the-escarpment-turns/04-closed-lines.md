## 4. Closed Lines

The tunnel announced itself before they saw it — a cold breath from the cliff, carrying the smell of wet stone and old iron and something underneath both, something sweeter and wrong. Tamsin felt it against his face as the boat swung toward the bank, that exhalation from the mountain's throat, and ten years of instinct stood up inside him like a man called by name.

"Port Kessarin," Brannoc said, and spat over the side. "What's left of it."

What was left was a shelf of rotted pilings and a crane arm rusted into a question mark, and above it all, cut into the escarpment itself, the mouth of the Vantrease Line's third tunnel — Portal Three, the old survey maps called it, though the men who'd drilled it had another name, one Tamsin had heard whispered in junction-town bars from here to the coast: the Long Breath. Two hundred and forty meters of rock between them and the river's upper gorge, bored through the shoulder of the mountain at a grade so gentle it had been considered an act of arrogance when it opened. Trains had run through it for eleven years. Then the collapse below, then the inquiry, then the closing, and now the mountain had begun taking it back.

Ivy hung from the portal's lip like a curtain half-drawn. Water seamed down the rockface in a dozen silver threads, feeding a green beard of moss that climbed to the keystone, where someone had chiseled the company crest — a wheel within a wheel — now soft-edged with lichen, a coin buried in velvet.

"You'll want the lamp," Sefa said behind him. She had come up on deck without being asked, a coil of rope over one shoulder, her bare feet silent on boards that groaned under everyone else. "The mountain doesn't like strangers looking at it in the dark."

"The mountain doesn't care about us either way," Tamsin said. "That's what I like about mountains."

Sefa looked at him for a long moment, the way she looked at the river when it changed its mind about something. "You say that now," she said, and went forward to make fast the bowline without waiting for orders.

* * *

Inside, the dark was total in a way the dark outside never was. His lamp pushed it back three meters and no further; beyond that circle the tunnel simply continued, black on black, and he had the old familiar sensation of walking down the throat of some enormous patient animal. The rails were still there under the muck — he could feel them through his boots, two lines of certainty running straight as a sentence into the dark. That was the thing about rail. Everything else in the world wandered. Rail committed.

He walked the centerline with his hand trailing the air beside him, counting paces out of habit, reading the tunnel by touch and echo the way other men read print. The drainage channels along the walls were silted but sound. The timber sets overhead were black with age but solid when he rapped them — good heartwood, laid by crews who'd known they were building for a century. Whoever had closed this line hadn't closed it because it failed. It had been abandoned the way a letter is abandoned in a drawer: not dead, just put away.

At forty meters in, he stopped.

The air had changed. Not the temperature — the texture. There was a current moving through the tunnel now, faint but definite, flowing toward the portal behind him, and it carried on it a smell he knew the way you know a voice across a crowded room. Blasting powder. Not the old smell, not the ghost of the drilling crews' work thirty years gone. Fresh. Days old at most, maybe hours.

Tamsin stood very still in the dark and let the engineer in him do what it did, which was refuse to be afraid until it had finished counting.

He crouched. Swept his lamp low along the wall. And there, where the moss grew thickest, where the green hung down like a curtain meant to be drawn across the evidence — there were marks in the stone. Drill scars. A ring of them, six inches apart, fresh-cut, the rock dust still pale in their bottoms, not yet washed dark by the seep water. Someone had drilled a charge ring into this wall within the last few days, then covered the work with cut moss, pressed flat against the stone with a care that was almost loving.

He put his fingers into one of the holes. The dust came away cool and dry.

* * *

He found the first charge ninety meters in, past the point where daylight behind him had shrunk to a gray coin and the black ahead had thickened to something with weight. It was set low in the wall's shoulder, tucked into a natural seam, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp, and wired — wired, not fused — with a length of copper line that ran up the wall and vanished into a crevice above, following the tunnel deeper in.

He did not touch it. He knelt there instead with his lamp held close and read the work the way he'd once read other men's bridge calculations, looking for the signature in the numbers. The charge was placed by someone who understood rock. Not a demolition man — a demolition man would have used four times the powder and taken the whole shoulder down in one statement. This was surgical. A shaped charge, seated to shear the crown of the arch and drop the ceiling in a controlled cascade, plugging the tunnel with fifty tons of mountain. Enough to seal it. Just enough. The work of someone who wanted the line closed quietly, permanently, and deniably — who wanted anyone coming after to find rubble and call it age, call it the mountain reclaiming what it owned.

Call it accident. Call it geology.

Tamsin had spent ten years watching people call things geology.

He sat back on his heels. The lamp flame leaned in a draft that wasn't there a moment ago, and far off in the black ahead — or perhaps not far off, sound lied in tunnels, folded distance double — he heard it: a knock. Three strikes, pause. Three strikes, pause. Not falling rock. Rock fell in avalanches and slumps, in long grinding voices. This was rhythm. This was a man with a hammer and a chisel, working.

Someone was in the tunnel ahead of him. Someone was finishing the job.

His body made the decision before his mind signed off on it. He killed the lamp.

The dark came down on him like water closing over his head, complete, absolute, the kind of dark that has weight and pressure and a slow heartbeat of its own. He stayed crouched by the charge, breathing through his mouth, shallow, quiet, and listened past his own pulse. Ten years of practice at being nobody served him now. He knew how to be furniture. He knew how to be part of the mountain.

The knocking came again. Closer, or louder — impossible to say. With it now, fainter, a scrape and shuffle, the particular drag-sound of a loaded barrow wheel with a flat spot on its rim. *Whump. Whump.* Rhythmic. Patient. Coming toward him or going away, and every second he knelt in the blind dark the ambiguity got more expensive.

He thought about Odile Vantrease on the deck of her chartered boat, saying *nine days silent* with her gloved hands folded and her eyes doing arithmetic. He thought about the triple pay, the threat dressed as generosity. He thought about the expedition upriver, nine days of nothing, and about how silence, in his experience, was rarely a natural condition. Silence was manufactured. Silence was what you got when someone decided the story should end at a certain page.

And now here was the proof, set in oilcloth and copper wire at knee height in a dead tunnel: someone was erasing the road ahead of them, methodically, professionally, and had been doing it since before Odile's expedition went quiet. Which meant the sealing wasn't a response to them. It was the reason for the silence. Cause, not consequence.

The barrow wheel squealed closer. Twenty meters, maybe fifteen. In the dark Tamsin's hand found the wall, found the drill scars, found the cold mouths of the holes, and rested there on the fresh-cut stone while his mind ran the geometry he didn't want it to run: if they'd charged this section, they'd charged others. How many rings between here and the upper gorge? How many portals? Was the whole route being dismantled behind a curtain of moss, mile by mile, so that by the time anyone fought their way upriver there would be nothing left to reach, no tunnel, no track, no evidence of anything except a river and a rumor?

His heart was doing something unprofessional. He told it to wait.

Fifteen meters. Ten. The lamplight he couldn't see but could feel now — a warmth in the air, a trembling of the darkness ahead, the faintest amber breath around the tunnel's curve. He flattened himself into the drainage channel, into the cold trickle of seep water, and pulled his own lamp tight against his chest, and waited to learn whether he was a surveyor or a witness.

The light swung around the bend.

It was a boy. That was the first obscenity of it — a boy of maybe sixteen, gaunt, hollow-eyed, in a salvage fleet slicker two sizes too big, hauling a barrow of drill steel and powder bags with the weary competence of a man three times his age. He passed within eight feet of Tamsin without seeing him, lamp swinging, whistling something tuneless under his breath, and Tamsin lay in the gutter-water of a dead railway and watched a child wire a mountain shut and understood, with a clarity that felt like a blow to the sternum, exactly what kind of money and patience was at work upriver. You didn't hire children to do this unless the work was too large for secrecy to survive adult wages. You didn't teach a boy to seal tunnels unless you intended to seal many of them.

The boy went by. The light shrank. The *whump-whump* of the barrow faded into the mountain's own silence, and Tamsin lay in the dark a moment longer, letting the cold water speak against his neck, arranging what he knew into rows.

One: the route ahead was being destroyed on purpose.
Two: the destruction predated their departure — this was planned, funded, scheduled.
Three: only one organization on this river had the money, the equipment, and the motive to schedule the erasure of a mountain railway.
Four: Odile Vantrease's expedition hadn't been lost. It had been *closed*, the way this tunnel was being closed. And whatever it had found up there, someone intended that no one else should ever stand where it had stood.

He got up out of the water. His knees cracked in the dark like small detonations, and he winced, absurdly, at the noise.

* * *

He didn't relight the lamp until he was fifty meters from the charge, and even then he kept it hooded, cupped, a coal rather than a torch. The walk back out took twice as long as the walk in; he moved like a man wading upstream through his own thoughts. At the portal the daylight hit him like a slap, huge and indifferent, and Sefa was there on the rocks below with a fish knife in her hand and a string of silver bodies already cleaned, and she looked up at his face and stopped filleting.

"Well?" Brannoc called from the boat, where he was re-packing a mooring line with unnecessary violence. "Is it walkable?"

"It's walkable," Tamsin said. His voice came out level, which cost him something. "The track bed's sound. Better than sound. Somebody built this line to outlive us all."

"But?" Sefa said. She'd risen. The knife was still in her hand, forgotten, dripping river onto the stone.

"But somebody's closing it." He crouched at the edge of the rocks, where they could all see him, and made himself say it plainly, because plainly was the only way it would hold its shape. "Fresh charges. Set within days. Shaped charges, professional placement, wired in series — whoever's doing it means to bring the crowns down and plug the tunnels with rubble and have us all believe the mountain did it in its sleep. I watched a boy no older than sixteen haul powder up that tunnel not twenty minutes ago. They're not hiding the work because they're ashamed of it. They're hiding it because it isn't finished."

Brannoc had come up the rocks without Tamsin hearing him, which was its own small lesson in the pilot's economy of motion. He stood looking at the portal, at the ivy and the lichen-softened crest, and his jaw worked once, twice, like a man chewing a word he didn't want to swallow.

"Halvard's fleet moored at the lower weir three weeks back," he said finally. "I heard it at the fuel dock. Six tenders, black hulls, riding high. Riding high means empty, and empty means they're carrying nothing *up*." He turned. "You don't send six empty ships upriver unless you're bringing something down. Or making sure nothing comes down."

"Or making sure nothing goes *up*," Tamsin said.

Sefa had begun, without any apparent decision, to coil her fish line. Her hands moved fast and precise. "The river's been strange," she said, not looking up. "Since the last full moon. Holding its breath. I told you the water was wrong, Tamsin Marchetti. I told you it was being kept somewhere."

"You said the water was moody."

"I said it was *kept*. There's a difference." She looked up then, and her eyes were the flat gray-green of deep water, and for the first time since he'd boarded this boat Tamsin saw that the superstition was not superstition at all but literacy — she read the river the way he read rail, fluently, natively, in a language the rest of them only spoke badly. "A river that's being held is a river being saved up. And you don't save up a river unless you mean to spend it on something."

Nobody answered that. Upstream, the escarpment ran north in long blue folds, fold behind fold, each one higher, each one holding — somewhere in its stony grip — a silent camp, a burned mast, an answer. The afternoon light was going bronze on the water, and the beauty of it sat strangely on Tamsin's chest, the way beauty always did now, like a coat that belonged to someone else.

"So we go around," Callum said from the boat. Tamsin had forgotten he was there; the young man sat hunched in the stern with a chart across his knees, and his voice had the brittle brightness of glass about to go. "There are portage trails. Old ones. We don't need the tunnels, we can—"

"The portage trails climb through the same gorges," Tamsin said. "If they're sealing the tunnels, they've covered the trails. And even if they haven't—" he looked back at the portal, at the dark mouth with its curtain of green, "—we're not racing to a place anymore. We're racing to a *record*. Something up there was worth six empty ships and a boy with a barrow of powder. Something up there was worth making a whole expedition disappear. I want to know what it is."

"And if it's nothing?" Callum's laugh was thin. "If we risk all our necks for a box of old salvage manifests?"

"Then it's nothing," Tamsin said, "and I'll have spent my fear on nothing, which is more than I usually get for it."

Brannoc snorted — the closest thing to laughter Tamsin had heard from him. But it was Sefa who settled it, in the way of people whose authority never announces itself. She dropped her coiled line into the boat, picked up her fish, and said, to the river rather than to any of them:

"We go at first light. The water's lying low tonight. It won't fight us till morning."

And that night, anchored in the lee below the portal, Tamsin lay awake in the narrow bunk listening to the mountain breathe, and did his own arithmetic in the dark. Nine days of silence. Weeks of charges. Six empty ships. Somewhere upstream, a man with money and a griddle-full of secrets was erasing a landscape one tunnel at a time, and had been doing it calmly, patiently, since long before any of them bought their tickets.

The dread he'd carried aboard like ballast shifted in him, settled, changed its nature. Dread was for things that might happen. This had already happened. The silence wasn't a mystery anymore; it was a *schedule*.

Which meant the race was real, and the clock had been running for weeks, and every hour they spent sleeping, eating, arguing, becoming — every hour the crew of strangers spent turning, however reluctantly, into a crew — the boy with the barrow was deeper into the mountain, wiring the dark.

Tamsin closed his eyes and saw, with terrible clarity, the drill scars under the moss. Six inches apart. Fresh-cut. Cool dust in the bottom of the holes.

They'd find the next portal tomorrow. He was almost certain he already knew what they'd find there.

Almost.
