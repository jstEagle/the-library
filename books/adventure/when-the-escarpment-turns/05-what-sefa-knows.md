## 5. What Sefa Knows

The river told Sefa things before it told anyone else.

She had learned its language the way other children learned prayers — from her grandmother, on the mudbanks below Vell's Landing, with her feet in the shallows and her grandmother's hand resting warm on the crown of her head. *Listen with your skin, not your ears,* the old woman had said. *Ears hear what the river says. Skin hears what it means.* And so Sefa had grown up fluent in a tongue no chart recorded: the way the current thickened before rain, the way silt rose in the water like breath held in a throat, the particular hush that fell over the Kessarin when something upstream had changed and the change was still traveling toward them, patient as grief.

On the fourth morning of the run, she woke before dawn in the bow of the *Marrow Bell* and knew, before she opened her eyes, that the river was wrong.

Not dangerous-wrong. Not the wrongness of flash flood or snags or the collapsing banks that made the upper gorges a graveyard for boats. This was a subtler wrongness, one she might have missed if she hadn't been born to it. The current was too even. That was the only way she could say it, even to herself. Rivers were never even. They stuttered and swelled and dragged at their own beds like animals testing a fence. But this morning the water sliding past the hull moved with the smooth, unbroken patience of poured oil, and beneath that smoothness ran a tension she could feel through the deck planks, up through her knees, into her back teeth.

She sat up in her nest of netting and canvas and looked downstream, because that was where the answer would be.

The gorge walls stood black against the graying sky, dripping. Mist lay along the water in long unbroken sheets, and the mist was moving upstream.

Sefa went very still.

Mist did not move upstream. Mist drifted where the air told it, and morning air in the gorges always flowed downriver, following the cold water toward the lowlands. She watched it for a long time — ten breaths, twenty — thin gray ghosts sliding past her shoulder against the current, against all sense, like souls walking backward out of the world.

Then she climbed down from the bow, quiet as she could, though the boat woke anyway. Boats always knew.

* * *

By full light they had tied off at a shelf of rock below the second narrows, where an old loading crane rusted over the water like the skeleton of a heron. Brannoc wanted the halt; the channel ahead narrowed hard around a bend he didn't trust without daylight on it, and Tamsin had agreed too quickly, in the way of a man who wanted his hands on solid stone for an hour. Callum was still asleep under the tarp amidships, curled small, as if he could make himself less of a burden by taking up less space. Odile Vantrease stood at the rail with her field glasses, sweeping the bend, and Sefa watched her watch and thought: *she counts everything, that woman. Even the things she pretends not to see.*

Sefa filled the kettle from the river, and that was how she confirmed it.

The water came up cold — colder than it should have been four days into the monsoon's approach, colder than the season allowed. She held the pail a moment, letting her palm read what her fingers already knew, then dipped again lower, near the silt line. The sediment was wrong too. Fine and pale and evenly mixed, the way silt looked when it had been sitting still somewhere for days and days, settling into layers, and had only recently been stirred loose and sent on its way.

Still water, released recently. Cold because it had come from the bottom of something deep.

Her grandmother's voice rose in her memory, clear across thirty years: *When the river runs smooth and the mist walks home, someone has put a hand on the water.*

There were stories about that. Old ones, half superstition and half warning, the kind the boat families told their children along with the songs. About the years of the great timber drives, when companies built hidden dams in the high gorges and held back whole seasons of water so they could release it on command and float their logs down a river that belonged to everyone else. About the villages drowned when those dams failed, and the fines that followed, and the law written afterward in letters every pilot learned: *No hand upon the Kessarin.* The river authority patrolled for weirs the way other authorities patrolled for murder. Because that was what it was, in the end. Holding a river was holding a knife at the throat of everyone downstream.

And yet the mist walked upstream, and the kettle water came up cold from the bottom of some deep held place, and the current ran smooth as glass over a tension you could feel in your teeth.

Sefa set the kettle down and went to find the engineer.

* * *

Tamsin Marchetti was crouched at the edge of the rock shelf with a surveyor's notebook open on his knee, sketching the bend ahead, and Sefa watched him for a moment before speaking because there was something she'd been learning about him these four days and it unsettled her more than the mist did. Most men who came upriver watched the water the way strangers did — as scenery, as obstacle, as money to be spent. Tamsin watched it the way she did. Not the meaning of it, maybe; he didn't have the skin for that. But the behavior of it. He read currents like timetables, eddies like schedules, and twice now he'd called a hazard before Brannoc did, and Brannoc had said nothing either time, which from Brannoc was a shout.

"Engineer," she said.

He looked up. He had a face that kept itself closed, but his eyes came up fast, always fast, like a man who'd learned that anything arriving slowly was already too late.

"The water is being held," Sefa said.

He frowned. "Held?"

"Upstream. A weir." She crouched beside him and laid her palm flat on the wet stone of the shelf, and after a moment he did the same, because he was the kind of man who checked a thing rather than laugh at it, and she had decided four days ago that this was worth following him up a river for. "Feel the shelf. The river's pulse is gone."

He was quiet a moment. Then: "It's true. I noticed the current was — I couldn't name it. Too regular." He said it almost angrily, as if the river had lied to him personally.

"You can't name it because it isn't supposed to exist." She took his hand — he flinched, just slightly, at being touched, another thing she'd noted — and pressed his palm to the stone where the wash lapped. "A living river stutters. Swells, slackens, swells. Like a heart. This—" she moved his hand a finger's width to where the water ran past the shelf, "—this is a heart held still by a fist around it. And feel the temperature. Cold from the depths. This water sat somewhere deep and dark for days before it was let go. Days, engineer. Someone filled a lake in our path and has been feeding it out slow."

Tamsin pulled his hand back and stared at the water as if it had changed shape in front of him. "That's illegal. Holding the Kessarin is — there are laws older than the escarpment towns. The authority burns weirs on sight."

"Yes."

"Who could—" He stopped. She watched him arrive at it, watched the color leave his face in the gray morning light, watched him turn his head slowly downstream toward the tender that had shadowed them since the narrows, black-hulled, keeping just beyond hailing distance, patient as the held water itself.

"The fleet," he said.

"The fleet," Sefa agreed. "Halvard's people didn't just buy the salvage rights and the survey permits and whatever else that woman's lawyers bought. They bought the tide." She heard her own voice go low and strange. "They're not racing us anymore, engineer. They're *scheduling* us. Six tides until the monsoon seals the gorges — that's what everyone keeps saying, six tides, six tides, like the river is a clock. But if they hold the water back, they decide when each tide comes. They decide how much river we get and when we get it. They can give us a drought in the middle of a monsoon." Her grandmother's voice again, underneath her own: *a knife at the throat of everyone downstream.* "Or they can take the drought away."

Tamsin had gone very still, the stillness of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't want the total of. "If they release it all at once," he said quietly, "in a narrow gorge—"

"It wouldn't be a flood. Floods have faces. You see them coming." Sefa looked at the smooth, silent water sliding past, wearing its borrowed calm like a mask. "This would be the river simply deciding, all at once, to be somewhere else. No face. No roar until it was already on you. My grandmother saw one when she was a girl, below the old timber dam at Karst Bend. Forty-one people. They said afterward that the survivors kept waking at night reaching for things that weren't there anymore."

The wind came down the gorge then, and for once it moved with the water instead of against it, and the mist tore apart and reformed, and Sefa shivered though the morning wasn't cold.

* * *

Brannoc took the news the way he took everything — with a grunt and a chew of his bitter leaf and a silence that lasted long enough to be its own opinion. Then he spat over the rail and said, "Knew a man ran cargo for Halvard's outfit, years back. Said they owned the weather in three valleys. Thought it was drink-talk." He worked the wheel spokes with his big knuckles, not looking at either of them. "Wasn't drink-talk."

Odile Vantrease received it standing, spine straight, field glasses hanging forgotten against her chest, and Sefa watched something move behind her eyes that was not fear and not surprise but a cold recalculation, a woman revising a ledger she'd believed balanced. "Then the silence from my expedition may not be silence at all," she said. "It may be *management*. If Halvard controls the water, he controls who reaches the Corvane and who doesn't, and he can make the river do his killing for him and call it an act of God." Her voice stayed level. Her knuckles, wrapped around the rail, did not.

Callum, woken at last and hollow-eyed, heard the word *weir* and laughed — one short, cracked note with nothing in it. "Of course they did. Why sink ships when you can sink *time*?" Nobody answered him, and he pulled the tarp closer and said no more, but Sefa saw him looking at the water after that the way a man looks at a debt collector.

They cast off within the hour. Brannoc wanted the miles while the channel was calm, and none of them argued, because the calm itself had become the threat — every smooth mile a reminder that somewhere above them, behind some bend they hadn't reached, men were standing at gates and deciding, hour by hour, how much river to allow them.

Sefa took the bow.

It was her place and her work — reading the channel, calling depth and drift to Brannoc — but today she did it with her palms bare and her whole attention split between the water ahead and the water itself, listening with her skin the way she'd been taught, and what she heard frightened her in a new way. Not the danger. She'd lived her whole life with the river's dangers; they were honest, at least, the way wolves are honest. What frightened her was the *obedience*. The Kessarin had moods the way her grandmother had moods, sudden and vast and never quite predictable, and Sefa had built her life on the faith that no man could do more than ride those moods. Now she rode a river that had been taught to heel. It felt like watching a falcon fly with its eyes sewn shut — all that power, all that wildness, bent to somebody's timetable.

*Nature itself, weaponized.* The phrase arrived in her head in Tamsin's dry engineer's cadence, and she hated how well it fit.

Near midday they passed the first sign of it: a surveyor's stake driven into the bank, fresh-cut wood still pale, painted with a mark Sefa didn't recognize — a circle bisected by a bar. A hundred yards on, another. Then a third, on the far side, and between them, faintly visible through the haze, a cable sagged across the entire channel, hung with small tin flags that turned and glittered.

"A gauge line," Tamsin said behind her, notebook out, voice tight. "Measuring stage height. They're logging the water level at intervals. That's not construction staging, that's *operation*." He wrote for a moment, then stopped writing. "Sefa. How far upstream is the weir?"

She considered. Listened. The cold in the water had a flavor, almost, a direction, the way smoke has a direction. "Two gorges," she said. "Maybe three. Where the river goes underground below the Split Rock — any hand put on the water there would be invisible from the air. From anywhere."

"That's inside the closed lines," Tamsin said. "Inside our survey grant. Which means the permits Odile bought cover the ground the fleet is illegally flooding." He laughed once, short and bitter, a sound very like Callum's from that morning. "They used our own paperwork as cover."

* * *

They made camp that night on a gravel bar in the lee of a cliff, and Sefa sat last watch, as she preferred, alone with the river and the small fire's reflection broken on the current.

The mist walked upstream again. She watched it a long time, those gray shapes sliding against the flow, and she found herself talking to it in the old way, under her breath, the way you talk to a sick animal. *I know you're in there,* she told the water. *I know this isn't you. Hold on.*

Because that was the thing she couldn't say aloud on the boat, not to Brannoc with his debts and not to the engineer with his closed face and certainly not to Odile Vantrease, who bought things: that the river was a captive now, and captives did desperate things. Her grandmother had taught her the moods, but the oldest story, the one under all the others, was simpler than moods. *The Kessarin remembers every hand ever laid on it,* the old woman had said, *and it settles its accounts in its own season.* A held river was not a safe river. A held river was a wound with a tourniquet on it, and tourniquets came off — gently, or all at once.

Somewhere upstream, two gorges away, men stood at gates. Somewhere upstream, forty-one ghosts from Karst Bend waited in the cold held water with the patience of the drowned. And somewhere downstream, behind them, the black tender kept its distance, its running lights burning steady and green, like a promise being kept.

Sefa fed a stick to the fire and watched the sparks climb, free for the length of their burning.

In the morning, she decided, she would tell Tamsin the rest of it — the part she'd been carrying since the mist first walked upstream, the part that had kept her quiet through the day's run. That the cold water's flavor had been changing since noon. Strengthening. Deepening. As if whatever held the river was not feeding it out slowly anymore, as the fleet had done for four days, but *gathering* it.

As if the gates upstream were being winched open inch by inch, and the fist around the Kessarin's throat was choosing, right now, this night, whether to squeeze — or to let go.
