## 6. The Magnate's Shadow

The name came up the river before the ship did.

Sefa heard it first — she heard everything first — standing at the bow with her bare feet braced on the wet planks, her head cocked like a heron's. "Engines," she said, in that flat way of hers that made every word sound like a stone dropped into still water. "Two of them. Big ones. Not ours."

Brannoc spat over the rail without looking up from the wheel. "Everything on this river has bigger engines than ours."

"No." Sefa's chin lifted a degree. "Not like this. These are *quiet* big. Fleet-quiet."

Tamsin set down the survey ledger he'd been annotating and came to the rail. The Kessarin ran high and brown between its walls of green, carrying the smell of drowned leaves and hot stone, and for a long moment there was nothing to see but water and the slow procession of cliffs — and then, around the bend below them, a shape resolved out of the heat-shimmer that was not a riverboat at all.

It was black. Not painted black — *built* black, hull and superstructure both, the color of wet slate, of iron left in rain, so that it seemed less a vessel than a shadow given displacement. It moved against the current without strain, twin wakes folding off its bow in clean white seams, and it flew no colors he could see. It did not need to. Anyone who put a ship like that on the Kessarin wanted to be known precisely as much as they were known now: seen, and not identified.

"Vantrease's tender?" Callum asked from the shade of the awning, where he'd been nursing a cup of tea gone cold an hour ago. His voice had the brittle brightness he used when he was afraid and didn't want the fear examined. "She said she'd send resupply at the second tide."

"She said," Brannoc agreed, in a tone that meant nothing of the kind.

The tender closed the distance with the unhurried confidence of something that had never once been made to wait. Tamsin watched it come and felt a coldness gather under his sternum that had nothing to do with the spray. He knew that hull. Not this ship — but that *class*, that yard, that money. He had spent ten years learning to recognize the shapes that power built for itself, the way a man who once survived a flood learns to read clouds.

He had also, though he told no one this, spent ten years reading names. Every inquiry transcript he could beg or buy, every newspaper archive in every junction town he'd passed through, every drunken engineer's story told too close to his ear in third-rate boarding houses — he collected them the way other men collected debts. And one name appeared in more of those documents than any other, always near the bottom, always in small type, always attached to salvage contracts, dredging rights, wreck-clearance permits, riverbed leases along exactly the stretch of coast where the Marchetti Bridge had come down with forty-one people on the morning train.

Renn Halvard.

*Corvane Salvage & Recovery,* the letterheads read. *Halvard Maritime Holdings.* The man himself almost never appeared — a signature here, a deposition absence noted there, a photograph in one trade journal, ten years old, showing a broad-shouldered figure at a christening with his face turned half away as if even then he resented being looked at.

"He's coming alongside," Sefa said. "He wants us to stop."

"How can you possibly—" Callum began.

"Because he's been matching our speed for two minutes," she said, "and now he's turning his wheel. You don't turn toward another boat unless you mean to talk."

Brannoc eased their throttle back. The old boat sighed, wallowed, settled into the current. Ahead, the black tender swung wide in a maneuver that must have cost its owner more than their entire vessel, engines reversing with a sound like a held breath released, and slid parallel to them across twenty feet of brown water. Lines went across. Fenders dropped. And a gangway ladder rattled down against their gunwale with the crisp authority of a knocker at a door that intended to be answered.

A voice carried over the gap before anyone climbed down. It was a good voice, Tamsin thought — low, level, pitched to carry without shouting, the voice of a man who had learned that volume was what you used when authority failed.

"Gentlemen. Ladies. I wonder if I might impose on your hospitality for an hour. I have tea. Better tea than whatever that is." A pause, aimed, Tamsin suspected, at Callum's cold cup. "And I have questions that are better asked aboard than shouted across open water."

Nobody moved. The river breathed against the hulls. Somewhere upslope a bird screamed twice and quit.

"We're working," Brannoc said at last. "Charter's exclusive. Captain's word is no visitors."

"Captain Vantrease hired you, I believe." The voice was pleasant about it. "And Miss Vantrease and I are old correspondents. She would want me aboard. Ask her yourself, if you like — my wireless will reach hers faster than yours will reach anything."

That was true, and everyone on deck knew it was true, and Tamsin watched the knowledge move through his crewmates like a current through water: Brannoc's jaw tightening, Sefa going very still, Callum already halfway to the rail because Callum had never met a rich man's invitation he could resist. Odile Vantrease was below, going over charts she did not share, and had been for three hours.

"I'll get her," Callum said, and disappeared down the companionway before anyone could argue.

Tamsin stayed at the rail. He told himself it was caution — his oldest habit, older than any of his forged credentials, the reflex of a man who had learned that being noticed was the first stage of being ruined. But underneath the caution something else was moving, something colder and more curious, the part of him that had spent a decade pulling threads through the wreckage of his own life. If the black ship belonged to whom he believed it belonged, then the man coming down that ladder was the closest thing to an author his ruin had ever had.

He found he wanted to look at him. That frightened him more than the ship did.

* * *

Renn Halvard came down the ladder the way heavy men descend when they've done it ten thousand times — without hurry, without apology, testing each rung as if the world owed him sound footing. He was perhaps sixty, thick through the shoulders and gone soft at the middle, with a face like a cliff that had been quarried rather than weathered: everything angular removed, everything useful retained. Grey hair cut short. Hands enormous, scarred across the backs in the manner of a man who had once worked rope and steel and had long since stopped needing to prove it. He wore plain canvas and salt-stained boots, and Tamsin understood immediately that the plainness was a costume, tailored and expensive, the uniform of a magnate performing modesty for an audience of working men.

His eyes, though, were the thing. They were pale, almost colorless, and they moved across the deck the way a surveyor's eye moves across land — measuring, valuing, filing. They touched Brannoc, Sefa, the winches, the draft marks on the hull, the stacked crates of supplies, and Tamsin watched each item get weighed and priced in real time. When the eyes reached him, they stopped.

For one heartbeat — no more — the professional appraisal cracked, and something else looked out. Recognition, yes. But more than recognition. *Identification.* The look of a man finding, in an unexpected place, a face from a document he had signed.

Then it was gone, sealed over so smoothly that Tamsin might have invented it, and Renn Halvard smiled the smile of a guest arriving slightly early.

"You'll be the engineer," he said, extending one of the vast scarred hands. "Marchetti. Tamsin Marchetti. Your reputation precedes you — Vantrease doesn't hire cheap, and she certainly doesn't hire quietly. Half the river offices between here and the delta were asking after you last month."

Every word was a hook dressed as flattery. Tamsin took the hand. It was like gripping a mooring bollard. "You have me at a disadvantage," he said. "I don't believe we've met."

"No," Halvard agreed pleasantly. "We haven't." He let go and turned, taking in the boat again with proprietary ease, as though the transfer of ownership were already underway. "But I make it my business to know every competent man on this water. Competence is the rarest cargo there is. Everything else floats."

Odile Vantrease emerged from below like weather. She had changed clothes — Tamsin registered it distantly, the fresh linen, the re-pinned hair, armor donned in ninety seconds — and she crossed the deck with her chin at the angle she reserved for boardrooms and gunfights, which in her case were frequently the same occasion.

"Renn." Her voice could have chilled the river. "This charter is private."

"It is." Halvard spread his hands. "Which is why I knocked. May I sit? Standing conversations on boats always end with someone overboard, and I'm too old to swim and too honest to pretend I'd enjoy it."

No one invited him to sit. He sat anyway, on the crate nearest the awning's shade, arranging himself with the comfort of a man in his own drawing room, and accepted the tea Callum — traitorously, eagerly — poured for him.

"Nine days ago," Halvard said, cradling the cup, "an expedition went silent above the Third Gorge. Sixteen people. Surveyors, divers, two of my own company's consultants among them. Good people. People whose families write to me." He sipped. "Miss Vantrease funded that expedition. I know because I tried to fund it first, and she outbid me, which I confess I admired. So you understand that I have a personal interest in its welfare — and a commercial interest in this river remaining *closed* until its welfare is established."

"The river isn't yours to close," Odile said.

"No," Halvard agreed mildly. "It belongs to the authority, which licenses it, and the authority is a reasonable institution staffed by reasonable people, and reasonable people do not reopen shipping lanes over unexploded ordnance, unstable banks, and missing survey teams. Not before monsoon. Not this year. Possibly not ever." He looked up at her, and for a moment the pleasantness thinned enough to show the machinery beneath. "You're racing a clock, Odile. I'm asking you to stop racing it. That's all this visit is. An old colleague's concern."

"And the concern has a price," Tamsin said.

It was out of him before he had decided to speak, and he felt the whole deck register it — Brannoc's head turning, Sefa's stillness sharpening, Odile's eyes flicking to him with something that was not quite surprise and not quite gratitude. Halvard turned his pale gaze back and regarded him with what looked almost like pleasure.

"There it is," he said softly. "Yes. There's a price. There's always a price; anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something worse. Triple what Vantrease is paying you — all of you, crew included, cash on delivery at the next landing. Plus passage out. Plus, Mr. Marchetti—" and here the voice gentled into something that was almost kind, which was how Tamsin knew it was the most dangerous thing in the sentence, "—plus a letter of reference under whatever name you're currently using, countersigned by interests whose word opens doors in every port from here to the capital. You strike me as a man who understands the value of a clean credential."

The river slapped the hull. Somewhere below decks, water gurgled through the bilge pump, patient as breathing.

Tamsin stood very still. Ten years of discipline held his face in place while his mind went white and roaring, because Halvard had just told him — casually, generously, in front of witnesses — that he knew. Not suspected. *Knew.* Knew that the credentials were forged, that the name was borrowed or bought, that the quiet engineer with the excellent references was a man running. Which meant Halvard had looked into him. Which meant someone had sold him the look, or he had dug it himself, which meant—

Which meant the man on the crate had connected Tamsin Marchetti, disgraced, to Tamsin Marchetti, hired, and had decided the connection was worth a lever rather than a bullet. That was its own answer. Men destroyed what they merely feared. They bought what they *needed.*

"You've had me investigated," Tamsin said. Flat. Careful.

"I've had everyone on this boat investigated," Halvard said, with the mildness of a man discussing freight rates. "It's nothing personal. It's hygiene." He rose, joints cracking, and straightened his plain canvas coat. "Consider the offer. All of you. There's no shame in it — the shame is in drowning for someone else's obsession." He paused at the rail, ladder waiting, and his colorless eyes found Tamsin's one last time, and held, and in them Tamsin saw the thing he had sailed ten years to find and would have given anything never to see:

Nothing. No guilt. No flicker. A man looking at a piece of his own work and seeing only an obstacle to be priced.

"Miss Vantrease. Captain. My tender will hold station astern until nightfall. My wireless is on channel four if you change your minds." The smile again, brief and warm as a lamp. "The river gets narrow ahead. I'd hate for anyone to be caught in it doing something foolish."

He went down the ladder without touching the rails, and the black tender cast off its lines, and the great quiet engines took the weight, and the shadow slid away downstream — *downstream*, Tamsin noted, toward the gorges, not away from them — until the heat-shimmer folded it back into the color of the water.

* * *

They stood on deck a long time after it was gone.

"Well," Callum said finally, into the silence. "Triple pay."

"*Callum.*"

"I'm just saying it's triple pay! We nearly died twice since the second gorge and we haven't even found a camp yet, and that man writes checks with a fountain pen—"

"He wrote you a eulogy while pouring your tea," Sefa said. "You didn't notice?"

Callum shut his mouth.

Brannoc hadn't moved from the wheel. His hands rested on the spokes and his eyes stayed on the channel ahead, and when he spoke it was low, for the deck generally. "Fleet tenders don't hold station astern out of courtesy. That's a shepherd's position. Anything comes at us from behind, he sees it first. Anything we signal, he hears it first." He worked his jaw. "We just grew a shadow."

"Then we lose it," Odile said.

"At night, in the narrows, with six tides left?" Brannoc shook his head slowly. "You don't lose a ship like that, miss. You just stop being able to see it."

Tamsin walked to the stern. The wake unspooled behind them, brown and brief, closing over itself as if they had never passed. He thought about the handshake — bollard-hard, deliberate, *offered* — and about the pause before Halvard's hand had extended, the half-second in which a man decides whether to pretend. He hadn't pretended. He had wanted Tamsin to feel it: *I know what you are. I knew before you boarded this boat. And I am telling you, gently, that I can unmake you whenever it suits me.*

Unless. Unless it wasn't a threat at all. Unless it was an *invitation* — the same offer wearing different clothes, the same bargain Halvard had made with a hundred smaller men in a hundred smaller rooms: take the money, keep the false name, stay bought, stay quiet. The collapse inquiry had been full of such men. Witnesses who recanted. Inspectors who retired early. Engineers whose reports arrived pre-written. He had read their testimony a hundred times, hating them, and here was the machinery of it laid out before him, courteous and warm, offering him a chair.

*Your past and this mission are the same story,* something in him said, with terrible clarity. *Same river. Same wreck. Same man. You didn't come up here by accident. Nothing about you has ever been accidental — you've just never been allowed to see the timetable.*

"Tamsin." Odile's voice, behind him. "You spoke to him differently than the rest of us. Like you'd met."

He turned. The late light lay gold on the water, on her face, on the three others watching him with varying degrees of suspicion — and beyond them all, far downstream, the place where a black shadow had dissolved into brown.

"No," Tamsin said. "We've never met."

He looked at his own hands, steady on the rail, and told the truth as much of it as he could afford.

"But I've read his signature before."

Behind them, faint under the engine drone, Sefa had gone to the bow again, head cocked, listening to the river — and whatever she heard there made her cross herself, quick and small, before she turned to call them forward.
