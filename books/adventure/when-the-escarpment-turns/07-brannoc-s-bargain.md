## 7. Brannoc's Bargain

The tender came upriver at dusk, black as a beetle's back, and Brannoc felt it before he saw it — a wrongness in the water, the way the current flinched around a hull too heavy for this stretch of the Kessarin.

He was alone on deck when it happened. The others had gone below: Vantrease to her charts and her bitter little lamp, Callum to his sulking, Tamsin Marchetti to whatever quiet corner engineers went to when they thought nobody was watching them count things. Sefa had banked the cookfire and was murmuring to the river the way she did every evening, feeding it rice grains and asking permission for tomorrow. That left Brannoc at the wheel, which was where he liked to be at dusk, when the gorge walls went purple and the water turned to hammered tin, and a man could almost believe he'd never done anything he was ashamed of.

Almost.

The tender ran no lights. It came around the bend below them riding the last of the current, and Brannoc's hands knew what it was before his mind admitted it — that low, greedy silhouette, the engine note tuned to a purr instead of a growl, the way it held its line against the cross-current like something that owned the river rather than borrowed it. Halvard's people built their boats the way Halvard built his arguments: sleek, patient, and impossible to argue with once they had you in their grip.

Brannoc cut the engine.

Not because cutting it would hide them — a boat drifting silent in the dusk was no quieter than one under power, not to ears like those aboard a fleet tender — but because his hands needed something to do that wasn't turning the wheel toward the channel that would take them past it. He stood there with the throttle in his fist and his heart going like a drum in a funeral procession, and he watched the black hull come on, and he thought about Maren.

He always thought about Maren. That was the trouble. A man without a daughter could have let this whole rotten business pass him by. A man without a daughter could have kept his eyes on the water and his mouth shut and let the heiress and the engineer and the nephew all sail off into whatever ruin Halvard had planned for them, and slept soundly after. But Brannoc had a daughter, and she was eleven hundred miles downriver in a boarding school whose fees were paid by a man who never let Brannoc forget it, and so Brannoc stood at the wheel of the *Kestrel's Daughter* — he'd named her for Maren, God help him, named the boat after the debt — and did the arithmetic he'd been doing for six years now, and got the same answer he always got.

The answer was: you don't get to be clean. Not yet. Maybe not ever.

* * *

Six years ago, in the spring of the bad floods, Brannoc had run cargo for Renn Halvard, and the cargo had been guns.

That wasn't how it started, of course. It never started with the thing itself. It started with a sick wife and a boat with a cracked hull and a river authority that wanted three months' fees paid by Friday or they'd break up the *Kestrel's Daughter* for salvage. It started with a smooth-voiced broker in a white suit who met him at a fuel dock and said, *Captain Brannoc, I hear you'll take freight other men won't.* And Brannoc, who was desperate but not stupid, had asked what the freight was, and the broker had smiled and said agricultural machinery, and Brannoc had looked at the crates — long, heavy, oil-papered, stenciled with a farm cooperative's name in letters too fresh to be honest — and he had known.

He took the job anyway. He told himself it was one run. One run, pay off the fees, patch the hull, and back to honest work. He even believed it, right up until the second crate shifted in the heavy water above Sorrel Bend and split along its seam, and he saw the dull blue steel inside, and understood that the farm cooperative stencil had been painted over another stencil, and that the other stencil read CORVANE SALVAGE FLEET, DIVISION SEVEN.

Halvard's own mark. Halvard didn't just buy the guns; he moved them himself, through his own boats, on his own river, and he used small captains with cracked hulls and sick wives to do it, because small captains were cheap and deniable and could be replaced.

Brannoc finished the run. What else was there? You couldn't sink the cargo — the crates were sealed and weighted, they'd have gone down intact and someone would have come asking. You couldn't report it — report to whom? The river authority took Halvard's money at every level from dockmaster upward; Brannoc had learned that later, the hard way, when he tried. So he finished the run, and collected his pay, and told himself he was out.

And then, eight months later, the bridge at Corvane Crossing came down, and forty-one people went into the river, and the inquiry began, and a railroad engineer named Tamsin Marchetti — a name Brannoc hadn't known then, wouldn't know for years — became the most hated man on the escarpment.

And Renn Halvard sent a man to Brannoc's mooring with an envelope, and inside the envelope was a photograph of the manifest from that gun run, in Brannoc's own handwriting, signed for, dated — and beneath it, a note in a hand like engraved silver:

*Captain. Accidents happen to bridges. They also happen to daughters. Your account remains open.*

* * *

"Brannoc."

Sefa's voice, soft behind him. He hadn't heard her come up. The old woman moved like smoke when she wanted to, and she usually wanted to around him, because she watched him the way she watched the water — reading moods, waiting for the change.

"The tender," she said. Not a question. She'd seen it too, then, or felt it. "You know that boat."

"I know the make."

"You know more than the make." She came and stood beside him at the wheel, small and brown and dry as a river stone, her eyes on the black hull sliding closer through the dusk. "I've been on this river sixty years, captain. I know what fear smells like on a man. Yours has been getting stronger since the weir. And now this." She clicked her tongue against her teeth. "That is Halvard's shadow-boat. The *Vane*. Everybody upriver knows the *Vane*. Nobody says so loud."

"Sefa—"

"I'm not asking." She folded her arms. "I'm telling you what I see. There's a storm coming on that man's face, and it isn't weather." She looked up at him, and her eyes were old and kind and absolutely unfooled. "Whatever you owe, captain, think on this: the river keeps accounts too. And the river's interest runs higher than any moneylender's."

She went below. Brannoc stood alone with the wheel and the dusk and the black hull coming on, and the taste of the old envelope in his mouth, paper and wax and shame.

* * *

Here was the thing about guilt that nobody told you: it didn't weigh on you. It steered you.

For six years Brannoc had told himself he was a free man who happened to owe a debt. But standing at the wheel tonight, watching the *Vane* close the distance with the patience of something that had never once been refused, he finally understood the truth of it. He wasn't a free man with a debt. He was a rudder in Halvard's hand. Every heading he'd chosen since the envelope — every cargo, every contract, every careful refusal of work that might draw attention — had bent, however slightly, around the shape of what Halvard wanted. He'd taken Vantrease's survey contract in the first place half because the money was good and half because a job far upriver, away from Halvard's main channels, had seemed like distance.

It wasn't distance. It had never been distance. The *Vane* had found them in four days, in a narrowing gorge, at dusk, running dark. That wasn't luck. That was a man who had always known exactly where his rudder was pointing.

And now the *Vane* was close enough that Brannoc could see figures at her rail, and one of them raised a lamp — two flashes, pause, one flash — and Brannoc's stomach dropped through the bottom of the boat, because he knew that signal. Everyone who'd ever run Division Seven freight knew that signal. *Report your heading.*

He could ignore it. He thought about ignoring it. He thought about it honestly, the way he tried to do one honest thing per day, some small private rebellion — he'd give up drink for a month, or return a stolen tool, or say his prayers twice — and tonight the honest thing was going to be ignoring the lamp. Let them wonder. Let them chase. Let the heiress's expedition vanish upriver into the monsoon with its secrets, and let Brannoc stand on the far side of it having finally, finally done one clean thing.

Then he thought about Maren's face in the last letter. Not the words — the words were cheerful, schoolgirl stuff, exams and a friend named Issa and a request for hair ribbons the color of river glass. The face. The particular way she smiled in the photograph she'd tucked inside, gap-toothed still, one front tooth newly come in crooked, and how she'd written across the bottom in pencil: *Papa, when you come for midsummer will we go on the boat? I want to see where you work.*

Where Papa works. Where Papa runs guns for the man who broke a bridge, and pays for your school with blood money, and smiles at you across a table twice a year with murder coiled behind his teeth.

Two flashes. Pause. One flash. The lamp waited.

Brannoc's hands made the decision the rest of him couldn't. They were old hands, river hands, scarred and capable, and they had betrayed him before — they'd signed for the crates, they'd taken the envelope — and they did it again now, smoothly, without trembling, which was somehow the worst part. He lifted his own lamp. Two flashes. Pause. One flash. Then, in the old Division Seven code, three short pulses and a long: the heading. Upstream. The narrows past the drowned viaduct, toward the high gorges, toward — though Brannoc didn't know it yet, only guessed it, only felt it in the water the way Sefa felt weather — toward whatever it was Halvard's expedition had found and Halvard's hunters had silenced.

The lamp on the *Vane* blinked acknowledgment. Once. Then it went dark, and the black hull swung in a wide, unhurried arc, and stood away downstream into the dusk, and was gone.

Brannoc set his lamp down. His hands were steady. That was the horror of it — they were perfectly steady, hands that had done this before, hands that knew the shape of the sin so well they could perform it without waking the rest of him.

Below, faintly, he could hear Sefa murmuring to the river. Asking permission for tomorrow.

*Don't bother,* he thought. *I've already spent it.*

* * *

He didn't sleep. He sat in the pilothouse with a cold cup of tea going bitter beside him and let the memories run the gorge, rapid after rapid, and each one ended at the same pool: the envelope, the photograph, the note in the engraved hand. *Your account remains open.*

People said debt like it was a number. Brannoc had learned it was a door. Halvard never came through the door — that was his genius, his terrible patience. He just stood on the far side of it, visible through the crack, forever, so that every time Brannoc reached for anything — a job, a plan, a hope — he first checked whether it would fit through the doorway Halvard was standing in. Six years of checking. Six years of shaping his life around a man he hated, until the hating and the shaping were the same motion, until he couldn't tell anymore where the debt ended and Brannoc Lockridge — no. Brannoc Vael. Even his name on this river was a forgery, bought the same year as the envelope, one more thing Halvard knew and Brannoc pretended he didn't.

Near midnight, footsteps on the deck. Tamsin Marchetti, wrapped in an oilskin, face pale in the starlight, climbing to the pilothouse because the engineer apparently slept as badly as the pilot did.

"Couldn't rest either?" Tamsin asked.

"River's noisy tonight."

Tamsin leaned on the rail beside him and looked downstream, toward where the *Vane* had gone. For a long moment neither of them spoke. Then the engineer said, quietly, "A tender passed us at dusk. Black hull. Running dark. I counted her engine beats — twelve to the revolution, geared low. That's fleet plant. Salvage fleet."

"Could be anyone's."

"It could be anyone's," Tamsin agreed, in a voice that meant it couldn't be anyone's. He turned and looked at Brannoc, and his eyes in the dark were tired and sharp and missed nothing, and Brannoc felt the old exposure crawl up his neck like river damp. "You went quiet when she passed. I was watching the water, but I heard the engine drop. You cut power before she rounded the bend. Before you could have seen her." A pause. "How did you know she was there, Captain?"

The question hung between them. Brannoc looked at his hands on the wheel — the steady, capable, guilty hands — and for one wild moment he considered telling the truth. All of it. The crates, the envelope, the lamp code, the daughter. Dump the whole rotten cargo into the river between them and let it sink or swim.

But Maren's face was in the way. Maren's crooked tooth. And so Brannoc did what he had gotten very good at doing: he lied with a riverman's plainness, the kind of lie that sounds like honesty because it's delivered flat and tired.

"Sixty years on this water, engineer. You learn the sound of a hull that doesn't belong. She was riding heavy and she was fighting the cross-current at the bend — no light boat does that. I cut power to listen better." He shrugged. "That's all it was."

Tamsin held his gaze a moment longer. Then he nodded slowly, and Brannoc understood that the nod was not belief. It was filing. The engineer was putting the moment away somewhere, in one of those precise compartments of his, to be taken out and reexamined later.

"Get some sleep if you can," Tamsin said, moving to the ladder. "Tomorrow we make the narrows past the drowned viaduct. After that the gorges start closing in."

"Aye," Brannoc said. "They do that."

When the engineer was gone, Brannoc stood alone in the pilothouse and watched the stars wheel over the black gorge walls, and did the arithmetic one more time, and got the same answer.

The *Vane* knew their heading now. Whatever came of this voyage — whatever burned camp or sunken wreck or buried truth lay waiting upriver in the closing gorges — a part of it would land on him. He had lit the lamp. He had given the heading. And the worst of it, the part that would keep him awake until the monsoon took the sky apart, was that some cold practical corner of him had already begun calculating whether the betrayal would be enough — whether one heading, one signal, would settle the account, or whether Halvard would come back for more, the way he always came back for more, standing patiently behind his door while Brannoc's life squeezed itself thinner and thinner trying to fit through.

Dawn came gray and slow. Below, he heard the crew stirring: Sefa's murmur, Callum's petulant cough, Vantrease's clipped orders, Tamsin's quiet voice laying out the day's water. A crew of strangers becoming, against everyone's intentions, something else. Brannoc washed his face in riverwater that felt like a judgment, took up the wheel, and turned the boat's bow upstream — toward the narrows, toward the drowned viaduct, toward the heading he had sold.

By noon they were in the gorges proper, walls rising sheer on either side, the river running green and deep and held-back, and Sefa stopped suddenly amidships, head cocked, listening to water that wasn't behaving.

"Captain," she called up to the pilothouse, and there was something in her voice Brannoc had never heard in sixty years on the Kessarin. Something like fear. "There's smoke ahead. On the left bank. Old smoke — a day, maybe two." Her eyes found his. "Somebody's camp burned up here. And somebody's still in it."
