## 8. Dead Air

Callum Vantrease had never been good at silence. He'd grown up in a house where silence meant someone was about to be disappointed in you — his mother's quiet at the dinner table, his uncle's quiet over ledgers, the particular hush that settled over Vantrease House whenever his father's name came up, which was rarely and then too often. So he filled silences. He talked to Sefa about the river's moods until she told him, not unkindly, that a man who talked that much was listening to himself instead of the water. He asked Brannoc questions about the gorge walls until the old pilot grunted and said rock didn't answer questions. He even tried conversation with Tamsin Marchetti, who had a way of looking through you at some middle distance where answers lived, and who gave back exactly as much words as politeness required and not one syllable more.

But on the fifth morning above the Kessarin's third bend, nobody talked at all.

The river had changed overnight. Callum noticed it first as a wrongness in the sound — the Kessarin had been talking to them for days, a low continuous mutter against the hull, and now it spoke in a different register, flatter, wider, like a voice trying to seem calm. The water spread out. It lost its green urgency and went the color of pewter, sliding slow between banks that leaned back to give it room, and the mountains on either side stopped being walls and became spectators. Somewhere ahead, Sefa said without opening her eyes, the river would narrow again. But here, in this slack water, everything felt held. Suspended. Like the whole valley was taking a breath before saying something terrible.

Brannoc brought them around the bend under oars alone, no engine, and Callum understood why before anyone said it. Smoke. Not cooking smoke — cooking smoke smelled of fat and resin and intention. This was the smell of things already finished burning, wet char and cold ash, drifting down through the morning mist in threads so thin they might have been imagination.

"There," Sefa whispered, and pointed with her chin.

The mast stood on a shelf of gravel at the river's edge, or what remained of it. A radio mast, guyed with wire, forty feet of galvanized steel — and it had been burned from the top down, the way lightning kills a tree, except there had been no storm. The upper third was blackened and warped into a question mark. The guys had been cut, not snapped; Callum could see the clean ends curling back from the anchors like burnt hair. Someone had climbed up or pulled it down deliberately, doused it, and set it alight so that any rescue party scanning the valley would find nothing but a dead pole and think: accident. Lightning. Bad luck.

"Nine days silent," Tamsin said quietly. He stood at the bow with one hand on the painter, and Callum watched him read the shore the way he read everything — systematically, reluctantly, like a man forced to look at something he'd hoped never to see again. "That mast burned three, four days ago. Rain's kept the ash wet. They wanted it dead, but not gone."

"They?" Odile's voice came from the stern, sharp as a cleat. She hadn't slept; Callum knew the family look she wore, the Vantrease jaw set against exhaustion like a flood gate set against spring melt. "Who is *they*, Mr. Marchetti?"

"I don't know yet." He didn't look back at her. "But I know what I'm looking at."

* * *

They beached below the camp because Brannoc refused to beach in front of it. "You don't walk into a room through the door when you don't know who's behind it," he said, and took them up through the trees instead, a steep scramble through ferns slick as wet hair, until they looked down on the expedition's base camp from above.

It had been a proper camp once. Callum could see the ghost of it — the rectangular scar of a mess tent, the ring of stones around a fire pit, tent platforms raised on pilings against the damp, a cache rack built high off the ground against animals. His aunt's money was written all over it: good canvas, good rope, equipment chosen by people who believed that competence was a kind of armor. Eleven people had come upriver in Odile's name. The manifest of personnel was folded in Callum's breast pocket, and he had read it so many times the creases had gone soft. Surveyor, two engineers, medic, four boatmen, camp keeper, radio operator, expedition lead. Names. He'd made himself learn the names on the second night, when the frictions aboard were still small enough to be funny, because it seemed like the decent thing — these were people whose salaries his family's money paid, and a man ought to know the names of the people his name was attached to.

Now he counted the tents still standing. Two. Both collapsed inward, cut from their guylines.

"No bodies," Sefa murmured beside him. She had her knife out, which Callum had learned meant she was frightened, though her voice never showed it. "No blood. No scavenger sign."

"They walked out," Tamsin said. "Or were made to."

They went down into the camp the way you enter a church where something has happened — slowly, quietly, each of them drifting to a different corner of the ruin as if drawn by separate currents. Brannoc went to the boats' landing and studied the mud. Sefa circled the fire pit, reading ashes. Odile stood in the center of the mess area with her hands clasped behind her back, very straight, very still, the way she stood when feeling anything would have been an unbearable expense. And Callum — Callum found the gear cache.

It had been opened, not broken. The latch was intact. Whoever opened it had known how, had taken their time, had left the lid propped with a stick as if meaning to return. Inside, the stores lay in careful disorder: rope gone, medical kit gone, the good lamps gone — but the tinned food untouched, the spare parts untouched, a crate of survey instruments sitting in its straw like eggs in a nest. Callum lifted out a theodolite and turned it over. Brass, oiled, immaculate. Worth more than the boat they'd come in on.

"They took the medicine and the rope," he heard himself say, "and left the money."

Tamsin crossed to him and looked into the crate for a long moment. "Rope and medicine keep you alive in the bush," he said. "Money doesn't." He crouched, ran his thumb along the cache frame, and came up with a sliver of wood, fresh-cut, pale as bone. "This was pried and re-nailed. Days ago. They weren't looting, Callum. They were provisioning. Somebody outfitted themselves from this camp and moved on — or moved somebody."

"Moved them where?"

"That," Tamsin said, standing, "is the question I've been afraid of since the bend."

* * *

It was Sefa who found the survivor.

She gave a short cry — the only time Callum ever heard her raise her voice without meaning to — and they all came running to the far tent, the one half-fallen against its cut guylines, and Sefa was kneeling in the doorway with both hands pressed over her mouth.

A woman lay inside on a stripped cot frame, and for one obscene moment Callum thought she was dead, because she was so still and so gray and the flies had already found her. Then her chest moved. A shallow flutter, barely lifting the sweat-soaked shirt, but moving.

She was maybe fifty, hollowed down to architecture, cheekbones like tent poles under skin the color of old paper. Her lips were cracked and dark. Her wrists, when Tamsin turned them gently toward the light, wore the healing ridges of rope burns — deep ones, the kind that come from being bound hard and long.

"She's been tied," Tamsin said, and his voice did something Callum had never heard it do — dropped all its engineering flatness and went rough. "Days ago. Long enough to heal wrong."

Odile pushed past them all into the tent. For a moment the imperious salvage heiress simply looked down at the wreck of the woman she'd sent upriver, and Callum saw his aunt's face do something he had never seen it do in twenty-six years of knowing it: crack. Just at the edges. Just for a heartbeat. Then she knelt, took the woman's hand, and said, in a voice stripped of every ounce of command, "Maren. Maren Ostrow. It's Odile Vantrease. Can you hear me?"

The woman's eyes opened. They were fever-bright and fixed on nothing, on some middle distance worse than anything in the tent, and her mouth worked. Sound came out in shreds.

"...told them... we'd seen nothing... told them..."

"You're safe," Odile said, which was a lie, and everyone in the tent knew it was a lie, and everyone let her say it anyway.

Maren Ostrow's hand closed on Odile's wrist with a strength that seemed impossible, dragged from somewhere below the fever, below the hunger, some cellar of the self where a person keeps the one thing they cannot drop. Her eyes rolled, found Odile's face, and focused — focused with terrible effort, the way a dying lamp focuses before it gutters.

"The manifest," she whispered. "Don't let them... it's not cargo. It was never cargo. The Corvane wasn't carrying what they said she was carrying. The papers—"

Her voice tore apart into coughing, wet and awful. Sefa was already moving, cutting the woman's shirt sleeve, finding the IV line they'd need, the fever chart Tamsin would start that night. Brannoc appeared in the doorway with the stretcher canvas. The machinery of care assembled itself around the survivor with the wordless efficiency of people who had been strangers nine days ago.

But Callum stayed kneeling where he was, and the word went through him like a fishhook.

*Manifest.*

He knew that word. Not from the expedition's paperwork — from older paper, from the locked drawer in his father's study that Callum had been forbidden to open at fourteen and had opened at fifteen, because that was the kind of boy he'd been and probably still was. Shipping manifests. Salvage manifests. The Corvane, listed among his father's early acquisitions, lost with all hands the year Callum was born, a name spoken in that house only in the tone reserved for things that had cost the family something and were never discussed.

And here was a woman who had starved and burned and been bound hand and foot to keep a secret about that same ship, whispering its name into the gray air of a murdered camp.

He looked up and found Tamsin watching him. Not the middle-distance look. A direct one, sharp and sorry at once, as if Tamsin had already done the arithmetic Callum was only beginning to face — as if he'd seen the family resemblance in the word itself.

Outside, the light was failing. Downriver, faint as a thought, came the sound Callum had been dreading all day and couldn't have named until now: engines. More than one. Deep-hulled, heavy, patient, coming up the pewter water at the exact speed of men who owned the river and knew it.

Sefa rose from the survivor's side, knife still in her hand, and looked at Brannoc.

"That's not a rescue," she said.

No one answered, because no one needed to. The camp around them — the cut wires, the burned mast, the re-nailed cache, the rope scars on a dying woman's wrists — answered for itself. The expedition hadn't been lost. It had been hunted, methodically, by professionals, and the hunters had left one witness alive either by mercy or by design, and either way she was whispering the one word that could burn down everything his family had built.

Callum put his hand in his pocket and felt the soft-creased list of eleven names, and understood, with a horror that was already curdling into something harder, that at least ten of them had been taken somewhere upriver — and that whatever waited there had been waiting for the Corvane's secret for thirty years, and had just heard them coming.
