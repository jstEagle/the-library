## 9. The Fever Chart

The survivor's name was Ilsa Vey, though she did not know it yet. For the first day she was no one at all — a bundle of salt-stiff canvas and burned skin they had carried down from the dead camp on a litter of paddles and rope, her lips cracked to gray, her hands opening and closing around something that wasn't there. Brannoc had wanted to push on. Odile had stood over the woman with her jaw set like a bulkhead seam and said, *We take her,* in the voice that ended arguments before they began. So they took her.

Now she lay in the stern under an awning of oiled canvas, in the boat's one patch of shade, and Tamsin sat beside her because he had appointed himself to it without asking anyone's permission. It was the kind of appointment a man makes when he cannot bear his own thoughts. The river ran wide here, brown-green and slow, breathing heat off its surface like a kiln door left open. Somewhere upstream, past the second bend, the gorge walls would begin again. He tried not to think about what waited between them and it.

Sefa brought him water boiled with willow bark and a strip of dried fish he had no appetite for. She looked at Ilsa Vey the way she looked at the river — reading, weighing, finding the current beneath the surface.

"Fever has a shape," Sefa said. "Like water does. You watch where it goes."

"Where is this one going?"

Sefa crouched, touched two fingers to the woman's wrist, and held them there a long moment. "Downhill," she said. "But not fast. Rivers give you time if you know how to ask." She pressed the fish into his hand anyway and went back to the bow, and Tamsin ate it because refusing kindness aboard this boat had begun to feel like a kind of theft.

* * *

Fever has a grammar. That was the thing nobody told you. A burning mind doesn't speak in sentences; it speaks in fragments, in the broken syntax of a chart torn into pieces and scattered on the wind. Tamsin had learned this years ago, in a charity ward in Corvane City, sitting three nights beside a brakeman who'd been pulled from the wreckage of the Marchetti inquiry's only surviving car. The man had talked in his sleep for two days, and Tamsin had written down every word, and in the end the words had meant nothing — or rather, they had meant everything, and no one with power had wanted them to mean anything, and so they were filed away as delirium and the man died on the fourth night with his secret intact inside him.

Tamsin had sworn he wouldn't do that again. Sit beside a burning stranger with a pencil and paper, mining a sick woman's dreams.

He broke the oath by noon of the first day.

It started small. Ilsa Vey surfaced once, eyes half-open, unfocused, fixed on the canopy above her as though it were a ceiling in some other life. Her mouth moved. Tamsin leaned close.

"...third span," she whispered. "Third span wasn't rated. Nobody checked the third span."

Then she was gone again, down into the brown water of the fever, and Tamsin sat very still with his heart going like a loose coupling.

Because there had been no third span. Not in the official record. The Kessarin Junction bridge — the one that came down ten years ago with forty-one people on the morning train — had been reported as a single-span failure, a pier scour, an act of the river. He knew the report by heart. He had read it so many times the pages lived behind his eyelids: *structural inadequacy attributable to undersized pier footing, responsibility assignable to chief surveying engineer.* To him. To the name he no longer used.

*Third span wasn't rated.*

His hands were steady. Ten years of practice had taught them to be steady. But underneath the steadiness something shifted, the way a hillside shifts before it decides whether to be a landslide or just a scar.

* * *

By evening she spoke again, and this time Sefa heard it too, and after that neither of them pretended the vigil was anything but what it was.

The monsoon's outer clouds had begun stacking up along the western escarpment all afternoon, enormous and blue-black, lit from within by lightning that never reached the ground. The air tasted of iron and green rot. Brannoc kept the boat tied to a stand of flooded gum trees, and Callum paced the gunwale like a cat in a sack, and Odile sat apart with her maps, and none of them said aloud what they were all counting: four tides left. Maybe five, if the river lied to them kindly.

Ilsa Vey's fever broke its banks around midnight.

She thrashed. Sefa held her shoulders; Tamsin held her wrists, gentle, saying nothing, just being a solid thing in a dissolving world, which was sometimes all nursing was. And through the thrashing the words came — not whispers now but full voice, a woman giving testimony to a courtroom only she could see:

"—it's *there*, do you understand me, it's been there the whole time, the river put it to bed like a child—"

"The hull's intact," Tamsin murmured, not knowing why, following some instinct older than caution. "Tell me about the hull."

Her eyes found his. Fever-bright, black in the dark, and for one uncanny moment she was entirely present, entirely lucid, looking at him as if she knew exactly who he was and had crossed a country to find him.

"You're the engineer," she said.

The river made its small sounds against the hull. Sefa's grip tightened on the woman's shoulders.

"I'm nobody," Tamsin said.

"They said you'd come." Her voice cracked, dropped back toward delirium, climbed out again. "Vantrease's people said — someone would come — someone who could read it—" Her hand shot up and caught his collar with shocking strength. "The strongroom. Forward hold, below the waterline, the door's a pressure seal, we couldn't — we didn't have time — Halvard's men came at dawn, they came with *fire*, Marchetti, they burned the mast and they shot Doss into the water and they were looking for us, they were looking for anyone who'd seen the manifests—"

And then she was gone, sagging, breath rattling, and Tamsin knelt in the stern of a boat on the Kessarin River with his own name hanging in the air between himself and two witnesses, and the world tilted on its axis with a groan like stressed steel.

*Marchetti.* She had called him Marchetti. Not the false name on his forged credentials — not Tam Reeve, journeyman surveyor, a man assembled out of paperwork and wishful thinking. *Marchetti.* The name on the inquiry transcripts. The name nailed to the collapse. The name he had buried in three provinces and two new alphabets of signature.

"How," he said. His voice came out sanded flat. "How does she know that name."

Sefa released the woman's shoulders slowly. In the dark her face was unreadable, but her voice, when it came, was quiet and certain as a depth-sounding. "The river tells things," she said. "People tell things faster." She paused. "Brannoc reads everything Odile's papers carry. Your credential traveled with a description. A man who runs from his name carries it written on him somewhere. Anyone who looks long enough finds it."

Tamsin looked down at Ilsa Vey. Her breathing had steadied — the crisis passing, or pausing, the way storms pause. Sweat stood on her forehead like dew. Whatever current the fever had been riding, it had carried her to this bank: the truth, spoken aloud, witnessed.

He should deny it. Every instinct honed across ten years of running said deny it, deflect it, let the false name close over the true one like water over a stone. That was the discipline that had kept him alive. That was the whole architecture of his survival.

But she had said *the manifests*. She had said *Halvard's men came with fire*. And underneath those words, waiting like a charge set in rock, was the other thing — the thing he had heard her say at noon, the third span, unrated, unchecked — and Tamsin Marchetti, who had spent a decade being told the ruin of his life was his own workmanship, felt the first hairline crack run through the wall of that sentence.

"Get me the chart case," he said to Sefa. "And the lamp. And don't wake the others. Not yet."

* * *

He worked through the small hours the way he used to work in the drawing sheds of his apprenticeship: lamp low, paper squared, mind narrowed to a point. On a fresh sheet he built a timeline, because timelines were the one honest language he trusted. Dates on the left. Events on the right. Sources marked — her words, his memory, the inquiry record he could recite like scripture.

Ten years ago, spring: Kessarin Junction bridge fails, morning train, forty-one dead. Official cause: pier scour, structural inadequacy. Chief surveying engineer censured. That was him; that was the wound he'd been bleeding from ever since.

But Ilsa Vey, twice now, had spoken of a third span. So he drew the bridge as it actually stood — he could still draw it from memory, every member, every plate, because a man never forgets the structure that killed his life — and he marked the third span, and he sat looking at it.

A single-span failure did not explain the damage pattern. He had known that even then, young and stunned and guilty, standing in the wreckage with his instruments. The downstream truss had failed *first* — he'd seen it in the twist of the steel, the direction of the fall. He had argued it, in his one appearance before the inquiry, and been told that grief made engineers see patterns, and his own report — the report bearing his signature — had contradicted him. A report he had never written. A report whose figures he did not recognize. He had signed it — of course he had signed it; the firm put it in front of him and the chairman smiled and the ink was dry before he understood — and that signature had been the noose. *We all sign papers we don't read*, Brannoc would say later, but that was later, and tonight there was only the lamp and the page and the cold spreading through him like water finding a bilge.

If the third span had been compromised — cut, undercut, its connections loosened in advance — then the failure wasn't scour. It was demolition wearing scour's clothing. And a demolition needs a reason, and a reason needs a beneficiary, and Tamsin wrote the next line with a hand that had finally stopped pretending to be steady:

*Who profited?*

The answer rose out of the dark of the decade like a hull rising out of a riverbed.

Corvane Salvage & Recovery. The company that had won the reconstruction contracts. The company whose tender assessments he had watched, numb, from the public gallery of the inquiry — the lowest bid, the fastest schedule, the chairman Renn Halvard testifying in a gray suit with a voice like poured concrete: *a tragedy, an anomaly, the river is a dangerous partner.* The company that had bought the wreck rights, stripped the fallen spans for scrap within the month, and sealed the site before any independent engineer could walk it.

He had known all of that. He had known it for ten years the way you know a stone in your boot — present, painful, unprovable. What he had never had was the mechanism. The *how*. The document that turned suspicion into fact.

And now a fevered woman lay three feet from him who had seen the manifests. Who had stood inside the Corvane herself — the salvage fleet's lost flagship, the ghost ship of the upper Kessarin, sunk or scuttled or simply *disappeared* twenty years ago and written off in Halvard's ledgers as a total loss. Written off. With a strongroom forward, below the waterline, sealed.

You didn't seal a strongroom on a ship you'd truly abandoned. You sealed a strongroom when it held something you could not afford to have found.

Tamsin put down the pencil. The lamp flame stood perfectly still in the windless heat, and outside, far upriver, thunder rolled along the escarpment like freight cars coupling in the dark.

* * *

Dawn came gray and thick, the sky lower than it had any right to be. Ilsa Vey slept a truer sleep, her fever down, her breathing even. Tamsin folded his timeline into his breast pocket, next to his skin, where the important papers lived.

Odile was awake at the bow, watching the water rise against the gauge stick Sefa had notched. Three inches overnight. The river was filling its lungs.

She glanced at him, then at the sleeping woman, then back. Something in his face must have shown, because she said, quietly, without her usual armor of command: "Well?"

"She knows where the wreck is. She's been inside it. There's a strongroom with the original manifests — shipping records, cargo declarations, twenty years of Halvard's paper trail." He paused. "And she called me Marchetti."

Odile went very still. He watched her decide, visibly, not to pretend surprise. "My uncle's people described you," she said. "Before I hired you. I chose to believe the description was wrong." A beat. "I'm done choosing that."

"Then choose this instead." He took out the folded timeline and opened it on the thwart between them, weighting the corners with two brass fittings. "Look at the third span. Look at the dates. Halvard's company held the reconstruction contract before the dust settled — bids closed nine days after the collapse, Odile. Nine days. You don't price a job that fast unless you knew it was coming."

The clouds grumbled. Down in the stern, Ilsa Vey stirred and murmured something neither of them caught, and both of them turned toward her as if pulled on the same line.

"And the manifests," Odile said. Her voice had changed — lower, hungrier, and afraid of its own hunger. "On the Corvane. They'd show the tender dates. The insurance filings. Whether the inspection reports were falsified before the collapse or—" She stopped. He watched her arrive at the rest of the sentence and refuse to say it, and understood that whatever her father's partnership with Halvard had been, she had spent years not saying exactly this.

"Whether the whole thing was scheduled," Tamsin finished for her. "Demolition dressed as disaster. Forty-one people. My name on the invoice."

The river pushed at the mooring lines. Upstream, past the bend, the gorges waited, and somewhere in them a black-hulled tender was moving with the patience of money.

From the stern, weak as thread but clear, Ilsa Vey spoke without opening her eyes:

"He'll open the weir before you reach it. He holds the water — he always held the water. Watch the tides. He'll spend the river before he lets you spend the truth."

Tamsin and Odile looked at each other across the chart, and the first rain of the monsoon's outer bands came ticking onto the canvas above them like a countdown beginning.
