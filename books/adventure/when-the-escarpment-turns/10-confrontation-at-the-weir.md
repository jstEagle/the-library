## 10. Confrontation at the Weir

Odile Vantrease had spent her life learning to read men the way her father read ledgers — for the small discrepancies, the entries that didn't balance, the number written in a hand too careful to be honest. She had thought herself good at it. She had thought, in the particular arrogance of women who inherit their fathers' money without their fathers' guilt, that she could always see the lie coming.

She had not seen Brannoc's.

It was Sefa who saw it first, which was the humiliation beneath the humiliation. The deckhand had come to her at dusk on the fourth day out from Dead Air camp, while the Kessarin ran brown and swollen below the rail and the mountains on either side had begun to close like a book. Sefa did not knock at the cabin door; she never knocked anywhere, having been raised on water where doors were a drowning hazard. She simply stood in the lamplight with river spray in her hair and said, in her flat island accent:

"The pilot signaled the black ship."

Odile had been annotating charts. Her pen did not stop moving, because stopping would have been an admission that the words had landed. "Explain."

"Two nights past. When we lay up below the split rock." Sefa's hands moved as she spoke, shaping the memory in the air — Odile had noticed this about her, that she spoke with her whole body, as though words were cargo to be handled carefully or they'd spill. "He hung a lamp off the stern davit. Green glass over it. Three flashes, then two. Then he took it down and scrubbed the soot off the glass so no one would find it."

"You watched him do this."

"I watch everything on my boat." A pause, and something almost apologetic crossed the girl's broad face. "Your boat. I watch everything."

"Three and two," Odile said. "That's a heading signal. Old coastal code."

Sefa nodded once. "Halvard's fleet uses it. My uncle ran fish out of Vell Port. He said you could always tell Halvard's boats by how they talked to each other in the dark — quick, cheap signals, like they were afraid the sea would charge them by the word."

The pen had stopped then. Odile set it down in its groove with the precision she used for everything, because precision was the only inheritance she'd ever been permitted to display openly, and looked at the chart she'd been marking. The route ahead. The gorge country. And somewhere in it, a weir holding back a river's worth of water, waiting.

"Say nothing," she told Sefa. "To anyone. Not yet."

"I've said nothing for two days," Sefa said. "I'm saying it now because tomorrow we enter the narrows, and if he means to sell us there, I'd rather know whose side the wheel is turning before the rocks do."

* * *

She waited until the next layup, because confrontation was a thing her father had taught her should be done on your own ground, at your own hour, with witnesses chosen rather than stumbled upon. The site she chose was the old weir itself — or the ghost of one, a stone training wall jutting from the shallows where the river narrowed before the gorge proper, built by some railway company fifty years dead to hold back spring floods. They moored against it in the last grey light, and Odile stood on the stones with the water talking around her ankles' height below, and called Brannoc down from the wheelhouse with a courtesy that cost her more than shouting would have.

He came heavy-footed along the deck, rolling with the boat's motion even standing still, a man shaped by thirty years of decks until land itself must have felt like a lie. His beard was going iron at the chin. In the lantern light his eyes were wary in the way of animals that have been trapped before and remember every detail of the trap.

"Ma'am."

"Mister Brannoc." She let the silence work. Her father had believed silence was a tool that only worked on guilty men, and she had learned since boarding this boat that her father had been wrong about nearly everything that mattered. "Three flashes, then two. Green glass. Two nights ago, below the split rock."

She watched it hit him. It went through him like a bullet through water — no splash, just a pressure wave, a stillness that spread outward from the center of him. His big hands, hanging at his sides, curled slowly into fists and then, deliberately, uncurled again.

"That's not what you think it is."

"No," Odile agreed pleasantly. "It's worse than what I think it is. What I think is that you sold our heading to Renn Halvard. What it actually is, I suspect, involves a daughter."

The sound Brannoc made was not quite a word. He came a step closer onto the stones, and for a moment she thought — genuinely, coldly thought — that he might put those hands on her, and she found she didn't care, which frightened her more than the hands did.

"You don't know anything about my daughter."

"I know she exists. I know Halvard knows she exists. I know that men like Halvard keep other men's daughters the way moneylenders keep promissory notes." Odile lifted her chin. The wind off the narrowing river pulled at her hair and she let it, because composure was easier to maintain when part of you was visibly at the mercy of something larger. "Did you imagine I hired a pilot with debts and didn't learn what they were? I bought this expedition the way I buy everything, Mister Brannoc. Completely. Including its weaknesses."

"And what did you buy *me* for?" His voice cracked across the stones, and above them on the deck rail two heads appeared — Callum's pale face, Sefa's dark one — because of course the whole boat had heard, of course they had, boats were made of wood and wood carried everything. "You want the truth, madam? Here's your truth, since you're so fond of buying it. I ran cargo for Halvard six years. Contraband, mostly — salvage goods stripped off wrecks he had no rights to, run through channels he'd bribed shut. Good money. Then the river authority started closing in and Halvard needed someone to take a fall, and I owed him too much to refuse, so I took the fall, and the price of taking it was that my girl went into his keeping. Not his custody. His *keeping*. There's a difference, and the difference is that custody ends."

The river filled the pause, talking its endless brown talk against the stones.

"So yes," Brannoc said, quieter now, and somehow that was worse. "I flashed his code. Heading and speed, that's all, nothing about the wreck, nothing about your survivor, nothing about Marchetti. Because every night I don't signal, he sends word to his people in Vell Port, and every night he sends word, my daughter eats supper in a room I can't get her out of. You want to call that betrayal, fine. Call it betrayal. But you'll say it knowing I did it for the same reason you're doing all this, whatever it really is — because somebody else holds the thing you can't live without."

"*Brannoc.*"

It was Sefa's voice, from the rail, sharp as a gaff hook. The deckhand swung down onto the stones with the easy disregard for ladders that drove Odile quietly insane, and planted herself between them — not in front of Brannoc exactly, but beside him, shoulder angled toward him, face toward Odile.

"He told you true," Sefa said. "I watched him carry it two nights, and it was eating him alive. A man selling you out doesn't lose sleep over it. A man selling himself does."

"This is not your—"

"On this river it is my business." Sefa's voice didn't rise but it thickened, like water deepening. "You paid for a boat. You didn't pay for the river, and you didn't pay for us. We came aboard strangers and the river's been making us crew ever since, and crew means when one man drowns we all feel the pull. So no, ma'am, it's my business. And I'm telling you — if you put him off this boat, or put him in irons, or make him choose between you and his girl in front of all of us, you'll have a mutiny on your hands, and I'll be standing at the front of it."

Above them, Callum's face withdrew from the rail. Odile noted its absence the way she noted all absences — automatically, and with a suspicion she filed away for later examination, because her nephew had been vanishing from view rather often lately, and vanishing was not a habit she associated with innocence.

"Stand down, Sefa," Brannoc rumbled.

"I'm standing *with*, not down. There's a difference, and the difference is—" she threw his own phrasing back at him with a flash of teeth, "—it ends."

* * *

Later — much later, after the lamps had been trimmed and the watches set and the boat creaked against the old training wall like a animal settling — Odile climbed back down onto the weir stones alone, and found she was not alone. Brannoc sat on a fallen block near the waterline, a tin cup in his hands, not drinking from it. He didn't look up when she came. That, at least, was a kind of honesty.

"You should sleep," he said. "We run the narrows at first light."

"So should you."

"I don't sleep well on stones that know what I've done." He turned the cup in his hands. "Go on, then. Say what you came to say. You've been circling it since sundown, and circling's a rich woman's habit. Out here it just wastes dark."

Odile stood at the edge of the lantern light, and understood that she had arranged this entire evening — the confrontation, the witnesses, the public accounting of Brannoc's sin — for precisely this reason: so that when the moment came, she would have no graceful way to avoid it herself. Her father would have been proud. Her father had taught her that the best interrogations were the ones where the interrogator answered first.

"My father," she said, "was Aldous Vantrease."

"I know who your father was. Half the coast knew."

"Half the coast knew the salvage magnate. Nobody knew the partner." She heard her own voice go flat and precise, the voice she used for invoices. "Twenty years ago there was a shipping concern called Corvane Coastal. It ran freight and salvage out of the upper Kessarin. It failed — publicly, messily, the way concerns fail — and its fleet was scattered and its records lost, and everyone agreed it was bad luck and bad management. My father bought the wreckage cheap. He always bought wreckage cheap. It was his religion."

Brannoc had gone very still.

"What nobody agreed to ask," Odile continued, "was why a failing company needed three strongrooms, or why its last manifest was sealed under court order and then the court order quietly expired and the manifest quietly disappeared, or why my father — who pinched copper out of lamp fittings — paid off four officials in the same month he was supposedly mourning a dead partnership. I grew up watching him be careful in ways careful men are only careful about crimes. And when he died, I went through his papers expecting to find the crime. Instead I found a map. River charts, forty years old, with one reach of the upper Kessarin marked in his own hand. And a note. *If the Corvane's hull held, the proof held.*"

The water talked. Somewhere upstream, faint as a struck bell, ice or rock shifted in the high country.

"So no, Mister Brannoc. This expedition was never about salvage rights. Salvage rights are what I told my lawyers, my insurers, and my nephew, because those are people who require stories. This expedition is about opening my father's ledger and reading the entry he spent twenty years and four bribes making sure no one would read. Whatever the Corvane carries — and the survivor from my first expedition whispered enough before her fever took her voice to tell me it's worth killing for — it isn't treasure. It's testimony. And I am going to read it aloud if it costs me every credit I inherited, because I have finally understood that the inheritance itself is the indictment."

Brannoc looked at her then, fully, for the first time since the confrontation began. Something in his weathered face had rearranged itself — not softness exactly, but recognition, the look of a man finding a familiar current in unfamiliar water.

"Halvard," he said slowly. "You think Halvard was in it. The collapse inquiry, the forged report, all of it — you think it goes back through him to your father."

"I think Halvard was my father's instrument, and instruments outlive their makers and get ideas." She wrapped her arms against the river chill. "And I think that whatever name Tamsin Marchetti is traveling under these days, he was on the receiving end of one of Halvard's ideas, and he knows it, and that is why he flinches every time the fleet's name comes up. But that's his confession to make, not mine. I've learned recently that taking a man's confession by force gets you a forgery."

Brannoc huffed — half laugh, half surrender. He drank from the cup at last, grimacing at whatever was in it.

"We're a fine crew," he said. "A liar with a stolen name at the wheel. A debt-ridden traitor running the engine. A superstitious girl who reads rivers better than any surveyor. And a rich man's nephew who goes pale whenever anyone mentions his uncle-by-marriage's business partners." He shook his head. "Every soul aboard is running from something, ma'am. Only difference between us is you can afford to stop."

"I don't want to afford it," Odile said. "I want to end it. There's a difference, and the difference is—"

"It ends," Brannoc finished, and something passed between them on the wet stones that neither would ever call forgiveness, because both of them knew the word was too clean for what it was. "Then hear me, and hear me plain. Tomorrow we pass the narrows, and Halvard will know our heading whether I signal or not, because I've signaled twice already and a third time buys nothing. So I'm done signaling. From here to the wreck, you have all of me. But when this is done — whatever happens to Halvard, whatever happens to the documents — you use that fortune of yours to get my daughter out of Vell Port. First thing. Before your testimony, before your reckoning. Swear it."

Odile looked at the old man sitting on the stones with his dead cup in his hands, offering her the only currency he had left.

"I swear it," she said. "On my father's ledger."

Something upstream boomed — long, low, wrong, a sound like a door closing underwater. Both of them turned. Far up the black throat of the gorge, where the stars ended, a white line of mist stood above the water where no mist should be, and even at this distance Odile could see it was moving. Growing.

The weir gates. Opening early.

Brannoc was already on his feet, cup forgotten, bellowing up at the dark boat: "All hands! ALL HANDS — flood tide coming down the gorge!"
