## 11. Two Days of Water

The river told us before anyone else did.

I was on deck at first light, checking the lashings on the survey gear, when I felt it through the soles of my boots — a tremor in the hull that had nothing to do with the current. A shudder, faint and rhythmic, like a pulse under the skin of the water. Sefa felt it too. She came up from below without her breakfast, barefoot on wet planking, and stood at the rail with her head tilted the way she did when she listened to the Kessarin the way other people listened to music.

"It's coming down," she said.

"What is?"

"The water." Her voice was flat, which frightened me more than any wobble could have. Sefa's voice only went flat when she was certain. "Two days early. Maybe less. He's opened the gates."

Brannoc came up the companionway behind us, and I watched him do the same arithmetic I was doing — the distance to the narrows above us, the height of the mooring line, the weight of everything we'd lashed to the deck because there was no room below. His jaw worked once, twice, like a man chewing a word he didn't want to swallow.

"Halvard," he said. "He's not waiting for us to turn back anymore. He's going to wash us off the river."

* * *

We had tied up the night before in a fold of the gorge called the Anvil, a basalt overhang where the cliff leaned out far enough to shelter a boat from the worst of the weather, if not from the river itself. It was the last good mooring for eleven miles. Above it the gorge narrowed into the Throat, where the water ran fast even in the dry season and the walls pinched close enough that Brannoc said you could shake hands with both banks at once. Below it lay six miles of open water we'd crossed yesterday without thinking much about it.

We thought about it now.

Odile stood at the rail with her hands gripping the wood, knuckles white, watching the river as though she could argue it lower by will alone. Callum hovered near her with the field ledger clutched to his chest like a shield, and I noticed — filed away without understanding yet — that he hadn't looked at me all morning. Not since the scout ahead. Not since whatever had happened out there that he wouldn't talk about.

"How long?" Odile asked me.

I looked at the water. It had dropped overnight — dropped, which was worse than rising, because a dropping river upstream meant a wall of held water somewhere behind us, gathering its patience. The exposed rock along the bank glistened black and green, a foot of stone that hadn't seen air in weeks now breathing again, and every inch of it was a promise of what was coming down.

"Depends how far upstream the weir is and how wide they open it," I said. "If they dump it all at once, we have hours. If they meter it—" I shook my head. "Either way, this mooring won't hold. The surge will come through here like a train through a tunnel. The Anvil shelters us from rain, not from forty feet of moving river."

"Then we move."

"Above us is the Throat. We can't run rapids ahead of a flood — the flood would be faster than we are, and it would come around the bend and take us from behind, broadside. That's how boats die in gorges." I turned to face her. "We go up. Off the boat, up the cliff path, and we tie everything down and pray the lashings hold. Or we cut loose and ride it."

"Ride it?" Callum's voice cracked on the second syllable. "Ride *what*, exactly? A wall of water through a canyon?"

"A surge wave," I said. "It's not a wall, not really. It's a hump. The river swells, runs maybe three or four times its normal speed for an hour, maybe two, then settles. Boats have survived them. Boats have also been folded in half against a rock face, so I want everyone to understand the odds before anyone votes."

Nobody voted. That was the thing about Odile Vantrease — she didn't put decisions to her crews, she issued them, and the strange part was that after two weeks on the river I'd stopped resenting it. There was a kind of mercy in a captain who took the weight.

"We ride it," she said. "Everything on this boat is the expedition. The documents, the charts, the gear. If we abandon ship, Halvard's men salvage it within a day and we've lost before the monsoon even arrives." She looked at me. "Can you keep us upright?"

I thought about the hull — shallow draft, steel-sheathed, built for shoal work, with a center of gravity already ruined by the extra fuel drums we'd stacked after the portage. I thought about the Throat downstream and the open water below the Anvil, and about the fact that a surge wave travels fastest in deep channels and slows where the river widens, which meant our best chance was to stay in the fast water, in the middle, away from the banks where the eddies would grab us and roll us.

"I can try," I said. "But I need everyone doing exactly what I say, exactly when I say it. No arguments. No heroics."

Sefa spat over the side, which I had learned was her version of a signed contract.

* * *

We spent the morning preparing, and it was the strangest work I've ever done — building a fortress against water, on water. We double-lashed the drums and wired them to the frame. We stripped the canvas awning so the surge couldn't peel it and take the rails with it. We rigged lifelines fore and aft, chest-high, with enough slack knots along their length that a man swept overboard might catch one on the way past. Brannoc took the engine apart and reassembled it with the intake raised eight inches, because the first thing a surge does is fill the river with silt, and silt kills engines before it kills boats.

Callum worked beside me in silence, hauling lashings tight, and when I handed him the marlinspike his fingers were shaking.

"You should eat something," I said.

"I'm not hungry."

"Callum." I kept my voice low, kept my eyes on the knot I was tying. "Whatever happened out there when you scouted ahead — you don't have to tell me. But if it has anything to do with what's coming down that river right now, I need to know. People drown keeping secrets."

For a moment I thought he'd speak. His mouth opened; his eyes went to Odile at the stern, then to the water, and something behind them shut like a door.

"It's nothing," he said. "Halvard's men saw me. They let me go. That's all."

That's all. Three words, and every one of them a lie, and I let it go because the river was starting to make noise.

You've never heard a river clear its throat unless you've stood under a gorge before a flood. The sound comes from downstream first — a low grinding, a shifting of boulders in the bed, the whole channel rearranging itself in anticipation. Then the birds go. All of them, at once, the way a room goes quiet when the dangerous man walks in. Sefa heard the silence before I did and grabbed my arm.

"Now," she said. "It's coming now."

* * *

I have ridden out bridge failures, boiler bursts, and one runaway grade that ended in a snowbank and a miracle, and I will tell you plainly: nothing I have survived prepared me for the sound the Kessarin made when Halvard opened his gates.

It came around the bend below us as a brown horizon. Not a wave — a horizon, the river standing higher than the banks in places, carrying whole trees in its teeth, rolling end over end like things drowning. The sound arrived a half-second later and it wasn't a roar, it was a pressure, a physical weight of noise that pushed against your chest and made your ribs hum. The boat lifted. Just lifted, gently, like a hand sliding under a hull, and then the world became brown water and white noise and the screaming of lines.

"Mid-channel!" I roared at Brannoc, and he was already there, hands on the wheel, feeding just enough engine to keep our bow pointed downriver — because the one law of riding a surge is that you point with it or it points you, and being pointed means broadside, and broadside means gone.

The first minutes were almost gentle. That's the trap of it. The surge picked us up and carried us at a speed no boat of ours could ever make under power, twelve miles an hour, fifteen, the gorge walls smearing past, and for one lunatic moment I thought: *this is easy*. Then the river hit the first constriction and remembered it was angry.

The water stood up. I don't know another word for it. Ahead of us the channel narrowed and the surge, with nowhere to spread, reared into standing waves taller than the mast, and Brannoc drove us straight into the trough between two of them because the trough was the only flat water left in the world. We went down into it and the walls closed overhead — actual walls of water, green-brown, lit from inside like stained glass — and then we went up the far side and the boat came out of the river entirely, airborne, all four drums leaving the deck, every soul aboard weightless for one full second.

I remember thinking, in that second, with a clarity I've never managed in calm water: *the starboard lashing will fail on landing.*

It did. The bow slammed down, the drum broke its wire, and two hundred pounds of fuel went across the deck like a thrown barrel in a tavern fight. Sefa caught it. I will see it until I die — this small woman, half the drum's weight, throwing herself bodily against rolling steel above a flooding deck, getting her shoulder under it, walking it back step by step while the boat yawed beneath her, until Callum got there and together they wrestled it into the remaining cradle and wired it down with shaking hands.

"Portside eddy!" Brannoc bellowed, and I saw it — a whirlpool the size of a chapel opening against the left bank where the surge met an outcrop, spinning slow and patient, eating a floating tree. Brannoc hauled the wheel hard over and the engine screamed and we slid past it close enough that the spin of it tugged the hull, dragged us a degree, two degrees, and I threw my weight on the wheel with him, four hands on the spokes, and the boat fought free with a groan of planking I'll hear in my sleep for years.

After that it was just time. The surge carried us through the Throat at a speed that should have killed us three separate ways, and each time the river reached for us someone aboard was already there — Sefa reading the water a beat before it moved, calling the waves before they stood up; Brannoc steering like a man who'd been born holding a wheel; Odile forward with a boathook, fending drifting logs off the bow, silent, relentless, her hair plastered to her skull; Callum bailing, always bailing, the pump overwhelmed, working the bucket like prayer.

And me. I stood at the wheel with Brannoc and steered us through the ruin of a river that a rich man had turned into a weapon, and I thought about the inquiry, ten years ago, and the forged report, and the thirty-one names read aloud in a courtroom while I sat in the gallery wearing someone else's coat. Halvard had drowned a valley once for profit and buried the paperwork. Now he'd opened a dam to bury us. And the fury that rose in me wasn't hot. It was cold and enormous and it fit perfectly in the space where fear had been, and I understood, riding that brown flood, that I was done running. Whatever waited at the end of this river, I would stand in front of it.

* * *

The surge lasted ninety minutes. It felt like nine days.

When the water began to settle — still high, still fast, but sane again, the standing waves collapsing back into current — we drifted into a widening below the Throat and let the engine idle. Nobody spoke. The boat was a wreck of shifted cargo and snapped line and one crushed rail where a log had kissed us in passing, and half our supplies were gone: the drum of spare fuel lost overboard in the first minute, the food locker stoved in and its contents a sodden paste, the survey instruments — my instruments, the theodolite I'd carried through ten years and four countries — smashed to brass and glass in the corner where the drums had traveled.

I counted it up in my head while the others breathed. Half. We'd lost half of everything, and the monsoon's outer bands were two days out, and the wreck of the Corvane was still three tides upriver.

Odile surveyed the damage the way she surveyed everything — completely, without flinching — and then she did something I didn't expect. She laughed. One short, hard bark of a laugh, aimed at nobody.

"He spent a fortune in water," she said, "to cost us half a pantry. He thinks he's beaten us." She turned, and her eyes found mine down the length of the wrecked deck, and they were bright and terrible. "Mr. Marchetti. How much farther on half rations?"

"Farther than he'd believe," I said.

Sefa was at the rail, looking back down the gorge we'd survived, and she made a small sound — not quite a word, not quite a prayer — and touched the water with two fingers and touched them to her brow. When she caught me watching, she didn't apologize. She never apologized for the old ways.

"The river gave us back," she said simply. "That doesn't happen twice."

Below us, far down the gorge, a black shape rounded the bend — the fleet tender, riding the same surge we had, unhurt, patient, a shark following blood it hadn't spilled yet. Brannoc saw it and his hands tightened on the wheel, and I saw something cross his face that I couldn't read then and would understand too well later.

"Three tides," I said to all of them, quietly, over the idling engine and the settling river. "From here on, we're racing for breath. Everything else comes after."

No one argued. The Kessarin ran high and brown and carried us upriver toward the truth, and behind us the black hull followed, and above us the first gray feathers of the monsoon were already combing westward off the escarpment, coming to meet us all.
