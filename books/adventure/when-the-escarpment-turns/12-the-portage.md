## 12. The Portage

The river ended at noon.

Not ended — Sefa corrected herself, spitting into the red mud — but *stopped*, the way a sentence stops when a man is strangled mid-word. The Kessarin that had carried them for three tides lay ahead in a brown lake, backed up against a mountain that had fallen into it. The landslide was a wound in the escarpment, raw rock and shattered trees, and where the channel had been there was now only a wall of earth and stone rising thirty feet above the waterline, with the river resuming its argument on the far side, audible through the rock like a voice through a closed door.

"Portage," Brannoc said, and the word came out of him like a stone out of a sling.

Sefa stood in the bow with her hand on the mooring line and felt the river's mood shift beneath her, the way she always felt it first — in her wrists, in the roots of her teeth. This water was wrong. Held water. Patient water. The kind that waited for permission to move, and whose permission came from a man with a ledger and a weir.

Above them, on the ridge, the first outer clouds of the monsoon were assembling themselves out of nothing, gray on gray, like a fleet of their own.

* * *

They built the sledges from the boat itself.

That was the arithmetic Brannoc laid out with a stick in the mud, and nobody liked it, and everybody nodded. The *Kestrel's Mercy* — Odile had named her, and Brannoc had grumbled that no boat should carry two birds' names, it was asking for confusion in the next world — was too heavy to carry whole. So they broke her into her bones: hull planks pried and stacked, the engine lashed to a frame of green saplings, the fuel drum and the water drum and the crate of documents wrapped in oilcloth riding on a second sledge built from the mast step and the after thwart. What couldn't be hauled was buried — cached in a cut bank above the flood line, marked with three stones in the pattern Sefa's grandmother had taught her, the one that said *a woman of the river passed here and means to come back*.

"You believe in that?" Callum asked, watching her set the stones. He was pale. He had been pale since the burned camp, since the survivor's fever-whispers, and Sefa had noticed that he no longer slept so much as he lay down near sleep and waited for it to change its mind.

"I believe in stones," Sefa said. "They were here before the river and they'll be here after. That's more than I can say for the rest of us."

She meant it lightly. He took it heavily. Everything landed heavily on Callum now, like rain on a tarp that had lost its slope.

The jungle above the slide was the old jungle, the patient jungle, and it took them the way the river would have, if the river had teeth and a grudge and a million small mouths. Leeches came down from the leaves like a dark rain with intentions. Within the first hour Sefa had pulled four off her ankles, two off the back of Brannoc's neck — he swore in three languages, two of them dead — and one from a place on Tamsin Marchetti's forearm that made the engineer go very quiet and very white and say, in the flat voice of a man consulting a manual, "I would prefer not to have learned that about myself."

"Salt," Sefa said, and handed him the little tin she kept in her shirt. "Press it, don't scrape. Scraping leaves the teeth in."

"You carry salt for leeches."

"I carry salt because the river gives and the river takes and a woman should carry something that keeps." She said it the way her mother said prayers — not for the meaning but for the rhythm — and Tamsin looked at her for a moment with an expression she couldn't read, something older than gratitude, and took the tin.

That was the first hour. The hours after that stopped having names.

* * *

The ground rose in switchbacks through the slide debris, and the sledges did not want to go where people wanted to go. That was the whole truth of the portage, Sefa thought: not strength, not endurance, but negotiation. The sledges were mules without opinions you could bribe. Every root was a debate, every slick of wet clay a referendum. Brannoc took the bow rope of the engine sledge and Tamsin took the stern, and between them they developed a language of grunts that Sefa found, privately, beautiful — two men who had met eleven days ago as strangers and now spoke a dialect of effort no one else on earth could parse.

Odile Vantrease hauled with the rest of them. Sefa had expected her not to. The woman had the hands of someone who had held nothing heavier than a fountain pen, and the voice of someone who had never been told no by anything softer than a lawyer. But Odile put her shoulder into the document crate's sledge at every root and every rise, and when her palms split on the second day — Sefa saw the blood before Odile did, dark on the wet wood — the heiress wrapped them with strips of her own shirt hem and said nothing, and when Sefa offered the salt tin she took it without thanks, which Sefa understood to be the highest form of thanks a proud woman could pay.

The documents. Everyone watched the documents' sledge the way sailors watch a compass in a storm. Sefa watched it too, but she watched it the way she watched the river — for what it *did*, not what it was supposed to mean. The crate rode on oilcloth and green wood and it was the heaviest thing on the mountain, heavier than the engine, though it weighed less. Weight like that wasn't measured in pounds.

Callum carried the water drum. He had insisted, and Brannoc had let him, and Sefa had said nothing, because she had seen the boy's hands shake and understood that he was not carrying water. He was carrying the burned radio mast, and the delirious woman's whisper — *the manifest, the manifest* — and whatever deal he thought nobody had seen him almost make on the ridge two nights ago. Sefa saw everything on a river. That was her whole profession. People thought a deckhand watched water. A deckhand watched people, because water never lied but people always did, and you needed one honest thing aboard.

On the afternoon of the second day the drum won.

* * *

It happened at the top of the last switchback, where the slide debris narrowed to a spine of rock with the drop falling away on both sides into green dark. Callum went up it first with the drum strapped to his back — they had rigged a carry-frame, because the drum had beaten the sledge and the sledge had beaten the rope and everything now was carried on backs, which was the jungle's final joke, that in the end all cargo was human.

He made it halfway.

Sefa, coming up behind with her hands under the engine frame, saw it the way you see a rope part — no cause, just consequence. One step his knee took, the next it didn't. He went down sideways, slow, almost polite, the drum rolling free and booming off the rock, and then Callum himself sliding, and Brannoc lunging and catching the boy's collar with one fist and the rock with the other, and for three full seconds the whole expedition hung on the grip of one gruff old pilot whose arms shook like a man holding a door against a flood.

They got him up. They got everything up. And then Callum tried to stand, and his legs said no, and he tried again, and Sefa saw it in his face — not pain, or not only pain. Shame. The specific shame of a young man who has been carrying a secret and has just discovered that the secret weighs more than the drum.

"Sleep," Brannoc said, lowering him against a tree. "That's an order from the deck."

"I can—"

"You can't. That's not an insult, boy, it's arithmetic. Even rivers stop." He said it gruffly, but Sefa heard what was under it, because she always heard what was under it: Brannoc had watched a man carry too much before. Brannoc knew exactly what weight did.

They redistributed the drum among four backs. And then, when it came time to move on, Callum tried to stand again, and failed again, and it was Tamsin Marchetti who crouched in front of him and turned around.

"Come on, then," the engineer said. "Arms over my shoulders. You're cargo now."

"I'm not—"

"Everyone's cargo," Sefa said, adjusting the strap across her own back. "That's what a crew is. A way of carrying each other."

Callum looked at her, and then at Tamsin's broad scarred back waiting for him like an offered hand, and whatever was corroding behind his eyes — Sefa saw it, the stone he was swallowing, she saw it plain as a snag in clear water — whatever it was, he set it down for one moment, or thought he did, and climbed on.

Tamsin stood with the boy on his back the way he did everything: without complaint, without ceremony, like a man following a specification. But Sefa, walking behind them for the next two hours, watched the engineer's hands come up and grip Callum's wrists where they crossed his chest, and grip them not like a man holding cargo but like a man holding on. And she thought: *this one has carried something alone for ten years and has forgotten that backs are for sharing.*

The jungle closed around them. The leeches found them again. Odile's wrapped hands left red prints on the rope. Brannoc sang, very low, a working song with no words Sefa knew, maybe words he'd invented, maybe words from a daughter, and the rhythm got into all of them, and their feet found it, and the mountain, which had been against them, became merely indifferent, which was as close to an ally as the Kessarin country ever came.

* * *

They came down to the river on the far side at dusk of the second day, and the Kessarin was waiting for them exactly where the mountain had interrupted it, running fast and brown and cold, as if nothing had happened, as if it had never been held.

They rebuilt the boat by lamplight. That was the work Sefa loved best in the world and had never told anyone: the reassembly, the bones finding each other, plank to frame, the hull remembering its shape the way a crew was remembering its shape. Brannoc fitted planks and swore at them tenderly. Odile held the lamplight, her ruined hands too bandaged for anything else, and did not complain about the demotion, which Sefa noted and stored away as further evidence that the woman was becoming someone new, or perhaps someone she had always been under the money.

Tamsin and Callum caulked seams side by side, and Callum's hands had come back to him with the rest, and they worked in the easy silence of people who have shared weight and therefore don't need to share words.

Sefa walked the bank and set three stones above the waterline, in the old pattern, facing back the way they had come.

"For the cache?" Callum asked, appearing beside her. He moved quietly now. The jungle had taught him that in two days.

"For us," Sefa said. "So the river knows we mean to come back this way. And so we know it too." She looked at him. He was still carrying the stone; she could see it in the set of his shoulders, the way he stood slightly apart from the firelight as if it might read his face. "Whatever it is you're hauling, boy," she said, not unkindly, "the mountain already took the drum off you. It can take the rest."

Callum went very still. The fire cracked. Downriver, far off, thunder spoke in the voice the monsoon used before it used its real voice.

"I don't know what you mean," he said.

"Of course not," Sefa agreed, and touched his shoulder once, briefly, the way you touch a hull to hear what it's hiding, and walked back toward the light.

She did not tell the others. Not yet. A deckhand watched, and waited, and chose her moment — and she had the feeling, watching the black clouds stack themselves upriver like freight cars on a grade, that the moment was coming down the water toward all of them faster than any of them knew.

That night, as she took the first watch and the rain began — soft, exploratory, the monsoon's fingertips — Tamsin Marchetti came and sat beside her at the bow without being asked. For a long time neither of them spoke. The river ran. The rain thickened.

"Sefa," he said at last, in the voice of a man stepping off a ledge. "There's something you should all know. About who I am."

She looked at him. Above the far gorge, lightning lit the escarpment from behind, turning the whole edge of the world gold for one held breath — and then the dark came back, and with it the rain, harder now, the sixth tide gathering itself beyond the mountains like a debt come due.
