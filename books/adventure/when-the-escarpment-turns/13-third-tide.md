## 13. Third Tide

The river at the halfway mark ran the color of gunmetal, and Tamsin Marchetti — who had not been Tamsin Marchetti for ten years, who had been a dozen names in a dozen junction towns, each one worn smooth as a river stone from handling — stood at the bow of the *Kestrel's Mercy* and felt the truth rising in him like water behind a weir.

They had made the third tide. Brannoc had threaded them through the Narrows of the Old King with barely an arm's width to spare on either side, the gorge walls so close that Sefa could have touched both at once with her outstretched hands, and when they shot through into the widening pool beyond, no one had cheered. They had simply breathed. All four of them, at once, a single long exhalation that seemed to empty the boat and refill it. That was the moment Tamsin understood he was lost. Not to the river — to them.

He had been counting the costs all morning, the way he counted everything: spans and loads and stresses. The cost of silence. The cost of Callum's glances, which had grown strange since the Gorge of Bells, since the night Sefa had steered them blind through listening posts by the sound of the water alone — though Tamsin couldn't know yet what those glances meant, only that they had weight, that they were aimed at him like survey instruments. The cost of Odile Vantrease waiting downriver on her own tender with half a fleet between her and ruin, having traded herself away without hesitation, which was the single most surprising thing he had learned about money in his entire life. And underneath all of it, the oldest cost, the principal accruing since the day the Marchetti Span came down: the cost of a name.

Tonight, moored in a cut of still water beneath cliffs the color of old iron, with the monsoon's outer bands dragging their skirts across the escarpment and the air thick enough to chew, he would pay it.

* * *

Sefa cooked. She always cooked on the nights after a hard passage, as if feeding them were a kind of navigation too — keeping the crew off the rocks of themselves. Tonight it was river fish wrapped in bitter leaves and roasted over coals in a drum lid, and rice gone smoky at the bottom of the pot, which everyone pretended not to love and everyone ate first.

Brannoc ate like a man settling accounts with his food, methodical, elbows wide. Callum picked at his portion and watched the fire. Sefa sat cross-legged with her bowl balanced on her knee, and her eyes kept going to the black water, where the river spoke its endless low commentary against the hull.

"The third tide," she said, to no one and everyone. "The river turns over tonight. My grandmother said whatever you carry past the halfway mark, you carry home."

"That's superstition," Callum said, but gently, the way he'd learned to say it.

"It's hydrology," Sefa said. "Everything upstream comes downstream eventually."

Tamsin set down his bowl. His hands were steady — engineer's hands, trained to be steady on the worst days, the day of the collapse most of all, when steadiness had been the only thing left to him and everyone had mistaken it for coldness. He looked at the fire. He looked at these three people who had hauled a boat over a landslide on their backs, who had shared leeches and fever watches and the last of the coffee, who had given him — without knowing it — the first weeks in ten years during which he had not once checked over his shoulder.

"My name isn't Tamsin Marchetti," he said.

The fire popped. Somewhere up the cliff face, a bird complained once and went quiet.

"It is," he continued, "and it isn't. I took it eleven years ago, off a dead man's credential in a freight yard in Corvane City. The man I was before that—" He stopped. Started again, slower, the way you talk through a bad stretch of track. "My name is Elias Voss. I was a structural engineer with the Meridian Rail Consortium. Ten years ago this autumn, I signed the inspection certificate for the Kessarin River Bridge at Halloway Crossing. Three months later, it came down under a loaded ore train. Forty-one people."

Callum had gone very still. Sefa's bowl had stopped halfway to her mouth.

"I didn't cause it," Tamsin said. "I need you to hear that, because I've spent ten years unable to say it to anyone who mattered. The inspection was real. My numbers were real. But the certification report that went to the consortium — the one that cleared the bridge for the heavier ore loads — wasn't mine. It was forged. Rebuilt from the ground up. My findings were replaced with someone else's, my signature traced onto the bottom of it, and by the time I understood what had happened, the bridge was down and the inquiry needed a name and mine was already written into the record in my own handwriting." He laughed, and there was nothing in it. "I ran. That's the whole of it. I've been running ever since. Every job temporary. Every town a platform I boarded with a forged credential. Until your aunt put triple pay on the table and I told myself it was just another job."

"Odile," Callum said quietly. "She's my aunt."

"I know," Tamsin said. "I've known since the second day. You have her chin."

Something almost like a laugh moved through Callum and died young.

"And here's the part I should have told you at the beginning, and didn't, because telling it makes me a liability instead of a hired hand." Tamsin looked at the fire because he could not look at them yet. "The company that forged my report was Corvane Salvage and Recovery. Its managing director at the time was Renn Halvard. The inquiry never proved it — his lawyers buried it under three feet of paper — but I found the discrepancy myself, years later, working a dredging contract. A load calculation in the certified report that matched, digit for digit, a template from Halvard's own office. The bridge didn't fail. It was failed. Cleared for loads it couldn't carry so the ore contracts would move faster, and when it fell, the fall was blamed on me."

He finally raised his eyes.

"So when I tell you that everything we're doing — the wreck, the manifest, the documents in that strongroom — when I tell you it's my ruin downriver in that hull, I mean it literally. Halvard didn't just kill forty-one people and bury the proof. He built his whole empire on top of the grave, and he used my name for the cornerstone. If those manifests say what the survivor says they say, then the man you know as Tamsin Marchetti stops existing somewhere downriver. And Elias Voss walks into the river authority and testifies under his own name for the first time in ten years."

Silence. The river talked against the hull. The fire settled.

It was Sefa who moved first. She set down her bowl, crossed the deck, and did something no one had done to Elias Voss in a decade: she took his hand, turned it over, and studied the palm like a chart.

"Steady hands," she said. "Grandmother always said you can tell a liar by the hands. Liars' hands are busy." She closed his fingers around nothing and pressed them shut. "Yours are quiet. You held the fever woman's wrist for six hours the other night and never once squeezed when she fought you. Liars can't do that. Their hands are always performing."

"Sefa," Callum started.

"Hush. The river's turning over." She went back to her place and picked up her bowl as if nothing had happened, as if she hadn't just performed the only act of faith Tamsin had received since the bridge fell.

Callum was looking at him with an expression Tamsin couldn't read — something between recalculation and grief. "Halvard released me," he said abruptly. "In the gorge. When he caught me scouting. He let me go and told me—"

"Callum." Brannoc's voice, low, a warning bell.

"—he told me things," Callum finished stubbornly, and then, visibly deciding, told them anyway: the offer, the terms, Brannoc's daughter, the price of Tamsin himself. The words fell into the firelight like stones into still water, and Tamsin watched the ripples cross every face, including his own, and found — to his genuine surprise — that he was not afraid. Whatever Callum had carried since the gorge, it had been eating him alive; Tamsin had seen the same look in mirrors for ten years. Now it was out. Weighed. On the ledger.

"You didn't do it," Tamsin said.

"Not yet."

"'Yet' is honest. I'll take honest."

Brannoc had not spoken. He sat with his forearms on his knees, staring into the coals, and the light moved on his scarred knuckles and his gray-shot beard, and Tamsin braced for it — the verdict, the voice raised, the hand on the tiller turning toward some port where Elias Voss could be put ashore, or worse, handed over, because there would be men downriver who would pay handsomely for the engineer whose signature was worth more than gold to anyone building a case against Halvard. Or defending him.

Instead Brannoc reached into his coat and took out a folded, oil-stained paper, softened nearly to cloth by years of handling, and unfolded it on his knee.

"Bond note," he said. "Halvard's house. Fourteen years back, when I was running cargo I shouldn't have been running and needed an engine overhaul I couldn't pay for. He wrote it generous. Too generous. I signed." His thumb moved across the paper, tracing a signature. "I didn't read the clauses. Nobody reads the clauses. That's how men like Halvard build their fleets — not on steel, on signatures. Half the boats on this river are chained to him by papers their owners never read." He looked up, and his eyes in the firelight were the flat gray of the water at dawn. "You signed a certificate. I signed a bond. Callum signed a bargain he means to tear up. We all signed papers we didn't read."

He folded the note away again.

"The difference is," Brannoc said, "you've been paying yours for ten years, and you paid it for a crime somebody else committed. As far as I'm concerned, the debt's paid. Paid double." He jerked his chin at the dark water, upriver, toward the wreck that waited for them in the narrowing gorge. "So tomorrow we run the fourth tide, and the day after the fifth, and on the sixth we go down into that hull and take back what's yours. Not for the money. Not even for Odile." A pause, and something roughened in his voice. "For the forty-one. Somebody owes them a testimony, and it might as well be the man whose name they hung it on."

Tamsin found that his throat had closed. He nodded, because nodding was all that worked.

Sefa rose, gathered the bowls, and paused beside him on her way to the drum of wash water. "Elias," she said, trying the name like a new channel, sounding its depth. Then she smiled, small and crooked. "No. Tamsin. The river knows you by that name now. Names change in the water. Grandmother said that too."

Afterward, when the others had turned in, Tamsin stood the first watch alone at the rail, and the river turned over beneath him exactly as Sefa had promised — a slow, visible shifting of the current, the surface reorganizing itself around some deep change in the channel below, the way a man reorganizes himself around a truth finally set down.

He was still watching it when he saw the light.

Far up the gorge, high on the cliff road that followed the old rail line — the line he had come to survey, the line that had led him here — a lantern swung twice. Pause. Twice again. A signal, deliberate, aimed downriver at the black water where a boat lay moored with its fire burned to coals.

And answering it, faint but unmistakable, from somewhere ahead in the dark: two flashes. Pause. Two more.

Someone knew they were coming. Someone was lighting the way — or laying the trap.

Tamsin's hand found the rail, and the rail, being honest iron, held.
