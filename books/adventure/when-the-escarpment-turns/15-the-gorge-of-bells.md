## 15. The Gorge of Bells

The gorge announced itself before we saw it — a low, resonant hum in the hull, like the boat had begun to sing against its will.

Tamsin felt it through the soles of his boots first, then through the deck planks, then up through his spine: a vibration too steady to be current, too deep to be wind. He climbed from the engine well and stood at the rail, and there it was ahead of them, blacker than the night around it. The Kessarin narrowed between two walls of stone so sheer they seemed cut rather than eroded, and somewhere inside that throat of rock, the river made a sound he had never heard water make.

Not rushing. Not roaring. *Ringing.*

"Sefa," he called softly. "Come hear this."

She came barefoot, as she always did at night, her toes reading the deck the way other people read instruments. She crouched at the rail, laid one palm flat against the gunwale, and went very still. In the lamplight spilling from the wheelhouse her face was half gold, half shadow, and what he could see of it frightened him more than the sound itself.

"You know it," he said.

"I know its name." She didn't look at him. "My grandmother sang about this place when I was small. She said the gorge holds bells that drowned men hung there so their wives could find them again." Her fingers spread wider on the wood. "It isn't bells, Tamsin. It never was. It's the way the wind moves through the narrows — it makes the rock sing. The old people used to run it blind at night because the song tells you where the walls are. Where the shoals are. Where the dead water waits."

"And now?"

"Now the fleet has learned the same trick." She finally turned to him, and her eyes were flat and dark as river stones. "They've strung their listening posts where the song is loudest. Wires across the channel, low, just under the surface, with bells on them — real ones, cheap ones, tin and brass. Anything that touches them rings. And every man they have posted on those cliffs has an ear trained to tell the difference between the river's voice and ours."

Brannoc appeared at the wheelhouse door with a lantern hooded in his fist, and behind him Callum, who had been quiet for two days in a way that Tamsin had noticed and chosen, for now, not to name.

"How wide?" Brannoc asked.

"Half a cable at the narrowest," Sefa said. "Maybe less."

"Current?"

"Fast enough to forgive nothing."

Brannoc grunted, which from him was a kind of prayer. He looked at Tamsin. "Your engines can't be heard. That's the only mercy tonight. We go through cold — no steam whistle, no pump, no generator. Batteries for steering light only. And we go by ear." He nodded at Sefa. "Hers."

* * *

They killed the fires an hour before the entrance and let the boat drift.

Tamsin stood in the engine well in absolute darkness, hands resting on the cold valve wheels, and listened to his own machinery die by degrees — the tick of cooling metal, the sigh of pressure bleeding off, the last shudder of the shaft bearing running down to stillness. Ten years of keeping engines alive, and tonight his whole craft consisted of making one silent. There was a poetry in that he might have laughed at, another time, in another life.

Above him, the boat became something else entirely. He heard Brannoc ship the oars and rig the sweep — the long steering sweep they'd carried lashed under the rail since the second day, a piece of dead weight Tamsin had privately resented until this moment. He heard Odile come up on deck without being called; she moved differently now than she had at the start, less like a woman descending to inspect and more like a crew member taking her station. She'd taken the bow watch without discussion. Whatever had happened at the weir, whatever she'd confessed under that wall of held-back water, had burned something out of her and left someone quieter standing in the ash.

He heard Callum take the stern line, coiling it soft-footed, and thought again about the boy's silence, and put the thought away in the box where he kept everything he couldn't afford to look at yet.

And he heard Sefa climb to the foredeck and stand at the stem like a figurehead, barefoot, arms loose at her sides, head tilted into the wind that funneled down the gorge toward them carrying the river's ringing voice.

"Slow water on the left side of the entrance," she said, in a voice pitched to carry no farther than the deck. "We hug the left wall until the first bend. Then I'll call it."

Brannoc took the sweep. The boat slid forward on the current alone, and the darkness swallowed them.

* * *

The gorge closed over them like a mouth.

Tamsin had run trains through mountain tunnels in weather that would strip paint off iron, and he had thought himself past being afraid of enclosed dark places. But a tunnel had walls you could measure, grades you could calculate, air you could trust to stay where the ventilation put it. This was different. This was a tunnel that breathed, that shifted, that sang — and that had been seeded, all through its singing dark, with the machinery of men who wanted them stopped or dead.

The walls rose on either side faster than seemed possible. He felt them more than saw them: a change in the wind's behavior, a sudden absence of stars where seconds before there had been a ragged seam of them overhead. The ringing grew until it wasn't a sound anymore but a condition, something they were floating inside of, the way a fish floats inside water. Harmonics stacked on harmonics. The stone itself was a bell the size of a cathedral, and the monsoon wind off the escarpment was drawing it, and every note of it said: *you are small, you are slow, you are already lost.*

"Left wall, ten feet," Sefa whispered. "Hold."

Brannoc held. The sweep creaked once — a sound like a knuckle cracking in church — and Tamsin winced at it, and the river swallowed even that.

Then, ahead and below, faint but unmistakable: a new note. Thin, metallic, wrong. A single tone that did not belong to the stone's great chord, hanging in the water like a splinter.

Sefa's hand came up. The boat drifted. The strange note faded, returned, faded — moving, Tamsin realized, swinging gently on its mooring line in the current. A bell on a wire. One of dozens, maybe hundreds, strung across the channel at intervals, each one waiting for a hull to kiss it and ring their position to every listening post on the cliffs above.

"They're spaced by sound," Sefa breathed. "Whoever set them knew the song. They hung each bell where the stone's voice would cover it — cover it for anyone who doesn't know how to listen." Her voice dropped lower still. "There's a gap. Between the third wire and the fourth, the song changes key. The fleet's listeners will be watching the loud places. The key change is our door."

"And if you're wrong?" Callum whispered from the stern. It wasn't accusation. It was the question of a man who needed someone else to have done the arithmetic because he couldn't bear to do it himself.

"Then we ring," Sefa said simply, "and we learn what's at the bottom of the Kessarin."

Nobody argued with that.

* * *

They passed the first wire so close Tamsin could have touched it.

He knew because he heard it — not the bell, which stayed silent, blessedly silent, but the wire itself, a humming filament note passing down the port side close enough that the hair on his forearm lifted. Somewhere in the dark a few feet from his elbow hung a brass bell the size of his fist, swaying on its line, and the whole survival of everyone he loved depended on the difference between a drift and a touch, a hand's width of current, a degree of rudder.

His engineer's mind, treacherous thing, kept trying to solve it. Kept wanting to know the wire tension, the bell mass, the damping coefficient of wet hemp, the exact velocity at which a displacement became a ring. He made himself stop. This was not his instrument. Tonight the boat was an instrument, and Sefa was the player, and his job was to keep the machinery dead and his fear quiet and trust a deckhand's grandmother's songs more than he trusted his own calculations.

That, he was learning, was what trust actually was. Not the warm feeling. The deliberate surrender of control to someone better suited to hold it.

"Second wire," Sefa called, barely audible. "Starboard side this time. Drift right. *Right.*"

The boat swung. The wall of the gorge shouldered close on the left — Tamsin felt the reflected wash of their own wake come back at them off the stone — and the second wire's filament note slid past on the right, near enough that Brannoc exhaled through his teeth.

"Third," Sefa said. And then, after a long moment in which the only sounds were the stone's vast ringing and the small wet sounds of the river working at the hull: "Now the key change. Everyone breathe. Nobody breathes."

* * *

The key change was real.

Tamsin heard it happen — the great chord of the gorge shifting beneath them like a floor tilting, the dominant note falling away and something darker rising in its place, and in that instant of acoustic shadow, as Sefa had promised, the world went deaf. If there were listeners on the cliffs above — and there were; they'd seen the ember-glow of a cookfire at a ledge camp an hour ago, smelled tobacco smoke riding the funnel-wind — then this stretch of the song was where their ears failed them. The stone covered everything here. Even a hull. Even a heartbeat.

"Door's open," Sefa whispered. "Brannoc — pull. Pull like the devil's rowing behind us."

Brannoc pulled. The sweep bit, the boat gathered way, and for ninety seconds they moved through the heart of the Gorge of Bells at speed, blind, trusting, the wires' positions held only in Sefa's memory and her grandmother's song, and Tamsin stood in his dead engine room with both hands flat on the cold valve wheels and prayed to nothing in particular with a fervor that would have embarrassed him in daylight.

A wire-note passed off the starboard bow — close, so close, the filament hum bending around them like a struck string still trembling from some earlier touch, some earlier boat, some earlier crew that hadn't been lucky.

Then another. Then open water-sound, the walls falling back, the chord releasing them, and the stars returning overhead in a ragged bright seam, and Sefa laughing — a small, cracked, disbelieving laugh, the first he'd ever heard from her.

"We're through," she said. "We're *through.*"

* * *

They anchored in a side eddy below the last bend, in the lee of a fallen pillar of stone, and nobody spoke for a long while.

Odile broke the silence first, coming aft with the thermos of chicory coffee that had become the night-watch ritual, pouring cups with hands that were almost steady. She handed Tamsin his without meeting his eyes, which was her way of being kind. Then she looked back up the gorge, at the black walls they'd slipped past, and said quietly:

"Halvard built all this in nine days. Wires, posts, crews, coal for the fires. Do you understand what that costs? What it *means*?" She wrapped both hands around her own cup. "He's not trying to protect salvage rights. Salvage rights are paper. This is a man spending a fortune to keep a secret from reaching the river authority, and the fortune doesn't matter to him, which means the secret matters more than money, which means—" she stopped, and drank, and finished differently: "—which means everything Sefa's survivor told us is true, and he knows we know."

"He knows we're coming," Brannoc said. "Question is whether he knows we got through clean, or whether somewhere upriver there's a man on a cliff writing in a logbook right now: *unidentified vessel, passed silently, 0300.*"

"If they'd heard us," Sefa said, "we'd know. The posts signal each other. You'd hear the relay — a lamp flash, a rocket. The river's been quiet since the third wire." She paused. "Quiet except for us."

Callum set his cup down on the rail. He'd barely touched it. In the low lantern light his face had the grey, stretched look of a man carrying something heavy in a place no one could see, and when he spoke his voice was carefully, deliberately light.

"So we're past the listening posts. What's next — the wreck itself?"

"The wreck," Tamsin said, "on the fifth tide. Which gives us one full day and one more night." He looked around the circle of faces — Brannoc's carved and unreadable, Sefa's still glowing with the afterburn of what she'd done, Odile's grim and clear, Callum's careful mask — and felt the strange, terrifying warmth that he'd spent ten years training himself never to feel. These people had just bet their lives on a deckhand's ears and a pilot's arms and an engineer's silence, and the bet had paid, and none of them had doubted, and he had not been alone in the dark.

"Get some sleep," he said. "All of you. Tomorrow we run the open water, and the day after, we dive."

They dispersed to their bedrolls. Tamsin stayed at the rail a while longer, watching the gorge recede astern, listening to the last fading harmonic of its buried bells.

He didn't see Callum slip away from the dying fire toward the bow, nor the way the young man stood there a long time, staring upriver into the dark, turning something over and over in his hands that caught the starlight — a small, folded paper, creased and re-creased until it was going soft at the folds.

But Sefa, sleepless, watching the water as she always watched the water, saw.
