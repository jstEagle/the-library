## 16. Loyalty's Ledger

Brannoc Vael had learned to read men the way he read water, and water, in the end, was simpler. Water lied only by omission. It hid its rocks beneath the surface but never pretended they weren't there. Men hid their rocks and called the smooth water honesty.

He had been watching Callum Vantrease for two days, the way you watch a stretch of river that has gone too quiet.

It was in the boy's hands first. Callum had always had restless hands — the hands of a man who had never worked for anything and didn't know what to do with himself when the work finally came. On the portage he had carried his share, had carried more than his share the day he collapsed and they'd hauled him through the leech-hung green like a sack of wet rope. But since the gorge, since the night Sefa had steered them through the bells by sound alone, his hands had gone still. Folded. Guarded. Hands that were holding something.

And it was in his eyes second. Callum had taken to standing at the bow in the grey hours, looking upriver, and there was a particular quality to that looking — not longing, not fear, but *calculation*. Brannoc knew that look. He had worn it himself, once, standing on the deck of a Halvard tender with a ledger open in front of him and his daughter's name written in the margin of every page.

*You know the arithmetic*, Brannoc told him silently, watching the boy's back in the pre-dawn murk. *You've done the sums. You just haven't signed yet.*

He meant to give him one more day. One more day to come to someone, to break, to spill it in the galley over burnt tea the way guilty men do when the weight gets past bearing. Brannoc had seen it a hundred times on a hundred decks. Guilt was a cargo that shifted in rough water, and Callum's sea had been rough for days.

He didn't get his day.

* * *

It was the fourth tide, the water low and mean between its banks, the gorge walls rising on either side like the pages of a closed book. They had moored in a side channel for the noon rest — Sefa's rule, never run the narrows with the sun high and flat, when the water showed you nothing — and Brannoc had gone up the scree to check a line he didn't trust. When he came back down, soft-footed, an old habit from years he didn't talk about, he heard voices from the shadow of the overhang where they'd stowed the spare fuel.

Callum's voice, low and ragged. And the other voice — that was worse, because it wasn't a voice at all. It was a radio. The small set they'd salvaged from the burned base camp, the one Tamsin had pronounced dead. Apparently it had enough life in it for one conversation, if you walked far enough from camp and kept your voice down.

Brannoc stood in the scree with his boots silent on the stone and listened to a boy sell them all.

He couldn't make out every word. The river took its toll of the sound. But he caught the shape of it, and the shape was enough: *yes — tomorrow — the fifth tide — the wreck — I can mark where they'll moor —* and then, in a voice that cracked like green wood, *you swear it. You swear on —* and then a pause, and then, smaller, almost a whisper: *her name. Say her name.*

Brannoc closed his eyes.

*Say her name.* Not *I want the money.* Not *I want out.* The boy had asked Halvard to swear on the name of Brannoc's daughter, and he hadn't even known he was doing it — hadn't known that the debt he was being asked to settle belonged to another man on the same boat, that the coin Halvard was paying him with had been minted from Brannoc's own blood.

He should have gone down there. Should have taken the radio out of the boy's shaking hands and dashed it on the rocks, or worse — should have dragged him back to camp and laid it all out before Tamsin and Odile and let the ledger fall where it fell. That would have been the clean way. The river way: name the rock, mark the channel, let the crew decide.

But Brannoc Vael had not been a clean man for a long time, and he had learned — at a cost he still paid interest on — what happened to a man who named other people's rocks while pretending his own water ran smooth.

He climbed back up the scree and sat in the sun and waited for dark.

* * *

The camp settled the way it always did now, and Brannoc noted, with a pain he kept in a closed compartment below his sternum, how much it had come to feel like a thing worth protecting. Sefa humming over the cookfire, some river-song in a language she only half remembered, the melody bending around the smoke. Tamsin hunched over his charts by lamplight, the false name long since burned off him, the true one sitting easier on his shoulders than the lie ever had — Brannoc had watched that happen and had been ashamed of how much it moved him. Odile Vantrease, who had bought this expedition the way her father had bought everything, sitting on a crate mending a sail with her own hands because there was no one left to pay to do it, and looking, for the first time since Brannoc had met her, like a person rather than a purchase order.

*This is what you're spending*, he thought, looking at them. *Know it. Before you sign anything else, know what the coin looks like.*

Callum took first watch. Of course he did. Guilt always volunteers for the lonely hours; it wants to be alone with itself.

Brannoc let him have an hour of it. Let him stand at the bow with the gorge breathing around them, the river muttering over its rocks, the escarpment a blacker black against the star-scattered sky. Then he rose from his bedroll without a sound, as he had risen from a hundred bunks in a hundred years he wished he could scuttle like a leaky hull, and walked to the bow, and stood beside the boy, and said nothing at all.

Callum started. Recovered. Said, too lightly, "Quiet night."

"Quietest kind," Brannoc said. "The kind where a man can hear himself think. Dangerous water, that."

He let the silence sit. Downriver, somewhere in the dark, the water made a sound like a door closing softly.

"I heard the radio," Brannoc said.

He watched it land. Watched the boy's face go through the whole litany — shock, denial, the quick scramble for a story, and then, because Callum Vantrease had never in his life been a good liar and the river strips even the bad ones bare, the collapse. His shoulders came down. His hands, those restless hands, hung still at his sides, and he stared at the water as if it might take him if he only stepped far enough.

"How much did you hear."

"Enough. The fifth tide. Where we'll moor." Brannoc kept his voice level, the voice he'd used for cargo negotiations in ports where a raised tone could get a man knifed. "And enough to know you asked him to swear on a girl's name."

Callum flinched like he'd been struck. "I didn't know it was — I didn't know who he —"

"He told you a daughter. He didn't tell you whose." Brannoc looked out at the dark water. "It's mine. Wren. She's fourteen. She lives in Saltmere with my sister, and every quarter-day a man in a good coat comes by the school and asks after her health, and every quarter-day I know she's alive because Halvard lets me know. That's the arrangement. That's been the arrangement for six years."

The boy said nothing. The river said plenty.

"Six years ago I ran a cargo for him," Brannoc went on, because he had decided, somewhere on the scree in the hot noon silence, that tonight he would do the thing he had never done — that he would open his own ledger and let another man read it. "Not contraband. Worse. Salvage equipment, up a closed river, to a site that didn't exist on any chart. I didn't ask what it was for. I'd just lost my berth and my boat and my wife was a year in the ground and there was a girl who needed feeding, and Halvard paid triple, and I told myself the same thing you told yourself, I'd bet the whole boat on it. *I'm not hurting anyone. I'm just steering. The cargo's not my crime.*"

"And it was," Callum said quietly. "It was your crime."

"It was my crime." The words came out of him like ballast off a sinking deck, and he was astonished — genuinely astonished, an old man surprised by his own hull — at how much lighter the boat rode without it. "The site was a staging depot. Everything they pulled out of the Kessarin gorges that year went through it. Everything they buried. Including, I found out later, the original manifests off a wreck called the Corvane. I steered the equipment that made the burying possible, and I took the money, and I bought Wren shoes with it, and I have hated those shoes every day of her life."

Callum made a sound that might have been a laugh if laughter knew how to weep.

"So when I say I know the arithmetic," Brannoc said, "understand that I mean it. I've run your sums. *Deliver the engineer, everyone walks free.* It's a good trade. On paper it's the best trade on this river. One man you've known a fortnight against a whole boat's worth of lives, and your own conscience thrown in free." He turned and looked at the boy straight on, in the starlight, and did not soften it. "I ran those sums for six years, boy, and I'll tell you what the ledger doesn't show. It doesn't show what you become. Halvard doesn't want Callum Vantrease. He wants a man who'll sell a boatload of people for a promise, and the moment he has him, he owns him forever, and the price goes up every quarter-day. That's not a deal. That's a mooring line. Once it's fast, you never cast it off. You just get towed."

"He swore," Callum said, and his voice was a boy's voice now, all the brittle Vantrease varnish gone, just the raw wood underneath. "He swore on her name, Brannoc, he said —"

"He swore on a name he thought was leverage." Brannoc shook his head slowly. "He doesn't know whose it is. Which means his oath is worth exactly what he thinks it's buying — a stranger's child. And a man who'll swear falsely on a stranger's child will swear falsely on anyone's." He let that sit in the boy like a hook set deep. "But here's what I came to say, and I'll say it once, and then we'll never speak of it again unless you make me."

He reached into his coat and took out the radio — Callum's eyes went wide, he'd hidden it well but not from a man who had once hidden cargoes from customs men for a living — and held it in both hands over the gunwale.

"I'm not going to expose you," Brannoc said. "Not to Tamsin, not to Odile, not to Sefa, who would gut you, and not to the river, which would be kinder. I'm going to give you what nobody gave me. A chance to choose knowing. Not scared, not cornered, not in the dark — knowing. You can take this set back and finish your message tomorrow, and I'll say nothing, and I'll live with it, because a man who's bought his daughter with a crime has no standing to preach. Or you can help me drop this in the river, and tomorrow we go to the wreck together, and you spend the rest of your life knowing you were the kind of man who could have sold us and didn't." He turned the radio slowly in his hands, the starlight moving on its dead grey face. "That's the whole ledger, boy. Two entries. Pick your line."

For a long time Callum didn't move. The gorge held its breath. Somewhere above them, on the escarpment, a night bird called three notes and got no answer, and the river ran on over its hidden rocks, honest in the only way it knew how.

"I was going to do it," Callum said at last, and it came out like a confession in a church with no priest. "Tonight. I had it all worked out. I'd mark the mooring on a scrap of chart and leave it at the bend, and by the time anyone knew, we'd —" He stopped. Swallowed. "I told myself it was for them. *Everyone walks free.* I told myself I was the only one risking anything, that it was almost noble, almost —" His voice broke clean in half. "God, Brannoc. I almost believed it."

"Everybody believes it," Brannoc said. "That's how the trade works. Halvard doesn't buy men's treachery. He buys their excuses. He's the best buyer of excuses on this coast." He held out the radio. "So. Are you going to be his excuse, or are you going to be my crew?"

Callum looked at the radio. Then he looked past it, upriver, into the dark where the Corvane lay drowned in the riverbank with the truth in her belly, and Brannoc watched something happen in the boy's face that he had watched happen in mirrors on bad nights for six years — the moment the arithmetic stops balancing, the moment the ledger's two columns refuse to reconcile, and a man understands that some debts can't be paid with other people's lives.

"Drop it," Callum said.

Brannoc dropped it.

They stood at the rail together and listened to the little dead machine go down into the big dead river, and neither of them said anything, because there was nothing left to say that the splash hadn't said better.

* * *

They did not shake hands. Brannoc would have found that obscene — a handshake was for strangers closing a deal, and whatever this was, it was the opposite of a deal. Instead Callum sat down on the foredeck with his back against the mast, all the varnish cracked off him, looking ten years younger and a hundred years more used, and after a while Brannoc sat down next to him, because old men know that sometimes the only absolution left in the world is company.

"Does it get lighter," Callum asked eventually, "or do you just get used to the weight?"

"Both," Brannoc said. "Mostly both." He tipped his head back against the mast. Above them the escarpment turned slowly against the stars, that great shoulder of the world, and he thought about what Tamsin had said around the fire a few nights back, the engineer with his charts and his second chance: *a river doesn't forgive its rocks. It just learns the channel around them.* "You'll carry it. The almost. That's the part that stays. But you'll carry it on a boat that's going somewhere, and that's a different cargo than carrying it on a boat that's for sale."

Callum was quiet a long moment. "Wren," he said. "Your daughter. When this is done — when Halvard's done — the man in the good coat stops coming by the school."

"Aye."

"And you'll go to her."

"If she'll have a father who smells of river and regret."

"She will," Callum said, with a certainty that surprised them both. "Girls like stories. And you're the best story I know."

Brannoc laughed — a short, rough, astonished sound, the first one in days — and clapped the boy once on the shoulder, and felt under his palm how thin he was, how young, how much river was still left in him to cross.

They sat until Callum's watch ended and Sefa came forward to relieve them, and she looked at the two of them sitting shoulder to shoulder in the dark, and she looked at Brannoc a moment too long, the way she looked at water before trusting it, but she said nothing, only nodded and went aft. Brannoc would tell her. Soon. Not tonight. Tonight the ledger was balanced enough.

He was nearly asleep when Callum spoke again, from the darkness beside him, in a voice stripped down to the keel.

"Brannoc. The message I sent before you — before tonight. The first one. He knows we're close. He knows the fifth tide." A pause, and the boy's breath went ragged. "He was already moving. I heard engines on the radio. A lot of engines."

Brannoc lay in the dark with the stars wheeling over the escarpment and did the only arithmetic that mattered: one night's head start, a gorge full of warning wires, a wreck with the truth in her hold, and a fleet with all the money in the world coming down on them like weather.

Tomorrow was the fifth tide.

They would have to be faster than forgiveness.
