## 17. Fifth Tide: The Wreck

The river told us before the sky did.

Sefa felt it first — she always did — standing at the bow with her bare feet braced against the deck plates, her head tilted like a woman listening to a conversation happening somewhere below the waterline. "She's swelling," she said, and there was no drama in it, only the flat certainty of someone reporting a change in the weather. "Two fingers since dawn. Maybe three."

I checked the gauge myself because I needed something to do with my hands. She was right. The Kessarin had risen three fingers overnight without a drop of rain falling on us, which meant the rain was falling somewhere else — high up, in the escarpment country where the monsoon's outer bands were already dragging their gray skirts across the ridgelines. What we were seeing was only the rumor of the storm, sent downriver ahead of the storm itself.

"Five hours," Brannoc said from the wheelhouse. He didn't look up from the chart. He'd been looking at that chart for two days the way a man looks at a debt he can't pay. "Maybe six before the current turns ugly. After that, nobody dives, nobody holds position, nobody does anything but pray and point downstream."

"Then we don't waste them," Odile said.

She stood at the rail with her binoculars raised, sweeping the northern bank where the gorge narrowed and the cliffs came down to meet the water in shelves of black slate. Her knuckles were white around the casings. Nine days of silence from her expedition, four days of running Halvard's gauntlet, one crew member lost to the surge at the weir, half their supplies gone downriver in the flood — and still she stood at that rail every morning like a woman waiting for a train she'd paid for in blood.

"There." Her voice cracked on it. "There — smoke. Or mist. Tamsin, look —"

I took the glasses. At first I saw nothing but the bank: jungle pressing down to the waterline, ferns hanging in green curtains, the slate shelves slick and dark. Then the current bent around something — a long, low distortion in the flow, a seam of smoother water where the river parted and rejoined itself — and above it, half-swallowed by the bank, a shape.

Not a shape, exactly. A suggestion of one. A curve of hull plating furred with moss, a rib of steel showing through the vegetation like the carcass of some enormous animal that the jungle had been eating for years. The Corvane. Half-buried, half-drowned, grown over so thoroughly that from fifty yards you'd take it for geology.

But I'd spent ten years memorizing salvage schematics I was never supposed to have seen, and I knew that curve. Knew it the way you know a face from a photograph of a funeral.

"She's listing hard to port," I said. "Buried to maybe the third strake. But the starboard side —" I passed the glasses back to Odile. "The starboard side might be dry inside. If the seals held."

"And if they didn't?" Callum asked quietly.

He'd been quiet since the Gorge of Bells. Quiet in a way that sat wrong with me, though I couldn't have said why — not then. He stood at the rail now with his arms crossed, watching the wreck come up out of the green haze, and his face had the careful blankness of a man who has practiced not having expressions.

"Then we've run five tides for nothing," Odile said, "and we die in a flooded ship instead of a flooded boat. Get the gear."

* * *

We moored against the wreck itself, which Brannoc hated and said so, twice, in the colorful vocabulary of a man who'd piloted everything from log booms to gunboats. The current curled around the buried bow and made an eddy on the downstream side, a pocket of slack water just big enough for the *Kestrel's Luck* — Sefa had named her, though she wouldn't say what the luck was for — to lie against the hull plates with lines fore and aft to roots the size of a man's leg.

Up close, the Corvane stopped being a shape and became a graveyard of details. Riveted plate gone to rust-bloom and velvet moss. A cargo crane folded against the superstructure like a broken finger. And along the waterline, where the river licked at the steel with every swell, a name in flaking gilt letters, mostly eaten: **CORV**— and then nothing, the rest of it drowned or rotted away.

Nobody spoke for a moment. Even the river seemed to lower its voice.

"She went down in '09," I said finally, because someone had to say something and my voice was steadier than theirs would be. "Officially, cargo shift in the narrows during spring flood. Unofficially—" I stopped myself. Unofficially was a story I wasn't ready to tell them yet. Some of it I'd already told, on the third tide, in the dark, with Brannoc's hand heavy on my shoulder afterward like a paperweight holding me down into honesty. But not all of it. Not the part that mattered most.

"Unofficially," Odile finished for me, "she was carrying things my father helped put aboard. And things he never wanted found again." She turned from the rail. "Dive stations. Tamsin, you're with me. Sefa, surface watch — anything moves on this river, you pull us out whether we want pulling or not. Brannoc, keep the engine warm. Callum—"

"I'll work the air line," Callum said. Too fast. Too smooth. "Someone has to."

I looked at him a beat too long, and he met my eyes with nothing behind his own, and I filed it away in the place where I kept all the things I didn't have time to be afraid of yet.

* * *

The water was warmer than I expected and darker than I liked.

We went down the starboard side on a shot line Odile had rigged from a mooring cleat, hand over hand through water that went from tea-colored to black in the space of six feet. My lamp carved a cone out of the murk and found — deck plating. Dry-side plating, tilted fifteen degrees, furred with silt but whole. The hull breach the inquiry reports had described was on the port side, buried under forty feet of riverbank, and the starboard seals had done what seals do when they're built by people who cared about their work.

Which meant the Corvane hadn't been sunk by accident after all. Ships that sink by accident don't get sealed shut first.

I tapped Odile's arm and pointed, and she saw it too — the telltale gleam of weld beads along a maintenance hatch, fresh-looking even under nine years of silt. Someone had sealed this ship deliberately, from the outside, knowing exactly which compartments mattered. Knowing what was inside them.

Her hand found mine in the dark and squeezed once, hard enough to hurt, and let go.

The hatch gave on the third try, exhaling a bubble-cloud of trapped air that smelled of rust and old oil and something else underneath — paper, dry and faintly sweet, the smell of a room that time had forgotten to enter. We pulled ourselves through into blackness and hung there while our lamps swept the space.

A corridor. Intact. Deck canted, overhead dripping condensation, but intact — and along both walls, stenciled in paint that the dark water had preserved like amber:

**STRONGROOM ACCESS — AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL ONLY**

Beyond it, a second door. Steel, five inches thick, with a combination lock the size of a dinner plate. Salt crust in the mechanism. Odile produced a key from around her neck — her father's key, she'd told us on the fourth tide, the one thing she'd taken from his estate besides his debts — and it fit the secondary hasp, but the combination lock was another matter.

I got my tools out in the black water, working by feel and lamplight, and it took eleven minutes that felt like eleven hours, my fingers going stupid with cold, the air line tugging at my shoulder harness like a patient child. Somewhere above us, muffled by steel and river, I thought I heard thunder. Or engines. I couldn't tell, and I decided not to decide.

The lock opened with a clunk I felt in my teeth rather than heard.

* * *

Inside the strongroom, the water had never been.

That was the thing I couldn't get past, floating there in the doorway with my lamp beam trembling — the compartment was sealed so perfectly that nine years of river had never touched its contents. Dry air, stale as a tomb. Shelves bolted to the walls. Crates stenciled with shipping codes I recognized from a lifetime ago, from manifests I'd once been handed by investigators who wanted me to explain cargo I'd never seen.

And in the center of the compartment, strapped to a frame built for the purpose, a document case. Government gray. Sealed with wax and wire and a stamp I knew better than my own handwriting: the Kessarin Valley Rail Authority, Office of the Chief Engineer.

My office. My signature block. The job they'd taken from me along with my name.

"Tamsin." Odile's voice came distorted through her regulator, thin as a ghost's. She was pointing at the crates along the wall. One had been pried open — recently, the metal bright at the edges — and was empty except for packing straw. Another stood open beside it, and in it, wrapped in oilcloth, were bundles of paper. Original bills of lading. Loading orders. And a ledger, bound in canvas, its spine cracked, its pages filled with a columned hand I recognized from the inquiry transcripts.

Halvard's company. Halvard's own freight office. The Corvane had been carrying the paper trail of the engineered collapse — the demolition charges logged as ballast, the bridge timbers pre-cut for failure, the insurance instruments bought weeks before the span fell — and Halvard had spent nine years and at least one expedition trying to make sure it stayed at the bottom of the Kessarin.

Except he'd been here. The pried crate proved it. He'd found the strongroom — or part of it — and he'd taken what he could carry and sealed the rest, because the original documents were the one thing his forged report couldn't survive. His forgery blamed the engineer. These pages named the buyer.

I got the document case loose from its straps and pressed it into Odile's arms, and we wrapped the ledger and the lading bundles in oilcloth and stuffed them into the dive bags we'd brought for exactly this, and all the while the water outside the hull was rising — I could feel it now, a pressure change in my ears, the river climbing the starboard plates inch by inch as the monsoon's outer bands dumped themselves onto the escarpment country and sent everything downhill at once.

Grim vindication. That was the phrase that surfaced in my mind as we worked, and it fit like a glove two sizes too small. Ten years I'd wanted this — proof, paper, the truth in an authority's own seal — and now I had it, and it weighed nothing in the water and everything everywhere else, and the price of carrying it out was a race against a river that Halvard owned the taps to.

We were halfway up the shot line when Sefa's signal cable went taut and jangling against my harness. Three pulls. Pause. Three pulls.

Emergency. Come up. Now.

* * *

We broke the surface into rain.

Not monsoon rain — not yet — but the outer bands, wind-driven sheets that came across the gorge in slanting gray walls and turned the world into water and noise. The *Kestrel's Luck* was bucking against her mooring lines, and Sefa was hauling on our tether with both hands, shouting something the wind ate alive, pointing upstream.

Brannoc got us aboard. I got the dive bags below before I got my own breath back, and then I climbed to the wheelhouse, dripping, and looked where he was looking.

Two miles upstream, where the gorge bent and the far cliffs cut off the view, lights were coming around the bend. Running lights, white and green, riding high and steady against the rain. Three of them. Behind them, faint as a bruise under the cloud base, the black silhouette of something bigger — a tender, or the fleet's salvage hulk itself, wallowing downriver with the flood it had released.

"He opened the upper gates again," Brannoc said. His voice was very calm, the way it went calm when things were worst. "Half a tide early. That's not a flood, lad, that's a message. He knows we're here. He's known since the bells."

Odile came up the companionway with the document case locked in her arms and rain streaming off her chin, and she looked at those lights the way a woman looks at a debt collector crossing her threshold, and then she looked at me.

"You have it," she said. It wasn't a question. "All of it?"

"All of it," I said. "Every page that matters."

"Then we have until the water reaches the mooring lines," she said, "to become the most valuable hostages in the history of this river. Brannoc — engine. Sefa — cast off the moment I say. Callum—"

Callum wasn't in the wheelhouse.

I noticed it the way you notice a missing tooth — a wrongness in something you'd stopped thinking about because it had always been there. I turned, and Sefa turned, and Brannoc's hand moved toward the engine telegraph and stopped.

The rain hammered the deck. The lights upstream grew by inches.

"Callum," Odile said again, louder, into the wind.

And from somewhere aft, faint under the rain, came the sound of the skiff's davit running free.
