## 19. Sixth Tide

The monsoon did not arrive so much as it descended — a wall of grey that swallowed the escarpment whole, rain falling not in drops but in ropes, in cables, as if the sky had been cut and everything in it was spilling out at once.

Tamsin stood at the bow of the *Kestrel's Daughter* and let it hit him. There was no point sheltering. In an hour there would be no dry place left on the river, and in two the gorge they were floating through would be a chute of brown violence, and every plan he had made in the last six tides would either hold or come apart like wet paper.

Behind him, Sefa had both hands on the wheel, her knuckles white against the spokes, her lips moving in some old prayer to a river god whose name she'd never told him. Brannoc crouched over the engine hatch, feeding it coal by hand because the stoker mechanism had jammed two tides ago and there was no time to fix it properly. Callum was lashed to the mast with the documents — three oilskin packets, strapped across his chest like a bandolier, his face the color of the water.

And above them, wedged into the throat of the gorge four hundred meters ahead, hung the old viaduct.

Tamsin had been staring at it since dawn, when the first outer bands of the storm had torn the mist open just long enough to show him what the river had been hiding. The Kessarin Gorge narrowed here to less than sixty meters between walls of black granite, and spanning that gap, a hundred feet above the present waterline, ran the skeleton of the Corvane Line's great engineering folly — the Upper Kessarin Viaduct, abandoned forty years ago when the mountain line closed, its stone piers still rooted in the channel itself, its iron deck half-collapsed, hanging like a broken rib cage over the water.

He had built things like it once. He had studied them for ten years afterward, in exile, in the small hours, tracing the failure points of structures he'd never touch again. He knew how they died. He knew exactly where to hit one so that it fell the way he wanted.

The fleet was behind them. He could hear it — three black-hulled ships running without lights, their engines a low animal growl under the rain, closing the last two miles of gorge. Halvard had sealed the upper gorge at the fifth tide; there was no way forward except through the viaduct's piers, and no way back except past Halvard's guns.

Which meant there was only one direction left, and it was down.

"Sefa!" he shouted over the storm. "I need you to put us against the north wall — the ledge below the second pier!"

She didn't ask why. That was the thing about Sefa he would never be able to explain to anyone who hadn't been on this river with her: she read water the way other people read faces, and she had already understood, somewhere in the last hour, that the viaduct was not scenery. She hauled the wheel over and the boat swung, groaning, toward the black wall where a shelf of rock jutted out beneath the ruined pier — wide enough for a hull, if the hull was willing to scrape.

Brannoc looked up from the engine, coal dust streaked across his face like war paint. "Tell me you're not thinking what I think you're thinking."

"The piers are keyed at the base," Tamsin said. "Corvane-era construction — they set demolition chambers in the footings when they built it, in case the line ever needed to be dropped fast. Flood control, military retreat, whatever they told themselves. The chambers are still there. I can see the access plates from here."

"And you know this how?"

"Because I wrote my thesis on Corvane gorge works." He almost laughed. Ten years of hiding his name, and it turned out the name was the one useful thing he owned. "Because I've spent a decade knowing exactly how the things I loved were built to fail."

Brannoc held his gaze a long moment, rain hammering off his shoulders. Then he nodded once, jerked his chin at the crowbar lashed beside the engine hatch, and went back to shoveling coal.

The boat kissed the ledge with a shriek of timber on granite. Tamsin was over the rail before it stopped moving, rope around his waist, Sefa and Brannoc paying it out behind him while the river tried to peel him off the rock. The climb to the pier footing was thirty feet of wet stone and old iron rungs, most of them rusted to lace. He climbed it with the crowbar slung across his back and the storm trying to comb him off the cliff with fingers of water, and he did not look down, because below him the sixth tide was already coming — he could feel it in the rock, a deep vibration, the river upstream gathering itself like a fist.

The access plate was where the drawings said it would be. Forty years of silt had welded it shut, but the crowbar found the seam, and Tamsin threw everything he had — ten years of running, one night of confession, every debt he'd ever owed — into the lever, and the plate tore free with a scream of metal.

Inside: the chamber. Dry, impossibly, after four decades. Corvane engineers had built better than they'd ever been credited for. The charge sat in its cradle like a sleeping thing, wires green with age, the primer socket crusted but intact.

No detonator. Of course no detonator. But Tamsin Marchetti had spent ten years working river salvage jobs under false papers, and a man who hides learns a hundred trades, and one of them, one long winter on the lower Vell, had been blasting.

He was wiring the charge to the spare battery cell from the boat's searchlight when the first shot rang out.

* * *

It came from downstream — a flat crack swallowed almost instantly by the rain, then another, then the deeper voice of something that wasn't a rifle. Tamsin flattened himself against the pier and looked back down the gorge.

The fleet had arrived.

Three ships filled the channel below the viaduct like a hand closing. The lead vessel — the same black tender that had overtaken them at the third tide, Renn Halvard standing at its rail beneath an umbrella someone else held — had come to a stop a hundred meters short of the *Kestrel's Daughter*, and men along its deck were aiming upward, at the boat, at the ledge, at the figure clinging to the pier.

"Hold your fire!" Halvard's voice carried impossibly well, amplified by the gorge walls, by the storm's strange acoustics. "Marchetti! I know it's you! Step out where I can see you!"

Tamsin stepped out.

There was a moment — he would remember it all his life — where he simply stood on the ruined pier of the Corvane Line, in the teeth of the sixth tide, and looked down at the man who had erased him. Halvard hadn't aged the way powerful men were supposed to age. He was still trim, still silver-templed, still wearing that expression of patient, reasonable disappointment, as though the world were a ledger that kept failing to balance and only he could see why.

"You're making this difficult," Halvard called up. "It doesn't have to be. Give me the manifests, give me the woman's claim papers, and walk away. You've been walking away for ten years. It's the one thing you're good at."

The words landed exactly where they were aimed. Tamsin felt the old flinch start in his chest — the flinch that had lived in him since the inquiry, since the forged report, since the day a bridge fell and a man he'd never met signed his name into ruin.

But that was before the crew. Before Brannoc saying *we all signed papers we didn't read*. Before Sefa reading the river for him. Before Callum carrying documents against his heart like a man carrying a child.

"I stopped running at the third tide," Tamsin called back. "You just haven't caught up yet."

He touched the wire leads in his pocket. Below, the river was rising — visibly now, the sixth tide arriving, the water climbing the pier bases at a rate no natural flood matched. Halvard had opened the upper gates again. He was going to flush them out, drown the ledge, take the boat intact. It was what Tamsin would have done. It was what the man in the forged report would have done.

"Last chance, engineer," Halvard said, and there it was — the word, the title, spoken aloud for the first time in ten years by anyone but Tamsin himself. Not an accusation. An offer. *Come back into the world. Come back on my terms.*

Tamsin looked down at the *Kestrel's Daughter*. Sefa stood at the rail looking up at him, rain streaming off her, and she raised one hand — not waving. Showing him the river's signal, the one she used for *the water is honest now*. Whatever happened next, the river was done lying.

He closed the circuit.

* * *

The charge in the pier footing did not explode so much as exhale. Forty-year-old powder, damp-compromised, fired at perhaps half its rated force — which was, as it turned out, exactly enough. The north pier did not shatter. It sighed. A crack opened along its base with a sound like the mountain clearing its throat, and then the whole pier leaned, ponderously, the way a tired man leans against a doorframe, and came apart into the channel.

Forty tons of dressed granite dropped into sixty meters of gorge, and the sixth tide, arriving at that exact moment, took it and ran.

The debris fan spread across the channel in seconds — not a dam, not a seal, but a reef, a jagged spine of broken pier and twisted iron lying just beneath the flood surface, right where any hull drawing more than a meter would open itself like a letter. Tamsin watched from the ledge as the lead tender's pilot saw it too late, engines slamming full astern, the black hull slewing sideways in the current, missing the reef by the width of a coffin.

All three ships were trapped. Upstream: the reef, the rising tide, the wreck of the Corvane buried in the bank above it. Downstream: the gorge narrowing, the flood building, and nowhere for three deep-draught vessels to turn.

Halvard's umbrella had blown away. He stood at the rail gripping it, hair plastered flat, staring at the reef with the expression of a man watching arithmetic betray him.

"You've destroyed the channel," he shouted, and for the first time his voice cracked, just slightly, on the last word. "You've destroyed a navigable river to save yourself—"

"No," Tamsin called down. "I destroyed a river somebody else already stole. Ask your weir-keepers. Ask the crews you burned out of base camp. Ask the eleven people on the expedition you hunted through five tides." He pulled the second battery cell from his coat and held it up. "And I've got enough charge left to drop the south pier too. Your choice whether your ships are trapped or sunk."

Silence, except for the rain and the river. On the tender's deck, the riflemen looked at each other. Men follow money, Tamsin thought, but money drowns like everyone else.

Halvard raised a hand, and the guns lowered.

* * *

They met on the flooding deck of the tender, because Halvard refused to come to the ledge and Tamsin refused to board without terms, and so the negotiation happened in the one place neither man controlled — a rope-and-plank gangway rigged between the tender's rail and the rocks, slick with rain, swaying over water that was eating the gorge alive.

Odile Vantrease stood at Tamsin's shoulder. She had insisted, and he had not argued; there were things in this conversation that belonged to her blood. Callum waited on the boat with the documents, and Brannoc stood at the engine telegraph with his hand on the lever, and everyone understood the geometry: whatever happened on the gangway, the truth stayed with the crew.

"You can't prove anything," Halvard said. Rain ran off his chin and he didn't wipe it. "Paper burns. Witnesses drown. I've been managing this river for twenty years, Marchetti. Do you imagine one survey boat changes the arithmetic?"

"It's not arithmetic anymore." Tamsin's voice was steady, and he marveled at that, distantly, the way you marvel at a bridge holding weight it was never rated for. "It's a timetable. Yours is finished. The river authority has a copy of the manifests — Sefa's cousin runs the pilot station at Vellmouth, and a coracle went downstream at the fourth tide with a packet sewn into its ballast. By tomorrow morning, your forgery is public record. By next week, the inquiry reopens. You can't buy the monsoon, Halvard. It doesn't sign contracts."

Something moved behind Halvard's eyes — not fear, not yet. Recalculation. The terrible patience of a man who had always found the price of everything.

"Then we discuss what the truth costs," he said softly. "Your crew walks free. The girl — Brannoc's daughter — released tonight. The Vantrease claim honored. Everyone keeps what they love. All it costs is the documents stay buried with the ship, and you—" the smile, thin as wire, "—you go back to being nobody. Which is what you are. What you've always chosen to be."

Tamsin felt Odile go rigid beside him. And he understood, with a clarity that felt almost like calm, that this was the real offer — it had always been the real offer, from the forged report onward. Not death. Oblivion. *Be no one, and live.* The trade he'd been making for ten years without ever seeing the contract.

The river chose that moment to speak. A swell came up the gorge — the true crest of the sixth tide — and lifted the tender on its mooring lines, and the gangway bucked, and water came over the tender's deck in a sheet that swept the feet of every man on it. Somewhere below, iron groaned. The Corvane, buried in the bank, was shifting in the flood, giving up its dead to the current at last.

"No deal," Tamsin said.

"Tamsin—" Odile started.

"He needs to hear the rest." Tamsin stepped closer on the swaying planks, close enough to see the rain beading in Halvard's silver eyebrows, close enough to say the last thing quietly. "Here's my timetable. In one hour, this deck is underwater. In two, your ships are on the reef or on the bottom, and your men are swimming, and men swim away from the man who spent their lives cheapest first. You have one hour to decide whether you negotiate with the river authority from a ship's bridge or from a prison dock. I don't care which. I've got a boat, a crew, and a name I'm taking back. You've got nothing the water wants."

He turned and walked back along the gangway, and the rain came down like applause, like judgment, like weather — just weather, finally, nothing more.

Behind him, for a long moment, Halvard said nothing at all.

Then, as Tamsin reached the rocks, the magnate's voice followed him up the gorge, stripped of its patience at last, raw as a boy's:

"The south pier won't save you! Nothing saves you! The river takes everything — it took your bridge, it'll take your truth, it takes *everything*—"

Tamsin stopped. Turned. And found, to his own surprise, that he was smiling.

"Not everything," he said. "It kept me."

Then the crest of the sixth tide hit the gorge, the world went white with spray, and the *Kestrel's Daughter* cast off her lines and turned her bow toward the wreck — because the documents aboard her were only copies, and the original manifest, the one no court would doubt, was still sealed in the Corvane's strongroom, and the water that was coming to drown them all was also, at long last, coming to give it back.
