## 20. What the River Keeps

The river did not celebrate.

Sefa had expected it to, somehow — some great exhale, some turning of the brown water silver, a sign the way her grandmother would have read one in the flick of a fish or the shape of a cloud. But the Kessarin ran on as it always ran, heavy with monsoon, indifferent to what had been decided on its banks. It carried silt and branches and the drowned smell of the jungle, and it carried, somewhere beneath its surface, the Corvane.

That was the thing Sefa kept returning to as she sat on the gunwale of their battered boat, watching the gorge walls slide past: the river had taken the ship the way it took everything, without malice and without ceremony. One morning there had been a wreck with secrets in its belly. The next morning there was only water.

She found she could not decide whether that was mercy or theft.

* * *

They had tied up below the old customs station at Vell's Landing, where the river authority kept a corrugated-iron office and a man named Deputy Ostrava who chewed betel and asked questions slowly, as though each word cost him money. Tamsin had gone in at dawn with the documents wrapped in oilcloth against his chest like a child, and Sefa had watched him go, and she had watched Odile watch him go too, from the deck, her arm bound in a sling of torn sailcloth, her face gray as ash but her spine still straight as a mast.

Odile Vantrease did not sit down. That was the first thing Sefa understood about grief, watching her: some people wore their wounds standing up.

The bullet had gone through the meat of her shoulder on the flooding deck of the tender, in the last minutes before Halvard's men lost their nerve and the water won the argument for everyone. Callum had held her head in his lap and talked nonsense to her — about the estate gardens, about horses she didn't own, about anything — while Brannoc lashed the wound and Sefa pressed the cloth and prayed to no god in particular, because her gods were river gods and river gods did not come inland for gunfights. But Odile had lived. Odile had opened her eyes and looked past all of them at the churning channel where the viaduct's wreckage dammed the fleet in place, and said, in a voice scraped down to wire:

"Did he get them out?"

Not *am I dying*. Not *how bad*. Did the documents get out. Sefa had thought, pressing the cloth, that this woman had been built entirely of questions like that, and that it must be a cold way to live, and a strong one.

Now they waited. Waiting was a thing Sefa knew how to do; she had been raised on it, on the long doldrums when the nets hung empty and the family sat in the hull-bottom and told stories to keep the hunger quiet. So she waited, and mended net that didn't need mending, and listened to the rain come down through the canopy in its thousand small voices.

Brannoc sat across the deck from her, sharpening a knife that was already sharp. He had not spoken much since the arrest. When the river authority boats had come up through the broken gorge — slow, official, flying the gray pennant — and taken Halvard off his own stranded tender in handcuffs that gleamed absurdly bright against his black coat, Brannoc had turned away and been sick over the side. Not from the wound in his ribs. Sefa had seen seasickness and bullet-sickness and fever-sickness, and this had been none of those. This had been ten years coming up out of him.

"You'll wear a groove in that blade," she said now, in the river-tongue, because some things were easier said sideways.

He grunted. "Better than wearing a groove in my skull."

"She's alive," Sefa said. "Your daughter."

"I know." The knife whispered along the stone. "Halvard sent word this morning. Through the deputy. She's to be released — the debt's void, he says, since the debt was only ever leverage, and leverage is worthless once the man holding it is in irons." He laughed, a short ugly sound with nothing in it. "Ten years I rowed cargo for that man because of a piece of paper. And the paper dies the moment he does — meaning the moment he's caught. All that fear, and it was only ever rented."

"What will you do?" she asked. "When you see her."

Brannoc stopped sharpening. He looked out at the water, at the place where the current bent around the sunken viaduct stone and made a long white scar on the river's face, and for a moment Sefa saw him as he must have been at twenty — big and easy and unafraid, before the debt bent him into the shape of a man who watched every horizon for black hulls.

"I'm going to tell her the truth," he said. "All of it. Even the parts that make me small." He resumed the sharpening, slower now. "Turns out that's the only cargo worth carrying."

* * *

Tamsin came back at midday with the oilcloth empty and his hands shaking, and Sefa pretended not to notice either thing, because that was the courtesy of the river: you let people arrive whole before you asked what pieces they'd left behind.

"It's done," he said. "Ostrava wired the regional court at Kessarin Mouth. They're sending an inquiry team up within the week. Halvard's ships are impounded where they sit — which is nowhere, thanks to—" he gestured vaguely upstream, at the viaduct, at the whole improbable architecture of what Tamsin had done with six tides of knowledge and one collapsing bridge, "—that."

"And the manifests?" Odile asked from her place by the rail. She had refused the cot below. Sefa suspected she would refuse cots for the rest of her life.

"Copied, sealed, witnessed. The originals go downriver under guard tomorrow." Tamsin sat down heavily on a crate, and for a moment the engineer's certainty fell off him like a wet coat, and underneath was just a tired man of forty-odd years who had spent a decade being someone else. "It's real, Odile. It's going to hold. Your father's name is in the partnership ledger, dated eleven years back. Halvard can't forge his way around a second set of books."

Something moved through Odile's face then — Sefa watched it the way she watched weather. Not relief. Relief would come later, maybe, if it came at all. What moved through her was closer to the look of a woman reading a letter from a dead man. Her father had been three years in his grave, and now here was his handwriting again, in a ledger, in ink, buying shares in a murder.

"He used to take me on the inspection runs," Odile said, to no one, to the rail, to the water. "When I was small. He'd lift me up to touch the couplings and say, *this is how civilization holds together, Odile — iron and schedules.*" Her mouth did something that was almost a smile and mostly wasn't. "He never mentioned what else holds it together."

Callum came up from below with tea, four tin cups balanced on a board, and the small domestic act of it — the nephew who had betrayed them in his heart and chosen them with his feet, carrying tea in the rain — undid something in the air. Brannoc took a cup. Tamsin took a cup. Odile took hers in her good hand and held it without drinking, warming her fingers, looking into it as though the leaves might arrange themselves into an answer.

Sefa drank hers and tasted the river in it, because everything tasted of the river now, and would forever.

* * *

In the afternoon the rain slackened and Sefa went alone in the skiff, upstream, to look at the place where the Corvane had been.

She told herself it was practical — she wanted to see what the flood had done to the bank, whether the moorings below the wreck-site would hold for the trip down, whether the channel had shifted. All true. None of it the reason.

The outer bands of the monsoon had done their work well. The river stood two fathoms higher than when they'd first run this stretch, and it ran faster, and where the Corvane's bow had jutted from the bank like a whale's skull it now showed only a hand's width of black iron, streaming, already furred with fresh silt. By next week it would be gone. By the end of the season even Sefa would not have been able to say exactly where it lay, and the river would keep it the way it kept everything — completely, and without telling.

She shipped the oars and let the skiff drift, and she spoke to it. To the ship. To the dead inside it, because there had been dead — the salvage crew Halvard had marooned years ago rather than share what they'd found, the ones whose bones the expedition had documented with shaking hands and a failing radio. Her grandmother had taught her that you speak to the drowned in their own country's language, so Sefa spoke in the river-tongue, low, the words half prayer and half apology.

*We got them out,* she said. *The papers. The names. It isn't justice — the river knows there's no such thing, the river takes the guilty and the innocent in the same flood — but it's true, and truth is the only cargo we ever carry that doesn't rot.*

The iron rib of the bow slipped under a curl of brown water and did not answer, and did not need to.

She understood then, drifting, why the ache in her chest was not all grief. Some of it was release. For nine days — for the whole mad length of it, the charges in the tunnel, the weir, the wires strung in the Gorge of Bells, the portage through leech-hung green dark with Callum unconscious across Brannoc's shoulders — she had carried the feeling that the river itself had been lied to. That men had put their hands on the water and made it do things water should not do: held back behind illegal weirs, released as a weapon, made to drown and strand and starve on schedule. It had offended something older in her than belief. And now the hands were off. The weirs would be torn out by court order; the tides would run on their own clock again; the river would be a river and not a machine.

Some things are won only by letting them sink. She hadn't known until this moment that she believed that, but drifting above the disappearing ship, she found it was true. If they had raised the Corvane — hauled it, salvaged it, put it in some magnate's museum behind glass — the proof would have become property, and property can be bought. Drowned, it had become testimony. The river had held the evidence safe the way only the river holds things: by making it impossible to steal.

She dipped her hand in the water and let it trail, cold and strong, and turned the skiff back downstream.

* * *

Odile was at the rail when she returned, and Sefa tied up beside the boat and climbed aboard and stood beside her, and for a while neither of them said anything, because they were, despite everything, alike in this: both of them watchers, both of them women who trusted weather more than words.

"The deputy says the inquiry team will want statements," Odile said finally. "From all of us. Under our own names." A pause. "I understand if yours has changed. Given the company you've kept."

Sefa considered this. "My name is Sefa. My mother named me for the tide that comes just before the big one — the one nobody sees coming. It's the only name I've got and it's always been enough." She looked at Odile's sling, at the bloodstain that had dried brown on the sailcloth. "Will you go back? To the estate. The company."

"The company is finished," Odile said, flatly, and then, quieter, with something underneath the flatness that Sefa recognized as the sound of a door opening after years locked: "Which means I am. Finished as I was built, anyway. Whatever comes next, I'll have to build it myself, out of whatever's left." She turned her head, and her eyes were dry and very clear. "Do you know, when I funded that expedition, I told myself it was about salvage rights. About what was owed my father's name. I think maybe I just wanted to find the bottom of the story. You can't build on ground you haven't sounded."

"The river says the same thing," Sefa said. "About sounding."

Odile almost smiled. Almost. "I'm beginning to think the river says quite a lot, and I've simply never learned to listen."

"Most people haven't." Sefa looked downstream, toward Vell's Landing, toward the mouth, toward the whole long patient journey of the water to the sea. "It doesn't mind. It keeps talking anyway."

* * *

Evening came violet through the rain-haze, and they ate together on deck — rice and river fish Brannoc caught with a patience that seemed to steady him, and the last of the good tea, hoarded for a celebration none of them had known how to name until now. Callum told the story of the portage badly and bravely, making himself the fool of it, giving the heroism away to Brannoc and Sefa and the mules they didn't have, and everyone let him, because they understood it was his way of paying. Tamsin sat with his cup and listened and occasionally corrected a detail — *it was a shear-pin, not a coupling* — and each correction was a small gift, proof that he was present, that the man who had spent ten years leaving was staying, at least through dinner.

Later, when the others had turned in, Sefa stayed up and kept the anchor watch, because it was hers by habit and because she wanted the night to herself.

The rain had thinned to a fine drifting mist. Upstream, the broken viaduct made its white scar in the current, and beyond it the gorge walls rose black against a sky just beginning, at its eastern edge, to consider clearing. Somewhere downriver, in a cell or a guarded cabin, Renn Halvard was learning what it was to be held by something stronger than money. Somewhere south, a daughter was waiting for a father who was finally, actually, coming home. Somewhere in the dark water beneath her keel, a ship full of dead men's secrets was settling into the silt that would keep it better than any vault.

Sefa put her hand on the gunwale and felt the river's pulse come up through the wood — that deep, unhurried beat, older than every timetable and every forgery, keeping its own time the way it always had.

*We won,* she thought, and then corrected herself, the way her grandmother would have corrected her, tapping the tiller: *No. We survived, and the truth survived, and the river kept what it keeps.* That was not winning. That was something rarer and harder to spend.

She was still holding that thought, turning it over like a smooth stone, when she heard footsteps on the deck behind her — Tamsin, wrapped in a blanket, moving quietly as a man who had spent years not wanting to be noticed. He settled at the rail a few feet away and looked upstream at the scar of white water, and neither of them spoke for a long time.

Then he said, "The inquiry team will want to talk to me first. About the collapse. Eleven years ago." His voice was steady, but Sefa heard the current under it. "Under my own name. Tamsin Marchetti, engineer, in front of the court, on the record." He exhaled. "I keep waiting to feel afraid, and it hasn't come. I don't know what to do with that."

Sefa looked at him — this land-man, this bridge-man, this runner who had washed up among them forged and frightened and had turned, tide by tide, into something that belonged — and she gave him the only blessing her people had for moments like this.

"The river's not afraid of arriving," she said. "Only of stopping."

Downstream, far off toward the mouth, thunder rolled — soft, distant, the monsoon speaking in its sleep — and Tamsin Marchetti nodded slowly, as if he had finally, after eleven years and six tides, begun to learn the language.
