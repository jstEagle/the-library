## 21. Testimony

The hearing room smelled of paper and river damp, the way old buildings downriver always did — as if the Kessarin had gotten into the mortar decades ago and never left. Tamsin Marchetti sat at a witness table scarred by generations of elbows, and waited for them to say his name.

Not *Marchetti*. Not the forged credential, not the alias he'd worn like a coat through ten winters of junction towns and closed lines. His real name, spoken aloud by a stranger with no reason to flatter it: *Tamsin Vey Marchetti, engineer of record, Corvane Line, Third District.*

It had taken him eleven days to get here — downriver on Odile's chartered packet, past the drowned gorge where the viaduct's bones still broke the current into white teeth, past the salvage fleet's impounded tenders riding at anchor under authority flags they'd never expected to wear. Eleven days of Sefa bringing him tea without being asked, of Brannoc saying nothing at all in the particular way that meant everything, of Callum hovering at the rail of an evening wanting to apologize for something Tamsin had already decided not to hold against him.

Eleven days was nothing. It had taken ten years to travel this distance.

The room held perhaps forty people: three commissioners behind a raised bench, clerks with their nibs scratching, a scattering of observers — river authority men in gray, a few journalists whose pencils hovered hungrily, and in the second row, hands folded in her lap like a woman attending a funeral she'd paid for, Odile Vantrease. Her arm was still bound from the sixth tide. She'd refused the surgeon's suggestion that she wait in her cabin. *I bought this hearing,* she'd said on the packet, not quite meeting his eyes. *Least I can do is sit through it.*

Behind the commissioners, mounted on an easel, hung the original manifests from the Corvane's strongroom, sealed in glass now, each page photographed and numbered. Tamsin could read them upside down from where he sat. He had been reading them, in one form or another, for a decade — in nightmares, in the forged report that ended his career, in every locked door and averted gaze between here and the coast.

"Mr. Marchetti." The middle commissioner, a dry woman named Halloway with spectacles that caught the lamplight, consulted a folder without opening it. "You understand the terms. You are testifying under your own name, restored to you by writ of the River Authority following verification of the documents recovered from the wreck of the *Corvane*. Your testimony today concerns the collapse of the Third District bridge, eleven years past, and its causes."

"I understand."

"You were the engineer of record."

"I was."

"And the official inquiry at the time concluded—" she paused, and there was something almost gentle in it, or perhaps just tired— "that your survey figures were falsified. That load calculations were altered. That you fled the district before the inquiry concluded, and were thereafter considered absconded."

The room made a small sound, the sound of forty people not quite breathing.

"That is what the report says," Tamsin said. "I wrote none of it."

He told them then. He had rehearsed nothing; he had promised himself he wouldn't. He simply stood — because some things you say sitting and some things you owe standing — and began where it had actually begun, which was not with the bridge but with a Tuesday in a survey office, when a courier from Halvard Consolidated Salvage delivered a revised load schedule with a signature on it that wasn't his, and a supervisor who wouldn't meet his eye and said, *Sign where indicated, Marchetti. The company handles discrepancies.*

"I refused," Tamsin said. "I filed the original figures with the district registrar the same afternoon. I have since learned — from the manifests recovered from the *Corvane* — that those original figures never reached the inquiry. They were intercepted. Replaced. The man who carried them, a registrar's assistant named Pell Ostrand, is listed in the salvage fleet's own ledgers as receiving payment dated four days after the collapse. He drowned two years later in a boating accident I no longer believe was an accident."

He watched the commissioners' faces do what faces did when a story stopped being about one guilty man and became about a machine. Halloway's pen stopped moving. The youngest commissioner, a heavyset man with river-pilot's shoulders, leaned forward until his forearms creaked on the bench.

"The bridge failed," Tamsin continued, "because the piers were poured over a scour channel the company knew about and chose not to remediate. The revised schedule moved freight tonnage onto a structure that should have been restricted to light traffic pending repairs. Forty-one people died. I have carried their names for ten years. I would like to read them into the record, if the commission permits."

Halloway looked at him for a long moment. "Proceed."

So he did. All forty-one, slowly, in the hush of that paper-smelling room, while rain worked at the high windows and the clerks' nibs went silent out of something that wasn't procedure. Some of the names he knew personally — the brakeman who'd taught him cards, the stationmistress's daughter, a crew of six off the morning freight. Others he knew only from the memorial wall in a town that had stopped letting him look at it. Saying them aloud didn't lighten anything, exactly. But it redistributed the weight. For ten years he had carried them as accusations. Now they went into the record as what they had always been: people, killed by decisions, and the decisions had authors.

When he finished, the room stayed quiet long enough that somewhere above the ceiling a beam ticked, settling, the way old buildings did when they finally stopped holding themselves stiff.

* * *

They broke at midday. Odile found him on the hearing house steps, under an awning, watching rain silver the street. She moved carefully, favoring the bound arm, and stood beside him without speaking for a while — a skill he hadn't credited her with, back when she'd been only an imperious heiress with triple pay and a threat.

"My father's name is in those ledgers too," she said at last. "Page sixty-one. Partnership shares, dated the year before the collapse. Halloway's commission will reach it tomorrow, when they examine the financial documents." She turned her face toward the rain. "I've known since I was nineteen. Found the correspondence in his effects after he died. I spent eleven years and most of my inheritance trying to buy my way to the truth instead of just... walking upriver and looking at it."

"It nearly killed us," Tamsin said. "Walking upriver and looking at it."

"Yes." A ghost of her old imperiousness surfaced. "Well. I've never done anything halfway. Ask anyone."

Down the street, Brannoc stood under the eaves of a chandlery with his arms crossed, pretending to study rope coils, keeping his distance the way he kept everything — close enough to catch you, far enough to pretend he hadn't. Sefa was somewhere with Callum, teaching him river signs, or so she claimed; mostly, Tamsin suspected, keeping him from drowning in his own guilt before the hearing released him from it.

"Halvard testified yesterday," Odile said. "Did you hear?"

"I heard."

Three hours of it, the whole town had heard — the magnate's voice carrying even through stone walls, smooth as dredged gravel, denying everything, citing forged ledgers as fabrications, invoking his lawyers, invoking his rights, invoking, at one point, the memory of the dead as though he owned it. But the manifests were verified. Three separate authorities had authenticated the paper, the ink, the dating. And the weir records — Sefa's river moods made legal, the illegally held water logged in the authority's own gauges — sat in the evidence room like a confession written in water levels.

"He'll be convicted," Odile said. "It's almost disappointing how certain it feels. I imagined this moment for years and it turns out to feel like..." She searched for it. "...like paying off a debt you'd forgotten you owed. Just arithmetic."

"What will you do? After."

She was quiet a moment. Then: "There's a mountain line above the Kessarin. Closed. Six tunnels, a viaduct, and a gorge full of people who know every rock in it better than any surveyor alive." She glanced at him sideways, and for once there was nothing brittle in her face at all. "I'm told it could be rebuilt. Properly. By someone who reads water and steel honestly. If such a person could be found, and funded, and given a crew that doesn't quit..."

Tamsin looked out at the rain. Ten years ago he had walked away from a district in ashes with a forged name in his pocket, and every job since had been temporary by design — signed short, left early, loved nothing. He had thought the running was caution. He understood now it had only been the shape his fear took.

"Afternoon session starts at two," he said. "Ask me again tomorrow."

Odile smiled — really smiled, brief and crooked, the first he'd ever seen on her. "Tomorrow, then."

* * *

The final session ran into the evening. The commissioners examined the intercepted survey figures, the ledger payments, the weir logs, the testimony of the fevered survivor — Ilse Varrow, expedition cartographer, recovered enough now to speak in complete sentences, who gave her account in a clear, unflinching voice that made Tamsin think of Sefa reading the river. When Halloway called him forward a last time, her manner had changed entirely. The folder was open now. Everything was open now.

"One final question, Mr. Marchetti. Not required by procedure, but the commission would hear it. Eleven years ago, you fled this district under a false name rather than stand trial for a crime committed against you. Why come back now? Why testify now?"

The lamps hissed. Rain on the windows. Somewhere outside, the Kessarin moved in the dark, doing what it had always done, keeping what it kept and giving back what it chose.

Tamsin thought about the answer for a moment — about the boat, and the portage, and Brannoc's rough voice saying *we all signed papers we didn't read*, about a gorge full of bells navigated by sound alone, about a woman trading herself for a crew's head start, about a boy carried through leech-hung jungle on the shoulders of strangers who'd known him nine days.

"Because for ten years I believed the truth was a thing I had to outrun," he said. "And then I met people who taught me it was a thing you carry together. I came back because they made it possible to stand still."

He stepped back from the table. Standing still cost nothing he couldn't pay. He had checked the arithmetic himself, line by line, and for the first time in a decade, the figures balanced.

Halloway closed the folder. "This commission finds," she said, and her voice carried to the back benches and through them, out into the wet dark where a river ran toward the sea, "the collapse of the Third District bridge to have been caused by documented corporate negligence and deliberate falsification by Halvard Consolidated Salvage and its principals. The engineer of record, Tamsin Vey Marchetti, bears no responsibility. The record is corrected."

The gavel fell — one clean knock, like a spike driven home.

Outside, the rain had stopped. On the hearing house steps, Brannoc handed him a flask without a word, and Sefa whooped loud enough to startle the pigeons, and Callum laughed for the first time in eleven days. Down the hill, the river ran silver under the lamps, and far upriver, past the drowned gorge and the escarpment turning toward morning, a closed mountain line waited in the dark with six empty tunnels — and someone had already drawn up the first survey schedule, in a hand that trembled only a little, signed plainly:

*T. V. Marchetti, engineer.*
