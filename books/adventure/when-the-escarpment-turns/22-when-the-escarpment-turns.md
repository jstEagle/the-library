## 22. When the Escarpment Turns

The first thing I built with my own hands was a fence post.

Not a bridge span. Not a tunnel crown. Not a viaduct over a gorge that wanted to eat trains alive. A fence post, driven into the red clay above the Kessarin switchbacks, to keep Brannoc's goats out of the survey stakes. It leaned two degrees off plumb and Sefa laughed at it for three days straight, and I let it stand anyway, because for the first time in ten years nothing about my work had to be perfect to be safe. Nothing had to be perfect because nobody was coming to burn it down.

That was the lesson the river taught me, though it took the whole year to sink in: perfection is what frightened men build when they can't build trust.

* * *

One year, almost to the tide.

I stood on the cut bench at dawn where the new line would climb toward the escarpment, boots sunk in mud that smelled of iron and wet grass, and watched the fog peel off the valley in long ribbons. Below me the Kessarin ran high and brown with the last of the monsoon's leavings, carrying silt from the high country down toward the delta, patient as an accountant. Somewhere under that water, past the sixth gorge, the Corvane lay where we'd left her — claimed by the river, as Sefa said she would be. The river authority had dropped a marker buoy over the site in the spring. A formality. No one would ever raise her now. Some things are won only by letting them sink, and some things are kept only by letting them stay lost.

But up here, on the escarpment's shoulder, everything was beginning.

The mountain line had been dead eleven years — closed the same season as the collapse, starved of funding, its tunnels seeded with rot and rumor. The river authority had reopened the charter in the winter, after Halvard's trial ended and the salvage fleet's stranglehold on the upper Kessarin came apart like wet rope. And Odile Vantrease, who had once bought loyalty the way other people bought gloves, put up the money for the rebuild without a single hidden clause. I read the contract four times looking for the trap. There wasn't one. She'd signed it in ink, in her own name, at a public table, with Callum standing behind her chair like a bodyguard who happened to share her jawline.

"You're going to wear a hole in that page," she'd told me, watching me read it the fourth time. "It says what it says, Marchetti. I'm done with paper that lies."

So here we were. The Vantrease Mountain Line, reconstruction phase one: fourteen kilometers of cliffside track, three tunnels, one viaduct across the gorge they still called Bells, because the wires were gone but the name stayed. And me, Tamsin Marchetti, chief engineer, my real name stamped on the drawings this time. Every sheet. In the corner, small and permanent, like a signature on a confession that had finally turned into a promise.

* * *

Brannoc found me at the bench before the sun cleared the ridge, carrying two tin mugs and the expression he wore when he'd been awake since three thinking about grades.

"Your drainage culvert's wrong," he said, handing me a mug. Chicory coffee, bitter enough to strip paint. He drank it that way on purpose, I think, as a form of conversation.

"It's not wrong. It's conservative."

"It's ugly."

"It's a culvert, Brannoc. It's supposed to be underground."

He grunted and settled beside me on the survey timber, knees cracking like green branches. A year ago he'd been a boat pilot running cargo for a man who owned his daughter's debts. Now his daughter worked the tally desk in the lower yard, debt-free, and Brannoc himself had opinions about culverts, which meant he had decided to have a future. That was how you could tell with him. When Brannoc started complaining about infrastructure, he was happy.

"Sefa says the river drops two hands by week's end," he said. "She wants the barge run up Thursday to bring the rail stock through the third gorge while the draft's kind."

"Sefa's usually right."

"Sefa's always right. That's the problem. A man can't argue with a woman who reads water better than he reads his own hands." He sipped. "Callum's got the blasting pattern drafted for Tunnel Two. Wants your sign-off."

"How is it?"

"Clean. Cleaner than mine would've been." Brannoc looked out at the fog. "Boy's come a long way from the nephew who couldn't look a hard day in the eye."

"He carried more than his weight on the portage," I said. "We all knew it then."

"We knew it then. Took him till spring to know it." Brannoc drained his mug and stood, joints announcing themselves. "Sign-off by noon. And Marchetti—" He paused, and there was something underneath the gruffness, the way there always was, load-bearing and invisible. "Good morning, is what I mean. It's a good morning."

It was. That was the strange part. Mornings kept being good, one after another, until I stopped waiting for the catch.

* * *

Tunnel Two breathed cold when we walked it that afternoon, our lamp flames leaning in the draft like a row of bowing servants. Eleven years of dripstone hung from the crown in pale curtains, and the old rails — the ones the closure crews never bothered to pull — ran into the dark ahead of us, furred with mineral bloom, silver as fish bones.

Callum had the blast plan spread on a crate, weighted with four stones and a half-eaten orange. He'd changed in a year the way weather changes a coastline: slowly, then all at once. Shoulders broader. Hands scarred. He still flinched at loud noises — the gorge had given him that, along with the rest of us — but he no longer apologized for the flinch, which was its own kind of engineering.

"The crown's sound for the first six hundred meters," he said, tapping the profile. "Past that, the old fire damage starts. I want to take it in shallow lifts, half charges, and re-bolt as we go. Slower than the contract allows." He glanced up, braced for argument. "I know it puts us behind schedule."

"Schedule's a tool," I said. "Not a master. Shallow lifts it is."

He blinked. A year ago, working for his aunt, he'd have expected the schedule to be wielded against him like a whip. I'd spent enough years under whips myself to know the difference between a plan and a punishment.

"There's one more thing." He turned the drawing over. On the back, in pencil, faint: a sketch of the old viaduct at Bells Gorge. My viaduct. The one I'd brought down across the channel on the sixth tide, with the monsoon screaming and Halvard's fleet sealed below it. "The authority wants a memorial plaque at the new crossing. They asked what wording you'd want."

I looked at the little pencil bridge for a long time.

"What did you tell them?"

"That the engineer would want to choose the words himself." Callum hesitated. "I told them you'd probably want them short."

I laughed — actually laughed, in the cold throat of the tunnel, and the draft took the sound and carried it away into the dark like a train departing. Short. Yes. I'd spent ten years writing long explanations to people who never read them: letters to inquiry boards, appeals, rebuttals of Halvard's forged report, pages and pages proving I existed. Now the truth was in the public record, testified under my own name, witnessed, weighed, and final. Halvard would die in a river-authority prison with a view of the water he'd tried to own. The dead of the original collapse had their names restored. What was left for me to say to a plaque?

Something short.

* * *

Sefa met me at the portal at dusk, and I knew before she spoke that the news wasn't about rail stock.

She had a way of arriving that made the day feel like it had been holding its breath. Barefoot on the cold rock, as always — she claimed boots dulled her feet's hearing, and after the night she'd navigated the Gorge of Bells by sound alone, none of us argued — with her hair tied back and her eyes doing that thing where they looked slightly past you, at the middle distance where rivers keep their secrets.

"Tide-runner came up from the delta today," she said. "Mail and cement. And a passenger."

"A passenger."

"A woman. Surveyor's papers. Says she's been hired to assess the upper gorges for a hydrographic chart." Sefa's mouth curved, not quite a smile. "Her papers are good. Very good. Almost as good as yours used to be."

The old reflex woke in my chest — the cold drop, the counting of exits, the decade-old arithmetic of forged credentials and borrowed names. I felt it move through me like a change in current, and then, just as quickly, I felt it pass. Because that was the difference now. The fear arrived; it simply no longer got to steer.

"Where is she?"

"Yard office. Talking to Odile." Sefa tilted her head. "Odile sent me to fetch you. Said, and I quote, 'Tell Tamsin if it's trouble, it's his kind of trouble, and if it isn't, it's still his kind of trouble.'"

Of course she did.

We walked down through the works together, past the spoil heaps going gold in the last light, past the goat fence with its one crooked post, past the cook tent where Brannoc's daughter was arguing cheerfully with the tally ledger. The escarpment above us caught the sunset the way it did every evening this time of year — the whole cliff face turning to hammered copper, then rose, then a deep burning gold, as if the mountain itself were being paid at last for everything it had held. The locals had a word for it. Sefa said it softly as we walked, the old river-word, *kessarin*, which didn't mean river at all. It meant *the turning*. The moment when what has been dark all day gives up its gold.

The yard office window glowed yellow. Through it I could see Odile at her desk, upright and unhurried, and across from her a figure in a travel-stained coat, hat on the desk, hair short and practical, a leather map case open between them. Even at this distance, even in silhouette, something about the set of her shoulders was familiar in a way that made the hair rise on my arms.

Sefa stopped at the door and didn't come in. She touched my elbow once, lightly, and stepped back into the gold light.

Inside, Odile looked up. Whatever she saw in my face made her rise from her chair — Odile Vantrease, who never rose first for anyone — and say, quietly, "Tamsin. Before you decide anything. Listen to her name."

The woman turned.

And I knew her. Ten years older, gray at the temples, lines around her eyes from years of squinting at gauges and timetables — but I knew her the way you know a chord you haven't heard since childhood. Merren Vasque. Assistant engineer on the Corvane inquiry. The only investigator who voted against Halvard's report, who was reassigned within the month, who vanished from the record so completely I'd assumed for a decade that she was either bought or broken.

She stood, and held out a folio, thick and soft-cornered with travel.

"Marchetti," she said. Her voice hadn't changed at all. "I heard you were building something up here. I've spent the last year making charts of every gorge on the upper Kessarin — depths, currents, flood stages, all of it, proper and public. The river authority wants the mountain line rebuilt right, and rebuilt right means knowing exactly what the water will do." She set the folio on the desk between us. "And there's a second matter. The Corvane's strongroom manifest listed a bonded vault in the delta — Halvard's private archive. The authority recovered it last month. Most of it's ash. But one item survived, sealed in oilcloth."

She opened the folio. Inside, in a clear sleeve, lay a single page — brittle, water-stained, but legible. The original loading order for the Corvane's final voyage. And in the margin, in a hand I recognized from ten years of nightmares, a note initialed R.H., dated nine days before the collapse:

*Cut it when she crosses the third span. The escarpment will do the rest.*

The escarpment will do the rest. The words that had built my exile, that had killed forty-one people, that had chased me across a decade of borrowed names — written down, in his hand, proof beyond any forgery, surfacing now like something the river had decided, after keeping it a full year, to give back.

"They'll want a witness," Merren said. "Someone who was there. Someone whose testimony already carries weight." She looked at me steadily, no pity in it, which I was grateful for. "You don't have to go downriver, Marchetti. You've paid enough. The record stands. This is only — the last stone in the wall."

Outside, the escarpment burned gold to its crown. Down in the yard, Brannoc was shouting amiably about culverts, and Callum's laugh carried up from the cook tent, and Sefa stood in the doorway behind me, feet bare on the threshold, reading whatever moved in the room the way she read water.

A year ago I would have been on the next barge before the question finished forming. Running was the only skill I'd trusted.

But I looked at the page in its sleeve, and I looked at the light on the mountain, and I understood that this was different. This wasn't running. This was the turning — the moment when what has been dark gives up its gold.

"I'll go," I said. "Tomorrow's tide. And I'll be back by the fifth day, because we pour the Tunnel Two crown on the sixth, and I'm not missing it."

Merren almost smiled. Odile sat back down, satisfied, already reaching for the freight schedules.

And outside, over the gorge they called Bells, the escarpment turned — slow, immense, and gold — and for the first time in ten years, everywhere I looked was somewhere I intended to stay.
