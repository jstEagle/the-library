## 1. Salt and Warrants

The thief left footprints like an apology — small, apologetic, already half-erased by wind. I crouched over the third set of the morning and read them the way other men read scripture: heel deep on the push-off, toe light on landing, a stride shortened by a pack that rode high and right. She favored her left knee. She'd been walking since before dawn. And she was good, better than good, but nobody was good enough to cross forty miles of salt flat without leaving a signature.

I signed my own name in the crust beside hers — one boot print, deliberate, so she'd know if she doubled back that I knew she hadn't.

"Run all you like," I told the horizon. The horizon didn't answer. It never did. That was mostly why I liked it.

My name is Ivo Zhou, though the man on my papers is called Teodor Vasch, a freight surveyor out of Corren's Landing with a face that looks nothing like mine and a license that expired two years before I bought it off a drunk clerk for the price of a decent horse. I have been Teodor for six years now. Before that I was someone else for eleven days, and before that I was myself for twenty-six years, which is the part I try not to think about while walking.

Here is what rest does to a man like me. Rest means a bed, and a bed means a register, and a register means a clerk squinting at your papers under lamplight, and clerks talk to constables, and constables have long memories and short imaginations. There is a warrant with my true name on it filed in a district office three hundred miles east, and there is a conviction behind the warrant that I did not earn honestly — or rather, I earned it very honestly, in the sense that other people worked hard to give it to me. A company wanted a valley. I had the poor judgment to own a testimony about the survey that erased it. So they gave me a crime instead: tampered gauges at a water station, falsified logs, a dead inspector whose death I still don't know how to feel guilty about because I never learned whether I was supposed to have done it. The court decided quickly. Courts always decide quickly when the paperwork arrives pre-written.

So I move. Six years of moving. You'd think it would wear grooves in a man, and it has, but the grooves run in circles, and circles are just prisons with better scenery.

* * *

I caught sight of her at midday, a smudge against the white, and did what a tracker should never do: I hurried. Hurrying prints deeper, breathes louder, thinks worse. But there was something about the way she moved — not fleeing, *traveling*, like the flats were a road she'd walked before — that made me want to close the distance while I could.

She was smaller than I expected from the stride. Grey coat, hood up, a pack that clinked once when she stumbled, which told me metal, which told me tools, which told me this was no desperate starving thief but a professional. She stopped at a salt-crusted boulder, took a reading off something in her hand, and adjusted course by degrees. Navigating by instrument across open flats. Nobody navigates by instrument toward nothing. She was going somewhere specific, and she was going there carefully.

That worried me more than the theft had. The theft was simple: three nights ago, in the trading post at Harrow Bend, someone cracked the strongroom of the land office and took a single document. Not coin. Not scrip. One folio — the original concession survey for the eastern escarpment, the one with the company seal fat and purple on its face. The postmaster offered a bounty, and the bounty-hunters all shook their heads, because the escarpment country has a way of eating bounty-hunters, and I was the only fool who said yes.

I'll be honest about why. It wasn't the money, though the money would buy me a new name, a better one, maybe even a life where I could stop flinching at registers. It was that the word *survey* snagged in me like a fishhook. I know what surveys can be made to do. I watched one do it to me.

* * *

By late afternoon the flats began to tilt, salt giving way to grey clay cracked into hexagons like the floor of some abandoned kiln, and I understood where she was going. There was a well out here. Old, pre-concession, marked on none of the company maps because the company maps only mark things companies own. My father — the man I was before I was a convict, before I was a fugitive, back when I had a hometown — used to say the escarpment peoples dug wells like they planted orchards: for grandchildren they'd never meet. This one had been dry for decades, but its stone housing still stood, a low round collar of fitted blocks rising out of the clay, and it was the only shade within ten miles.

She'd reach it near dusk. A careful traveler beds down in shelter, even dry shelter. A careful traveler also checks her backtrail, which meant I couldn't follow the direct line anymore. So I swung wide, south, letting the sun walk down ahead of me, and came at the well from the blind side with the last light flat in my eyes.

She was there. Sitting with her back against the stones, boots off, rubbing her left knee, a tin cup balanced on her knee and her pack open beside her. In the open pack, catching the last orange light, lay a folded folio with a purple seal.

I stepped around the well mouth, put a stone wall at my back, and said, "Evening."

She didn't jump. That was the first remarkable thing. She looked up at me the way you look at weather — assessing whether it would pass or stay — and said, "You've been following me since the flats."

"Since Harrow Bend."

"The postmaster's man." She pulled her boot back on, unhurried. "He said he'd send someone. I gave him three days."

"You gave him three days?"

"I wanted to see who'd come." She studied me. "Not a bounty-hunter. Bounty-hunters dress like they're sorry already. You dress like a surveyor."

"Teodor Vasch," I said, and heard how thin it sounded out loud, a paper name spoken in open air. "Freight surveyor."

"Mm." She reached slowly — too slowly, telegraphing every inch — into her coat and produced a strip of dried meat, offering half. "Sable," she said. "And before you ask, no, I won't give the folio back, and yes, I know what it says inside."

"You've read it?"

"I stole it to read it." She tore her half of the meat with her teeth. "Do you know what's in a concession survey, Mr. Vasch? Boundary stones. Flow rates. A list of 'unencumbered parcels.' Unencumbered." She laughed, short and bitter. "My family has farmed the Vetch bottom for four generations. Four generations of encumbrance, apparently. The survey says our land was empty when the company came. Says it right there in ink. Somebody signed their name to a lie and got paid for it, and now the permit expires in five weeks and after that the seizure is permanent, and the only copy of the proof that the lie was a lie—" she tapped the folio "—was sitting in a strongroom where nobody was ever going to look at it again."

I stood very still. Somewhere behind my ribs, six years of running shifted its weight.

"That's a story," I said, because saying anything else felt like stepping off a cliff.

"It's a survey," she said. "Read it yourself, if you don't believe me. Or don't read it, and take it back to the postmaster, and collect your blood money, and go back to whatever name you were before you were Teodor Vasch."

The name hit like cold water. I must have shown something in my face, because she almost smiled.

"Your papers are forged," she said. "Third-generation forgery, Corren style, the clerk who sold it to you got sloppy with the laminate. I noticed at the well yesterday when you stopped to check your water. You keep your real documents sewn into your coat lining. Old habit. Convict's habit." She shrugged. "I read pockets. It's how I make my living."

"And what exactly did you read of mine?"

"A warrant file. Folded soft from carrying. Your name isn't Zhou, is it? But the warrant says it is." Her voice lost its edge, just slightly. "Water tampering. The Corren Valley case." She looked at me for a long moment. "They convicted you on that survey's sister document. Same surveyor's initials. Same company."

The sun went down while neither of us moved. The clay went from orange to violet to a purple so deep it was nearly black, and the temperature dropped like something falling off a shelf, and I became aware that we were both very thirsty and there was one waterskin between us and it was hanging on my hip.

"Here's what happens now," I said finally, because someone had to say something practical. "You give me the folio. I give you a head start, and I tell the postmaster the trail went cold. That's mercy. Don't test how much of it I have."

"Or," she said, "you sit down, we split your water, and you listen to the rest, because the part I haven't told you yet is why I need the folio *and* everything else in that strongroom's index, and the index says there's a second archive. Under the escarpment. On the coast. And I can't get into it alone."

"There's no archive under the escarpment."

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "There wouldn't be, on a company map."

I reached for the waterskin to buy time, to think, and she moved — fast, faster than her knee suggested, up off the ground and through the well's shadow — and what happened next happened in the space of one bad decision. I grabbed for her collar. She turned inside my grip, and I felt her hands at my belt, quick as a pickpocket's, and then she was past me, running barefoot across the cracking clay with her boots in one hand and my waterskin swinging from the other, and my warrant file — soft-folded, carried six years, the only honest thing I owned about myself — gone from my coat with her.

I stood at the dry well holding nothing, listening to her footsteps fade, and realized I was laughing. Actually laughing, alone in the dark, for the first time in years.

Because here was the thing she'd said, the hook of it, working in me already: *a second archive.* And here was the thing I knew that she didn't: I'd grown up on the escarpment's edge, and my grandmother used to sing a song about doors that opened with the tide.

I had no water, no warrant, and no name I trusted. Tomorrow I'd follow her anyway.

The coast was three days east, and the storm season was early this year.
