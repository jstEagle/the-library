## 2. The Tide Opens

The sea found me before Ivo Zhou did.

I'd been running the coast path since dawn, boots slick with spray, the satchel of stolen papers banging against my hip like a second heartbeat. Behind me, maybe a mile back and closing, came the ranger who had chased me across three hundred miles of salt and stone. Ahead of me, the cliffs simply stopped, sheared off into a gray churning nothing. The storm that had been growling on the horizon all morning finally arrived, and it arrived like an unpaid debt.

So there I was: thief cornered between a lawman and a wave.

I want it noted, for the record of whatever gods keep records, that I did not panic. Panic is for people with options. I assessed. The cliff face to my left was rotten sandstone — one handhold and it would come away like wet bread. The beach below was already drowning, the tide coming in fast and angry, eating the shingle in greedy bites. And behind me, crunching steadily over the pebbles, was Ivo Zhou, walking like a man who had all the time in the world, which was insulting, because he didn't have any either. Nobody standing on this coast in this weather had any.

"Sable." His voice carried flat over the wind. Not shouting. He never shouted. That was somehow worse. "You're out of coastline."

"I'm never out of coastline," I called back. "Coastline is infinite. It's you who's run out."

He came closer anyway, hands loose at his sides, no weapon drawn, which was its own kind of threat. Ivo Zhou didn't need weapons. He needed patience, and he had the patience of a man who had spent years learning that everything ends eventually — footprints, storms, fugitives.

"You took my water," he said.

"You took my freedom. We're even."

"I also took your file." He patted his coat pocket, where my warrant papers sat — my name, my face, the price on both. "You know what's funny? You stole from me first. Back at the well. So technically—"

"Technically nothing. Technically is how companies talk." I backed along the cliff edge, feeling the wind shove at my shoulders like a drunk uncle. "Come any closer and I'll jump, and then you'll have to explain to your employers why their bounty drowned."

"You don't work for them anymore?" Something flickered across his face — not quite hope. Curiosity, maybe. "The people who hired you to steal those documents?"

"That's a long story."

"We've got weather," he said dryly, "and weather takes hours."

And that's when the sea decided to settle the argument.

It came around the headland without warning — not a wave but a wall, storm-driven, black-green, taller than either of us. I heard Ivo shout something — my name, or God's, hard to tell — and then the world went white and loud and upside down. I remember grabbing for rock. I remember rock declining the invitation. I remember water filling my ears and my mouth and every opinion I'd ever held about my own competence.

When I coughed myself back into existence, I was ten feet lower than I'd been, wedged in a notch of the cliff, soaked to the soul, and the beach I'd been standing on was gone. Gone. The tide had swallowed it whole. Somewhere above me, Ivo was clinging to a ledge, looking down at me with an expression I'd learn later was his version of screaming.

"The cave!" he bellowed, pointing.

"What cave?"

"Behind you! Move!"

I turned. There, where a moment ago there had been solid cliff, the retreating surge had peeled back like a curtain — and revealed a mouth. A sea cave, tall as a chapel door, its threshold streaming with foam. It hadn't been there before. Or rather, it had always been there, and the sea only allowed visitors by appointment.

I didn't deliberate. Deliberation is for people whose alternative isn't drowning. I swam.

* * *

Inside, the noise of the storm dropped to a distant heartbeat, and the light changed. That's what I noticed first — not the cold, not the water streaming off me, but the light. It came through some fissure high in the rock, green-gold and trembling, and it fell on walls that were not walls.

They were shelves.

Carved shelves, row upon row, rising into the dark above the tideline, and on them — I wiped my eyes and looked again — objects. Tablets of some pale stone, stacked like loaves. Cylinders wrapped in waxed cloth. Flat sheets of hammered metal, thin as leaf, inscribed edge to edge with writing in a script I half-recognized from museum postcards and never expected to see outside one.

"It's an archive," I said stupidly, to nobody.

"It's an archive," Ivo agreed, behind me, dripping.

We stood there, two enemies, shoulder to shoulder in a flooded entrance hall, staring up at several thousand years of somebody's careful bookkeeping. I felt something crack open in my chest — right between the hostility ribs — and I resented it immediately. Wonder is a vulnerability. Wonder is how they get you.

"How is this here?" I whispered. "Everyone knows the escarpment peoples kept no written records. That's the whole justification — *no permanent settlements, no claims, nomadic, uncivilized* — that's what the surveys said. That's what got the land opened to concession."

"Then everyone," Ivo said quietly, "was wrong. Or lying."

"Those aren't mutually exclusive."

He moved past me, and I braced for him to grab my collar, demand his papers back, do the lawman thing. Instead he crouched beside a low doorway — sealed, fitted with a stone slab so precisely cut that rain couldn't find the seam — and studied the carvings around it with a ranger's eye. Tracking signs. Reading ground. Even underground, even here, he read.

"These doors were maintained," he said. "Recently. Look — the wax seals are fresh. Someone's been keeping this place."

"Keeping it for whom? The escarpment peoples are gone. Scattered, assimilated, dead — pick your euphemism."

"Apparently not all of them."

The voice came from everywhere and nowhere, dry as old paper, and we both spun toward it. At the back of the chamber, where the carved shelves gave way to a passage leading up into the cliff's heart, stood a woman. Old — old the way the escarpment is old, weathered and upright anyway. She wore layers of undyed wool and carried a lamp, and she looked at us the way you look at cats that have gotten into the pantry: not surprised, exactly. Inconvenienced.

"Well," she said. "Two of you. The sea usually sends one."

Ivo's hand drifted toward his belt. The old woman watched him do it with magnificent unimpressed-ness.

"You can put that away or leave it," she said. "Either way, you're here until the tide turns, and the tide turns in six hours. I'm Maren. I keep this place. Come up out of the wet before you ruin the floor, which my grandmother cut, and her mother before her, going back further than your company has existed, which I mention because I suspect you're company people, and company people always need the arithmetic explained slowly."

"We're not—" I started.

"She's a thief," Ivo said. "I'm the one chasing her."

"Mm." Maren turned and walked up the passage, clearly expecting us to follow, because apparently authority doesn't require volume. "A thief and a ranger. The sea has a sense of humor. Come along. There's tea, and there are things you two need to see, though only one of you knows it yet."

I looked at Ivo. Ivo looked at me. Six hours, one exit, and a tide that answered to no warrant ever issued.

"After you," he said.

"Chivalrous."

"Strategic. If anything in there eats people, I'd rather it start on you."

* * *

Her dwelling was a chamber hollowed from the living rock, warm from a small stove, lit by lamps and by a shaft of daylight from a vent far above. Every surface was orderly in the way of places tended for generations. And on one wall, framed in carved stone older than any nation I could name, hung a document that made my breath stop.

Not paper. Metal sheet, engraved, but unmistakably a survey. A map of the entire escarpment range — every valley, every spring, every grazing terrace — annotated in that same dense, beautiful script, with boundary markers and usage rights and seasonal rotations. A legal geography. A civilization's deed to itself, written in stone because stone doesn't burn.

Beneath it, in translation — someone had added modern letters, careful and recent — a single line:

*Let no survey be accepted that does not proceed from this record.*

"They falsified theirs," Maren said, watching me read. "The concession surveys. I've seen copies, over the years, smuggled down to the coastal towns. They wrote *uninhabited*. They wrote *no fixed boundaries*. They wrote *wasteland*, which is a word meaning land they wanted." She poured tea into three cups without asking if we wanted any. "My family kept this archive for eleven generations. Warden's duty. We knew someday someone would come needing it. I confess I imagined someone more presentable."

"I'm very presentable," I said.

"You're dripping on a four-hundred-year-old floor."

I set down my cup. My heart was doing something complicated. Because I hadn't come to the coast for treasure, whatever the warrants said. I'd come for exactly this — proof, any proof, that the surveys were lies — because the company had seized my family's farm on the strength of those falsified maps, and the seizure becomes permanent when the permits finalize at month's end. I had told no one this. I had barely admitted it to myself.

But Maren was looking at me the way old women look, which is straight through.

"And you," she said. "You broke into the survey office at Redgate. That was you. Half the coast heard about it."

I said nothing. Ivo said nothing, but I felt him go very still beside me, recalculating.

"You took the wrong thing," Maren continued, gently, terribly. "You took the permit drafts. Copies. Worthless without the originals they certify. But the originals—" she touched the metal map, lightly, the way you touch a sleeping child "—the originals were always here. Waiting. The question is whether anyone can still carry them out in time, and whether anyone will believe a thief, a fugitive, and an old woman when they do."

Outside, muffled by tons of rock, the storm went on shouting. And beneath us, faint but unmistakable, came another sound — engines. More than one. Drawing along the coast road, slow, thorough, the way men search when they're being paid to be thorough.

Maren closed her eyes briefly, listening. When she opened them, she looked, for the first time, genuinely old.

"Well," she said. "They've found the tide tables too."
