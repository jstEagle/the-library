## 3. What the Company Buried

I had not had guests in forty-one years, and the first two arrived by breaking in.

Well — one broke in. The girl came through the tide door like water, all urgency and salt, and then the ranger followed her with a lamp and a drawn look, and I stood at the top of the archive stair with my hands folded and waited for them to notice me. It took longer than you'd think. People never look up. Forty years of watching the door taught me that much: whatever comes through it will be staring at its own feet.

"Evening," I said, when they finally did.

The ranger's hand went to his hip. The girl's went to her satchel. I have learned to read hands; they are more honest than faces.

"You're standing in the Long Room," I told them. "Mind the third tile. It's load-bearing."

They looked where I pointed, which was nowhere, because there is no third tile worth minding, but it gave me the two seconds I needed to decide they weren't company men. Company men announce themselves. They knock like they own the door. These two looked like weather had chased them in, which, as it happened, was exactly true.

"I'm Maren," I said. "Last warden of the escarpment. And you're trespassing, so we'll want to sort out who owes whom an apology."

* * *

The Long Room is what my grandmother called it, and her mother before her, back to a woman named Ashka who carved the first shelf with tools we'd call crude now and she'd have called Tuesday. It is not a treasure vault, whatever the concession maps say. It is a library with better manners. Rows of stone cabinets, each sealed against damp, each holding what the escarpment peoples thought mattered enough to survive them: land claims cut into slate, survey stones with their original markings intact, harvest tallies, marriage lines, a complaint about a neighbor's goat written with such venom that I've read it aloud on bad nights just to feel less alone.

Forty-one years I kept it. My mother kept it thirty before me, and her father sixty before that, and somewhere in those numbers is the whole sad arithmetic of a people who kept meticulous records of everything they owned and were still erased from every map that mattered.

I lit the good lamps — whale oil, don't ask, the last of it — and watched the two of them come apart a little. That's the thing about the Long Room. Nobody walks in angry. The room won't permit it. Even the ranger's shoulders came down from around his ears, though he tried to pretend otherwise.

"What is this place?" the girl asked. Sable, she'd said on the flats, or rather hadn't said, and he'd said it for her, and she'd glared. A thief with a name she guards tighter than her loot. I liked her immediately.

"It's the reason your permits cost four hundred marks instead of nothing," I said. "It's the reason your valley has a fence on it. Sit down. Both of you. This takes an hour if you're patient and three if you keep interrupting."

* * *

Here is what the company buried, and I will say it plainly because I have said it to empty rooms for four decades and I am out of practice saying it to faces:

In the year the surveys were struck, the escarpment held eleven recognized settlements. Not camps. Settlements — wells dug, terraces cut, boundary stones set and witnessed. The survey commission walked our land for six weeks, ate our bread, slept under our roofs, and then produced maps showing eleven *uninhabited seasonal shelters*, unclaimed, unworked, unwitnessed. They moved our boundary stones. I know because my grandmother helped move them back, at night, and re-cut the witness marks, and those stones are in cabinet nine, and they match the originals in cabinet eight, and no honest survey produces two sets of stones that agree with each other and disagree with the official map.

"They didn't just take the land," I told them, laying the slates out on the reading table like a hand of cards. "They took the *evidence* first. You can't contest a claim if the claim itself has been declared imaginary. Every permit issued since rests on those maps. Every fence, every concession line, every eviction notice. It's not theft, children. Theft implies something was taken. This is forgery — they wrote us out and then charged rent on the space where we used to be."

The ranger — Ivo, he'd finally offered, grudging as a man paying a debt — turned a survey stone over in his big careful hands. He handled it like something that might go off.

"This would hold up," he said quietly. "In a registry court. Witnessed originals against the commission copies. It's not even close."

"It would," I agreed, "if anyone were permitted to bring it. Which no one has been, for forty-one years, because the registry office sits inside the concession district, and the clerks answer to the company, and the company answers to no one I've ever been introduced to." I smiled, and it felt strange on my face, like a coat that no longer fit. "So I kept the room. On the theory that someday someone would walk through that tide door who could carry it further than I can."

And then I looked at Sable, because I had watched her since she entered, and I had seen what she looked at. Not the gold — yes, there's gold, a modest amount, ceremonial mostly, and she glanced at it once the way you glance at a stranger's nice coat. What she stared at, hungry, was cabinet twelve. The land claims. The valley records. Our farms.

"Well?" I said. "You didn't come here for the view. The tide door doesn't open for tourists."

She flinched. Good. Honest people flinch.

"My family has a farm," she said. "Had. Upper Alder Valley, forty hectares, my grandfather cleared it. The company holds the permit now. It expires at season's end, and when it does, they finalize the transfer and we're off land we've worked for sixty years." Her jaw worked. "I came to steal proof. Not treasure. Documents. Something — anything — that showed the surveys were wrong, that the land was claimed before their maps existed. I heard stories about a cave treasury. I thought maybe the old people kept records too."

"The old people," I said, "kept *everything*. We were a bureaucratic civilization, child. We invented the witness mark. We argued about goats in triplicate." I slid cabinet twelve open and put the Alder Valley slate in her hands. Her name is on it, three generations back. She read it and cried without any noise at all, which is the escarpment way, and I pretended not to see, which is also the escarpment way.

"So you're not a thief," Ivo said. There was something in his voice I couldn't place at first. Then I placed it: relief. A man who'd spent weeks chasing a criminal discovering he'd been chasing a farmer's daughter instead. Though I'll note he didn't look entirely relieved. Some men prefer their world simple, and I was about to make it worse.

"No," I said. "But you are, ranger. Or near enough. Forged papers, old warrant, and you flinched when I mentioned the company's name earlier — you flinched like a man who's met it in an unpleasant room." I folded my hands. "I'm old, not blind. If we're going to do what I think we're going to do, I'll need to know whose trouble you're carrying."

He was quiet a long moment. The lamps ticked. Somewhere above us, wind worried at the cliff mouth.

"The Meridian Concession surveyed my father's station the same way," he said finally. "Falsified boundaries. He contested it. Formally. Loudly." His thumb moved along the edge of the survey stone, back and forth. "Six months later I was convicted of theft I didn't commit, with evidence I've never seen, and my father stopped writing letters. I've been running on forged papers for nine years. So yes. I'm carrying trouble. Same trouble. Older vintage."

There it was. I had wondered, these past hours, why the escarpment had sent me a thief and a fugitive after forty-one years of sending me nobody, and now I knew. It sends helpers the way it sends storms: late, wet, and not what anyone ordered.

* * *

We were still at the table — I was showing Ivo how the witness marks align, holding two slates to the lamp so the cuts throw matching shadows — when Sable went still.

Not startled-still. Ranger-still. She tilted her head the way my mother used to when listening for weather, and I remembered that she'd grown up on farmland, and farmland teaches you engines.

"That's not surf," she said.

Ivo was already moving, lamp cupped, crossing the Long Room on the balls of his feet. I followed more slowly, because I am seventy-three and dignity is the only thing left I can move quickly. At the mouth of the entry gallery, where the tide door gaped black and the sea breathed in and out below, we heard it clearly: engines. Two of them. Grinding down the coast track toward the headland, with the particular unhurried confidence of machines whose owners have never once been told no.

"Company?" I asked, though I knew.

"Concession enforcers run coastal patrols in storm season," Ivo said. "Looking for smugglers." He paused. "Or looking for a thief who hit a pay wagon at Kestrel Crossing ten days ago. Word travels faster than people."

Sable made a small sound, half laugh, half despair. "I robbed them to fund the trip. Of course I did. Of course they followed me *here*."

Below, lights swung across the water line. Voices, thin with distance, giving orders. The tide was coming back in — two hours, maybe three, before the door closed and the cave became what it had always been meant to be: unreachable, unfindable, safe. But enforcers with lamps and rope and no respect for schedules might not wait for the sea's opinion.

Ivo turned to me, and in the lamplight I saw the thing underneath his tiredness, the thing he'd been outrunning for nine years: a man desperate for a reason to stop running and terrified of finding one.

"How many ways out of this mountain?" he asked.

"One," I said. "The upper galleries. They climb through the escarpment's spine and come out on the high shoulder, a day's walk from anything. My grandmother used them. Nobody else knows they exist." I looked at the two of them — the thief clutching her ancestors' farm deed, the fugitive already calculating angles — and I felt something I hadn't felt in forty-one years, which was hope, arriving inconveniently, the way it always does.

"Then we'd best pack the archives," I said. "The important ones. Cabinet twelve first." I was already moving toward the shelves, pulling the document cases my mother had sewn against exactly this night. "Because if the company wants this room sealed badly enough to send men in a storm, then everything in it is worth more than I ever dared believe. And I intend to make them regret teaching me that."

The lights below swept closer. Somewhere in the dark, the sea began to close its door.

"Upper galleries," Ivo said. "Rope order. Sable, you're second. Maren—"

"I'm seventy-three, not furniture," I said. "Middle. Where the rope can argue with gravity on my behalf. Now move, children. The past just became urgent."
