## 4. Three Strangers, One Rope

The upper galleries of the cave smelled like a library that had drowned and decided to make peace with it. Salt-crusted stone, old paper, something mineral and sweet underneath — the breath of a mountain that had been holding its secrets a long time and wasn't in any hurry to exhale.

I went up first because I was the one who'd found the crack in the ceiling, and because going first is a habit I've never managed to break. Going first means you don't have to watch anyone's back. It's not bravery. It's just loneliness with better footwork.

"Slow down," Maren said behind me, her voice dry as the ledgers she'd spent eighty years guarding. "The warden's oath includes a clause about not killing visitors on the premises."

"That's a real clause?"

"It will be if you fall on me."

Sable said nothing. She was three steps back, hugging that satchel of documents to her chest like it was a child, or a bomb, or both. She hadn't let go of it since Maren opened the archive seals. Paper proving the concession surveys were forged — paper proving my own conviction was built on the same forgery — and she carried it like she expected me to snatch it and run. Which, to be fair, was exactly what she would have done in my position, and probably what I'd have done in hers.

We were all thieves in that cave. We just disagreed about what we were stealing.

* * *

The galleries climbed in switchbacks cut by hands — actual hands, centuries ago, chiseled stairs and rope-grooves worn into the rock like scars. Lantern niches every twenty paces, some still holding stubs of tallow candles that Maren lit with a flint she kept on a cord around her neck. The light made the walls come alive: carvings, thousands of them, spirals and tally-marks and figures with their arms raised toward a ceiling none of us could see.

"Who were they?" Sable asked, catching up beside me for the first time since the cave mouth.

"People of the escarpment," Maren said, from behind, breathing harder now. "Before the escarpment had a name anyone wrote down. They kept the records. Rain years. Herd counts. Who married whom. Who owed whom." A pause, and then, quieter: "Who was wronged, and how it was set right."

"Sounds better than our courts," I said.

"Our courts are descended from theirs," Maren said. "Badly. Like everything else."

Then the floor ended.

Not dramatically — no yawning chasm with wind moaning out of it. Just a gallery that simply stopped, its far side four meters away across a gap where the span had collapsed sometime in the last century, rubble fanned down into blackness below. The drop wasn't bottomless. It was maybe fifteen meters onto broken stone. Fifteen meters is plenty. Fifteen meters is a career-ender, a name-on-a-wooden-marker kind of distance. I've seen men survive worse and I've seen men die from less, and the difference is never the rock. It's the rope.

"We go around?" Sable asked.

"There is no around," Maren said. "This is the Warden's Span. There was a bridge once. My grandmother walked it." She looked at the gap the way you look at an old friend's empty chair.

"And now?"

"Now we improvise," I said, and started uncoiling the rope from my shoulder.

I'd carried that rope for six years without once needing it, which is either superstition or foresight, depending on how charitable you're feeling. Forty meters of braided hide-and-hemp, salt-stiff but sound. I tested a length between my hands, then anchored it around a carved pillar that had been holding up this mountain since before anyone alive was born. If the pillar failed, we'd have bigger problems than gravity.

"One at a time," I said. "Maren first. Then the girl. Me last."

"I'm not a girl," Sable said.

"You're younger than me and lighter than both of us. You go second so someone's ahead of you and someone's behind. That's not sentiment, it's arithmetic."

She glared at me, which I was starting to understand was her native language, and tied in. I showed her the knot twice; she got it faster than I taught it, which annoyed me in a way I didn't examine too closely.

Maren crossed like she'd done it before, slow and deliberate, one hand on the rope, her boots finding holds in the rubble-edge that I couldn't even see from my side. Eighty years old and she moved like the mountain had personally invited her. When she reached the far side she turned and held the rope taut, taking Sable's weight as she came across, and for a moment — just a moment — the two of them stood together on the far edge, and I thought: *that's what it looks like when people trust each other.* I wouldn't know personally. I'd read about it.

Then it was my turn, and I stopped thinking in complete sentences.

* * *

Here's the thing about crossing a gap on a rope over a fifteen-meter drop: your body knows things your mind won't admit. Every step is a negotiation between the part of you that wants to live and the part of you that's curious about the dark below. I was halfway across, weight on the rope, boots on a ledge the width of a hymnal, when Sable's satchel strap caught on a spur of rock behind her and tore.

I heard it before I understood it — that wet ripping sound of old leather giving up — and then the satchel swung free, bounced off her hip, and slid toward the edge of the ledge she was standing on.

Everything after that happened in the space between two heartbeats.

She lunged for it. The lunge took her past her balance point. The rope snapped taut as Maren threw her whole weight backward, and Sable skidded to the lip of the ledge with her knees over air and one hand closing on the satchel's torn strap, and the strap — torn, remember — began to slide through her fingers like it had somewhere better to be.

And I did the stupidest thing I have ever done, which is saying something, because I once forged a magistrate's seal using a spoon and candle soot.

I let go of my foothold.

I dropped the last meter and a half on the rope, swinging out over the void, and caught the satchel's strap above her hand just as it finished sliding through. For one long, ridiculous moment I hung there over the drop, my whole life suspended from a knot I'd tied around a pillar older than my country, looking straight down at the rocks that were very interested in meeting me, while above me Sable hauled and Maren braced and the rope creaked like a ship in weather.

"Don't," Sable grunted, hauling, "you dare," haul, "let go," haul.

"Wasn't planning on it," I said, through my teeth.

"Give me — the bag —"

"No."

That surprised her enough that she almost stopped hauling. I could see it land in her face even upside down: the thief's arithmetic running behind her eyes. *Why would he risk his neck for the documents? Why would he hold on?*

Because they weren't just her family's proof anymore. They were mine. Somewhere in that satchel was a survey ledger with a magistrate's signature in it, and under that signature, a name that matched the name on my forged papers. Six years of running, and the reason I'd been running had been sitting in a cave under the salt flats the entire time, waiting for someone dumb enough to come get it.

I got a boot onto the ledge. Then a knee. Then Sable's hand closed on my collar and dragged me the rest of the way onto solid rock, and the three of us lay there in a heap, breathing like landed fish, the satchel pinned under my chest.

For a while nobody said anything. The lantern-light trembled on the walls. Somewhere below us, water dripped with the patience of geology.

"You could have died," Sable said finally. "For paper."

"For true things," I said. "There's a difference."

Maren sat up, brushed grit from her sleeves with the unhurried dignity of a woman who had clearly decided decades ago not to be impressed by anything, and looked at us both.

"Well," she said. "Now that you've bled for the archives, in spirit if not yet in fact, I suppose you'll want to know what you're carrying."

* * *

We made camp in a side gallery with a natural chimney that pulled the smoke from a small fire — small, because smoke meant enforcers, and enforcers meant the men who'd arrived at the cave mouth an hour after we fled upward, whose voices we'd heard echoing through the lower halls, calling each other by numbers instead of names. Company men. They wanted the cave sealed and its claim buried, and buried along with it anyone who'd seen inside.

So: over the range, ahead of the snows, to the registry office at Corvane Crossing. Three days' hard walking if the weather held. Two days if it didn't forgive us. The permits finalized at month's end. The paperwork, as always, was the most dangerous animal on the mountain.

We ate hardtack and dried fish and Maren's astonishing tea, brewed from something she called "warden's weed" that tasted like pine needles had opinions. And around the fire, in the way that cold and fear strip people down to their load-bearing beams, we talked about the crossing.

"The pass closes early some years," Maren said. "This will be an early year. I can smell it."

"You can't smell snow coming three days out," Sable said.

"I'm the last warden of the escarpment, girl. I can smell whatever I decide to smell." But there was no iron in it, and we all heard that too.

"If we wait," I said, "the permits finalize. If we rush, the mountain kills us. Those are the options."

"There's a third option," Sable said quietly. "We split up. I take the documents down the fast route alone. You two—"

"No," Maren and I said, at the same time, which startled all three of us.

Sable stared. "You don't even like me."

"I don't trust you," I corrected. "Liking is irrelevant. But those documents are worth more than either of us alone, and a lone runner on the fast route in early snow is a headline, not a courier." I turned the satchel over in my hands, feeling the tear in the strap, the weight of everything inside it. "Besides. I've spent six years being a lone runner. The reviews are mixed."

"What's the alternative?" she asked.

I looked at the rope coiled by the fire, stiff with salt, scarred from the day. One rope. Three strangers. The arithmetic was ugly and simple.

"We tie in," I said. "All the way over. One falls, the other two hold. Nobody runs ahead, nobody gets left. Not because we love each other — we met yesterday — but because the mountain doesn't care whether we trust each other, and it's cheaper to pretend than to bury anyone."

Maren nodded slowly, like I'd recited something old. Maybe I had. Maybe it was just the oldest sentence there is.

Sable looked at the fire for a long time. When she spoke, her voice had lost its edge for the first time since the dry well.

"My family's farm is at stake," she said. "If we fail—"

"Then we fail roped together," I said. "Which beats failing apart."

Outside the chimney, high above, the wind changed key. Maren lifted her head like a deer scenting rain, and the look on her face made the fire seem suddenly smaller.

"That," she said, "is the smell of snow."
