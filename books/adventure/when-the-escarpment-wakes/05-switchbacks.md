## 5. Switchbacks

The escarpment did not believe in gentle introductions. By mid-morning of the second day it had stopped being a mountain and become a staircase built by someone who hated people — switchbacks stacked on switchbacks, each one promising a summit that turned out to be another lie with better views.

I counted the turns to keep my mind off my legs. Forty-one. Forty-two. Behind me, Maren's breathing had developed a rasp like a file drawn across soft wood.

"We should rest," I said, for the fourth time in an hour.

"We'll rest when I say," she answered, for the fourth time, and kept climbing.

She was seventy-something years old, possibly older — wardens apparently didn't bother keeping calendars — and she was setting a pace designed to kill us both out of sheer spite. I let her have her pride for another three switchbacks before I planted myself on a boulder and refused to move.

"Maren. Sit down or fall down. Those are your options."

She glared at me with eyes the color of the salt flats we'd left behind. Then, to my genuine astonishment, she sat. Her hands, when she pulled off her gloves, were trembling in a way that had nothing to do with cold.

"I climbed this face at nineteen," she said. "Carried a full archive satchel. Didn't stop once."

"You also weren't seventy and running from men with guns."

"Sixty-eight." She accepted the water skin I handed over and drank like it was a personal insult. "And they didn't have guns then. They had lawyers. Worse."

Ivo had gone ahead to scout the next bend — of course he had; the man treated standing still as a medical condition — so it was just the two of us on the ledge, the valley dropping away below us in folds of brown and rust, the company's survey stakes glinting down there like teeth.

"Your family's farm," Maren said suddenly. "The one you're doing all this for. Tell me about them."

It wasn't a question, exactly. Wardens, I was learning, didn't ask questions. They opened doors and waited to see if you'd walk through.

"My mother runs it now. My father died in the blight year — the year the company's irrigation canal 'accidentally' diverted our water rights upstream." I heard my own voice go flat and careful, the way it did when I was handling something fragile. "They seized the east fields for non-payment of the new concession levy. A levy on land we'd held for four generations, assessed by a survey nobody ever saw. My mother signed the surrender papers rather than lose the house too. I told her not to sign. I told her I'd fix it."

"And instead you ran away to be a thief."

"Instead I ran away to be a thief," I agreed. "Very heroic. Very effective. Two years of picking locks and lifting ledgers, and what do I have? A satchel of documents I can't read half of, an old woman who outruns me, and a fugitive ranger who—" I stopped.

"Who what?"

"Nothing."

Maren smiled. It was not a comforting smile. "You were about to say who watches you sleep by the fire every night and pretends he's checking the perimeter."

"He is checking the perimeter."

"He is lonely," Maren said, with the terrible authority of someone who had been alive long enough to recognize loneliness at forty paces, "and so are you, and neither of you will admit it because admitting things is how people get hurt. I buried everyone I ever admitted anything to. But I'll tell you what I learned." She levered herself up, joints cracking like green wood in a fire. "The walls don't keep the hurt out. They just make sure you're alone inside them."

She started climbing again. I sat on my rock a moment longer, feeling seen in a way I actively resented, and then followed.

* * *

We made camp that night in a hollow below the fifth ridge, sheltered from the wind that had been trying to peel us off the mountainside all afternoon. Ivo built the fire small and screened — old habits — and cooked the last of the dried meat into something almost edible, which was as close to tenderness as his cooking ever got.

Maren fell asleep mid-sentence, sitting up, which I chose to interpret as a sign of trust rather than exhaustion. That left the two of us and the fire and the enormous dark pressing in past the rocks.

"She's slowing down," Ivo said quietly.

"I noticed."

"If it comes to it—"

"It won't come to it."

"Sable." He looked at me across the flames. In the firelight his face lost its ranger blankness and became something younger, more tired. "If it comes to it, we carry her. Or we turn back. We don't leave her on this mountain to save a filing deadline."

"I know." I fed a twig to the fire. "I'm not the monster you arrested me for being."

"I never arrested you."

"You tried. Twice."

"The second time was barely trying," he admitted, and there it was — the ghost of a smile, rare as rain on the flats. Then it faded, and he was quiet long enough that I thought the conversation was over. When he spoke again, his voice had changed. Flatter. Careful. The voice of a man handling something fragile.

"You told Maren why you steal. Fair's fair."

"You don't owe me your story, Zhou."

"No," he said. "But you're going to hear it anyway, because if we're going to die on this mountain together, you should know who you're dying with."

He stared into the fire the way people stare into fires when they're actually staring at something else entirely.

"Eight years ago I was a junior surveyor. Legitimate work — mapping water tables for the frontier authority. Honest maps. Then the Meridian Concession Company bought the contract for the eastern range, and my supervisor asked me to redraw a section. Just a section. The escarpment holdings. There were villages up there, Sable. Farms. People whose families had worked that land since before the company existed. My map put their claims under a geological survey zone. Uninhabited. Resurvey required, compensation deferred, permits fast-tracked." His jaw tightened. "I refused. So they didn't fire me. They framed me. An embezzlement charge — funds I never touched, routed through accounts I'd never seen. Trial took eleven days. My testimony about the falsified surveys was dismissed as the desperate fabrication of a convicted criminal. Neat, isn't it? The lie protects itself."

The fire popped. Somewhere above us, wind moved through stone with a sound like slow breathing.

"So the warrant file you stole from me," I said slowly, "wasn't just your name on it."

"It was the whole case history. Every forged ledger entry, every witness who recanted after the company's lawyers visited them, every document I spent eight years collecting so that someday, somewhere, I could stand in front of a court and prove it." He laughed, short and bitter. "And a pickpocket on the salt flats lifted it off me along with my water. Do you know what I thought when I realized it was gone? I thought: eight years, gone. I thought about turning back. Letting the flats have me."

"Why didn't you?"

"Because you went east," he said simply. "Toward the coast. Toward the caves. Nobody runs toward the thing they stole unless they mean to use it. I figured if I couldn't have my life back, I could at least watch you spend it on something."

I didn't know what to say to that. So I said the true thing instead, which was a novelty for me.

"When I took your file, I couldn't read most of it either. But I could read the seal on the survey documents. Meridian. Same company that took our fields. I thought — maybe the same lies. Maybe the same proof." I hugged my knees, watching him. "I've been telling myself I'm doing this for my mother. And I am. But some part of it — a big part — is that I wanted to burn something of theirs down. Anything. Even if it burned me too."

"That's not theft," Ivo said. "That's justice with bad planning."

"Is that what you call your eight years?"

"Mine was revenge with excellent filing."

Despite everything — the climb, the fear, the sleeping warden between us — I laughed. Actually laughed, out loud, into the dark, and Ivo's mouth twitched like the sound surprised him too.

* * *

Maren woke near midnight, which meant none of us had really been asleep, and insisted on taking first watch, which meant we pretended to sleep while she sat wrapped in her blanket like a small fierce bird of prey. Around the second hour I gave up pretending and crawled over to sit with her.

"Your mother," Maren said, without preamble. "Does she know where you are?"

"She knows I'm alive. I send letters through a relay station in Kettleford. Unsigned. She probably knows they're mine anyway. Mothers do."

"What do the letters say?"

"'Still working on it. Don't sign anything.'" I picked at a loose thread on my sleeve. "Two years of that. She must think I'm dead or useless. Probably both."

"Probably," Maren agreed, maddeningly. "Or she thinks exactly what I think — that her daughter bit off something enormous and is chewing anyway." She shifted, winced, pressed a hand to her side where the altitude had been digging its thumb into her all day. "Guilt is a heavy thing to carry up a mountain, girl. Set some of it down. You didn't cause the fraud. You didn't seize the fields. You're just the only one stubborn enough to climb up here and do something about it. That's not a sin. That's a spine."

"My father had a spine," I said. "Look where it got him."

"Look where it got him," Maren repeated softly. "A daughter who crossed the salt flats alone, cracked a warden's cave, and is dragging his story up a mountain to set it right. Yes. Look where it got him." She closed her eyes. "Now let an old woman watch the sky in peace."

I stayed a while anyway. The stars over the escarpment were brutal and beautiful, more of them than seemed legal, and for once I didn't count anything — not switchbacks, not risks, not the hours until the permits expired. I just sat, and breathed, and let the walls Maren had lectured me about stay down a little longer.

* * *

It was Ivo who found the problem, near dawn, when he went to feed the fire.

"Get it down," he said. Quietly. Which from Ivo was a scream.

I was awake instantly. "What?"

He pointed east, down-valley, where the sky was going grey at the edges. At first I saw nothing. Then the nothing moved — a speck against the brightening haze, drifting in slow, patient grids. The kind of grids a machine flies when it's searching, not wandering.

"Patrol drone. Company registration — see the wing flash?" He was already kicking dirt over the coals, smothering the fire with practiced hands. "Meridian runs them along the high routes during permit season. Looking for claim-jumpers. Squatters. Thieves."

"How close does it need to be?"

"To spot a fire? We're already spotted. It saw us twenty minutes ago. Question is whether it cares." He watched the speck hang, pivot, hold position. My stomach dropped. Drones that wander drift on. Drones that care hover.

Maren had risen without a sound, blanket around her shoulders, squinting downvalley with eyes that had been reading this land since before either of us was born. "It's relaying," she said. "That pause — it's sending. There's a relay mast on the shoulder of the sixth ridge." She turned to us, and all the age had gone out of her voice, leaving something older and harder underneath. "Which means whatever it saw is already known. We have maybe two hours before someone comes up that valley to look at us properly."

Ivo slung his pack. His face in the pre-dawn light was calm in the way of a man who has decided something expensive.

"Then we don't give them two hours," he said. "We give the pass everything we've got left — and we pray the snow gets there before they do."
