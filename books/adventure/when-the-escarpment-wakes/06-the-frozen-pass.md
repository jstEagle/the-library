## 6. The Frozen Pass

The snow came in the way bad news comes — first a rumor on the wind, then all at once, everywhere, and no way left to argue with it.

We'd made the pass by midafternoon, the three of us strung out along the ridge like beads on a fraying cord. Maren had gone quiet an hour back, which I'd learned to read as her version of a weather vane: when she stopped talking, the mountain was talking instead. Sable was ahead of me, moving with that low, economical stride she used when she didn't want to admit how tired she was. And I was doing what I do best, which is counting exits.

Except there weren't any. The sky had gone the color of wet slate, and the wind had that particular smell to it — iron and cold and something older underneath. Anyone who's spent a winter above the treeline knows it. The mountain was clearing its throat.

"We need shelter," Maren said, without turning around. "Now."

"I saw smoke-stain on the rocks below the saddle," Sable called back. "Shepherd hut, maybe."

"Maybe," Maren said, "is a word for people with options."

We found it half-buried against the lee side of the saddle — a dry-stone hut no bigger than a stable stall, roofed with slabs of shale, its doorway facing away from the weather like a man who's learned not to argue. Inside: a stone hearth blackened by generations of fires, a shelf, two blankets stiff with age and mouse, and a shepherd's crook hung on a peg like the last tenant had only stepped out and meant to come back.

Sable got a fire going from scrub pine she'd stripped off the windward trees before the climb. I wedged the door shut with the crook. Maren sat down on the floor with her back to the wall and let out a breath that seemed to have been held since the cave.

And then it snowed.

Not the polite, decorative snow of the lowlands. This was the early snow, the one the old-timers call *the liar* because it comes ahead of season and tells you winter will be reasonable, and then doesn't stop until spring. It came sideways, then downward, then sideways again, packing against the door until the hut felt less like shelter and more like a held breath.

Three days, the storm gave us. Three days in a room the size of a grave, with two women I'd known for less than a week, and nothing to do but talk.

I've spent nine years making sure I never had to do that.

* * *

The first day, we rationed ourselves like we rationed the food — small portions, carefully chewed.

Sable told the story of her family's farm the way you'd tell about a scar: factually, without flinching, but touching it only at the edges. Forty hectares on the eastern benchlands, terraced by her great-grandparents, worked by everyone since. Then the concession company's surveyors came through with their instruments and their clipboards, and the farm stopped existing — not physically, but on paper, which is the only place that matters anymore. It became "unimproved acreage within Parcel 44." Her father became a squatter on land his family had broken sod on for three generations.

"They didn't even look at the terraces," she said, staring into the fire. "Old Man Corven walked the whole property with his little brass transit and wrote down *scrubland, unworked*. My mother was standing right there. She was hanging laundry."

"And your father signed the appeal papers anyway," Maren said. It wasn't a question. She'd heard the story before, or enough of it.

"He filed eleven appeals. Eleven. Each one dismissed faster than the last. By the end they weren't even sending letters back — just stamps. *Insufficient documentation.*" Sable laughed, a short ugly sound. "You know what insufficient documentation means? It means the truth needs more paperwork than a lie."

Maren reached over and put her hand on Sable's knee, briefly, the way you'd steady a lamp. Then she went back to grinding dried herbs between her fingers — she did this constantly, some warden habit, keeping her hands busy the way other people keep their prayers.

I said almost nothing that day. I listened the way I track: quietly, from cover, noting everything. But somewhere in the dark after the fire burned down to coals, Sable asked me a question across the cold air — not accusing, just asking — and I found myself answering before my caution could catch up.

"Why do you run, Ivo? Really?"

I told her about the conviction. Not the sanitized version I keep loaded for strangers — *clerical error, mistaken identity, all cleared up eventually* — but the real one. How I'd been a ranger out of the Fort Meridian station, twenty-six years old, and I'd noticed things in the company's haulage ledgers that didn't add up. Water rights sold twice. Grazing permits issued over land already claimed. And how, three weeks after I filed a report, I was arrested for falsifying patrol logs — the very crime I'd reported, turned inside out and pinned on me like a medal awarded backward.

"They didn't frame me because I mattered," I said. "They framed me because I was cheap to frame. No family. No local ties. A man nobody would miss."

"And so you've been missing ever since," Maren said softly. "On purpose."

The fire popped. Outside, the wind tried the door and found it stubborn.

"It works," I said. "Mostly."

"No," Maren said, with the mild certainty of someone stating that water is wet. "It doesn't. You move like a man being chased, Ivo Zhou. Even when nothing's behind you. Especially then."

I wanted to argue. I lay there in the dark composing arguments, and every one of them collapsed under its own weight, and somewhere in the middle of that exercise I fell asleep — deeper than I'd slept in years, in a hut full of strangers, while a blizzard erased the world outside.

That should have frightened me. Instead I remember thinking, on the border of sleep: *so this is what it's like.*

* * *

The second day, Maren taught me the oath.

It happened the way she does everything — sideways, mid-task, as if the important thing were the task and the meaning were incidental. She was showing Sable how to read the archive seals, the wax-and-knot system the old wardens used to authenticate documents, using a practice seal she'd carried forty years in a pouch against her skin. And then she looked up at me and said, "Hold out your hand."

"Why?"

"Because I'm old and my arms are short. Hold out your hand."

I did. She placed the seal in my palm and closed my fingers over it, and her hands were surprisingly strong, surprisingly warm for a woman her age at altitude in a freezing hut.

"The wardens' oath isn't written down anywhere," she said. "That's deliberate. Writing can be forged. A thing spoken, witnessed, and remembered — that has to be carried." She nodded at my closed fist. "You're carrying it now. So say it."

"Maren—"

"You nearly died on that span holding a satchel of paper that wasn't yours. You roped yourself to two strangers rather than cut loose. Say the oath, ranger, or give the seal back and explain why."

There was no explaining why. That was the trap of it, and I think she knew. I looked at Sable, who was watching me with an expression I couldn't file under any of my usual categories — not suspicion, not pity. Something more dangerous. Recognition.

So I said it. Maren spoke the lines and I repeated them, my voice strange to myself in the small stone room:

"*What was buried, I will dig. What was erased, I will write. What was broken, I will hold — not because I am strong, but because the holding must be done by someone, and today that someone is me.*"

The words went through me like cold water through a crack. Simple words. Almost plain. But I've lived nine years on forged papers and false names, and there is a particular vertigo in promising, out loud, to be exactly what you are.

"There," Maren said, taking back the seal. "Now you're a warden. Badly paid, poorly housed, hunted by the same people as always. Congratulations."

Sable snorted. And then — I don't know who started it — we were laughing. All three of us, in a frozen hut in a whiteout, laughing at nothing, at everything, at the sheer absurdity of three fugitives administering oaths to each other over a fire made of stolen kindling. It went on too long and it wasn't really about anything funny, and when it finally wound down, Sable wiped her eyes and said:

"My mother used to say laughter like that means somebody's about to cry."

"She sounds wise," Maren said.

"She sounds like someone who's been poor a long time," Sable said, and then told us about her mother properly — the laundry line, the singing, the way she could make a stew out of anything and call it a recipe. And I found myself talking about my grandmother, who raised me after my parents went, who taught me to read stars and tracks and never once asked me where I was going, only whether I'd eaten.

By the time the fire burned down that night, the hut didn't feel like a grave anymore. It felt like a kitchen. I don't know when the change happened. Things like that don't announce themselves; you just look up one evening and the walls have moved closer together, in the good way.

* * *

The third day, the storm broke.

It went out the way it came in — all at once, no transition, the wind simply dying between one hour and the next, leaving a silence so total we could hear our own heartbeats. We dug out the door together, and the light that came in was so clean it hurt.

The pass was half-closed. The drifts had built against the southern wall of the saddle in a smooth white rampart ten feet high, and the northern descent — the fast way down, the way we'd planned — was a steep white throat with the wind-carved crust over who knew what voids. Maren studied it for a long time.

"We go slow," she said finally. "Roped. Ice-edges only, where the rock shows through."

"And below?" Sable asked.

Below, the pass opened onto the long scree fan that ran down toward the valley road, and the valley road ran to the registry office, and everything — the documents, the deadline, Sable's farm, my testimony, whatever I was now that I'd said an oath I couldn't unsay — waited at the bottom of it.

I swept the slopes with my eyes the way I'd once swept them for sign of poachers and smugglers, reading the white country the way my grandmother taught me. And that's when I saw it. Or rather, didn't see it.

No birds.

A valley that size, after a storm, should have been loud with them — choughs working the wind shear, a hawk riding the thermals off the dark rock. Nothing. The silence below wasn't empty. It was *held*, the way a room is held when someone's waiting in it behind a door.

Then the light caught it: a glint on the scree fan, maybe two miles down and east. Glass. Binocular glass, catching the sun and flashing it once, deliberately, before vanishing.

"They're not trying to hide," I said.

"Then they want us to know," Sable said.

Maren pulled her shawl tighter and looked down the pass with an expression I was beginning to know well — the calm of a woman who has buried a great deal and expects to bury more. "How many?"

"Can't tell. Four, maybe five. They'll have the road. They'll figure we have to come down or freeze up here, and either way they win."

"So we don't come down the way they expect," Sable said.

I looked at the white throat of the northern descent, at the crust hiding its hollows, at the wind-scoured ice beneath. I thought about ropes, and spans, and the weight of a satchel of paper that wasn't mine and had somehow become the heaviest thing I owned.

"No," I said. "We don't."

Behind us, the sun was dropping toward the ridge, and the pass was turning the color of old gold, beautiful and fatal, and somewhere below in that held silence, men were waiting with a warrant that had my real name on it.

I checked the rope knots. Twice.
