## 7. Descent Into Wind

I have outlived two husbands, one avalanche, and every warden who ever swore the oath beside me, so you would think I would know better than to trust a clear morning on the windward face of the escarpment. But the sky that dawn was the color of a rinsed blue plate, and the snow at the pass had stopped falling, and I made the mistake old women always make: I believed my body still belonged to me.

"We go down today," Ivo said, crouched at the mouth of the shepherd hut with his breath steaming. "The enforcers will have moved to the valley road by now. They'll expect us to come down the way we came up."

"Then we won't," Sable said.

She had been awake all night, I think. She sat with her back against the wall and her satchel of documents hugged to her chest like a sleeping child, and she had that look young people get when they have decided something is simple because they have not yet lived long enough to see how it isn't.

"The windward face," I said. "You want to take an old woman down the windward face."

"I want to take *you* down the windward face," Sable said, "because Ivo can't carry you and I can't carry him, and because nobody in the history of this range has ever looked for a fool coming down that side."

"She's right," Ivo said. "It's terrible logic. It might work."

That is the trouble with desperate people. We are excellent company for each other.

* * *

The first hour was almost kind. The face falls away from the pass in long shelves of gray stone stitched together with scrub pine, and the wind, which the mountain sends up that side like a dog sent to guard a door, was still asleep in its kennel. I went third on the rope — they insisted, and I let them insist, because there are battles worth winning and battles worth losing gracefully, and at seventy-one years old I have learned to tell them apart mostly by which ones make my knees hurt.

Sable led. She moved the way water moves, finding the low places without being told, and I watched her and thought about her mother, whom I had never met, and about land, which is the only inheritance that keeps breathing after you do. The girl was going home to a farm she might already have lost. She climbed anyway. There is a word for that in the old tongue of the wardens — *haleth*, it means something between stubbornness and prayer — and I said it under my breath as a blessing, because blessings are free and I am a miser with everything else.

Ivo came last, walking backward half the time, watching the ridgeline above us for the glint of drones or worse. He had been quiet since the hut. Quiet in the way a man is quiet when he has finished deciding something and is only waiting for the right moment to say it aloud. I had seen that quiet before, on other faces, in other winters, and it frightened me more than the drop did.

"Talk to me, warden," I called up to Sable. "A climbing song. Something vulgar."

"You'd know any?"

"I know all of them. I invented half."

So she sang, badly, and I corrected her verses with verses considerably worse, and Ivo laughed once — a short bark, surprised out of him — and for an hour we were just three fools on a rope, and I let myself forget what the quiet at the back of our line meant.

* * *

The mountain took its payment around noon.

We had come down past the tree line into a country of scree and stunted pine, where the slope steepened and the footing turned to loose shale that slid and rang like broken crockery underfoot. And somewhere in that stretch the thin air, which had been patient with me for three days in the hut, presented its bill.

It began as a ringing in my ears. Then the pines tilted, politely, as if bowing. Then the whole gray world swung sideways like a door on a bad hinge, and I heard Sable shout my name from very far away, and then I was sitting down, though I had no memory of sitting, and my hands were tingling and my heart was hammering like a bird trapped in a room.

"Maren." Her face swam over mine. "Maren, look at me. Look at me."

"Rude," I managed. "Interrupting a lady when she's resting."

"Don't joke. Breathe. Slow. With me."

I breathed. It cost more than it should have. Every breath felt drawn through wet cloth, and my legs, when I tried them, answered with the flat refusal of strangers. Altitude sickness does not care what oaths you have sworn or what archives you carry in your head. It is the one honest creditor on this whole escarpment.

Ivo crouched beside us, and I saw him look up-slope and down-slope and do the arithmetic that men like him do quickly and hate doing at all. We were too high to stay. Too slow to descend together before dark. And below us, somewhere on the valley road, the company's enforcers were waiting with their sealed orders and their clean gloves.

"There's a notch," he said. "Half a mile down, east side. Old shepherd track, sheltered. You could rest there overnight."

"And you?" Sable said.

"I'm going ahead."

"No," she said. Flat. Immediate. The way you refuse a thing you saw coming.

"Sable—"

"They'll catch you. You know they'll catch you."

"That's the idea."

The wind chose that moment to wake up. It came off the face in a long cold shove, flattening the pines, and I lay there against Sable's shoulder and watched these two children of the flats argue over me like the mountain itself wasn't listening. But mountains always listen. That is the price of living on one.

"Listen to me, both of you," I said, when I had breath enough. "I've carried those archives sixty years. Carried them when carrying them meant nothing but loneliness and frostbite. If I die on this slope, the truth dies with me, and the company wins by default, which is their favorite way of winning. So nobody is dying today. Nobody is being caught today. We—"

"He's already decided," Sable said bitterly. "Look at him. He decided in the hut three days ago."

And Ivo did not deny it. He knelt in the shale and took my hand, and his grip was steady, and I thought: here is a man who has spent ten years running from a name, and he is about to walk down a mountain and give it back.

"The warrant file," he said. "My conviction. It names the surveyor who falsified the escarpment maps — the same man whose signature Maren found in the cave records. If I'm taken, I'm taken to a frontier court. Courts keep records. Records can be read." He almost smiled. "Ten years I've run from that paper. Turns out it's the most valuable thing I own."

"You could die," Sable said. "Or worse. They don't need you alive."

"They need someone alive to blame. Better me than the documents." He stood, brushed the shale from his knees, and looked down the slope toward the notch, toward the valley, toward whatever waited there. "Besides. Somebody has to draw them off the road so you two can reach the registry. I'm fast, I'm loud, and I have a talent for making authority feel stupid. It's the only gift I've got."

"It isn't," Sable said.

He paused.

"It really isn't," she said again, quieter, and something passed between them that I pretended not to see, because some debts should be settled in private.

* * *

They rigged the carry with the rope and a pine bough, the way shepherds move injured ewes, and Sable took my weight across her shoulders like it was nothing, like I weighed less than her satchel of paper truths. She was stronger than she looked. She was stronger than she knew. I hung against her back with my cheek against her hair and listened to her breathe and thought about all the wardens who had carried me, in one way or another, and how the oath was never really about guarding treasure at all.

"What's the oath actually say?" she asked, after a while. "The real version. Not the one you made Ivo swear in the hut when he was too cold to argue."

I smiled into her hair. "*I hold what must be held until hands come to take it. I do not choose who comes. I only choose to be here when they do.*"

"That's it? That's the whole thing?"

"That's the whole thing. Wardens were never the point. The holding was. Anyone who holds is a warden." I closed my eyes against the sway of her walking. "Congratulations. You've been ordained by a sick old woman on a mountainside. Very prestigious. No pay."

Her laugh shook both of us, and I held on.

Below us, faint through the trees, Ivo's voice rose — singing, of all things, that same vulgar climbing song, loud and deliberately awful, a flare of noise moving east along the contour while we moved west and down. Drawing the eyes. Spending himself like a coin tossed to buy us passage.

"Will they hurt him?" Sable asked.

"Yes," I said, because she deserved the truth even now. "Probably. But he has decided that some things are worth being hurt for, and that decision, child, is the entire difference between surviving and living. He learned it late. Some of us learn it late."

We came down through the notch as the light went copper, and behind us the singing faded, and then stopped, and neither of us said anything about the stopping, because saying it would make it a fact, and facts at dusk on a mountain are heavier than rope.

* * *

We walked through the dark by Sable's memory of the map and my memory of the land, and near midnight we came down off the shoulder onto the flats' edge, where the ground finally leveled and my lungs remembered their trade. There was a farm road ahead, and beyond it, twenty miles on, the town with the registry office, and the permits that expired at week's end.

Sable set me down against a fence post and checked my pulse with two fingers, frowning like a doctor, which she was not, and like a daughter, which she was beginning to be whether either of us had authorized it or not.

"He gave himself up," she said. "For us. For documents. For a family that isn't even his."

"Family was never about blood," I said. "Blood is just the paperwork families arrive with. The rest is built. He's building."

"He didn't even say goodbye properly. He just started singing louder."

"That *was* the goodbye. Men like Ivo put their farewells inside jokes the way you put medicine inside honey. Didn't your mother teach you that?"

Something crossed her face — grief, old and familiar — and she looked away down the dark road toward the town lights, small and yellow and impossibly far.

"The clerk will refuse us," she said. "Unnotarized documents, no standing, no name. I've played it out a hundred times in my head. He'll refuse us, and everyone will watch him refuse us, and that will be that."

"Then you'll stand up in front of all of them," I said, "and you'll tell them what's under the escarpment, and whose signatures are on the false surveys, and whose farm was erased from the maps by a man who never walked the land he stole. And when you're afraid — and you will be — you'll remember that somewhere behind you there is a man in a cell who traded his freedom for your voice, and an old woman on a fence post who would get up and fight if her legs would permit it, which they emphatically will not."

She was quiet a moment. Then she lifted the satchel strap over her head and settled it across her chest, and squared her shoulders, and I watched a warden finish being made.

"Registry opens at dawn," she said.

"Dawn," I agreed. "And Sable—" She turned. "Whatever happens in that office, whoever refuses you, however many doors close — the holding doesn't stop. That's the oath. Doors are just weather."

Far off to the east, past the black shoulder of the escarpment, a light flickered — brief, deliberate, twice — a signal or a searchlight, impossible to tell at this distance. Sable saw it too. We looked at each other, and neither of us said his name, and we turned west toward the town and the dawn and the doors that were just weather.

Behind us, the mountain kept its silence, and somewhere inside it, I knew, the tide was already turning toward the cave.
