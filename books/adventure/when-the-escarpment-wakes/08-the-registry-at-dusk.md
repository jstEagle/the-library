## 8. The Registry at Dusk

The registry office of Harrow's Bend smelled like ink, dust, and the particular despair of people who had come to argue with bureaucracy and lost. I knew that smell. I'd grown up downwind of it.

We came down off the windward face with the sun already leaning hard toward the ridgeline, Maren a dead weight across my shoulders and my knees filing formal complaints with every step. The town announced itself in pieces — a fence post, then a goat, then the tin flash of rooftops — and by the time we reached the main street my legs had stopped being legs and become two suggestions someone else was operating.

"Put me down," Maren said, for the fourth time since the tree line.

"You said that an hour ago."

"And I meant it an hour ago. My feet are touching gravel now. Congratulations. You may release the warden."

I lowered her onto a bench outside the chandlery, where she sat with the terrible dignity of a woman running a fever of a hundred and two and pretending it was a mild inconvenience of the atmosphere. Her lips were gray. Her hands shook so badly she had to press them flat against her thighs to make them stop.

"You look like death's unpaid intern," I told her.

"I look like a woman who has walked further today than the escarpment itself has managed in four hundred years," she said. "Go. The office closes at dusk bell. Don't argue with me about carrying me, because you can't, because you're made of wet paper and guilt."

She wasn't wrong on either count.

* * *

The registry office was a squat stone building with a queue spilling out its door like something the building had eaten and couldn't keep down. Farmers mostly, holding deeds and grievances, a surveyor with his instruments wrapped in oilcloth, two women from the fishing co-op arguing quietly about mooring rights. At the front of it all, behind a counter worn smooth by decades of elbows, sat the clerk: a narrow man named Pellwick with spectacles, a stamp, and the untroubled face of someone who had never once been surprised by anything, including fire, flood, or the end of the world.

I joined the line and did arithmetic. Dusk bell rang at half past six. It was ten past five. There were eleven people ahead of me. Each transaction took between four minutes and forever, depending on how much the clerk enjoyed saying no.

I used the time to inventory myself. Satchel: present, strap repaired with bootlace, contents — the cave archives, three sealed folios, Maren's warden's ledger, and the falsified survey plates — intact, thank every indifferent god. Clothing: ruined. Face: I caught my reflection in the chandlery window and decided not to think about it. A woman who'd spent nine days crossing a mountain range does not arrive at a government office looking like a person whose paperwork should be taken seriously. She arrives looking like a cautionary tale.

The line moved. A farmer ahead of me lost an argument about a boundary marker and left clutching his hat. The fishing women won theirs, barely, and celebrated by not smiling. And then it was my turn, and Pellwick looked up at me over his spectacles with the expression of a man preparing to enjoy a small, routine disappointment.

"Name."

"Sable Voss."

"Purpose."

"Land claim verification. Parcel forty-one, the Voss holding, upper Alder Creek." I set the satchel on the counter and began unbuckling it. "I have original documents from the escarpment archive establishing prior claim and demonstrating that the concession surveys filed against this parcel were—"

"Stop." He raised one hand, palm out, gentle as a man stopping traffic he didn't believe existed. "Are these documents notarized?"

"Notarized." I said the word the way you'd repeat an insult to be sure you'd heard it correctly. "These documents are four hundred years old. They're carved slate and sealed vellum out of the warden's archive under the escarpment. Notarization as a concept postdates them by roughly three centuries."

"Then they're not admissible." He was already reaching for his stamp, already looking past me to the next person in line. "Unnotarized documents cannot be entered into the registry. Come back with certified copies from a licensed notary in—"

"There is no notary who will touch these," I said, "because the company that wrote the surveys you're about to accept pays for every notary within two hundred miles, and the permits expire tonight."

"Permits expire when the law says they expire. Next."

Something in my chest went very quiet and very cold, which is what it does instead of panicking. Nine days. A rope over a collapsed span. Maren coughing blood-flecked air into her scarf at eleven thousand feet while I carried her satchel because she couldn't carry her own name. Ivo walking back down into the enforcers' guns with his hands up and a smile like a man settling an old debt, buying us this hour with his freedom. All of it funneling down to a stamp and a man who wanted his tea.

"No," I said.

Pellwick paused. Around us, the queue went still in that way crowds do — everyone suddenly fascinated, everyone pretending not to be.

"I beg your pardon?"

"No. You don't get 'next.' You get the truth, and then you get to decide what kind of man stamps things after hearing it." I climbed onto the bench beside the counter before my sense of self-preservation could file a complaint, turned around, and faced the room. Eleven strangers. A fly buzzing against the window. One boy, maybe twelve, sitting with his mother, staring at me with his mouth open.

Good, I thought. Audiences are easier when they're young enough to still want the world to be interesting.

"My name is Sable Voss," I said, "and everything I'm about to tell you is either going to save my family's farm or get me arrested, and honestly at this point both feel like progress."

Someone near the door laughed. That helped. Laughter means they're listening.

"Three weeks ago the Meridian Concession Company filed survey plates with this registry — plates certifying that the high escarpment country was unclaimed, uninhabited, and legally vacant. Those permits finalize at dusk bell tonight. What the surveys didn't certify — what they were written specifically to erase — is that the escarpment has been inhabited for four hundred years. There is an archive under the coast cliffs older than any deed in this building. Stone records. Sealed folios. Grant-charters signed by the founders of every settlement from here to the salt flats, granting the high country to its peoples in perpetuity — perpetuity being a word the company's lawyers apparently haven't met."

I pulled the first folio from the satchel and held it up. The vellum inside was stiff as frost, the founder's seal pressed deep in green wax, and even across the room you could see it was real — real the way old things are real, with a weight new paper doesn't bother faking.

"The woman who kept that archive is named Maren Ostergaard. She is the last warden of the escarpment, and right now she is sitting on a bench outside the chandlery with a fever she earned carrying those records over a mountain pass in early snow, because the alternative was letting them burn with everything else the company wants buried. She is seventy-one years old. She made that crossing so that the truth would exist somewhere other than a locked cave the company intends to seal with all of us on the wrong side of it."

A murmur ran through the queue. The surveyor with the oilcloth bundle shifted his weight. The mother put her hand on her son's shoulder, though whether to hold him back or steady herself, I couldn't say.

"And there's more, because of course there is. The survey plates themselves are forged. The originals were altered — dates moved, claims struck, boundaries redrawn — before they were ever filed here. I know because I've held both versions. I know because the man who could prove it, a ranger named Ivo Zhou, is at this moment in the company's custody precisely so that proof never reaches this room. They took him on the mountain two days ago. He let them. He walked into their guns so I could walk into yours."

My voice cracked on that last part. I let it. Wry only carries you so far; after that you need the truth underneath it, raw side up.

"So no, Mr. Pellwick, I don't have notarized copies. I have the actual past, carried out of the ground by the last person alive sworn to keep it. And in—" I checked the light through the window, doing the arithmetic one more time, "—about seventy minutes, if you stamp nothing, the permits finalize, the high country gets fenced, and every one of you learns what your own signature looks like on somebody else's theft."

Silence. The fly hit the window again. Somewhere outside, a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

Pellwick removed his spectacles, polished them on his sleeve with maddening thoroughness, and put them back on. When he spoke, his voice was quieter than before, which I hadn't expected.

"Miss Voss. Do you know why this office requires notarization?"

"To protect the registry from fraud."

"To protect the registry from *me*," he said. "From clerks. From anyone paid, pressured, or persuaded to enter what shouldn't be entered. The rule exists because rules are all a clerk has." He looked down at the counter, at his stamp, at his own hands. "It also exists so that when a clerk decides to break it, everyone knows exactly what it cost him."

He opened a drawer. From it he took a form I'd never seen — thick stock, red border, a heading reading EXCEPTIONAL ENTRY — VERBAL ATTESTATION — and laid it flat.

"This provision hasn't been invoked in this office in nineteen years," he said. "It requires a declarant to state their claim publicly, before witnesses, and it requires those witnesses to stand and be counted by name. If fewer than five stand, the entry fails and we never speak of it again. If five stand—" he glanced at the clock, then at me, "—then the entry goes in, provisional, pending review, and the permits cannot finalize against a provisional entry. The review board convenes in the spring."

He picked up his pen. "So. Declarant. State your claim."

I stated it. Parcel forty-one. The Voss holding. Prior claim per the escarpment archive, folios one through three, attested by the last warden, Maren Ostergaard, whose statement I would fetch from the bench outside if her fever allowed her to stand, which it might not, which terrified me more than the enforcers had.

And then Pellwick did the thing I'll remember until I die. He stood up himself, behind his own counter, in front of his own queue, and said, "Witness, Pellwick, registrar," in a voice that dared anyone to laugh.

The surveyor stepped forward. "Witness, Anselm Crete, licensed surveyor — and I'll add that I've always wondered why my instruments read differently up-country than the filed plates say they should."

The mother stood, pulling her son up with her. "Witness, Petra Ilves. Witness, Tam Ilves." The boy grinned like he'd been handed a sword.

One more needed. Four standing, dusk bell twenty minutes out, and the room full of people suddenly finding their boots fascinating.

Then the door opened, and the cold came in with her. Maren stood in the doorway, upright through what must have been pure spite, one hand braced on the frame, her voice cracked but carrying.

"Witness, Maren Ostergaard," she said, "last warden of the escarpment — and I have brought the seal to prove it."

She held up her hand, and in it, the warden's ring, old bronze, worn smooth by four hundred years of exactly this.

Pellwick stamped the form. The sound it made was small. It sounded like a mountain waking up anyway.

And that was when we heard the engines — three of them, coming up the main street, slow and unhurried, the way men drive when they already know how the story ends.

They were about to find out they hadn't read the latest draft.
