## 9. Testimony

They took my boots first.

I want to say I understood the logic of it — a man who'd spent seven years running was, by definition, a man whose boots were a flight risk — but mostly it just felt like an insult delivered with bureaucratic efficiency. The enforcer who confiscated them had kind eyes and terrible posture, and he wrote *one (1) pair, worn* on his manifest like my boots were evidence of something. Which, in a way, they were. Evidence that Ivo Zhou had finally stopped running, and done it on purpose, which was the part nobody in the company's custody chain could quite believe.

"You could've kept going," the tall one said. He was the one who'd caught me on the windward switchbacks, or rather, the one I'd let catch me, jogging downhill with my hands half-raised like a man greeting a bus. "We'd have chased you to the coast. Might've lost you in the salt."

"I know," I said.

"Then why?"

It was a fair question. It deserved better than the answer I gave him, which was nothing, because I didn't have the words yet. Words were what I was saving. I'd been saving them for years without knowing it — every forged signature, every false name, every night I slept in a ditch with one ear open, all of it apparently just compound interest accruing toward this: a holding cell in the concession company's regional office, bootless, waiting to be asked the right question by someone with the authority to make my answers matter.

The cell wasn't cruel. That was the unsettling thing about it. A cot, a blanket that smelled of machine detergent, a window of reinforced glass looking out on a yard where enforcers drilled in the mornings. They fed me twice a day. They let me read the warrant file they'd taken off Sable at the well a lifetime ago — my file, returned to me now like a mirror I'd been avoiding. *Zhou, Ivo. Convicted, falsification of survey records, obstruction.* Seven years old. Seven years of running from a crime I hadn't committed, committed against me by people who'd since gotten very good at committing larger versions of it.

I read it until I could recite it. Then I read it again, because there's a particular kind of courage that only arrives after you've made yourself sick of your own fear.

* * *

On the third day, a woman came to see me. Company counsel, by her coat; the kind of grey that costs more than most people's houses. She sat across the table with a tablet she never looked at and studied me the way you study weather.

"Ivo Zhou," she said. "Or do you prefer another name? You've had several, I understand."

"I've had several coats," I said. "The name's held up fine."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth. Not warmth. Recognition, maybe — the look of someone who'd read my file too and found it more interesting than threatening. "Here's how this goes," she said. "In four days, the concession permits finalize. After that, the escarpment claims are administratively closed. Whatever documents your associates are carrying become historical curiosities. You, meanwhile, will be processed as a fugitive and transferred to the frontier court at Graywater. There is a version of events where that transfer is quiet and your testimony is never requested, because dead men and silent men don't complicate surveys." She let that sit between us like a stone on a table. "And there is a version where you're useful."

"You're offering me a choice between disappearing and informing."

"I'm offering you a choice between two kinds of silence."

I thought about Sable then. I'd been thinking about her for three days, in the gaps between reading my own file — Sable climbing the switchbacks with Maren on her back, Sable standing up in a registry office full of strangers because courage was the only currency she had left. I'd run ahead to draw the enforcers off so those two could make the descent. I'd gotten exactly what I'd planned: captured, deliberate, a decoy with a heartbeat. What I hadn't planned, back on the mountain, was how much I'd hate the silence afterward. Silence had been my trade for seven years. I'd mistaken it for safety, the way you mistake a locked door for a home.

"The second kind," I said. "Tell me about the second kind."

She tilted her head. "You'd testify. Openly. Under your real name, under your real conviction, before the frontier court at Graywater. You'd put the falsified surveys on the record — the ones they used to erase the escarpment peoples' claims, and the ones they used to convict you when you flagged them eight years ago. You'd be handing the company its own noose and calling it a rope bridge." She paused. "You should understand what that costs. Your pardon isn't in my gift. Testifying doesn't undo the conviction. It might, eventually, through review — but 'eventually' is a word lawyers use when they mean *maybe*. You could walk out of Graywater still guilty on paper. You could spend years clearing your name. Or never."

"And if I stay silent?"

"Then you're transferred quietly, and we'll see what the road provides."

There it was. I almost laughed. Seven years of running, and here was the whole architecture of my life laid out in two sentences: keep moving and maybe survive, or stand still and maybe matter.

"Maren Voss," I said. "The warden. Is she alive?"

"That's not—"

"Is she alive? And the woman with her — Sable. Did they reach the registry?"

The counsel looked at me for a long moment, and whatever she saw in my face made her decide I was worth the truth. "They reached it. The claim was lodged. It's contested — unnotarized documents, procedural challenges — but it's lodged, and it bought them a hearing. Four days. Same clock as the permits." She gathered her coat. "You should know the hearing goes nowhere without corroboration. A thief's word and a dying warden's archive aren't enough. The court needs the man the fraud was built on. It needs *Zhou*, the original witness, the one they convicted to bury him." At the door she stopped. "Which is why they'll fight dirty to keep you silent. Consider that before you decide anything loudly."

She left. I sat with my warrant file and did the arithmetic of my life.

* * *

That night I dreamed about the escarpment. Not the caves, not the archives with their carved ledgers older than any registry in the territory — I dreamed about the wind on the switchbacks, the particular sound it makes coming off the high snowfields, like the range itself breathing. In the dream Maren was teaching me the warden's oath again, her voice cracked and patient: *What is buried is not lost. What is erased is not gone. I am the record now.* I'd repeated it in a shepherd hut while snow sealed the pass, thinking it was a nice piece of liturgy, the kind of thing old people say to make cold rooms feel less empty. In the dream I understood it differently. The oath wasn't about remembering. It was about refusing. Refusing to let erasure finish its work. Every year I'd run, I'd been letting it finish its work on me — erasing Zhou, the surveyor, the man who'd once believed that a map was a promise you made to everyone who'd ever live on the land it described.

I woke up certain. Certainty, in my experience, lasts about as long as morning coffee, so I moved fast.

I asked for paper. The guard — kind eyes, terrible posture — brought me three sheets and a pencil stub, watching me like I might eat them. I wrote out everything. The original survey, the one I'd signed and then repudiated. The names on the falsification orders, or the ones I remembered; eight years blurs edges. The date they arrested me, the questions they asked, the offer they made me — sign a retraction, keep your pension, forget the escarpment exists — and my answer, which was no, and the price of no, which was everything. I wrote until the pencil stub was a thumbnail and my hand ached, and then I wrote the last line, the one I'd been carrying since the windward face:

*I, Ivo Zhou, surrender voluntarily and testify under my true name. I ask no protection. I ask only that the record be corrected.*

* * *

The courtroom at Graywater smelled of wood polish and wet wool, and it was smaller than I'd imagined — I'd spent seven years building it into a cathedral of judgment in my head, and instead it was a room with bad acoustics and a stove that ticked. The public benches were full, though. Word travels fast in frontier towns, faster than snowmelt, and somewhere between the registry and the courthouse Sable's story had grown legs. I scanned the crowd for her face and found it third row left, next to a bundled figure I knew even wrapped in blankets was Maren, upright by sheer administrative stubbornness. Sable looked like she hadn't slept in a week. When our eyes met she didn't smile. She nodded, once, the way you nod across a rope span — *I've got my end.*

The company sent six lawyers. Six. For a surveyor in borrowed boots.

They went first, naturally, and they were good — smooth men painting me as unstable, a fugitive, a forger of identities whose every document was suspect, including, presumably, the ones I was about to present. The magistrate, a dry woman named Okonkwo with spectacles like winter ice, listened with her chin on her fist and then said, "The accused will state his name for the record."

And there it was. The moment I'd run from for seven years, arrived at last, wearing a cheap suit of ordinary procedure.

"My name," I said, and my voice cracked on the first word, which I decided to let stand, because hiding the crack would have been one more forgery, "is Ivo Zhou. Surveyor, fourth class, license 4471-C. Eight years ago I certified a boundary survey of the eastern escarpment. Then I discovered it had been altered before filing — elevations shifted, watercourses removed, and eleven registered community claims replaced with blank terrain. I reported it. For that report I was convicted of the falsification myself, and I have been running from that conviction ever since." I set my three handwritten pages on the rail. "These are my originals. My memory, my hand, my name. I'm done renting other people's names. This one has work to do."

The room made a sound — not a gasp, frontier crowds are too weathered for gasps, more a collective inhale, the sound of a hundred people deciding to pay attention.

Okonkwo read my pages for a long time. The company lawyers conferred in a huddle that got progressively less smooth. Somewhere behind me a door opened and closed, and I didn't turn around, because turning around felt like checking for exits, and I had decided, permanently, to stop checking for exits.

"Surveyor Zhou," Okonkwo said at last. "Your conviction was entered on the basis of a comparison between your filed certification and these alleged originals. If your account is accurate, the filed certification is the forgery, and the conviction is void in substance if not yet in law." She looked over her spectacles. "Do you understand that I cannot simply void it today?"

"I understand," I said. "I'm not asking for today. I'm asking for the record."

"Then the record is amended pending formal review." She struck a note on her desk that sounded, absurdly, like the ticking stove. "Furthermore — the escarpment claim lodged at the registry cites corroborating testimony from the original certifying surveyor. That citation now stands substantiated. Formal review of all concession permits touching the eastern escarpment is opened effective immediately, with suspension of finalization until the review concludes." She paused, and something almost human crossed the dry landscape of her face. "Four days remained on those permits. They will not finalize in four days. They may not finalize at all."

The company lawyers rose as one, like a flock deciding on a direction, and began producing objections, and Okonkwo began absorbing them the way a cliff absorbs weather, and none of it touched me. Because Sable was on her feet in the third row, and Maren beside her, and the old woman's hand was raised — not in protest, but in the small, precise gesture wardens use, I'd learned, to mark a thing as *witnessed*.

Afterward, in the corridor, they processed me back toward custody — light service, they were saying, suspended sentence, cooperative testimony, terms I barely heard — and Sable broke from the crowd and walked beside me between two guards as casually as if we were crossing the flats again.

"You stank in there," she said. "Voice cracking. Borrowed boots."

"Had to establish credibility somehow."

Her mouth twitched. Then, quieter: "Maren says the review will take seasons. She says you'll be assigned to the escarpment district for your service. She says—" Sable stopped, and I watched her decide whether to say the rest. "She says the wardens need a surveyor. Someone who knows what maps are supposed to promise."

I looked down at my borrowed boots on the Graywater flagstones, and for the first time in seven years, the ground under them didn't feel temporary.

"Tell her," I said, "that I've stopped running. She should check whether I can stop working, too."

Sable laughed — actually laughed, loud enough that a guard flinched — and then the guards walked me on, and behind us the courthouse doors swung open onto the street, where a crowd was already gathering around a news-sheet, and I heard, drifting up the corridor, the first murmurs of a word I'd learn the shape of soon enough:

*Suspended. The permits — suspended pending review.*

And beneath it, sharper, angrier, from men in company grey pushing through the crowd: not finished. Not by half.
