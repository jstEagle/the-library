## 10. When the Escarpment Wakes

### Sable

The first thing the thaw did was drown the south field.

I stood on the porch of my family's farm with my boots in six inches of meltwater and thought: this is mine. All of it, including the mud. Especially the mud. The registry clerk's stamp had gone onto our claim papers in November, official as a scar, and when the concession permits were suspended pending review in January, nobody came to take it back. Nobody came at all, except the surveyors — honest ones this time, hired by the review board, who walked our boundaries with instruments and kept asking questions I actually enjoyed answering.

My mother still didn't quite believe it. She kept setting an extra plate out of habit and then standing there holding it, like the plate itself might be revoked. My father had taken to reading the permit suspension notice aloud at breakfast, every morning, like scripture. "Suspended pending formal review," he'd say, and my little brother would groan, and I'd drink my tea and let them have their rituals. People need rituals after a famine. Ours had been a famine of paperwork.

The strange part was how ordinary everything felt. I had crossed a mountain range in early snows carrying documents that could have gotten us all shot, and now my great crisis was drainage ditches. There is a version of me from two years ago who would have found that insulting. The current version finds it miraculous. You fight for something so hard that when you win it, the winning looks like nothing at all — just water where water should be, just a farm that stays a farm, just mornings that don't require a plan for running.

Maren arrived on the fourth day of thaw, riding a mule she refused to name because, she said, naming livestock was how you ended up talking to them. She stayed three nights, slept in my room, ate us nearly into ruin, and on the last evening she sat me down at the kitchen table the way she'd once sat me down in a cave full of ten-thousand-year-old archives.

"The wardens' school opens at solstice," she said. "Two students confirmed. A third place is yours if you want it."

"I have a farm."

"You have a farm *and* a warden's blood in you, girl, and the escarpment doesn't care about your scheduling conflicts." She leaned forward. Her hands were steadier than they'd been on the pass, but her eyes hadn't changed at all — those eyes that had watched alone for forty years and learned to see through stone. "The cave woke up, Sable. Word travels. People are coming to look, to ask, to claim. It needs wardens who remember why the doors were sealed in the first place."

I looked out the window at my drowned south field, at my mother hanging laundry in air that finally smelled like growing things instead of fear.

"When do I start?"

She smiled like a door opening. "You already did. First lesson: never trust a dry well."

### Maren

Forty years I kept the archive alone, and I will tell you what no one asks about solitude: it makes you a terrible teacher. I had forgotten how long it takes hands to learn a lock, how many times a student will ask *why* before the *how* means anything. Sable asked *why* more than anyone I had ever taught, and I discovered, to my considerable irritation, that I loved it.

The second student was a shepherd boy named Tam with a stammer and a memory like wet clay — everything stuck to it. The third was a surveyor's daughter who had read her father's falsified maps and come to apologize to a mountain. We were an absurd institution: one old woman, three misfits, and a mule, meeting in a sea cave twice a day when the tide permitted, which meant the escarpment itself set our class schedule. I approved. Any school worth attending should be governed by something older and less negotiable than a headmistress.

In spring the cave changed. The low tide pulled back farther than I'd ever recorded, and we found galleries none of my predecessors had marked — a chamber of seed vaults, still sealed; a wall of names going back further than any registry in the territory. Sable stood in front of that wall a long time. Then she took out her knife and cut her initials small and neat beneath a column of names four thousand years old, and when I raised an eyebrow she said, "What? I'm in the ledger now. Might as well sign it."

I let it stand. The wardens before me would have objected. But the wardens before me had also believed the doors could stay shut forever, and look how that went.

The company's enforcers were gone by then — dismissed, mostly, when the review board published the falsified surveys and the concession charter collapsed like a bad roof. A few lingered in the valley towns, drinking and telling stories in which they had been heroes. Let them. Every empire's foot soldiers rewrite themselves eventually. What mattered was that the escarpment peoples' claims were back in the ledgers, notarized, witnessed, real — and that the review had been triggered by testimony given freely, under a true name, by a man who had spent seven years believing his name was the most dangerous thing he owned.

Ivo came up to the cave on the equinox. He stood in the entry gallery with his hat in his hands like a man at a funeral, and I made him recite the warden's oath, all of it, word for word, because he had learned it in a freezing hut in December and I wanted to know if it had survived the thaw.

It had. He stumbled only once — on the line about remaining — and I pretended not to notice, which is the kindest thing a teacher can do.

### Ivo

They gave me ninety days of light service and a choice of posting, which is frontier-court language for: we don't know what to do with you either. I could have picked anywhere. Coastal patrol. Registry work in the capital, even, where they'd have given me a desk and a title and a reason to keep moving every few years, the way I always had.

I picked the escarpment.

The sentence said *trail maintenance and boundary verification under supervision*, and the supervision turned out to be Maren, who supervised the way a cliff supervises erosion. My first assignment was repairing the switchback wall above the frozen pass — the same pass where we'd sheltered three days while the snow closed the world outside our little hut and opened something inside it. I laid stone where the melt had undercut the trail, and every rock I set felt like a sentence in a letter I'd been writing my whole life without knowing the address.

Here is what I know about running: it works. That's the trap of it. For seven years it worked perfectly. New territory, new papers, new name spelled slightly differently, and no one ever close enough to ask the wrong question. I told myself I was free. I was actually just fast.

Then a thief stole my warrant file at a dry well, an old woman fed me soup in a cave full of dead civilizations, and somewhere between a rope over a collapsed span and a shepherd hut full of stories, I stopped being fast. I got captured on purpose on a mountainside and it was the first decision in seven years that didn't feel like fleeing. Testifying under my real name — Ivo Zhou, convicted, pardoned, reviewed, reinstated — should have been the end of me. Instead it was the end of the running, which turned out to be a different thing entirely.

Spring on the escarpment smells like wet stone and manure and the green insistence of things that refuse to die. I walked down from the pass in April to check the boundary markers and found Sable's family farm half-drowned and thriving, her mother pressing bread on me like I was underweight, her father reading the suspension notice aloud again — I think he just likes the sound of it now. Sable walked the field edge with me, mud to her knees, and pointed out where the old survey stakes had been. "Company markers," she said. "We're leaving two of them up. Turned around backward, so they point at our land instead of away from it." She grinned. "Maren says that's the most warden-like thing she's ever heard."

Evenings, I climbed to the cave when the tide allowed, and Maren drilled me on the oath, and Tam asked me eleven hundred questions about enforcers, and the surveyor's daughter redrew the falsified maps with corrections in red ink until the truth of the range lay flat and legible on paper. I had spent years sleeping in places I refused to learn the names of. Now I knew the name of every gully between the pass and the sea, and knowing them felt like being known.

On the last night of spring's first month, Sable and I sat on the rim above the cave mouth watching the tide come in and seal the doors for the night. Below us, the water folded over the entrance the way it had for ten thousand years, patient, certain, keeping what it was given.

"You could still go," she said. "Sentence is served in June. Papers are clean. Nobody's looking for you anymore."

"That's exactly it," I said. "Nobody's looking."

She considered that with the expression she wore when a lock finally gave. "So what keeps you?"

I thought about the honest answer, which was long, and gave her the short version instead: "The trail above the pass still needs work. And somebody has to supervise Tam before he asks a question that collapses the cave."

She laughed, and the sound went down into the dark below us, and I swear the tide seemed to carry it inward, deeper, past the sealed doors, into all those galleries of names and seeds and patient stone.

Later — much later, when the others had gone and the mule had been (against Maren's policy) named — I stood alone at the cave mouth with my hand flat against the wet rock, and I felt it. Faint, deep, unmistakable. Not water. Not wind.

Waking.

Something under the escarpment, older than the wardens, older than the frauds and the warrants and the running — turning over in its sleep like a person who has decided, at last, that it is safe to open its eyes.

And I understood that whatever it remembered, whatever it wanted when it fully woke, it would want wardens. It would want people who stayed.

I took my hand off the stone and went home.
