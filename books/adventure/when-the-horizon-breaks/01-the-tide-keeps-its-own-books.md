## 1. The Tide Keeps Its Own Books

The tide went out at 4:17 in the morning, and Ines Nakamura was there to meet it, because the tide kept its own books and it did not extend credit.

She worked by headlamp in the blue-black hour below the cliffs of Punta Serrada, on a terrace of wave-cut rock that three months ago had belonged to a man named Ovidio Ferran. Ferran had held a salvage permit for this stretch of coast for two years, during which time he had visited it exactly twice, gotten sunburned both times, and concluded — correctly, as far as the archaeology went, incorrectly in every other way that mattered — that there was nothing here worth his trouble. When the permit came up for renewal he had let it lapse, the way men like Ferran always did, and the way women like Ines never could.

Now the terrace was hers. Not legally — legally it belonged to whoever filed next, and the filing fee alone was more than she owed the chandlery — but hers in the older sense, the sense that predated permits: hers because she was the one who showed up when the water pulled back and gave her ninety minutes of exposed seabed to read.

And the seabed, tonight, was talking.

She knelt in six inches of retreating water over a scatter of ballast stones, running her fingers along their faces the way her father had taught her before she was old enough to know she was learning anything. *Stone remembers*, he used to say, crouched beside her on some other shore in some other life, turning a nodule of coral-encrusted rock in his broad hands. *Wood rots, iron rusts, paper drowns. Stone just sits there and keeps its mouth shut until you ask the right question.* She asked the question now, tilting each stone into the lamplight, looking for the smoothness on one face and the roughness on the other that said these rocks had ridden in a ship's belly across an ocean and been dumped in a hurry.

They had. And they were arranged wrong.

Ballast scattered from a wreck spreads in a fan, downstream of whatever current did the scattering. These stones lay in a line — a short, stubborn line, maybe four meters long, oriented north-northeast, parallel to the cliff base. A ship's keel-line, or what remained of one, its timbers long since eaten or carried off, but its skeleton's shadow still pressed into the seabed like a footprint in drying cement.

Ines sat back on her heels and let out a breath she hadn't known she was holding, and watched it fog the cold air inside her headlamp's cone.

A wreck. An undocumented wreck, sitting forty meters from a permit boundary that had just lapsed, on a coastline where the historical record said no vessel of any consequence had ever run aground.

Her first thought — the professional thought, the thought she'd have charged a client for — was: *unrecorded means unclaimed means mine*. Her second thought arrived half a heartbeat behind it, the way the bad thoughts always did, wearing her father's voice: *Unrecorded doesn't mean undiscovered, mija. It means someone got there first and decided you shouldn't know.*

She told the voice to shut up. The voice, as usual, ignored her.

* * *

By full light she had staked the site with survey flags torn from an old rice bag and marked with her initials in waterproof ink, which would not stop anyone from taking anything but would establish, if it ever came to a hearing, that she had been here first and knew what she was looking at. Then she climbed the goat track back up the cliff face, hauling her kit bag, and stood at the top with her hands on her knees, breathing hard, and looked out at the morning she'd earned.

Isla Verde in the early light was the kind of beautiful that people paid money to look at and locals stopped seeing by the age of ten. The cliffs ran gold-to-rust along the eastern shore; beyond them the harbor town of Puerto Aliso stacked itself up the hillside in white and faded teal, church tower at the crown, fish market already shouting at the waterline. Fishing boats were coming in with the dawn catch, their wakes stitching silver seams across a sea so calm it looked upholstered. Somewhere inland a rooster was making an idiot of itself, as roosters did.

Ines catalogued it all without warmth, the way she catalogued everything: tide high at 10:40, wind out of the southeast building toward afternoon, tourist season two weeks past peak, which meant the hostelries would start discounting rooms and the chandlery would start pressing harder about her account. Six hundred and forty pesos, plus the air fill she'd put on credit last week, plus the rope. Call it seven hundred. Call it more than she had.

She was twenty-nine years old and she lived in a rented room above a net-mender's shed with a roof that leaked in exactly the pattern of the constellations, and she made her living the way a gull made its living: off what other people dropped, discarded, or failed to notice. Other salvors' expired permits. Other divers' abandoned grids. Other people's discarded everything. She was good enough at it that the harbormaster's office had started keeping a folder on her — she'd seen it once, a thin folder, *NAKAMURA, I., IRREGULAR ACTIVITIES* — and not good enough at it that the folder had ever turned into anything except a reason to be polite to the harbormaster's secretary, who liked rum and gossip in equal measure.

Seven hundred pesos. She turned the number over the way she'd turned the ballast stone, looking for a face on it.

There was one more option, and she hated it, and she took the cliff road toward town anyway because hating an option had never once stopped it from being the only one.

* * *

Doña Petralia ran the pawnshop on Calle del Cordel, and she ran it the way a spider runs a web: patiently, comprehensively, and with a certain dignity that made the eating of flies seem almost ceremonial. She was seventy-some years old, built like a figurehead, and she had known Ines's father.

That last fact hung between them every time Ines pushed through the shop's door and set off the bell, and Doña Petralia never once acknowledged it directly, which was its own kind of acknowledgment.

"Niña Nakamura." She looked up from her ledger. Behind her, shelves climbed to the ceiling, dense with the island's surrendered possessions: guitars, wedding silver, diving watches, a taxidermied iguana nobody had ever claimed. "You are selling something today or borrowing against something today?"

"Selling." Ines set the sextant on the glass counter. It had been her grandfather's, then her father's, then hers, and it was the finest object she owned, brass and ebony, its arc engraved with a maker's name from a shipyard that no longer existed. She had sworn twice before, in worse months than this one, that she would never pawn it, and both times she had taken it back out of the shop within the week, poorer but intact in the place where it counted.

Doña Petralia picked it up without hurry. Turned it. Held it to the window light and sighted along the index arm at something only she could see — perhaps the horizon, perhaps the past.

"This is Kenji Nakamura's instrument," she said.

"It's mine."

"Mm." She set it down on a square of velvet with the gentleness of a woman handling a relic, which, Ines supposed bitterly, was exactly what it was. "Two hundred."

"It's worth eight hundred anywhere on the mainland."

"You are not on the mainland." Doña Petralia folded her hands atop the ledger. "Two hundred and fifty, and I will keep it in my own cabinet, not on the shelf, and you may redeem it whenever you wish at the same price, no interest for sixty days. Because I knew your father. And because I do not think you will be gone longer than sixty days."

"I'm not coming back for it at all," Ines said. "Sell it. Sell it and give me three hundred."

"No." The old woman's voice didn't rise; it simply became final, the way bedrock is final. "Two hundred and fifty, on redemption terms. You will take it or leave it, niña, but I have watched you sell things for six years now and you have never once sold a thing you truly wanted gone. You always come back. Take the two hundred and fifty and go find whatever it is you're going to find, and stop pretending you're the kind of person who pawns her grandfather's sextant."

Ines stared at her. The bell over the door ticked in a draft. On the shelf behind the counter, between a chipped Madonna and a brass ship's bell polished bright by someone's devotion, sat a framed photograph Ines had never let herself look at directly in six years: a younger Doña Petralia, laughing, one hand on the shoulder of a lean dark-haired man in field clothes crouched beside a trench, grinning at the camera with dirt on his cheek and the whole future in his face.

Kenji Nakamura, eleven years ago. Before. When his name had opened doors instead of closing them, when *Nakamura survey* had meant something on this island besides a cautionary tale, when Ines had still signed her name with pride instead of flinching at the sound of it in other people's mouths.

She took the two hundred and fifty.

* * *

The rest of the day she spent being competent, because competence was the one thing nobody could repossess.

At the harbormaster's office she paid down two hundred of her account and talked Secretary Luz into letting the remaining balance ride another month, using nothing but arithmetic performed aloud and a rumor about the customs inspector's wife that she delivered like a gift rather than a weapon. At the chandlery she bought rope, a new dive slate, and a tin of lamp oil, and talked Chinto down fifteen percent by pointing out a hairline crack in one of his stock masks that he swore afterward he'd known about all along. At the fish market she traded a favor — she'd once found a lost anchor for old Bautista's son, and favors on Isla Verde compounded like interest — for two kilos of red snapper and a length of lead sheeting she intended to cut into weights.

By late afternoon she was home, if the room above the net-mender's shed deserved the word, frying snapper on a single burner while the light went amber through the salt-hazed window. The room contained a cot, a table, a chair, a sea chest, and four years of notebooks — her own surveys, her own tide logs, kept in the same format her father had taught her, because the format was the best thing he'd ever given her and formats, unlike fathers, could be trusted.

She ate standing up, looking at the wall where she'd pinned the chart of the eastern coast, and found her eyes going again and again to the small penciled cross she'd added that morning. *Wreck, unrecorded, keel-line NNE.* Below it, in her own handwriting, the note she'd written without deciding to: *Why does nothing else lie near it?*

Because wrecks attracted wrecks, that was why — ships broke on the same hazards, clustered like bones in the same channels. A lone wreck on an empty stretch of coast meant either extraordinary bad luck or something else entirely. A jettisoned cargo. A scuttling. A ship put down deliberately, quietly, by people who very much did not want it found.

Or — and here was the thought she kept stepping around, the way you step around a hole in a floor you've walked your whole life — a ship that *had* been found, decades ago, by someone who wrote reports for a living, and whose finding of it had somehow never entered any record she could locate. She had spent the whole climb up the cliff telling herself the coincidence meant nothing. That the coast was long. That wrecks happened. That her father's ghost did not get a vote in her geology.

But the ballast line ran north-northeast. And eleven years ago, in the summer she was eighteen, she had sat at her father's elbow while he drafted a coastal survey of exactly this stretch of Punta Serrada, and she remembered — the memory arriving now with the sickening clarity of things noticed too late — him stopping at this precise section of chart, pen hovering, and then drawing the coastline through clean and unbroken, as if the cliffs there had never hidden anything at all.

He had been humming. He always hummed when he was lying, a little tuneless habit she'd teased him about, and she'd forgotten it for eleven years, and now it came back to her over fried fish in a rented room and sat down across the table like a guest who intended to stay.

"Damn you," she said quietly, to a man who was presumably somewhere far away being disgraced and alive, or possibly dead — the last she'd heard, three years ago through a shipping agent in another port town, was that Kenji Nakamura had been seen in the interior, old and gray and alone, and that he'd asked after her, and that she had told the agent, with a steadiness she'd rehearsed on the walk over, to tell him nothing back.

The lamp guttered. Outside, the evening tide was turning, filling the channel, covering the ballast line and the keel-shadow and the questions, keeping its books as it always did, patient, exact, owing nothing to anyone.

Ines washed her plate, checked her kit by touch in the dark — mask, fins, slate, knife, the coil of new rope — and lay down on the cot with the chart pinned above her and the penciled cross glowing faintly in her mind's eye.

Six hundred and forty minus two hundred, plus two hundred and fifty, minus rope and slate and oil and lead. She did the sums the way her father had taught her, twice, in columns, because the tide kept its own books and expected her to keep hers.

Somewhere below the cliffs, under tomorrow's grey-green water, a ship that official history insisted had never existed waited with its cargo and its silence. And somewhere in the space between that silence and her father's humming pen, Ines fell asleep with the unfought argument still in her mouth — *you knew, you always knew, you drew the coast clean and let them hang you for it anyway* — not knowing yet that the answer was already moving toward her through the island's slow postal system, in an envelope postmarked the week before the world said her father died.

It would reach her in two days. It would smell of mildew and old paper and, underneath that, faintly and impossibly, of the survey ink she'd grown up breathing.

But that night there was only the tide, coming in over Punta Serrada, keeping its books.
