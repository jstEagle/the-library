## 2. A Letter Six Years Late

The letter came on a Tuesday, which was the day the mail boat ran, and it arrived the way most things arrived in Puerto Sombra: late, damp, and handled by too many people who didn't care about it.

Ines found it wedged under the door of her rented room above the chandlery, its envelope soft-cornered from sea air, one edge darkened where someone had set a wet tin on it. The postmark had been stamped twice — once illegibly at some mainland sorting office, once clearly enough to read: *Isla Verde, Puerto Sombra* — and beneath the second stamp, a date.

She stood in the doorway with it in her hand for a long moment before she understood what she was looking at.

The date was from six years ago.

No — not quite. She turned it toward the light from the stairwell window, tilted it, squinted against the grain of the cheap paper. The postmark read the third week of October, six years back, give or take a few days. Which meant it had been mailed the week before her father died. Before the cliff road washed out. Before the memorial that eleven people attended, four of them there for the free food.

Six years in the postal system. Six years of this envelope riding ferries and sorting floors and dead-letter shelves, surfacing now like something the tide had decided, finally, to give back.

Her first thought — the honest one, the one she'd have been ashamed to say aloud — was: *he wrote to me.*

Her second thought was: *it doesn't matter now, does it.*

She went inside and put the letter on the table without opening it, the way you'd set down something that might be venomous. Then she made coffee she didn't want, because her hands needed a task that wasn't trembling, and she sat across from the envelope and looked at it while the water boiled and the morning fog burned off the harbor below her window.

The handwriting on the front was his. That was the worst part. Not the date, not the delay, not even the fact of it — but the handwriting itself, that narrow, disciplined script that leaned slightly left, as if even his letters were always turning back toward something behind them. She knew that hand better than any living person's. She'd grown up watching it move across tide tables and site reports and the margins of her own schoolwork, correcting her arithmetic in red ink, annotating her childhood drawings of shipwrecks with notes like *good eye — but check your draft marks.*

*Ines Nakamura,* the envelope said. No address beyond the chandlery's, no title, no *my daughter.* Just her name, written as if he were labeling a specimen.

She drank her coffee and did not touch it.

* * *

By noon she had convinced herself she wasn't going to open it, which is why she spent the afternoon doing everything possible to avoid being alone with it. She went down to the chandlery and argued with old Ferro about the price of nylon line until he gave up in disgust and knocked ten percent off just to be rid of her. She walked the breakwater to the fuel dock and watched the Corvus Fleet's tender refueling, its crew laughing about something, their orange coveralls bright as warning flags against the gray water. She checked on her dig cache in the sea cave below the north cliffs — the small one, the legal-adjacent one, the one that fed her — and inventoried her finds by feel in the dark: two coins too corroded to sell, a brass fitting, a shard of transfer-printed ceramic worth maybe forty pesos to the right collector. Her whole season, cupped in two hands.

*I'm scraping other people's leavings off the rocks,* she thought, standing in the cold drip of the cave, *and my father's ghost has been sitting on my kitchen table since morning.*

That wasn't fair and she knew it. He wasn't a ghost. Ghosts didn't write letters. Dead men didn't address envelopes in that careful left-leaning hand. Whatever was in that letter had been written by a living man who had, at some point in the last week of his life, thought of her — thought of her specifically, sought out her address, sat down with pen and paper — and she had never known. Six years she had carried the specific weight of believing that his final thoughts had been of his ruined career, or his excavations, or nothing at all. That she hadn't made the list.

And now this envelope arrived to tell her she might have been wrong about that, and it was six years too late to matter, and she hated him for it — hated him with a fresh, bright, stupid heat, as if he'd planned the timing personally, as if dying the week after writing to her were just one more way Kenji Nakamura had found to make her feel like an afterthought.

She climbed back up the cliff path in the failing light, salt wind pushing at her shoulders like a hand between the shoulder blades, and let herself into the room, and the letter was exactly where she'd left it, patient as a trap.

* * *

She opened it at midnight, which was not a decision so much as a surrender she dressed up as one.

Actually — no. Let her be precise, because precision was the one inheritance she admitted to. What happened was this: she took the letter to the stove, intending to burn it. She had the matches out. She had it held over the burner grate, corner down, the paper already curling faintly with the heat rising from the pilot flame, and she had told herself the speech she'd rehearsed all evening — *you don't get to write to me now, you don't get to need me now, I built a life out of the shape of your absence and I'm not renovating it for a dead man's postscript* — and then, in the last second, with the paper beginning to brown, she had snatched it back.

Not because she forgave him. Because burning an unread letter was a luxury, and she had learned long ago on this island that luxuries were things you paid for twice. Information was currency. Even a dead man's words were currency. And Ines Nakamura did not burn money, no matter whose face was printed on it.

That was the story she told herself, kneeling there on the cold floorboards with the unburned letter clutched in both hands like something rescued from a fire. It was a good story. It was almost true.

She sat on her bed with her back against the wall and slit the envelope open with her dive knife, carefully, preserving the flap, because the archivist in her never entirely slept.

One page. Both sides. Written fast — the discipline of the hand breaking down toward the bottom, letters crowding, the pen pressed hard enough in places to emboss the next page. He had written it in a hurry, or in anger, or in fear. Possibly all three. Her father had never done anything in only one register in his life.

She read it. Then she read it again. Then she sat for a long time with the page hanging loose from her fingers, listening to the harbor slap against pilings, feeling the floor of her certainties tilt beneath her the way a deck tilts when a swell comes through that nobody forecast.

*Ines —*

*I'll keep this short because short things get finished. I've found it. You know which it. The Meridian. Forty-one years everyone's chased rumors and I found it by reading the sediment instead of the legends, which is the only lesson I ever gave you that mattered.*

*There's a complication I can't put on paper. Someone in the survey office has been asking questions I don't like the shape of. If anything happens to me, or if I simply stop answering letters, go to the old survey station at Punta del Faro. There's something there with your name on it. Literally. I labeled it. You'll understand when you see it.*

*I know what you think of me. You said it plainly enough the last time we spoke, and you were right about most of it. But whatever else I am, I am not wrong about tides, and I am telling you: come before the spring series in November. After that the coast will change and the window closes, possibly for years.*

*Come, Ines. Or don't. But if you don't, know that the thing I regret isn't what you think it is.*

*— Your father*

She read it a fourth time, hunting the lie in it, because there was always a lie in him somewhere — an omission, a self-serving slant, a door quietly closed that she wouldn't notice until she walked into it. That was the man she'd grown up under: brilliant, meticulous, and constitutionally incapable of telling her the whole truth when a partial truth served him better.

But the letter was either true or it was an elaborate fabrication composed by a dying man for no purpose she could construct, and either way it contained three things she could not put down.

First: *I've found it.* The Meridian Star. The wreck every treasure hunter in the Caribbean had chased for forty years and every academic had dismissed as a sailor's fable — the colonial packet ship that vanished in 1789 carrying a cargo the manifests never explained, the legend that had put her father on the map and then, six years ago, taken the map away with him. If he'd found it, it would explain everything that happened afterward. It would explain the survey office's sudden interest in his permits. It would explain the report — that flat, bloodless document, *An Assessment of Claimed Findings, Nakamura Survey 4-C*, concluding that his evidence was "consistent with natural deposition and wishful interpretation" — the report that had ended his career, revoked his concessions, and made his name a joke in every faculty lounge from here to Seville.

Second: *someone in the survey office has been asking questions I don't like the shape of.* He'd written that a week before he died. A week before the storm, the washout, the road. An accident, they'd called it. Everyone had called it an accident, including her, including her especially, because the alternative was unthinkable and also because thinking it would have meant caring enough to investigate, and she had been so tired of caring about him by then.

Third: *the old survey station at Punta del Faro. There's something there with your name on it.*

Punta del Faro was two hours' walk up the coast. The station had been abandoned since the government consolidated the survey offices into the capital — abandoned, but not demolished, because nothing on Isla Verde got demolished, things just got left to the weather like the island couldn't be bothered to finish any of its sentences.

He had labeled something with her name. Six years ago. And she had never gone to look, because she had never received the letter, because the postal system had swallowed it the way the sea swallows everything eventually, and given it back only now, on its own schedule, for its own reasons.

* * *

She didn't sleep. Around three in the morning she gave up pretending and lit the lamp and laid out the letter beside her father's last published survey chart — the one she kept folded in the bottom of her kit, though she'd have denied it under torture — and began, methodically, furiously, to cross-reference.

The old survey station at Punta del Faro appeared on the chart as a small square with the notation *met. stn (disused)*. She remembered it from childhood, barely: a field trip, age nine or ten, her father pointing out how the station's rain gauge had been sited wrong, how the whole building sat in a wind shadow that made thirty years of rainfall data quietly useless. *Even instruments lie if you place them carelessly,* he'd said, and nine-year-old Ines had nodded along, and that memory — that stupid, warm, useless memory — rose up in her now with such force that she had to set the chart down and press the heels of her hands against her eyes.

Anger cracked open, and underneath the anger, where she had spent six years assuming there was bedrock, there was longing instead. It was worse. Anger she could build with; anger was load-bearing. Longing was water finding every crack.

Because here was the thing she could not stop seeing, now that the letter had shown it to her: *come before the spring series in November.* He had known the coast would change. He had known the window would close. Her father, who could read a beach like a ledger, who taught her that sand remembered every storm, had looked at the cliffs near Punta del Faro and seen a future tide doing something to them — and he had wanted her there to see it too. Not the authorities. Not his colleagues, what few remained. Her.

Or — the colder voice in her head offered — he'd wanted a witness. Someone to find his labeled package after he was conveniently dead, someone young and strong enough to work the site, someone whose legitimacy he could borrow one more time even from beyond the grave. Maybe the letter was love. Maybe it was logistics. With him it had always been impossible to tell the difference, and that impossibility was the whole wound, wasn't it? That was the actual wound, the one under all the others: not that he'd disgraced himself, but that she had never once been able to tell whether she was his daughter or his best instrument.

The lamp hissed. Outside, far off, the northeaster that the barometer had been muttering about all evening sent its first long swell against the cliffs, a sound like a held breath being slowly released.

She looked at the letter one more time. *The thing I regret isn't what you think it is.*

"You don't get to do this," she said aloud, to the empty room, to the dead man, to the six lost years. Her voice came out rougher than she intended. "You don't get to leave me homework."

But she was already moving. That was the humiliating truth of it — her body had decided before her mind consented, the way it always did, the way he'd trained it to. She pulled her kit from under the bed and began laying gear across the blanket with the economy of someone who had packed for other people's abandoned digs a hundred times: rope, two lights and spare cells, the good knife and the backup knife, waterproof wraps, the tide tables for the coming lunar series copied out in her own hand. She counted her cash. Two hundred and fourteen pesos, which was not enough for anything except exactly this.

At the bottom of the pack, wrapped in oilcloth, went the survey chart. And after a moment — a long moment, during which she stood holding the letter and the match again, one last negotiation with the part of her that wanted to burn the whole past and be clean — the letter went in too, sealed in its own waterproof sleeve, riding against the chart like a splinter working its way toward the surface.

She would go to Punta del Faro. Not for him. For the information. For the leverage. For whatever was in that labeled package that might be worth something to someone, because Ines Nakamura was twenty-nine years old, in debt to Ferro and half the chandlery's suppliers, and sentiment was a luxury she had already established she couldn't afford.

That was the story. She was getting very practiced at it.

She doused the lamp and lay down fully clothed and listened to the storm assembling itself offshore, and just before sleep took her, unbidden, her father's voice surfaced from twenty years down: the lesson on the mudflats, her small hands in the wet sand, him saying, *the tide keeps its own books, Ines. Everything the sea takes, it writes down. Nothing stays lost unless the sea decides it should.*

The swell boomed against the cliffs, closer now, and closer.

She woke before dawn to a changed silence and a changed light, and the sound of the whole harbor talking at once — and when she pushed open the shutters and looked south along the coast toward Punta del Faro, she saw that the sea, overnight, had begun keeping its books in earnest.
