## 5. The Concession

The radio in the harbormaster's office had been broken for two years, which was why Ines heard the news from a boy selling fried plantain on the seawall instead of from anything official.

"Concessions," he said, mouth full, holding up his phone like it was a reliquary. "Magistrate's announcing them at noon. Big one. The cave."

Ines stopped walking.

The boy — Mateo's youngest, she thought, though all the harbor children blurred together after a while — scrolled with his thumb and read aloud in the singsong cadence of someone reciting words he didn't understand. *"Emergency archaeological salvage concessions, issued under provisions of the Coastal Recovery Act, to be consolidated under a single licensed operator pending survey completion."* He looked up. "That means the wreck, right? The Meridian Star? My uncle says—"

"When," Ines said. "Noon today?"

"Noon. Plaza del Correo. Everybody's going."

She was already moving.

* * *

The plaza was fuller than she'd seen it since the festival of San Telmo three years ago, when the fishing fleet came home under strings of lights and everyone pretended, for one night, that Isla Verde belonged to the people who worked it. Today there were no lights. There was a platform of rough scaffolding planks erected in front of the customs house, a bunting of island colors gone gray at the edges, and a row of folding chairs occupied by men whose clothes cost more than most boats in the harbor.

Aurelio Vance stood at the microphone in a linen suit the color of bone.

He had aged well, Ines thought, with the detached appraisal of someone studying a specimen. Six years since she'd stood close enough to see the pores in his face, six years since that night in the hearing room when he'd read aloud the findings of the independent review board in a voice like warm oil — *no evidence of significant cultural material at the surveyed coordinates; the claims advanced by Dr. Kenji Nakamura cannot be substantiated* — and her father had sat very still with his hands flat on the table as if pressing down something alive. Vance had been deputy magistrate then. Now the title was his alone, and so was everything that came with it: the suit, the chairs, the microphone, the plaza, the island's patience worn thin enough to drape over any stage he liked.

"Ladies and gentlemen of Isla Verde," he began, and his voice carried the way voices do when they've spent decades learning exactly how far sound travels across open stone. "The sea has given us a gift."

Ines stood at the back, beneath the frangipani tree whose roots had cracked the paving stones, and let the crowd press around her like water around a piling. She'd pulled her hair back and put on the canvas jacket that made her look like every other salvage hand on the coast — competent, interchangeable, invisible. Her hands were steady. She noticed this with a kind of clinical interest, the way you notice your own pulse during a near miss.

"The storm of two nights past has revealed to us a piece of our shared history," Vance continued. "The wreck of the *Meridian Star*, lost with all hands in 1847, a vessel whose story belongs to every family on this island. It would be irresponsible — reckless — to allow unlicensed salvage of such a site. Looting. Destruction of heritage." He spread his hands, a man burdened by necessity. "Therefore, effective immediately, the Office of the Magistrate is issuing emergency concessions under the Coastal Recovery Act. And I am pleased to announce that after careful consideration of capability, equipment, and proven expertise, the primary concession has been awarded to the Corvus Salvage Fleet."

There was applause. Not universal — Ines marked the faces that didn't clap, the fishermen and the old women outside the church and a knot of students from the coastal school who'd been passing out leaflets about the reef last month — but enough. Enough applause to make it legitimate.

And there, rising from the second row of chairs to acknowledge it with a modest half-bow, was a broad man in a white captain's jacket with silver buttons and a smile like a dock cleat: Emiliano Corvus. Vance's brother-in-law. The man who owned eleven vessels, two cranes, a decompression facility, and — according to the tax filings Ines had once helped a journalist friend pull before the friend moved to the mainland and stopped answering letters — a mortgage held by a bank whose board included Aurelio Vance's college roommate.

*A rigged game,* she thought, and the phrase arrived without heat, without surprise, with the flat certainty of a tide table. She had known, watching the timbers stamped MERIDIAN STAR come apart in her hands yesterday morning, that this moment would come. She simply hadn't known how fast.

"Now, some will ask why the concession process has been expedited." Vance's tone shifted, took on gravity. "The answer is the sea itself. Our oceanographers inform me that the spring tides beginning six days from now — the perigean spring series — will likely resubmerge the cave mouth permanently. Possibly for a generation. We have a window, friends. A brief window. And I intend that this island meet it with its best."

Six days.

Ines did the arithmetic standing under the tree, automatic and merciless. Six days until the spring series peaked — but the usable working tides inside that window, the low waters deep enough and long enough to run a proper operation through the flooded passage, would be fewer. Five. Maybe four, once you subtracted the days Corvus would spend positioning equipment, establishing their exclusion zone, posting their notices. The clock hadn't started this morning with the announcement.

It had started two nights ago, with the storm. While she'd slept in her bunk above the cliffs with the wind screaming like something in pain, the sea had opened the door and the machinery of the island had already begun closing it again — permits drafted, concessions awarded, a fleet fueled and provisioned, all of it moving in the dark hours while she'd been dreaming of nothing. They had known. Or they had been ready, which amounted to the same thing. You didn't award an emergency concession within forty-eight hours unless the paperwork had been sitting in a drawer waiting for the excuse.

Waiting for the cave to open.

Her eyes went to the strongbox in her memory — the lead-sealed box with her father's survey marks scratched into the strap hinges, K.N., 11°-something-something, the notation system only he used, taught to her at twelve years old on a beach not ten miles from where she now stood. He had found this first. Years ago. And he had told no one except whoever had carried his discovery to Aurelio Vance like a gift wrapped in betrayal.

Vance was still speaking. "The Corvus Fleet brings jobs to Isla Verde. Real wages. Dredge operators, line handlers, conservators. I have directed Mr. Corvus to hire locally wherever possible—" scattered, thinner applause "—and I have further directed that all recovered material be catalogued under magistrate supervision, with proceeds allocated to the island's restoration fund, less operational costs."

Less operational costs. Ines felt something cold settle behind her sternum, colder than fury, colder than the flooded passage she'd have to swim. She knew how those ledgers worked. Operational costs would eat everything. By the time the restoration fund saw a centavo, the *Meridian Star*'s cargo would have been dispersed through three holding companies and the manifest — the true manifest, whatever it said — would be sealed in a magistrate's archive that no researcher could access for fifty years. Her father's name would stay dirt. The record that ended him would stay intact, because the man who wrote it would be the man controlling what came up out of the water.

Unless someone got there first.

* * *

She stayed through the end of it because she made herself. Because you learned nothing by running, and because she wanted to watch the choreography — who went to whom afterward, which hands were shaken longest, where the money visibly flowed. It was a skill her father had taught her without meaning to, or perhaps meaning to: *Watch the meeting after the meeting, Ines. The decision is never made where they say it's made.*

The crowd broke toward the harbor like water finding its level. Vance descended the scaffold into a cordon of well-wishers and officials, and Ines tracked him sideways through the bodies — a tall woman in naval whites, probably the port authority liaison; a priest; a cluster of men in matching polo shirts with CORVUS FLEET embroidered on the chest, their leader carrying a leather document case the way soldiers carry ammunition. And then, briefly, the crowd thinned between them, and Aurelio Vance looked directly at her.

It lasted perhaps a second and a half. His gaze passed over her face the way light passes over water — registering, dismissing, moving on. He didn't know her. Of course he didn't know her. She'd been twenty-three and furious and far away when her father fell, and she'd built her life since out of other people's discarded permits precisely so that no one like Aurelio Vance would ever have a reason to learn her face.

But her father's face was another matter. And she had his jaw, his eyes, the same stubborn set of the mouth that six years ago had stared out from every newspaper on the coast above the headline DISGRACED ARCHAEOLOGIST'S CLAIMS UNFOUNDED.

Vance's gaze snagged. Came back. Rested on her for one heartbeat longer than courtesy required, and something moved behind it — not recognition yet, but the approach of recognition, a ship sighting landfall in bad visibility.

Ines turned and walked toward the fish market, unhurried, and bought a red snapper she didn't want, and haggled over it competently, and left, and did not look back.

* * *

The climb to her camp above the cliffs took forty minutes in the heat, and she spent all of them thinking.

Not panicking — she noted that too, with the same clinical detachment. Panic was a luxury billed later, with interest. What she felt instead was a kind of terrible clarity, the clarity of a diver who has just realized the current has shifted while she was below and will now carry her somewhere she did not choose.

At the top, she knelt at the edge of the cliff path where the ground dropped away to the cove, and looked down at the place where the sea had torn open the rock. From here the cave mouth was a dark seam below the tideline, guarded by the wreckage fan of the *Meridian Star*, and beyond it — visible even at this distance, even to the naked eye — the first shapes of the response: a survey buoy already moored off the point, its orange paint obscene against the blue. Someone had been busy last night. Someone had been busy while she'd sat in her tent reading her dead father's letter for the ninth time.

She went to the tent and spread her materials on the camp table: the tide tables for the month, hand-corrected in her own shorthand; the chart of the coastline with the cave penciled in; the notes from her two descents into the outer chamber; the letter, soft-cornered now from handling.

*If you are reading this, then either I am dead or I have finally found the courage I should have had years ago. Either way, come to the old survey station. Come alone. What I buried there, they will dig up eventually, and when they do, I need the person who understands what it means to be the one holding the shovel.*

They. He had written *they*. At the time, in her anger, she'd read *they* as paranoia — the persecution fantasy of a ruined man, the same delusion that had made him spend his last functioning years writing letters to ministries and journals that stopped replying. Now, standing in the shadow of a concession announced with paperwork pre-drafted, *they* sounded less like paranoia and more like a guest list.

Kenji Nakamura had found the *Meridian Star*. Had mapped it, sampled it, documented it — the strongbox proved that much, his marks cut clean and confident into the metal. And then he had reported it, because reporting was the whole architecture of his integrity, the load-bearing wall of the man. And someone had taken that report to Aurelio Vance, and Vance had understood immediately — the way certain men understand certain things instantly — that a legendary wreck on Isla Verde's coast was not a discovery. It was a liability. Or an asset. Depending on who controlled the story.

So the report became worthless. The archaeologist became disgraced. The coordinates were filed away in a drawer, and the drawer waited six years for a storm to do what no honest process ever would.

And the letter — postmarked the week before his death, arriving months late through whatever failure of forwarding addresses and island postal service — the letter meant he had known the drawer was still full. Known they would come back for it. Had tried, at the end, to arm the one person who might stand between them and the erasure of everything.

"I'm here," she said aloud, to the tent, to the letter, to the man six years dead or six years silent — she still didn't know which, and the not-knowing was a splinter she'd learned to live around. "I'm here, and they're late, and so am I. So we're even."

Then she sat down and did the work, because grief was a tide and work was the keel, and her father had taught her that too, back when the teaching was still a thing she received gratefully, before it became a thing she flinched from.

* * *

The numbers came together under her pencil like bones assembling into an animal.

Spring series peak: six days out. Working lows before consolidation of the Corvus permit zone: she counted the tidal curve with her fingertip, marking each trough deep enough and long enough to matter. Tomorrow's afternoon low — marginal, short, maybe ninety minutes of viable bottom time in the outer chamber. Then two good lows. Then a neap-troughed day that was nearly useless. Then the final pair before the springs swallowed everything.

Five tides. Call it four and a half once you priced in Corvus's exclusion buoys, their dive rotations, their floodlights on the night water. Four and a half tides to get through a flooded passage she'd never penetrated, into an inner chamber she'd only theorized, and recover proof sufficient to break a magistrate — against a fleet with compressors, cranes, a decompression chamber, and the law on its side.

Against that: one salvager with a skiff, a borrowed regulator, and a name nobody respected.

She laughed. It came out wrong, a single dry note with nothing behind it, and she pressed her knuckles to her mouth until it stopped.

The truth was she couldn't do it alone. The passage into the interior — she'd probed its throat on her second descent, felt the current breathing in and out of it like the gill-slits of something enormous — required a diver better than her. Required someone who knew mixed-gas discipline, who could run a line blind in silt, who wouldn't panic when the overhead closed in. Required, in short, exactly the kind of professional who gravitated to fleets like Corvus, because the island's legitimate archaeology budget wouldn't fund a rowboat.

Which meant she needed a diver from the other side of the fence. Someone who'd worked Corvus water. Someone with debts — because debts were levers, and she had no money, only leverage, and the difference between the two was the whole story of her life.

She knew a name. Everyone on the coast knew the name Teo Brandt, usually spoken with a shrug or a sneer depending on the speaker's own history with borrowed money. The diver who drank his advance. The diver who'd walked off the *Santa Lucia* contract. The diver who owed everyone.

Owed everyone, Ines thought, rolling a fresh page into her waterproof notebook. Which meant he could be hired by anyone. Which meant he could be hired by her — if she could find him before Corvus's recruiters did, and if she could offer him the one thing his creditors couldn't.

A clean job. One clean job, and a share of whatever vindication was worth when the gold had all been claimed by men in linen suits.

Outside the tent, the light was going long and amber, and far below, the survey buoy blinked its first blink of the evening, patient as a held breath. Somewhere out in the harbor, she could hear the Corvus fleet's generators spinning up, a low industrial hum riding in over the water — the sound of the future being claimed by the people who could afford to buy it.

Four and a half tides.

Ines clicked off her headlamp, lay back on her cot with the letter folded over her heart like a plate of armor, and listened to the sea working at the cliff below, opening and closing its dark door.

Tomorrow she would find Teo Brandt.
