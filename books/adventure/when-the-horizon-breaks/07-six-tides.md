## 7. Six Tides

The tide tables her father had taught her to read were still pinned above the desk in the survey station, yellowed at the corners, annotated in his small precise hand. Ines stood before them with a cup of cold coffee going stale in her grip and let herself look at them properly for the first time since she'd arrived.

*Spring tides, fourteenth and fifteenth,* he'd written in the margin of the lunar chart, years ago, in a different ink. *Everything else is negotiation.*

She set the cup down and pulled the current almanac toward her — hers, bought secondhand in Puerto Sombra, its pages soft from handling. The moon was waxing gibbous. Four nights from now it would be full, and the full moon would drag the sea up the cliffs like a hand closing a drawer.

She did the arithmetic the way he'd taught her: not with a calculator, but with her eyes on the water and her finger moving down the column of times.

Today was the third day after the storm. The cave had been exposed for three tides already. She'd spent two of them in the outer chamber, mapping, photographing, hauling the strongbox up to the cache she'd cut into the cliff scrub where nothing heavier than a goat would think to look. Two tides spent, then.

That left six.

Six usable tides before the spring series came — before the water rose high enough and stayed high enough long enough to drown the outer chamber completely and begin the slow work of resealing the cave mouth with silt and storm-tumbled stone. Six tides before the permits Vance had announced consolidated fully into Corvus Fleet's hands, and anything she took from inside became theft instead of salvage.

She wrote the numbers on the back of an old survey sheet, because writing made them real and real things could be fought.

*Tide one: tomorrow, 0447, low. Window of maybe ninety minutes before the inner passage floods past wadeable.*

*Tide two: 1712, low. Same window, shorter — the ebb would be lazy, the flood eager.*

And so on, each window shrinking as the moon fattened, each low water standing a little higher against the cave walls, until the sixth tide — the last one — would barely drop the water below the lip of the flooded passage at all.

After that, the sea would take the Meridian Star back for another generation, and this time it would take it under Corvus's flag.

Ines sat back and looked at what she'd written. Six windows. Ninety minutes each, best case. Maybe seven hours of working time total, spread across five days, most of it in the dark, half of it underwater.

Against that: a fleet.

She'd watched them come around the headland that morning while she'd pretended to fish off the rocks below the station — three vessels, gray and white, working-class hulls dressed up with new paint and too many antennas. The flagship was a converted island freighter called the *Corvus Anvil*, and she sat at anchor in the roadstead like she owned the water, which, legally speaking, by Friday she would.

Divers' flags flew from her rail. A work barge with a crane swung at her hip. And on her foredeck, visible even at this distance through cheap binoculars, a compressor plant and a rack of surface-supply umbilicals — the kind of equipment that didn't care about narrow passages or bad air, the kind that could put eight men on the bottom for hours at a stretch.

Ines lowered the binoculars and pressed the cold bridge of them against her forehead until the metal ached.

They would not rush. That was the thing about fleets; they never had to rush. They'd spend a day on survey, a day on staging, a day letting the magistrate's paperwork catch up to their ambition. They thought they had all six tides and every one after them besides. Why wouldn't they? The game was rigged in their favor so thoroughly that haste would be almost insulting to it.

Which meant the only move left was the one the riggers never planned for: get there first, go deeper than they intended to go, and take out the one thing they couldn't afford to lose.

Because she'd thought about it all night, lying on the survey station's cot listening to the surf work at the cliff base, and she'd understood something about the shape of Vance's announcement. He hadn't claimed the site for archaeology. He hadn't mentioned preservation, or documentation, or a single word about historical record. Emergency salvage concession, awarded without competitive bid, to a commercial fleet with a crane and a compressor plant.

You didn't send a crane for history. You sent a crane for cargo.

Whatever was inside that cave — whatever her father had found and marked and sealed and told no one but a traitor — it wasn't artifacts to Aurelio Vance. It was evidence. And evidence, in the magistrate's arithmetic, was the one kind of treasure you destroyed rather than recovered.

Her father had known that. That was why the strongbox bore his survey marks and nothing else — no report filed, no claim registered, no name attached. He'd found the proof of what had ruined him, and he'd buried it deeper rather than hand it to a system he knew would eat it. And then he'd waited six years to write her a letter, and died — or vanished, or whatever the truth of him was — before she could ask him a single question about any of it.

*Fine,* she thought. *Then I'll ask the cave.*

* * *

She spent the afternoon preparing, and the preparation steadied her the way it always did — the ritual of gear laid out in order, checked twice, each piece given its place. This was the other thing he'd taught her without meaning to: that fear was just energy with nowhere to go, and the cure was to give it somewhere to go, one buckle and knot and pressure check at a time.

Her dive kit was old but honest. Regulator serviced in Puerto Sombra by a man who owed her money and couldn't refuse. Tank filled at the same shop, tested twice. Line reels — three of them, because one line in a cave was a suggestion and two were a habit and three meant you expected things to go wrong. Slate and pencil. Three lights, one primary, two backup, because the same arithmetic applied.

What she didn't have was enough air for the flooded passage, or any certainty about how deep it went, or anyone watching her line from the surface.

That last gap in the inventory was the one that kept catching her eye.

She'd worked alone for six years and told herself it was discipline. Standing over the gear now, she admitted what it actually was: armor. If you dove alone, no one could betray you. If you surveyed alone, no one could sell your coordinates. If you grieved alone, no one could watch you do it.

But caves didn't care about armor. Caves kept their own books, and the entry fee was always paid in full, and the divers who went in alone paid it alone.

*I need a diver,* she wrote on the slate, and then wiped it clean, because writing it made it a decision and decisions deserved more than a sentence scratched in the dark.

She thought about who. The island's diving population was small and mostly employed, and everyone employed on the water here answered eventually to Corvus or to people Corvus owned. She needed someone outside that web — someone with cave experience, someone desperate enough to say yes to a job with no permit behind it, someone whose desperation hadn't yet curdled into the kind of greed that sold people.

The list was short. The list, honestly, was one name long, and she hated it.

Teo Brandt.

She'd seen him around Puerto Sombra's harbor the way you see a wreck on a chart — noted, avoided, occasionally useful as a warning to others. Ex-Corvus. The story everyone told was that he'd been Voss's lead diver once, good enough to run the deep jobs, and then something happened — nobody agreed on what — and he'd come ashore with nothing but a duffel bag and a reputation that opened exactly no doors. Now he patched hulls for cash and drank half of it and owed the other half to people who didn't extend credit out of kindness.

He was, in other words, exactly the kind of man who would understand what she was offering: not money she didn't have, but a chance to be on the right side of one thing. One job. One clean job, before the sea or the magistrate or his own history finished with him.

She didn't trust him. That was fine. Trust was for afterward, if there was an afterward. What she needed was competence and motive, and Teo Brandt had both in quantities that almost embarrassed him.

* * *

Evening came down over the water like a lid. From the station's window she watched the *Corvus Anvil* light her deck floods, turning the roadstead into a stage, and she imagined Mara Voss — if Voss was even aboard; captains sometimes sent their ships ahead and followed later — standing on that lit deck looking at the cliff line, seeing what Ines saw: a rock face with a secret in its teeth.

Let her look. Let the whole fleet look. Looking cost them a day, and a day was a tide, and a tide was ninety minutes, and ninety minutes was the width of the door she intended to walk through while they were still reading their own permits.

Ines packed the last of the kit into two waterproof bags and sat down at the desk with the almanac one final time. She copied the six tide times onto a strip of paper, folded it into her breast pocket where the letter used to live, and felt the strange comfort of numbers against her sternum. Her father had believed the sea kept honest accounts — that every tide repaid exactly what it borrowed, no more, no less. She'd spent six years calling that sentiment sentimental. Tonight it sounded like strategy.

Six tides. Seven hours. One chance to reach whatever lay beyond the flooded passage before the fleet's compressors started coughing air down their umbilicals and the cave filled with men who answered to Vance.

She looked at the old tide tables on the wall, at his handwriting holding the margins like fence posts, and allowed herself one sentence aloud to the empty room.

"You found it first," she said. "I'll finish it."

Outside, the first stars came out over a sea already beginning to turn, the moon climbing fat and white behind the cloud shelf, pulling at the water with patient, enormous hands. Five days until spring tide. Six windows until the door closed.

At dawn, she was going to find Teo Brandt.
