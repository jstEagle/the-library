## 8. The Diver Who Owes Everyone

Teo Brandt woke to the sound of someone knocking on the hull of his boat, and for a long moment he lay in his bunk and pretended it was the tide.

The tide knocked too, sometimes — a soft percussion against the *Pelícano*'s waterline when the swell came around the point wrong, a patient fist that never stopped asking. He'd learned to sleep through it. Six years of living aboard a thirty-foot ketch with a cracked mast step and a bilge pump he trusted more than most people had taught him which sounds mattered and which were just the sea clearing its throat.

This knock came again. Knuckles on fiberglass. Three raps, deliberate, spaced like someone counting.

He knew before he opened the hatch who it would be, because there was only one person on Isla Verde who knocked on boats at dawn instead of shouting, and she'd been asking after him at the cantina for two days. He'd been avoiding her for two days. It seemed the two days were over.

"Ines Nakamura," he said through the closed hatch, because saying her name out loud made it real and gave him one more second to decide whether to open it.

"Teo Brandt." Her voice carried the flat calm of deep water. "I know you're awake. Your stovepipe's smoking."

He looked up at the little tin chimney over the galley, trailing its thin gray thread into the morning air, and swore under his breath. Then he swung the hatch open.

She stood on the dock with her hands in the pockets of a canvas jacket gone soft as cloth from years of salt, and she looked exactly the way the island described her: Kenji Nakamura's daughter, all sharp angles and sharper eyes, hair tied back with what appeared to be a length of surveyor's twine. Behind her the harbor was waking up — gulls fighting over fish guts at the cleaning station, the first of the pangas chugging out past the breakwater, the Corvus Fleet's three white-hulled ships sitting at anchor in the roadstead like a hand of cards nobody wanted to be dealt.

"I've been standing here five minutes," she said. "You peeked twice."

"I did not."

"You have a gap in your hatch curtain the size of a coin. You peeked twice, decided I wasn't leaving, and started making coffee so you'd have something to do with your hands when you let me in." She stepped aboard without being invited, ducking under the boom, and settled herself on the port settee like she'd done it a hundred times. "You're a creature of habit, Teo Brandt. That's going to make this easier."

He closed the hatch behind her and put the coffee on, mostly because she was right about the hands.

* * *

The *Pelícano* smelled of diesel, mildew, and old rope, and Ines sat in the middle of it all with the posture of someone visiting a museum exhibit titled *Decline*. Teo poured two cups of coffee that had been on the heat since yesterday and sat across from her, and for a while neither of them spoke, because they both knew why she was there and both of them were deciding how much it was going to cost.

"You've been asking about me at the Marlin Room," he said finally.

"I've been asking about everyone. You're the only one who ran."

"I didn't run. I had engine work."

"Your engine has been disassembled on your foredeck for eleven months. I counted the parts."

He almost smiled. Almost. There was something in this woman that made smiling feel like losing ground. "So ask whatever you're going to ask."

"The Corvus Fleet is working the sea cave below the north cliffs. The Meridian Star site." She said the ship's name the way people say the names of saints or storms. "Magistrate Vance awarded the concession six days ago. They've got divers down already, pumps running, a crane barge anchored off the point. And I need to get inside that cave before they take everything that matters, and I can't do it alone, and every other diver on this island either works for Corvus, owes Corvus, or is smart enough not to go near Corvus." She lifted her cup. "Which leaves you."

"Flattery will get you nowhere."

"It wasn't flattery. It was triage." She drank. Grimaced at the coffee but didn't put it down, which he noted, and filed away, and found himself oddly pleased by. "You worked for them. Two years on the *Corvus Belli* as lead diver. You know their methods, their equipment, their rotation schedules, and their captain. And then you left, and nobody on this island will tell me why, which means it's bad, which means you're the only honest man available."

"That's not how honesty works."

"It's exactly how honesty works on Isla Verde."

He got up and looked out the porthole rather than look at her. The morning light was coming up gold behind the Corvus ships, and even at this distance he could pick out the *Corvus Belli* by the rake of her mast — could have picked her out blind, by engine note, by the particular way her wake curled off the stern quarter. Two years of his life lived inside that hull. Two years of the best diving of his career and the worst sleeping of his life, and he still caught himself reading the fleet's silhouette on the horizon the way other men read weather.

"Why did you leave?" Ines asked quietly.

And there it was. The question, straight down, no line, no decompression stops.

He could give her the version the cantina had pieced together — the drinking, the fight with a deckhand, the walking off mid-contract in Puerto Oscuro. It was even true, every word of it, the way a map is true. What the map didn't show was the night in the chart room when Mara Voss handed him a sealed report and told him to sign the dive log confirming a wreck site was worthless, silted over, nothing there — and he signed it, because the alternative was a blacklisted ticket and a debt to the fleet's credit office that would follow him to whatever coast he washed up on next. What the map didn't show was whose name was attached to the original find they were erasing. He hadn't known then. He knew now; the whole island knew now; Kenji Nakamura's disgrace was island history, told in bars with the volume turned low.

He didn't know if Ines knew he'd been the diver on that survey. He suspected she'd come to find out.

"I left because I took money to say a thing wasn't there that was there," he said.

The cabin went very quiet. Somewhere outside, a gull screamed and was answered.

Ines set her cup down slowly. "What thing?"

"A wreck. A survey site. Silted-over cave system off the north shore, six years back. Corvus ran the preliminary dive under contract from the magistrate's office. I went down, I found cargo. Intact cargo, a lot of it, in an inner chamber behind a flooded passage. I came up, I wrote my report, and Captain Voss rewrote it before it went ashore. Called the site unstable, the chamber collapsed, nothing recoverable. I signed the log." His voice stayed level, which surprised him. He'd rehearsed this confession in the dark more times than he could count and it had never once come out level. "That's why I left. Not the drinking. The drinking came after."

"And the man who originally flagged the site—"

"Was your father." He turned from the porthole. "Yes. I know. I've known since the letter came out that he was dead. I did the arithmetic a long time ago, señora. I just never had anyone to say it to who'd understand what it meant."

Ines Nakamura looked at him for a long moment, and he watched her decide something — watched it happen behind her eyes like weather changing over water. When she spoke, her voice had lost none of its flat calm, but it had gained a depth that frightened him more than anger would have.

"My father spent six years ruined because of that report," she said. "Because of your signature on it."

"Yes."

"And you're telling me this. To my face. On the first morning we've spoken."

"You asked."

"I asked why you left Corvus. Confession was optional."

"Confession is always optional." He sat back down across from her. "That's what makes it worth doing."

* * *

She laid it out for him then, and it was a good plan — better than good, it was the kind of plan that could only have been built by someone raised on tide tables, because it used the fleet's own schedule against them. The Corvus divers were pumping the outer chamber and working the easy cargo, but the inner passage was still flooded, still unbreached, and Voss — he felt the old chill move through him at the name — Voss would keep her best people off it until the spring tides dropped the water level, because Voss did not spend divers she didn't have to. Which meant there was a window. Narrow, cold, and closing, but real.

"I need someone who can run that passage," Ines said. "Blind, in zero visibility, on a guideline, with a bottom timer and no backup. And I need someone who knows what Voss will do when she figures out someone else is in her cave. That's not a job description. That's a biography."

"It's also a death notice," Teo said. "That passage killed two men in 'nineteen. Before my time on the fleet, but the story was still circulating. Flooded tunnel, single entrance, overhead rock the whole way. If your line fouls, you don't surface. You just stop."

"I know what overhead environment means."

"Do you? Because you're a dirt archaeologist, señora. You dig *down*. Down is forgiving. Down lets you climb back out." He leaned forward. "Cave diving doesn't forgive anything. It takes the mistake you made forty minutes ago and delivers it to you personally."

"Then it's fortunate I'm not asking you to forgive anything." She reached into her jacket and produced a folded sheet of paper, and unfolded it on the table between them, and turned it to face him.

It was a sketch of the cave system. Hand-drawn, precise, annotated in two different hands — one small and cramped, one broader, older. Depth profiles. Passage bearings. A marked air pocket in the inner chamber. And at the top corner, a survey stamp he recognized instantly, because he'd seen it on the original site file six years ago: the crossed-tide-gauge mark of Kenji Nakamura, licensed surveyor.

"He mapped it," Ines said. "Before any of you ever went down. This is his preliminary survey, from the year before the Corvus contract. I found it in his effects." Her finger rested on the air pocket notation. "He knew about the chamber. He knew about the passage. He knew the depths and the timings better than your fleet ever will, because your fleet surveyed it once and lied about it, and he surveyed it four times because he was careful." She looked up. "I'm not a dirt archaeologist playing at diving, Teo Brandt. I'm carrying his numbers. I just need someone who can swim them."

Teo stared at the survey until his eyes burned, and what he felt was not fear, though fear was in there somewhere, familiar as an old bunkmate. What he felt was the specific vertigo of seeing a debt itemized. Every line on that page was a line he'd helped bury. The cramped annotations were the handwriting of a man whose ruin he had countersigned, and here it all was, resurfaced, exact, patient — the sea keeping its own books, and his name in the margin.

"What do you want out of it?" he asked. "Money? There's cargo down there worth more than this whole harbor. If we get inside before the fleet does—"

"I don't want the cargo."

He blinked. "Everyone wants the cargo."

"I want paper. Manifests, logs, ledgers, anything with writing on it. The Meridian Star's records. My father's name is buried under a lie and I intend to dig it out with documents, not doubloons." She began folding the survey along its creases. "You can have whatever gold we cross. I mean that. I want the truth and you want a clean slate and I suspect those are priced differently."

"They are." He thought of the credit office ledger with his name in it, the fleet scrip he owed, the way every door in every port from here to the mainland closed a little when Corvus mentioned his ticket number. "But clean slates aren't bought with gold either. Believe me. I've checked the exchange rate."

"Then what do you want?"

He was quiet for a while. Outside, the harbor noise went on — halyards clinking, a winch turning over at the fuel dock, the far diesel mutter of the fleet's generators carrying across the water like a second heartbeat for the island. He tried to remember the last time anyone had asked him that question and waited for the answer.

"One job," he said finally. "One job where I go down, and what I bring up is what I say it is, and nobody rewrites my log, and nobody pays me to disappear a thing. One clean job. That's all. After that I'll take whatever's left of my ticket and go wherever it's still worth anything."

"That's not much of a dream."

"It's the only one I can afford."

Ines studied him across the table, and then she did something he didn't expect: she extended her hand across the coffee rings and the salt stains, and waited.

"You understand what this costs," she said. "Voss will know within a day that it's you down there. Your old crew will know. Renn Okafor will know." She said the name lightly, but he saw her watching his face when it landed, and he understood she'd already done her research, all the way down, the way she apparently did everything.

"Renn and I dove together four years," Teo said. "He'll be hurt I didn't come to him first. Then he'll sell me out by lunchtime. I've already budgeted for it."

"And Voss?"

The question hung there. He looked out the porthole at the white ships in the roadstead, at the *Corvus Belli* with her mast raked against the brightening sky, and he thought about Mara Voss standing in her chart room with a pen in her hand, rewriting his report in her neat, unhurried script, and the look on her face while she did it — which had not been cruelty. That was the thing he'd never managed to explain to anyone, the thing that kept him awake in bad weather. It hadn't been cruelty. It had been duty, performed cleanly, by a woman who believed she was keeping her ship and her people safe inside a machine that ate everything it touched. He could hate that. He had hated it. But he had never once been able to despise her, and some traitorous part of him understood that if anyone on that flagship was going to itch at what Vance had buried, it would be her.

"Voss will do her job," he said. "Her job is the problem. But she's not Vance. Whatever else that woman is, she reads charts for a living, and charts don't lie unless somebody makes them." He took Ines's hand and shook it once, hard, the way you set a bone. "I'm in. One clean job."

"Good." She stood, tucked the survey away, and paused at the hatch with the morning light behind her. "We dive on tomorrow's ebb. First slack. Wear your own gear — nothing traceable to fleet issue. And Teo?"

"Yeah."

"When we're down there, and it goes wrong, and it will — you follow my father's numbers. Not your instincts. His numbers kept him alive in that cave four times." A ghost of something moved across her face, there and gone, grief wearing work clothes. "They're the only inheritance I've got. Don't make me spend it on you."

She dropped through the hatch and was gone, footsteps quick on the dock, and Teo sat alone in his cabin with two cups of bad coffee and the shape of what he'd agreed to.

* * *

He didn't sleep that night. He told himself it was the coffee.

Instead he dug the kit bag out from under his bunk — the one he hadn't opened since Puerto Oscuro — and laid his rig out on the deck planks under the lantern: mask, fins, the old double-hose regulator he'd rebuilt himself because he trusted his own hands more than any factory, the dive knife with the broken tip he'd never replaced because the break reminded him of something he couldn't name. Fleet-issue gear had been confiscated when he walked off the contract, all except this, the personal kit, the stuff that was his body's own history.

At the bottom of the bag, wrapped in an oilcloth he'd forgotten, he found the notebook.

Small, waterproof pages, grease-pencil scrawl. His working log from the Corvus years — the real one, the one he'd kept in his chest pocket on every dive, because Mara Voss had taught him early that a diver's private notes were the only record on a salvage ship that couldn't be edited above his head. She'd meant it as professional advice. He'd kept it as insurance. And there it was, page after page, in his own younger handwriting: the north-shore cave survey, six years old. Depths. Timings. The inner chamber, the intact crates stacked in rows, the manifest chest he'd touched with his own gloves and described in detail — and beside it, the sentence he'd written on the surface afterward, the sentence that had ended a stranger's career and started his own long drowning:

*Report amended per Capt. Voss. Site logged as unrecoverable. Signed.*

He sat with the notebook open on his knees until the lantern burned low, listening to the tide knock softly against the hull, and he understood that Ines Nakamura hadn't recruited a diver tonight. She'd recruited a witness, and the deposition had already begun, and it would continue at depth, in the dark, behind the rock, where the sea kept its books and expected every entry balanced.

Tomorrow's ebb. First slack.

He closed the notebook, stowed it against his chest where the oilcloth had kept it dry for six years, and went up into the cockpit to watch the Corvus lights burning on the black water — and to check, for the hundredth time, the tide table pinned above the wheel, where tomorrow's numbers waited in his own handwriting, corrected in someone else's.

He'd copied Ines's figures over his own that evening without thinking much about it. Lying in the dark afterward, he thought about it a great deal.

Her father's numbers. He'd know soon enough whether the dead man's arithmetic still held — and whether the living woman's did, and whether his own name, once it went back down into that cave, would come up belonging to someone he could stand to be.

The ebb would start at 0440. He set no alarm. Men who owed as much as Teo Brandt owed had never needed one.
