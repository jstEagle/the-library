## 9. First Descent

Teo Brandt had a rule about caves: never go into one you couldn't see out of. It had kept him alive through eleven years of salvage work in waters that ate boats and men with the same indifferent appetite. The rule had failed him exactly once — off Punta Salinas, in a flooded quarry, when he'd been twenty-four and stupid enough to trust a line someone else had set — and it had cost him two fingers' worth of feeling in his left hand and most of his faith in other divers.

He was about to break it again.

The cave mouth yawned below him like something breathing. From the surface of the inner pool — a lens of green-black water cupped in the rock forty feet beneath the cliff shelf where Ines Nakamura crouched with her lines and her ledger-precise tide tables — he could see perhaps ten feet down before the light gave up and became rumor. Beyond that: nothing. A throat of stone swallowing itself.

"You don't have to do this," Ines said. Her voice came down the rock oddly flattened, stripped of its usual edge. "We can wait for slack water tomorrow. Run better lines. Get more light down there."

"Tomorrow's tide comes in higher." Teo checked his gauge for the third time, though he'd checked it thirty seconds ago. "You said it yourself. Every hour we wait, the passage gets shorter and the fleet gets closer. And I owe everyone on this island money, but I don't owe them my patience."

"That's not an argument, that's a joke."

"It's both. Best kind." He spat into his mask and rinsed it, watching the salt water sheet away. His hands were steady. That surprised him. Inside, some old animal part of him was already screaming, the part that remembered the quarry, the blackness closing like a fist. But his hands were steady, and that was the only part of himself he'd ever really trusted. "Talk me through the line one more time."

Ines recited it without looking at her notes, which annoyed him and impressed him in equal measure. "Primary line runs from the anchor bolt at the pool's edge, down through the first constriction at eight meters, along the left-hand wall — *your* left going in — to the second constriction at fourteen meters. After that the passage dog-legs right and opens up; we don't know how far. I've tied a directional marker every three meters. If you lose the line, you follow your guideline back the way you came. If you lose *that*—"

"I don't lose lines."

"—you stop, you breathe, you feel for the wall, and you come back slow," she finished, as if he hadn't spoken. "I'm not losing you in there, Teo. You're the only diver on this island who'll work for what I can pay, which means you're the only diver whose death would be an inconvenience."

"There she is." He grinned around his regulator, fitting it into his mouth. "I was worried you were going soft on me."

Her face did something complicated — the ghost of a smile fighting a tide of something older and sadder — and then she turned away to tend the line reel, and Teo let himself fall backward into the water.

The cold hit him like a confession. Even in the shallows of Isla Verde the sea held winter in its bones this time of year, and the cave pool was fed by some deep vein of it, a chill that climbed through his wetsuit and settled against his spine like a hand. He pulled himself under, found the primary line by touch, and squeezed twice — the signal — before letting the darkness take him.

* * *

Diving a cave was not diving. Teo had tried to explain this to civilians once, at a bar in Puerto Cielo, and had given up halfway through because there was no way to say it without sounding either mad or religious. Open-water diving was conversation: you and the sea, talking, negotiating, each of you free to leave. Cave diving was prayer. You went in knowing that everything you needed to survive was either strapped to your body or strung out behind you on a thin braided line, and that the entire ocean had been replaced by a single corridor of stone, and that if you made one mistake there would be no surface, no boat, no sky — only the long patient dark, waiting to be forgiven.

He descended through the first constriction at eight meters, shoulders brushing rock on both sides, exhaling bubbles that raced upward toward a light that was already just a memory. The line ran taut and true through his gloved fingers. Ines had tied her markers well — small plastic tabs, each one knotted differently so he could count them blind. One. Two. Three. He passed the second constriction at fourteen meters and felt the passage narrow until his tank scraped stone behind him, a sound like a fingernail drawn down the world's longest chalkboard, and for a moment he hung there in the throat of the earth, unable to go forward, unwilling to go back, breathing the metallic taste of his own fear.

Then the dog-leg. Then open water.

Not open, exactly. But wider — the beam of his dive light suddenly falling into space instead of wall, the line going slack in his hand as the passage exhaled him into a chamber whose far side his lamp couldn't find. He hovered there, weightless in a cathedral of drowned stone, and let the numbers run: depth eighteen meters, air at two-thirds, elapsed time six minutes. Turn point. Whatever he was going to find, he'd found it, and now—

His light swept across something that was not stone.

Wood. Timbers — massive ones, black with age but sound as church beams, running in parallel rows into the dark. And between them, stacked with the neatness of a warehouse rather than a wreck, crates. Dozens of them. Hundreds. Their staves were oak, their bands iron gone to orange lace, and stamped into the wood of the nearest one, crisp as the day a dockworker's mallet had struck it, were words that stopped Teo's heart mid-beat:

MERIDIAN STAR — CARGO MANIFEST 44 — ISLA VERDE CONSULATE

He knew the name. Everyone on the island knew the name. The Meridian Star was the ghost story mothers told children who asked too many questions about why the old families were rich — a treasure ship lost with all hands in the great storm of '31, carrying funds that officially had never existed, bound for accounts that officially had never been opened. Forty years of divers had hunted it. Forty years of historians had dismissed it. Kenji Nakamura had spent the last decade of his career insisting it was real, and the last decade of his reputation being destroyed for saying so.

And here it was. Not scattered across a reef. Not broken on some distant bar. Here, tucked inside a sea cave like a letter sealed in an envelope, intact, patient, *waiting*.

Teo's light traveled the rows and his breath went strange in his own ears. There was order here. Deliberate order. These crates hadn't been dumped; they'd been *stowed*, and then the ship had been brought here — or sunk here — with care. His mind, trained by years of reading wrecks the way other men read faces, began assembling the story unbidden: scuttled, not lost. Sunk on purpose, in a place no honest ship had reason to be, by people who needed cargo to disappear so completely that even the sea would forget it.

Above him, the ceiling of the chamber curved back and rose — and there, catching the weak scatter of his lamp, a shimmer. Air. A pocket of it, trapped in the highest vault of the rock, silver-bellied and trembling.

He should turn. Six minutes out meant six minutes back, plus reserve, plus the constriction, plus the cold. Every rule he owned said *turn now*.

He kicked upward instead.

* * *

The air pocket was the size of a small chapel. Teo surfaced into it slowly, one hand raised ahead of his mask, and broke through into a darkness that smelled of stone and old salt and something else — something dry and human, like the inside of an old chest. He pulled his regulator out and breathed, and the air tasted stale and mineral and impossibly sweet, because it was air, real air, in a place where air had no business surviving.

"Hola," he said aloud, just to hear a voice, even his own. The chamber echoed it back, softened, respectful.

He braced his shoulder against a shelf of rock and lifted his light high, sweeping the full arc of the pocket, and what he saw made him forget, for a long moment, that he was a man on a clock hanging by a line over a flood.

The hold of the Meridian Star filled the chamber below the waterline, but above it — stacked, lashed, and tarped against the centuries — the upper cargo deck ran the length of the cave. Crates in ranks. Barrels in bays, their hoops rusted to crowns of red lace. And along the near bulkhead, mounted on brackets that some careful hand had bent outward before the flooding, lanterns — glass still intact, wicks still seated, as if the crew had expected to come back and relight them.

Nobody had come back. Forty years, and nobody had come back, and the ship had waited in the dark with its lamps trimmed and its cargo squared away, keeping its own counsel, keeping its own books.

Teo thought of Renn, who owed him nothing and whom he trusted anyway, because the alternative was trusting no one. He thought of the Corvus Fleet's cranes swinging over some other man's fortune. He thought of Ines Nakamura up there on the shelf, running her lines with her father's precision and her father's stubbornness and none of her father's luck, and he understood — suddenly, completely, the way you understand a wave only after it has already broken over you — why she had looked at him that morning with something close to desperation. This wasn't treasure. This was a verdict. This was forty years of lies sitting in a courtroom of stone, waiting for someone brave enough or foolish enough to read them aloud.

He laughed. It came out ragged in the dead air, and the chamber took it and gave it back gentler, like forgiveness.

Then he heard the engines.

* * *

At first he told himself it was his own pulse. Divers heard things — blood in the ears, breath in the reg, the sea muttering through stone. He killed his light and hung motionless in the black chapel, listening with his whole body, the way he'd learned to listen in the navy years ago, back when listening was the difference between coming home and becoming a coordinate on a chart.

There. Again. Low, diesel-heavy, rhythmic. Not one engine. Several. The sound carried strangely through the rock, arriving less as noise than as vibration — a tremor in the water below him, a shiver in the stone against his shoulder, the whole cave humming like a struck bell.

Boats. Big ones. Coming around the headland into the cove above.

Teo's watch said he had four minutes of bottom time left before he violated his own turn pressure, and his body said *go now*, and his mind said something colder: *the fleet*. Vance's emergency concessions had been announced yesterday morning; the Corvus survey vessels were supposed to take two days to mobilize, two days of permits and provisions and the particular bureaucratic slowness of men who believed they owned time itself. Ines had built her whole plan on those two days.

Either she'd been wrong, or someone had moved fast — faster than permits move, faster than provisions move — which meant someone wanted this site badly enough to skip the paperwork, and men who skipped paperwork skipped other things too. Like warnings. Like mercy.

The vibration deepened. He felt it in his teeth. Somewhere above, anchors were going down — he could almost hear the chain, though that might have been imagination, might have been the cave speaking, might have been forty years of drowned men finally clearing their throats.

Teo did the arithmetic that had kept him alive longer than his skill deserved. Four minutes of air in the chamber, comfortable. Eight minutes back through the passage, tight. The constriction at fourteen meters, where his tank had sung against the rock. The pool. The light. Ines.

If the fleet was in the cove, Ines was alone on a cliff shelf directly above whatever was about to happen next, holding a line attached to a diver the fleet didn't know existed, on a site the magistrate had just handed to men who solved problems with cranes and, if the stories were true, occasionally with less subtle tools.

He jammed the regulator back into his mouth and dropped below the surface, killing his light entirely. No sense advertising. He found the guideline by feel — left hand, smooth braid, Ines's knots counting themselves under his fingers — and swam the return leg through absolute blackness, faster than wisdom allowed, the walls of the passage pressing close enough to touch on both sides, his own bubbles the only stars in the universe.

At the second constriction his tank caught again, hard, and for one lurching second he was pinned — wedged in the throat of the cave with his air ticking down and the vibration of engines thrumming through fifty feet of stone above him — and then he turned his shoulders the way he'd taught himself to turn them, the quarry lesson, the lesson that had cost him two fingers' worth of feeling and bought him his life, and the rock let him go.

Eight meters. Five. Three. The water warmed by fractions. Light appeared above him — gray-green, trembling, real — and then he was rising through it into the pool beneath the cliff, breaking the surface into gray morning air and the smell of rain-wet stone.

Ines was already hauling on the line, reeling it in with fast, economical pulls, and when she saw him surface she went still in a way that frightened him more than the engines had.

"They're here," he said, spitting the reg out. "Early. More than one boat. Big."

She didn't ask how he knew. She was already turning toward the cliff top, shading her eyes against the brightening east, and Teo watched her face as she looked out at the cove and saw what he could feel through the soles of his feet: the Corvus Fleet, arriving like a verdict of its own, three hulls standing in past the headland with their working lights still on against the dawn.

"Ines," he said quietly. "How much time did you say we had?"

Her answer came flat and cold across the pool, and it was worse than any number.

"Less than I thought."
