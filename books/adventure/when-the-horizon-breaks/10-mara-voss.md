## 10. Mara Voss

The *Corvus Queen* rode at anchor a half-mile off the cliffs like a statement of intent, and Captain Mara Voss stood on her quarterdeck making sure the statement was being read correctly.

She had commanded salvage vessels for nineteen years — tramp steamers in the Sulu Sea, dredge barges off the Yucatán, and for the last six, the finest fleet of its class anywhere in these waters. She knew what her ship looked like from shore: black hull, white superstructure, the corvus — the boarding crow — stitched in gold on her funnel. She knew that to the fishermen pulling their nets along Isla Verde's coast this morning, the *Queen* said one thing and one thing only: *this water is spoken for.*

Good. Let them read it. Let them carry it up into the town, into the cantinas and the magistrate's office, until everyone understood that whatever the storm had given back last night belonged to Aurelio Vance now.

"Captain." Her first mate, Dario Pell, came up the ladder with the morning report folded in one fist. He was a good officer in the way that mattered most aboard a working vessel: he was punctual, he was sober before noon, and he did not ask questions twice. "Divers are suited at the outer chamber. Current's slack for another forty minutes. Voss—" He caught himself, corrected. "The survey team wants your eyes on something before they go down."

"What kind of something?"

"The kind they don't want to put in writing."

Mara took the report anyway, unfolded it, and found exactly what she expected: nothing. Timbers counted, sediment depth estimated, visibility poor but workable. A neat little document with a hole in the middle of it where the interesting part should have been.

"Burn it," she said.

Dario fed the page into the galley stove without comment. That was another thing she valued in him. On the Corvus Fleet, paper was a liability. Everything that mattered lived in heads, and heads could be trusted or replaced.

She went forward to the rail and looked at the cliffs.

The northeaster had done in one night what geology had been refusing to do for three hundred years. The sea cave below the eastern headland — the one every chart marked as a shoal hazard and no chart marked as anything else — stood open like a mouth, its throat flooded at high water, its teeth a scatter of black oak timbers stamped with letters she could not read from here but did not need to. She'd seen the photographs the recon divers brought up at first light. *MERIDIAN STAR.* Burned into the wood with a branding iron, the way colonial shipping companies marked their hulls when they expected their cargo to outlive their paperwork.

Every salvor on this coast grew up on the story. The Meridian Star, treasury transport, lost with all hands and all accounts in 1841 somewhere between the mainland mint and the colonial seat — lost so completely that within a generation people argued about whether she had ever existed at all. Half the island's oldest fortunes were supposed to have been built on what went down with her. The other half were built on never having to explain where their money actually came from.

And now she was lying in a cave below Isla Verde's cliffs, exposed by a storm, three days before the emergency concessions would vest permanently in the Corvus Fleet.

Three days. Six tides, if you counted the way Mara counted, which was honestly.

"Captain?" Dario again, at her shoulder. "Magistrate's launch coming out."

"I see it."

Vance's launch was white and too clean for the water it sat in, flying the magistrate's pennant though pennants meant nothing outside a harbor. It came alongside with more fuss than was necessary — fenders down, lines thrown, two of Vance's personal security standing in the bow with the particular stiffness of men who wanted to be seen carrying weapons without ever having to discuss why. Mara watched the gangway go down and made herself comfortable against the rail. If Vance wanted to board her ship, he could climb her ship.

He climbed it. Aurelio Vance came over the rail with the help of two hands and a great deal of dignity, and stood on her deck in a linen suit the color of bone, adjusting his cuffs as though the sea air had personally offended them.

"Captain Voss."

"Magistrate." She didn't offer a hand. He hadn't offered one either; between the two of them, courtesy was a currency neither wished to be caught holding. "You're up early."

"The island doesn't govern itself." He smiled the smile he used on donors and widows. "I trust the operation is proceeding."

"You trust a great deal. The divers go down inside the hour. We'll have the outer chamber mapped by tonight."

"Mapped." He turned to look at the cliffs himself, and for a moment — only a moment — Mara saw past the linen and the smile to the thing underneath, which was not confidence at all. It was appetite with fear wrapped around it, like meat gone slightly off. "Mapping is well and good, Captain, but I want you to understand priorities. There is a chest aboard that wreck. Lead-lined, iron-banded, roughly a meter long. The manifest chest. When your divers find it — and they will find it — it comes to me first. Before any cargo. Before any specie. Before anything else in that cave touches daylight."

"A manifest," Mara said. "Paperwork."

"Paperwork is history, Captain. And history is the most valuable cargo a wreck can carry." He said it lightly, but his eyes stayed on the cliffs, and his jaw worked once, like a man chewing on something that wouldn't go down.

*You're afraid of a piece of paper,* Mara thought. *Three hundred years old, soaked in saltwater, and it frightens you more than the gold does.*

Aloud, she said: "Condition will be bad. Paper doesn't survive flooding unless it's sealed, and if it's sealed we open it in controlled conditions, not on an open deck—"

"I'm aware of conservation protocols." His voice cooled by ten degrees. "I am also aware that my brother-in-law holds the note on this fleet, and that the concession terms give me final authority over recovery order. Open it however you like, Captain. Just make certain that when it opens, I am looking at it first."

There it was. Not the request — requests could be negotiated. The structure underneath the request: money, authority, and the implicit reminder that the *Corvus Queen* floated on his family's credit. Mara had spent nineteen years learning to hear the keel beneath a man's words.

"And after you've looked at it?" she asked.

"After?" Vance finally turned from the cliffs and gave her the full weight of his attention, and she understood that this was the real conversation, the one under the conversation. "After, Captain, the site will be documented, the find will be registered through proper channels, and the historical record will reflect whatever the evidence supports. That is how archaeology works. Isn't it?"

It wasn't how archaeology worked. It wasn't even how salvage worked. But it was, she recognized with a cold settling in her stomach, how erasure worked, dressed in its Sunday clothes.

"I'll send word when we have it," she said.

"Do." He paused at the gangway, one hand on the rail, and looked back at her with something almost like candor — the flicker of a man who needed, just once, to be understood by someone competent. "Captain. Do you know what happens to discoveries that come at inconvenient times?"

"They get managed," Mara said.

"They get *managed*," Vance agreed warmly. "I'm glad we're professionals together."

He went down the gangway. The launch pulled away, trailing its wake across the flat morning water, and Mara stood at the rail watching it shrink until Dario appeared beside her with coffee she hadn't asked for.

"Well?" he said.

"He wants the manifest first. Personally. Before anything else surfaces."

Dario drank his own coffee and considered that. "Manifests don't usually scare magistrates."

"No."

"Unless there's something in them."

"Yes."

They stood together in silence while the divers' tender pulled away toward the cave, its engine a low mutter across the water. Mara finished her coffee and thought about the Meridian Star, and about stories, and about which parts of stories got written down and which parts got bought.

* * *

Her father had told her the legend properly, the way a salvor tells it — not as romance but as arithmetic.

She was nine years old, sitting on the wheelhouse roof of his tramp steamer while he smoked below and shouted up at her through the hatch. *Everyone thinks the Star sank in the deep water past the shelf, girl, because that's where treasure sinks. But think. A treasury transport runs heavy, runs slow, runs scared. She hugs the coast. She dies in shallow water, close to land, close to whoever wanted her dead.* He had tapped ash into the wind. *Wrecks don't lie. Wrecks can't lie. They sit exactly where somebody put them, and everything else — the charts, the reports, the official histories — that's all just people talking.*

People talking. Aurelio Vance, talking. The colonial registry, talking. Every official record of the Meridian Star's loss, talking — and beneath all of it, a lead-lined chest sitting in the dark saying something else entirely.

Mara went below to the chart room and shut the door.

The room was the oldest part of the ship, kept deliberately archaic: paper charts, brass instruments, a table scarred by thirty years of dividers. She unrolled the coastal survey of Isla Verde and weighted its corners. Then she took out the folder she kept locked in the drawer beneath the table — the folder Dario didn't know about, that no one aboard knew about — and laid its contents beside the chart.

Six years of documents. Not stolen, exactly. Accumulated. Salvage was an industry of leaks; information moved through harbors the way bilgewater moved through a hull, and Mara had simply never bailed. Survey reports filed and then withdrawn from the record. A permit application in Kenji Nakamura's name, dated seven years back, covering precisely this stretch of coastline — denied, with prejudice, by the office of Magistrate Aurelio Vance, on grounds described in language so vague it could only have been deliberately vague. A follow-up letter from Nakamura appealing the denial. No reply on file. A second application, six months later. Denied again. After that, nothing. The name Kenji Nakamura vanished from the registries the way names do when someone powerful decides a man is finished.

And underneath those papers, the item she had paid a harbor clerk far too much money to copy: the original geological survey of Isla Verde's eastern headland, commissioned privately, never published. In its margin, in a hand she now recognized because she had seen it that morning on a dozen official memos, a single annotation:

*Anomaly consistent with hull timbers at depth 4–7m. Recommend no further investigation. A.V.*

Dated eleven years ago. Nine years before Vance held office. Nine years before he had any authority to recommend anything — except that whoever commissioned the survey had evidently taken his recommendation, buried the survey, and remembered the favor. Men rose on favors like that. Men built whole careers on knowing where a body was buried and making sure it stayed buried.

Mara sat back in the chair and looked at the annotation for a long time.

*A.V.* Eleven years ago. Which meant Vance had known about the Meridian Star — or at least suspected — since before the fleet existed, since before the concessions, since before any of the machinery he'd spent a decade building to legally own this coastline. The storm hadn't given him the wreck. The storm had merely forced his hand. He'd been waiting on this cave the way a spider waits on a web it built years ago, and now the web was shaking, and he was frightened, and frightened men with power do not recover manifests out of scholarly interest.

They recover them to burn them.

She heard the dive boat return early and went up top. The recon diver came aboard gray-lipped and shaking his head, reporting current surge inside the passage, poor bottom time, nothing recovered. Routine setbacks. But his eyes went to hers and held, and when the others had gone below he lingered at the rail beside her.

"There's timber inside the inner chamber, Captain. Intact decking. And crates — dozens of them, stacked in rows, still lashed." He wiped spray from his face. "That wreck didn't break up. Somebody put her away neat."

"Somebody scuttled her," Mara said.

"In a cave? With the entrance sealed behind her?" The diver shook his head slowly. "Somebody wanted her found someday, Captain. Or wanted her found by nobody. One of the two."

Mara looked at the cliffs. The cave mouth gaped in the morning light, deceptively calm, holding its breath around whatever it had carried for three centuries.

"Rig the second dive," she said. "Inner passage. I want eyes on that manifest chest before the tide turns."

"And when we find it?"

She was quiet a moment. Nineteen years of professional loyalty sat in her chest like ballast — solid, necessary, the reason she was still afloat. Ballast kept a ship upright. It also kept her from moving.

"When we find it," she said, "you'll bring it to me. And I'll decide who looks at it first."

The diver nodded and went aft. Dario appeared at her elbow, reading her face the way good officers do.

"That's not what the magistrate ordered," he said quietly.

"No," Mara agreed. "It isn't."

Above the cliffs, gulls wheeled and screamed over the cave mouth, and the tide, patient as arithmetic, began to turn. Somewhere inland, Mara thought, a woman named Nakamura was probably doing her own calculations. Harbor gossip traveled faster than launches; the whole coast would know by nightfall whose daughter was poking around the headland with a survey kit. Vance knew it too — she'd seen it in the set of his shoulders, that extra degree of tension he couldn't quite iron out of his linen.

Which meant the clock was running on more than tides.

"Dario," she said. "Find out everything the fleet knows about Ines Nakamura. And keep it off paper."

He raised an eyebrow but wrote nothing down, which was its own answer. "Thinking of hiring her?"

"Thinking of finding out who else is racing us," Mara said, "and whether the magistrate plans to let either of us surface."

The *Corvus Queen*'s flag snapped in the offshore breeze, gold crow on black field, and below the cliffs the second dive boat pulled away toward the dark mouth of the cave — toward the lead-lined chest, and the paper inside it, and the truth that Aurelio Vance had spent eleven years and one man's career keeping underwater.

Mara Voss watched it go and felt, for the first time in six years of profitable service, the ballast shift.
