## 11. Second Tide: The Manifest

The second tide came in gray and mean, a low ceiling of cloud pressing down on the water like a lid on a pot that wanted to boil. Ines felt it in her teeth before she saw it — the pressure change, the way the air went thick and mineral, tasting of iron and old storms. Her father used to say the sea kept its own books, and on mornings like this she believed it absolutely. The sea remembered everything. It was only the people on land who forgot.

She crouched at the cave mouth with Teo's dive slate balanced on her knee, running the numbers again because the numbers were the only thing in her life that had never lied to her. Slack water at 9:40. Window of ninety-one minutes before the flood current turned the inner passage into a washing machine. Enough time. Barely.

"You always check twice," Teo said behind her, hauling the tanks down the slick rock. He moved like a man who'd spent twenty years learning where not to put his weight, which on Isla Verde's basalt was most places. "Your father do that too?"

Ines didn't answer. She clipped the line reel to her harness and checked the anchor stake driven into the crack above the cave mouth — twice.

"Right," Teo said. "Touchy subject."

"It's not touchy." She tested the stake with her boot heel. "It's a closed file."

"Sure." He grinned, and there was something in the grin that made her want to hit him and something that made her want to laugh, and she resented both impulses equally. "Closed files are my specialty. Ask anyone."

* * *

The flooded passage swallowed them at 8:15, and for eleven minutes there was nothing in the world but the green murk, the guide line singing faintly through her gloved fingers, and the sound of her own breathing amplified inside the mask until it seemed to come from somewhere outside her body. Ines had logged two hundred dives, maybe more, but every one began with the same small death — the moment the surface closed over her head and the world she understood ended, and the world she didn't began.

Teo swam point. He was better in the dark than she was; he'd admitted as much on the skiff ride out, without shame, the way a man admits to knowing how to pick locks. "Corvus trained me to work black water," he'd said. "No visibility, no bottom reference, just your hands and your nerve. Voss used to say the ocean doesn't care what you found yesterday. I hated her for saying it. Then I started saying it to the new kids."

Now his light swept ahead of hers, catching the passage walls — not natural rock here, she noticed, not anymore. Cut stone. Dressed blocks fitted without mortar, the kind of work you saw in colonial harbor foundations, except this was forty meters inside a cliff that no chart had ever marked as anything but solid. Someone had built this. Or widened it. Or known about it and kept it off every map that mattered.

Her father's voice, six years dead to her and — she still couldn't finish the thought — rose unbidden: *Charts are arguments, mija. Every one of them is somebody's opinion about where things are. Find out whose opinion paid for the chart, and you'll find out why the reef isn't on it.*

She'd been seventeen and thought he was being dramatic. She was thirty-one now, swimming through a hole in the world that four official hydrographic surveys had failed to notice, and she understood him completely, and it made her furious, because understanding him was the one thing she'd sworn off like liquor.

The passage kinked left, then opened.

Teo's light found the surface first — a trembling silver ceiling, an air pocket the size of a chapel. They broke through into a darkness that their lamps could only argue with, and the beams went out and out and out and came back with shapes.

Crates. Rows of them, stacked on a shelf of natural rock above the high-water line, lashed with tarred cordage that had gone hard as sculpture. Oak, mostly, some teak, stenciled with faded lettering that the salt had half-eaten. And along the far wall, half-tumbled as if dropped in haste, a scatter of smaller chests — iron-bound, lead-sheathed, the kind of containers men built when they wanted the contents to survive fire, flood, and prying hands.

"The hold," Teo breathed. His voice did something strange in the enclosed air, arriving from everywhere at once. "This is the hold. We're standing in the actual hold of—"

"Don't say it." Ines pulled herself onto the rock shelf, boots finding purchase between the crates. "Don't name it yet. Names bring people."

"People are already coming. You heard the engines yesterday."

"I heard engines. Engines aren't names." But she'd heard them too — the deep-throated churn of Corvus fleet trawlers working the outer anchorage, close enough that the sound carried into the cave through the water itself, a vibration more felt than heard, like a predator's purr through a floor. They had maybe a day, possibly less, before Voss's divers pushed past the outer chamber and found the passage. Everything they took after that would be taken under armed supervision, catalogued by Vance's clerks, and disappeared into the same ledger-shaped darkness that had eaten her father's career.

So: today. This tide. The chest.

She'd marked it on the first descent — third row, second position, lead-sheathed, smaller than the specie boxes and heavier than it looked, with a hasp that wasn't rusted shut like the others but deliberately sealed, soldered over, as if someone had wanted it closed forever. Lead lining meant documents. Documents meant paper worth protecting from seawater, which meant paper worth reading.

Which meant, if the letter in her pack was telling the truth, paper worth killing for.

"Ines." Teo had gone quiet in the way that made her nervous, because quiet on Teo Brandt was effortful. He was crouched by the soldered chest, his lamp angled low, and his face when he looked up had lost all its easy humor. "There's a survey mark on this. Stamped into the lead."

She crossed the shelf in three steps and knelt beside him.

There it was, punched clean into the soft metal: a triangle over three horizontal bars, with a serial code beneath — KN-1147-C.

Kenji Nakamura. Survey station 1147, coastal series. Her father's registration mark, the one stamped into every datum pin he'd ever driven, every benchmark he'd ever set, back when his word about this coastline was law instead of joke.

The cave went very still around her. Even the drip of water from the passage ceiling seemed to pause, listening.

"He was here," Teo said softly. "Before any of this. Before the storm, before the permits, before Vance ever smelled money in it. Your old man stood right where you're kneeling and put his mark on this box."

"And then told someone." Ines heard her own voice come out flat and cold, a stranger's voice. "And that someone sold him. That's the only version that fits. He finds the Meridian Star, seals the manifest, reports it through channels — and channels eat it. Six years later his license is revoked, his name is mud, and this box sits here untouched because nobody who mattered wanted it opened."

"Or nobody who mattered knew where it was."

"Somebody knew." She ran her thumb across the serial code. "Somebody always knows. That's how the world works, Teo. Knowledge doesn't die. It just changes owners."

* * *

Opening the chest took forty minutes and cost them most of their margin.

The solder seal gave way to Teo's knife and a patience Ines hadn't expected from him — he worked the seam like a surgeon, heating the lead with a chemical heat pack from his kit until it softened, peeling it back in curls, muttering apologies to the box as though it were a sleeping animal. Inside the lead sheath: tin. Inside the tin: wax-sealed oilcloth bundles, dry as the day they'd been wrapped, and beneath them a layer of wool packing gone musty but intact.

And on top of everything, a single envelope, heavy stock, addressed in a hand Ines recognized before she finished drawing breath.

*Nakamura, K. — personal.*

Not her father's writing. The address hand was cramped, hurried, the ink feathered where the pen had pressed too long in one place. But the paper was good paper, the kind officials used, and the seal beside the name had been broken and re-stuck carelessly, as if whoever opened it last hadn't cared who knew.

She should have handed it to Teo. Some part of her registered that — protocol, chain of custody, all the rules she'd learned in the two semesters of archaeology coursework she'd paid for before the money ran out. Instead she tore it open with wet fingers and read it by lamplight in the hold of a ship that had been legally dead for a hundred and sixty years.

It was short. A cover memo, typed, with a handwritten postscript.

*Dr. Nakamura — Office of the Territorial Magistrate acknowledges receipt of your preliminary report ref. site 1147-C ("Meridian Star, conjectural"). Be advised that findings of this nature require corroboration prior to formal registration. Your submission has been forwarded for review. Do not attempt independent verification or public disclosure pending outcome of review. — Office of A. Vance, Magistrate.*

The postscript, in the same cramped hand:

*Burn your field notes, Professor. Some cargo stays sunk. Consider this professional courtesy. It will not be repeated.*

Ines read it three times. The words didn't change. That was the horror of words — you could read them until your eyes bled and they stayed exactly what they were.

"Burn your field notes." Teo had leaned in close enough to read over her shoulder, and his voice had dropped into something she hadn't heard from him before — not the salvor's banter, not the debtor's shuffle, but a flat, old anger, banked coals. "He wrote that. Vance wrote that. Before Vance had the office — look at the date. This is from when he was just a clerk in the territorial survey office. He was *in the survey office* when your father filed."

"Six years ago." Her voice sounded far away. "The report that ended him. The one that said his Meridian Star claims were 'unsupported by any physical evidence and inconsistent with established hydrographic record.' The review board cited it. Three members. One of them was Aurelio Vance, before he ran for magistrate on a platform of cleaning up the permit system." She laughed, and the sound bounced wrong off the crates. "He cleaned it up, all right. He cleaned it so thoroughly that the biggest wreck discovery in the hemisphere vanished into his brother-in-law's concession pipeline."

"Ines." Teo's hand hovered near her shoulder, not quite landing. "Breathe. Then look at the rest. There's more in here, and we've got maybe fifty minutes before that passage turns ugly."

She breathed. She looked.

The oilcloth bundles held the manifest.

Not the manifest — the official one, the shipping abstract archived in the colonial registry, the document every historian had quoted, the tidy list of trade goods and ballast that explained the Meridian Star's loss as routine: overloaded, caught in a gale, lost with all hands off the Verde coast, tragedy, mystery, footnote. Ines had read that abstract a dozen times growing up, first as her father's bedtime story material — he'd loved the wreck the way other fathers loved dragons — and later, after the disgrace, as the object lesson in what obsession cost a man.

This was not that document.

This was the real one, kept by someone aboard who had known the official version was a lie. Pages of ship's stationery in a fine copperplate, cross-referenced against a printed cargo abstract that had been annotated, corrected, contradicted — line after line struck through and replaced in the same careful hand. Where the registry listed cotton and cochineal, the true manifest recorded specie: minted silver, weighed and crated, provenance noted with initials and dates that mapped onto the colonial treasury disbursement ledgers like a key fitting a lock. Where the registry listed machinery parts, the true manifest recorded bearer bonds. Where the registry said *ballast, stone,* the true manifest said, in letters pressed deep enough to score the page beneath: *ballast, coin, 41 crates, see vault.*

"See vault," Ines whispered. "There's a vault room."

"Later." Teo was already photographing pages with the waterproof case camera, working fast, lamp braced between his knees. "Keep reading. What else?"

What else was the part that stopped her heart and restarted it wrong.

Bound into the back of the manifest, sewn in with the binding cord, was a sheaf of correspondence — copies, carbons, the bureaucratic sediment of a cover-up in progress. Requisitions. Transfer orders. And a memorandum, dated eight days before the Meridian Star sailed on her final voyage, signed by the colonial treasurer and countersigned by three names that Ines knew — not from history books, but from the island itself. Names carved into courthouse lintels. Names on the founding plaques of banks and sugar estates and the cathedral roof. Old families. The island's bones.

The memo was brief. It authorized the transfer of treasury funds "for secure offshore custody pending resolution of audit irregularities." It instructed that the transfer be conducted "without entry in the general register." And it specified the vessel.

The Meridian Star was never lost. She was scuttled — or meant to be — carrying the proof of an embezzlement so large its authors needed the evidence itself to disappear. The ship was the shredder. The sea was the filing cabinet.

Except the sea, as her father liked to say, keeps its own books.

"Teo." She couldn't lift her head. The lamp light swam. "The report. The review board report that killed his career. Read me the justification section. From memory — you've seen it, everyone's seen it, it got reprinted when the license revocation made the papers."

Teo recited it like a liturgy, because on this island it was one: *"The claimed site exhibits no anomaly consistent with wreck deposition. Sediment profiles are unreconciled with stated coordinates. Conclusion: the reported discovery is unsupported by any physical evidence."*

"Now look at page nine of the manifest."

He looked. She watched his face do what hers had done — the slow collapse of comprehension, the moment when a man sees the shape of the machine he's been living inside.

Page nine was a sediment log. The Meridian Star's own survey notes, taken during an earlier voyage, charting the very stretch of coast below the cliffs — including a notation of anomalous depth readings and an undertow pattern at the cave mouth, flagged with a sketch of the passage. Coordinates that matched, to the decimal, the ones in Kenji Nakamura's disgraced report.

Unreconciled. Unsupported. Except here they were, reconciled and supported, in ink older than the lie, in the handwriting of a dead captain, sitting forty meters inside a cliff that four official surveys swore was solid.

"They didn't just ruin him," Ines said. Each word came out separately, placed like stones across a current. "They used his own evidence to do it. His coordinates were accurate. That's what convicted him — the board said the sediment data was impossible, and the sediment data was *real*, it's in this book, it was real a century before he was born. They took the one true thing he ever found and made it the reason nobody would believe him."

The cave dripped. Somewhere beyond the passage, muffled by forty meters of water and rock, an engine note changed pitch — a fleet tender shifting anchorage, indifferent, enormous.

Teo said quietly, "Vance signed the courtesy note before he had power. Then he got power, and he sat on this site for six years waiting for the sea to hand it to him. And now the sea has, and he's going to pump it dry, burn whatever burns, and bury the rest under a legitimate salvage operation with permits stamped by himself." He clicked off the camera. "We have the manifest. Copies?"

"Two sets of negatives, one on each of us." She'd shot the pages herself while he'd worked the chest — redundancy was free, and paranoia was cheap insurance. "Plus the originals go in the cliff cache tonight, split from the negatives."

"Then we're done here. Tide's turning — I can feel it in the line tension." He paused, and in the lamplight his face was younger and older at once. "For what it's worth — your father didn't fail. Any version of this story where a man finds this and reports it honestly and gets destroyed for it? That's not failure. That's the system working exactly as designed. He just wasn't told he was the design flaw."

Ines looked at the envelope in her hand. *Burn your field notes.* She folded it along its original creases and slid it inside her suit, against her skin, where the copies lived.

"No," she said. "He wasn't the flaw. He was the audit."

She was reaching to repack the manifest when her lamp caught something she'd missed — a corner of paper protruding from the false bottom of the chest, wedged where the solder seal had buckled inward. Not correspondence. A single sheet, folded once, in her father's handwriting this time, unmistakable, the tight vertical script she'd grown up watching fill tide tables and birthday cards alike.

Dated six years ago. The week before the review board convened.

*If you are reading this, the seal was broken by someone who is not me. Whoever you are: the manifest is only half the proof. The vault room holds the ledgers. The ledgers hold the names. I could not reach it alone and I will not get another chance. Trust no one holding a permit. — K.N.*

Below it, almost as an afterthought, smaller:

*If it's Ines: the spring tide table is in your mother's recipe box. I'm sorry about everything. Check the third column.*

The line tension sang taut against the anchor stake. The flood was coming.

Ines folded her father's note beside Vance's threat, two pieces of paper six years and a whole ruined life apart, now riding together against her ribs — and above her, through the chapel-dark of the air pocket, she heard it clearly this time, no mistaking it, no blaming the tide:

Engines. Close. More than one. Coming around the point toward the cave.

The third tide belonged to Corvus — and they were early.
