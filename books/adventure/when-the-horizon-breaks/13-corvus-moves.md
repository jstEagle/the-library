## 13. Corvus Moves

Teo Brandt had spent eleven years learning the sound a salvage fleet made, and the Corvus Fleet sang a song he knew by heart. It started before dawn — the deep-throated churn of the *Corvus Anvil*'s pumps being tested, the clatter of the davits, the flat bark of Mara Voss's voice carrying across the water the way a captain's voice always did, pitched to reach the last man on the last line without ever seeming to rise.

From the cliff shelf above the cove, Ines heard it too. She lay flat on her stomach at the lip of the overhang, binoculars pressed to her eyes, and said nothing for a long time.

"They're pumping," she said finally.

"I know."

"You can't know from here."

"You can hear it." He could. The *Anvil* sat anchored two hundred meters off the cave mouth, and even at that distance, over the wind, Teo could pick out the arrhythmic stutter of a centrifugal pump finding its rhythm — *whump, whump-whump, whump* — the sound of the sea being told, politely and with great force, to leave a room it had owned for two hundred years. "They're going for the outer chamber first. Drop the water level, get their people walking on the deck instead of swimming over it. It's what I would do."

"It's what you *did* do," Ines said. "Eleven years ago."

He didn't answer that. There wasn't an answer that helped either of them.

Below, the Corvus tenders were already shuttling gear — hose, generators, the steel shoring frames that meant they intended to be in that cave for weeks, not days. Floodlights on articulated arms. A dive station rigged off the tender's stern with the crisp, ugly efficiency of a company that had done this a hundred times and never once wondered whether it should.

"They'll have the outer chamber walkable by tomorrow night," Teo said. "Maybe the day after, if the surge keeps fouling their intake. After that, they start on the inner passage."

"The inner passage is where the manifest is."

"The inner passage is where *everything* is. Which is why we go in tonight, while they're still setting up, and we go in quiet, and we take what we can carry, and we cache it up here where their divers will never think to look." He tapped the rock beneath them. "Nobody pumps a cliff."

Ines lowered the binoculars. In the gray pre-dawn light her face had the particular stillness he'd learned to read as calculation rather than calm — the look of a woman doing arithmetic with things that couldn't be counted.

"Tonight," she agreed. "And Teo — we work in their shadow. Not their light. If a Corvus diver sees so much as a bubble trail that doesn't belong to their rotation, Vance will have the permits amended by lunch and us off this island by dinner. Or worse."

"Or worse," Teo agreed. He'd seen worse. He'd *been* worse, once, in a way he was not yet ready to say out loud, and every hour he spent in this woman's company the confession climbed another rung of his throat.

He rolled onto his back and watched the sky go from iron to ash to a thin, unwilling pink, and tried not to think about how much the pumping sounded like a heartbeat.

* * *

They went in on the ebb, two hours before slack, when the current through the cave mouth ran outward and would carry their exhaust bubbles out to sea instead of back into their faces. Teo went first with the line reel; Ines followed at ten meters, running the second line from the cliff-side anchor they'd set two nights before, a thin braided poly that no sonar would read and no diver would bother to notice.

The water inside the outer chamber had already dropped. He felt it in the way the passage changed — the ceiling coming down to meet him, the green daylight of the cave mouth giving way to the amber murk of disturbed sediment. The Corvus pumps had the whole chamber breathing. Every rock face wore a fine veil of silt that had been settled for two centuries and was now, thanks to the *Anvil*'s machinery, permanently in motion, turning the water into something like weak tea, like looking through smoked glass.

It was, he had to admit, good work. It was exactly what he would have done. That was the problem with knowing a thing from the inside: you could never quite convince yourself the people doing it were fools.

He found the deck of the *Meridian Star* by feel — the ridge of her keelson rising under his glove like the spine of a buried animal — and worked forward along it, hand over hand on his line, into the section they'd mapped on the first descent. The cargo crates stood in their rows in the dark, patient as church pews, and above them the collapsed section of hull let in a knife of gray light from some fissure in the cliff that Ines had found on the chart and he had sworn he'd never fit through.

They didn't need to fit through it. They needed it for what it was: an exit that wasn't the mouth.

The lead-lined chest was where they'd left it, wedged under the fallen spar, and getting it out was the work of forty minutes and most of his air. Teo had salvaged engines, ordnance, a church bell once, off a wreck in forty meters of current so bad the priest who'd chartered them had wept on the deck. He had never moved anything as carefully as he moved that chest. Two hundred years of water pressure had done what water pressure does to lead — it had flowed, settled, sealed itself around the chest's seams like a second skin. The manifest inside might as well have been in a bank vault. That was the only reason it had survived. That was also why getting it open without tools, without noise, without light bright enough to be seen through the fissure, was going to cost him.

Ines had the answer, because Ines always had the answer, and it had cost her a full day in the island's only hardware store buying things a woman buying rope and epoxy had no business wanting. A cold chisel. A soft-faced mallet. A wedge of hardwood. And a tube of marine epoxy she'd used to bed the chisel's edge with crushed coral grit, so that when it struck lead it struck with the sound of a knuckle on wood — a dull, dead *tok* that the water ate three meters from its source.

They worked in shifts of breath. He'd strike, count to ten, listen, strike again. The chamber answered with its own sounds — the distant stutter of the Corvus pumps transmitted through the rock like a pulse through a wrist, the tick and groan of the hull settling as the water around it dropped, changing pressures that had been balanced for two lifetimes. Once, something large shifted somewhere above them in the dark, and both of them froze for a full minute, gloves on stone, hearts going like trapped birds, until the sound resolved into nothing — a rock, a crab, the cave clearing its throat.

The seam finally gave on his ninth attempt, a thin bright line of daylight-colored metal showing along the chest's lid like a wound. He worked the hardwood wedge in, tapped it home, and felt the lid lift a centimeter with a sigh of trapped air that bubbled up past his mask and was gone.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth that had somehow, impossibly, kept its charge of tallow, lay the manifest.

He couldn't read it down there. He didn't try. He wrapped it in the second oilcloth Ines had brought, zipped it into the dry bag clipped inside his jacket, and gave Ines the signal — *up, out, cache* — and they backed out along their lines the way they'd come, two shadows leaving a room before the owners came home.

* * *

The cliff cache had been Ines's idea, and like most of her ideas it was unglamorous and perfect.

Halfway up the cliff path, behind a fall of tumbled karst that a goat would think twice about climbing, there was a hollow — a solution cavity in the limestone, dry, deep, and invisible from the sea. She'd spent a morning sealing a steel ammunition box inside it with epoxy and rubble, then rebuilding the rockfall over it so precisely that Teo, who had helped her build it and still couldn't find it again without her.

They climbed up in the last light, wet and exhausted, and Ines knelt at the fall of stone and moved three pieces that looked identical to the other three hundred pieces, and the cavity opened like a mouth.

The manifest went in. The strongbox documents from the outer chamber went in beside it — her father's survey marks, the seal she'd found the first week, everything that proved Kenji Nakamura had stood on this deck before any of them. Teo watched her arrange the dry bags in the box with hands that shook slightly from the cold and the climb, and he thought: *she handles them like they're alive. Like they can feel it.*

"Voss's divers start the inner passage tomorrow," he said. "Day after, latest. Once they're walking that deck, we're done working it. Anything left down there stays down there until this is over."

"Then we get everything," Ines said. "Tomorrow night. The logbook, if we can find it. The captain's cabin should be aft of the hold —"

"Should be. Wrecks don't read their own charts." He sat back against the karst, letting the stone take his weight. Below them, the cove had lit up like a carnival. The *Anvil*'s worklights turned the water a hard industrial green, and the pump hose ran from her stern into the cave mouth like an umbilical, and men moved on her decks in the flat way men move on the fourth day of a job they don't love.

"Teo." Ines was looking at him across the cache, her face half shadow. "The diver on rotation tonight. The one running the hose intake. Did you see him?"

"I saw him."

"Look again."

He looked. The intake diver was climbing the ladder onto the tender, hauling his fins, and even at this distance, even in the flat green light, Teo knew the way the man carried his gear — the particular carelessness of someone who had done it so long his body had stopped negotiating with him. Broad back. Cropped hair. The habit of slinging his mask by the strap over one shoulder like a bandolier.

"Renn," Teo said.

"Renn," Ines said.

* * *

He didn't sleep. He told himself it was the watch rotation — they were taking turns, one of them always on the cliff with the binoculars — but when his watch ended he lay in the shelter of the rocks above the cove and stared at the stars and did the arithmetic he'd been avoiding since the first night.

Renn Okafor had been his dive partner for six years. Renn had taught him the breathing trick that saved his life in the collapse off Cayo Blanco. Renn had carried him, once, half-drowned, up a ladder in a swell that would have killed a lesser man, and had never once mentioned it afterward, because that was Renn — he did the enormous thing and then pretended it was nothing, and you loved him for it, and you never asked what else he did and pretended was nothing.

And Renn had been on the cover-up dive. Teo knew that now. He'd known it since the night Ines found the strongbox and the shape of the whole rotten thing had finally assembled itself in his head: the survey that was never done, the report that was written before the dive, the find declared worthless by men who had never gotten wet. Renn had been the diver on that survey. Renn had come up out of water that held a lost ship and told the magistrate's man there was nothing there, and had taken his envelope, and had bought his boat, and had never once mentioned that either.

Or — Teo turned it over, the way you turn a stone to see what's underneath — maybe Renn hadn't known. Maybe he'd been told to survey a sand shelf and had found a sand shelf, because the light was bad and the silt was thick and the man paying him had made it very clear what the report needed to say. Teo knew how that worked. Teo had been that diver, more times than he could count, in the years when the difference between a find and a sand shelf was whatever the money said it was.

That was the thing about shame. It didn't make you certain of other people. It made you certain of nothing, ever, including yourself.

Below him, the pumps stuttered on. *Whump, whump-whump.* The sea leaving a room it had owned for two hundred years, one heartbeat at a time.

* * *

The near-thing came on the third night.

They were deep in the inner passage — deeper than they'd ever worked — with Ines holding the light low and shielded behind her body while Teo pried at the aft cabin's collapsed doorway. The captain's cabin, if the wreck's proportions meant anything, lay beyond it, and the logbook, if God loved fools, lay somewhere inside. The Corvus pumps had dropped the outer chamber so far that their air pocket had migrated, stretched, thinned — the passage's ceiling now ran in a low, wet arch, and the water in the flooded section was colder, moving, restless, the whole cave adjusting itself around the theft of its sea.

Teo had his chisel bedded in the doorjamb's rot, counting his ten seconds between strikes, when Ines's hand closed on his arm.

Not the *listen* grip. The *freeze* grip.

He went still. She killed the light.

In the black, the cave spoke. Water sounds — the pump's pulse through the stone, the drip and settle of two hundred years rearranging itself. And under it, faint and wrong and getting less faint: a hiss. Regulated exhalation. The rhythmic bubble-song of a diver on open circuit, coming up the passage from the outer chamber.

Toward them.

Teo's mind did what it did — laid the map over the dark. The Corvus rotation ran a survey line up the center of the hold. This diver was off-line, or the line had shifted, or he was running a hunch, following the same instinct that had brought Teo to this door: *the captain keeps his secrets aft.* The exhalations came in a slow, easy rhythm — a good diver, relaxed, on a long hose, probably with a tender man topside watching his bubbles through the fissure.

Thirty meters. Twenty. In the absolute dark, Teo could hear the scrape of the man's tank valve against rock, the whisper of his fins. He could smell the man's exhaust — the flat chemical sweetness of it hanging in the dead air.

Ines's hand found his in the dark. He couldn't see her at all. Her grip said what it needed to say: *I'm here. Don't move. Think.*

He thought. The passage behind them was open water — swimming out meant noise, bubbles, a silhouette crossing whatever ambient light leaked from the outer chamber. The fissure above was a crawl he couldn't make with a chest full of gear. The cabin door was half-fallen, and behind it the cabin was a black pocket that might be flooded to the ceiling or might hold air or might be a coffin with good acoustics.

The exhalations stopped.

The diver had stopped moving. Teo could picture him exactly — he'd been that diver a thousand times — hanging in the murk, head cocked, listening, because something had reached him. Not sound. *Absence.* Two divers breathing in a cave make a certain silence, a certain stillness in the water, and a good diver feels it the way you feel someone watching the back of your neck.

Ten seconds. Twenty. The pump pulsed through the stone. *Whump. Whump-whump.* Teo's own breath was down to the bare minimum, a sip at a time, his regulator's exhaust valve clicking like a beetle in the silence. Ines's hand in his was steady. Her pulse, where his fingers lay across her wrist, was not, and somehow that steadied him more than calm would have — she was afraid and she was holding anyway, and that was the whole job, that had always been the whole job.

The diver exhaled, once, slow — the sound of a decision — and turned.

The fin-beats faded down-passage, unhurried, professional, back toward the light and the hose and the green-lit world. Teo counted to a hundred. Counted to two hundred. Then Ines's hand left his, and in the darkness he heard her do the thing he would think about for years afterward: she waited another full minute, and then she struck the chisel herself — one soft, dead *tok* against the doorjamb, testing — and when no exhalation came back up the passage, she relit the lamp shielded behind her palm and went back to work.

They got the door open that night. The cabin beyond was silted to the knees and stripped by time and tide, but against the aft bulkhead, in a recess where a careful man would keep the thing he couldn't afford to lose, they found a small iron box, fuse-welded to the frame, its lock long since rusted to lace.

They couldn't open it down there. They wrapped it, bagged it, and carried it out through the black water, past the empty passage, past the outer chamber where the Corvus floodlights now reached in long green fingers through the cave mouth, up the lines, up the cliff, to the cache.

Ines sealed the box inside with the manifest and the strongbox papers and rebuilt the rockfall over it, stone by stone, in the dark, from memory.

"Tomorrow," she said, when the cache was a cliff again and nothing else. "The logbook's in that box. I can feel it."

"Feelings don't open iron," Teo said. But he was already thinking about tools, and tides, and how many hours they had before Voss's divers worked their way aft.

It was nearly midnight when they came down the cliff path to the skiff, and the *Anvil*'s worklights were dying one by one across the cove, and Teo — who had spent eleven years reading fleets the way other men read weather — stopped dead on the shingle.

The launch was dark. Running without lights, low in the water, coming out of the cave mouth and swinging wide around the *Anvil* toward the harbor. And standing in its stern, silhouetted against the last worklight, broad-shouldered, mask slung over one shoulder like a bandolier, was Renn.

He wasn't looking at the water. He was looking up.

At the cliff path.

Teo pulled Ines down behind the rocks, and they lay flat on the cold shingle, breath held, and watched the dark launch carry Renn Okafor and whatever he'd seen — or thought he'd seen — into the harbor, where the lights of Magistrate Vance's residence burned all night, every night, like a man who had stopped being able to sleep.
