## 14. Third Tide: The Captain's Log

The third tide came in gray and mean, a low ceiling of cloud pressing down on the water like a lid on a pot that had begun to boil. Ines felt the change in her chest before she saw it in the sky — some animal inheritance from her father, the sense that the sea was rearranging its furniture and they had better know where everything stood before it finished.

She crouched at the cliff cache at first light, wrapping the manifest pages in oilcloth for the second time, checking the knots twice because once was a habit and twice was paranoia, and paranoia had kept her fed for six years in a business that ate the careless. The copies rode against her ribs in a waterproof wallet, strapped under her shirt. The originals went into the cache — a hollow behind a slab of karst that Teo swore no one knew but him, which meant, Ines thought grimly, that at least three people knew it.

"You're doing the thing again," Teo said from above her, where he sat on the slab with his legs dangling, working grease into his regulator's first stage. "The counting thing."

"I'm not counting anything."

"You're counting what happens if we lose this." He didn't look up from his work. His hands were steady; his voice wasn't quite. "You count it every time you touch that oilcloth."

Ines tied off the last knot and stood, brushing grit from her knees. Below them, the cave mouth breathed. That was the only word for it — the surge ran in through the submerged passage and exhaled back out with a sound like something enormous sleeping, and every few minutes the whole cliff seemed to sigh around them. Somewhere beyond the headland, hidden by the curve of the coast, the Corvus Fleet's pumps throbbed like a second heartbeat. Voss's divers were draining the outer chamber by inches, and every inch they gained was an inch of territory Ines and Teo would never get back.

"Third tide," she said. "The inner passage opens widest two hours after slack low. After that we're fighting the flood all the way to the hold."

Teo snapped the first stage closed and looked at her. He had a face built for weathering — salt-scoured, creased at the eyes, the kind of face that made strangers assume he was older than he was and more honest than he'd ever been. Six years ago he had been the diver who told a review board that Kenji Nakamura's find was worthless silt and ballast stones. Three days ago he had told Ines that himself, standing in the rain outside her tent with his guilt held out like something bleeding.

"Your father dove this passage," he said. "Did you know that? Back then. Before the report."

She hadn't known. She filed it away in the place where she kept everything about Kenji Nakamura now — not the bitterness drawer anymore, though that still existed, but a newer one, colder and more careful. An evidence drawer.

"He dove it," Teo went on, "and I reported it empty. And I've been diving it in my sleep ever since, trying to figure out what I missed." He shouldered his tank. "Come on. Let's go find out."

* * *

The flooded passage was exactly as bad as memory promised and worse than hope allowed.

Ines ran the line from the outer chamber's lip, paying out rope hand over hand while Teo went down first, his dive light carving a tunnel of green-gold out of the black water. The passage dropped at forty degrees, narrowed, widened, narrowed again — a throat swallowed by the sea when the cave system collapsed in some century-old storm, and only reopened now because the northeaster had torn the cliff's face open like a letter someone finally decided to read.

Her father had taught her to read water the way other children learned to read street signs. *The current tells you the shape of what you can't see,* he used to say, kneeling beside her on the mudflats of Surigao with a tide table in one hand and her small wrist in the other, timing the ebb against his watch. *Water is honest, Ines. It's people who lie.* She had been nine years old and had believed him completely, and it had taken her twenty years and one destroyed career to understand that he'd been warning her about himself.

She pushed the thought down with the line.

At the bottom of the throat the passage bent left, and Teo's light vanished around the bend, and for eleven seconds — she counted, because counting was better than imagining — there was nothing but the rope trembling in her gloved hands and the thunder of her own blood.

Then three tugs. *Clear.*

She followed him down.

The inner chamber opened around her like the inside of a cathedral that had drowned mid-sermon. Her light found columns of rock hung with pale sea fans, a floor of silt rippled into dunes by six decades of tidal breathing, and rising out of the dunes — row upon row, tilted at drunken angles, furred with coral and anemone — cargo crates. Dozens of them. Hundreds. The Meridian Star's last lading, preserved in cold and darkness while the world above forgot the ship had ever existed except as a story drunk fishermen told about lights on the water.

Teo hovered near the far wall, beside a dark rectangle in the rock that their survey of the outer chamber had promised: the entrance to the hold proper, sealed since the wreck by a cargo door warped shut. He was working a pry bar into the seam with the patience of a man defusing something.

Ines checked her gauge. Fifty-one minutes of bottom time if she stayed conservative, less if the swim back fought the flood. She swam to the nearest crate and swept her glove across its lid.

Stenciled letters emerged from the coral film: *M.S. MERIDIAN STAR — CONSIGNED: HACIENDA CRUZ, PUERTO ALTO.* Beneath it, a manifest code she recognized from the lead-lined chest they'd opened on the second tide — the true manifest, the one that listed cargo that existed in no official record. Machinery parts, the official manifests said. Agricultural equipment. Crates bound for plantations that had burned down decades ago, consigned to families that had received nothing, billed anyway, and paid — paid and paid and paid — into accounts that had never bought a single plow.

Embezzlement on a colonial scale. The money that had built half the island's old families, laundered through a shipping company and sunk — literally sunk — when the auditors got too close.

And somewhere in this drowned cathedral was the proof with a signature on it.

Three sharp tugs from Teo. The hold door groaned, gave an inch, stuck, gave another. Silt bloomed around them in brown clouds, and Ines backed off to let it settle, watching the particles hang in her light like a galaxy being born. Her father would have loved this, she thought, and hated herself for thinking it, and thought it again anyway.

The door came free with a shriek she felt in her teeth.

* * *

The hold was smaller than the chamber — a gutted belly of a ship, ribs of black iron arching overhead, and the smell of it reached her even through the regulator: old wood, old iron, old time. Her light swept across collapsed shelving, a broken sextant case, a officer's boot standing upright in the silt like a grave marker.

And there, lashed to a frame beneath the rear bulkhead, a brass cylinder the length of her forearm.

She knew what it was before her brain finished naming it. Every wreck diver did. Ships' papers went in the safe or the tube, and the tube went where the captain could reach it, and captains who expected their ships to sink put them somewhere they could survive the sinking.

The lashings had rotted to lace. She cut them with her knife and drew the cylinder free, and it came up light — too light, her heart said, *empty*, — until she turned it and felt the weight shift inside, and heard, faintly, the dry whisper of paper.

Paper. Surviving sixty years underwater. It happened — she'd seen it happen, in air pockets, in tar-sealed tubes, in the strange chemistry of luck — but it happening here, to the one document that mattered, felt less like archaeology and more like the sea settling a debt.

She signaled Teo, and they rose together toward the air pocket.

* * *

The pocket sat high in the chamber's ceiling vault, a dome of trapped air the size of a chapel, replenished by each tide's compression and slowly going sour between floods. They broke the surface side by side and pulled their regulators, and the air tasted of salt and rust and something mineral and ancient, like drinking from a well.

Ines got the cylinder open on the fourth try, her fingers stupid with cold.

Inside, wrapped in waxed canvas gone stiff as bark, was a book.

It was not much to look at. A ship's log, standard merchant issue, its cover stained the color of dried blood, its corners soft with rot. She lifted it with both hands the way she'd been trained to lift things that had waited longer than she'd been alive — and then stopped, because her training said *document it in situ, photograph, log position*, and none of that mattered, because in ninety minutes the flood would come and Corvus's divers would be in this chamber within the day, and a photograph would not survive Vance's demolition charges. Only removal would.

"*Read it,*" Teo said quietly. "*Read it here. If the water takes it, I want to have heard it.*"

She turned to the final entries.

The handwriting was a clerk's copperplate at first — course, wind, position, cargo inspections, all the dull liturgy of a working ship. Then, eleven days before the last entry, the copperplate changed hands. A different pen. A tighter, angrier script.

*Oct. 14. Boarded at Puerto Alto by persons claiming customs authority. No credentials shown. Ordered to take on additional "machinery" consignment, destination withheld. Told my manifest will be amended ashore. I am master of this vessel and I do not recognize these orders.*

*Oct. 17. Have examined the new consignment. It is not machinery. It is specie — gold coin and bullion, crated and mislabeled, no fewer than forty cases. I have counted. The amounts correspond to no cargo list I have signed. Mr. Vance's agent came aboard tonight and offered me ten thousand pesos to say nothing and sign anything put before me. I declined. He smiled at me the way men smile when the price has not yet found its number.*

Ines's breath went shallow. She read the name again. *Vance.* Not Magistrate Vance — the man had held no office then, had been nothing but a shipping agent's son with ambitions, according to everything Imelda Cruz's people had told her. But the name was the name, and the dates fit, and the shape of it — the offer, the smile, the escalating pressure — fit the man she'd watched from a crowd at the concession announcement, silver-haired and benevolent, talking about heritage and opportunity while his brother-in-law's fleet anchored over a crime scene.

*Oct. 21. They have stopped pretending. This morning four crates were moved into my hold without my knowledge. My chief mate has disappeared ashore. I am told the auditors in Manila have been "handled." I begin to understand that this ship is not carrying cargo. This ship is carrying the end of something, and I am to be the man who loses it.*

*Oct. 23. Decision made. If the gold reaches its destination, it will be counted as legitimate and the theft will be complete — laundered into land, into sugar, into names that will sit in government offices for fifty years signing laws. If the ship sinks, the money sinks with it, unrecoverable, worthless, a story. Better a story than a fortune built on this. I have consulted no one. God forgive me. I will scuttle her in the deep channel past Isla Verde and report loss to weather. Let the sea keep their books. The sea keeps honest books.*

The last entry was dated October 24th, and it was one line long:

*We go out on the evening tide. There is no other way it can be done cleanly. To whoever finds this — the fault is not the sea's.*

Ines lowered the book onto her knees inside the air pocket and discovered that her hands were shaking.

Not fear. Something older than fear. She had spent six years believing her father had been ruined by incompetence — that he had staked his reputation on a phantom wreck, misread his surveys, chased a legend into professional oblivion, and dragged her childhood down with him. She had built her whole self out of that belief. *I will never be the kind of fool my father was.* It had been the load-bearing wall of her life.

But the Meridian Star had never been a phantom. Kenji Nakamura had found a real wreck — had found *this* — and the reason his find had vanished from the record, the reason the review board had called it worthless silt, the reason his permits had been revoked and his name struck from the registry, was that a man named Aurelio Vance had needed the wreck to stay worthless. Had needed it so badly that he'd falsified a survey, ended a career, and let a family break apart rather than let one archaeologist's discovery point at a ledger entry from 1948.

The logbook wasn't history. It was a weapon. It was the only kind of weapon that couldn't be confiscated, discredited, or bought — because it was already true, and truth was the one cargo Aurelio Vance had never managed to sink.

"Ines." Teo's voice, very quiet, in the dome of trapped air. "The date. Look at the date of the scuttling."

She looked. *October 24th.* And her father's original survey permit — she knew it by heart, had stared at the revocation notice often enough — had been issued for a survey season beginning October 26th, nineteen years ago. Two days. Kenji had found the wreck two days after it died, following the same channel, reading the same water, because the sea keeps honest books and her father had known how to read them.

He hadn't chased a legend. He'd answered a distress call sixty years late.

"We have to get this out," she said. Her voice came out flat and steady, and she was grateful to it. "Tonight. Not the fifth tide. Tonight."

"The flood—"

"I don't care about the flood." She wrapped the logbook in the spare oilcloth with hands that had stopped shaking, because purpose does that, her father used to say — *fear is just energy looking for a job.* "Vance ordered the pumps running in the outer chamber. The moment his divers reach this hold, this book goes into a crate marked 'corroded documents, pending assessment,' and pending becomes never. We've seen how he handles pending."

Teo was silent for a moment. In the dim green light filtering up through the water below them, his face looked carved out of something patient and sad. "My report," he said. "The one that buried your father. I wrote 'no artifacts of significance recovered.' I wrote it because Voss's predecessor told me to, and because I owed Corvus eleven months of wages, and because it was easier than the alternative." He looked at the oilcloth bundle in her arms. "That report gets read aloud in a courtroom someday, I want it read next to this. Next to what actually happened. You understand?"

"I understand."

They went down together into the green dark, and the passage took them back the way a throat returns what it cannot swallow, and above them the gray sky had begun, very softly, to rain.

* * *

They surfaced in the outer chamber to the sound of engines.

Not the fleet's pumps — those throbbed constant, distant, ignorable. These were boat engines, close, multiple, cutting to idle in sequence with the crisp discipline of a crew that drilled. Through the cave mouth, thirty meters of open water away, Ines could see hulls sliding past the headland: the Corvus flagship and two tenders, coming around into the lee with the afternoon light behind them.

And standing at the flagship's rail, unmistakable even at distance, silver hair immaculate despite the spray, stood Aurelio Vance.

He had come down the cliff himself. Chapter twenty of her life, arriving early.

Teo's hand closed on her arm. "Cache," he breathed. "Now. Before their divers hit the water."

They hauled out on the rocks below the cliff path, dripping, and Ines pressed the oilcloth bundle flat against her stomach beneath her jacket, feeling its edges through the wet fabric like a second heartbeat. Forty meters of exposed rock between them and the cache. Above the cache, the cliff path. On the cliff path, already descending, unhurried, flanked by two men in the gray uniforms of the magistrate's marine detail —

Vance was not here to inspect. Magistrates inspected from boats, from offices, from safe distances. Vance was here because something had made him nervous enough to leave them, and men like Aurelio Vance only got nervous about one thing: loose ends that could testify.

Ines started up the rocks, keeping her body between the bundle and the water, and climbed toward the cache and the cliff path and the man who had destroyed her father's life with a signature, carrying in her jacket the document that would unmake him — if she could get it off this coastline alive.

Behind her, the first of Voss's divers splashed into the cave mouth, and the Meridian Star's drowned cathedral filled, inch by inch, with the sound of men who had come to bury the truth a second time.
