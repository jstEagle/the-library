## 15. An Offer on the Water

Mara Voss had learned to read water the way other people read faces, and the skiff coming down the coast at first light was telling her everything she needed to know.

It was a good boat — that was the first thing. Not a fisherman's boat, not a tourist's toy. A working hull with a patched sail and an engine that had been rebuilt by someone who loved it, running low and quiet along the cliff shadow where the swell was flattest. Whoever helmed it knew the coast well enough to use the cliffs for cover from the Corvus fleet anchored off the cave mouth, and careless enough of her own safety to come alone.

Voss stood at the rail of the *Corvus Queen* with a mug of coffee going cold in her hand and watched the little boat thread the reef line south of the dig site. The dawn was doing its usual theater — gold spilling over the eastern ridge, the sea turning from iron to wine to hammered silver — and she found, as she often did, that beauty made her suspicious. Beautiful mornings on Isla Verde were usually the ones that ended badly.

"Small craft, bearing one-six-five," said Dario at the wheelhouse door. "Running dark until just now. Want us to run her off?"

"No." Voss took a last sip and set the mug down. "Run out the launch. Two men, no guns showing. And get me the file."

"The file?"

"You know which file. The one I had you pull the night we anchored here." She watched the skiff dip behind a breaker and rise again, stubborn as a thought. "The Nakamura girl."

Dario hesitated. He was a good first mate, Dario — steady, obedient, not too curious — but he'd been with her long enough to know when a hesitation would cost him more than obedience. He went below without another word.

Voss turned back to the water. Three days now since the northeaster had peeled the cliff open like a letter, three days since the *Meridian Star* had come up out of legend and into her manifest, and in those three days she had learned several things that were not on any chart.

She had learned that someone else had reached the cave before the fleet did. She had learned it from the small signs: a line anchor set with a fisherman's hitch instead of a salvage man's bowline, a cache smell of lamp oil and canvas in a hollow above the tideline, a set of boot prints in the drying silt of the outer chamber that were narrow and light and went exactly where a careful person would go and nowhere else. She had learned that whoever it was had been inside the flooded passage — the air pocket beyond the second choke still held the faint chemical ghost of a diver's exhalations, if you knew to look for it, and Mara Voss always knew to look.

And she had learned, from a name whispered over the fleet's radio traffic and confirmed in a creased newspaper clipping Dario had brought her, that the person doing all this careful, patient, trespassing work was Ines Nakamura. Daughter of Kenji Nakamura. Daughter of the man whose report — the worthless-rock report, the nothing-here report, the report that had closed this whole coastline to survey for six years — sat in the magistrate's archive with a signature on it that Voss had looked at longer than she should have.

She hadn't told Vance about the signature. That was a decision she had made quietly, the way she made most of her important decisions, and she was still deciding whether it had been loyalty or self-preservation or something older and less comfortable than either.

The launch came alongside. She stepped down into it, took the tiller herself, and told the two crewmen to sit in the stern and keep their hands visible and their mouths shut.

* * *

The skiff saw them coming — of course it did; Voss didn't bother with subtlety at this range — and for a moment she thought the woman at the tiller might run. The engine note climbed, the hull lifted, and then, deliberately, the throttle came back down and the skiff swung broadside, riding the swell, waiting. Choosing to be caught rather than chased. Voss filed that away too. Chased things did stupid things. Things that chose to be caught had already decided what the catching was worth to them.

They came together in the lee of a sea stack, the two hulls kissing fenders, and Voss got her first proper look at Ines Nakamura in the flat morning light.

The clipping had prepared her for the face — the Nakamura jaw, the eyes that were her father's exactly, dark and level and slightly amused at the world's expense. It had not prepared her for the hands. They were wreck-diver's hands, sediment-stained at every nail, rope-burned across the palm, and they rested on the tiller with the particular economy of someone who never wasted a motion because motions cost calories and calories cost dives. This was a woman who had been living close to the edge of her own strength for a long time. Voss recognized the condition. She'd spent ten years of her own life there.

"Captain Voss," the woman said. No surprise in it. "I wondered how long you'd let me work before you came down to count your losses."

"Not losses yet." Voss kept her voice even, unhurried. "Inventory. There's a difference, and it matters to my books."

"And to the magistrate's?"

The question landed precisely where it was aimed, and Voss felt it, and did not let it show. So the girl had heard the radio traffic, or read the concession postings, or simply understood what everyone on this island understood: that the Corvus Fleet moved when Aurelio Vance said move, and stopped when he said stop, and that the emergency permits pasted to the harbor board three days ago had been printed before the storm ever touched the coast. Voss had seen the dates. She doubted very much she was the only one.

"I don't speak for the magistrate," she said. "I speak for the fleet. It's a narrower job and I'm better at it."

"You're better at it," Ines agreed, and there was no mockery in it, which somehow made it worse. "That's why I'm curious what you want. You could have run me off with the launch. You could have reported me to the harbor police and had my skiff impounded and my permit applications — I don't have any permit applications — burned. Instead you're sitting here with two crewmen who've been told to keep their hands visible. That's not enforcement, Captain. That's negotiation."

Voss studied her for a long moment. The swell rocked both boats in slow agreement, gunwale to gunwale, like two animals circling toward the same carcass.

"Nakamura," she said. "Ines Nakamura. Born here, left at nineteen after the inquiry, salvaging permits off other people's failures ever since. Your father taught you tide tables before he taught you to ride a bicycle — assuming anyone taught you the bicycle. You can read sediment like a ledger. And six years ago your father signed a report saying there was nothing under these cliffs worth a shovel, and his career died of it, and so did your last name." She paused. "I've done my reading. Have I got it right?"

Something moved behind the woman's eyes — not flinching; filing. "Close enough. What of it?"

"Because I think you know what's down there." Voss nodded back toward the cliffs, where the cave mouth showed as a black seam at low water, guarded by the fleet's buoys and floodlights and the slow, patient churn of her pump barge. "Not treasure-story knowledge. Real knowledge. Survey marks. Strongboxes. A route through the inner passage that nobody could find without a chart somebody drew years ago." She let that sit. "And I think the reason you know is written in a hand you grew up watching."

The silence between the boats was filled only by water slapping hulls and, far off, the diesel drone of the pump barge. Ines Nakamura did not confirm anything. She also did not deny anything, and Voss had negotiated with enough liars to know that a silence shaped like that was worth more than a confession.

"All right," Voss said. "Here's how it stands, and then I'll make my offer, and then you can tell me I'm a thief and we'll part as professionals."

"I wouldn't call you a thief," Ines said mildly. "You have paperwork."

Voss almost smiled. Almost. "The fleet holds the concession. Six tides of exclusive rights, after which the claim vests permanently and this site belongs to Corvus Salvage on paper and to Aurelio Vance in fact. My orders are specific. Recover the ship's papers first — manifest, logbook, correspondence. Cargo second. Specie third, if there is specie, and between us, I'd be surprised if there isn't." She watched the girl's hands tighten fractionally on the tiller and noted it. "Now. A reasonable person asks why the papers matter more than the gold. Gold is heavy and fungible and loud. Papers are quiet. Papers say who did what, and when, and to whom. Papers are the only thing on this whole coast that can end a career instead of enriching one."

"And whose career are your orders aiming at?"

"There it is," Voss said quietly. "You do ask the right questions."

* * *

She had rehearsed the offer twice in the night, standing at her cabin window with the fleet's lights burning on the black water, and both rehearsals had failed, because the offer wasn't really negotiable and they both knew it, and pretending otherwise would insult a woman who clearly couldn't be insulted into stupidity.

"One cut," Voss said. "Fifteen percent of whatever cargo value comes up while you walk away clean. No record, no arrest, no questions about how you knew where to dive. Your debts — and I'm told you carry some — disappear into a number. You take your fifteen and you go back to scraping other people's discarded permits, except afterward you'll scrape them from a position of comfort instead of hunger. And in exchange, the site is ours, the story is ours, and the story stays the story the magistrate wants told: tragic wreck, sad history, no names attached, no old reports reopened, no dead careers resurrected." She held the girl's gaze. "It's a good offer, Nakamura. It's the best offer anyone on this island will make you all year, possibly all decade, and I am not in the habit of making offers twice."

Ines Nakamura was quiet for a while. Long enough that Voss checked the water around them out of habit — the tide was beginning to turn, the ebb starting to comb seaward through the reef gaps, and some animal part of her mind was already calculating drift and fuel and the hour.

Then the woman spoke, and her voice was not angry. That was the remarkable thing. Voss had expected fury — she'd built the offer expecting fury, expecting to absorb it, expecting to leave the encounter with the moral high ground ceded and the practical ground gained. Fury she could handle. Fury was a currency she traded in daily.

What she got instead was something worse.

"My father used to say the sea keeps honest books," Ines said. "Every tide, it writes down what came in and what went out, and sooner or later it balances. He believed that. He believed it right up until the day a report with his name on it told the whole world the sea had nothing to say." She looked past Voss, at the cliffs, at the black seam of the cave. "Six years I hated him for signing that report. Six years I built a life on the assumption that he'd sold out, or broken, or both, and that the only inheritance I'd ever get from Kenji Nakamura was the shape of his disgrace. And then a storm opened a hole in the coast and I found a strongbox with his survey marks on it, hidden before the report was ever signed, and I understood that he didn't sell out. He was silenced. Somebody buried him alive in paper." Her eyes came back to Voss, and they were terrible and calm. "So no, Captain. I won't take your fifteen percent. Not because I'm noble — I've been hungry enough lately that fifteen percent of that hold would buy me five years of sleep. But because you just told me your orders put the papers above the gold, and there is exactly one reason a corrupt magistrate wants a wreck's papers recovered before anyone else reads them, and it's the same reason my father's career was murdered. You aren't offering me a cut of treasure, Captain Voss. You're offering me a cut of the cover-up. And I have finally, finally figured out what side of that I'm on."

The words went into Voss like a hook going into a fish — clean, deep, and impossible to spit out without tearing something.

Because the girl was right. Voss had known she was right since the night she'd pulled the file, since the night she'd held the photocopy of the old survey report under her cabin lamp and looked at the marginal annotations in a young Aurelio Vance's handwriting — annotations dated four years before he held office — and understood that she was not employed by a salvage fleet. She was employed by an alibi. The *Meridian Star* wasn't a job. It was a wound the island had been salving with silence for sixty years, and Vance meant to close it forever, and the papers in that flooded hold were the knife that could reopen it.

She had known all of it, and she had come out here anyway to make the offer, partly because orders were orders and partly — and this was the part she did not enjoy examining — because she wanted to see the daughter of Kenji Nakamura with her own eyes and find out whether the bloodline was weak.

It was not weak.

"Your father," Voss said slowly, "tried to expose this. Years ago. Did you know that?"

Something crossed the younger woman's face — a crack in the calm, quickly mortared over. "Say that again."

"He surveyed this coast before the report. Before the falsified report. He wrote letters — I've seen references to them, in the file, in the parts of the file I probably wasn't given. He tried to go over Vance's head, and it cost him everything, and the report was the nail." Voss heard her own voice and was distantly surprised by it: low, almost gentle. She hadn't intended gentleness. "I'm telling you this because you deserve to hear it from someone who isn't lying to you, and because everyone else on this water is lying to you."

For a moment Ines Nakamura looked, for the first time, like what she actually was — a woman in her late twenties running on coffee and grief and a debt she hadn't told anyone the size of — and then the composure came back down over her like a hatch cover, and Voss respected it even as she regretted it.

"Why?" Ines asked. "Why tell me that? Why any of this? You could have sunk my skiff and gone back to your pumps."

Voss looked out at the water. The ebb was running harder now, silvering the reef tops, dragging the morning seaward. Somewhere behind them the pump barge throbbed like a bad heart.

"Because I've worked for Aurelio Vance for four years," she said, "and in four years I have watched him spend people the way other men spend coin, and I have told myself every time that I was different, that I was essential, that captains who deliver don't get spent." She turned back. "And three nights ago I read a file I wasn't supposed to read, and I started wondering what happens to a captain who has seen the inside of a vault room."

The admission hung between the boats. It was more than she'd planned to give, and she watched the girl understand that — watch her recalibrate, adversary to something adjacent to ally, the way a navigator watches a bearing shift.

"So here is what happens now," Voss said, brisk again, because feeling was a leak and leaks sank ships. "I go back and report that I intercepted you, warned you off, and found nothing but a poacher with a grudge. You go back to whatever hole you're diving out of, and you stay ahead of my divers, and you do not get caught, because if you get caught I cannot protect you and neither can anyone. And when the papers come up — and they will come up, Nakamura, one way or another, because that hold cannot stay sealed forever — you had better hope they land in hands steadier than mine." She pushed off from the skiff's gunwale, and the two hulls parted with a knock like a knuckle on a door. "Fifteen percent stays open for one more tide. After that, the offer drowns, and we're only enemies."

"We were never anything else," Ines said. But her voice had lost its edge, and they both heard it.

Voss took the tiller of the launch and turned them back toward the flagship. Halfway there she glanced astern once. The skiff was already moving, running the cliff shadow south, small and stubborn against the brightening water, and Mara Voss caught herself thinking — with something that was not quite admiration and not quite dread — that Kenji Nakamura must have been a hell of a teacher, and that Aurelio Vance had no idea what kind of storm he had actually bought a concession to.

Behind her, the ebb current carried the skiff out of sight.

By nightfall, Voss knew, the whole balance of the site would change. The girl would not walk away — that much was certain now, written in the set of her shoulders and the arithmetic of her debts. Which meant the next few tides would decide everything: who surfaced with the papers, who was standing nearby when they did, and whether Mara Voss was, when the reckoning came, holding the knife or the handle.

She gave the order to double the night watch, and stood a long time at the rail, watching the horizon where the sun had gone down, waiting for it to break.
