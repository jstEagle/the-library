## 16. Fourth Tide: The Air Goes Bad

*Ines*

The fourth tide came in wrong.

Ines felt it before she saw it — a pressure change in the cave's throat, a low hum in the rock like a struck tuning fork that wouldn't die. She was on the ledge above the flooded passage, coiling line with hands gone stiff from three days of salt work, when the sound arrived: not water this time, but stone. A deep, grinding exhalation from somewhere inside the cliff, as if the island had been holding its breath since the storm and finally let it go.

"Teo." She said it quietly first, then louder, into the dark of the passage mouth where his dive light had vanished eleven minutes ago. "Teo, get out. Now."

The passage swallowed her voice and gave nothing back.

She checked her watch against the tide table taped to her forearm — her father's tide table, copied out in his small, precise hand, the one thing of his she'd carried for six years without admitting it. Slack water was ending. The inner chamber would begin to breathe again, current pushing through the siphon like blood through a valve. Teo had eleven minutes of margin. He'd been gone twelve.

Then the rock spoke a second time, and this time it wasn't a hum. It was a crack — sharp, wet, final — followed by a sound like a mountain clearing its throat, and the whole cave shuddered around her. Dust boiled out of the passage mouth in a grey plume. Pebbles skittered down the ledge and dropped into black water. Somewhere below, deep in the flooded throat of the Meridian Star's last refuge, something very large had come loose.

The air pocket. She understood it all at once, the way you understand a wound is yours only when you see your own blood. The trapped pocket of air above the inner chamber — the thing keeping Teo alive between dives, the thing they'd been using as a waystation — had shifted. Or been buried. Or ceased to exist.

"TEO!"

Her voice cracked off the walls and came back to her sounding smaller than she was.

She looked at the water. It lay flat and opaque as poured lead, and beneath it ran forty meters of flooded passage — narrow, blind, threaded with old cargo netting and now, presumably, fresh rubble — leading to a chamber that might no longer have any air in it at all. Every rule of diving she'd learned secondhand from Teo said what she was about to consider was suicide. Every rule of diving he'd taught her also assumed there would be someone topside running the lines, someone to haul you home when your nerve failed.

There was no one else. There had never been anyone else. That was the whole architecture of her life.

Ines pulled the spare regulator from the cache kit, checked the bottle — two-thirds full, enough if she didn't waste breath on panic — and cinched the weight belt over her hips with hands that were, she noted distantly, perfectly steady. Her body had decided before her mind caught up. That was her father's inheritance too, though she'd never thanked him for it: the reflex of competence, the muscle memory of someone raised on expeditions where hesitation cost more than fear.

"If you're dead," she muttered to the black water, "I'm going to be so angry with you."

She spat in her mask, rinsed it, and went under.

* * *

*Teo*

Teo had known he was going to die twice before in his life. Once off Saba, tangled in a trawl net at thirty meters, and once in a Corvus crew bar in Puerto Cristóbal when Renn put a bottle in a man's hand and pointed him at Teo's head. Both times, death had turned out to be negotiable. This time felt less like negotiation and more like a signed contract.

He'd heard the collapse coming — a groan in the rock, the kind of sound divers learn to hear through their bones rather than their ears — and he'd been moving toward the exit when the ceiling behind him let go. Not the whole ceiling. Just enough. A slab the size of a fishing dory came down across the passage behind him with a concussion he felt in his chest, and the water went from clear to chocolate in half a second, and his dive light showed him a wall of rubble where the way back had been.

He'd felt, absurdly, almost relieved. At least it was quick. Then he'd found the second problem.

His regulator was breathing wrong. He'd noticed it on the swim in — a faint rasp on the inhale, like drawing breath through gritted teeth — and told himself it was sediment in the first stage, nothing, he'd service it tonight. But sediment didn't get worse by the minute. Something had scored the diaphragm or cracked the housing, and now each breath came harder than the last, the regulator delivering less air and demanding more effort, a machine dying by degrees exactly parallel to himself.

He found the air pocket by memory more than light. Or he found where it used to be. His head broke surface into a space the size of a coffin, and the ceiling here was lower than yesterday — the collapse had compressed everything, squeezed the chamber like a fist around a sponge — and the air tasted of stone dust and old salt and something faintly organic, the breath of a drowned ship. But it was air. He pulled his mask up onto his forehead and hung on the rocks and breathed it in great greedy swallows, and laughed, actually laughed, a thin crazy sound in the dark.

Then he did the arithmetic, because arithmetic was the only prayer he'd ever trusted.

Bottle: maybe twenty minutes at rest, ten if he worked. Exit route: buried. Alternate route: none that he'd mapped; the siphon on the far side of the chamber ran two hundred meters to open water and even Mara Voss's best divers called it a coffin with a view. Surface interval until the next slack: four hours. Air pocket volume: shrinking, if the rock kept settling, which rock tended to do after it started.

He thought about Ines up top. He could picture her exactly — crouched on the ledge, watch face lit, lips moving as she counted his margin down. He'd watched her do it every dive, this woman who claimed she'd never worked with a diver before and who ran lines like she'd been born in a harness. She'd wait until the numbers told her he was dead. She was disciplined that way. It was one of the things he liked about her, and right now it was the thing most likely to kill him, because a disciplined woman does not swim into a collapsed passage on a hunch.

"Hunch," he said aloud, to the dark. "Come on, Nakamura. Be undisciplined just once."

His regulator hissed when he tested it. The rasp was worse. He put it back in his mouth anyway, because the alternative was learning how long a coffin-sized pocket of bad air could keep a man thinking clearly, and he already suspected the answer was not long enough.

He began, methodically, to move rock.

* * *

*Ines*

The passage tried to kill her in the first eight meters.

A sheet of cargo netting — two hundred years old and still doing its job with malicious efficiency — wrapped her left arm as she finned through the murk, and for one white-hot moment she was fighting it instead of swimming, her heart slamming, bubbles tearing from her reg in silver ropes. She forced herself to stop. Her father's voice, unbidden, in the flat tone he used for lessons: *Panic spends air faster than work ever will. Fear is a budget, mija. Spend it wisely.* She drew the knife from her calf sheath and cut herself free, stroke by stroke, and the net drifted away into the dark like the ghost of whatever cargo it had once held.

Visibility was an arm's length and lying about it. The collapse had filled the passage with suspended silt, and her light made a brown halo that seemed to shrink as she watched it. She followed the guideline they'd run on the second tide — Teo's line, tied off every five meters, his knots neat as everything else about him — and prayed to no one in particular that the line still went through.

At fifteen meters she found the rubble.

It came down from the ceiling in a fan of broken slabs, and the guideline disappeared beneath it. Her light probed the pile: gaps between the blocks, some big enough to see darkness through, none obviously passable. Above the pile, the ceiling was fractured, hanging, waiting. Below it, the floor of the passage sloped away into deeper black.

She could go over, under, or through. Over meant squeezing along the fractured ceiling where the next breath of settling rock would make her part of the pile. Under meant a gap barely wider than her shoulders, floored with jagged spall, in zero visibility, alone.

*Fear is a budget.*

She went under.

It took everything she had. The gap closed around her like a throat — rock scraping her tank, her mask pressed sideways against stone, the regulator rattling its dying breath in her ears — and halfway through, wedged, unable to advance or retreat, she felt the full animal truth of her situation arrive in her body at once: she was alone in the dark inside a mountain, underwater, stuck, and no one in the world knew precisely where.

She let herself feel it for exactly three seconds. Then she exhaled, slow and complete, flattened her chest as much as her ribs allowed, and moved forward the way a snake moves — not strength but commitment, one vertebra at a time. Her tank caught. She twisted. Caught again. She reached up, hooked her fingers over a lip of rock, and dragged herself through by her arms while her hips screamed, and then suddenly the passage opened and she was through, floating in brown water, alive, breathing, so flooded with relief that it took her a moment to notice the sound.

A scraping. Rhythmic. Ahead and above, where the air pocket should be.

She rose toward it, and her head broke surface into a pocket of air the size of a confessional booth, and Teo Brandt was sitting waist-deep against the far wall with a slab of rock across his legs and a look on his face that she would remember for the rest of her life: not fear, not gratitude, but a kind of stunned, embarrassed wonder, like a man who has just been informed he is loved and doesn't know where to put it.

"You," he said. His voice was wrecked with dust. "You absolute — I told you the plan was I dive and you run lines—"

"The plan changed." She got an arm over the rock shelf beside him, coughing silt. "Your regulator sounds like a dying accordion. I could hear it from the passage."

"That's not possible."

"It's hyperbole, Teo. Keep up." She was already moving, assessing — the slab across his thighs, the angle of it, the way his left leg was pinned at the knee. "Can you feel your foot?"

"Wiggling. Hurts like sin, but wiggling." He grimaced as she tested the slab with both hands. "It's the pinning that's the problem. Rock's seated on a ridge underneath. You can't lift it straight, you have to roll it, and rolling it means—" He stopped.

"Means my hands stay where your leg is," she finished. "Yes. I did the math on the way in."

"There wasn't time for math on the way in."

"I'm very fast." She set her shoulders against the slab. "On three. And Teo — when it rolls, don't pull. Let me take the weight. If you pull, we both lose."

He looked at her for a long moment, and something passed between them that neither of them would ever quite name afterward — the specific trust of two people who have decided, independently and for their own reasons, to bet their lives on each other's hands.

"On three," he said.

* * *

*Teo*

Later, trying to reconstruct it, Teo could only remember fragments. Her count — one, two — and the slab groaning, pivoting on its ridge with a sound like a door in hell. White pain going up his leg into his hip. Her hands, small and salt-cracked and unbelievably strong, sliding under the edge as it rolled, taking the weight he'd been sure would crush her, her whole body becoming a lever, a fulcrum, a promise kept. The moment his knee came free and he scrambled backward on his elbows through water and rubble while she shoved the slab away and let it drop with a boom that shook dust from the ceiling of the world.

They surfaced in the pocket together, gasping, and for a moment neither of them said anything, because there was nothing to say that wasn't either too large or too small.

"Your regulator," she said finally, when she'd gotten her breath back. "Give it here."

He handed it over and watched her test it, watching her face in the glow of his light as she listened to the rattle of the failing diaphragm. She frowned, unscrewed the hose at the first stage, blew through it, tapped the housing against her palm the way he'd shown her during gear checks on the first night — *sediment settles, tap it free, always tap before you trust* — and handed it back.

"Better?"

He breathed. The rasp was still there, but thinner, survivable. Enough.

"Enough to get out?" she asked.

"Not through the way you came. Not with this leg." He flexed the knee experimentally and his vision whited at the edges. "But the siphon's out too. So that leaves—" He stopped, because saying it made it real.

"The far side," Ines said. "Two hundred meters of siphon that you told me even Voss's divers won't run."

"I told you that when I was planning to impress you with caution." He was already pulling himself upright along the wall, testing weight on the leg, finding it would hold if he hated every step. "It runs shallow in the middle third. There are three air bells — I sounded them on the survey dive, marked them on the slate in the cache. We leapfrog it. You go first, find bell one, breathe, signal me through. I follow. Repeat until we're out past the point."

"And if one of the bells is gone? Collapsed, flooded, shifted like this one did?"

He met her eyes. In the dive light her face was pale, streaked with silt, absolutely level.

"Then we'll know," he said, "at the same time."

She almost smiled. "You're a terrible optimist, Brandt."

"I'm a terrible everything. It's why I owe everyone." He checked his gauge, then hers, and did the arithmetic out loud, because she deserved to hear it: "Combined air, if we share at the bells and sprint the deep sections — it's enough. Barely. It's enough if we don't talk down there and we don't think down there and we just move."

"Then stop talking," Ines said, and pulled her mask down, and stepped off the shelf into the black.

* * *

*Ines*

The siphon was the closest thing to being unborn that Ines ever hoped to experience. No light but her own, no bottom, no top, no sound but her heartbeat and the wet mechanical respiration of the borrowed regulator and, somewhere behind her, the slower rhythm of Teo's breathing as he followed her fins by touch and faith. The passage narrowed, widened, narrowed again. Twice she passed through chambers where her light simply failed to find any wall at all, and she swam through open blackness on a heading she'd memorized from his slate — bearing two-sixty, depth six meters, count forty strokes — trusting a dead man's survey marks in a living, shifting mountain.

The first air bell was there. She surfaced into a dome the size of a chapel, stalactites hanging like the pipes of some drowned organ, and stood chest-deep on a shelf of rubble and pulled her mask off and breathed real air and wanted, wildly, to laugh or weep or both. Thirty seconds later Teo's head broke the surface beside her, and they stood together in the dark cathedral, two drowned souls sharing the last dry room in a sinking world, and he said nothing, and she said nothing, and it was the finest conversation she'd had in years.

The second bell nearly killed them. It was there on the slate and gone in fact — half-flooded, its ceiling dipped below the surface, a brown mirror with no air behind it. They hung in the water above it while Teo's gauge needle trembled near reserve, and Ines watched him weigh the options with the terrible calm of a man spending his last coin, and then he pointed onward, deeper, and she went.

The third bell saved them. It was small — barely room for two heads — and the air in it was foul, tasting of sulfur and ancient rot, but it was air, and they shared it in turns, masks pushed up, foreheads nearly touching in the dark, passing the single functioning regulator back and forth like a sacrament while their bodies shook with cold and adrenaline.

"When we're out," Teo whispered, his voice a ruin, "I'm telling you something. About Corvus. About the first dive. About your father."

Ines went very still. The foul air sat between them.

"Not now," she said.

"No. Now. While I'm still scared enough to be honest." His eyes in the dive light were wet, and not from the water. "I was the diver, Ines. The one who reported the site worthless. The report that ended him — my name's in the chain of it somewhere, small print, but it's mine."

The words landed in her like stones dropped down a well. She waited for the anger — she had a lifetime's supply ready, forged and sharpened — and found instead something stranger: recognition. This man in the dark, offering her his worst self like an open wound, because honesty was the only currency he had left.

"We're not out yet," she said softly. "So you don't get to confess yet. You get to swim. Confession's for people who survive."

And Teo Brandt — disgraced, indebted, half-drowned — began, hoarsely, to laugh, and the laugh echoed up through the little bell of bad air like a bell in a church, and Ines found herself laughing too, helplessly, the two of them shaking together in the dark while the sea outside turned toward the fifth tide and Magistrate Vance's demolition crews, miles up the coast, unpacked their crates of explosives in the rain.

They went under one last time, sharing air, and the passage ahead of them — she could see it now, a grey-green shimmer, open water, the world — grew brighter with every stroke, until they broke the surface together beyond the point, in the cold honest swell of the open sea, under a sky going bruise-dark with evening, and Ines turned toward shore and saw, on the clifftop above the cave, lanterns moving. Too many lanterns. And among them, unmistakable even at this distance, the glint of a magistrate's official sash, gold in the lamplight.

"He's early," she breathed. "Teo — he's come down the cliff himself."
