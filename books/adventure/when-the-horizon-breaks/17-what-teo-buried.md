## 17. What Teo Buried

The fever broke on the second night, and Teo Brandt woke to the sound of the sea doing what it always did — keeping its own time, indifferent to whether he lived to hear it.

He was in Ines Nakamura's camp, in her tent, under a blanket that smelled of woodsmoke and salt and something faintly medicinal — kelp poultice, maybe, or the liniment island women had been boiling for generations before anyone thought to call it medicine. His left shoulder was strapped tight against his ribs. His ribs themselves were strapped tighter. Every breath arrived with an invoice attached, and he paid it, because the alternative was not breathing at all.

The collapse had come down on him like a fist wearing rings. He remembered the crack of stone, the air pocket shivering into chaos, his regulator hose pinched under a slab the size of a church pew, and then — through the gray murk — Ines coming for him. Coming *through* the flooded passage alone, no buddy line, no backup, running the route he'd mapped for her only in words and gestures because there hadn't been time to show her anything else. She'd found him by touch in water so thick with silt it might have been soup, jammed her spare regulator between his teeth, and hauled him out along the guide line hand over hand while the cave groaned around them like something waking up angry.

He owed her his life. That was the arithmetic of it, clean and simple.

The problem — the thing that had been sitting on his chest heavier than the rock since he'd surfaced coughing blood into the shallows — was that he'd known, going into that passage, exactly how much he deserved to be crushed under it.

* * *

She came in at midmorning with two tin mugs and the particular walk of a woman who had been awake since four. The storm light through the canvas made her look carved out of something harder than flesh. She set one mug by his good hand without asking if he wanted it. It was coffee, boiled down to a consistency approaching tar, and it was the best thing he had tasted in a year.

"You should still be flat on your back," she said.

"I've been flat on my back for two days. I know how the story ends."

"It ends with your lung scarring over if you're stupid." She sat on the crate across from him, elbows on knees, and studied him with those eyes that read sediment and people by the same method — patiently, layer by layer, until they got to whatever was buried deepest. "Voss's divers are back in the outer chamber. Pumping. We lost the third tide and most of yesterday. I'm telling you because you'd hate it worse if I lied to make you rest."

"I would."

"So don't make me lie." She drank. Outside, a gull said something rude and repeated it. "Teo."

He knew that tone. He'd heard it from captains, from creditors, from his mother, God rest her, in the last year of her life when she'd stopped pretending she didn't know where his money went. It was the tone people used when they already knew the answer and were giving you one last chance to say it yourself.

"Ines." He turned the mug in his fingers. The tin was dented; everything she owned was dented, and everything she owned worked anyway. "How much do you remember about six years ago? About your father's last survey?"

Her face did something small and controlled, like a door closing one inch. "I remember the report. Everyone remembers the report. *Site assessed: negligible cultural or salvage value. No further investigation recommended.* Eleven words that ended a thirty-year career."

"Twelve," Teo said. "You forgot *the.*"

"Don't."

"He wrote it himself, Ines. The diver who ran the confirmation survey. The one Vance's office hired to go down after your father flagged the site, take a look, and file the finding that let them bury it." Teo set the mug down because his hand had started to shake and he didn't want to watch the coffee tremble. "That diver was me."

* * *

The silence afterward lasted long enough for him to count his own heartbeats, which was a mistake, because his heart was going fast enough to make the counting feel like an accusation.

Ines didn't move. That was the worst part. If she'd thrown the coffee in his face, if she'd called him a liar or worse, if she'd walked out of the tent and left him alone with the gulls — any of it would have been easier than the way she simply went still, the way divers go still when they hear something moving in the dark below them and need to know what it is before they spend their next breath.

"Say it again," she said quietly. "All of it. From the beginning."

So he did, because a wound that's been opened has no sense but to drain.

He'd been twenty-six. Younger than she was now, though he couldn't have told you that at the time; at twenty-six he'd felt like the oldest man alive, carrying debts his father had signed and his brother had multiplied. Corvus Fleet had picked him up out of a bad season the way gulls pick up scraps — Mara Voss herself had signed him on, back when she still shook hands with new hires, and he'd repaid her by being the best working diver on her roster inside eighteen months. Fast, calm, cheap to insure. He could hold his breath longer than men who liked him, and more importantly than men who didn't.

"The call came through Voss's office," he said. "A private survey contract. One dive, one report, top rates, discretion clause. Your father had filed a preliminary flag on a submerged feature off the north cliffs — acoustic anomaly, timber density consistent with period hull construction. Big deal, on paper. Corvus wanted a working diver to confirm or deny before it went anywhere official."

"And you knew whose survey it was."

"I knew the name Nakamura. Everybody on this coast knew the name Nakamura." He laughed, and it hurt, and he let it hurt. "What I didn't put together — what I told myself I didn't put together — was why a magistrate's office was brokering a private confirmation dive on an archaeological find. I was twenty-six and the fee was eight months of my brother's debts. You can build a cathedral out of reasons like that. I built a whole chapel."

He'd gone down on a gray morning with a two-man support crew who'd been paid not to ask questions. The site was deeper than the cave mouth the storm had opened — the old entrance, collapsed even then, accessible only through a fissure that took nerve and forty feet of line. And inside—

Inside, he said, and had to stop, because the memory arrived whole and undimmed, the way the important ones do.

Cargo crates. Rows of them, stacked with colonial-era care in the flooded hold of a ship that had gone down intact, keel straight, masts folded neatly against the seabed like a man folding his hands. He'd run his glove along stenciled lettering he could still draw from memory: MERIDIAN STAR, PORT ROYAL REGISTRY. He'd found the strongbox wedged in the captain's cabin, lead-lined, lock fused shut by two centuries of salt. He'd understood, hanging there in the green silence with his light playing over wealth beyond anything Isla Verde had ever admitted to owning, that he was looking at the answer to the oldest question on the island — *where did the Meridian Star go?* — and that the question had stayed open for two hundred years because somebody very powerful needed it to.

"And you reported it worthless," Ines said. Not a question. A nail, driven home.

"I reported it worthless." His voice came out flat as a chart. "I came up, dried off, and wrote twelve words on a government form. *Negligible cultural or salvage value. No further investigation recommended.* I signed it. I took the fee. I went back to Corvus and never dove that fissure again, and neither did anyone else, because my report closed it. Officially, the Meridian Star is still a legend. Unofficially—" he gestured at the tent wall, beyond which lay the cliffs, the cave, the fleet pumping out her father's discovery, "—it's a hole in the ground that Aurelio Vance has spent six years making sure stays a legend."

* * *

"Why?" She said it without heat, which was somehow more terrible than shouting. "Not the money. I understand the money; money explains itself. Why did you stay quiet after? Six years, Teo. My father lost everything. His position, his name, his marriage — my mother didn't leave him because of the disgrace, she left him because he wouldn't stop talking about a wreck everyone had official paper saying didn't exist. He became a joke. He became a *madman*. And every year on the anniversary of the report he'd get drunk and say somebody falsified the survey, somebody was down there and lied, and nobody believed him because the liar's signature was clean and certified." Her hands had closed around the empty mug until the tin creaked. "Why?"

And here was the bottom of it, the place he'd been diving toward for six years without ever quite reaching the wreck.

"Because I told myself it didn't matter," he said. "That's the truth, Ines, and it's uglier than a bribe. A bribe would at least mean someone forced me. Nobody forced me. I looked at that hold full of history and I saw a payday for other people and nothing in it for me, and I decided a dead ship's cargo wasn't worth crossing men like Vance. I told myself the past keeps. It'll keep. Nothing down there is drowning." He shook his head slowly. "But your father was up here. Drowning on dry land, in front of everybody, for six years. And I knew. I was the one man on this coast who knew he was right, and I let him drown because admitting he was right meant admitting what I'd done, and I have never in my life paid a debt on time."

The mug creaked again. Then, deliberately, Ines set it down on the deck boards, and unclenched her hands one finger at a time, the way he'd seen her do at the edge of the cave when the tide was wrong and waiting was the only move.

"My father taught me that the sea keeps its own books," she said finally. "Every entry gets settled eventually. He used to say it like it was a comfort." She looked at him, and her eyes were wet and furious and, beneath both, unbearably tired. "It isn't a comfort, Teo. It's just true."

"I know."

"Do you want absolution? Because I can't give it. I'm not the one it belongs to."

"No." The word came out of him with surprising steadiness, the way things do once they've stopped being held down. "Absolution's another debt. I'm done taking those on. I just needed you to know before we go back in there — before you trust me in that passage again — that the last man who dove it sold you out. You deserve to dive with your eyes open."

For a long moment she said nothing. Outside, the surf worked the cliff base in its patient, bookkeeping way. Then she stood, collected both mugs, and paused at the tent flap with her back to him.

"The fourth tide's tomorrow morning," she said. "There's a logbook in that wreck somewhere — captains keep logs, and logs record orders, and orders record *who gave them*. If your report was the shovel, the log is the hand on it." She glanced back, and something in her expression shifted — not forgiveness, nothing so generous, but a kind of grim companionship, two people standing in the same trench. "You're the only one who knows that fissure well enough to run it fast. So you'll dive. And when we find what names Vance, you'll sign your testimony to it — real signature, witnessed — the same way you signed the report. Full circle, Teo. The sea keeps its books, and so will we."

She held the flap open. Gray daylight fell across him like cold water.

"One more thing," she said. "Renn. Your old crewmate, the one who's been bringing supplies and asking a lot of friendly questions about our cache. He worked that confirmation dive too, didn't he? Six years ago. Support crew."

The floor of Teo's stomach dropped away like a weighted line cut loose.

"Yes," he said. "He did."

Ines nodded once, slow, as if a suspicion had finally finished paying itself out. "Then we have a problem," she said, "and about half a day to solve it."
