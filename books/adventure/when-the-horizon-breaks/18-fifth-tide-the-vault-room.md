## 18. Fifth Tide: The Vault Room

The fifth tide came in gray and mean, a low ceiling of cloud pressing down on the water like a lid on a pot. Ines felt it in her wrists before she saw it in the gauge — that heavy, dragging pull the old fishermen called *la mano del mar*, the sea's hand closing around whatever it wanted to keep.

She stood at the lip of the flooded passage with Teo's dive slate in her hand and the manifest copies — the real ones, the ones that mattered — sealed in oilcloth against her ribs. Three tides they had worked this cave now, three tides of hauling crates through black water while Corvus pumps throbbed in the outer chamber like a second heartbeat they could never quite stop hearing. Three tides of hiding their finds in the cliff cache above the high-water line, of diving on borrowed air, of speaking in hand signals because sound carried too well down here.

And still the cargo hold kept giving them cargo, and nothing else.

"Another crate," Teo's voice crackled through the comm line, tinny and patient. "Rope, pitch, salt pork by the smell of it. Same as the last six."

Ines crouched at the waterline, running her thumb along the survey marks her father had chiseled into the rock forty years ago — or rather, along the copy of them she'd found stamped into the strongbox, and which now lived in her memory like a scar. *K.N., 19—*, followed by the small crossed-tide symbol he'd invented himself, the one no textbook used, the one he'd taught her to draw in the sand when she was seven years old.

He had been here. He had stood where she was standing, or close enough, and he had known what lay beyond the cargo hold, and he had told someone, and that someone had fed him to the magistrate and let his career die on the beach like a gutted fish.

But the hold itself had yielded only freight. No papers. No proof of the falsified survey. The manifest was damning — cargo lists that contradicted every official record, including the report that had ended Kenji Nakamura's life as an archaeologist — but a manifest alone was an accusation without a face. Vance could deny it. Vance would deny it, from behind his magistrate's seal and his brother-in-law's fleet, and half the island would nod along because it was easier than believing their own history was rotten.

"There has to be more," she said into the comm. "A ship scuttled to hide money doesn't just carry the money. It carries the *reason*. The orders. Something signed."

A pause. Bubbles rose past her boots in silver strings.

"You're thinking captain's quarters," Teo said.

"I'm thinking captains don't scuttle their own ships for free."

* * *

They went in together on the fifth tide, which was a risk Ines would have refused on any other day. Two divers in the flooded passage meant two regulators drawing on the same margin of error, and the passage had already tried to kill Teo once. But the ebb window was short — ninety minutes, maybe less, with the spring tide building toward its sixth-day peak — and the section of hold aft of the main cargo deck had never been mapped. Her father's old survey notes, transcribed in her own careful hand onto waterproof paper, ended at a penciled question mark exactly where the corridor narrowed.

The water inside the wreck was the color of strong tea, visibility measured in arm-lengths, lit only by the lamps they carried. Ines swam last, following the guide line Teo paid out, watching the walls of the Meridian Star close around her like the ribs of something enormous and long dead. Copper sheathing gone green. Timber furred with white bacterial mats. A rat's nest of cable and chain draped across the corridor mouth, and beyond it —

Beyond it, the ship changed character.

The forward sections had been working space: holds, decks, the honest grime of a merchant vessel. This aft section was different. Paneling, rotted but recognizable. The ghost of wallpaper clinging to a bulkhead in pale strips. A door hanging off one hinge, and through the door, a room.

Teo's lamp swept it first. She saw the shapes resolve out of the murk one at a time: a desk bolted to the floor, drawers burst open decades ago. A bunk frame. A shelf of books fused into a single brick by corrosion. And set into the aft bulkhead, half-hidden behind a collapsed section of overhead plating, a door of a different kind entirely — iron, riveted, small as a coffin lid, with a lock mechanism that even after a century underwater still looked like it meant business.

"Vault," Teo said simply. His voice over the comm had gone quiet in the way it did when his hands were busy and his mind was racing. "Ship's captain, carrying contraband funds, scuttling on purpose. You build yourself a strongroom. You put your real cargo where the salvage crews won't think to look — aft, behind the officers' country, not down in the holds."

"He knew they'd come looking eventually," Ines said. "Whoever scuttled her. He knew the sea gives things back."

"Your father teach you that?"

Her jaw tightened inside the regulator. "My father taught me everything except how to forgive him. Let's open the door."

* * *

The lock defeated them for forty minutes.

Not through force — there was no force left to apply; the mechanism was fused into a single mass of corrosion and concretion — but through patience. Teo worked it with picks and a small pry bar and a diver's knife, muttering to himself in three languages, while Ines held the lamp steady and watched the air gauge and thought about her father's hands.

That was the thing about memory. It didn't give you whole scenes; it gave you fragments, and you built the rest yourself, and sometimes the rest you built was kinder than the truth. She remembered Kenji Nakamura's hands turning a rusted lock on a site box outside Cebu, when she was nine, showing her how corrosion was just time made visible, how every mechanism had a logic and every logic could be learned. She remembered being bored. She remembered wanting to go swimming instead.

She would have given a year of her life, right then, at the bottom of the sea, for one more hour of being bored in her father's classroom.

"It's giving," Teo said. "Hinge side's the weak point. On three — pull, don't push, the door swings out."

The door came away with a groan that traveled up through the water and into Ines's chest. Beyond it, a space barely larger than a phone booth, dry until the scuttling, flooded ever since — and packed.

Not with gold. Not yet. That realization hit her a half-second before relief did, and she noted both, filed both, because the other thing was more important.

Papers. Dozens of them. Ledgers bound in oilskin, wrapped in waxed canvas, stacked with a deliberateness that spoke of a man who had expected the ship to die and wanted certain things to survive it. And beneath the ledgers, bundled separately, tied with cord that had rotted to lace, a packet of letters.

Teo gathered them into the mesh bag with the reverence of a man handling relics, and Ines watched the bag fill and felt something in her chest she couldn't name — not triumph, not yet, something rawer, the feeling of a hand reaching out of the past and taking hers.

"Bag's full," Teo said. "We go up now. Air's at a third."

They went up.

* * *

They surfaced in the inner air pocket as the tide turned, and Ines got the ledgers out of the bag with shaking hands and laid them on the flat rock that served as their staging shelf, and opened the first one under the lamp.

The handwriting was precise, clerkish, beautiful in the way official handwriting is beautiful — each figure ruled and confident, each entry dated. The ledger recorded disbursements. Colonial administration funds, routed through the Meridian Star's voyages, entered as shipping costs and exited as — she turned pages faster — land purchases. Sugar estates. A bank charter. A harbor improvement scheme whose contractor's name she recognized from the plaque on the customs house.

Names ran down the margins in a second hand, annotations added later, ink darker: initials, sums, and beside some entries, small checkmarks that looked almost gentle and were the coldest thing Ines had ever seen.

"Teo." Her voice came out wrong, scraped thin. "Look at the marginalia."

He leaned in, dripping, and read, and she watched his face do what hers had done — move from interest to recognition to something like nausea.

"That's the Aldana family mark," he said. "And that — I know that initial. Everyone on this island knows that initial. V."

Vance. Not Magistrate Vance. Not the public servant with the seals and the speeches. Some earlier Vance, younger, hungrier, signing his share of stolen colonial money into existence a generation before he took office — or engineering his way into office precisely so the money would be safe forever.

"The report," Ines said. "The survey report that ended my father. Find it. There has to be a draft, correspondence, something—"

Teo lifted the bundle of letters free of the rock, and the cord gave way in his fingers, and the topmost page fell open under the lamp, and Ines stopped breathing.

She knew the handwriting.

Of course she knew it. She had grown up inside it — in the margins of her schoolbooks, in the tide tables taped to the kitchen wall, in the birthday cards that arrived late and over-explained. The narrow, upright script, the crossed-tide symbol used as a personal flourish, the habit of underlining twice when he meant something absolutely.

It was her father's hand. Writing to someone whose name had been cut away by water damage, pleading.

*I have completed the survey of the exposed cave system at Isla Verde and can confirm the identity of the wreck as the Meridian Star, lost 18—. More importantly, I have recovered documentary material from the captain's strongroom which establishes, beyond any argument, that the vessel was deliberately sunk and that the funds she carried were diverted from the colonial treasury by persons who remain prominent in the administration of this territory. I am prepared to present these findings formally. I ask only that you stand with me when I do, as agreed.*

The next letter was shorter.

*You were not at the meeting. I am told the presentation has been rescheduled indefinitely. I am told also that my permit has been revoked on grounds of procedural irregularity, though no irregularity has been described to me. If you have been pressured, tell me, and we will go to the federal authorities together. I will not believe you have betrayed me until you say so to my face.*

The next letter was not a letter at all. It was a single sheet, unsigned, in a hand she didn't know — clipped, bureaucratic, final:

*Dr. Nakamura — Your findings have been reviewed by this office and determined to be without scholarly merit. The wreck in question is consistent with the known record of the coastal trader San Ildefonso, and your claims regarding its identity are hereby rejected. Further work at the site is unauthorized. Repeated applications will be considered evidence of professional misconduct.*

Beneath it, in her father's hand, one line, pressed so hard the pen had torn the paper:

*They have erased the ship. They will erase me next. If anyone reads this — the truth is in the water. K.N.*

* * *

For a long moment neither of them moved. Water dripped somewhere in the dark. The Corvus pumps thudded faintly through the rock, keeping their stupid, faithful rhythm.

"He wasn't killed," Ines said finally, and heard her own voice as if it belonged to someone standing nearby. "All these years I thought — everyone said he'd gotten too close to something dangerous, that men like that disappear, that the sea took him or worse. And he wasn't killed. He was *filed away*. Stamped *rejected*. Sentenced to a lifetime of small museums and rented rooms and a daughter who—" 

Her throat closed.

"—and a daughter who believed the erasure," she finished. "Because it was cleaner than the truth. Because it was easier to hate him for abandoning us than to understand that they took everything else first."

Teo said nothing for a while. When he spoke, his voice was rough. "I reported his find as worthless." He was looking at the torn paper, not at her. "Twenty years ago. I was the Corvus diver they sent down after your father was pulled off the permit. I swam this same passage. I saw the timbers, the stamping. And I wrote in my log that the site was a debris field of no archaeological value, because Voss's predecessor told me to write that, and because I owed Corvus eleven months of wages and a debt I couldn't swim away from." He exhaled. "I've been waiting since the cache to tell you that. Now seemed like the time."

Ines looked at him. She should have felt rage — some part of her noted distantly that rage was probably the correct response — but what she felt instead was a terrible, clarifying symmetry. A man silenced by paperwork. A man complicit in the silencing. Both of them here now, at the bottom of the lie, holding the pages that undid it.

"Then you're the second person to betray my father who's going to help un-betray him," she said. "The first was whoever those letters were written to. At least you showed up."

Something moved through Teo's face — not forgiveness, not yet, but the loosening of a knot he'd carried so long he'd forgotten it was a knot.

"They didn't kill him," he said slowly, "because killing him would have made noise. A dead archaeologist is a story. A failed archaeologist is a footnote. Vance understood that. God help me, you have to admire the efficiency."

"Don't admire it," Ines said. "Count it. Every name in these ledgers, every checkmark, every initial — count them all. We have five hours before the sixth tide, and after the sixth tide this cave seals for a generation. Whatever we take out of here, we take out *now*, and we take all of it."

She reached back into the vault's dark interior, sweeping the lamp beam along the iron shelves, cataloguing. Ledgers. Letters. The unsigned rejection. And at the rear of the shelf, wrapped in its own oilskin, heavier than paper had any right to be, a shape she had deliberately not looked at directly since the door came open.

Small canvas sacks. Dozens of them. The weight of them singing through the rock and the water and her own tired bones.

Gold. The embezzled specie itself, sitting in the dark where it had waited out a century, enough to erase every debt she carried, enough to buy back the family land, enough to make the word *Nakamura* mean success instead of scandal — all of it within arm's reach, all of it hers if she could carry it, and carrying it would slow her down.

Behind her, Teo's lamp swung toward the sacks too. She heard him weigh the same arithmetic.

"Ines," he said quietly. "How much can you carry?"

She didn't answer. Outside, far off through the flooded passage, she heard a new sound threading under the pump-throb: engines. Not the fleet's familiar idling pattern — something smaller, faster, coming down the coast toward the cliff.

And above them, faint but unmistakable, the first distant crack of demolition charges being tested.
