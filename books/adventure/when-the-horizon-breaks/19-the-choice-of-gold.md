## 19. The Choice of Gold

The vault room breathed cold.

Ines felt it before she understood it — a draft of older air seeping through the gap behind the cargo hold, carrying the mineral smell of stone that hadn't met daylight in a century and a half. She stood with her lamp raised, water to her thighs, and let the beam travel the walls of a chamber no larger than a chapel: iron strapping gone to lace, a captain's desk fused to the deck by coral, and along the far bulkhead, a strongroom door hanging open on hinges that had surrendered sometime before she was born.

The ledgers lay where Teo had stacked them, wrapped in oilcloth, already counted twice. Eleven volumes. The names of every man who had turned stolen colonial funds into family dynasties, written in a clerk's patient hand, annotated in a magistrate's arrogant one. Proof. Her father's vindication, sitting in a flooded room at the bottom of a cave that would swallow itself within two tides.

She should have been thinking about the ledgers. She was thinking about the door.

The strongroom gaped behind its fallen hinge, and inside, in the lamp's trembling circle, the Meridian Star's last secret caught the light and threw it back at her in coins.

Gold.

Not the romantic gold of storybooks — not doubloons fanned across a beach — but the ugly, practical gold of an embezzlement: minted specie in sealed canvas bags, stacked in wooden racks that had mostly rotted away, so that the bags had slumped and settled against one another like sleeping animals. Some had split. Coins had spilled across the deck plating in a loose tide of their own, wedged in the coral, glinting up at her through an inch of clear water. Pesos of the old colonial mint. Sovereigns. A few bars, stamped with a banking house whose name she recognized from the ledgers, from the ledgers' margins, from Vance's handwriting.

She stood very still.

Her debts had names and faces. The equipment lease on her survey gear, held by a man in Puerto Sombre who smiled too easily. The two seasons of back rent on her room above the chandlery. The loan against her skiff, her one unencumbered possession until it wasn't. Fourteen thousand, six hundred and forty pesos, and every peso of it a leash. She had done the arithmetic so many times it had worn a groove in her: what she owed, what she earned per season scraping other people's abandoned permits, how many years of her life the difference represented. Eleven years, if nothing went wrong. Eleven years of digging in the leavings of luckier people.

The bags on the deck plating were worth more than that. The bags were worth more than that by a factor she couldn't compute without laughing.

"Ines." Teo's voice came distorted through the passage behind her, thin and echoing. "Air's at a third. Tell me you've got the ledgers and we're moving."

"They're packed," she called back, and did not move.

She waded closer. The water was waist-deep now at the room's center, and she had to lean across a fallen rack to reach the nearest bag. The canvas had gone the color of old bone, stiff with a century of salt, but the seal was intact — a lead medallion pressed with the Meridian Star's name, the captain's own countersign beside it. She turned it over in her hands. Heavy. Absurdly, gorgeously heavy, the way only gold was heavy, the way nothing else in the world was quite so honest about its own weight.

One bag. She could take one bag. It would weigh perhaps forty pounds — nothing, on the ebb, with Teo hauling beside her. One bag and she could walk into the Banco del Pueblo, pay her debts in full, and still have enough left to outfit a proper survey season. Her own permits. Her own name on her own paperwork, not scavenged, not borrowed. The thing she had wanted since she was nineteen years old and watched her father's reputation burn: to be solvent, to be legitimate, to be *clean*.

Her fingers were already working at the canvas seal. She made them stop.

*Think*, she told herself, in her father's voice, the voice from the good years, from the tide-table years. *The sea keeps its own books. Read them before you spend anything.*

She made herself look at the room the way she'd been trained to look at a site. Not as a treasure. As a problem.

The passage out was flooded, narrow, and unstable — the collapse on the fourth tide had shifted rock in three places, and Teo had threaded it on the way in by feel, by memory, by luck. The air pocket that had made the vault room accessible was shrinking; she could hear the difference in the way her own voice carried now, flatter, closer to the water. The spring tide was coming. When it came, this room would be a chimney of churning current, and anything not already out would stay here until the sea decided otherwise — which might be a generation, might be forever.

The ledgers were light. Oilcloth, paper, eleven volumes that together weighed less than one of her boots. She could carry them through the passage in a single run, strapped flat against her chest, and be out with air to spare.

The gold was not light. The gold was forty pounds a bag, and there were — she counted, sweeping her lamp — perhaps thirty bags still whole, plus the loose coin, plus the bars. Even one bag changed the mathematics of the dive. Slowed her kick. Changed her buoyancy, which changed her trim, which changed everything in a passage where trim was the difference between sliding through a gap and lodging in it. She had watched Teo calculate loads for two weeks now; she knew the litany. Weight was time, and time was air, and air was life.

One bag. Forty pounds. Maybe ninety seconds slower.

Maybe.

And there was another mathematics, the one she liked less. Corvus was in the outer chamber even now. She could hear the pumps through the rock, a heartbeat of machinery, the fleet's appetite made audible. Voss's divers were working the hold, cataloguing, recovering — under orders, Teo believed, to find the manifest first and let the rest follow. If the fleet found the vault room, they would find the gold, and the gold would buy Vance everything: lawyers, silence, a second career of the same crimes under a different flag. Every bag she left was a bag that bought the man who had destroyed her father.

But — and here the thought turned cold in her chest — if the fleet found the vault room, they would find it *because of the ledgers' absence as much as their presence*. An empty strongroom, stripped of its papers, told a story. A story someone would pay to hear. The gold, left behind, was bait; the gold, taken, was a confession. If she stripped even one bag, and Corvus reached this room and found the rest, the arithmetic of what had been removed would point at her like a compass needle. They would know what she had. They would know it was worth killing for. They already suspected; she had seen it in Voss's eyes on the water. Taking the gold wouldn't just slow her dive. It would sign her name to the theft in the only ledger Vance respected.

Whereas the ledgers — the ledgers were worthless to Corvus. Worse than worthless. The ledgers were the one cargo on this ship that Vance would pay any price to see burned, which meant they were the one cargo she could not afford to lose and the one no one would think to guard, because no one but her and Teo knew they existed.

*Read the sea's books*, her father's voice said again. *Then read the men's.*

She looked down at the bag in her hands. The lead seal pressed against her palm, the Meridian Star's name worn smooth but legible, and beneath it the countersign: *E. Marchand, Capt.* A man who had scuttled his own ship to hide his owners' theft, who had kept meticulous accounts of the crime because it was the only honest work left to him. She thought of him suddenly, vividly — a man at a desk in a flooding ship, writing in a clerk's patient hand, knowing that the only way to tell the truth was to seal it in iron and let the sea keep it for a hundred and fifty years.

The sea had kept its books. It had waited for a storm to open its vault, and it had given the reading to her — not to Vance, not to Corvus, not to any of the men whose names were written in the margins. To her. The daughter of the man they had ruined.

And what would Kenji Nakamura have done?

She almost laughed. She knew exactly what he would have done, because she had watched him do it, in miniature, a dozen times. The season a developer had offered him triple rate to certify a shoreline that would have buried a midden site, and he had refused, and they had lost the house, and her mother had cried, and Kenji had sat on the porch with his tide tables and said, *Ines, some things you don't get to price.* She had hated him for it. She had been fifteen and she had hated him for it, and she had spent six years telling herself that his scruples were a kind of vanity, that principle was what men called their pride when they wanted it to sound noble.

The bag hung in her hands, heavy as a verdict.

She set it down.

Not gently — she was not that composed — but she set it on the deck plating, upright, seal intact, and stepped back from it, and the stepping back was the hardest physical act of her life, harder than the fourth-tide rescue, harder than the swim through the collapse. Her whole body argued. Her hands ached with the shape of the bag. Some animal part of her brain, older than principle, older than names, howled at her for walking away from food in a famine.

"Teo," she said, and her voice came out steadier than she felt. "I'm coming out. Ledgers only."

A pause, long enough that she heard the water move. Then: "You found something else." Not a question. Teo Brandt had been a salvage man too long not to hear a choice in a woman's voice.

"There's specie," she said. "A lot of it."

Another pause. Through the rock, faintly, the pumps kept their rhythm.

"How much is a lot?"

"Debt-erasing. Fleet-buying. Revolution-funding." She heard herself laugh, a strange small sound in the shrinking air. "A lot."

"Can you carry one bag?"

"I can carry one bag."

"Then carry one bag," Teo said, "and we'll both pretend you made the calculation on trim and air time and not on anything else."

And there it was — the offer, the open door, the permission she had not asked for and could not have asked for, handed to her by a man who owed everyone and wanted one clean thing. She stood in the cold water with her lamp on the gold and understood that the choice was not actually between the gold and the ledgers. The ledgers were going out regardless; she would die before she left them. The choice was whether she also took the poison, whether she let Vance's money — because that's what it was, every coin of it, money stolen from a colony, laundered through a century, waiting in the dark for the next pair of hands — into her own account. Whether she funded her clean future with the dirtiest money on the island.

If she took it, she would tell herself the story everyone told: that it was recovered, that it was salvage, that possession was nine-tenths. And the story would hold until the day it didn't, until some auditor or journalist or magistrate's successor asked where Ines Nakamura's startup capital had come from, and she would have to choose, again, between truth and solvency. And she knew — with the same certainty she knew tide tables, with the knowledge her father had poured into her across a childhood she'd spent resenting — that she would lie. That she would become a smaller version of the thing she hated. That her name on her own permit would be a name she couldn't quite look at.

*Some things you don't get to price.*

"I'm leaving it," she said. "All of it."

Silence from the passage. Then Teo's voice, and there was something in it she'd never heard before — not mockery, not pity. Recognition.

"You're sure."

"No," she said honestly. "But I'm right. The gold is Vance's confession too — every bag. If it stays here, it's evidence. If it goes to Corvus, it's his war chest. But if it goes to *me*, it's just theft with better manners." She looked at the spilled coins glinting in the coral, at the split bags sleeping against each other, at the fortune that had ruined an island's history and was now offering, generously, to ruin hers. "Let the courts decide where it belongs. Let the ledgers do the talking. I'll take the part that can't be bought back."

She worked quickly then, because the air was going bad and the tide was turning and sentiment was a luxury the sea did not extend. She wrapped the eleven ledgers flat against her chest in the oilcloth, strapped them tight, checked the straps twice, then a third time with Teo's voice in her head running the checklist. She took one last look around the vault room — the desk, the open strongroom, the captain's patient accounts — and doused her lamp to save the filament, and let the darkness have the gold.

The passage out was exactly as bad as she remembered and worse in two new places. She went through it by touch and by Teo's voice ahead of her, counting her kicks, managing her trim, the ledgers a flat pressure against her sternum like a second heartbeat. At the worst pinch — the squeeze where the fourth-tide collapse had dropped a slab across the old route — she had to exhale almost everything and pull herself through on her fingertips, and for three seconds she was a body wedged in stone with the whole weight of the ocean waiting behind her, and she thought, with a clarity that felt like a gift: *this is what the gold would have cost. Not forty pounds. This. Every dive, every day, forever.*

Then she was through, and the air pocket received her, and Teo's hand found her arm in the dark and hauled her onto the shelf rock.

They lay side by side in the black, breathing, the pumps of Corvus thudding faintly through the stone like the cave's false heart.

"You left it," Teo said at last. It wasn't a question either.

"I left it."

"Voss's divers will find it. Maybe tonight."

"Then it becomes evidence in federal hands, or it becomes a bribe we can name. Either way, it's on the record. That's worth more than fourteen thousand pesos." She said it firmly, and mostly believed it, and knew that at three in the morning for the rest of her life she would do the arithmetic again and have to live with the answer. That was the price. That was the actual weight of it. She had thought leaving the gold would feel like loss. It felt, instead, like setting down something she'd been carrying since she was fifteen years old — the belief that she was her father's daughter only in the ways that shamed her.

"Kenji would've left it too," Teo said quietly.

"Don't," she said. But she didn't mean it, and she suspected he knew she didn't mean it, and in the dark beside the breathing sea she let the words sit there, unargued, true.

They gave the air pocket another five minutes, no more. The tide was inside its last hour of slack, and after that the chimney would run. Teo went first, as agreed — his body between her and the unknown, his lamp burning low — and Ines followed the pale loom of him down through the flooded gallery, along the guide line she'd run on the first tide, out through the throat of the cave into water that suddenly tasted of storm and stars.

They surfaced in the outer pool under a sky rinsed clean by the northeaster, the cliffs black against the first grey of morning. The Corvus flagship rode at anchor a quarter mile out, her lights burning, her pumps silent for the change of watch. No one had seen them. No one ever would see the dive that mattered.

Ines trod water and pressed her palm flat against the oilcloth bundle over her heart. Eleven ledgers. One captain's log. A manifest with Vance's handwriting in the margins. The whole rotten architecture of a hundred and fifty years, light as paper, worth more than the room they'd left behind.

Teo swam for the cliff cache. Ines floated a moment longer, looking back at the dark seam in the rock where the sea was keeping her father's books — and keeping the gold, for now, for whoever came next.

Then the current took her, and she let it.

She was halfway to the cache when she saw the light.

Not from the flagship — from the cliff path above the cave mouth, a lantern swinging low and deliberate, the way a man walks when he owns the ground he's standing on. It stopped at the lip of the overhang. It looked down at the water. And Ines, treading black water in the grey dawn with a century of proof strapped to her chest, watched Magistrate Aurelio Vance come down her cliff to see what his money had bought — and what, she would learn within the hour, he had decided to bury.
