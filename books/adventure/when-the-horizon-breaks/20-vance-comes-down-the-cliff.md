## 20. Vance Comes Down the Cliff

The launch *Aurelia* did not belong to the magistrate's office, and that was the point of it. Aurelio Vance had learned long ago that a man who wanted things done quietly should never arrive in anything with an official seal on the hull. So he came down the coast in his brother-in-law's spare boat, wearing a linen jacket the color of wet sand and carrying, in a leather case under his arm, documents that would have ended three careers if read aloud in the wrong room.

He stood at the bow as the cliffs of Isla Verde slid past, green-black in the morning haze, and he did not look at them. He looked instead at his hands, which were steady, and at the water, which was not.

The northeaster had done what six years of careful paperwork could not. It had opened the coast like a letter, and everyone on the island could read it now — every fisherman, every beachcomber, every drunk in Puerto Sombra who remembered the old stories about the Meridian Star and her cargo of secrets. Vance had spent a career making sure those stories stayed stories. He had buried the survey report under a mountain of bureaucratic sediment; he had buried Kenji Nakamura's reputation under the report; and he had believed, with the calm certainty of a man who has never once been caught, that the sea itself would keep the rest.

The sea, it turned out, kept its own books.

"Magistrate." The pilot, a Corvus man named Ossler, touched his cap. "We'll make the landing beach in ten minutes. Captain Voss is expecting you."

Vance smiled the way he smiled at budget hearings. "Is she."

It was not a question, and Ossler, wisely, treated it as one anyway. "Yes, sir. She came down from the flagship at first light. Set up a survey station above the cave mouth."

Vance turned his face back to the water. *She came down at first light.* Of course she had. Mara Voss was the best salvage captain on this coast, which meant she was the most dangerous thing in his brother-in-law's fleet, which meant she was the most dangerous thing in his life at present. He had recommended her himself, two years ago, over brandy in the harbor club — *a woman who follows orders and doesn't ask what they're for.* He had thought that was praise. Now he understood it had been a diagnosis, and diagnoses could change.

Because Voss had spent four days inside his concession perimeter, and in four days she had filed reports that were too clean. Too complete. Every crate catalogued, every dive logged, every hour accounted for — and not one word, in forty pages of documentation, about the manifest chest. Not one word about the lead lining he knew was down there, because he had signed the order to leave it where it lay, twenty years ago, when he was a junior surveyor with ink-stained cuffs and a future to protect.

Voss wasn't hiding the manifest. She was *waiting*. A woman like that didn't sit on a discovery out of laziness. She sat on it because she was deciding what it was worth, and to whom, and whether the man who owned the concession was worth more than the man who could buy her silence.

He adjusted his cufflinks. His hands were still steady. That was the one discipline he had never let fail him — not since the night, twenty-two years ago, when a young archaeologist named Kenji Nakamura had spread tide tables across his desk and said, in that soft, infuriatingly certain voice, *the sediment doesn't lie, Aurelio. Only the reports do.*

Vance had laughed then. He had signed the falsification with the same steady hand, and he had watched Nakamura's career come apart over the following months like wet rope, strand by strand, and he had slept well. He had slept well for twenty-two years. That was the arithmetic of it: one signature, purchased with nothing but ambition, against two decades of untroubled nights. Anyone could see it was a fair trade.

Except lately the nights had developed a texture. A grain. He woke at odd hours listening for engines on the water, and told himself it was the wind off the harbor, and poured a drink, and slept badly anyway. Power, he had discovered late in life, was mostly a matter of accounting — of knowing exactly how many people knew exactly how much — and his ledger had recently begun to show entries he hadn't authorized.

There was the girl, for one. Ines Nakamura. He'd had her file pulled weeks ago: petty permits, scraped-together digs, debts like barnacles. A nobody. But nobodies had a way of being somebodies' daughters, and daughters had a way of asking questions their fathers had died — or in this case, *hadn't died* — asking. He had ordered her watched, lightly. The reports since had been unsatisfying. She was either very good or very lucky, and both possibilities annoyed him equally.

And there was Renn, the diver, who had sold him a cache location for a promise, and there was Ossler, and there were eleven men on the demolition crew whose names he made a point of knowing, because a man who knows names can buy men, and a man who buys men can bury mistakes. It had all worked beautifully for twenty-two years.

Until the storm gave the coast back to history.

* * *

The landing beach was gray shingle below the cliff path, and Voss was waiting at the top of it, arms crossed, silhouetted against a sky the color of dishwater. She wore working clothes and no greeting, which was, for Voss, a kind of courtesy.

"Magistrate." She didn't offer a hand. "You didn't file a site visit."

"I don't file my mornings, Captain. I own them." He climbed the last of the path, breathing harder than he liked — the cliff stairs were a young man's punishment — and stopped beside her survey table, which was covered in charts weighted with shells. "Show me your progress."

"You've read my reports."

"I've read your reports. I want to hear you say it."

Something moved behind her eyes — amusement, maybe, or its colder cousin. She turned to the chart. "Outer chamber pumped and mapped. Cargo hold accessed through the flooded passage, air pocket stable as of yesterday's dive. Recovered so far: timber samples, ceramic shards, two crates of colonial-era trade goods. Catalogued and photographed." Her finger traced the hold on the chart without quite touching it. "The heavy material sits deeper. We're rigging lifts."

"The chest," Vance said.

"What chest?"

"Mara." He used her first name deliberately, the way one uses a knife at dinner. "I've read the original survey notes. There is a lead-lined document chest in the hold, portside, under the third row of crates. I want to know why it isn't in your recovery log."

For a moment the only sound was the wind worrying the guy-lines of the survey tent. Then Voss straightened, and her face did something remarkable: it became completely, professionally empty.

"We haven't reached it yet," she said. "The passage narrows past the hold. My divers flagged it as unstable. I don't spend divers on unstable ground for paper, Magistrate. Paper keeps."

"Paper keeps," Vance repeated softly. "Yes. It does. That's rather the problem."

He walked to the edge of the bluff, where the ground fell away toward the cave mouth far below, its dark opening breathing with each swell. From here he could see the Corvus barge anchored off the point, the pump lines running down into the water like veins. His investment. His exposure. Everything he had built resting on a hole in a cliff that the ocean had decided, after twenty-two years, to reopen.

"Do you know what's in that chest?" Voss asked behind him.

"I know what's *said* to be in it. Legends charge admission, Captain. I've never paid."

"No," she agreed. "You've always made other people pay."

He turned. She was watching him with an expression he couldn't catalogue — not insolence, not quite challenge. Something patient. And patience, in a subordinate, was the most expensive quality there was.

"Here is what happens next," Vance said. "The spring tides begin in two days. After they crest, this cave reseals — possibly for a generation. The permits vest fully in Corvus at that point regardless of what's recovered. Which means everything of value must be out before then, or it stays down there under our name and we've paid for a hole full of seawater."

"That's consistent with the operational plan."

"The operational plan has changed." He opened the leather case and drew out a single sheet — not the demolition orders themselves, those would come later, properly laundered through the harbormaster's office, but the survey justification that made them legal-shaped. "Geological assessment shows the cliff face above the chamber is fractured. Storm damage. There is a risk of catastrophic collapse onto the site. For safety reasons, the site will be sealed by controlled demolition ahead of schedule."

Voss looked at the paper for a long moment. "Sealed with the cargo still inside."

"With everything still inside." He folded the sheet away. "The Meridian Star has waited two hundred years, Captain. She can wait two hundred more. What matters is that whatever is down there stays *down* there, under a hundred tons of rock, where no dredge, no diver, and no journalist will ever reach it."

"And the salvage value? Corvus has capital in this water. My brother-in-law—"

"Your employer's capital is insured," Vance said, "by a company I chair. He'll be compensated generously and thanked publicly. He may even believe the story." He permitted himself a thin smile. "That's the beauty of a collapse, Mara. Everyone grieves the loss. No one audits it."

Voss said nothing. She looked past him, down at the cave, and when she spoke again her voice had gone flat and even, the voice of a woman reading instruments. "When?"

"Tomorrow night. High slack water, so the charges seat properly." He snapped the case shut. "Your divers will be out of the water by dusk. All equipment withdrawn. You'll log it as a safety stand-down."

"And if there's anything in the inner chambers we haven't recovered—"

"There won't be. By tomorrow night, there won't *be* any inner chambers." He started up the path toward the waiting car, then paused, because there was one more thing, and he had learned that the last thing said is the thing remembered. "Captain. One more item. The Nakamura woman. Ines. If she comes near this site again — and I'm told she has a talent for coming near things — she is to be detained. Not harmed. Detained, questioned, and held aboard your vessel until I say otherwise. Consider it... insurance."

Voss's silence lasted three seconds. He counted them, the way he counted everything.

"Aboard my ship," she said finally.

"She's already been aboard once, hasn't she. The skiff interception." He smiled at whatever crossed her face. "Nothing happens on this coast that I don't eventually hear about, Captain. It's why I'm the magistrate and you're the captain. Detain her. Question her gently. And if she's carrying anything — papers, photographs, anything that looks like a document older than her grandfather — it comes to me. Directly. Not through your logs. Not through Corvus. To me."

"And if I decline?"

"You won't." He descended two steps, then turned back, and for a moment — only a moment — the mask slipped, and she saw what lived underneath: not confidence but arithmetic, frantic and precise, a man counting exits in a room that was getting smaller. "Twenty-two years ago a man found something in these cliffs and I made one phone call and he lost everything. Do you imagine your position is sturdier than his was? Your debts alone, Mara — I've seen the ledgers. Corvus owns your hull, your insurance, your diving gear, and your sister's medical bills. You are not a captain. You are a collateralized asset. Assets don't decline."

He climbed the rest of the stairs without looking back, because looking back was for men who doubted, and he had made a career of never doubting where anyone could see him do it.

* * *

The harbormaster's office in Puerto Sombra smelled of tar and old decisions, and Vance sat in it for an hour after dark, signing things. Demolition permits, laundered through three offices so that no single signature pointed back at him. A requisition for forty cases of gelignite, logged as road work on the north causeway. A wire to the mainland, coded, confirming that the correspondent from the *Coastal Ledger* — the one sniffing around the salvage story — would find the ferry delayed by weather for the foreseeable week.

Small moves. Clean moves. Each one deniable, each one boring, each one the kind of administrative dust that history never thinks to sift. This was his true talent, he reflected, initialing the gelignite requisition with a fountain pen his wife had given him for their anniversary: not vision, not courage, but *tidiness*. Other men committed crimes. Vance committed filing errors.

Ossler appeared in the doorway, rain-wet, uneasy. "Crew's loaded, Magistrate. Eleven men, plus the blasting engineer from the mainland. He asks a lot of questions, though. Wants to know why a road job needs marine fuses."

"Tell him the causeway has a drainage problem." Vance blotted the signature. "And Ossler. The engineer works tomorrow night and takes the morning boat home. He doesn't tour the site, doesn't speak to the crew, doesn't speak to *me*. If he's curious again, double his fee and shorten his memory."

"Yes, sir." Ossler hesitated. "One more thing. The watch post on the point reported lights on the water last night. Small craft, no running lamps, moving quiet. They think it might be the Nakamura girl running supplies to a cache somewhere along the cliff line."

Vance set down the pen. For a moment he felt the old, familiar steadiness — the pleasure of a problem resolving into a solution. The girl was digging. Of course she was. Scraping at the edges of his tidy arrangement like a crab at a hull.

"Then she's given us a gift," he said. "If she's caching finds along the cliff, she's leading us to everything she's recovered. Tomorrow, before the demolition, I want that cache found and emptied. Whatever she's got — documents especially — it comes to me before the charges go in. Nothing leaves that coast except rock and rumor." He stood, took his coat from the chair, and paused at the window, where the harbor lights smeared across the black water like spilled paint.

Somewhere out there, a woman was holding a piece of paper with his handwriting on it. He could feel it the way one feels weather coming — not evidence, just pressure, just the animal certainty that the barometer had turned. Twenty-two years of steady hands, and now this grain in the nights, this itch beneath the arithmetic.

After tomorrow, he told himself, it would pass. Rock kept secrets better than people did. He had built his whole life on that simple truth, and tomorrow night he would build a hundred tons of cliff on top of it, and sleep.

He was almost at the door when Ossler spoke again, reluctant, the way messengers always are.

"One more thing, Magistrate. Captain Voss put in a request an hour ago. Through channels. Official form." He handed over a slip of paper. "She's asked permission to run one final dive tomorrow. Before the stand-down. Inner passage, two divers, four hours. Says there's material in the vault room she wants logged for the insurance claim."

Vance read the request twice. The handwriting was neat, professional, unremarkable — and that was what chilled him, because Mara Voss did not file official forms for small things. She filed them for things she wanted witnessed. Documented. *On the record.*

She wasn't asking permission. She was building an alibi.

He looked out at the harbor a moment longer, at the dark water where his fortune and his crime and his twenty-two quiet years all floated together on the same tide, and he felt the grain in the night deepen into something with weight.

"Deny it," he said. "No dives. Full withdrawal by dusk. And Ossler—" he folded the request into quarters, precisely, "—if Captain Voss objects to that order, I want to know within the hour. Because if she objects..."

He left the sentence unfinished, tucked the folded paper into his breast pocket over his heart, where he kept the things he intended to think about later, and stepped out into the rain.

Tomorrow night the cliff would come down, and everything underneath it — the gold, the ledgers, the handwriting, the whole buried architecture of his tidy life — would stay down forever.

He only had to make sure that nothing, and no one, came up first.
