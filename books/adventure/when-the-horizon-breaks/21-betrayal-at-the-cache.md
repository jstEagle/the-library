## 21. Betrayal at the Cache

The cache was a lie before they reached it.

Teo felt it in the way the cliff path went quiet — no gulls working the updraft over the point, no goats, nothing. Isla Verde's cliffs were never silent. They were a racket of wings and wind and the sea chewing at the base of everything, and when that racket stopped, it meant something with hands had passed through recently and the island was still deciding how to feel about it.

He said nothing. Ines was twenty meters ahead with the tide tables folded into her back pocket and her father's habit of reading ground written all over her — she'd already slowed, already gone soft-footed on the limestone. Teo had learned in eleven days of working with her that Kenji Nakamura had built his daughter out of patience and suspicion in roughly equal measure, and that both materials had held.

She crouched where the trail bent behind the sea fig, and he saw her hand come up flat: *stop*.

Then she pointed down.

The cache was a cavity beneath a slab of collapsed karst, half-hidden by saltbush, dry above the spring-tide line because Teo had spent two full hours of his life making sure of it. He'd wedged the slab with coral rubble so it could be lifted without noise. He'd laid a false spill of loose stone over the approach so that anyone walking it would leave prints in exactly the wrong places for anyone following to read. He'd done it the way Corvus taught him, which was the way nobody else did it, which was supposed to be the point.

The saltbush was broken. Snapped at knee height, three stems, green ends browning already — a day old, maybe less. And in the dust of the approach, where his false spill should have shown them clean stone, there were boot prints. Two sets. One heavy, one light, walking in together and walking out heavier.

Ines reached the slab first. She lifted it the way he'd built it to be lifted — silent, balanced, no scrape — and looked into the cavity for a long time without moving.

Teo looked past her shoulder and felt the bottom drop out of his chest with a slow, hydraulic certainty, like water finding a crack.

Empty.

Not disturbed-empty. Not searched-and-left-some-empty. The cavity was scoured. Every crate lining, every oilcloth wrap, every wax-sealed packet they'd cached across four tides of nerve-shredding work — the manifest's duplicate plates, the logbook's second copy, the survey notes, the brass depth gauge stamped with the Meridian Star's registry — gone, down to the last scrap of waterproof paper. Someone had taken the time to lift the oilcloth out from under the finds rather than drag it, someone who knew that dragging left fibers on the limestone. Someone had even re-set the coral wedge.

That was what finished him. Not the theft. The tidiness. Only two kinds of people re-set a wedge after robbing you: professionals, and friends.

"Renn," he said. His voice came out flat and strange, like someone else's.

Ines turned. She didn't say anything. She didn't have to; her face did the arithmetic faster than he wanted it done.

"Those are his boots." Teo crouched at the print, not touching it. "Wide heel, worn outside edge. He favors his left knee since the *Andamar* job — you can see it, the right print's deeper. That's Renn Okafor. That's—" He stopped, because saying *that's my crewmate* out loud, after eleven years, after everything, made it real in a way the empty hole hadn't managed.

"Your friend," Ines said. Not cruel. Just precise, the way she was precise about sediment layers and about people.

"He's not my—" Teo started, and then shut his mouth, because the sentence had nowhere honest to go. Renn wasn't his friend. Renn was the last person alive who'd been beside him on every bad dive of the worst decade of his life, which is its own kind of friendship, the kind that doesn't survive daylight. Renn had pulled him off a fouled anchor line in the Sunda Strait. Renn had sat with him through the night his mother died, drinking the cheap rum and telling lies about better years. Renn had been the one who vouched for him with Voss when Corvus took him on at nineteen and broke him in like a colt — kindly, almost, by their standards.

And Renn had been standing on the deck of the *Corvus Maid* the day they'd faked the survey dive that ended Kenji Nakamura's career. Teo had known that for six days now. He'd confessed it to Ines with his hands shaking around a tin cup, guilt offered like an open wound, and she'd taken it the way she took everything — without flinching, without forgiving, filing it somewhere she could find it later.

Now the wound had a shape he recognized. It had always had this shape. He'd just spent eleven years calling it by other names.

*You always thought this would happen,* said the voice in his head that sounded like Renn and also like himself. *You always knew you were the kind of man things get taken from. Because you're the kind of man who takes things.*

"Say it," Teo told her. "Whatever you're thinking. Say it."

Ines sat back on her heels. Below them the sea worked the cliff base, patient as an auditor. "I'm thinking we lost the physical copies of everything except what I carry. I'm thinking whoever did this knew the cache location, which means either they followed us or you told someone." Her eyes came up. "I'm thinking I need to know which."

"I didn't tell anyone." It came out too fast and they both heard it.

"You told me you trusted him."

"I told you I *knew* him. There's a difference and I've had eleven years to learn it and apparently I haven't." Teo stood. His knees ached the way they did before weather and after shame. "Three nights ago. The night before the fourth tide. Renn found me at the Anchorline. I told you I was getting spare regulators. I got regulators. I also had two rums with Renn Okafor, and I told him the site was live, and I told him—" He made himself breathe. "I told him I was finally on a clean job. I said it like it was a boast. Like I needed him to know I'd become somebody."

Ines said nothing. The silence had weight and texture, like deep water.

"And he asked where we were caching, joking, the way he asks everything, and I gave him the point, the north bend, the sea fig. I didn't give him the slab. But Renn doesn't need the whole answer. Renn reads water the way your father reads sediment. You give him one current and he'll find the rest." Teo heard his own voice go thin. "I handed him the current."

"So Vance has the logbook copy. The survey notes. The gauge."

"The gauge proves the wreck's registry. The notes prove the cave was surveyed before the official record says it was." Teo ran both hands through his hair. "Vance can't unmake the manifest — you kept that on your body, thank God, thank whatever — but he can muddy everything else. He can make it look like we fabricated corroboration. A lawyer with a week and a bribe can do a lot with a stolen notebook."

"A lawyer with a week and a bribe," Ines repeated softly, "is exactly what Aurelio Vance keeps on retainer."

They stood there in the wind with the empty cache open between them like a grave dug for the wrong person, and Teo understood that something in him had been waiting for this moment for eleven years — had been, in fact, built out of the waiting for it. Every job he'd ever walked away from in the middle. Every debt he owed in four ports. Every morning he'd woken on a bunk or a beach or a jail bench with the same sentence sitting on his chest: *you are the kind of man who gets sold.* He'd thought the sentence was about his luck. It had never been about his luck.

It was about his aim. He aimed his trust low and wide, like a man scattering seed on ground he already knew was salt.

"We're not done," Ines said.

He blinked. "What?"

"The manifest survives. On my body, in oilskin, against my ribs, where it's been since the second tide because I don't trust caves, cliffs, boats, or people." She tapped her sternum once, twice. "Vance stole our corroborating paper. Fine. Paper was always going to be the weak leg of this. Your captain—"

"She's not my captain. Not anymore."

"Captain Voss," Ines continued, inexorable, "has a fleet, a conscience developing like a photograph, and a first mate whose loyalty we have no reason to trust. Imelda Cruz has the oral history and the reach. We have the true manifest and the vault room still sitting behind the cargo hold with the ledgers inside it, assuming Vance's divers haven't cracked it yet." She stood, brushing limestone dust from her knees. "We lost a battle over paper. The war is still underwater."

Teo looked at her — this woman who'd been robbed of her father's name twice, once by Vance and once by time, and who responded to catastrophe the way other people responded to a change in tide: recalculating, adjusting, going on. He felt the familiar pull of two currents in him, the old one that said *run, you've been burned, this is the proof, get out while you've only lost paper*, and a newer one, eleven days old, that said *she counted you among the things worth trusting and you will not make her regret it the way everyone else has.*

The newer current was winning. That frightened him more than Renn did.

"There's something else," he said. "If Renn sold us, he didn't sell us for money. Money's never been Renn's price. Renn sells for belonging. Somebody made him feel like the inside of something again, and the only somebody on this island handing out belonging is Vance." He turned the thought over and hated its weight. "Which means Renn isn't done. A man selling for belonging keeps selling until the feeling holds. He'll be watching us now. He knows we'll know. He'll expect us to run or confront him, and either way he reports it."

"Then we do neither." Ines was already moving up the trail, back toward the cove, and the set of her shoulders had changed — grief compressed into forward motion, the way ore compresses into something you can forge. "We let him watch us do exactly what we'd have done anyway. And tonight you're going to tell me everything about Renn Okafor. What he loves. What he fears. What he'd do if he learned the man buying his loyalty plans to sink the ship he's standing on."

Teo stopped dead. "What?"

"Voss suspects it. I saw it in her face on the water. A magistrate who erases archaeologists does not retire salvage captains who've seen his handwriting on a forged survey." Ines glanced back at him, and for one moment the fury under her control showed through, banked and white-hot. "Renn sold us to a man who burns his tools. When Renn learns that — and he will learn it, because you're going to tell him — he becomes the most dangerous man on this island. To Vance."

"That's if he believes me."

"He believed you enough to rob us instead of reporting the cache directly. Think about why." She started walking again. "A stranger would have reported you. Renn cleaned up after himself. He hasn't decided yet, Teo. Men who've decided don't tidy up."

* * *

They came down to the cove at slack water and found the skiff untouched, which told Teo the thieves had come overland, which told him Renn had hiked the cliff path alone at night with a hundred pounds of paper and brass on his back rather than take a boat any Corvus watcher might log. Alone. That detail lodged in him like a splinter. Renn had never in his life done a heavy job alone if he could share it. Either he'd had no one to trust with it —

— or he'd been protecting someone from knowing.

Teo hated himself for noticing. Hope was just another way of getting robbed.

They loaded the skiff in silence, and it was while he was coiling the line that he saw it: tucked under the stern sheet, weighted with a shell, a square of oilcloth folded to the size of a cigarette case.

His hands knew the fold. It was Renn's cache-fold, the one from the Sunda days, the one you used when you wanted something to survive a dunking and a search. Inside was a strip of paper torn from a tide table, and on it, in Renn's blunt pencil, pressed hard enough to emboss:

**DIDN'T TAKE THE MANIFEST PLATES. COULDN'T STOP THE REST. WATCH THE FLAGSHIP'S BOATS TOMORROW. NOT EVERYONE ON HER IS VOSS'S. — R**

Below that, a single line that undid Teo entirely:

**YOU WERE RIGHT. IT WAS NEVER A CLEAN JOB. NONE OF THEM EVER WERE. I'M SORRY, LITTLE BROTHER.**

Teo read it three times. The cove sounds came and went around him. *Little brother.* Eleven years, and Renn had never once called him that sober.

"What is it?" Ines said.

He handed her the paper and watched her read it, watched her face move through suspicion, recalculation, and something almost like pity — though whether for Renn or for him he couldn't tell.

"He took everything but left us the one thing that matters," she said slowly, "and warned us there's a traitor on Voss's ship. That's not a thief's note. That's a man trying to stand in two boats at once." She folded it along Renn's crease and handed it back. "Tomorrow, when the flagship's boats move — we follow them. And Teo—"

"Yeah."

"When you talk to him again, you tell him the truth about Vance. All of it. Before someone else tells him a prettier version."

Teo pocketed the note against his chest, next to the guilt that had lived there for eleven years, and found that for the first time the two weighed about the same — and that both, maybe, could be put to work.

Out past the point, low and dark against the afternoon glare, one of the flagship's boats was already pulling for shore.
