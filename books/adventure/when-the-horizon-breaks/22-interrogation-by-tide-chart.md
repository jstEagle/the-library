## 22. Interrogation by Tide Chart

Mara Voss had interrogated smugglers, mutineers, and one memorably unrepentant customs officer, and she had learned long ago that the first three minutes told her everything. People revealed themselves in how they entered a room. The guilty came in hunched, eyes flicking to the exits. The frightened came in fast, wanting it over. The stupid came in loud.

Ines Nakamura came in like a woman arriving at a dinner party she'd rather not attend but had dressed for anyway.

Voss watched from behind the chart table in the *Corvus Dawn*'s stern cabin as the guards brought her in — not dragged, she noted, though the guards had clearly expected to drag her. Nakamura's wrists were bound in front of her with cord, and she held them slightly away from her body the way one holds a borrowed coat, an inconvenience rather than an indignity. Her hair was still wet from the skiff, salt drying white along her temples, and there was a scrape on her jaw from where Voss's boarding hook had caught her — she'd moved toward it rather than away, which Voss had found interesting at the time and found more interesting now.

"Sit," Voss said.

Nakamura looked at the chair. It was a plain bolted-down stool, the kind meant for charts and men waiting to be yelled at. She sat down without being asked twice, crossed her ankles, and settled in with the particular patience of someone who had decided the interrogation would happen on her schedule or not at all.

"Water?" Voss asked.

"I'd rather know what this costs me."

"Nothing yet. That's what makes it an interrogation and not a negotiation."

Something moved at the corner of Nakamura's mouth. Not quite a smile. An acknowledgment of the shape of the game.

Voss let the silence run. This was the other thing she'd learned: whoever spoke first out of discomfort lost altitude. She poured herself coffee from the pot on the warming plate, unhurried, and studied the woman across the table while pretending not to.

She had read the file, of course. Vance kept files on everyone who mattered, and Voss made it her business to read everything Vance kept. Ines Nakamura, thirty-one. Daughter of Kenji Nakamura, the surveyor whose name had been scrubbed off half the coastal charts after the Meridian Star affair. Licensed under a provisional salvage permit that had been renewed four times through clerical charity rather than merit. Debts with two lenders, one of them the kind of lender you didn't owe past harvest season. No crew to speak of except Teo Brandt, which was its own kind of information, because Brandt had dived for Corvus for two years before Voss had cut him loose, and everything Brandt touched had a smell of desperation that never quite washed out.

The file said: competent, bitter, undercapitalized, dangerous only to herself.

The file, Voss was beginning to suspect, had been written by someone who had never sat across a table from her.

"You boarded my skiff in open water," Nakamura said, when the silence had gone on long enough to become hers instead of Voss's. "Under whose authority? The concession permits cover the site, not the approaches."

"The permits cover whatever I say they cover. I'm the concession holder."

"You're the concession holder's employee. Corvus Fleet is owned by Aldous Vance-Reyes. The magistrate signs your orders." Nakamura tilted her head. "Or does he? I've been wondering about the chain of command out here. It seems fluid."

*Sharp*, Voss thought. *And she knows exactly where the soft tissue is.* "You've been studying us."

"I study water. You're floating on it."

Voss drank her coffee. Outside the stern windows, the afternoon light lay flat and silver on the swell, and the outer chamber of the cave breathed its slow tidal breath below them, full of Corvus pumps and Corvus divers and, somewhere in the flooded dark beyond the rubble throat, whatever Nakamura and Brandt had been pulling out of it for three days without anyone catching them at it.

That was the real question. Not what she knew — what she'd *taken*. The manifest chest had been breached when Voss's divers reached the hold; the lead lining peeled back with tools, not corrosion. Someone had gotten there first by hours, maybe a tide. And the strongbox from the outer chamber had vanished entirely between tides, along with any number of smaller recoveries the dive logs recorded as "unlocated" because admitting otherwise would mean admitting someone was working inside their perimeter like a tick under the hide.

"We both know why you're here," Voss said. "So let's not spend the hour pretending. Things have been coming out of that cave that aren't coming out through my manifest. I want to know what you have."

"Do you?"

"It wasn't a rhetorical question."

"No," Nakamura said. "It was a bad one. You want to know what I have so you can decide whether to take it, buy it, or drown me for it. I'd rather establish the order of operations before we get sentimental."

Voss set down her cup. There it was again — that steadiness. Most people, tied to a stool on a hostile ship, managed their fear badly or not at all. Nakamura wore hers like ballast. It didn't lift her; it kept her level.

"Order of operations," Voss said. "All right. First, you tell me what you've recovered. Second, we discuss terms. Third, you leave this ship either richer or wetter. Your choice which."

"And if I don't tell you anything?"

"Then third happens anyway, just with less conversation."

Nakamura looked at her bound wrists, then up, and something in her expression shifted — not softer, exactly. More deliberate. As if she'd been holding a card back and had now decided the moment had come to play it face-up, just to see what Voss did.

"Before I answer," she said, "I have a question. One question, and then I'll talk all you like."

Voss gestured: proceed.

"When did Vance last ask you for a progress report in person? Not by signal flag, not by courier. In person, aboard this ship."

The question landed somewhere Voss hadn't armored. She kept her face still, but she felt the hit register, the way you feel a hull strike below the waterline — no visible damage, a change in the sound of the ship.

"Three weeks," she said. "He came down for the announcement."

"He announced the concessions from your deck and hasn't set foot on it since. He sends Renn's people to check your stores. He routes your fuel through his brother-in-law's depot. And he ordered the vault room sealed behind a pump schedule only your chief engineer has." Nakamura paused. "You didn't know I knew about the pump schedule. Your engineer talks when he drinks. Everyone talks when they drink. It's the one universal current."

"What's your point, Nakamura?"

"My point is a question I'd like you to sit with while you decide whether to drown me." She leaned forward as far as the cord allowed, and her voice dropped into something quieter and colder than anything she'd said yet. "What do you think happens to Captain Mara Voss, holder of nothing, witness to everything, once the vault is recovered and the site is closed? Do you imagine there's a bonus? A share? A letter of commendation from the magistrate's office?" She shook her head slowly. "You've read enough ledgers to know how the last page of that story reads. Loose ends don't get pensions, Captain. They get weather. *Lost at sea in a sudden squall.* The fleet will mourn. Vance will send flowers to your sister — does he know your sister? He knows things like that. Men like him collect what they might someday need to hurt."

The cabin was very quiet. Somewhere above them, boots crossed the deck; the ship creaked through its slow roll. Voss became aware of her own heartbeat and disliked it.

Because the terrible thing — the thing she would not say, would barely admit in the privacy of her own skull — was that she had already had the thought. Not fully formed. Not written down anywhere. But three nights ago, standing at the rail watching Vance's launch recede toward the harbor, she had looked at the black water and thought, with a diver's cold clarity: *he will not want me able to testify to what was in that vault.* And she had put the thought away the way you put away a knife you aren't ready to use, and told herself it was disloyalty, and poured a drink over it.

And now this soaked, indebted, half-drowned stranger had walked onto her ship in cord-bound wrists and picked the knife back up off the deck and laid it on the chart table between them.

"That's a story," Voss said. Her voice came out even. She was proud of that. "A pretty one, built to make me sympathetic. You're sitting in my chair, on my ship, in my ropes."

"Yes," Nakamura agreed. "And you haven't called the guards back in. I noticed. When I mentioned your sister, your hand went to the chart drawer where you keep the letters she sends you from Puerto Cielo. I noticed that too." She sat back. "We could keep noticing things at each other all afternoon, Captain. Or you could untie me and we could talk like the only two people on this coast who understand what Vance actually is."

* * *

Voss untied her. She told herself it was tactical — a subject spoke more freely with free hands, and a free subject in a locked cabin was no threat on a ship this size — and the excuse was even mostly true. But her fingers were quicker at the knots than strictly necessary, and she was aware of that, and filed it away for later examination, the way she filed away everything she didn't have time to feel.

Nakamura rubbed her wrists and accepted the coffee this time, drinking it black, straight, like medicine.

"Tell me about the manifest," Voss said.

"Why?"

"Because I read official cargo lists for a living, and the ones in the archive are wrong. Tonnage doesn't reconcile. The Meridian Star drew too much water leaving Port Amara for what she was supposedly carrying. I flagged it years ago in a fleet audit and got told to mind my own ledger." She watched Nakamura over the rim of her cup. "So I already know the records lie. What I don't know is what the truth costs, and who's paying."

For a long moment Nakamura just looked at her, and Voss recognized the look, because she used it herself: the rapid silent arithmetic of whether the person across from you was a mark, a wall, or a door.

"Your father," Voss said quietly. "Kenji Nakamura surveyed that coastline eleven years ago. His report is the one that declared the site geologically worthless. Except his field notes — the few pages that survived the archive fire — show sediment cores from exactly where the wreck sits. He found it. He reported it honestly. And then someone rewrote his conclusion, and the world decided he was a drunk or a fraud, depending on which tavern you asked." She set the cup down. "I'm not asking you to trust me. I'm asking you to stop pretending I don't already know the shape of this."

"Who told you about the field notes?"

"A dead archivist who talked too much over brandy. Don't worry — Vance never knew what she gave me. It's the only reason I took this commission without vomiting." Voss leaned back against the chart table. "Here's what I can see from where I stand. Vance wants the vault recovered before the spring tide seals the site, because the vault contains paper with names on it. Not gold — paper. Gold he'd melt down and forget. Paper he has to burn. And everything he's done since the storm — the early demolition schedule, the pump rotation, the way he looks at my divers like he's counting them — says the burning is going to be literal and soon, and that the list of people standing near the fire when it starts is shorter than the list of people currently employed."

"And you're on the short list or the long one?"

"I'm on whichever list ends with the sea," Voss said. "So is Brandt. So, probably, is Renn, though Renn is too stupid to be frightened, which will save everyone time." She folded her arms. "Now. The manifest. What does it say?"

Nakamura turned her cup slowly on the table, one full rotation, thinking. Voss let her think. She understood the weight of what she was asking — handing over the manifest's contents to the concession holder's captain was either the smartest move of Nakamura's life or the last one, and she was watching the same calculation happen behind those dark eyes that Voss herself would have been running.

"The official record says the Meridian Star carried machinery, timber, and general freight," Nakamura said finally. "The true manifest lists specie — minted silver, colonial treasury marks — crated as machine parts. Eleven crates of it. Enough money to build a district. Which it did." She looked up. "Your district, Captain. The old families' wharves, the bank on Calle Mayor, the magistrate's own house on the hill — all of it paid for out of a scuttled ship that officially sank of natural causes with a hold full of lumber."

"The ship was scuttled."

"Deliberately. The logbook confirms it. Seacocks opened, boats sent away with a skeleton crew who were told a story, captain went down with his lie." Nakamura's voice was flat and precise, each fact placed like a core sample. "It wasn't a disaster. It was a transaction. And every official document that says otherwise was signed or supervised by Aurelio Vance, years before he held office, when he was a junior clerk with excellent handwriting and no scruples."

Voss stood very still. She had suspected the fraud — the audit discrepancy had whispered it for years. But suspicion was a sketch, and this was the painting, in colors she hadn't wanted to look at directly.

"The annotations," she said. "In the margin of the manifest. I've heard there are annotations."

"You've heard correctly."

"In whose hand?"

"Whose do you think?"

Voss closed her eyes briefly. *Junior clerk with excellent handwriting.* She had seen Vance's handwriting a thousand times — on concession orders, on pay chits, on the memo firing Brandt's whole crew without cause. A neat, upright, faintly ornamental script that looked like honesty itself. The idea of that script in the margins of a forged manifest, dated a decade back, was somehow worse than the forgery. Forgeries could be desperate. Handwriting that neat was *practiced*.

"He was young," Voss said. "Younger than me."

"They always are, at the first one."

The words sat between them. Voss found she couldn't pick them up.

Outside, the light was going copper; the tide was turning, and she could feel it in the ship's motion, the subtle change in the rhythm of the water slapping the hull. Sixth tide coming. Everything narrowing.

"Here's what I don't understand," Voss said. "Why come aboard? You had copies — you must have copies, you're not fool enough to work a job like this without them. You could have stayed in the mangroves until the end and handed your papers to the press and never been within reach of my hooks. Instead you let my boarders take your skiff. You *wanted* to be brought here." She narrowed her eyes. "Why?"

"Because you're the only piece on this board that moves both directions," Nakamura said. "And because I needed to see your face when I told you about the pump schedule and the demolition charges. I needed to know whether you already knew." She paused. "You didn't. Your face did something small, just here—" she touched her own temple— "when I said *sealed*. You've been commanding a demolition site for six days and nobody told you the charges were already staged."

"They're not staged. Vance wouldn't move without—"

"Your chief engineer received the detonators Tuesday. Crated as pump spares. He logged them and said nothing because he assumed you knew, and he assumes you know because Vance's courier told him *the captain handles all technical matters*. Captain — you are the technical matter. When this cave comes down, the signature on the accident report will be yours."

The cabin seemed to tilt, though the sea was calm. Voss gripped the edge of the chart table and made herself breathe slowly, once, twice, the way she'd taught younger divers to breathe when the bottom dropped out of a plan.

*The signature on the accident report will be yours.*

She thought of the memo firing Brandt's crew. She thought of the archivist, dead of a fever that came on very conveniently. She thought of her sister's letters in the chart drawer, and of Vance's mild, patient voice saying, at the announcement three weeks ago: *Mara, you're the only one I trust with the technical side.* She had taken it as a compliment. She had gone to sleep that night warmed by it.

God, she thought. God, I have been *managed*.

When she looked up, Nakamura was watching her with an expression Voss recognized from mirrors: not triumph, not pity. Recognition. The look of one professional taking the measure of another and finding the measure agreeable.

"Now you understand why I came aboard," Nakamura said.

"I understand you're the most dangerous person on this coast," Voss said, "and it isn't because of what you stole from that cave."

"What I stole from that cave is evidence. What I'm doing right now is the actual weapon." Nakamura finished her coffee and set the cup down with a small, exact click. "Captain. The sixth tide is tomorrow night. After it, the site seals, the permits vest, and whatever Vance intends to happen to witnesses happens quickly. You have a ship, a crew that trusts you, and access he needs. I have documents he'd burn the island to destroy. Between us, we have maybe forty hours to decide whether we're on the same side of the water when it closes over."

"And if I say no? If I hand you to the guards right now and radio Vance?"

"Then you'll be signing the accident report with a clear conscience," Nakamura said. "And your sister will get flowers."

Voss looked at her for a long moment. Then she crossed to the cabin door, opened it, and spoke to the guard outside without turning her head.

"Ms. Nakamura's skiff is being repaired below. She'll remain aboard overnight as my guest while it's patched. No restraints. If anyone asks, she's assisting with hydrographic review." She closed the door and slid the lock.

When she turned back, Nakamura had raised an eyebrow.

"Hydrographic review," Voss repeated dryly. "Don't make me regret the fiction. And Nakamura—" she pulled the chart drawer open, took out the bundle of letters tied in sail twine, and held them a moment before putting them back— "if this is another kind of trap, the kind where I end up in the water, know that I've spent twenty years learning exactly how this ship sinks."

"I'd expect nothing less," Nakamura said. "It's why I came aboard."

They stood across the chart table from each other in the failing light, two women who had spent their lives reading water for a living, and who had just discovered they'd been reading the same lie from opposite shores.

Above them, faint through the deck planks, came the sound of the pumps changing cycle — and beneath it, deeper, the sea beginning its long pull toward the sixth tide.
