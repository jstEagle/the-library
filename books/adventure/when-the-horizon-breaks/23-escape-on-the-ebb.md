## 23. Escape on the Ebb

They gave her a bunk in the forward hold because there was nowhere else to put her.

Ines understood the arithmetic of it. The *Corvus Pride* was not built for passengers; she was built for lifting, for pumping, for the patient violence of salvage, and every square meter of her served that purpose. So Ines Nakamura got a sailor's hammock slung between two cargo winches, behind a bulkhead that smelled of diesel and old rope, with a hatch above her that someone had thoughtfully dogged from the outside.

Thoughtfully. She turned the word over in the dark and found it had teeth.

Two days aboard Voss's flagship had taught her the ship's language — the groan of her plates in swell, the rhythm of watches changed overhead, the particular clatter of the galley chain when the cook was in a good mood, which was rare enough to be navigational. She had learned it the way her father had taught her to learn everything: as a system, with rules, and therefore with seams. Every system had seams. Her father used to say the sea itself was a system, and men who forgot that drowned politely, believing to the last that they were in control.

She lay in the hammock now and listened to the tide.

It was not a thing most people could hear — the difference between flood and ebb, the way water moved against a hull like breath drawn in or let go. But Kenji Nakamura had put his daughter's ear against a piling when she was seven years old and made her listen until she could feel it, and some lessons outlive their teachers' usefulness. The current was turning. She could feel the ship swing gently at anchor, bow falling off a point to starboard, the anchor chain paying out its soft complaint. Ebb tide. In three hours it would be running hard toward shore, a brown-green river pouring off the shoals, fast enough to carry a swimmer — or to drown one.

Three hours until slack ended. Then the window began closing again.

She had spent the interrogation cataloguing what she knew of the ship instead of defending what she knew of herself, and the habit had paid better than she'd hoped. Supply launch came out from Puerto Sombra every second morning: water, fuel for the pumps, fresh bread wrapped in oilcloth, mail. It came at mid-morning on the flood, riding the current out, and it left on the early ebb, riding home. Tomorrow was a supply day. She knew this because Teo's name had been spoken twice through the bulkhead in low voices — once by the first mate, Delacroix, whose loyalty Voss trusted the way you trust a rope with a fray in it, and once by a deckhand complaining that Brandt was coming aboard with the launch and there wasn't room below for his gear and his mouth both.

Teo was coming aboard. That was either a rescue forming or a catastrophe arriving, and Ines had decided she could not afford to wait and find out which.

Because there was a third possibility, the one that kept her awake with her hand flat against the waterproofed bundle beneath her shirt: that whatever Teo intended, Vance would not permit it. She had seen the magistrate's shadow in everything Voss did — in the manifest priority, in the sealed orders, in the way Delacroix counted the dive skiffs each evening like a man counting someone else's money. And she had watched Mara Voss's face across the chart table during their strange, bladed conversation and seen something there that frightened her more than hostility would have: doubt. A captain beginning to suspect that the man who owned her concession owned nothing else about her fate. Men like Vance solved that kind of problem with ballast and deep water.

If the flagship went down, the logbook went down with it. If Ines went down, the logbook went down with her. Either way, Kenji Nakamura stayed ruined, and Aurelio Vance stayed golden, and the sea kept its own books forever.

She was tired of the sea keeping books. She wanted to write one.

* * *

Morning came gray and windless, the kind of light that makes distances lie. Ines heard the supply launch before she saw anything — the flat slap of its hull a mile out, then the rattle of fenders being readied, then boots on deck directly above her head. She had dressed in the dark an hour before dawn, logbook strapped against her sternum inside a diving undersuit she'd filched from the wet locker, cut-down wetsuit top over it, and over everything the borrowed canvas jacket of a man two sizes bigger than she was. Beneath the hammock, coiled where a careless guard would read it as spare line, waited thirty meters of 8-mil dive cord she'd braided into a swim line over two nights, working by touch, working slow, working like her father had taught her: *the hands can be honest even when the heart is furious.*

The hatch dogs rattled. She was lying in the hammock, breathing like a sleeping woman, when Delacroix opened it.

"Up," he said. "Voss wants you fed. Captain doesn't interrogate skeletons."

"Charming man," Ines said, and climbed down, and let him walk her aft past the galley, past the dive station, past the rail where the supply launch was bumping alongside with its cargo of crates and cans and one familiar figure hauling gear up the pilot ladder with the exaggerated innocence of a man trying very hard not to look at anyone.

Teo Brandt looked terrible. He'd been diving hard for days and it showed in the gray under his eyes and the way he favored his left shoulder. He did not look at her. He looked at the deck, at the crates, at the horizon, with the studied blankness of a man performing ignorance, and only when he passed within arm's reach did he murmur, so quietly it might have been a cough:

"Ebb runs at ten-forty. Northeast corner. They're short-handed on the pump watch."

Then he was past her, and a deckhand was shouting at him about stowing gear, and the moment closed over like water.

Northeast corner. The lee side, away from the superstructure, away from the helm's sightline, where the anchor chain came down and the hull fell away in a tumble of fouling and ladder rungs. Short-handed on the pump watch meant the deck crew thinned to skeleton numbers while everyone capable stood the transfer. Ten-forty. Ninety minutes.

Ines ate what they gave her — rice, salt fish, coffee strong enough to strip varnish — and let them watch her eat, because a prisoner who eats is a prisoner who has given up, and she needed them reading surrender. She sat where they put her, on a coil of hose near the stern rail, hands loose, eyes down, and inside she was running the tide table the way her father had taught her to run it: not as numbers but as a story. Flood slowing. Slack — the sea holding its breath, the moment of maximum danger for a swimmer because the water gives you nothing. Then the turn. Then the ebb, building, building, a river in reverse, and if you entered it at the right place at the right minute it would carry you like a hand under your back all the way to the shallows off Punta Clara, four miles down-coast.

Enter it wrong, or enter it late, and it carried you out past the shelf instead, into water too deep to swim and too cold to survive, and the sea would keep you the way it kept everything — without malice, without memory, without returning anything.

*The tide keeps its own books,* she thought, and almost laughed. She'd built her whole bitter life on that sentence. Maybe today she'd finally make it mean something else.

* * *

At ten-twenty the launch cast off, riding light, and the deck emptied by half. Ines felt the change in the ship's sound — fewer boots, looser talk, the pump watch calling its numbers in bored voices. Delacroix had gone below to argue with the engineer about fuel contamination, which she knew because she'd listened through the bulkhead the night before and knew he argued with the engineer every supply day, same complaint, same hour. Systems had seams. This one had been showing her its seams for two days, waiting for her to notice.

She walked to the head — permitted, expected, unremarkable — and did not come back the way any escort would have assumed. There was a second door. There is always a second door on a working vessel; ships are mazes built by practical people for practical purposes, and no one builds a maze with only one way out except prisons, and the *Corvus Pride* was not a prison. It was worse. It was a machine that believed it was a family.

The second door let her into the chain locker passage, and the chain locker passage let her onto the forecastle, and the forecastle at ten-thirty-five held exactly one person: a boy of maybe seventeen, coiling line, who looked up at her with wide eyes and open mouth.

"Don't," Ines said.

He didn't. Whether it was fear or sense or something he'd seen in her face — the thing she could feel living there now, the thing exhaustion had refined into something harder than anger — she would never know. She stepped past him to the northeast corner, swung her legs over the rail, and dropped into the water between the hull and the world.

The shock of it took her breath. Even tropical water at depth-chill steals heat like a thief with a plan, and the wetsuit top was thin, cut down, imperfect. She hung in the hull's shadow with the anchor chain rising above her like a rusted spine, and she breathed, and she checked the bundle against her chest — one strap, one knot, the logbook sealed in its double wrapping of oiled silk and inner tube rubber, pressed tight over her heart where her father's letter had once lived in her jacket pocket for a week before she could decide whether to burn it.

Everything important, she thought, I carry against my heart. That's going to get me killed someday.

Not today.

The slack was ending. She could feel it — the faint lean of the water, the first suggestion of direction returning to a sea that had gone still and thoughtful. Above her, the ship's bell rang the half hour. Ten-forty.

She kicked free of the hull and swam.

* * *

The first hundred meters were the worst of her life.

A swimmer alongside an anchored ship is a swimmer in a kill zone. Any lookout glancing down, any deckhand crossing the rail, any idle pair of binoculars sweeping the waterline — and she was done, hauled back aboard, and this time there would be no chart-table conversation, no grudging respect, no bunk behind a dogged hatch. Voss had been straight with her, in her way. Vance would not be.

So Ines swam the way her father had taught her to move through country that was watching: low, slow, unhurried, part of the landscape. Face down, breathing through the snorkel she'd taken from the dive station in a theft so small it would take days to notice. Letting the growing ebb do the work. Not fighting the chop, not lifting her head, not existing. Four miles of this. Four miles of being nothing but a shadow under gray water, moving at the speed of the current, which was also the speed of patience.

Her father's voice came to her unbidden, the way it always did in the worst moments, clear as if he were treading water beside her: *You don't beat the sea, Ines. You agree with it faster than anyone else does.*

"I'm agreeing," she said into the snorkel, and the words came out as bubbles, and somewhere under the exhaustion something in her chest cracked open and laughed.

Because this was the truth she'd been swimming from for six years: that every skill she had was his. The tide tables in her head, the sediment-reading eye, the patience, the stubbornness, the willingness to be cold and tired and alone in service of a thing nobody else believed in — all of it, all of it, handed down from the disgraced man she'd sworn never to forgive, learned at his knee on cliffs and mudflats while other children learned normal things. She had spent six years trying to build a self out of materials that weren't him, and the whole time the best parts of her bore his fingerprints like a recovered artifact bears its maker's mark.

Fine, she thought, driving her arms through the green water. Fine. You taught me everything except how to forgive you. So I'll use everything else, and we'll see which of us the sea keeps.

The ebb strengthened around her. She felt it take hold properly at last — the long pull of the outgoing tide funneling between the island and the shoal, gathering speed, and suddenly she was moving, really moving, the shoreline sliding closer with every stroke instead of receding in mockery. The gray water turned green, then brown-green with the sediment of the flats, and the taste of the shallows came to her on the current: silt, mangrove, land. Land doing what land does. Reaching.

Behind her, faint across the water, a bell began ringing fast — the pattern that wasn't time, the pattern that meant alarm.

They'd found the empty hammock. Or the boy had talked. Or Delacroix had come up from his argument with the engineer and done his count. It didn't matter. What mattered was the sound of an engine turning over, distant and angry, and the knowledge that the *Corvus Pride* could raise a chase boat in perhaps eight minutes, and that the ebb ran at two and a half knots and she was already a mile into it, already small, already gone.

Let them come, she thought, and the thought arrived with a ferocity that surprised her, that burned clean all the way down. Let them spend their fuel and their fury chasing a woman who had spent six years learning how to be underestimated. Let Voss stand on her deck deciding whether she truly wanted to catch her. Let Vance scream into his radio from his office ashore, ordering boats, ordering men, ordering the world to behave, discovering as tyrants always discover that the tide does not take orders.

Exhaustion was still in her arms, still in her shoulders, still in the cramp knotting her left calf every fourth kick. But somewhere in the last mile it had stopped being exhaustion. It had transmuted, the way ore transmutes in fire, into something with an edge. She was not fleeing anymore. She was delivering. Every stroke was a sentence in a report Vance could not falsify, written in the only ink he'd never managed to buy: distance covered, current ridden, document intact.

The mangroves rose ahead — a black-green wall breathing with the tide, roots like the ribs of some drowned cathedral. Behind her the engine noise grew, resolved into a chase boat's white wake visible now even against the dull water, a quarter mile back and closing slowly, fighting the same current that carried her. Ahead, through a gap in the roots, she could see the mudflats of Punta Clara shining like hammered pewter, and beyond them the coastal road, and beyond that, inland, the old Cruz estate with its decaying grandeur and its matriarch whose name appeared in a ledger Ines carried against her heart.

Doña Imelda Cruz. The ledgers named her grandfather. Shame was currency on Isla Verde, older than gold, and Ines was about to spend it.

The chase boat's engine bellowed frustration a hundred meters back as she slid into the channel between the mangrove roots, where no boat drew less than a meter and hers drew nothing at all. She stood at last in thigh-deep warm mud, gasping, legs shaking, and turned to look back through the lattice of roots at the white wake circling uselessly in the channel mouth.

A figure stood in the bow — Voss herself, binoculars raised. For a long moment the lenses held on her, motionless. Then, deliberately, Mara Voss lowered the binoculars, turned away, and said something to her helmsman that swung the boat back toward the flagship.

Ines stood in the mud of Punta Clara with the tide draining out of the flats around her, pulling the whole bay seaward, emptying itself, and she laughed — a raw, cracked, disbelieving sound that scattered birds from the mangroves — because she understood what she had just been given. Not mercy. Arithmetic. Voss had counted the cost of dragging her back and found it higher than the cost of letting her go, and Ines intended to make sure that arithmetic aged very badly indeed.

She wrung out her hair, tightened the straps over the logbook, and started inland.

Four miles of mudflat, one coastal road, and a conversation with a woman whose family name was written in a book that could bring down a magistrate. The ebb was still running. So was she.
