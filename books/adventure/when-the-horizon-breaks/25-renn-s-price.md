## 25. Renn's Price

The harbor at Puerto Sombra smelled of diesel and dead fish and old money, and Teo Brandt had spent enough years in harbors like it to know which smell paid the bills. He sat on a stack of crab traps across from the Cantina del Farol, a beer he wasn't drinking sweating in his hand, and watched the door.

Renn Okafor came out at half past nine, exactly as he always had. Some men changed after they sold you out. Renn hadn't changed at all — same rolling gait, same laugh pitched too loud for whatever joke he'd just heard, same faded Corvus Fleet cap pulled low over eyes that had once been the sharpest pair on any deck Teo had worked. That was the thing that made his chest hurt, if he was honest about it. Not the theft. Not even the cache stripped bare while he lay bleeding-adjacent and exhausted in Ines Nakamura's camp. It was that Renn looked *happy*. Looked like a man who'd finally gotten what was coming to him, and hadn't noticed yet that it was coming from behind.

Teo set the beer down untouched and followed him along the seawall, past the ice house, past the chandlery with its shutters half down, out to where the pilings went rotten and the harbor gave way to the mudflats. Renn stopped there because Renn always stopped there — used to light a cigarette off the wind-sheltered side of the last piling and watch the fleet lights out on the water like a man counting his own future.

He didn't hear Teo until Teo wanted him to.

"You always were loud," Teo said.

Renn spun, hand going to his hip, finding nothing there because Corvus security didn't let its divers carry on shore leave anymore — not since the incident two seasons back that everyone pretended hadn't been Renn's fault. His face did three things in quick succession: surprise, calculation, and then a grin he probably thought looked easy.

"Brandt." He spread his arms. "Heard you were dead. Or diving for scraps with the archaeologist girl. Same thing, out here."

"Which one did you tell them?"

"Tell who?"

Teo stepped closer. The mudflat wind pushed between them, carrying the groan of halyards from the moored boats. "Whoever bought my cache, Renn. Whoever walked off with four days of our work while I was under the water. I want a name."

Renn's grin held, but his weight shifted back onto his heels, and Teo read it the way he'd learned to read currents — by feel, by the small betrayals of the surface. "I don't know what you're talking about, man. Somebody hit your little cliff fort? That's island life. Things walk."

"Things don't walk uphill through karst carrying a sealed strongbox and a lead-lined chest. Things get carried by someone who knows the cache is there." Teo kept his voice level. Below them, a night heron stabbed at the shallows and came up with nothing. "Somebody told them."

"And your first thought is me." Renn laughed, but it came out thin. "That hurts, Teo. That genuinely hurts. We go back—"

"We go back fifteen years, yeah. We've gone back since the day you signed on with Voss's crew and told me I was the only honest man left in salvage, which should have been my first warning, because you only say things like that when you're measuring how much honesty costs."

For a moment neither of them moved. Out on the water, the Corvus flagship rode her anchor lights, the *Corvus Belli*, black-hulled and patient as a shark over a reef. Somewhere aboard her, Mara Voss was either planning their ruin or — if the message Teo had slipped her tender crew two days ago had landed right — wondering whether she already knew it.

Renn lit a cigarette. His hands weren't steady, and he saw Teo see it, and that made them worse. "You want to know who took your junk? Fine. You want the truth?" He exhaled smoke sideways. "It wasn't me. I just pointed. There's a difference, and I need you to hear the difference, because I can live with pointing."

"You can't," Teo said quietly. "You never could. You still wake up some nights thinking about the Nakamura survey dive. I know because I do too, and I'm the one who wrote the report."

Something flickered across Renn's face then — not guilt exactly, but the memory of guilt, the way a scar remembers pain. He looked out at the water instead of at Teo.

"They promised me a stake," he said at last. "A real one. Not diver's wages, not a percentage of somebody else's find. A stake. Vance himself — the magistrate, in person, in that white suit like he was going to a wedding — sat across a table from me at the Farol and said, 'Mr. Okafor, the island rewards men who understand which way history is flowing.' Which way history is flowing. Like it's a tide chart." He laughed again, and this time there was nothing in it at all. "Ten percent of the concession value. Ten percent of whatever comes up out of that cave, minus fleet expenses, minus the magistrate's share, minus the magistrate's brother-in-law's share, which by my math leaves me ten percent of a rounding error. But I signed anyway. Because ten percent of something is more than a hundred percent of what I had."

"What did you have?"

"A name people remembered for the wrong reasons." Renn flicked ash into the water. "Same as you, once. Difference is, you got out and hated yourself quiet. I stayed and hated myself loud."

Teo felt the old anger rise and, strangely, found he could look over the top of it now, the way you look over a swell to see what's coming behind. "The manifest's gone. The strongbox. Everything we cached."

"Not everything." Renn said it fast, watching him. "Vance has the strongbox. The lead chest too — he wants it melted down before anyone asks why a colonial-era wreck needed lead lining. But the girl—" He caught himself. "Nakamura. Word below is she kept copies on her body. Vance knows it. He's not worried. He figures paper without the site is just paper, and after the sixth tide the permits vest and the site is legally his to bury. Literally bury. He's got demolition charges staged at the cliff top already. I saw the crates come off the launch yesterday. Marked as 'survey equipment.'" Renn's mouth twisted. "Man doesn't even lie well anymore. He's stopped needing to."

*Charges.* Teo filed it away, cold and bright, next to everything else. "And Voss? Where does the captain stand in all this flowing history?"

Renn hesitated. It was a small hesitation, but Teo had spent fifteen years reading this man in bad light and worse weather, and he knew what it meant: *there's more, and the more is worse.*

"Renn."

"Don't use the captain voice on me."

"I'm not your captain. I'm the guy standing close enough to take that cigarette away from you. Talk."

Renn looked out at the flagship again, and this time his gaze had a different quality — not covetous but afraid, the way you look at a boat you've decided you might die on. He dropped his voice, though there was no one within fifty meters but the heron and the mud.

"There was a meeting yesterday. Afternoon. I wasn't supposed to be anywhere near the aft cabin, and I wasn't — I was on the ladder to the paint locker, which shares a bulkhead, and the ventilation grate carries sound like a church organ. Don't ask me why I was there. I'll tell you anyway: I was going to listen for my name. Men like me always are." He drew on the cigarette until it flared. "Voss was in there with Vance. And Vance was talking about the vault."

"The captain's vault. The ledgers." Ines had told him what they'd seen behind the cargo hold before the collapse cut them off — rows of bound books, letters under wax seal, a strongroom door with the Meridian Star's crest. They'd gotten the logbook out. The rest was still down there, behind rock that fell in the fourth tide, waiting for the spring lows.

"The ledgers," Renn confirmed. "Vance wants them recovered before anything else. Before the specie, before the cargo. He told Voss the vault contents come up first or the whole operation stops. And Voss asked him — cool as you please, you know how she is, like she's asking about ballast — *recover them for what purpose, sir?* And Vance laughed." Renn's hand shook hard enough that ash fell. "He laughed and said, 'Captain, you've been at sea too long. History isn't recovered. It's *edited*.'"

The wind came off the flats and pushed the cigarette smoke flat along the piling. Teo stood very still. He could feel the shape of what was coming the way a diver feels pressure change before his ears pop.

"And then?" he said.

"And then Vance said the part I keep hearing when I try to sleep." Renn turned and looked at him straight on, and his eyes in the dark were wet, whether from wind or something else Teo couldn't say and didn't want to. "He said: 'Once the vault is secured, Captain, you'll want to make ready for the crossing to San Cristóbal. I'd advise you to make it at night. Deep water. The *Belli*'s insurance lapsed last month — did you know that? Terrible paperwork error. If anything happened to her out there, with all hands... well. Condolescences would be sent. To whoever was left to send them to.'"

The words landed in Teo's chest like a dropped weight. He heard them twice — once in Renn's rough voice, and once in Vance's imagined silk, and both times they meant the same thing. *Sink the flagship. With Voss aboard. With every diver and mate and cook who'd ever seen the inside of that cave, who'd ever heard the word Meridian Star spoken in the magistrate's presence. Erase the witnesses, erase the evidence, erase the whole dirty season of it, and call it an insurance loss in deep water.*

"He's going to scuttle her," Teo said. "His own sister's husband's flagship. His own operation."

"He's going to scuttle *everyone who knows*," Renn said. "The ship's just the method. That's the part you're not getting, Teo, the part I didn't get for six years until tonight made it simple: Vance doesn't clean up messes. He deletes witnesses. Your Kenji Nakamura — he didn't kill him. He killed his *name*. Made him unemployable, unbelievable, a crank telling stories about a ship nobody officially lost. And when names aren't enough anymore—" He gestured at the dark water, at the anchored lights of the *Corvus Belli*, forty souls aboard her if the full complement had signed on for the salvage season. Forty souls, and a captain who'd intercepted Ines's skiff and offered her a cut and let her swim away, and who was, right now, anchored over a hole in the world that a man in a white suit intended to close over her head.

"Why are you telling me this?" Teo asked.

Renn stared at him. "Because you asked."

"People don't sell a man out on Tuesday and warn him on Thursday."

"No." Renn ground the cigarette under his heel, slowly, like he was putting out something bigger than tobacco. "They don't. Unless Thursday is when they figure out what Tuesday actually cost." He looked up, and his face in the harbor light was older than fifteen years could account for. "I did the Nakamura dive, Teo. I was on the line when your report came up, and I watched them take a good man's life apart with a rubber stamp, and I said nothing, because saying something cost money I didn't have. Then I pointed Vance's men at your cache for money I still don't really have, because ten percent of a rounding error is a rounding error. And now I've heard a man plan to drown forty people and call it paperwork, and I understood something." His voice cracked and he let it. "There's no amount. There's no number they can put on a table that makes me one of theirs. I keep trying to buy in and all I ever buy is a closer seat to the fire."

Teo breathed out. Fifteen years of anger, and here at the end of it, what he felt mostly was tired — and underneath the tired, something moving, something with direction, like a current finally finding its channel.

"Then get off the fire," he said.

"I can't just walk. Vance owns the harbor master, the union card office, half the constabulary—"

"I'm not asking you to walk. I'm asking you to point." Teo stepped in close, and for the first time all night Renn didn't shift back. "You're good at pointing, Renn. Point it the other way. Voss needs to hear what you heard, word for word, and she won't believe it from me — I'm the disgraced diver running errands for the archaeologist. She might believe it from her own bosun's mate. And Ines needs to know about the charges on the cliff. And I need to know the demolition schedule, the guard rotations, which launches carry fuel and which carry men. Can you give me that?"

Renn was quiet a long time. Out on the water, a bell buoy clanked its slow idiot rhythm, and somewhere aboard the flagship a bosun's whistle piped the change of watch — forty souls turning over their shifts, not knowing they were already scheduled for the deep.

"If I do this," Renn said finally, "there's no going back to the fleet. There's no going back to anything. You understand that? I'll be exactly what you are."

"Yeah," Teo said. "I know. It's not so bad. You get to sleep at night."

Renn laughed — a real one this time, short and broken and human. He pulled his cap off, ran a hand over his scalp, and put it back on backwards, the way he used to wear it when they were young and the water was warm and none of them had sold anything yet.

"The demolition crew musters at dawn," he said. "Two days from now. Charges go in on the fifth tide, detonation on the sixth, spring low, when the cave mouth's widest — Vance wants the collapse to look natural, storm damage, so the site seals itself behind his story. And the *Belli*—" He swallowed. "The *Belli* makes her 'crossing' the night after the vault comes up. That's the order. That's the whole arithmetic."

Teo nodded once. Two days. One tide of margin, maybe less, and a captain who didn't yet know she was cargo.

"Come with me," he said. "Tonight. Both messages, delivered by the man who heard them."

Renn looked at the flagship a last time — at the anchor lights, at the dark water holding her up, at the deep water waiting beyond the harbor mouth to take her under. Then he turned his back on fifteen years and fell into step beside Teo, and the mudflats took their footprints and gave nothing back.

Behind them, far out on the black water, the *Corvus Belli* rode her anchor, patient as a held breath — and somewhere above the cliffs, in a locked shed marked SURVEY EQUIPMENT, two days' worth of thunder waited for its orders.

* * *

They found Ines at the camp above the cave, sitting cross-legged by a lantern with the copied manifest pages spread around her like a dealt hand, and when Teo finished telling it — Renn filling in the gaps in his raw, reluctant voice, the aft cabin, the ventilation grate, *history isn't recovered, it's edited* — she didn't move for a long moment. The lantern flame leaned in the sea wind. Then she began gathering the pages, quick and precise, squaring their edges against her knee.

"Two days," she said. "Then we stop racing the tide." She looked up at Teo, and in the lantern light her eyes had gone the color of deep water over rock. "Now we race Vance."
