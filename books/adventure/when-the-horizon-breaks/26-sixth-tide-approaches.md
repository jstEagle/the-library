## 26. Sixth Tide Approaches

Ines woke before the alarm she hadn't set, her body keeping its own tide table now, and lay in the dark of the survey station listening to the sea breathe against the cliffs below. It sounded different tonight. She had noticed it for three nights running — a deeper pull, a longer drag on the shingle, the water retreating farther out before it came back hungrier. The spring tide was gathering itself like a fist.

She lit the lamp and unrolled the chart across the table, weighting the corners with a brass shell casing, a stone from the cave mouth, a tin of tea gone stale, and her father's tide tables — the 1971 edition, soft-cornered, annotated in his small precise hand in margins that had nothing to do with tides. Little observations. *Gulls go quiet before the second swell.* *The reef at Punta Sur lies about a fathom shallower than the charts say.* She had hated those marginalia once, the way they made the tables feel less like science and more like a diary, a man talking to himself about the ocean instead of to his daughter about anything that mattered. Now she read them the way other people read letters.

The moon was two days from full. The syzygy would stack sun and moon and earth into a single line of pulling force, and the sixth tide — the last usable one — would come up over the sill of the cave mouth at 4:17 in the morning, peak at 9:52, and begin its long retreat. After that, the following high water would crest nearly a meter higher, driving silt and storm-surge sand back into the passage like a cork going into a bottle. The cave would seal itself for a generation. Maybe longer. The sea kept its own books, and it was about to close this account.

And when the sixth tide ended, so did something else. The concession permits — Vance's emergency concessions, signed in triplicate with the ink still warm from his brother-in-law's pen — vested fully in Corvus Fleet at the close of the final working tide. Everything found after that belonged to them by law. Everything found before it belonged to whoever could carry it away.

Ines ran the numbers again anyway. She had run them eleven times since midnight, and each time they came out the same, which was the nature of arithmetic and the cruelty of it too. One tide left. Perhaps thirty hours of real work inside the flooded passage if the collapse zone held, and no reason on God's green water to believe it would hold. Teo had said the rock above the air pocket sounded wrong when he tapped it — *like a drum with a crack in the skin* — and Teo Brandt did not say things like that unless he meant them.

She looked at the documents spread beside the chart: photographs of the manifest pages, the logbook wrapped in oilcloth and waxed canvas, her copies of the vault ledgers reduced to a shorthand cipher of her own devising because carrying the originals everywhere felt like carrying a lit fuse. On the mainland there was a federal inspector who might be honest. There was a correspondent for the *Meridian Courier* who owed Doña Imelda a favor measured in decades. There was a magistrate who owned everything else.

Thirty hours. Then the horizon closed like a book.

* * *

Teo was sitting on the steps of the station when she came out at first light, which meant he had walked up from the harbor in the dark, which meant he hadn't slept either. He looked better than he had four days ago, after the collapse — the bruising along his ribs had gone from black to a yellow-green that meant healing or meant dying slowly, depending on how much you trusted his color sense, which Ines did not. But his hands were steady around the thermos he offered her, and his eyes were clear, and that was more than she'd had any right to expect.

"You've been up all night," he said.

"You look worse."

"I've been told I'm beautiful at dawn." He nodded at the chart visible through the open door behind her. "Same answer as last time?"

"Same answer." She took the thermos and drank. The coffee was terrible and hot and she was grateful for both. "One tide. We get everything movable out of the vault room tomorrow night, and we're done. After that the cave belongs to Corvus and the crabs."

"And the gold?"

The question sat between them the way it had sat between them for days now, patient and heavy. Ines watched the light come up over the water — gray first, then a thin band of tarnished silver where the horizon would break later — and gave him the only answer she had.

"We take what we can carry without slowing down. The ledgers, the letters, the captain's papers. If there's room and time, we take specie. If there isn't—" She shrugged, and the shrug cost her something, and she let him see that it cost her, because lying to Teo about this felt like a different order of betrayal than the ones already in play. "If there isn't, it stays. A generation, maybe forever."

He was quiet for a while. Below them, at the base of the cliff, the exposed shelf of the low spring tide gleamed wet and black, littered with the Meridian Star's bones — rib-timbers, a windlass, the great curved piece of keel that the fishermen had started calling *la ballena*, the whale. Tomorrow night the sea would begin taking all of it back.

"My whole life," Teo said finally, "I figured the difference between me and an honest man was about forty kilos of gold. That's what I told myself. Get one clean score, buy my way back onto the right side of the ledger, start over." He turned the thermos cap in his fingers. "Now there's a vault down there with enough coin in it to buy a small country's silence, and I find myself thinking about paperwork."

"People change."

"No." He shook his head. "People don't change. They just finally see what they were always looking at." He stood, wincing, and shouldered his dive bag. "Come on. If we've got one tide left, I want to spend today making sure the passage doesn't kill us before Vance gets the chance."

* * *

They spent the morning running lines.

It was work Ines knew in her hands before her head caught up — tying off anchor points on the old mooring rings her father had driven into the cliff face years ago, checking knots with the automatic suspicion he'd taught her (*a knot is a promise, mija, and the sea is a creditor*), paying out the guide lines into green water that swallowed them without comment. The cave breathed below them. At slack water the inner passage ran nearly clear, and through the surface she could see the ghost-shapes of cargo crates stacked in rows, furred white with sediment, patient as coffins.

Twice during the morning she stopped and simply listened — not to the sea but for engines. The Corvus fleet lay at anchor off Punta Sur, three vessels riding their moorings with the insolent stillness of things that know they own the water. Voss's flagship, the *Corvus Mare*. The pump barge. The tender. Since the night at the cache — since Renn — the fleet had changed its rhythm. More boats in the water at odd hours. Searchlights sweeping the cliff line after dark. And yesterday, a launch that had come within two hundred meters of the survey station and idled there for ten full minutes, close enough that Ines could see a man on the bow with binoculars, studying the station the way a man studies a house he intends to buy, or burn.

"They know we're here," she said, coiling line. "They've known since the cache."

"They know *someone's* here," Teo corrected. "There's a difference. Vance wants us gone, but he can't very well put out a bulletin — *wanted: archaeologist's daughter, knows too much* — not while he's telling the island this is all legitimate salvage and historical preservation." He spat over the edge, with feeling. "Man has to murder people quietly or not at all. It's the one thing constraining him, and it's the only reason we're still breathing."

"It won't constrain him past the sixth tide."

"No," Teo agreed. "Past the sixth tide, we're just loose ends. And I've worked for men who tidy loose ends." He tested the main guide line, hauled himself hand over hand along it to check the tension, and came back. "Which is why I went to see Renn last night."

Ines went still. "You *what*?"

"Easy." He raised both hands. "I didn't tell him anything. I wanted to see where his money was coming from, and who was paying it, and whether he'd grown a conscience anywhere in the last ten years." His jaw worked. "He hasn't. He's drinking on Corvus credit at the Anchor & Chain, telling anyone who'll listen that he's got a stake coming in the biggest salvage in island history. He's spending money he doesn't have yet on money he hasn't earned. And he's scared, Ines. Under all the noise, he's scared the way a man gets scared when he's realized the person holding his leash doesn't need a reason to yank it."

"What did you hear?"

Teo glanced toward the cliff path, empty in both directions, then lowered his voice even though the wind was doing its best to drown everything. "I heard him arguing with a Corvus purser about his cut. I heard the purser tell him the terms had changed — that after the sixth tide there'd be a consolidation, and certain arrangements would be reviewed. Renn asked what that meant. The purser smiled and said, *it means Captain Voss shouldn't get comfortable.*"

The words dropped into Ines's chest like cold lead. "Voss."

"Voss." Teo nodded grimly. "I don't love the woman. She detained you, she'd have taken every document we ever touched if it suited her, and she'd sell us out tomorrow for a favorable audit. But she's the only person on that fleet who's ever treated the site like something other than a bank vault, and she's the only one aboard who doesn't owe Vance her soul." He met Ines's eyes. "And if I'm right about what I heard, she's got maybe two days to live, and she doesn't know it."

* * *

The afternoon brought the wind around.

It came out of the northeast in long slow gusts, the same quarter that had birthed the storm that opened the cave, and Ines stood at the cliff edge reading it with her whole body the way her father had taught her — the taste of it, salt and iron and something colder underneath; the way the swell periods shortened, six seconds, five and a half, the sea stacking itself into steeper, meaner shapes. The spring tide wasn't coming gently. It was coming like a debt collector.

By late afternoon the swell was breaking white over the cave mouth, and the timbers at the threshold groaned and shifted, and Ines felt the clock in her chest speed up. Not thirty hours of work. Less. If the surge built overnight, the sixth tide's working window would shrink from hours to minutes — a brief slack-water lull at the turn, maybe ninety minutes of survivable conditions in the inner passage, and then the sea would come in behind them like a door slamming.

She spread the charts again under the lamp and drew the timeline in pencil, and the pencil lines looked like bars.

*Tide turns: 4:17. Slack window: approximately 90 minutes, weather permitting. Teo dives the vault room via the flooded passage — one run only, no second attempt, the collapse zone won't forgive a repeat. Extraction of documents and whatever specie can be carried at weight. Meanwhile—*

Meanwhile what? That was the question she kept circling and couldn't land. Even if they got everything out, even if the ledgers reached the mainland, Vance still held the permits, the fleet, the magistrate's seal, and the willingness to use explosives on his own heritage site. Documents alone wouldn't stop a man like that in time. They needed the site itself to survive long enough for someone official to stand in front of it. They needed the fleet — or part of it — pointed somewhere other than at them.

They needed Voss.

Ines sat back and looked at the lamp flame and thought about the interrogation aboard the *Corvus Mare* — the two of them circling each other across a chart table, sharp questions like knives laid flat between them, and the moment near the end when Voss had paused over the manifest photograph and something behind her eyes had shifted, some professional loyalty developing a hairline fracture. *I run a clean operation,* Voss had said, and Ines had understood it wasn't a boast. It was the last plank of a deck going soft underfoot.

"She'll never believe it from us," Ines said aloud, to the empty room. "Not from me. Not from Teo. We're the competition. We're the problem she's been paid to make disappear."

But Renn wasn't. Renn was family — rotten family, bought family, but the kind of witness whose word carried weight precisely because it should have gone the other way. And Renn was scared. Scared men talked, if you cornered them gently enough, if you showed them the shape of the trap they were already standing in.

Outside, the wind rose another notch, and somewhere down the coast thunder muttered like a committee deciding something unfavorable.

* * *

Teo came back at dusk soaked to the waist and grinning, which on Teo Brandt was such a rare configuration of features that Ines stared.

"Good news," he said, dripping on her floorboards. "Two pieces."

"I could use them."

"One: I checked the passage at slack. The collapse zone has settled — the key block's wedged solid, and the rubble downstream of it has compacted. It's passable. Tight, ugly, single-file, and you'll hate every meter of it, but passable. Two—" He peeled off his wet shirt and wrung it out with the frank economy of a man who had stopped performing dignity years ago. "Renn drinks at the Anchor & Chain every night until closing. Alone, lately. His crewmates won't sit with him since the cache job — even thieves have standards, apparently. He's a man alone in a room full of people, waiting for a payday that keeps moving further away." Teo looked up at her. "A man like that will listen to almost anyone who offers him a way out that isn't a bullet."

"You want to go back. Tonight."

"I want to go back tomorrow night," Teo said. "During the tide. While you're running the overland timing and I'm supposed to be recovering the vault." He held up a hand before she could explode. "Hear me out. The dive run takes me forty minutes down, twenty in the vault, forty back — call it two hours if the passage behaves. The Anchor & Chain is twenty minutes' walk from the harbor. Renn starts his third bottle at nine. I can be there, planted next to him, planting seeds, and back before the slack window closes. And Ines—" his voice dropped, went serious in a way that made the lamplight seem dimmer, "—if what I heard is true, if Vance means to sink that flagship with Voss aboard, then every hour we don't warn her is an hour she spends anchored over the exact spot where a demolition charge would do the most good. We need her. Not because I like her. Because she's got a ship, a crew, a radio, and a federal inspector's direct line, and right now all of those are aimed at us by default."

Ines stood at the chart table with her palms flat on the paper, on the penciled bars of her timeline, and felt the whole plan tremble like the guide line in current. Too many moving parts. Too little time. One tide. One chance. And now a detour into a tavern to negotiate with the man who had robbed them, on behalf of a woman who had imprisoned her, to prevent a murder neither of them could prove was planned.

Her father's voice, from six years and a lifetime ago: *The sea doesn't reward the careful, mija. It rewards the accurate. Careful people freeze. Accurate people move.*

She picked up the pencil and added a line to the timeline. *2230 — Teo, Anchor & Chain.*

"If you're not back by one o'clock," she said, "I'm coming in after you, and I won't be subtle."

"Noted." Teo smiled, then didn't. "Ines. One more thing. When I was leaving the harbor, the *Corvus Mare*'s gig was being lowered. Voss's personal launch, heading for shore. Heading here." He nodded east, toward the cliff path, where the last light was failing. "She didn't signal. Didn't hail the station. Just came alone, at dusk, the day before the last tide."

Ines turned to the window. Far down the darkening coast, small and deliberate, a lantern was climbing the switchbacks of the cliff path toward the survey station — steady, unhurried, unmistakable.

Captain Mara Voss was coming to her door, and the sixth tide was fourteen hours away, and everything — the cave, the permits, the truth, possibly all of their lives — was about to turn on whatever she said when she knocked.

Ines blew out the lamp and waited in the dark to hear the footsteps arrive.
