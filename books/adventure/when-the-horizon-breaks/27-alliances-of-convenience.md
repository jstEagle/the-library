## 27. Alliances of Convenience

Mara Voss had commanded the *Corvus Gull* through two hurricanes, a mutiny off Punta Sal, and one memorable night when a cargo of dynamite decided it would rather be fire. She had learned to read trouble the way other captains read weather — in the set of a man's shoulders, in the silence that fell too completely, in the way her own crew stopped meeting her eyes.

Tonight, all three signs were up at once.

She stood at the rail of the flagship's aft deck, watching the harbor lights of Puerto Sombra smear across the black water, and let the signs accumulate. Renn had come aboard that afternoon with salt still drying white on his shoulders and a story about engine trouble that explained nothing. Her first mate, Okafor, had re-rigged the watch rotation without asking her — putting his own men on the midnight hours, hers on days she'd already committed to the survey. And down in the hold, behind a tarpaulin and a padlock she hadn't authorized, sat crates of demolition explosive that no salvage concession had any legal reason to contain.

Vance's explosives. Vance's lock. Vance's arithmetic, in which Mara Voss was a line item that could be subtracted.

She'd known the magistrate for eleven years. She had watched him ruin Kenji Nakamura with a signature and call it scholarship. She had carried out his errands and told herself they were simply the price of concessions in a territory where every permit flowed from one desk. But she had never seen him frightened before this week, and frightened men did not pay their tools. They buried them — preferably at sea, where the tide kept its own books and issued no receipts.

The skiff came out of the dark at half past eleven, running without lights, and Mara felt something loosen in her chest that she refused to name as relief.

Teo Brandt stood in the bow like a man who had rehearsed being brave and wasn't quite convinced by his own performance. Beside him, wrapped in an oilskin against the spray, sat Ines Nakamura — thinner than her father had been at that age, harder in the eyes, watching Mara across thirty feet of open water with the expression of someone who had already decided how the next five minutes would go and was merely waiting for the world to catch up.

They came alongside. Teo caught the line Mara threw, made it fast with hands that remembered the work even after everything, and climbed aboard first. Ines followed. Neither spoke until they were in the shadow of the deckhouse, away from the lanterns, and even then Teo only said:

"You got my note."

"I got your note." She'd found it folded inside the tide table he'd left in her chart drawer — a trick so old it circled back around to clever, because no one checked a chart drawer anymore. *Your captain is scheduled to become ballast. The Nakamura woman knows where. Midnight. Come alone or don't come.* "You could have just knocked."

"Knocking gets people thrown overboard." He glanced at the deckhouse, at the dark windows of her cabin. "Okafor aboard?"

"Asleep. Or pretending. His men have the midwatch." She turned to Ines. "You brought the documents?"

Ines reached into her jacket and drew out a waterproof packet, oilcloth over oilcloth, tied with waxed cord. She held it against her body the way a mother holds a child in a crowd — visible, untouchable. "The manifest copies. The logbook. Pages from the vault ledgers." Her voice was low and level, but Mara heard the strain under it, the sound of a woman who had been carrying more than paper for six tides now. "I'm not handing them over. Before you ask. I've heard what happens to things I hand over."

"What happened to your cache wasn't my doing."

"No," Ines agreed. "It was Renn's doing, sold to your fleet, bought with Vance's money. Which makes it your problem whether or not it was your order." She tilted her head. "That's usually how command works. I've read about it."

Teo made a small sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. Mara let the insult sit between them, weighing it, and found — to her own irritation — that she didn't mind it. The girl had earned her contempt honestly. Most people flattered their way into rooms; this one had nearly drowned her way in.

"The ledgers," Mara said. "I need to know what's in them. Not for Vance. For me."

Ines studied her for a long moment. Then she loosened the cord, peeled back the oilskin, and passed a single folded page across the space between them.

Mara read it by feel more than light, tilting it toward the faint glow from the binnacle lamp. Names. Dates. Amounts. The Meridian Star's insurance settlement routed through a shipping concern she recognized because her own first berth had been on one of its coastal runners. Colonial funds embezzled, scuttled, laundered into stone mansions and sugar land and — there it was, third column, circled in faded ink — the Vance family holdings, going back four generations. Half the old families of Isla Verde were on that page. It wasn't a ledger. It was a genealogy of theft.

"My grandmother's maiden name is in here," Mara said quietly.

"I know," Ines said. "I looked you up."

Of course she had. Mara handed the page back. "Vance ordered me to recover the manifest first. Before anything else. Before the gold, before the cargo. I asked why and he told me history was heavy and needed handling." She looked out at the harbor lights. "He wasn't going to preserve it. He was going to burn it. And then he was going to burn the ship it came from, and the divers who brought it up, and the captain who supervised them." She paused. "Me."

"You're sure?" Teo asked. Not skeptical — hopeful, in the terrible way that a man hopes for confirmation of his worst reading of the world because being right hurts less than being fooled twice.

"Renn came aboard today with engine grease on his shoulders and no engine trouble to show for it. Okafor moved my watch without asking. There are explosives in my hold under a lock I don't hold the key to." She recited it flatly, like a cargo manifest. "I've been sunk before, Brandt. I remember what the water feels like coming in."

* * *

They talked for two hours in the shadow of the deckhouse, voices pitched under the wind, and what emerged was less a plan than a mutual disarmament.

Mara laid out what she knew: the demolition schedule, the charge placements Vance's engineer had marked on charts she'd photographed and hidden in the false bottom of her sea chest, the timing of the supply launch that would bring the detonators aboard tomorrow evening. Ines laid out what she had: the documents, Doña Imelda Cruz's corroboration, and a correspondent from the mainland papers who would be on the Friday ferry whether anyone escorted her or not. Teo laid out what he could do: the underwater passage, the vault room, the final recovery run — if there was going to be one.

"And if we get it all out," Ines said, "what then? We split it? The gold?"

"No," Mara said.

Ines raised an eyebrow.

"There is no split." Mara leaned forward, elbows on knees, and let herself say the thing she'd been carrying since the interrogation aboard this very deck, when the Nakamura girl had looked at her with those patient, dissecting eyes and planted the first seed of doubt about Aurelio Vance. "There's no fortune at the end of this. There's a trial, maybe, if the federal inspector does his job. There's restitution claims that'll take ten years to process and lawyers who'll eat most of it. Whatever specie comes out of that cave goes into evidence lockers, not pockets. If you're in this for the gold, walk now, because I will personally sink any boat trying to leave that site with contraband aboard. That hasn't changed since the day I took Vance's concession."

"That's the first honest thing anyone's paid me this month," Ines said. "So we split nothing."

"We split nothing," Mara confirmed. "We split survival. You get your father's name cleared. I get to not be murdered by my employer. Brandt gets—" she glanced at him.

"A clean job," Teo said. "One. That's all I ever wanted. One job where nobody lies to anybody and everybody goes home."

"Modest," Ines murmured.

"It's the most expensive thing on the island," Teo said. "Nobody sells it."

Mara almost smiled. Almost. "Terms, then. First: no one acts alone. Not Brandt, not you, not me. Second: the documents stay on your body, Nakamura, until they're in the correspondent's hands or the inspector's. Third: if any one of us is taken, the other two finish it. No rescue attempts, no negotiations. Vance wins the moment we start trading ourselves back."

"And fourth?" Ines asked.

Mara hesitated. This was the part she'd turned over all evening, the part that tasted like ash and like clean air at once. "Fourth. When it's done — whatever's done — I testify. Publicly. On the record. What Vance ordered, what I carried out, what I suspected and didn't stop." She met Ines's eyes. "I'm not asking forgiveness. I'm asking to be believed, and belief has to be earned in front of witnesses."

The wind gusted off the harbor, carrying the smell of bilge and diesel and distant rain. Ines looked at her for a long time — the same long look from the interrogation, but changed now, the dissection replaced by something more dangerous: assessment. A captain assessing another captain.

"My father used to say the sea doesn't forgive," Ines said finally. "It just forgets slower than people do. You're asking the island to do what the sea won't."

"I'm asking it to remember accurately," Mara said. "That's different."

Ines put out her hand. It was rope-hard and salt-cracked, a working hand, a salvor's hand, and Mara took it and found the grip steady and ungenerous and true.

"Alliances of convenience," Ines said. "My father warned me about those. He said they dissolve exactly when you need them most."

"He was right," Mara said. "Which is why ours isn't convenient. It's necessary. Those hold better."

* * *

They left separately, as agreed — Teo first, sliding down the ladder into the skiff with the economy of a man who had spent years leaving ships quietly; then Ines, pausing at the rail with the document packet pressed flat against her ribs.

"For what it's worth," Ines said, not looking at her, "the page with your grandmother's name on it. Imelda Cruz says the island remembers too. The scuttling. The night the Meridian Star went down with her holds full and her crew paid off suspiciously well. The old families built their houses on it and called the stories superstition." She turned. "You're not the only one who wants the record straight. You're just the only one with a flagship."

Mara watched the skiff fade into the dark, running without lights toward the cliffs, and stood a long while at the rail after it vanished.

Below her, in the locked hold, Vance's explosives waited with the patience of all ordnance. Above her, Okafor's men walked the midwatch with boots too careful, too quiet. Somewhere inland, a magistrate who had never once gotten his hands wet was arranging the drowning of everyone who had.

Mara Voss went to her cabin, took the demolition charts from the false bottom of her sea chest, and began — methodically, precisely, with a captain's love of a working plan — to figure out how to sabotage her own ship's destruction.

By dawn she had the answer, and it required her to be somewhere she had sworn she would never be again: standing beside Ines Nakamura in the ruins below the cliff, with the sixth tide coming in and the whole rigged architecture of Aurelio Vance's island beginning, at last, to groan.
