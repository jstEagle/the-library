## 28. The Plan of Three Currents

The meeting place was Teo's idea, because everything reckless was Teo's idea, and this one at least had the virtue of being reckless in a defensible direction: the old salt works on the island's windward shore, abandoned since before Vance had learned to sign his own name with a magistrate's flourish. Its stone basins still held rainwater gone green as bottle glass, and its walls were thick enough that even if someone stood outside with an ear pressed to the rock, they would hear nothing but the sea working the reef.

Ines arrived first. She always arrived first now; it was a habit her father had drilled into her before she was old enough to resent it — *the person who arrives last is the person who has already lost the argument* — and she sat on the rim of a drying basin with her boots dangling over green water and let herself feel, for exactly one minute, how absurd it all was. Three days ago these people had been her enemies. Two days ago one of them had been her jailer. Now she was waiting for them in a ruin that smelled of brine and rust, about to plan an operation against the most powerful man on Isla Verde with nothing but a stolen logbook, a stack of copied pages sewn into her jacket lining, and a conviction so raw it felt less like hope than like an open wound that refused to close.

Mara Voss came down the goat path from the north, moving with the unhurried economy of a woman who had spent thirty years making decks obey her. She wore no uniform today, no Corvus colors — just salt-stained canvas and a face that gave away nothing except, when her eyes found Ines's, the faintest acknowledgment that they were both still alive, which between the two of them counted as warmth.

Teo came last, limping slightly where the collapse had bruised him through to the bone, carrying a bundle wrapped in oilcloth that he set down on the basin's rim with the reverence of a man laying out relics.

"Charts," he said. "Every current table I could copy from the *Corvus Star*'s locker before anyone noticed the locker was light."

"You stole from your own ship," Voss said.

"I stole from *her* ship." He tipped his chin at Ines. "I just work for her now. Apparently for free."

"Not free," Ines said. "For clean. That's worth more."

He looked at her a moment too long, and she saw him turn the word over — *clean* — the way he'd been turning it over since the night he'd confessed what he'd done on the cover-up dive, what his signature of approval had cost her father. Guilt sat differently on Teo than it did on other men. On other men it was a coat to be taken off indoors. On Teo it was load-bearing; remove it and she suspected the whole structure would come down.

Voss unrolled the charts across the flat stone between them and weighted the corners with four pieces of coral, and for a while none of them spoke. The chart showed the coast below the cliffs, the cave mouth, the deep channel where the *Corvus Star* rode at anchor, and — drawn in Voss's own hand, in pencil so fresh it still shone — the positions of the demolition crew's equipment caches, marked two days ago from the flagship's rail.

"Vance moved up the schedule," Voss said without preamble. "My first mate tells me charges go in tomorrow afternoon. Detonation the morning after — which is the sixth tide, or near enough that it doesn't matter. He's not waiting for the permits to vest. He wants the site gone before anyone can ask why a magistrate is so eager to erase it."

"He knows you know," Ines said.

"He suspects everyone knows everything. It's what makes him manageable." Voss's mouth did something that was almost a smile and entirely without humor. "What he doesn't know is that his first mate sold me to him for a purse and a promise, and that Renn told me the promise was worthless, and that I believed Renn, which should tell you what my opinion of my own crew has become."

"So we have one day," Ines said. "Maybe two."

"We have one day," Voss confirmed, "and three problems, and I've been awake since the mutiny thinking about how they fit together. So listen, because I'll only say this once."

* * *

She laid it out the way she must once have laid out salvage operations to her officers — brisk, geometric, leaving no room for sentiment — and Ines listened with half her mind and watched with the other half, because there was something instructive in watching Mara Voss plan. The woman thought in currents. Not metaphorically; literally. Every element of the plan was assigned a current, a tide, a window in which the water itself would do the work that muscle couldn't.

"Three prongs," Voss said, tapping the chart. "Three currents. First current: me. Tomorrow at first light I put the *Corvus Star* under way and run her slow across the demolition zone's approach — engine trouble, a fouled anchor, whatever story keeps me on their radio for the longest hours of the day. Every hour their launch is circling me is an hour nobody's counting heads at the cliff top. I can't stop the charges going in. I can make sure fewer eyes are watching them go in."

"And when they realize you're stalling?" Teo asked.

"They won't. Stalling is indistinguishable from incompetence if you commit to it fully, and I have thirty years of practice committing." She tapped again, further along the coast. "Second current: Brandt. The underwater passage off the shelf reef — the one you used on the fourth tide. The collapse shifted it, but the survey lines are still set and the squeeze at the forty-meter mark opened up when the charges testing cracked the far wall. You go in on the morning ebb, when the outflow runs hardest and any diver Vance posts on the outer mouth will be fighting the current instead of watching it. You reach the vault room, you take the ledgers, the letters, every scrap of paper — paper only, nothing else — and you come back the same way."

"And the gold," Teo said.

"The gold stays."

Ines heard the words land in Teo like stones dropped down a well. She knew what they cost him. She knew what they cost herself, if she was honest — she had lain awake two nights doing arithmetic on that gold, the way a starving woman does arithmetic on bread behind glass. Enough specie to erase every debt, buy back every permit, fund ten expeditions under her own name with no magistrate's thumb on the scale. It was sitting forty meters under a collapsing cave, and by tomorrow night it would be rubble, unrecoverable for a generation, maybe forever.

"The gold stays," Ines said aloud, and was surprised at how steady her voice came out. "It's ballast. Ballast kills divers. And it's the one thing Vance will tear the island apart looking for if he learns it ever existed. Paper convicts him. Gold just makes him desperate, and desperate men with explosives are worse than guilty ones."

Teo exhaled slowly through his nose. "You're both insane," he said, without heat. "Fine. Third current?"

Voss looked at Ines.

"Third current is mine," Ines said, because saying it out loud made it real, and real things could be planned for. "While you two hold the water, I go overland. East ridge trail through the karst, down to the mangrove ford, then the old plantation road to Puerto Sombra. The mainland ferry docks Thursday at dawn — the *Alba*, the packet boat, six-hour crossing. Imelda Cruz has already sent word to a correspondent from the *Meridian Gazette* who'll be aboard. I hand him the originals — the manifest copies, the captain's log, the ledgers, Kenji's letters — with the naval inspector's name already written in the margin, because Imelda says the inspector owes her family a debt older than the republic. By the time Vance's launch reaches the dock, it's not evidence anymore. It's news."

"It's thirty kilometers of karst and mangrove," Voss said, "with Renn somewhere on it, because Renn knows the overland trails better than anyone on this island, and Renn knows you'll take them."

"I know," Ines said. "That's why I'm taking the trails Renn doesn't know. My father surveyed this island's interior twenty years ago, before anyone cared what was inland. His field notes are in my kit. There's a sinkhole route through the karst that isn't on any map the magistrate's office owns."

Voss studied her for a long moment — the look of a captain assessing whether a hull would hold sea state she couldn't control. "You understand that if any one of the three currents fails, the other two drown."

"I understand that's been true since the storm gave back the cave."

* * *

They worked through the afternoon, and the salt works filled with the quiet industry of people building something out of almost nothing. Voss redrew the chart in triplicate — one copy each, memorize and burn — marking the demolition crew's watch rotations, the gaps between their supply launches, the exact bearing of the *Corvus Star*'s planned obstruction run. Teo checked and rechecked his dive gear with the tenderness of a man handling the only honest thing he owned: regulators stripped and rebuilt twice, the backup strapped where his hands would find it blind, line reels flaked and coiled so they'd pay out without a kink at depth. Ines sat apart with her father's field notes spread across her knees and built her route the way he had taught her to build anything — from the water backward, because water was patient and roads were not.

*Start from the flood,* he used to say, chalk in hand, young Ines cross-legged on the survey station floor. *Everything on a coast starts from the flood. Ask what the water is doing first, and the land will tell you the rest.*

She had hated those lessons once. She had sat in that room with her arms crossed and her jaw set, furious at a man who loved tide tables more than he seemed to love her, who taught her sediment reading the way other fathers taught prayers — as inheritance, as obligation, as the only language he trusted himself to speak. She had left that room at nineteen and told herself she'd never use any of it.

Now she traced his faded pencil lines through the karst and felt the strangest sensation: that the wilderness ahead of her was not empty ground but a conversation, decades old, that she was finally willing to answer.

By dusk the plan had flesh. Voss would make her obstruction run at first light and hold the fleet's attention through the midmorning supply window. Teo would drop off the shelf reef at slack-before-ebb and be in the vault room within the hour, out again before the ebb turned hostile. Ines would leave tonight — tonight, not tomorrow, putting the karst between herself and the cliff country before Vance's men woke up — and push through the sinkhole route by torchlight, sleeping one hour at the dry cave above the second ford, reaching Puerto Sombra with darkness still to spare before the ferry.

They burned the extra charts in the old lime kiln and watched the ash rise on the windward wind.

"One thing more," Voss said, as the fire died. She reached inside her coat and brought out a small object, setting it on the stone between them: a brass diving knife, Corvus-stamped, worn smooth at the grip. "Renn carried this the day he sold your cache. I took it off him when he tried to sell me next. I'm told returning stolen property is customary."

Teo picked it up and turned it over once, and Ines watched something move behind his face — not gratitude, exactly. Recognition. The knife was proof that Renn had played them all, and proof that Voss had chosen, somewhere in the last two days, to stop playing.

"Captain," Teo said, "when this is done—"

"When this is done," Voss cut in, "you'll owe me a drink and I'll owe you an apology, and we'll see which debt is heavier. Until then, nobody thanks anybody. Thanks are for funerals and paydays, and I intend to collect on neither."

* * *

Ines left the salt works alone, as agreed, walking the goat path south until the ruin was a shadow behind her and the cliffs rose black against the last of the light. Her pack was lighter than it had been in weeks — she'd left the climbing gear, left the spare line, left everything that wasn't documents, water, torch, and her father's notes — and still it felt heavy, because of what was sewn into the lining. Six years of her father's silence. One forged report. A ledger of names that went back further than her own life. All of it pressed against her spine with every step, the way a debt presses, the way a promise presses.

At the cliff top she stopped once and looked back at the water. Far out, past the reef line, the riding lights of the *Corvus Star* burned small and patient, and beyond them, invisible in the dark, the cave mouth waited under its fathoms with its paper ghost and its drowned gold and its generation of sealed secrets. Tomorrow the water would begin giving all of it back — or burying it forever. There would be no third option. The tides didn't offer thirds.

*Six usable tides,* she had calculated in another life, five chapters of exhaustion ago. This was the last of them, and it would be spent running.

She touched the jacket lining once, feeling the stiff rectangle of the copied manifest, and started east toward the karst, toward the sinkholes, toward the ferry that would carry the truth off this island or die trying.

Behind her, far out on the dark water, the *Corvus Star* began to move.
