## 29. Demolition Day

Mara Voss had commanded the *Corvus Queen* through two hurricanes, a mutiny off the Serrana Banks, and one season of running guns she did not like to remember. She knew the sound a ship made when something was wrong with it — the small wrongness in the rigging's voice, the half-tone shift in the engine's rhythm, the way a crew's footsteps changed when they were afraid of their captain or ashamed in front of her. She had built her career on hearing what other people missed.

So when the *Pelayo* — Vance's survey tender, a squat grey vessel with too much machinery on its deck for honest surveying — came around the northern headland at half past five in the morning, Mara heard it before she saw it. Not the engines. The absence of them. The tender had cut power a full mile out and was drifting in on the tide the way a man walks soft across a floor he doesn't own.

She stood at the rail of the *Corvus Queen*'s aft deck with a cup of coffee going cold in her hand and watched the dawn come up the color of old pewter, and she did the arithmetic she had been refusing to do for two days.

The permits said demolition could not begin before the sixth tide. The permits were the whole legal architecture of this operation, the paper wall Vance had built to keep the federal inspectors and the archaeological societies and the mainland press on the far side of it. Vance loved paper. Vance hid behind paper the way a coward hides behind a bigger man. He would no sooner violate his own permits than he would walk into the sea cave himself.

Which meant the *Pelayo* drifting in at half past five, dark and quiet, with its crane arm already rigged, was not there to survey anything.

"Quist," she said, not raising her voice.

Her first mate appeared at the hatch the way he always did, a big flat-faced man with the patient stillness of a dockyard crane. "Captain."

"Get the glass on the tender. Tell me what's on her deck."

Quist took the binoculars from the bracket, braced his elbows on the rail, and was quiet long enough that Mara's coffee went from cold to an insult.

"Crate," he said finally. "Wooden, about the size of a coffin. Two men handling it like it's full of eggs. And a third man I don't know. Civvies. Hard hats."

"Hard hats."

"Yellow ones. And coils of wire, captain. A lot of wire."

Mara looked at the water. The ebb was just beginning to make, the current sliding south along the cliff face toward the cave mouth, and somewhere down in that drowned dark Teo Brandt was running his lines toward the vault room with the last of the ledgers, betting his life on the hours she was supposed to buy him.

Vance was not going to wait for the sixth tide. Vance was going to blow the cave now, this morning, while the evidence was still inside it and the witnesses were still scattered and the story could still be buried under a hundred tons of cliff.

"Signal the tender," she said. "Ask them their business in our concession waters."

Quist relayed it. The answer came back in under a minute, relayed by a voice Mara didn't recognize, flat and official: *Acting on direct order of Magistrate Vance. Emergency structural assessment. All vessels to hold clear.*

"Structural assessment," Mara repeated. "With a coffin full of wire."

"That's what they said."

She drank the cold coffee anyway, because her father had been a fisherman who believed waste was a sin, and because she wanted her hands to have something to do that wasn't shaking. Then she went below to the chart table, locked the door, and took out the thing she had been carrying for three days like a stone in her boot.

It was a page of the Meridian Star's cargo manifest — a copy Ines Nakamura had let her see and not keep, back when they were still enemies, back before the arithmetic of survival had made them something else. Vance's handwriting crawled down the margin in that careful, clerkish script of his. *Survey amended per field conditions.* Dated eleven years before he held office. Dated, if the ledgers were true, three weeks after the *Meridian Star* went down with the colonial treasury in her hold.

Mara had grown up in the shadow of the old families. She had gone to school with Vance's nieces. She had watched men like him take the island's history the way gulls take fish from a drying net — casually, endlessly, with the serene entitlement of creatures who have never once been told no.

She had told herself, for eleven years, that she was a captain, not a judge. That her job was the ship and the crew and the tide, and the rest was weather that belonged to someone else's sky.

The *Pelayo* drifted closer. On her deck, in the grey light, men in yellow hard hats were lowering a wooden crate with the tenderness of pallbearers.

* * *

By seven the demolition crew had established itself on the cliff top above the cave — six men, a compressor, a spool of detonation wire, and a foreman with a clipboard who answered to a name that Mara was certain was false. She watched them through the glass from the *Corvus Queen*'s bridge while her crew went about the morning's work around her, and she made herself look at each man's face and ask the question she had been avoiding since the letter from her sister-in-law in the capital had arrived three weeks ago: *Mara, what exactly is it you think you're salvaging?*

The answer was: she didn't know anymore. That was the truth of it. Somewhere between the manifest and the interrogation of Ines Nakamura aboard her own deck — the girl with the tide tables and the eyes like her father's, who had turned Mara's own questions back on her like a mirror held up in a dark room — the job had stopped being a job.

Vance wanted the vault. Vance wanted the ledgers. And when he had them, Mara had understood for two days now, Vance would want the *Corvus Queen* at the bottom of the channel with her captain aboard, because Mara Voss had seen the handwriting and asked one question too many, and Aurelio Vance did not keep loose ends. He sank them.

"Captain." Quist again, at the bridge door. "Brandt's skiff just came out of the cove. Heading for the cliff cache."

Mara took the glass. Teo Brandt stood in the stern of the little boat, gaunt and salt-rimed, hauling on the tiller like a man who had made his peace with the water and was asking only that the water make its peace with him. Behind him, half-hidden under tarpaulin, the shape of a strongbox.

The ledgers. The last of them. Everything.

And between Teo and the mainland ferry, six miles of open water with the *Pelayo* now anchored square in the middle of it.

"Quist. How many of the crew would follow orders they didn't like?"

Quist considered this with the gravity he gave all questions. "Depends on the order."

"An order to let the magistrate's men aboard."

The silence went on long enough to be an answer. Mara set down the glass.

She had known, in the way captains know things they refuse to say aloud, that the crew was bought. Not all of them — old Ferro in the engine room would follow her into a fire, and the boy Pelli would follow Ferro — but the deckhands had been drinking Vance's money for a month, small sums, casual sums, the kind a man accepts without admitting he's accepted anything. She had watched it happen the way you watch rust spread along a hull. Slow. Certain. Beneath the paint line where no one inspects.

And she had done nothing, because doing something meant naming it, and naming it meant a confrontation she might lose, and a captain who loses a confrontation loses the ship.

Well. The rust had reached the frame.

"Get me the tender on the radio," she said. "And then get my boat in the water."

* * *

The *Pelayo*'s foreman — false name, false clipboard, true explosives — met her at the tender's rail with the smile of a man who has already been paid.

"Captain Voss. The magistrate sends his regards."

"The magistrate can send his regards from the far end of a very long pier." She stepped aboard without invitation, because the first rule of boarding a hostile deck was to act like you owned it. "Those are my concession waters. Those are my permits. Nothing detonates on that cliff before the sixth tide or I will put this tender under arrest and tow it to the harbor myself, and the magistrate can explain to the federal inspector why his own demolition crew is sitting in impound."

The foreman's smile didn't move, which was how she knew he'd been told to expect her. "Captain, I have written orders. Signed. Dated this morning."

"Then your signature is worth the paper it's on, because the permits say—"

"The permits," the foreman said pleasantly, "are being amended. As of this morning. You might want to check your radio. There's a message for you from the magistrate's office."

There was. She played it in the tender's cabin, standing rigid while a secretary's recorded voice read out, in the bloodless grammar of bureaucracy, the dissolution of the Corvus Fleet's operational authority — *pending review of safety compliance* — and the transfer of site control to the magistrate's office, effective immediately. Signed. Dated. Filed at six o'clock that morning, which meant Vance had drafted it last night, which meant he had planned this before dawn, which meant everything she was doing now he had already counted on.

The foreman watched her with his clipboard and his smile. "So you understand, Captain. You're welcome to observe. From a distance."

She understood. She understood that the paper wall had been dismantled overnight and rebuilt facing the other way, and that the only thing standing between the charges on the cliff and the vault room below it — where Teo Brandt was, at this moment, thirty fathoms down in the dark with the last proof of everything — was whatever she could improvise in the next hour.

She thought about Ines Nakamura's face across the chart table. *You know what he buried,* the girl had said. *The only question left is whether you're the kind of woman who helps him keep it buried.*

Mara had not answered then. She answered now, with the only currency she had left.

* * *

The detonators were in a wooden crate the size of a coffin, and the crate sat on the cliff top under a tarpaulin, guarded by one bored man with a rifle he carried like a fishing rod. The firing wire ran from the crate down the cliff path to the compressor station, and from the compressor station the foreman would, in his own good time, run the test circuit and begin placing the charges in the seam above the cave mouth.

Mara went up the cliff path alone, in her working clothes, with a toolbag over her shoulder and the expression of a woman who intended to inspect the operation and file a complaint. The guard let her pass because she was, after all, still — on paper that had not yet caught up with the morning — the concession captain, and because men do not stop women carrying toolbags. Toolbags are furniture. Toolbags are weather.

The detonators were electric blasting caps, twenty of them, coiled in wax paper like a nest of silver eels. She had rigged charges in her gun-running years; she knew the anatomy of the thing better than the foreman did. An electric cap fires when current crosses the bridge wire. A cap that has been soaked, corroded, or subtly shorted fires when it feels like it — which is to say, never, or worse, unpredictably, and no demolition man alive will arm a circuit he doesn't trust.

She could not steal the crate. The theft would be discovered within the hour and the foreman would simply radio for more caps, and the delay would cost her nothing she could keep.

So she did not steal them. She killed them.

It took eleven minutes, crouched behind the crate with her body angled to block the guard's line of sight, her hands moving with the unhurried precision of a woman reorganizing her toolbag. A sliver of conductive foil, slipped between the leg wires of each cap where it would read as insulation damage. A saltwater damp cloth, wrung out over the wax paper — not enough to be obvious, just enough to begin the slow work of corrosion at every solder joint. Twenty caps, one by one, each one a small silver eel going quietly dead in its nest.

When the foreman ran his test circuit — and he would, because careful men test — the bridge wires would read open. Faulty stock. Bad batch. And a careful man does not detonate a cliff with a bad batch. A careful man waits for replacement caps.

And replacement caps, Mara intended to make certain, would take a very long time to arrive.

She was closing the crate, wiping the salt damp from her hands, when the guard's radio crackled and she heard the foreman's voice, thin with distance: *Send the captain's boat back. Tell Voss the magistrate wants her aboard the flagship. And tell the crew — new orders. Nobody leaves the site.*

The guard looked at her. She looked at the guard. And she saw it happen in his face — the slow shift from deference to arithmetic, the moment a bought man remembers what he was bought with and starts counting whether it was enough.

"Captain," he said, not unkindly, "you're to wait here."

* * *

They came for her at noon.

Not the foreman — the foreman had no authority over her and knew it. They sent Quist. Her own first mate, her own boat, her own crew, standing on the cliff path below her with his big flat face carefully empty, and behind him four deckhands who would not meet her eyes.

"Captain," Quist said. "The magistrate's office has ordered you detained pending the compliance review. They say you interfered with the demolition operation."

"Did they say that, Quist? Or did you?"

His jaw worked. "Captain, I—"

"Eleven years, Quist. Eleven years and you won't look at me."

"I'm looking at you." He was. That was the worst of it — he made himself, and there was something in his face that was not treachery but shame, the shame of a man doing a thing he will spend the rest of his life explaining badly. "They've got Pelli's wages and Ferro's license, Captain. They've got everything. They told us it was just until the review."

"Everything," Mara said, "is exactly what it's never just until."

She could have fought. She thought about it, standing on the cliff top with the sea going silver and violent below her and the dead detonators sleeping in their crate six feet away. She was stronger than two of them and meaner than all of them, and there was a boat hook within reach, and for three full seconds she let herself feel the whole animal architecture of the fight.

Then she looked south, toward the open water, toward the ferry lane, and thought: *every hour.*

That was the arithmetic. That was all of it, now. Teo Brandt needed hours, and Ines Nakamura needed hours, and the dead detonators had bought perhaps one day — two if the foreman was careful, and he was careful, thank God, he was a careful man — and every hour she could stand between Vance's machinery and that cave mouth was an hour purchased with the only coin she had left, which was herself.

A captain's last command, she thought, is the one she gives with her own body.

"Quist." She held out her hands, wrists together, an old joke between them from the gun-running years, when they'd rehearsed surrendering to customs cutters as a drill. He didn't take them. "Don't. Just — walk me down. And Quist? When this is over, and it will be over, one way or the other, before the week is out — check the bilge of the *Queen*, portside, under the fuel bladder. There's a packet there with your name on it. Wages, letters, the deed to your mother's house that I've been holding since the Serrana year. It's yours whether I'm standing or not."

"Captain—"

"That's an order, First Mate."

He walked her down the cliff path with his hands empty at his sides and his head down, and the deckhands fell in behind them like a funeral, and below them the sea heaved against the cliffs where the *Meridian Star* had lain for a hundred and forty years, keeping her secrets, waiting — as the sea always waits — to see what people were actually made of.

At the *Pelayo*'s rail, the foreman was waiting with his clipboard and his smile, and behind him, on the tender's deck, a new crate was being swung ashore. Fresh caps. The radio had been faster than she'd hoped.

Two hours, then. Maybe three.

Mara lifted her chin and let them take her below, and through the porthole of the cabin they locked her in, she watched the cliff top where men in yellow hard hats unrolled new coils of silver wire — and she thought of a girl with tide tables in her blood, running for the ferry through six miles of mangrove and karst, and she whispered the only prayer she owned, the one her father the fisherman had taught her, which was not a prayer to any god at all:

*Run, girl. The tide's on your side. I bought it for you.*
