## 30. Last Dive

The charges started at dawn, or what passed for dawn under a sky the color of wet slate.

Teo felt the first one through the soles of his boots before he heard it — a deep, bone-shaking thud that traveled up through the limestone of the headland and into his teeth. He was crouched at the mouth of the sea cave with his dive gear half-rigged, and the impact made the coils of line at his feet shiver like living things.

"Too early," he said. "Vance isn't supposed to blow the outer shelf until the vault's confirmed clear."

"He's not following anyone's schedule anymore," Ines said. She was at the tide line, hauling their skiff higher up the shingle, her movements quick and precise the way they always got when fear got worked down into something usable. "Voss bought us hours, not a day. Maybe not even that. The detonators she sabotaged — her first mate will find replacements. He's already been paid."

Teo looked at the cave. The outer chamber was flooded to within a meter of the ceiling now, the spring tide pushing gray-green water deep into the rock throat of the passage beyond. Somewhere past that flooded throat, past the squeeze they'd named the Needle, past the collapse zone from the fourth tide, lay the captain's vault — the ledgers, the letters, the proof that would break Aurelio Vance.

And the gold.

He could feel the gold the way you felt a bad tooth. Nineteen crates of Spanish colonial specie, minted silver and gold coin fused into bricks of coral and concretion, sitting in the dark behind a door that was about to become a tomb. Enough money to buy back every debt Teo Brandt had ever signed his name to. Enough to start over in a place where nobody knew what he'd done on the cover-up dive, what he'd written on that worthless-find report, what he'd traded his integrity for and never even gotten paid in full.

"Teo." Ines's voice cut across his thinking. "Look at me. You don't have to go. We can wait for the ebb, take the long passage around the collapse —"

"The long passage doesn't exist anymore. The fourth-tide collapse sealed it. You know that. I mapped it myself." He pulled his hood up over his hair, and the world went small and rubber-smelling and familiar. "It's the Needle or nothing. And the Needle is mine. I'm the only one of us who's been through it twice."

"Three times," she said quietly.

"Three times." He almost smiled. "See? I'm getting good at it."

The second charge went off, closer, somewhere up the headland where Vance's demolition crew was working the cliff face above the cave mouth. Rock dust sifted down through the water like brown snow. Ines flinched; Teo didn't. He'd spent eleven years on Corvus boats listening to things blow up. He knew the sound of demolition the way sailors knew weather.

What he didn't know — what he'd never learned in eleven years — was how to be the kind of man who did one clean thing at the end of a dirty life.

He was about to find out.

* * *

The plan was simple, which was the only kind of plan that worked underwater. Ines would run his line from the outer chamber, keeping tension on the guide rope and watching his bubbles. He would go through the Needle on a single tank, no backup, no stage bottle — no weight allowance for anything that wasn't coming back out with him. He would fill the recovery bag with the ledgers, the letters, the manifest pages they hadn't already copied. He would come back through the Needle before his gauge read fifty bar, and if it read fifty bar and he wasn't through, he would drop the bag and swim.

That was the part Ines had made him say out loud, twice, with his hand on the line.

"Drop the bag and swim," he'd said. "I know."

"Say it again."

"Ines."

"Say it again, Teo."

"Drop the bag and swim," he'd said, and she'd held his gaze for three full seconds, and whatever she'd seen there had made her look away first.

Now he stood at the edge of the flooded chamber, regulator in his mouth, and did the last thing divers did before the dark: he thought about his breathing. Slow in. Slower out. The water took him at the ankles, the knees, the waist, cold enough to make his chest seize and then release. The third charge went off while he was still in the shallows, and this one he heard clearly — a crack and a rumble and then a sound like the whole island clearing its throat — and the water around him shuddered, and somewhere deep in the rock, something old and load-bearing shifted its weight.

He went under.

* * *

The outer chamber was a cathedral with the lights out. His dive light carved a cone through the murk, catching the ghost-shapes of the Meridian Star's ribs — black timbers furred with marine growth, the hull opened like a ribcage, the cargo crates still lashed in their rows as if the crew had only stepped ashore. He'd been in here a dozen times now and it still did something to him, this preserved moment, this ship that had been murdered and buried and kept.

He followed his own line to the Needle. The squeeze was exactly what he'd named it: a limestone throat barely wide enough for a man with a tank, a meter and a half of rock you went through on faith and exhalation, exhaling to flatten yourself, pulling with fingertips, the line singing taut behind him. First time through, three tides ago, he'd panicked — a small, controlled, professional panic, but panic — when his tank valve had scraped the ceiling and the sound of it had filled his mask like a scream. Second time, he'd gone through angry, and angry was better. Angry was fuel.

Today he went through calm, and calm was best of all, because calm was what you had left when you'd finally stopped lying to yourself about what you were doing down here.

He wasn't doing it for the gold. He'd known that since the fifth tide, since he'd sat in the vault room with the ledgers open on his knees and read his own name in a Corvus dive log dated six years back, entered in Mara Voss's handwriting: *T. Brandt, bottom survey, site value assessed nil, report filed.* His name in the machinery of the thing that had broken Kenji Nakamura. He wasn't doing it for redemption either — he'd stopped believing in redemption around the same age he'd started believing in tide tables. He was doing it because it was the job, and it was clean, and a man got maybe one clean job in a life, and this was his, and he was going to finish it if the island came down on his head.

The Needle spat him into the inner passage. The collapse zone from the fourth tide was ahead, a jumble of fallen slabs with a gap at the top that they'd widened with hand tools across two dives. He squeezed through it sideways, tank scraping, light beam dancing over fresh fractures in the rock — and stopped.

The fractures were new. Not fourth-tide new. *Today* new. Thin white cracks ran up through the ceiling of the passage like frost on a window, and from somewhere above and behind him — back toward the outer chamber, back toward Vance's charges — came a sound he knew from bad nights on Corvus wrecks: the deep, patient creak of rock deciding what it was going to do.

The spring tide was pushing water into places it had never been. The explosives were shaking loose what the water had begun. The cave was coming down around the vault, and it was going to come down whether Teo Brandt was inside it or not.

He checked his gauge. Two hundred ten bar. He kicked for the vault room.

* * *

The captain's vault was a stone-lined cavity behind the collapsed stern cabin, its iron door long since pried, its contents laid out in the order they'd catalogued them on the fifth tide: ledgers wrapped in oilcloth, the letter bundle, the specie crates stacked against the far wall where the Corvus divers hadn't reached and now never would.

He went to the ledgers first. No hesitation, no ceremony. He'd made his peace with the arithmetic of it on the swim in, and the arithmetic was simple: the bag held twenty kilos of comfort. His lungs and his tank held less. The ledgers were the case. The ledgers were Kenji Nakamura's name written back into the world. The ledgers were the thing that, carried overland to a ferry dock and a correspondent's hands, would do what six years of Teo Brandt's silence had not.

He wrapped them. Oilcloth, cord, into the bag. The letters. The final manifest pages. His hands were steady, which surprised him. His hands were always steady underwater; it was the rest of him that shook.

Then he turned to the gold.

One crate had broken open on the fifth tide — Voss's divers' crowbar work, or the collapse's — and the coins had spilled across the stone floor in a fan, gold and silver catching his light and throwing it back warm, the way treasure was supposed to, the way it always did in the pictures. Escudos and pieces of eight, mint faces blurred by two centuries of salt, and every one of them worth more than Teo Brandt made in a season of honest diving, which was to say every one of them worth more than Teo Brandt had ever made at all.

He knelt there. He had perhaps ninety seconds of slack in his plan and he spent forty of them kneeling in front of gold, and he was not ashamed of it, because a man who wasn't tempted by it would have been lying, and Teo Brandt had done enough lying for one lifetime.

He thought about the debt on his mother's medical bills, still unpaid, the collection letters that came to a dead woman's address because she'd died before the debt did. He thought about a boat — his own boat, a clean little salvage boat, *no Corvus, no Vance, no favors* — and the thought was so vivid he could smell the paint. He thought about walking into some mainland bank with a single escudo and starting the rest of his life.

He thought about Ines Nakamura, on the line above him right now, watching his bubbles, holding his life in a length of rope, trusting him with the only thing she had left of her father.

The cave creaked. A crack propagated somewhere overhead with a sound like a knuckle popping, and a slurry of silt came down through his light beam, and the decision made itself the way the best ones always did — not as a choice but as a recognition of what he'd already chosen, long ago, on a dive six years back when he'd written *nil* on a survey form and set all of this in motion.

He took one coin.

Just one. Not for the bank. Not for the boat. He took it because a man ought to carry out one piece of the thing he'd helped bury, one piece to hold in his fist on the hard days, one piece to remind him what gold cost. He closed his fist around it — small, heavy, warm-seeming in the cold water — and zipped the bag, and left the rest.

The fan of coins on the stone floor gleamed after him as he turned away, and he did not look back again.

* * *

The Needle was worse on the way out. It was always worse on the way out; you went in on adrenaline and came back on arithmetic, and the arithmetic said his gauge was at one hundred sixty bar and the cave was making noises like a ship breaking up. Halfway through the squeeze his tank valve caught the ceiling again — the same scrape, the same scream of metal on stone — and this time the scrape was followed by a sound he felt rather than heard: a deep, grinding shift, and then silt and grit raining through the water behind him, and the passage he'd come through narrowing by a body's width.

He exhaled, flattened, pulled. The line sang taut behind him — Ines taking up his slack, reading him through the rope the way she'd been taught, the way her father had taught her, tide tables and sediment and now this, the grammar of a line going tight because a man was moving and alive at the other end of it.

His gauge said one hundred ten when he cleared the Needle into the outer chamber. It said ninety when he came up the guide line into the air pocket at the chamber's high corner, where Ines waited in shallower water with the skiff line in her fist and her face above him a pale smear of lantern light.

"Bag," she said, the word distorted through his regulator, and he lifted it, and she hauled it out of the water with both hands, and for one second — one suspended, weightless second — Teo Brandt hung in the water and watched her open it and saw her face do the thing it did: the sharp check, the count, the ledger-keeper's scan.

"Ledgers?" she said.

"All of them."

"The letters?"

"All."

Her eyes went to the bag again, then to him, and he watched her understand — watched her do the arithmetic he'd done on his knees in the vault room, the twenty kilos that weren't there, the gold that was still behind the Needle in a room with a cracking ceiling. Her face went through something fast and complicated and landed on something that was not pity and was not quite gratitude but was adjacent to both.

"Teo —"

"Don't," he said, pulling his regulator out. "It was heavy."

It was a lie and they both knew it was a lie and it was the best lie he'd ever told, because it let them both keep moving.

The fourth charge went off then — close, *inside* the headland close — and the outer chamber answered with a sound like the sea clearing its throat of an anchor. The air pocket shuddered. Water slopped up the chamber walls a half-meter in a single convulsive surge, and from the throat of the Needle came a deep, final, grinding roar, and the silt plume that billowed out of it was the color of the end of things.

Teo looked at the place where the passage had been and understood, with the flat professional calm that was the last thing he had left, that the Needle was gone. Sealed. For a generation, maybe for good. Whatever was still in the vault room — the gold, the crates, the ship's bones — was behind a wall of collapsed limestone now, and no diver was going through it, and no charge was going to open it without bringing the whole headland into the sea.

The Meridian Star had kept its treasure. The island had taken its payment.

"Out," Ines said. "Now. The whole cliff face is letting go — Vance's crew is blowing the shelf above the cave mouth. Out *now*, Teo."

They swam.

* * *

They came up the shingle beach under a sky raining dust. The headland above the cave mouth was bleeding rock — a slow, continuous avalanche of chips and slabs kicked loose by the demolition charges, sliding down the cliff face and plunging into the surf with white concussions. Vance's crew had stopped pretending. They were working the shelf directly above the site now, and from the rhythm of the blasts, they were not cataloguing or careful. They were erasing.

Ines dragged the bag above the tide line and stood over it, breathing hard, her hair plastered to her face, and Teo came up beside her and knelt in the shingle with his tank still on his back because he hadn't decided he was done being a diver yet.

"The ferry," she said. "The correspondent. Imelda's contact boards at dawn tomorrow — today, now, it's today, the tide table doesn't care what day it is." She was already repacking the bag, cinching cords with fingers that didn't shake. "The ledgers go overland tonight. Cross-island, through the mangrove and the karst, to the ferry dock on the far side. It's the only way — the coast road is watched, Vance's men are on every landing."

"I'll come with you."

"No." She said it without looking up. "You're half-dead and you know the back channels to Voss. She's arrested or worse by now — her own first mate took her off her own deck. If she's alive, she's in the brig of her own flagship, and you're the only person on this water she might still listen to. Get her out, Teo. She's the testimony that makes the ledgers mean something. I'm just the courier."

He wanted to argue. He was too tired to argue, and she was right, and they both knew that too. It was, he was beginning to understand, the whole architecture of the thing: everybody doing the one job that only they could do, and trusting the others to do theirs.

"One coin," he said.

She looked at him.

"I took one coin out of there. Out of the whole vault. I'm going to carry it until the day I die and then probably get buried with it, and I want you to know that's all I took, and I want you to know why." He opened his fist. The escudo sat in his palm, small and heavy, its mint face eaten to a ghost. "So I remember what it cost. Not what it's worth. What it *cost*."

Ines looked at the coin for a long moment. Then she reached out and closed his fingers back over it, her hand cold from the water, her grip stronger than he expected.

"Keep it," she said. "You paid for it."

She shouldered the bag. Above them, another charge cracked the headland, and the whole cliff shuddered, and somewhere down in the dark behind the collapsed Needle, the Meridian Star settled a little deeper into the rock that had hidden her for two hundred years.

"The tide turns at 0340," Ines said, already turning up the cliff path, the bag riding high on her back. "The ferry boards at dawn. If I'm not on it —"

"You'll be on it," Teo said.

She looked back at him once — a quick, fierce, unguarded look that he would keep, he suspected, longer than the coin — and then she was gone up into the mangroves, and the dust was falling, and Teo Brandt stood alone on the shingle with a single piece of gold in his fist and turned toward the water, where the lights of the Corvus flagship burned through the murk like a ship in trouble, which was exactly what she was.

He had a captain to break out of her own brig.

He waded in.
