## 31. The Overland Run

The documents rode against her chest in a waterproof oilskin roll, strapped flat the way her father had taught her to strap a survey notebook — high enough to clear water, tight enough to keep from slapping against her ribs with every stride. The logbook was inside it, and the manifest copies, and the pages she had cut from the captain's vault ledger with a diver's knife while Teo held the light and the cavern groaned around them like something being flayed.

Everything else — the gold, the rest of the ledgers, the vault itself — was behind her now, sealed under Corvus tarps and Corvus guns at the cave mouth. It didn't matter. She had learned that on the fifth tide, standing in the vault room with a fortune at her feet: paper outlives gold. Gold can be melted down and made anonymous. Paper keeps names.

She had names. Now she only had to keep them moving.

The ferry to the mainland left from Puerto Sombra at dawn. It was late afternoon when she came down off the cliff trail above the ruined cave, and Puerto Sombra was eleven kilometers east as the frigate bird flew — which meant fourteen or fifteen the way a human being would have to travel, through mangrove swamp, across the karst fields where the island's limestone bones showed through thin soil like the ribs of something enormous and half-buried, and along the coast road where every passing truck would be a question she couldn't afford to answer.

Fifteen kilometers. One night. Two hundred and forty pounds of magistrate's men somewhere between her and the dock.

Ines ran.

* * *

The first rule of tracking, Kenji Nakamura had told her when she was nine years old and furious about being dragged up another cliff before sunrise, is that you are always tracking two things. You track the person you're following, and you track the ground they're walking on. The ground remembers everything. The ground forgives nothing.

He'd crouched in the red dirt and pressed his daughter's small fingers into a bootprint, feeling the crumbled edges, the way the walls had already begun to slump inward.

"See? Fresh. An hour old, maybe less. The ground forgets slowly, but it never lies. People lie. Maps lie. The ground just records."

She had hated him for making her kneel in the dirt. She loved him for it now, in the way you love a hand that taught you to hold a knife — without tenderness, without distance, because the love and the skill had grown into each other past any hope of separation.

Because an hour into the run, crouched at the edge of the coastal road where it bent inland around a collapsed culvert, Ines found tire tracks in the wet clay of the ditch, and read them the way her father had taught her, and knew.

Two vehicles. Heavy tread, recent — the clay still glistening in the wheel ruts, edges not yet dried to matte. Heading east. Toward Puerto Sombra.

They weren't chasing her trail yet. They were getting ahead of it. Someone — Renn, probably, or one of Vance's local men who knew the island's veins — had guessed she wouldn't stay on the roads, and someone else had decided the smart play wasn't pursuit but interception. Cover the ferry dock. Let the island do the hunting.

Her heart did something complicated — fear first, then a cold bright satisfaction underneath it, almost like laughter. They thought she was prey. They were reading her like prey, predicting her like prey, and every prediction they made was built on the assumption that she would move the way frightened people moved: fast, straight, toward help.

Her father had spent six years teaching her that the shortest line between two points was usually a trap.

Ines turned north, away from the road, away from the coast, into the karst.

* * *

The limestone country opened around her like the interior of some drowned cathedral. Rain and time had eaten the bedrock into blades and pinnacles, into sinkholes deep enough to hold their own weather, into slots and fissures where the soil had washed through into darkness below and left only honeycomb stone. The locals called it el país de los dientes — the country of teeth. Cattle wouldn't graze it. Vehicles couldn't cross it. Even on foot it was a landscape that punished hurry; a misplaced step could drop you ten feet into a fissure and break your ankle on the way down.

Which was exactly why she chose it.

She slowed instead of speeding up, testing each foothold, reading the rock the way she'd been taught to read sediment — weight, wear, water. Her boots were salvager's boots, resoled twice, and they gripped the wet limestone where dress shoes and army boots alike would skate. She moved through the teeth-country as dusk came down, picking her line along the spine of a ridge where the karst rose highest and thinnest, and behind her the last light caught the whole broken field and turned it copper, and she understood suddenly why her father had loved this island even after it ate his career. It didn't forgive anything. It didn't forget anything. But it didn't take sides either. It took the patient. It took the careful. It took whoever learned its grammar.

For six years she had believed the wilderness owed her nothing, and she had been right, and there was a strange comfort in that — no debt, no betrayal possible from a place that had never promised you kindness. But tonight, moving through the teeth with her father's lessons running under her breath like a tide table, she felt something shift. The country wasn't taking her side. It was simply refusing to take theirs. And against men who expected the land to be neutral, refusal was enough.

* * *

Full dark found her at the edge of the mangrove swamp above Bahía Salada, and here the rules changed entirely.

Mangrove was not karst. Karst rewarded patience; mangrove demanded fluency. The trees stood on their stilt roots in black shallows, the canopy knotted overhead so thickly that the moon became a rumor, and the mud between the roots sucked at every step and held every print like wax. You could not move quietly through mangrove. You could only move *knowingly* — with the tide, not against it.

Ines stood at the swamp's margin and checked her watch, then the moon, then the smell of the air, doing the arithmetic her father had drilled into her until it lived in her hands. The tide had turned an hour ago. Ebb. Which meant the channels between the mangroves were draining, the water dropping, the mud rising — and in three hours the flats would be bare enough to walk the channel beds themselves, where the mud was firm under a skin of water and bore no prints at all.

Three hours. She had maybe five before dawn, and the last four kilometers of open ground before Puerto Sombra would need every minute of darkness she could get.

She ate while she waited — hard bread and a tin of sardines from the kit, chewing slowly, forcing herself, because the body was a tool too and tools got maintained. She checked the oilskin roll. She checked the knife at her belt. She listened to the swamp breathe.

That was when she heard the engine.

Far off, back west, on the coast road — then quiet, then again, closer, turning off onto the track that ran to the salt works at the bay's head. Headlights swept the treetops briefly, a pale smear through the canopy, and died.

Ines went still as stone and did the worst kind of arithmetic. The track to the salt works dead-ended at the bay. Nobody drove down it at night unless they meant to be there a while. They weren't passing through. They were setting a net — and this end of the swamp, the easy end, the end with the fisherman's path through it, was about to have men in it.

Renn. It had to be Renn leading them. Teo had described him often enough, in the early days, before the confession: *knows country like a book he wrote himself, but he reads it lazy, Vesper — he reads it the way he does everything, trusting the obvious.* Teo had said it with the fondness of old crewmatehood. Tonight those words were worth more than a gun.

The obvious path through the swamp was the fisherman's path, and the fisherman's path was about to be watched. So Ines did what the obvious path would never suggest. She waded straight into the swamp's deepest throat, upstream of the ebb, and let the falling tide carry her.

* * *

Moving with the ebb through a mangrove channel by feel alone is an education in humility. The water was thigh-deep and going shallower, warm as blood, opaque as ink. Roots hooked at her ankles. Things moved in the mud below — crabs, eels, worse — and once something large and unhurried displaced water against her hip and kept going, indifferent, and she stood frozen for a full minute with her heart slamming until the night settled again.

But the current did the work. The ebb pulled her eastward through the labyrinth, channel after channel, and where the water grew too shallow she stepped up onto the root mats themselves, walking the mangroves' own architecture, distributing her weight the way her father had shown her on a rope bridge in Luzon when she was twelve — *don't fight the structure, join it* — and the roots flexed and held and left no trace.

Twice she heard voices. Once, close — twenty meters, maybe less, through the wall of trunks — a flashlight beam strobed across the water ahead of her, and she sank to her chin beneath an arch of roots and watched the light crawl along the surface an arm's length away, close enough to see the dust of it on the film of the water. Men's voices, low and bored and a little nervous. Swamp work at night makes everyone nervous. Nervous men watch the obvious paths harder.

The light passed. The voices faded east. Ines breathed again, very slowly, and noticed that her hands were steady, and was surprised by that, and then wasn't. Six years of other people's abandoned digs had taught her patience; one night in a flooding cave had taught her the rest. Fear was just information. Her father had said that too, once, in a different life, holding her hand over a snake den on Isla Verde's south shore: *the fear tells you where to look. That's all it's for.*

She looked where the fear pointed, and kept moving.

* * *

She surfaced from the swamp at two in the morning on the eastern flats, mud-sheathed to the collarbones, and lay in the shadow of a beached derelict pirogue for a long time, watching the coast road.

Puerto Sombra glowed faintly to the southeast — harbor lights, the dock floodlamps, the ferry's masthead riding at anchor in the roadstead. Four kilometers of open ground: salt pans, pasture, and the last stretch of road into town. No cover worth the name. And between her and it, at the bend where the road crossed the estuary ford, a fire burned.

She glassed it with the little monocular from her kit — a salvage tool, for reading hull numbers at distance — and counted shapes around the flames. Five. Maybe six. A vehicle parked with its lights off. A man walking a slow circuit of the ford, cigarette ember bobbing.

The ford. Of course the ford. It was the only crossing for a vehicle, and nearly the only dry crossing for a man, and anyone coming out of the swamp at night toward Puerto Sombra would funnel right into their firelight like a moth.

Anyone who hadn't been raised on Kenji Nakamura's tide tables.

Ines lay in the sand and studied the estuary with the monocular, and the estuary studied her back with the flat black patience of all water. It was a hundred meters wide at the ford, shallow there, gravel-bottomed — that was why the road crossed it. But estuaries don't have one bottom. They have a deep channel somewhere, cut by the ebb, meandering with the seasons, and if you knew where it ran you could swim it in the dark and come out downstream of the fire, behind the net instead of through it.

And she did know. Not from memory — from inference, from everything her father had ever taught her laid end to end like a chain of survey markers. The salt works upstream diverted fresh water; the channel would hug the far bank. The spit of shingle downstream had grown since the last storm — she could see the new sand, paler than the old — which meant the ebb had been depositing on the outside of the bend, which meant the cut scoured deepest on the inside, near the mangrove fringe on the near side. Every feature of the estuary was a sentence in a language she had been made fluent in before she could ride a bicycle, and the estuary was telling her exactly where its deep water ran.

*The ground forgives nothing,* her father used to say. *It forgets less. But if you learn to listen, sometimes it takes your side.*

She stripped the oilskin roll higher on her chest, cinched every strap, and slid into the water.

* * *

The estuary was colder than the swamp and honest about it. Ines swam the ebb with a slow sidestroke, feet dangling into the channel's depth, letting the current walk her downstream while the fire on the ford shrank behind her and the harbor lights grew ahead. Halfway across, her trailing foot found the channel's true edge and the bottom fell away and she hung in black moving water with a hundred pounds of documents dragging at her chest, and for three long strokes she was fighting rather than floating, and the shore seemed very far, and the night seemed very large.

Then her foot found the bar on the far side, and she hauled herself up the shingle spit and lay gasping in the wet sand, alive, across the line, behind them.

From here the town was a kilometer of salt pans and the back streets of the fishing quarter — streets that woke early, that would fill with women firing bread ovens and men mending nets well before dawn. A woman walking out of the estuary mud would draw eyes, but eyes were survivable; bullets were not. She rinsed the worst of the mud in a rain pool, wrung out her hair, and put her shoulders back and her chin down and walked into Puerto Sombra like a woman who had every right to be there, because the oldest trick her father ever taught her was that confidence is just a costume, and costumes fit better the longer you refuse to check whether anyone's looking.

The fishing quarter received her without comment. A dog barked twice and lost interest. An old man smoking on a step nodded at her the way you nod at neighbors at odd hours — *night work, eh* — and she nodded back, and that was all. She bought coffee from the one stall already lit, and her hands shook around the cup, finally, now that it was safe to shake, and she drank it scalding and let them.

The ferry office opened at four. She was second in line. The clerk, half-asleep, sold her a deck ticket without looking up, and Ines walked out onto the quay with the oilskin roll warm against her sternum and the eastern sky still black, and allowed herself — for exactly thirty seconds, timed against her watch — to believe she had won.

Then she saw the launch.

It came around the breakwater slow and unlit, running on its wake alone, and swung toward the quay's southern end where the customs shed threw its shadow. And standing in the bow, outlined against the last of the harbor lights, rifle held loose and easy across his chest, was a big man with a lazy way of reading country — a man Teo Brandt had once called a friend, and had spent the last two days calling himself a fool for it.

Renn had missed her at the ford. So Renn had done the thing the smartest hunters do when the trail dies: he had gone to where the trail must begin again.

The ferry docked at dawn. Dawn was ninety minutes away.

Ines set down her coffee cup, rolled her shoulders, and started walking toward the one place on the quay with shadows deep enough to think in.
