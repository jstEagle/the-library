## 32. Renn at the Ford

The mangrove gave way to karst the way a lie gives way to truth — all at once, and with no apology. One moment Ines was pushing through green dark, salt mud sucking at her boots, the document tube strapped against her spine like a second vertebra; the next she stood blinking in open light, the canopy torn away above a river that ran wide and shallow over white stone. The ford. Her father's maps had marked it with a small, confident cross and a note in his cramped hand: *crossable at low water only; current lies about its depth.*

*Current lies about everything,* she thought. *So do men.*

She had been running since before dawn. Nine hours through mangrove tunnel and sinkhole country, past termite cathedrals and the bone-white ribs of a beached dinghy, using every trick Kenji Nakamura had ever pressed into her hands across a decade of field seasons — reading broken spiderwebs for passage of bodies, checking mud for the fresh print of boots, doubling back twice through water so the trail would die. The wilderness had been indifferent to her for six years, and then, somewhere around the third hour, it had stopped being indifferent. It had started testing her. And she had passed, again and again, until she understood that this was what her father had actually been teaching her all those summers: not archaeology, not sediment and tide tables, but how to move through hostile country without leaving anything behind for your enemies to read.

Except, apparently, one thing.

Because when she waded out onto the gravel bar at the ford's center, the water barely kissing her knees, she saw him already there.

Renn sat on a fallen palm trunk on the far bank, as if he'd been waiting for a ferry. He was a big man gone soft in the middle, with a diver's shoulders and a dockworker's hands, and he had a machete laid casually across his thighs the way other men wore ties. His face was familiar from Teo's stories and from the Corvus crew manifests she'd memorized: Renn Okafor, twenty years with the fleet, the man who had once been Teo Brandt's closest friend and who had sold their cache to Vance's men for a promised stake in gold he would never see.

"You're a hard woman to follow," Renn said. "Almost didn't."

"Then you should have let almost win." She kept walking. Not toward him, not away — along the bar, unhurried, as if she were simply another traveler and the ford belonged to everyone. Her heart was slamming against the document tube, but her voice came out level, and she was grateful to her father for that too, for the thousand boring hours of patient fieldwork that taught her panic was just data you hadn't organized yet.

"Vance wants the papers," Renn said. "And he wants you not to reach the ferry."

"I know."

"He paid me good money."

"No," Ines said, "he didn't. He paid you *some* money. There's a difference, and you're standing in it."

Renn laughed, but it was a short sound, quickly closed off, like a drawer shoved shut. He stood, and the machete stayed on the log, which she noted, and noted that she noted. "You've got a mouth on you. Teo always said—"

"Teo says a lot of things." She stopped, finally, ten meters from him, mid-river, the current combing around her calves. "He says you were the best diver he ever worked with. He says you pulled him out of a collapsed wreck off Punta Áspera when nobody else would go down. He says that for twenty years, if you told him a thing was true, he built his whole plan on it." She tilted her head. "Funny, isn't it. The man whose word used to be worth more than salvage rights. Look what it buys now."

Something moved behind Renn's eyes — quick, involuntary, like a fish under ice. She had hit something. Good. You didn't beat a man like Renn with force; she'd seen his forearms, and she knew exactly how that contest ended, with her face in the gravel and the documents in his hands. You beat him with the one thing he couldn't cut with a machete.

"The stake," she said. "Vance promised you a stake in the recovery. That was the arrangement, yes? Cache location for a share of the vault gold. Let's talk about that number, because I've read the ledgers, Renn. I've spent two days with them, and I know how Aurelio Vance does arithmetic."

"There's no ledger with my name in it."

"Not yet," she agreed pleasantly. "That's rather my point. Let me tell you what *is* in them. Every man who helped Vance bury the Meridian Star got paid exactly once. One payment, cash, no record — and then a debt. A permanent debt. Because the moment you've taken his money for a crime, you're not a partner, you're an *entry*. Something to be balanced. Do you know what happened to the harbormaster who signed the false tonnage reports? He drowned. Officially: drunk, fell off his own dock. Unofficially: he'd started drinking because he wanted out, and men who want out are the only ones who ever actually leave. Do you know what happened to the survey clerk who kept copies of the original manifest?" She paused. "Neither does anyone else. That's the point. There isn't even a story. Men who keep copies don't get drownings. They get *absences*."

The river talked over the silence, patient, indifferent, saying nothing in a hundred voices.

"Vance is going to blow the cave," Ines went on. "You know that. Maybe you even helped carry the charges up the cliff path. And here's the arithmetic you should have done before you sold us out: a man who will destroy a national heritage site to hide his crimes — permits be damned, his own fleet be damned — what does he do with a loose end like you? You're not family. You're not Voss, with twenty years of Corvus behind her and a flagship full of witnesses. You're a hired hand who knows where a cache was, knows what was in it, and has now watched the daughter of Kenji Nakamura walk out of the mangroves carrying the one thing Vance cannot allow to exist." She shook her head slowly. "You didn't sell our location, Renn. You signed your own name into his book. And the only question left is whether he balances the entry before or after the cave comes down."

"You're lying," Renn said, but his voice had lost its floor. It was the voice of a man standing on a deck that had begun, very quietly, to tilt.

"Am I? Then answer me one thing." She held up a finger. "Has he given you the stake yet? Actual gold, in your actual hands?"

Silence.

"Has he given you a *date*?"

More silence, and now Renn looked down at the machete as if he'd forgotten why it was there, as if it belonged to a man he used to be.

"Twenty years," Ines said softly. "You worked twenty years for Corvus, and the best Vance could offer you was a promise with no date attached. I can tell you what that means, because I grew up watching men make promises like that to my father. A promise with no date is a debt wearing a gift's clothing. It means *when this is finished*. And Renn — men like Vance finish things. That's all they do. They finish sites, they finish careers, they finish people. The only variable is the order."

A heron lifted off the far shallows, unhurried, wings slapping the air like slow applause, and the sound seemed to break something loose in him. When Renn spoke again, his voice was lower, rougher, stripped of the dockside swagger. "Teo sent you to say this."

"Teo doesn't know I met you here. Teo thinks I'm three kilometers east, cutting through the cane fields." She let that land. "I chose this ford because I read the ground better than your trackers did, and I chose this conversation because someone owes you the truth before Vance pays you in the only currency he has left. Call it mercy, if you want. I do. Mercy is just strategy with the cruelty removed."

"And if I say no?" He picked up the machete — not threateningly, she thought, but the way a man holds a thing to remind himself he still owns decisions. "If I take the papers anyway? Take you back to him? There's a bonus in that too, maybe."

"Then you'll do it," Ines said, "and I'll fight you, and you'll win, because look at us. And you'll hand Vance the documents, and he'll thank you, and shake your hand, and somewhere between the handshake and the demolition charge you'll realize you've become the last witness who knows where everything is buried — including you." She took one step closer, water swirling, and made her voice as calm as tide tables. "Or you choose the other thing. You let me walk to that ferry. You go back and you tell Vance whatever keeps you alive for the next forty-eight hours — tell him you lost me in the karst, tell him I doubled back north, tell him anything, he'll believe incompetence before he believes treachery, it flatters his worldview. And then, when this breaks — and it *will* break, Renn, the ledgers are already on a boat, Imelda Cruz's testimony is already typed, Voss is already turning — when it breaks, you stand up in front of the federal inspectors and you say the truest sentence of your life: *I was there, and I can name every date.*"

"That's not mercy," Renn said hoarsely. "That's a bill."

"It's both," Ines said. "Everything honest is." She spread her empty hands, palms up, showing him what he must already have seen: no weapon, no radio, nothing but a tired woman standing in a river with paper against her spine. "I'm not going to beg, and I'm not going to run, because I can't outrun you and we both know it. This is the whole hand. Vance offered you a share of stolen gold and a place at the bottom of the sea. I'm offering you a share of the truth and a seat at the hearing. One of those payments clears. Choose."

For a long moment the only sound was the water, lying about its depth, going about its ancient business of moving everything downhill eventually. Renn looked at the machete. He looked at her. And then, most tellingly, he looked back the way she had come — south, toward the coast, toward the cliff and the cave and the man on the bluff with the detonator box — and she watched him do the arithmetic she had assigned him, watch the numbers come in one by one, ugly and undeniable, the interest compounding with every second he stood in this river.

"You really think it breaks?" he asked finally. "Voss, the ledgers, all of it. You think it actually comes down on him?"

"I don't think," Ines said. "I *know* the same way you know a wreck's holding before you see it — by the bubbles coming up. Everything Vance built is venting right now. He can hear it too. That's why he's lighting fuses instead of signing papers. Strong men close doors. Panicked ones burn houses."

Renn exhaled, long and shuddering, twenty years leaving his body all at once. Then he did the last thing she expected: he sat back down on the fallen palm, heavily, like a man setting down cargo he'd carried too far, and laid the machete flat on his knee — not put away, but no longer pointed anywhere.

"Nakamura," he said. "Your father. I was on the boat. The cover-up dive, 'nineteen. I didn't touch the strongbox, but I was there when they called it worthless. I heard them laugh about him." His jaw worked. "Is he—"

"Alive," Ines said, and the word cost her something and gave her something back. "Inland. Running a museum of nothing much. He'll outlive Vance. That's the whole moral of this island, if it has one."

Renn nodded slowly. Then he reached into his shirt pocket and drew out a folded, sweat-soft map — her own route, annotated by Vance's trackers, the ferry road marked in red — and held it out over the water.

"Burn it after," he said. "And Nakamura—" He hesitated, and for a moment he was twenty years younger, hauling a friend out of a collapse, believing his word meant something. "Tell Teo the ford at Punta Áspera. Tell him I remember. He'll know what it means."

She took the map. Their fingers didn't touch.

"I'll tell him," Ines said. "And Renn — the hearing's in three weeks. Wear a clean shirt. History remembers who showed up looking like they'd already won."

She turned and crossed the rest of the ford, the current trying its old lies against her legs and finding her unmoved, and when she reached the far bank she did not look back. Looking back was for people who doubted their exits. Behind her she heard the splash of a big body entering the river, heading south, heading toward Vance's camp with a story about a woman lost in the cane fields — a story that would buy him hours, and maybe, if the tides turned the way she'd promised, a clean shirt and a chair in the front row.

The document tube pressed warm against her spine. Two kilometers to the ferry road. Four to the dock. The correspondent's boat sailed at dawn, and dawn, for once in her life, was on her schedule.

Ines broke into a run.

* * *

The karst opened ahead of her like a country remembering itself — sinkholes breathing cool air, limestone teeth gnashing with orchids, the light going long and amber as the afternoon leaned west. She ran the way her father had taught her to work a site: steady, rhythmic, spending nothing she couldn't replace. Twice she heard engines on the coastal road and dropped into a sinkhole's shadow until the sound passed. Once she passed a village well and drank without stopping, cupping water in her hands while an old woman watched her from a doorway with the expression of someone who had seen fleeing people before and recognized the posture.

At the top of the last ridge, the sea appeared — vast, hammered silver, the mainland a blue smudge on the horizon — and below it, strung along the shore like beads, the lights of Puerto Sombra coming on one by one as dusk fell. The ferry dock. The correspondent. Dawn.

She allowed herself ninety seconds to stand there and breathe, and in those ninety seconds she thought of her father's note in the margin of the old survey map: *the current lies about its depth.* She had spent six years believing the deepest lie of all — that distance was the same as safety, that bitterness was the same as strength. But the ford had taken neither. The ford had asked only what she was willing to carry across, and she had answered with the truth, and the truth had weighed less than the machete.

She touched the tube at her spine once, the way a diver checks a regulator, and started down toward the lights.

Behind her, far to the south, a dull concussion rolled across the water — the first test charge at the cliff, Vance getting impatient, the architect beginning to demolish his own blueprint. The sound reached her a heartbeat after the flash, the way truth always arrives after the explosion.

Ines didn't flinch. She ran faster.
