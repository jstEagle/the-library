## 33. The Ferry at Dawn

The mangroves gave up their grip on her an hour before dawn, and after that it was only mud and moonlight and the long flat shimmer of the causeway road.

Ines ran the way her father had taught her to run — not fast, but *evenly*, the pace of a woman who understood that the race was measured in hours and not minutes, that the body was a tide chart of its own with a flood point past which everything failed at once. Her legs had passed that point somewhere back in the karst country, when Renn's flashlight had swept the sinkhole rim behind her and she'd dropped into cold black water without knowing how deep it went. Since then she had been running on something older than muscle. Call it inheritance. Call it spite. She didn't have time to name it.

The documents rode against her chest in a waterproof sleeve, bound tight beneath her shirt with two wraps of dive cord — the ledgers, the captain's log, the manifest copies, the letters in Kenji Nakamura's careful hand proving he had tried, six years ago, to burn this whole rotten edifice down with nothing but paper and courage. They weighed almost nothing. They weighed more than the gold ever would have.

The sky over the eastern water had gone the color of a bruise healing — deep violet at the top, then green, then a thin seam of molten copper where the horizon would break. And there, riding that seam like a splinter, the ferry: the *Doña Rosa*, island packet boat, forty years of rust and stubbornness, sitting at the end of the long wooden pier at Puerto Sombra with her stack already breathing smoke into the still air.

Ines stopped at the edge of the last stand of palms and made herself look. Made herself count.

Two men on the pier. Dockhands, by their posture — one leaning on a bollard, one coiling line. A cart of crates waiting to go aboard. The gangway down, unguarded. No launch tied alongside. No Vance.

Yet.

She could hear the launch before she could see it — the low diesel burble carrying across flat water the way sound carries at dawn, when the sea is holding its breath. She turned and looked south along the coast and found it: a white wake cutting the glassy channel, running dark against the pale water, coming up from the direction of the cave site at a speed no legitimate errand required. Even at this distance she could pick out the silhouette — Vance's personal launch, the *Aurelia*, all polished teak and arrogance, the boat he used for ribbon-cuttings and, she now knew from the ledgers, for meetings he never wanted minuted.

Twenty minutes, maybe less, if the channel stayed kind to him.

The ferry departed at first light. That was the schedule; Imelda Cruz had confirmed it twice, in the coded shorthand of old families who remembered what it meant to pass messages under a magistrate's nose — *the Rosa sails when the sun touches the water, not before, not after.* First light was now. Which meant Ines had perhaps five minutes to cross four hundred meters of open pier with every eye on the island able to see her do it.

She checked the sleeve against her chest one more time. She thought, absurdly, of her father's hands — the way he'd held a tide table, smoothing it flat on his knee as though the numbers might wrinkle and lie. *The sea keeps its own books,* he used to say. *Your job is just to read them honestly.*

"All right," she said aloud, to no one, to the mangroves, to him. "Let's read."

* * *

She did not run the pier. Running drew eyes, and eyes were the one currency she could not afford to spend. Instead she walked — a salvage diver's walk, tired and salt-stiffened, a woman coming off a night job, which was exactly what half the island would assume anyway. She'd stripped off the torn outer layer back in the tree line and now wore only the stained work shirt and shorts she'd started the expedition in, hair crusted stiff, hands nicked and gray with dried mud. Nobody looks twice at a laborer at dawn. The whole architecture of her invisibility was built on the island's oldest habit: not seeing the people who did its work.

The dockhands glanced up as she passed. One nodded. The other went back to his coiling. Behind them, the *Doña Rosa*'s deckhand leaned on the rail above the gangway, chewing something slowly, watching the southern channel with an expression Ines recognized — the expression of a man who could also hear the launch, and was doing arithmetic about it.

She climbed the gangway.

"Passage?" the deckhand said, not moving from the rail.

"I'm not taking passage." She kept her voice level, pitched under the engine idle. "I'm looking for a passenger. Mainland man. Correspondent."

Something shifted behind the deckhand's eyes — not surprise, exactly. Confirmation. Whatever Doña Imelda had set in motion, it had reached this rail before Ines did. He tilted his head toward the stern cabin. "Forward of the engine housing. Says he's been up all night waiting on a delivery." A pause, and then, dry as driftwood: "Says the delivery's late."

"It was a long swim," Ines said, and went aft.

* * *

The correspondent was not what she expected, which was her own fault for expecting anything. She had pictured someone crisp, metropolitan, a press card and a pressed collar. What she got was a heavyset man in his fifties asleep upright on a bench, glasses pushed into thinning gray hair, a notebook open facedown on his chest, wearing a guayabera wrinkled beyond any hope of ironing. He came awake the moment her shadow crossed him — fully, instantly, the way men come awake who have spent years in places where sleeping deeply is a career-limiting behavior.

"You're Nakamura's daughter," he said. Not a question.

"Ines Nakamura." She sat across from him, keeping her body between him and the open cabin door. "You were tipped by Doña Cruz."

"Imelda Cruz doesn't tip. Imelda Cruz *instructs*." He found his glasses, settled them on his nose, and looked at her with eyes that were sharper than the rest of him. "Tomás Ferreol. *El Meridiano*, mainland edition. I've been writing about Aurelio Vance for eleven years. I've been *allowed* to write about Aurelio Vance for eleven years, which is a different thing entirely — little pieces, corruption-adjacent pieces, the kind a man lets you print because they make him look like a reformer fighting his own system." His mouth twisted. "I always figured the real story was buried somewhere I couldn't dig. Turns out it was buried underwater."

"And you believe me. Just like that."

Ferreol picked up the notebook, turned it over, tapped the cover. "I've spent three days on this island being fed dinner by three different families, each of whom told me a different piece of an old story about a ship called the Meridian Star, and each of whom pretended the other two hadn't told me anything. Old families don't coordinate lies, Ms. Nakamura. They can't help telling the truth in fragments — it's the only way they know how to confess." He leaned forward. "So yes. I believe you. Show me."

Out on the water, the wake had resolved into the launch itself, white hull catching the first true light, close enough now to pick out figures on her deck. Four. Maybe five.

Ines pulled the dive cord loose, worked the waterproof sleeve out from under her shirt, and laid it on the bench between them. Her fingers were shaking. She noticed it with a strange detachment, as though the shaking belonged to someone else — some younger version of herself standing outside this moment, watching a woman she barely recognized trade away the last thing she had.

Inside the sleeve, through the clear face, the ledgers showed their spines: the Meridian Star's true manifest. The captain's log, final entries intact. And the vault ledger itself — names, dates, sums, the whole colonial embezzlement written out in a dead captain's careful script, annotated years later in handwriting Ines knew better than her own grief: Aurelio Vance's, dated six years before he ever held office.

"The manifest contradicts the official survey," she said. Her voice came out steadier than her hands. "The official survey — the one that ended my father's career — was falsified. By Vance. Before he was magistrate. It's in his hand, it's dated, and the ink analysis will hold up if anyone bothers to check, which your paper should bother to check, because if you don't, they'll say it's forged."

"They'll say it's forged anyway."

"Then get three analysts. Get ten. Get the naval inspector — there's one arriving with the federal custody order, Imelda has the wire already moving. But get it printed *first*. Print it while the ink is wet on the island's memory. Once it's public, it can't be unpublic. That's the only weapon paper has, and it's enough, but only if you fire it today."

Ferreol had gone very still, the way men go still when they are reading. He wasn't even touching the sleeve yet. He was reading the spines, the water-stained canvas, the wax seal broken cleanly, and Ines watched him understand, item by item, what he was looking at.

"This is the vault ledger," he said quietly. "From the ship herself."

"From the captain's personal strongroom, behind the cargo hold, forty feet under a collapsed limestone shelf, recovered approximately ninety minutes ago by a man named Teo Brandt, who nearly died doing it, and who left several hundred pounds of gold coin on the seabed to make weight." She heard her own voice crack slightly on the word *gold*, and hated it, and let it. "I want that in your story too. Not the gold. The leaving of it. Because when Vance tells the world I'm a treasure hunter trying to extort him, someone needs to be able to write down what I walked away from."

Ferreol looked up at her then, really looked, and whatever he saw made him take off his glasses and clean them unnecessarily on his guayabera tail. "How much?"

"Enough to erase every debt I have, twice over. Enough to buy my father a real museum instead of a room in an exile town. Enough that I'll spend the rest of my life knowing I could have had it." She met his eyes. "And it's sitting under sixty feet of water in a cave that's about to be blown apart by the man who stole it, and I find — I genuinely find — that I don't care. Write that down too. Quote me."

He wrote it down. She watched him do it, the pencil moving fast, and something in her chest unclenched a single degree — the first degree of unclenching since the storm tide had shown her those timbers stamped MERIDIAN STAR, six tides and one lifetime ago.

Then the deckhand appeared at the cabin door, and the morning changed shape.

"Launch is making for the pier," he said. "Not slowing."

* * *

They came out onto the stern deck together, and Ines made herself assess it the way her father had taught her to assess a site: quickly, completely, without flinching at the worst feature.

The *Aurelia* was three hundred meters out and closing, throttle wide open, bow wave a white fan in the copper water. On her foredeck, four men. She knew two of them by sight — Vance's dock security, the ones who'd guarded the concession office during the first tides. The other two moved like hired muscle anywhere moves: weight forward, hands free, scanning. At the helm, a fifth figure, and beside him —

Renn.

Her stomach dropped through the deck planking. So he'd chosen wrong. Or he'd chosen right and been brought along anyway, a leash on a dog the magistrate no longer trusted. Either way, he was pointing at her now — she saw the arm come up, the shout swallowed by distance and engine noise — and the *Aurelia* adjusted her approach, swinging wide to run parallel to the pier rather than to it.

Not docking. Interdicting. Vance wanted the handoff stopped in open water, where an accident could happen without witnesses on land.

"Ms. Nakamura." Ferreol's voice was calm in a way that cost him something; she could see the price in his neck. "How much time do we have?"

"Less than we need." Her mind was running tide tables again, the old reflex, the only prayer she knew. The *Doña Rosa* cast off at first light — and first light was *now*, the sun's lower limb just kissing the horizon, the water going gold in a long lane straight down the channel. If the ferry left on schedule, the launch would have to choose: block the ferry illegally in the harbor mouth, in front of the dockhands and the morning fishermen already putting out, or let her go and lose the story forever.

Vance wouldn't hesitate. That was the thing the ledgers had taught her about him. He had never once hesitated to make a crime worse in order to hide a smaller one. He'd blow the cave; he'd board a ferry.

But hesitation wasn't the variable. *Witnesses* were the variable. And the dawn was filling the harbor with them — two fishing pirogues, a third putting out from the far quay, the dockhands frozen mid-task on the pier, staring. Every eye on Isla Verde that mattered at this hour was pointed at the water.

"Captain!" Ines raised her voice toward the wheelhouse. "You're cleared for first-light departure. Doña Cruz confirmed it. Are you sailing?"

A pause. Then the deckhand grinned — slow, wide, a man who had been waiting all night for exactly this cue — and cupped his hands. "The Rosa sails when the sun touches the water! Captain says so!"

Below them, the engine note deepened. The gangway rattled up. Lines came in.

On the pier, the two dockhands stepped back with the studied blankness of men who had suddenly become extremely busy with absolutely nothing, and Ines understood that Imelda Cruz's instruction had traveled further than one correspondent. The old families had decided, quietly and all at once, which side of the ledger they stood on. You couldn't buy that. You could only earn it, over generations, or — apparently — over six tides, with the truth.

The *Doña Rosa* began to move.

The *Aurelia* cut her angle hard, throttling up to intercept across the harbor mouth, and for ten seconds Ines stood at the stern rail doing the geometry and hating every centimeter of it. The launch was faster. The launch would reach the channel narrows first. There was a moment — she felt it arrive like cold water — when she understood that the papers might leave the island inside her chest after all, that the whole run through mangrove and karst and ford might end here, fifty meters short, with Renn's face looking down at her from a gunwale.

Then Ferreol stepped to the rail beside her, took off his glasses, folded them, and put them in his pocket. And raised both arms over his head, notebook in one fist, and shouted — in a voice built by thirty years of shouting over press scrums:

"AURELIO VANCE! TOMÁS FERREOL, EL MERIDIANO! YOU'RE ABOUT TO ASSAULT A CIVILIAN FERRY IN FULL VIEW OF THE HARBOR, AND EVERY WORD OF IT WILL BE IN TOMORROW'S EDITION WITH YOUR NAME SPELLED CORRECTLY!"

The launch wavered.

It was small — a hitch in the throttle, a half-second of confusion on the foredeck, the two security men turning to look at their own helmsman. But Ines had spent her life reading water, and water taught you that the smallest shift in current decides where the whole flood goes. The *Aurelia* had come to stop a smuggler. She had found, instead, a newspaperman with a name and a deadline and witnesses multiplying by the minute — pirogues converging now, drawn by the spectacle, fishermen standing in their boats to watch — and the arithmetic of the crime was changing under Vance's feet.

The launch fell off, throttled down, and began to shadow the ferry at fifty meters, pacing her like a shark that has realized the thing in the water is bigger.

"He'll follow us out," Ferreol said quietly. "He'll send men aboard at the mainland dock, where there's less audience."

"He won't get the chance." Ines turned from the rail. "Because you're not waiting for the mainland dock. Hand me your notebook."

He blinked. "Why?"

"Because the handoff happens *now*, in front of God and the harbor, and then you're going to sit in your cabin and write until your pencil dies — and when we dock, the story walks off this boat ahead of me, in your pocket, where no search of my person will ever find it." She held out her hand. "The sleeve stays with me. If they take me, they take decoys — copies, partials, things that will buy me hours and cost them credibility. The originals ride with a credentialed correspondent on a vessel they cannot legally board twice in one morning."

For a long moment Ferreol just looked at her, and Ines watched the professional calculation move behind his eyes — risk, scoop, liability, the ancient gambler's arithmetic of his trade. Then he laughed, short and disbelieving, and handed her the notebook.

"You know," he said, flipping to a fresh page as the ferry gathered way and the island began, at last, to fall away behind them, "eleven years I've chased this man, and the story turns out to have been carried the last hundred meters by an archaeologist's daughter with mud to the knees." He glanced at the *Aurelia*, pacing them sullenly in the gold light, and his voice dropped. "That's Vance's brother-in-law's flag signal hoisted — see the yellow shape? That's a recall. Someone aboard just told him to break off."

Ines looked. The little yellow pennant snapped at the launch's halyard, and even at this distance she could read the fury in the figure at the rail — the rigid posture, the arm gesturing at the ferry, overruled. Mara Voss, she thought, or Teo's warning finally landing, or simply the sight of too many witnesses. It didn't matter which. What mattered was that the *Aurelia* slowed, wallowed, and turned a slow, defeated circle back toward the island, growing smaller against the breaking dawn.

Ines exhaled — a breath she felt she'd been holding since the storm.

Behind them, Isla Verde burned copper and green on the horizon, the cliff line hiding the wound Vance had made of the cave, the whole corrupted weight of it receding with every turn of the ferry's screw. Ahead lay open water, the mainland, the press, the naval inspector, the federal wire already in motion. The ledgers rode against Ferreol's chest now, wrapped in his notebook, ordinary as luggage.

It was done. It was actually, impossibly done — the handoff made, the story loose in the world, no longer hers to carry or to lose.

So why, she wondered, watching the island shrink, did her hands still shake? Why did the relief taste so much like dread?

Then she saw it: a skiff putting out from the far quay, small and fast, running a course that intercepted nothing and nobody — except that at its tiller sat a slight figure in a Corvus Fleet jacket, and as the distance closed between them, the figure lifted one arm, not in threat but in salute, and Ines's blood went cold, because she knew that silhouette, and the last person it should belong to out here was Teo Brandt — unless something had gone very wrong ashore, unless the pact had cracked, unless—

The skiff swung alongside, matching the ferry's speed, and Teo Brandt shouted up across the water, breathless, salt-white-lipped, one hand pressed to a bandage at his temple:

"Ines! It's Voss — Vance's men took her at the site — and the demolition crew's heading back to the cave with the *rest* of the charges—"
