## 35. Testimony

They kept Mara Voss in the flagship's aft cabin for two days, which was two days longer than she intended to allow.

The lock on the door was a joke — brass, salt-corroded, older than her career. She could have opened it with a hairpin and contempt. What held her was arithmetic: Renn had taken the deck with six men who owed Vance money and Voss nothing but wages, and the *Corvus Star* rode at anchor in a roadstead full of federal cutters that hadn't been there a week ago. The mathematics of mutiny had changed overnight. She sat on the bunk with her hands folded and let the numbers finish their work.

They finished on the morning of the second day, when the door opened without a knock and First Mate Aldous Renner stood in it looking like a man who had slept in his clothes because his bed had been repossessed by his conscience.

"Captain," he said. His voice had gravel in it that hadn't been there before. "The inspector's launch is alongside. They're asking for you by name."

"Which name?"

He almost smiled. It cost him visibly. "Yours. The real one."

She rose, straightened her collar, and paused at the door long enough to look at him — really look, the way she looked at weather. "You sold me to him for a promise."

"I did." He didn't flinch from it, and she gave him a point for that. "And then I read what Teo Brandt showed me. What Vance does to loose ends." A breath. "I'd rather be your loose end than his kept man."

"You'll testify," she said. Not a question.

"I'll testify."

"Then we'll see whether the sea forgives you. I haven't decided yet." She stepped past him onto the deck, into light so clean it felt like an accusation, and the federal launch waited below with its gray paint and its government efficiency, and Mara Voss went down the ladder toward the strangest thing she had done in twenty years at sea: telling the truth to someone authorized to hear it.

* * *

The naval inspector was a compact woman named Okafor with reading glasses on a chain and the patient stillness of someone who had spent her career waiting for men like Aurelio Vance to overreach. She received Voss in the customs house at Puerto Sombra, in a room with a ceiling fan that turned like it was being paid by the hour, and she did not offer coffee and did not perform sympathy.

"Captain Voss. You held salvage concessions issued under Magistrate Vance's authority."

"I did."

"Those concessions have been voided as of this morning. Pending investigation." Okafor laid a folder on the table, square and gray. "You are not under arrest. I want to be precise about that, because what you say next determines whether that changes."

Voss sat. The chair was hard; she approved of it. Hard chairs kept testimony honest.

"Then let me be precise too," she said. "Aurelio Vance did not send my fleet to salvage a wreck. He sent it to bury one. My standing orders were to recover the manifest first, secure it personally, and report the site's condition as unstable — structurally compromised, unsalvageable. His words. He wanted history to handle the rest." She recited it flatly, the way you read soundings. "I have those orders in writing. In his hand. I kept them because they made my skin itch, and twenty years at sea has taught me that itching skin is data."

Okafor turned a page. "You suspected falsification."

"I suspected a man who pays for silence is buying something worth more than he says. The specifics came later. The manifest contradicts every official record of the Meridian Star's cargo. The captain's log shows deliberate scuttling. And there are ledgers—" Voss stopped herself, recalibrated. "There are documents recovered from the vault room that name names. Old names. Founding-family names. Including his."

"And Nakamura?" Okafor said, and watched her carefully when she said it. "Kenji Nakamura. Your fleet's original survey, six years ago, reported his find as worthless. That report ended his career."

Voss was quiet for a moment. Outside the shuttered window, a gull argued with the harbor about something neither would win.

"The diver who filed that survey assessment was working for Corvus at the time," she said finally. "His name is Teo Brandt. He'll tell you himself — he's already offered, which tells you what kind of week he's having. But understand what actually happened, Inspector. Nakamura found the cave. He marked it, he documented it, he tried to take it public. Vance buried the discovery through my company's report, destroyed Nakamura's credibility, and waited six years for the sea to do the demolition work for free. When the storm exposed the wreck anyway, he moved within hours. Concessions to his brother-in-law. Demolition charges before the permits even vested." She met Okafor's eyes. "That isn't corruption of office. That's office as camouflage for theft. There's a difference, and your paperwork should reflect it."

Okafor wrote for a while. The fan turned. Somewhere below, the tide was coming in, keeping its own books.

"You were prepared to carry out his orders," Okafor said at last. "Recover the manifest. Bury the site."

"Yes." Voss didn't soften it. "I was. Right up until I intercepted a skiff belonging to Ines Nakamura and discovered that the daughter of the man we ruined was diving circles around my fleet on half a tank of air and a grudge." Something moved at the corner of her mouth — not quite a smile, more the memory of one. "She refused my offer. I've made that offer perhaps a dozen times in my career, Inspector. She's the first one who refused it correctly."

"Correctly."

"She refused it for reasons instead of pride. Pride you can buy. Reasons you can't." Voss leaned back. "I've spent my life taking orders from men who thought the ocean was theirs because they owned boats. This week I learned the ocean keeps different accounts. I'm here to settle mine while the settling's good."

* * *

Renn testified that afternoon, and it went worse for him than for her, which was justice of a rough local vintage.

He came in wet-eyed and scrubbed raw, wearing the only shirt he owned without a stain on it, and he talked for three hours. He talked about the cache in the cliff — how Vance's men had come to him through a broker, how the price had been a salvage stake and a new start, how he'd told himself it wasn't betrayal because Teo Brandt had betrayed everyone first, years ago, and what was one more stone on that cairn. He talked about overhearing the plan at the fuel depot: once the vault contents were secured, the *Corvus Star* would suffer an engine-room fire at sea. Insurance. Tragic loss of vessel and, presumably, of any crew members whose memories proved inconvenient.

"He named the insurance figure out loud," Renn said, and his voice cracked on it. "He named it like he was ordering lunch. Captain Voss, the divers, the whole complement — he priced us before he ever planned to sink us. We weren't witnesses to him. We were ballast."

Okafor asked him to repeat the figure for the record. He did. Then he put his head in his hands, and no one in the room hurried him.

It was Ines Nakamura, waiting outside afterward in the corridor with salt still in her hair, who said the thing Renn needed to hear — or maybe the thing Mara needed to hear her say. Voss passed them in the hallway and slowed, not quite stopping.

"You let him choose," Ines said. It wasn't a question either. These two women had stopped trading questions somewhere off the reef, in the wash of an ebb tide.

"He chose wrong for years," Voss said. "I figured he'd earned one chance to choose right under supervision."

Ines looked at her for a long moment. "My father used to say the sea doesn't punish. It just records."

"Your father," Voss said, "sounds like a man I owe an apology to. Add it to the ledger."

* * *

The ledgers did the rest.

Okafor's forensic accountants worked through them for nine days in a rented room above the chandlery, and the island held its breath. Mara Voss spent those days aboard a ship she technically no longer commanded, watching federal launches come and go, watching the Corvus Fleet's colors struck from the customs house flagpole on the fourth day, watching the town do what island towns do with a fallen magistrate — which is everything at once, loudly, in the streets, and then very quietly, in doorways, recalculating forty years of favors and fears.

She learned the shape of it in fragments, the way you learn a coastline in fog. The Meridian Star had gone down in 1897 carrying colonial treasury funds that never reached the treasury. Half the marble in the old quarter had risen from that water. The Vance family's plantation house, the Cruz family's shipping line, three banks and a cathedral dome — all of it floated up from the bottom of the sea on the buoyancy of other people's money. Kenji Nakamura had found the proof of it, and a young Aurelio Vance — not yet magistrate, merely ambitious, already corrupt — had signed the false survey that killed the truth in its cradle. His handwriting was in the margins of the manifest. Okafor showed Voss the authentication analysis, and Voss studied the looping, confident script and thought: *he wrote it like a signature on his own future.*

On the eleventh day, the warrant came down from the mainland. Federal custody, effective immediately, charges enumerated across four pages: falsification of official surveys, conspiracy, obstruction, embezzlement by descent, and — appended almost as an afterthought, though everyone knew it was the heart of it — attempted destruction of a protected archaeological site and conspiracy against the lives of persons employed therein.

They took him at the governor's residence, where he'd gone to plead jurisdiction. Voss heard the account from three different people before noon, each version growing in the retelling, but the constant was this: Aurelio Vance did not struggle. He walked to the launch in his good coat, and at the gangway he stopped and looked out at the harbor — at the *Corvus Star*, at the cliffs where the cave had been, at the horizon where the Meridian Star still slept under her fathoms of quiet — and he said, audibly, to no one:

"I built half of what you're all standing on."

And Doña Imelda Cruz, who had come down to watch, answered him across the water in a voice that carried the way old voices can when they've been waiting decades for the tide: "No, Aurelio. You built it on top of what you stole. The difference is now on the record."

The permits were voided that same afternoon. All of them. Every concession, every claim, every paper trail Vance had woven through the customs house for six years — dissolved in a single administrative stroke, and the waters of Isla Verde reverted, legally and completely, to the open sea.

Voss stood at the rail of the ship that was no longer hers and watched the flag come down off the customs house, and felt something in her chest unclench that she hadn't known was clenched. Twenty years she had operated in the space between law and water, where captains make their own weather and the record is whatever survives the voyage. She had expected justice, if it ever came for her, to arrive the way it always did at sea: privately, in the dark, in the form of consequences.

Instead it had come in daylight, with paperwork. With testimony given in a hard chair and corroborated by a broken man in his only clean shirt. With ledgers doing what ledgers do best — refusing, page after patient page, to forget.

Justice outside the law, walking through the law's front door. She found she liked the view from inside.

* * *

Okafor found her at dusk on the quarterdeck, climbing the ladder uninvited, which Voss decided to interpret as respect.

"The investigation will want you on the mainland eventually," the inspector said, settling at the rail beside her as though invited. "Formal hearings. But the substance is settled. The documents, the testimony, the corroboration — it holds. Whatever happens in the courts now is just the echo."

"Echoes matter," Voss said. "Ask anyone who's listened for a wreck bell."

"Mm." Okafor looked out at the water. The last light was going copper along the swells. "There's a consortium forming. Archaeological. Legitimate — mainland universities, proper funding, the kind of permits that get reviewed rather than purchased. They've applied to reopen the site." A pause, delicately managed. "Their proposed lead salvor is listed as I. Nakamura. Dive master, T. Brandt. And they're in the market for a vessel and a captain with local knowledge and, apparently, a recent demonstrated commitment to the historical record."

Voss was silent for a long moment. The fan of the fading wind moved the rigging overhead, that low music ships make when they're thinking.

"They know what they're asking for," she said. "I ran a cover-up fleet for a corrupt magistrate for six years."

"They know exactly what they're asking for. Nakamura's words, I'm told, were: 'I want someone who knows how the burial was done. Nobody else can dig it up right.'" Okafor pushed off the rail. "Think about it, Captain. The answer's wanted by Friday."

She went down the ladder. Voss stayed at the rail until the copper light failed and the first stars came out over the water, and she thought about horizons — about how a horizon looks like an ending your whole life until the day you sail far enough to learn it was an opening all along.

Below her, in the lamplit cabin that had briefly been her prison, a lamp burned where Teo Brandt was teaching Ines Nakamura the Corvus Fleet's old survey codes, preparing her for the dive season, for the wreck, for the long patient work of raising a truth the sea had guarded for a century. Their voices rose faintly through the deck planks, arguing happily about sediment layers.

Mara Voss watched the dark water where the Meridian Star lay sleeping, and made her decision the way she made all of them: quickly, and for keeps.

Friday, then. She'd give them Friday.

But first — she turned toward the companionway, toward the lamplight and the voices — first she was going to go down there and tell them the tide tables were wrong, because they were always wrong, and somebody on this expedition had to know how the sea actually kept its books.
