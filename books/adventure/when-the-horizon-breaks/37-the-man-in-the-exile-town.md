## 37. The Man in the Exile Town

The bus stopped running at San Remedios, so Ines walked the last eleven kilometers.

She could have hired a car in the capital — the consortium's advance had cleared her account that morning, a number so large she'd stared at it for a full minute before closing her phone — but something about this journey felt wrong to arrive at quickly. Her father had spent six years at the end of a road that buses abandoned. It seemed only honest to walk it the way he must have walked it: slowly, in the heat, with time to reconsider every step.

The road climbed away from the coast through country that had nothing to do with the sea. Sugarcane gone feral along the verges, red dust that rose in small ghosts behind each of her footfalls, hills folded green and patient against a sky scrubbed clean by last week's storms. Twice she passed shrines at the roadside — little niches of painted concrete holding plastic flowers and photographs gone amber with sun — and twice she stopped, not to pray exactly, but because her father had taught her that you read a landscape the way you read a document: for what people had bothered to mark on it. Someone had marked these miles with devotion. That told her something about the town ahead, though she couldn't have said what.

By early afternoon the road tipped over a saddle of land and San Remedios appeared below her, and Ines understood immediately why Kenji Nakamura had been sent here and nowhere else.

It was not a poor town. That was the first thing. It was simply a finished one — a place where nothing new would ever be built again because everything worth building had been built by someone's great-grandparents and no one since had seen the point. A church with a bell tower leaning like a tired man against a wall. A plaza of old trees whose roots had shouldered up the paving stones. Shuttered arcades the color of bone. Perhaps four hundred people, moving at the speed of syrup, all of whom turned to look at her as she came down the main street with a pack on her back and salt still in her hair, because strangers did not arrive in San Remedios; they merely failed to.

An old woman selling fruit from a cart watched her pass, then called out, not unkindly:

"You'll be wanting the museum."

Ines stopped. "Is it that obvious?"

"You're the first visitor since the rains." The woman weighed a mango in her palm as if considering whether Ines deserved it. "He'll be in the back room. He's always in the back room. The front room is for tourists, and there are none."

* * *

The Museo del Pueblo de San Remedios occupied the ground floor of a building that had once been a customs house, back when anything here had been customed. Its door stood open to the street. Inside, the air was cool and smelled of paper, dust, and camphor — the exact smell of her childhood, of every archive and survey tent and hotel room where her father had spread his maps across the beds, and Ines stood in the doorway for a long moment before she could make herself go in.

The front room was, as promised, a museum of nothing much. Glass cases held chipped ceramics of no particular period. A wall of photographs showed the town's founding families, its floods, its one visit from a bishop. Hand-lettered cards identified everything in careful, slightly old-fashioned Spanish, the letterforms precise as engraving. She recognized the hand before she understood why her chest had gone tight. She had grown up watching those same letters form across site reports and birthday cards and the tide tables he'd drawn for her in ink when she was nine years old, laminated against salt spray, carried until they disintegrated.

*He'd always had beautiful handwriting,* she thought, absurdly, standing among the chipped pots. *Of everything we lost, I never thought to miss his handwriting.*

"Closed," said a voice from the back, in English, flat with routine. "Open Saturdays. Today is not—"

The voice stopped.

Kenji Nakamura came through the curtain that divided the rooms carrying a teacup, and Ines watched her father see her, and saw the cup begin to tremble before any other part of him moved.

He was smaller. That was the fact that arrived first and refused to leave. Six years and whatever those years had done to him had pared him down — the broad-shouldered man who'd hauled dive gear and core samples like they were luggage had become narrow, stooped, his hair gone fully white and cut short in a way that suggested either a barber or a hospital. His skin had the papery look of a man who lived indoors now. But his eyes were unchanged, dark and quick and startled behind old glasses, and they went over her face the way he used to read stratigraphy — layer by layer, hunting for the sequence, for what belonged to which age.

"Ines," he said.

"Dad."

Neither of them moved. Somewhere outside a dog began barking at nothing and gave up. The teacup trembled in his hand and he looked down at it as if it had betrayed him, and set it on top of a display case with exaggerated care, and Ines realized he needed the delay more than she did, and let him have it.

"You got my letter," he said finally.

"I got your letter." She kept her voice level. "Six years late. Postmarked the week before—" She stopped herself. The word *before* hung there, unfinished, because the truth was worse and stranger than death: postmarked the week before the world learned he was dead, except he wasn't, he was standing in a museum of nothing much drinking tea. "I almost burned it."

"I know." He said it without defensiveness, the way he'd say *the tide will turn at 14:40.* "I'd have burned it too."

"No, you wouldn't have. You'd have catalogued it and put it in a case."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth — not quite a smile, but the place where a smile used to live. "That's fair."

* * *

They drank tea in the back room, which was not a room so much as an excavation. Every surface held papers. Boxes labeled in that beautiful hand lined the walls, and a long table under the window bore the corpse of some project half-finished — a local land registry, she guessed, or parish records, the patient archival work of a man who had been forbidden to dig and had decided instead to read everything that had already been dug up.

He poured with steady hands now, having recovered himself, and asked questions in the manner of a man walking on river ice: How had she come? Was the coast road washed out past the ford? Had she eaten?

"Yes," she said, to the third one, though she hadn't, and he pushed a plate of dry biscuits at her anyway, and for a moment they were just two people in a hot room with tea, and it was almost bearable.

Then he said, "Tell me about the Meridian Star," and the ice cracked straight down the middle.

"You know about it." Not a question. "You've known since the storm."

"The radio here gets the capital stations." He wrapped both hands around his cup. "There was a story. Salvage concessions, a magistrate arrested, federal inspectors, a wreck identified as the *Meridian Star* after a hundred and forty years." He paused. "And a woman named Nakamura listed as lead salvor of record."

"That was mostly luck," Ines said. "And Teo Brandt. And a captain named Voss who decided she'd rather survive than obey. And Doña Imelda Cruz, who you apparently tried to warn, once."

His head came up sharply. "Imelda answered my letter?"

"She keeps everything. Including your letter." Ines set down her cup. "Dad. I found your strongbox in the outer chamber. Your survey marks. You found that cave years ago, didn't you. Before Vance. Before any of it."

Her father looked at the table for a long time. When he spoke, his voice had dropped into the register she remembered from lectures, the one he used when the material was heavy and he wanted it handled gently.

"I found the timbers after a storm, the year before the report. Just the outer chamber — I never got through the flooded passage, I didn't have the equipment or the money. But I knew what the stamps meant. Anyone who knew the legend knew what they meant." He turned the cup slowly. "I filed a preliminary notice. One page. And then Aurelio Vance came to my hotel room at two in the morning, very friendly, very reasonable, and explained to me what my career would look like if that notice ever matured into a claim. And then Teo Brandt dove the site for Corvus and reported it as ballast stones and driftwood, and the official report buried me, and I—" His hands tightened on the cup. "I ran. That's the part the ledgers don't say, Ines. They didn't destroy me and I stayed to fight. They destroyed me and I *ran*. I let them have it. I took the exile they offered because fighting meant risking you, and I was a coward about it, and I have been a coward about it every day since."

"You wrote to Imelda," Ines said. "That's not nothing. That's a man keeping a fuse lit."

"It's a man writing letters he has no right to expect anyone to act on." He looked up at her then, and his eyes were wet and furious at themselves. "You acted on it. My daughter finished what I abandoned. Do you understand how that lands? I taught you tide tables and sediment reading and I never once taught you how to forgive me, because I never gave you anything that could be forgiven *from*. I left you the debts and the name and the anger, and you turned all three into—" He gestured helplessly at her, at the whole impossible fact of her sitting in his back room. "Into that."

"Don't," Ines said quietly.

"Ines—"

"Don't do the eulogy, Dad. You're alive. I spent two weeks believing you were dead because everyone said you were dead, and I dug your grave in my head and planted things in it, and now you're pouring me bad tea in a town with no bus service, and I don't know yet what to do with that." Her voice held, barely. "So don't make speeches. Just answer questions. Can you do that?"

He nodded. He had always been able to take instruction; it was apology he'd never mastered.

"Why here?"

"Vance chose it. It's his wife's family's home valley. Putting me somewhere visible to his own people was a message — *look what happens* — and also insurance. A broken man in a town that owes his in-laws doesn't cause trouble." He almost smiled. "It was clever. It was also a mistake, because San Remedios is kind, and kind towns adopt their prisoners. I've been happier here than I deserve."

"What happened to the report? The original one, the fake survey?"

"Burned, presumably. Or in Vance's files." He frowned. "Why?"

"Because the ledgers prove he falsified it, and the manifest proves the cargo contradicts it, but the report itself is the document the world believed. If it survives anywhere, it's evidence with his signature on it." She paused. "Also — and I want you to hear this part clearly — the consortium that's reopening the site wants the original survey methodology. Yours. The notes you filed before Vance shut you down. They say your approach to the flooded passage was twenty years ahead of anything else in the region."

Her father stared at her. "That's not possible. Those notes were rejected by every journal on three coasts."

"They were rejected by editors Vance leaned on. The consortium found them anyway. Dad—" She made herself slow down, made herself say it plainly, because she had rehearsed this on the road and the rehearsal kept collapsing. "They're offering you a position. Consulting archaeologist. Not honorary. Working. They want you on the water in the spring."

The silence that followed was the longest of the day. Outside, the light had begun to go gold, laying itself across the boxes of papers, the beautiful handwriting, the museum of nothing much that a broken man had built around himself like a reef.

"I'm sixty-eight years old," he said at last.

"So am I going to have to tell them you said no?"

"I didn't say that." He removed his glasses and pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes, and for a moment he was just an old tired man at a table, and Ines felt the anger she had carried for six years shift inside her like sediment settling — not gone, not resolved, but finding a lower, quieter level. "I need to think. I need to—" He put the glasses back on and looked at her, and there was something naked in his face she had never seen there before, not even in the old days. "Will you stay tonight? There's a room. It's not much. Nothing here is much."

* * *

The room was behind the museum, off a courtyard with a single lemon tree, and it contained a bed, a chair, and forty years of her father's life in boxes. She unpacked almost nothing. She sat on the bed while the light died and listened to the sounds of San Remedios evening — church bells, dogs, someone's radio playing a song older than both of them — and at some point she heard him moving in the front room, and then not moving, and she knew he was standing among his cases looking at nothing.

She went out. He was at the long table under the lamp, and he had taken out a sheet of paper and was drawing on it absently, and she saw with a jolt that it was a tide table. San Remedios had no coast worth naming, and still his pen moved through the familiar columns — times, heights, the moon's phases — the way another man might doodle faces.

"You still do that," she said.

"Habit." He didn't look up. "You never know when you'll need to know where the water is."

"You always know where the water is."

"That was the idea." He set down the pen. Under the lamp his face was all planes and shadow, older than sixty-eight, older than the wreck. "Ines. I owe you an accounting. Not the speech — the real one. What I cost you. I've had six years to write it in my head and it comes out different every time, and I suspect the true version is simply: everything. I cost you everything, and you paid it, and you built something out of the wreckage that I couldn't build out of the intact ship." He turned the tide table toward her. On the bottom margin, in that beautiful hand, he had written a single line while she watched: *I am sorry. I will keep saying it wrong until I say it right.*

Ines looked at it for a long time.

"It's a start," she said. Her voice came out rougher than she intended. "Don't laminate it. Some things should be allowed to wear out."

He laughed — a short, surprised sound, rusty from disuse — and then his face changed, and he reached beneath the table and brought up a box she hadn't seen before, smaller than the others, unlabeled.

"There's something you should have," he said. "I was going to send it with the letter and lost my nerve. Then I was going to give it to you tonight at dinner and lost my nerve again. So." He opened the lid.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth, lay a notebook. She knew it instantly — the same brand he'd used all her childhood, canvas-bound, salt-stained. Her father's field notebook from the season of the *Meridian Star*: the original survey notes, the ones the journals had rejected, the ones the consortium wanted. But tucked into the cover was something else, a photograph gone soft at the corners, and she lifted it into the lamplight and forgot how to breathe.

It was her. Fifteen years old, maybe sixteen, standing on the foredeck of a survey launch in a life jacket too big for her, squinting into the sun, holding up a core sample like a trophy. On the back, in his handwriting: *First solo core. She read the sediment better than I did.*

"You kept this," she said. "In the box. With the survey notes. All these years."

"Those were the two things worth saving," he said simply. "The work, and the reason for it. Everything else was negotiable."

Ines sat down across from him. The lamp hummed. Somewhere in the plaza the bells rang the hour, and neither of them counted it.

"We'll need to talk about the spring," she said finally. "The site, the permits, the dive plan. There's a lot to rebuild and most of it is underwater."

"Yes," her father said. "There is." He picked up his pen again, and turned to a fresh page, and began, carefully, in that beautiful hand, to draw the coastline.
