## 38. The Meridian Reopened

The permit arrived on a Tuesday, in an envelope heavy enough to feel like a promise.

Ines stood at the counter of the harbormaster's office on Isla Verde and turned it over twice before she opened it, the way she'd learned to turn over anything that came bearing good news. Six years of other people's discarded permits had taught her that paper could lie. Paper had lied for Vance for thirty years — survey reports, manifests, a career's worth of clean official ink over rot. But this envelope held a document with a federal seal at the top and her own name typed beneath it: *I. Nakamura, Lead Salvor, Meridian Star Archaeological Recovery Project.* Not Nakamura the disgraced's daughter. Not the woman who scraped cliff scree for other people's leavings. Her name, signed to something legitimate, countersigned by people who had actually read the ledgers.

She read it three times in the harbor light, then folded it along its original creases and put it inside her jacket, against her chest, where she'd once carried a waterproofed logbook through an ebb current that wanted to take her apart.

Outside, the morning was doing what Isla Verde mornings did — burning off the haze in gold sheets, the water going from pewter to hammered brass. The *Meridian* — their vessel now, not the wreck but the ship they'd bought with consortium money, a former research trawler with a new crane arm and a dive platform welded broad as a dance floor — sat at berth four with her hull freshly painted the blue-gray of deep water. Someone had stenciled the name in white letters tall as a man. Below it, smaller: *Meridian Star Recovery Expedition.*

Teo was already aboard. He was always already aboard; he slept on the boat more often than not, she suspected, though he'd never said so and she'd never asked. He stood at the rail with a mug of coffee going cold in his hand, watching the site buoy two miles out where it bobbed above the cave like a period at the end of a long sentence.

"You're smiling," he said as she came up the gangway.

"I'm reading."

"You're smiling while reading. That's worse." He handed her the coffee without being asked. It was terrible — he made coffee the way he dove, with more enthusiasm than method — and she drank it anyway. "Voss wants lines out by nine. She says the tide doesn't care about our paperwork."

"Voss is right."

"Voss is always right. It's exhausting." But he said it the way he'd been saying things for months now, with the shame burned off him like fog off water. Renn's testimony had done part of it. The rest, Ines thought, had come from the last dive — from hauling himself through a collapsing passage with ledgers strapped to his chest and leaving a fortune in gold coin sitting in the dark because the weight would have killed him. Nobody came back from a choice like that unchanged. Teo had come back lighter than any cargo manifest could measure.

She looked at the buoy again. Two miles of open water, and beneath it, forty feet down behind a throat of flooded rock, the Meridian Star lay exactly where the sea had kept her for a hundred and sixty years — or most of her did. The parts Vance hadn't blown apart. The naval inspector's report ran to ninety pages and used the word *irretrievable* eleven times, and every time Ines read it she heard the detonation again, felt the cliff shudder under her boots, watched the smoke roll out of the cave mouth like the island exhaling after holding its breath for decades.

He had tried to bury it. Instead he'd opened it to the world.

That was the arithmetic she'd stopped fighting months ago. The blast had cost them the outer chamber, half the cargo hold, and — for a black week when no one knew if Voss would surface — nearly everything else. But it had also cracked the vault room open like a walnut, laid the ledgers out in the rubble where the press photographers' floodlights found them, and put Magistrate Aurelio Vance on a federal launch in handcuffs made of his own signature. The architect buried by his blueprint. She'd written that line herself, later, in the margin of a newspaper she still couldn't throw away.

"Ines." Voss's voice came down from the wheelhouse, flat and precise as a depth reading. "Tide turns in fifty minutes. Are we working or are we having feelings?"

"We're working," Ines called back.

"Then move. Brandt, stop flirting with the horizon."

"He started it," Teo said, but he was already moving, and so was she.

* * *

The first dive of the reopened expedition was, by every professional standard, unremarkable. Survey transects. Photogrammetry passes over the stern section. Teo ran the dive brief with the dry competence of a man who'd found the one job he was built for, and the two graduate students the consortium had sent — Okafor and Lindqvist, young enough to be starstruck and trying very hard to hide it — hung on his every word about silt discipline and line protocol as though he were reciting scripture.

Ines went down with them anyway. She didn't have to. Lead salvors signed permits; they didn't have to get wet. But she'd spent six years learning this coastline with her hands, and there was a debt between her body and this water that paperwork couldn't settle.

They descended the shot line into water gone green-gold with morning, and the wreck assembled itself out of the murk the way it always did — first the shadow of the hull, then the ribs, then the detail: copper sheathing furred with a century of growth, the great fallen mast lying across the sand like a spine, coral colonizing the capstan until it looked less like machinery than like a reef dreaming of being a ship. The students' lights swept and darted. Ines let hers stay low and steady, tracing the sediment fans she'd learned to read before she learned cursive, her father's voice in her head the way it always was down here: *the seabed keeps its own books, mija. Your job is just to be honest about what's written.*

There was a new book open on the seafloor now. Where the demolition charges had torn the cliff face, the rock had settled into fresh talus slopes, and the naval teams' recovery work had left clean-cut channels through the debris — and there, exposed at the edge of the collapse zone, a section of inner passage that had been sealed since before anyone alive was born. The inspectors had flagged it, photographed it, and left it for the archaeological phase. For her.

She hung in the water at its mouth for a long moment, breathing slow, watching her exhalations climb toward a surface she couldn't see. Behind that wall of collapsed stone, the vault room's far reaches waited. Maybe nothing. Maybe more ledgers. Maybe the captain's personal papers, the ones the inventory called *miscellaneous correspondence, water-damaged, low priority* — which was how official language always described the things that mattered most.

Her light caught movement at the edge of vision and she turned, hand going to her knife out of six years of habit, and found only Teo, hovering ten feet away, watching her watch the passage. He tapped his wrist gauge, then pointed up: time. She nodded. But as they rose together through the green, he caught her fin and slowed her, and behind his regulator his eyes were asking the question he'd been too disciplined to ask on the surface.

*Tomorrow?*

She looked back down at the dark seam in the rock, at the hundred-and-sixty-year-old silence behind it, and answered the only way that mattered: she pointed at the passage, then held up one finger.

Tomorrow.

* * *

Doña Imelda Cruz came to the dock at noon, which meant the entire harbor rearranged itself around her arrival the way iron filings obey a magnet. She was seventy-eight years old and walked the planks like she owned them, which morally speaking she did; her family's shipping money had built half this waterfront before anyone thought to ask what it was built on. After the ledgers, everyone asked. Imelda had answered publicly, in front of cameras, with a composure Ines would study for the rest of her life: *Yes. My grandfather's name is in those books. I will not insult my ancestors by pretending otherwise, and I will not insult the living by hiding from it.*

She arrived at the *Meridian* carrying a covered basket and an expression that dared anyone to comment on either.

"Lunch," she announced, handing the basket up to Teo. "Because you three eat like salvage divers, which I suppose you are. And because I wished to see it with my own eyes." She turned a slow circle on the deck, taking in the crane, the dive platform, the stenciled name. "A ship named for a wreck. Only archaeologists would think that was fitting instead of unlucky."

"It's fitting," Ines said. "The wreck is the best thing that ever happened to this island. It just took a hundred and sixty years and a lot of bad men to say so out loud."

Imelda's eyes, sharp as a gull's, settled on her. "And on you. Don't be modest, girl, it doesn't suit you and no one believes it. You carried the truth across this island in your shirt. My grandmother told me stories about the night the Star went down — how the old families' houses were lit with candles paid for by money that never should have existed. I thought they were just stories." She paused, and something moved behind the composure, brief as a fish under a boat. "It is a strange inheritance, learning your family's fortune was theft. But it is a cleaner one than pretending."

"That's why the consortium wanted you on the oversight board," Ines said. "Not despite the ledgers. Because of them."

"Yes, well." Imelda set her chin at the angle of a woman declining to be moved. "Someone must make sure you dig honestly. And someone must make sure the island's share of whatever is recovered stays on the island — the museum, the school, the records office. Vance stole from this coast for thirty years. The least the next chapter can do is give some of it back." She said *the next chapter* deliberately, Ines noticed, the way she said everything deliberately, and Ines felt the phrase land somewhere under her ribs and stay there.

Before she left, Imelda paused at the gangway and looked back. "Your father's museum," she said. "The little one, inland. I have sent them boxes. Proper archival storage, cataloguing help. A real collection deserves—" She stopped herself, recalibrated. "When you see him. Tell him the Cruz family remembers what he tried to do. We were not brave enough to stand with him then. We can be honest enough to honor him now."

Ines managed to say thank you. It came out steadier than she expected. The gangway rattled; the magnet moved on; the harbor filings settled.

* * *

Late afternoon, Voss found her in the wheelhouse running tide tables.

Mara Voss captained the way she'd commanded the Corvus flagship, except that now the orders she gave were ones she'd helped write, and Ines had learned to read the difference in her shoulders — a looseness that hadn't been there a year ago, when she'd stood on another deck under another flag knowing her employer planned to sink her with it. The mutinous first mate was long gone, arrested with the rest of Vance's apparatus. The crew Voss had hired since were people she'd chosen herself, and it showed in everything from the maintenance logs to the way nobody flinched when she raised her voice.

"Okafor tells me you flagged the inner passage for tomorrow," Voss said, leaning against the chart table. "Against my recommendation."

"Your recommendation was to wait for the structural survey."

"My recommendation was correct. The passage is unstable, the ceiling shows fresh fracturing, and we have a federal mandate to prioritize safety over—"

"Over what?"

Voss's jaw worked. "Over momentum," she admitted finally. "You want to go in tomorrow because waiting feels like losing, and I understand that better than anyone alive. But I've buried divers, Nakamura. On Corvus jobs, we buried them and filed the paperwork and sailed home. I am not doing that on this ship. Not for momentum. Not even for you."

Ines closed the tide table. Outside, the evening light was turning the water to wine-dark bronze, and the site buoy blinked its small patient red.

"The structural survey comes back Thursday," Ines said. "If it clears, we go Friday. If it doesn't clear, we shore it, brace it, and go when it does. I'm not Renn, Mara. I'm not going to trade anyone's life for what's behind that wall." She met Voss's eyes. "But I am going to go. Eventually. Because my father spent six years knowing what was down there and dying of it slowly in a town where nobody believed him, and I spent six years hating him for sins that weren't his. Whatever's in that passage belongs to both of us, and I'm not leaving it in the dark one season longer than the science requires."

Voss studied her for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, she smiled — a rare event, weather-changing, noted by the crew the way sailors note a halo around the moon.

"Friday, then," she said. "Assuming the rock agrees." At the door she paused. "For what it's worth — the board asked me yesterday if I had concerns about you as lead salvor. Given your history. Given your father." She let that hang. "I told them the person I want holding the permit is the one who knows exactly what it costs to lose one."

* * *

She stayed on deck after dark, which had become a habit she no longer fought. The anchor lights of the harbor swung in their reflections. Somewhere inland, past the mangrove line and the karst country that had once hunted her, a small museum with proper archival storage now held a modest collection of potsherds and survey instruments and one framed newspaper clipping — and a man who had once been the most celebrated archaeologist on this coast, keeping it all dusted.

Six tides. That was all it had taken, in the end. Six tides to undo thirty years. She'd counted them like heartbeats in the worst weeks of her life, and now here she stood on the far side of them with a permit bearing her own name and a ship named for the truth.

Teo came up the companionway with two mugs, handed her one, and leaned on the rail beside her without speaking, which was one of the many things she'd come to value about him — his understanding that some silences were load-bearing.

"Structural survey team arrives Wednesday," he said eventually. "Good people. I checked."

"You checked."

"I check everyone now. Occupational hazard." He sipped his coffee — he'd made this batch properly, she noted, progress — and nodded toward the dark water where the buoy blinked. "You know what's funny? A year ago I'd have sold this whole operation's location for enough to clear my debts. Part of me still wakes up sometimes and waits for the version of me that would do it." He turned the mug in his hands. "He doesn't show up anymore. I keep waiting and he just... doesn't."

"I know," Ines said. "I keep waiting for the version of me that burns the letter."

"And?"

"She doesn't show up either." She looked east, where the horizon was a black seam between darker blacks, and thought about tomorrow's dawn, and the day after, and the passage behind the rock that held whatever it held. "Turns out we're stuck with each other. The improved models."

"Tragic," Teo said, and drained his coffee, and went below.

Ines stayed. The tide was turning — she could feel it before she could see it, the harbor's slow exhalation, the moored boats swinging on their chains to face the new current. Her father had taught her to read that turn in the set of a floating log, in the color of the water over the bar, in everything. He had taught her everything except how to forgive him, and she was learning that part herself now, one visit at a time, awkward and unfinished and real.

Below the buoy, behind the fractured rock, the Meridian Star kept her last secrets. Tomorrow the survey team would tell her whether the sea was ready to give them up. And on Friday — if the rock agreed, if the tide tables held, if the improved versions of themselves showed up the way they'd promised each other they would — Ines Nakamura would lead the first legitimate dive into the inner passage in a hundred and sixty years, with her name signed to the permit and her father's tide tables in her kit bag, into the dark where the truth had waited all this time for someone honest enough to carry it into the light.

The buoy blinked red, red, red, patient as a pulse.

Two miles out, something in the deep water shifted — a swell moving against the wind, the kind of long low pulse her father would have called a groundswell from a storm two thousand miles away, still traveling, still arriving, right on schedule.

The sea, Ines thought, turning toward her cabin and the tide tables waiting on her desk. It always keeps its own books.

Friday, she'd start reading the next page.
