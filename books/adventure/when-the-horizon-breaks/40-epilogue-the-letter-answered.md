## 40. Epilogue: The Letter Answered

The letter had lived in Ines Nakamura's kit bag for eight months now, riding through the collapse of the cave, the overland run, the ferry at dawn, and every quiet morning since. It had gone soft at the folds, its edges furred from handling, the postmark — that impossible postmark, dated six years after it should have been, one week before she'd learned her father was alive — worn nearly to a ghost. She kept it in the same oilcloth sleeve as her tide tables, which was either sentiment or superstition, and she had stopped pretending to herself that there was much difference between the two.

Tonight she took it out again.

The museum apartment — hers now, technically, though the consortium's lawyers were still arguing about whether a lead salvor could also hold a residential lease on a building owned by the historical trust — smelled of salt and old paper and the coffee Teo had left brewing before he walked down to check the moorings. Through the window, Isla Verde's harbor lay flat and pewter-colored under a rising moon, and beyond the breakwater, where the Meridian Star still slept in her flooded tomb, the water held the light like something patient. Like something willing to wait.

She spread the letter on the desk beneath the lamp and read it for what she told herself was the last time.

*Ines —*

*I don't know if you'll come. I don't know if you should. I have spent six years learning how to be sorry in ways that don't require an audience, and I find I'm not very good at it. But the survey station is still standing, and so am I, and if you ever want to see the tide tables I made for you when you were nine — the ones with your name on them, because you insisted — they're in the third drawer of the chart cabinet, where they have always been.*

*There is a wreck off the north cliffs. I found it before anyone. I need you to know that, even if you never believe anything else about me.*

*— Your father*

Short. Terrible. Honest in the way that only frightened men are honest, which is to say incompletely, and too late, and with the important parts hidden in the unimportant ones. She had almost burned it, that first night in chapter two of this whole disaster, standing over the stove in her rented room with the match already struck. She had packed her kit instead. And everything that followed — the cave, the manifest, Vance's handwriting in the margins of a falsified survey, the ledgers, the gold she'd left behind in the dark, the man she'd found in the exile town running a museum of nothing much — all of it had grown out of that single struck match that failed to fall.

She touched the paper once, lightly, the way you touch a scar to confirm it has stopped hurting.

Then she took out fresh paper, and a pen, and began to write.

*Dear Father,*

She stopped. Crossed it out. *Dear Kenji.* Stopped again. The lamp hummed. Somewhere below, the harbor water lapped against pilings with the unhurried rhythm of a tide keeping its own books, indifferent to human accounting, and she thought — not for the first time — that her father had taught her everything he knew about reading that rhythm except how to forgive it, and how to be forgiven by it, and that those last two lessons had no tables. You learned them the way you learned the sea: by going out into them and being wrong, sometimes catastrophically, and going out again anyway.

She started over.

*Dad,*

Better. Smaller. True.

*Your letter took six years to reach me, or I took six years to be ready for it; I've decided we can split the difference. I'm writing back from the harbor at Isla Verde, in a room above a museum that is mostly your fault. You'll be pleased to know the Meridian Star is exactly where your survey marks said she'd be. You'll be less pleased to know it took a storm, a corrupt magistrate, two near-drownings, and the complete collapse of Aurelio Vance's career to prove it. But then you always did say the sea keeps its own books. It turns out it does. It just needed someone to read them out loud.*

*Vance is in federal custody. Voss captains our vessel now — she asked me to tell you that a good captain knows which orders to refuse, and that she refused his for eleven years before she finally got the chance to say so out loud. Teo sends his regards and won't explain why he goes quiet whenever your name comes up. I think you two should meet. I think it would cost him something he needs to pay. I think you'd let him.*

*The gold is gone. Most of it went down with the inner chamber when Vance blew the cave, and what's left is buried under forty tons of limestone that the consortium estimates will take three seasons to clear. My debts are not erased. I am, in fact, poorer than I was the day I found the strongbox with your marks on it. And I want you to understand that I have never once regretted trading it, because what I bought instead was the thing you spent your whole career trying to give me and I spent my whole adulthood refusing to accept: a name that means what it says.*

*They put my signature on the permit, Dad. Mine. Not yours, not Vance's, not some committee's. When the new expedition opens the site next month, it will be under a permit that reads NAKAMURA, I., LEAD SALVOR, and there is a line in the provenance file — the official one, the permanent one — that credits the original discovery to K. Nakamura, field survey, and notes that the intervening report was falsified. Your name is back in the record. Not clean — nothing comes back from that kind of drowning clean — but back. Legible. Findable by anyone who goes looking, which is more than they ever intended to allow you.*

*I found the tide tables. Third drawer, chart cabinet, just like you said. They were in the exile town museum, in a crate marked PERSONAL EFFECTS — DO NOT CATALOGUE, which I have decided to interpret as your way of saying you hoped someone would catalogue them eventually. They're on my desk now. I use them. Your margins are full of corrections in your terrible handwriting, and every time I check a spring tide against your figures I get to be angry at you and grateful to you in the same breath, which I suspect is simply what having a father is, and which I intend to keep doing for a long time.*

*I'm not writing to say it's finished between us. I don't think things like us finish; I think they turn, like tides, and some of them take years to come back around, and some of them come back changed — lower, or higher, or carrying silt from places neither of us expected. I'm writing because you wrote first, and because someone had to answer, and because I have learned — expensively, at the bottom of a flooded cave with a failing regulator and a man's life in my hands — that waiting for the perfect moment is just another way of letting the water close over the entrance.*

*So here is my answer, six years late and one street early:*

*I'm coming to see you. Not at the old survey station — the consortium has it scheduled for structural assessment, and besides, I've done enough meeting people in ruins this year. Sunday. Your museum. I'll bring the tide tables so you can correct my corrections. I'll bring coffee, because I remember how you take it and I refuse to let you drink whatever it is they serve in exile towns. I'll bring the manifest copy, because you earned the right to read your own vindication in the original hand that buried it.*

*I won't bring forgiveness, because I don't think forgiveness is a thing you carry; I think it's a thing you practice, daily, like checking a tide table against the actual water. But I'll bring the beginning of it. That seemed fair.*

*Come to the door when you hear me on the stairs. I know you'll pretend you didn't hear me. Pretend badly.*

*— Ines*

She read it through twice. She did not cross anything else out. Then she folded it along clean lines, slid it into an envelope, and addressed it in block letters:

**KENJI NAKAMURA**
**THE LITTLE MUSEUM OF ISLA VERDE HISTORY (FORMERLY NOTHING MUCH)**
**CALLE DEL FARO, ONE STREET OVER**

She sat with the envelope for a while, weighing it in her palm. Eight months ago it would have been easier to burn. Six months ago, easier to bury in the cliff cache with the rest of what she couldn't yet face. Even a month ago, standing in the ruined cave at low water, watching the sea give back nothing but stone and silence where a fortune used to be, she might have found a reason to wait one more tide, and then one more after that.

But the horizon had broken open instead of her, and she was tired of letters that traveled six years to arrive, and the distance between her door and his was, she had measured it twice, four hundred and twelve paces. A person could carry a lot across four hundred and twelve paces. A person could carry it themselves.

Outside, the moon had cleared the headland, and the tide was turning — she could feel it in the changed note of the water against the breakwater, that subtle slackening her father had tried to teach her when she was nine and she had pretended not to care. She pressed the stamp down firmly, all four corners, the way he'd taught her that too — *a letter that isn't sealed properly is just a wish, mija* — and set it upright on the desk where the lamplight caught it.

Tomorrow she would walk it to the box on Calle del Faro. Four hundred and twelve paces, plus one street. Some deliveries you made yourself.

Some tides, she thought, turning off the lamp and letting the harbor light take over the room, some tides take a long, long time to turn.

But they always do.

And hers — hers was coming in.
