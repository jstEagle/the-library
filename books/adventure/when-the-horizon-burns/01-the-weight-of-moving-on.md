## 1. The Weight of Moving On

The town had three names and none of them mattered.

On the Peruvian side of the bridge they called it Puerto Ceniza—though the river that gave it the name ran brown and sluggish now, thick with silt from a watershed nobody was allowed to visit. On the Bolivian maps it was Villa Esperanza, hope town, a joke so old the locals had stopped laughing at it. The truck drivers who passed through called it nothing at all. They just slowed for the military checkpoint, paid whatever the sergeant asked, and kept going toward the coast or the mines or wherever men without choices were still needed.

Ansel Sandoval had been there eleven days. That was eight days too long.

He knew it the way he knew weather coming over a ridge—not from any single sign but from the accumulation of small wrongnesses. The market woman who'd sold him coffee every morning since he arrived had started looking at him a half-second too long. A boy on a bicycle had pedaled past the hostal twice yesterday evening, slow, not even pretending to deliver anything. And this morning, when Ansel walked down to the river to wash his face, he'd found a cigarette butt floating in the shallows that wasn't there before, still dark at one end, dropped by someone standing exactly where he stood.

Eleven days. He'd stayed longer in worse places and lived. But those places hadn't shared a border with Peru, and Peru had a long memory where he was concerned, and the kind of men Peru hired to enforce its memory drank beer at the cantina two doors down from his room and played cards until midnight and never once looked at him directly, which was itself a kind of looking.

So this morning he did what he always did when the ground started whispering: he took inventory.

Not of gear—he could do gear blindfolded, in the dark, in a storm, and had. He took inventory of people. Because gear could be replaced in any city between here and Ushuaia, but people, good ones, ones who stayed—that was the only currency he'd ever had that appreciated instead of rotted.

* * *

Teo was in the back courtyard of the hostal, cross-legged on the packed dirt, surrounded by the exploded anatomy of three outboard motors and a water pump that had no business being in this country at all. He worked the way he did everything—fast, silent, slightly offended by the object in front of him. His forged passport lay tucked inside a plastic sleeve under a loose brick by the drainpipe, because Teo trusted bricks more than pockets.

"Fuel pump's shot," Teo said without looking up. "Both spares are shot. I can keep one running if we baby it."

"Baby it," Ansel said.

"I'm serious. No hard starts, no running dry, no letting Pico near it."

"Pico doesn't touch the engines. That's been the rule since the Orinoco."

"The Orinoco was a spark plug. This would be an engine block through somebody's skull." Teo finally looked up. He was thirty-four and looked fifty around the eyes, the way men did who'd spent their twenties fixing things in places that wanted them broken. "How long are we staying?"

"Leaving soon."

"That's what you said four days ago."

"And it's still true. It just keeps becoming true later than I want." Ansel crouched, picked up a piston ring, turned it in the light. "Anything else?"

Teo hesitated. That was the thing about Teo—his silences were load-bearing. You learned to read them like cracks in rock.

"There's a man," Teo said. "Been asking about us at the fuel depot. Not the police. Private. Boots too clean for here."

"How many times?"

"Twice that I know of. Maybe more." He wiped his hands on a rag already black with grease. "Ansel. Eleven days."

"I know."

"Do you? Because you've got that look. The one where you're doing arithmetic and you don't like any of the numbers."

Ansel set the piston ring down. "Ilsa up yet?"

"You're avoiding the question."

"I'm sequencing it. Ilsa first. You second. The question third, after coffee."

Teo snorted and went back to the motor, which meant the conversation wasn't over, only deferred—and deferral with Teo had a shelf life of about six hours. Ansel filed that away with everything else and went to find his medic.

* * *

Ilsa kept her kit packed. She'd told him once, in the blue hour before dawn somewhere on the Marañón, that she'd slept better in field hospitals than she ever had in apartments, and Ansel understood it completely, because a packed bag was a promise you made to yourself: *you can leave, you will leave, leaving is always available.* He suspected that for both of them the bag was less about readiness than about permission.

She was awake, of course. She was always awake before him, though he rose at five. She sat on the narrow bed in her room with the window open to the courtyard, mending a strap on a pack with surgical thread because it was what she had and it held better than anything sold in this town.

"Teo says we're leaving soon," she said, before he'd finished crossing the threshold. "Which means you haven't decided."

"I've decided. I'm waiting for the decision to finish cooking."

"Mm." She bit the thread, tied it off, tested the strap with a sharp yank. Her hands were steady. They were always steady; it was the rest of her that occasionally trembled, late at night, in ways she thought nobody saw. "We're low on amoxicillin. Low on sutures. Out of the good painkillers entirely—I've been rationing placebos and prayer. If we take another job like the last one, I need a real pharmacy, and the nearest real pharmacy is four days north, in a country where your face is on a list."

"So we don't take another job like the last one."

"We always take another job like the last one." She looked at him then, direct, unblinking, the look she used on patients who were lying about their symptoms. "That's the business model, Ansel. We take what nobody else will. Nobody else won't take it because it's easy."

He leaned against the doorframe. Below them, in the courtyard, they could hear Pico's voice rising and falling as he talked to the boats—or rather to himself while working on the boats, which amounted to the same thing. The kid narrated everything. Knots, repairs, meals, dreams. Ten years ago Ansel would have found it exhausting. Now the silence felt wrong whenever Pico wasn't filling it.

"He asked me last night," Ilsa said quietly, "why you got blacklisted. He's never asked before. Three years he's been with us and he's never asked."

"What did you tell him?"

"That it wasn't my story to tell." She coiled the thread. "But he's twenty-two, Ansel. He's going to stop accepting that eventually. Kids do. They start needing the whole shape of a person, not just the parts you hand them."

"Then he'll be disappointed."

"Or he'll understand you better. Those are both possible outcomes." She stood, slung the med kit over her shoulder out of pure habit, and paused beside him at the door. Her voice dropped to almost nothing. "There's a man asking questions at the fuel depot. Boots too clean. Teo thinks you don't know. I'm telling you I know that you know, so you'll stop pretending the danger is theoretical."

Ansel held her gaze. "Eleven days," he said.

"Eight too many," she said, and went down to check on Pico.

* * *

Pico had grown up on the water somewhere south of Iquitos, in a stilt village whose name changed depending on the flood season, and he handled boats the way some people handled language—as something native, inherited, unquestioned. Right now he was upside down in the lead boat's bow, re-lashing a fuel tank, his legs kicking gently in time with whatever story he was telling himself.

"—so then the caimán, right, he's got my flip-flop, and Tía Rosa's screaming at me from the dock, and I'm thinking, okay, either I swim after him or I walk home one-footed, and honestly the swimming was mostly pride—"

"Pico."

The legs stopped kicking. He surfaced, grinning, hair full of sawdust and river grit. "Boss. Good morning. Beautiful morning. Slightly ominous morning, maybe, but beautiful."

"We move tomorrow. Dawn. Everything packed tonight, everything checked twice."

The grin dimmed by one degree—the degree it always dimmed when Ansel said *we move*, because Pico had learned early that the word carried weight beyond logistics. It meant the town had turned. It meant eyes on doors. It meant the rule.

Pico had never been told the rule explicitly. He'd absorbed it the way he absorbed everything, through the skin. *Never stop moving.* Ansel had built his whole crew around it, built their reputation around it, built the strange niche they occupied in the underworld of expedition work around it: the outfit that arrived, did the impossible job, and evaporated before anyone could ask follow-up questions. Clients loved it. Clients who needed deniability *adored* it. And Ansel had convinced himself, over ten years, that the rule was strategy—a competitive advantage, a brand.

Some mornings, washing his face in cold rivers, he admitted the truth: the rule wasn't strategy. The rule was scar tissue. You didn't stop moving for the same reason a man with a burned hand didn't grip things. Not because gripping was impossible. Because the last time he'd gripped something and held on, it had died in his arms.

"Tomorrow, dawn," Pico repeated. He was quiet a moment, which for Pico was seismic. Then: "Boss, can I ask you something?"

"You just did."

"A different something." He fiddled with the lashing, pulled it tight, pulled it tighter than it needed to be. "The river east of here. The Ceniza. Why does everybody go quiet when it comes up? Even the truck guys. Even the priest. It's just a river."

Ansel watched the water of the actual Río Ceniza slide past the town's edge, brown and patient, carrying its cargo of mountain silt down from a valley that eleven families had learned, in the worst possible way, was off-limits to grief.

"It's just a river," Ansel said. "Rivers don't kill people. People upstream kill people."

Pico blinked. "That's not an answer."

"No," Ansel agreed. "It isn't." He clapped the kid on the shoulder—solid, warm, annoyingly young—and walked away before the next question could form, because the next question was always *why*, and *why* was a door Ansel had welded shut a decade ago and did not intend to test the welds on today.

* * *

The afternoon he spent on accounts, which is to say on debts.

The ledger lived in his head, as all his important records did—paper could be seized, searched, burned, read aloud in a courtroom. In his head it was safe, and also inescapable, which was the price of the arrangement.

He owed the boatyard in Yurimaguas four hundred dollars against the hull repair, payable whenever he passed through, which might be never. He owed Ilsa six months of promised wages he'd covered with promises instead, and she'd never once raised it, which made it heavier, not lighter. He owed Teo a new passport—the current forgery was aging badly, the laminate lifting at the corner, and Teo's cover story required a nationality that no longer issued visas to anyone from his actual hometown. He owed Pico an answer to a question the kid hadn't yet found the courage to ask straight out.

And beneath all of it, unpayable, unlisted, compounding at a rate no ledger could hold, he owed eleven people in a flooded valley the truth about how they'd died—and he didn't have the truth. That was the cruelest line item of all. Even his guilt was counterfeit currency. He'd confessed, in his own head, ten thousand times, to causing the disaster, and the confession had never once fit the facts, because the facts had drowned along with everything else in the missing hour at the bottom of his memory.

A guide who couldn't remember the worst night of his life. A blacklisted man whose crime was a blank page. He'd built a career on other people's blanks—the unmapped stretches, the sealed watersheds, the jobs with the names torn off—and maybe that wasn't coincidence either. Maybe he'd been drawn to unnamed things because he was one.

The light was going amber over the rooftops when the letter came.

It came the way such things came in towns like this: not delivered but *appeared*. Ansel came down from his room at dusk to eat, and the hostal's ancient proprietor—Doña Rufina, who had seen everything in forty years and acknowledged none of it—held out a plain envelope without comment, without meeting his eyes, already turning back to her television.

No stamp. No postmark. No return address. Just his name on the front, written in careful block capitals: **A. SANDOVAL**. Someone had walked it in. Someone who knew he was here, knew the room he kept, and preferred not to exist on paper.

Ansel stood in the doorway a long moment. Behind him the street noises did their evening work—motorcycles, dogs, the cantina warming up two doors down, the card players arriving for their midnight ritual. Somewhere among those sounds, possibly, were boots too clean for this town.

He took the envelope upstairs, sat on the bed, and held it under the bare bulb for a long time without opening it.

Letters were how trouble found him. Every bad turn of the last decade had begun with paper: the incident report with his thumbprint on it, the tribunal notice, the blacklist confirmation from two governments' worth of ministries. Paper was how the world reached into rooms and rearranged lives. And this envelope had no return address, which meant whoever sent it already knew he couldn't write back—which meant they already knew where he'd be, or didn't care, or wanted him to come find *them*.

He opened it.

One page. No salutation. No signature. Block capitals, the same careful hand:

**TRIPLE YOUR STANDARD RATE. ONE JOB. NO NAME ON THE CONTRACT. THE RIVER YOU DON'T TALK ABOUT. ANSWER BY FRIDAY OR THE OFFER DIES.**

Ansel read it three times. The bulb buzzed. Down in the cantina, someone laughed too loudly at something that wasn't funny.

*The river you don't talk about.*

His hands were steady holding the page. His hands were always steady; it was the rest of him that had started, very faintly, very deep down, to tremble—the part of him that had spent ten years walking around a certain valley the way you walk around a grave, eyes forward, breath controlled, never once looking down.

He should burn it. That was the protocol. That was the rule beneath the rule: paper goes in the flame, the flame goes in the sink, the sink gets rinsed, the night passes, the crew moves at dawn, and whatever ghost wrote block capitals in careful, patient strokes can haunt some other border town.

He crossed to the candle stub on the windowsill—it was how he saved the bulb, cheap habits dying hard—and held the corner of the page to the flame.

The paper caught. Orange light crawled up the words. *Triple your standard rate* curled and blackened. *The river you don't talk about* glowed, blistered, broke apart into ash that drifted onto the sill like gray snow.

And Ansel Sandoval stood there in the dark with the smell of burning ink in his nose, watching the last embers die, and understood—with the flat, cold certainty of a man reading his own pulse—that he could recite every word of that letter from memory, and that Friday was three days away, and that he was not going anywhere at dawn.

Downstairs, faintly, Doña Rufina's television laughed at its own joke.

Upstream, in a valley with no legal address, the Río Ceniza began its long brown journey toward the sea, carrying, as it had for ten years, everything no one was allowed to say out loud.
