## 2. A Name He Buried

The letter had no return address, and that was the first thing wrong with it.

Ansel Sandoval turned it over twice under the bare bulb of his rented room in Puerto Hondo, a border town that smelled of diesel and river rot and existed for no other purpose than to be left behind. The envelope was good stock—thick, cream-colored, the kind of paper that cost more per sheet than Pico made in an hour. No stamp. Hand-delivered. The postmark was absent because there had never been one, which meant someone had walked it past the landlady's table, past the dogs, up the stairs, and slid it under his door without waking him.

He didn't sleep deeply enough not to wake.

His name was written across the front in ink, blue-black, with a fountain pen's slight bleed at the terminals: *A. Sandoval*. Not *Ansel*. Not *Señor Sandoval*. Just the initial and the name, the way you'd address someone you'd once known well enough to shorten and never bothered to learn again.

He held it over the kerosene lamp before he opened it. That was the rule he'd built his life on, the same rule that kept his crew alive: anything that arrives uninvited is either a trap or a bill, and both are cheaper burned.

The corner caught. Flame crawled up the cream paper, and through the thinning burn he saw three words before the fire took them—*triple the rate*—and then the whole thing curled into black lace and dropped into the tin dish he kept for exactly this purpose.

Ansel sat back on the cot and listened to the town settle. A truck downshifting on the switchbacks above. The river, always the river, talking to itself in the dark. Somewhere below, Teo's radio murmured a Bolivian station at a volume only insomniacs could love.

He should have felt relief. Burning things was supposed to feel like relief.

Instead he lay awake until four in the morning doing arithmetic he didn't want to do, and the sum at the bottom of the column was a name he'd spent ten years burying in the deepest sediment of himself, and the name would not stay buried.

*Mara Voss.*

* * *

He knew the handwriting. That was the unforgivable part. Ten years, two expulsions, one blacklist that read like an obituary—*Sandoval, Ansel M.: unfit for professional guiding, conduct unbecoming, see attached incident report*—and his hands still knew her handwriting the way they knew a rope's lay or the give of bad ice. The way she crossed her T's like she was cutting something. The pressure she put on downstrokes, as if every word were being driven into the page rather than written on it.

She'd written him exactly one letter before this one. Eleven years ago, from a hotel in Coyhaique, after the hearings, after she stood up in a room full of men in company blazers and said, in a voice like a blade going into a sheath: *I was on the ridge. I saw what he did. I can't testify to what he intended, but I can testify to what he failed to prevent.*

That letter had contained two sentences. *I'm sorry it came out this way. It shouldn't have been you.* He'd burned that one too, but slower, watching each sentence die separately, and he'd remembered both sentences verbatim ever since, which told him everything about how well burning worked.

The trouble with Mara Voss was that she'd been right about the facts and wrong about everything else, or possibly right about everything else and wrong about the facts—he'd stopped trying to sort it out around year six—and either way she'd taken the one thing he had left, which was a reputation clean enough to work with, and she'd done it while looking him in the eye. That was the part he couldn't forgive. Cowards he understood. Mara had testified the way she climbed: directly, without protection, accepting the fall as a possibility she'd already priced in.

And now she'd found him. In Puerto Hondo, of all the fly-blown terminals of the earth, where he'd gone precisely because no one looked for anyone here.

Which meant no one had been looking very hard, or she'd been looking harder than anyone.

Both possibilities were bad.

* * *

Morning came gray off the water. Ansel went down to the dock where the crew was working, because the crew was the only inventory he trusted, and even them conditionally.

Teo was under the old footbridge with a welding torch, sparks raining into the current like orange snow. The bridge belonged to a gravel concern that paid late and asked no questions, which made it Ansel's favorite kind of client. The planks had rotted through in the middle span, and a loaded truck going through would drop forty feet into the Ceniza—no, not the Ceniza, he corrected himself, it was the Río Bravo here, different river, different country, different life—but the body memory of rivers named Ceniza sat in his chest like a stone he'd swallowed and never passed.

"Third cross-brace is shot," Teo called out without surfacing. "Whoever built this thing was optimistic and drunk."

"You built its twin in Cajamarca," Ansel said.

"I was younger and drunker." Teo slid out, pushed his goggles up. He was fifty-one and built like something assembled from salvage, all angles and repairs, and his passport said he was a Chilean named Esteban Fuentes who did not exist. "We need plate steel. Real plate, not the sheet metal the supplier tried to sell Ilsa yesterday."

"How much?"

"Enough that you'll make that face."

Ansel made the face.

Ilsa arrived mid-morning with the medical kit slung over one shoulder and a boy of maybe nineteen limping behind her—a deckhand from one of the fuel barges who'd gashed his shin on a cleat. She sat him on a bollard, cleaned the wound with the unhurried economy of someone who had done ten thousand of these and been sued over one, and talked to him the whole time in a low stream of reassurance that Ansel had learned was not kindness so much as technique: a patient who was listening couldn't watch the needle.

Pico was up on the far abutment, seventeen years old and permanently in motion, re-lashing a cable with the frantic competence of a boy proving something. To whom, Ansel had never asked. There were questions you didn't ask people who'd joined your crew, the same way there were doors you didn't open in houses you didn't own. Everyone here was running from something with a paperwork trail. That was the hiring criterion. Ansel called it *experience with consequences*.

"Pico," he called. "Run to Orellana's yard. Tell him we need plate steel, eight millimeter, and tell him if he says sheet metal again I'll take my business to the woman in Villa Franca."

"The woman in Villa Franca hates you."

"She hates everyone. It's honest hatred. I trust it." He fished bills from his jacket. "And buy bread. Real bread, not the sweet stuff you like."

Pico caught the money out of the air, grinned, and was gone down the dock at a dead sprint, because Pico did nothing at less than a dead sprint. Ansel watched him go and felt the familiar ache that had no name in any language he spoke—the ache of being idolized by someone who didn't know what you'd done. Someday the boy would find out. Everyone found out eventually; it was the only reliable law in Ansel's life. The trick was to be somewhere else when it happened.

The morning proceeded. The bridge got braced. The boy got stitched. Ilsa drank coffee standing up, staring at the river with the expression of a woman conducting a private argument with it, and won.

It was a good day, by the standards of days. And underneath all of it, like a current under a calm surface, the letter burned in the tin dish upstairs kept un-burning itself, and the arithmetic ran on, and by evening Ansel admitted what the arithmetic was for.

Triple the rate. Three words visible before the flame took the rest. Triple the rate meant the job was either enormous or poisonous, and with Mara Voss those had always been the same category.

* * *

He went back up at dusk and dug the ashes out of the tin dish, which was the single most humiliating thing he'd done in years, and he'd once crawled half a kilometer through a crevasse field with a dislocated shoulder.

There wasn't much. A fragment of the corner, charred but legible. A strip with the tail end of a sentence. He laid them out on the table like a man reconstructing a wreck, and the fragments said:

*...triple the rate...*

*...no names on the contract, yours or mine...*

*...you'll want to hear the location before you refuse...*

And then, on the largest surviving piece, in handwriting pressed so hard the pen had nearly torn the paper:

*You remember the last hour, Ansel. You just don't know it yet.*

He sat down slowly on the cot.

The room tilted—not literally, but the way a room tilts when something you've built your balance on shifts a few degrees. Because there it was. The thing. The hour. The hole in the middle of the worst night of his life, the gap between the dam lights going out and waking up downstream in the mud with his lungs full of river and Tomás gone, a gap he'd spent a decade treating like missing teeth—you learned to talk around it, eat around it, build a face around it.

Everyone else had accepted the story. The inquiry had accepted the story. The incident report with his thumbprint on it had accepted the story. *Guide error, inadequate margin assessment, failure to halt the crossing.* His disgrace had a signature line and he had signed it, and the signing was the only part of that night he could actually remember, his own thumb pressing ink onto paper in a room that smelled of cigarettes and wet wool, and men whose faces he could not retrieve no matter how hard he reached.

*You remember the last hour. You just don't know it yet.*

What kind of sentence was that? What did it even mean? Either he remembered or he didn't. Unless—

Unless the memory wasn't missing. Unless it was *stored*. Put somewhere, the way you cached supplies on a route you expected to come back to. Sealed against the day someone came asking.

He got up and paced, three steps to the window, three steps back, the dimensions of every cheap room he'd ever lived in. Outside, the Río Bravo muttered in the dark, and he hated that he heard it as the Ceniza, hated that every river was the Ceniza now, hated most of all that some traitor part of him had known, from the moment he saw the handwriting, that he would not let this go. That the burning had been theater. That he'd performed destruction for an audience of one, and the audience hadn't believed it, and neither had the actor.

Ten years ago, Mara Voss had stood in front of a hearing room and ended his life with the truth as she understood it. She had not lied. He'd checked, obsessively, vindictively, for years—hired a lawyer in La Paz with money he didn't have, pulled the transcripts, gone over her testimony line by line looking for the flaw, the exaggeration, the bought moment. There was nothing. She'd said what she saw. What she saw had destroyed him.

So why was she writing to him now? Why triple pay and no names and *you'll want to hear the location before you refuse*?

Why did a woman who'd watched him fail write to him like a woman who suspected he hadn't?

Ansel stopped at the window. Down on the dock, a lantern moved—Teo, checking the braces a final time, because Teo checked everything a final time and then one more after that. Safe. Contained. The life Ansel had built out of motion and small contracts and never sleeping two nights in the same bed: a life designed so that no question could ever catch up with him, because he was always leaving before it finished being asked.

*You remember the last hour, Ansel.*

He thought about Tomás. Not the way he usually allowed himself—one guarded photograph's worth of grief, rationed, at altitude, where no one could see his face—but fully, the way you'd think about a man standing next to you. Tomás laughing at the put-in with a rope coil over his shoulder. Tomás arguing hydrology with a company engineer and winning. Tomás saying, the last morning Ansel could cleanly remember, *the numbers don't work, hermanito, somebody upstream is lying about how much water that dam is holding,* and grinning, because Tomás loved finding lies the way other men loved finding gold.

Tomás had gone upriver that afternoon to look at the intake works. He'd said he'd be back by dark.

The dam failed at 11:40 p.m.

Between those two facts lay eleven bodies and one blank hour, and for ten years Ansel had carried the blank like a debt, and now a woman who hated him—or had hated him, or had watched him with the cold disappointment of someone who'd once respected him—was telling him the debt had a different shape than he thought.

He went downstairs. The landlady's table was cluttered with the mail of a dozen transient lives, and he stood over it in the dark like a thief in his own house, and there was nothing new, of course. But he checked. Because whoever had delivered the first letter knew where he slept, and people who know where you sleep can deliver a second.

Back upstairs, he took out the expedition ledger—the real one, the one with the debts—and turned to the page where he tracked what they owed and what they were owed, and he looked at the column of small survivable numbers that constituted his entire remaining life, and he asked himself the question properly this time, without flinching:

If Mara Voss walked through that door tomorrow and named a price and a place, and the place was anywhere near that river—

He closed the ledger.

He didn't sleep. Around dawn, he heard footsteps on the stairs, unhurried, deliberate, and a voice at the landlady's table below, low and level and unmistakable after eleven years, asking for Señor Sandoval's room.

Not a letter this time.

Mara Voss had come herself.
