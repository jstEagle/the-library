## 3. Crew of the Unwanted

### Teo

The bridge had been dying for years, and everyone in Puerto Ceniza knew it except, apparently, the men who drove gravel trucks across it twice a day.

Teo Quispe stood at the northern abutment with a clipboard he'd stolen from a customs office in Iquique and a hard hat he'd bought honestly, and watched the structure breathe. That was the word for it. Every time a truck hit the deck, the whole span exhaled—a slow lateral shudder, cables singing a note too low to hear and too wrong to ignore. He'd been listening to structures sing for twenty years, and this one was singing a funeral hymn in the key of deferred maintenance.

"Deck's cracked through at pier three," he said, without looking up. "You can see daylight through the concrete if you lie down."

"I'm not lying down on a bridge that's about to fall into a river," said Ilsa.

"That's why I'm telling you what I see when *I* lie down."

Below them the Río Ceniza ran brown and fast with the week's rain, carrying silt down from the high country toward the coast. Teo had never seen the river's source. Almost nobody had, not since the valley above had been closed off and erased from the maps that tourists bought. He'd grown up two hundred kilometers downstream, and the Ceniza had been a fact of life the way gravity was a fact of life—present, powerful, and never explained.

"Ansel's going to want to do it live," Ilsa said.

"Ansel's going to want to do it live," Teo agreed, "because Ansel believes that if you're fast enough, physics will negotiate."

He clipped his harness to the handrail cable and swung out over the water.

* * *

The job paid four hundred dollars and a place to sleep for two weeks, which in Puerto Ceniza made them wealthy. The municipality had no budget for the bridge and no legal authority to close it, so they'd hired the only crew in the region with a reputation for doing impossible things quietly. That reputation had a name, and the name was Ansel Sandoval, and Teo had followed that name across three borders with a passport that would get him shot in one country and extradited in two others.

His real name was Teodoro Quispe Vargas. His passport said Mateo Ruiz, civil engineer, born in Valparaíso, and the forgery was good enough that Teo himself sometimes half-believed it, which was the most dangerous part. Forging a document was a technical problem. Believing it was a spiritual one.

The problem with pier three was simple to describe and miserable to fix: the earthquake seven years back had sheared the rebar inside the concrete column, and the column had been quietly folding ever since, like a man pretending not to be shot. The repair required shoring the span, cutting away the damaged section, and pouring new steel and concrete into the wound—all while trucks kept crossing, because the municipality had also no legal authority to stop the trucks.

"Ansel," Teo said, when the boss came down the embankment at dawn, "I want to shore it from below. Barges, cribbing, the whole circus."

"There's no barge in this town."

"There's no bridge in this town either, in about four months. We're just deciding the date."

Ansel crouched at the abutment and looked at the river the way other men looked at old enemies or old lovers—like he already knew how the conversation would go. He was forty-six, maybe, with a face weathered down to its load-bearing elements, and he had the particular stillness of a man who had learned that moving fast was fine as long as you never moved in panic.

"Then we do it from above," Ansel said. "We hang the shoring off the deck itself. Counterweight the far span with the gravel truck."

"With the *truck*."

"The truck is forty tons of free ballast with a driver who wants to get home. Ilsa, can you get the driver to sit still while we load the deck?"

Ilsa, who was reorganizing the medical kit for the fourth time that morning, said, "I can get a man to do anything if I make it sound like a medical recommendation."

"Teo?"

Teo did the math in his head, then did it again slower, because the first time it had come out to *possible*, which frightened him more than *impossible* would have. "It's possible," he said. "It's also insane."

"Those are usually the same thing," Ansel said, and walked off to talk to the truck driver, and that was how the day began.

* * *

By noon the shoring was hung, and by two o'clock Teo was inside the column, cutting out dead concrete with a hydraulic saw while the river hissed past four meters below. The work was close and dark and loud. It was also the only place Teo had ever felt entirely honest: inside a structure's wound, you couldn't lie about anything. The concrete told you the truth. The steel told you the truth. People were the ones who forged their documents.

He was thinking about that—about how he'd once been a man with a real name and a real license, before the collapse in Arica, before the inquiry, before the word *negligent* got stapled to him like a tag on a corpse—when the deck above him shuddered wrong.

Not the breathing shudder. A different one. A *tearing* one.

"Stop the saw," he said into the radio. "Everybody off the span. Now."

The radio crackled. Above him, through the hole he'd cut, he could see the underside of the deck, and along a seam that had been hairline that morning, a crack was walking—slow, patient, a finger of darkness extending toward the pier.

"The counterweight's shifting," Ansel's voice said, calm as a weather report. "Truck's rolling two meters. Teo, how long can you hold there?"

"Hold? I'm not holding anything. I'm *inside* it."

"Then how fast can you not be inside it?"

Teo looked at the crack. The crack looked back. He killed the saw and began to climb.

### Ilsa

Ilsa Brandt had treated four hundred patients in the refugee camps outside Mazar-e-Sharif, delivered sixty babies, amputated eleven limbs, and lost exactly one patient in a way that had followed her across an ocean and two continents. Her name had been Daniel Ferreiro. He'd been nineteen. He'd had a ruptured spleen and a blood pressure that lied to her, and she had trusted the numbers over her gut, and by the time she'd trusted her gut he'd been gone.

The tribunal had called it a judgment error. The press had called it negligence. Her own hands, at three in the morning, called it murder, and her hands were the only tribunal she couldn't resign from.

So now she was the medic for a crew of the unemployable, in a town whose name matched a river whose source was a secret, and she spent her mornings reorganizing a medical kit that did not need reorganizing and her afternoons watching Ansel Sandoval do things that should have killed someone.

Today, the thing that should have killed someone was Teo.

She heard the crack before she understood it—a sound like a bone going, transmitted through the bridge's skeleton into the abutment where she stood. Then Ansel's voice on the radio, flat and precise, and then the whole span groaned and the gravel truck on the far side slid two meters with a shriek of brakes, and the counterweight that was holding the shoring over the river became, in the space of three seconds, a pendulum.

"Teo's in the column," Pico shouted, already running for the deck. The boy was nineteen and ran everywhere, as if walking were a tax he refused to pay.

"Rope him," Ilsa said. "I'll be right behind you."

"You shouldn't be on the deck—"

"Nobody should be on the deck. Get moving."

She grabbed the trauma bag out of pure habit—she'd have grabbed it if the building were on fire, if the world were ending—and followed Pico onto a bridge that was, by any rational assessment, finished. The deck swayed under her boots like the deck of a ship. Downriver, four meters of brown water moved with the patience of something that had been collecting debts for a very long time.

Ansel was already at the column hole, flat on his stomach, reaching down into it. "Teo. Talk to me."

"I'm coming up," Teo's voice said, echoing. "I'm coming up, stop grabbing at me, you'll pull us both—"

"Then climb faster and I'll stop grabbing."

Ilsa knelt at the edge of the hole and looked down into the dark. Teo was climbing the inside of the broken column with his harness still clipped to a cable that was, she could see even from here, attached to the failing section. The crack had reached him. A chunk of concrete the size of a suitcase sagged away from the wall above his head, held by one bent rebar, and the rebar was the color of rust and bad decisions.

"Teo," she said, in the voice she used for patients in shock, low and level, "the rebar above you is going to let go. Don't look up. Climb toward my voice."

"Which voice? There are three voices."

"The prettiest one."

He laughed—she heard it, a short, startled bark—and climbed. That was the trick she'd learned in the camps, the one they never taught in medical school: fear paralyzes, but embarrassment moves. Get a man laughing and his hands remember what they're for.

The rebar let go when Teo's boots were a meter below the rim. The concrete slab swung down past him, clipped his shoulder, and dropped into the river with a sound like a door closing. Ansel had his wrist. Ilsa had his harness. Pico had Ansel's belt, which was not in any rescue manual but was very Pico. Together they hauled him out onto the deck, and the four of them lay in a row on a dying bridge, breathing hard, watching a cloud of dust rise off the water.

"My shoulder," Teo announced.

"Let me see."

"It's dislocated, isn't it."

"It's dislocated," Ilsa confirmed. "Congratulations. You get the fun one."

"Can you fix it here?"

"Here, now, or the clinic, tonight. Your choice."

"Here," Teo said. "Now. I want to watch the crack stop."

She set his shoulder against the deck planks with the old army maneuver—foot in the axilla, smooth traction, one rotation—and he made a sound like a kettle and then went quiet, and above them the bridge shuddered once more and then, for the first time all day, held still.

"Counterweight settled," Ansel said. "It's done moving. Teo, your column?"

Teo flexed his rebuilt shoulder and squinted into the hole. "Sheared clean. Everything below the crack is sound. If we pour tonight—"

"Then we pour tonight."

Ilsa sat back on her heels and looked at the three of them—Ansel already uncoiling rope, Teo already sketching in the dust with his good arm, Pico already hauling the concrete bags—and felt the thing she never let herself feel in daylight, the thing that had no clinical name. She'd lost a hospital, a license, a country, and a version of herself she'd trusted completely. And somehow, on a broken bridge over a dirty river, with a forger and a boy and a man with a dead brother and a ruined name, she'd found the only operating theater she could stand to work in: the kind where the patient was everyone.

"Ansel," she said. "If we pour tonight, who drives the truck back across at dawn to test it?"

He smiled without turning around. "The driver. He doesn't know enough to be scared."

"That's malpractice."

"That's engineering," Teo said.

"It's the same thing with worse paperwork," Ilsa said, and went to find the driver.

### Pico

Pico had three memories of his father and none of them were moving pictures—they were stills, photographs in his head: a man's back in a doorway; a hand setting a cup on a table; a pair of boots by a bed. His father had worked the high mines and come down once a month, and then one month the boots didn't come back, and Pico was seven, and after that it was his mother and the road and whatever work the road had.

So maybe that was why he followed Ansel Sandoval. Because Ansel was the only man Pico had ever met who walked into dangerous places the way his father's boots had looked like they'd walked out of them—like the danger was a country and the man had citizenship.

He'd never asked. That was the rule he'd made for himself in his first month with the crew, after he'd asked Ilsa why she'd left Germany and watched her face close like a shutter, after he'd asked Teo why his passport had a different birthday than his driver's license and gotten a look that could have cut cable. Everybody on this crew was running from something. Ansel was running from something so big it had its own weather system. You didn't ask a man about his weather. You just made sure you were standing next to him when it rained.

Tonight they poured concrete by headlamp.

The whole town had turned out, in the way of towns that had nothing else to watch: the truck driver, the mayor's cousin, a dozen kids on the far bank, an old woman selling empanadas out of a basket as if a structural emergency were a festival. Teo directed the pour from the deck with his arm in a sling he'd already taken off twice. Ilsa mixed and monitored and pretended she wasn't having the time of her life. And Ansel worked the chute, shirtless in the cold because he ran hot, moving with a rhythm Pico had spent a year trying to learn and hadn't, because the rhythm wasn't physical. It was whatever was underneath the physical.

"Hey," Pico said, during the third pour, when Ansel paused to let the mix settle. "Can I ask you something that's not about the thing?"

"That's a very careful sentence."

"I've been practicing it."

Ansel looked at him then, really looked, the way he did maybe twice a year, and Pico felt it like a searchlight. "Ask."

"The bridge. You could have waited. The municipality would have closed it eventually, or it would've fallen, and either way it's not your name on it. So why are we here? Why do we always take the jobs nobody else will touch?"

Ansel was quiet long enough that the river filled the silence. Downstream, Pico knew, the Ceniza ran on and on toward the coast, past towns and farms and the drowned memory of a valley nobody talked about. He didn't know why nobody talked about it. He'd arrived too late. The whole crew had arrived too late; that was practically the hiring requirement.

"Because jobs other people will touch," Ansel said finally, "are jobs other people have already decided the answer to. You learn nothing. You fix nothing that matters. But a job nobody will touch—" he knocked his knuckles against the fresh concrete, still warm, still soft, still deciding what it would become, "—nobody's written the ending yet. You get to find out who you are while the cement's still wet."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only kind I have."

Pico laughed. "Ilsa says you used to be famous."

"Ilsa says a lot of things."

"She says you were blacklisted from two countries."

"Ilsa should mix concrete."

"Teo says it was a dam."

The change was small. Pico almost missed it—Ansel's hands going still on the chute, the rhythm stopping, a man stepping out of the current of himself for half a second. Then it passed, so smoothly that Pico could believe he'd imagined it, except he'd spent a year learning to read this man the way Teo read cracks in concrete.

"Teo," Ansel said, "talks too much."

"He says the dam was on this river."

"The pour's settling. Grab the vibrator wand."

Pico grabbed the wand. But he kept the sentence in his pocket, the way you keep a stone you found on a trail without knowing why. *The dam was on this river.* The river they'd been working beside for a week. The river that ran brown past the town every evening, faithful as a heartbeat, coming down from somewhere high and closed that no one would name.

### Teo

At midnight the pour was done, the forms were set, and the bridge over the Río Ceniza was going to hold, and Teo sat on the abutment with a beer he was too tired to open and watched Ilsa stitch a gash on Pico's forearm by headlamp.

"Hold still."

"I am still."

"You're still the way a river is still. Ansel, hold him."

Ansel held the boy's arm, and Pico glowed like a boy being held by a famous man, which was exactly what he was, and which was exactly what he didn't know. That was the strange arithmetic of this crew, Teo thought: the boy worshipped the man for reasons that were buried in the man like rebar in concrete, load-bearing, invisible, and someday—someday soon, if the letters that had been arriving with no return address meant what Teo thought they meant—the whole structure of it was going to crack open, and Pico was going to find out what his idol had been holding up all these years, and what it had cost.

Because Teo had seen the letters. He was the one who collected the mail; it was part of his cover, the respectable engineer who signed for things. Two envelopes in two weeks, no return address, expensive paper, and Ansel's face after the second one had looked like the underside of pier three—sound for years, and then one day a hairline crack, and then a finger of darkness walking.

"You're staring at the boss," Ilsa said, tying off.

"I'm staring at the bridge."

"You're staring at the boss."

Teo opened the beer at last. "I'm an engineer. I stare at things that are about to fail."

Downstream, the river kept coming down from its forbidden source, brown with the country's secrets, patient as debt. And up the dark valley road, far too late at night for visitors, an engine sound approached the town—big, expensive, wrong for Puerto Ceniza—and Pico stood up first, the boy's ears always youngest, and said:

"Somebody's driving up from the coast."

Teo looked at Ansel. Ansel was already looking at the road, and his face, in the headlamp light, had gone very still—the stillness of a man hearing, after many years, a sound he had buried and recognized instantly anyway.

"I know that engine," Ansel said.
