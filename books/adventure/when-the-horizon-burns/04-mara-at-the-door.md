## 4. Mara at the Door

The knock came at dusk, three strikes spaced like a man counting his own pulse.

Ansel knew every knock in Puerto Hambre. The landlady knocked twice and waited—she'd learned that years ago. The customs officer knocked four times because he liked the sound of his own authority. The boys who ran errands for the fishing cooperative didn't knock at all; they shouted his name from the street like a curse.

Three measured strikes belonged to no one he knew, which meant it belonged to someone who had studied him before coming.

He set down the rope coil he was re-braiding and did not move. Through the window's salt-hazed glass he could see the street going violet with evening, the fishing boats dragging their shadows up the mudflats, a dog trotting somewhere with something dead in its mouth. An ordinary dusk in an ordinary town at the end of the map. The kind of place where a man could be erased any day of the week, and mostly was.

The knock came again. Same rhythm. Patient.

He opened the door on the second round not because he wanted to but because waiting longer would tell her—he was already sure it was a her—that she'd gotten under his skin.

Mara Voss stood on his step with a duffel bag over one shoulder and ten extra years carved into her face.

* * *

He had last seen her at the inquiry in Cochabamba, seven years ago, when she'd sat three meters away behind a table of officials and read her testimony in a voice flat as lake ice: *I observed Mr. Sandoval's route plan. I observed his decision to proceed despite weather warnings. I observed.* She had worn a gray blazer then. She had looked at him exactly once, and whatever lived in that look had made him stop sleeping properly for a year.

Now she wore a battered red shell jacket gone pink at the shoulders, cargo pants stiff with old mud, and boots resoled twice past the point of dignity. Her hair was cut short and streaked with gray she hadn't bothered to dye. A scar split her left eyebrow, new since Cochabamba. She looked, he thought, like someone who had stopped performing toughness and simply become it.

"Hello, Ansel." Her voice was lower than he remembered. Everything about her was lower, denser, like rock that had been compressed.

"You're supposed to be in Patagonia," he said.

"I was. Then I wasn't."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only kind you get for free." She glanced past him into the room—the cot, the rope, the single burner, the wall where he kept his maps weighted down with tins. "Nice place. Very temporary."

"It's meant to be."

"Can I come in, or do we do this on the step where your landlady can hear?"

"There is no *this*."

Her mouth did something that was almost a smile and cost her something to make. "There's always a *this* with you, Sandoval. You just haven't been told what it is yet."

He should have shut the door. He catalogued all the reasons to shut the door the way a man counts exits in a burning building, and then he stepped aside and let her in, because some fires you walk toward.

* * *

She took the one chair. He stayed standing, arms crossed, which put them in the geometry they'd always used—her seated and composed, him upright and braced, as if he were the one being examined. Maybe he was. Maybe he always had been.

She set the duffel between her boots and didn't open it. "You got my letter."

"What letter?" He heard himself say it and hated how fast it came out.

"The letter you burned. Don't bother denying it—I know your habits. You burned my first offer twelve years ago too, after the Illimani job. Then you spent two weeks walking around your camp like a man with a stone in his boot until you called me." She leaned back. "You're two days into the two weeks."

"I'm not taking the job."

"I haven't named the job."

"You will. And I'm not taking it. Whatever it is. Triple pay, quadruple, I don't care. My answer was in the ashtray before you even wrote the letter."

Something flickered across her face—not hurt, he noted, but recalculation. She had expected more fight and gotten a wall instead, and walls interested her more than fights. Fights meant you could still be moved. Walls meant fear.

"Your crew," she said. "Teo Reyes—no relation to anyone important, forged papers, best high-altitude engineer I've ever seen wasted on bridge repairs. Ilsa Brandt, who walked out of a Hamburg clinic ahead of a malpractice inquiry that half the surgeons on that staff deserved more than she did. And a kid everyone calls Pico, who follows you around like you hung the moon and doesn't know why you're hanging in it." She ticked them off on fingers calloused as his own. "You've built yourself a family of people nobody else will insure. That's not a crew, Ansel. That's a wound wearing a team jacket."

"Careful."

"I'm being very careful. It's costing me." She reached into the duffel—not for the thing he feared, not yet—and pulled out a flask, unscrewed it, drank, offered it. He shook his head. She screwed the cap back on. "Ten years ago you'd have taken the drink and tried to out-drink me. Ten years ago we were equals."

"We were never equals. You testified against me."

"Yes." No flinch. No apology riding in behind it. Just the word, set down on the table between them like a heavy tool. "I did. And if you want to spend this visit on that, we'll spend it on that, and then I'll leave, and in six months you'll read a news story about the Ceniza valley and you'll understand what you refused tonight, and it will eat you alive. Your choice. But I'd rather show you first."

The name went through him like cold water through a crack in a dam.

*Ceniza.*

He kept his face still. He'd had practice; his face had been still for ten years while reporters asked him about the flood, while lawyers asked him about the flood, while drunk men in bars leaned close and asked him about the flood with their breath full of pity or contempt depending on the bar. Stillness was the one skill the disaster had given him.

"There's nothing up the Ceniza," he said. "It's closed. Sealed. Nobody goes there."

"Right," Mara said softly. "Nobody goes there. So tell me why somebody's paying triple rates to have a guide take a survey team to the source of a river that officially doesn't exist above the resettlement line."

His heart was doing something he refused to acknowledge. "Who's paying?"

"Client wants anonymity. Client gets anonymity. You know how it works."

"I don't take anonymous money anymore."

"You don't take *any* money anymore unless the job's rotten enough that no legitimate outfit will touch it. That's your brand, Ansel. The guide of last resort. You've made a career out of other people's forbidden places." She stood, slowly, joints audible. Older and harder, yes—but also tired, he realized. Tired in a way that went deeper than muscle. "So here it is, named, since you insist. The Río Ceniza. Upstream. All the way to the source. The glacier valley. The one they sealed after the dam."

"No."

He said it flatly, immediately, without the pause he knew she'd be listening for. *No* was the easiest word he owned. He'd been saying it to the valley in his head for a decade.

Mara nodded as if he'd confirmed a hypothesis. "That was fast."

"The answer doesn't need to be slow."

"Everything true is slow, Ansel. Fast answers are just fear wearing boots." She hoisted the duffel onto her shoulder and crossed to the door, and he thought—with relief that felt disgracefully like loss—that she was actually leaving. Then she stopped, turned, and looked at him with those eyes that had once ended his career.

"Eleven people died up there. You know their names better than I do. One of them shares yours." Her voice didn't rise at all, which somehow made it worse. "And every year, the company that killed them gets a little better at making sure no one ever proves it. They dissolved on paper. Their fences didn't. Their patrols didn't. Their guns didn't. Somebody is up in that valley right now doing something they'll pay men like you triple rates to help hide—or help find. And you're going to sit in this beautiful temporary room and braid rope until the light goes out of the sky, and then you're going to do it again tomorrow, forever, because moving is easier than knowing."

"You don't know anything about me."

"I know you better than you wish I did." She opened the door. Evening air came in, cold off the water, smelling of diesel and fish. "I'm at the Hostal del Faro until Thursday. Room four. If the answer changes, you know where I am. If it doesn't—" she paused on the threshold, backlit by the last of the light, "—then burn this too. Save yourself the two weeks."

She set something on his table on her way out. Not gently. Not carelessly. With the deliberate placement of a woman laying a card face-up.

Then she was gone, boots ringing down the wooden steps, and the street swallowed her, and Ansel Sandoval stood alone in his room with the photograph.

* * *

He didn't look at it for an hour.

He braided rope. He cleaned the burner. He ate rice and sardines standing up, the way he ate everything, the way a man eats when part of him expects to be running before the plate is empty. Outside, the town folded itself into night—generators coughing, a radio playing something mournful, the dog from earlier barking at nothing with real conviction.

The photograph lay face-down on the table like a dead bird.

He knew what it was before he turned it over. Of course he did. There was only one photograph in the world that could have made Mara Voss cross a border and knock on his door with three patient strikes.

When he finally flipped it, the room got colder.

Aerial shot. Drone, probably—high resolution, recent, the colors still honest. The Río Ceniza gorge running bottom-left to top-right like a scar someone had tried to stitch and given up on. The resettlement village as a scatter of tin roofs. Then the gorge narrowing, whitening, the river going from brown to gray to the pale arterial blue of meltwater. And there, at the top of the frame—

The valley.

He had never seen it from above. From below, from the river, from the access road, in the one hour of his life he could not remember—it had been walls of rock and cloud. From above it was worse. It was beautiful. A bowl of stone cradling the glacier's toe, the ice stained gray with a decade of moraine dust, and at its lip the broken crown of the dam, visible even from this height as a pale jagged line, like a jaw with teeth missing.

Someone had drawn on the photograph. Red ink, fine-tipped, precise: a circle around the dam ruins, an arrow to the glacier tongue, coordinates written small along the margin in handwriting he almost recognized and refused to try to recognize.

And in the lower corner, stamped faintly, half-cropped by the frame's edge—a logo. A company mark. The consortium's mark, the one from the inquiry documents, the one that had been *dissolved*, per every registry on two continents, eight years ago.

Fresh imagery. Recent flight. A dead company's stamp on living paper.

Ansel sat down on the cot because his legs informed him sitting was happening whether he agreed or not. His hands were steady—that was the horror of it, the practiced steadiness, the muscle memory of a man who had learned to hold himself together in public while the inside fell. Cold fury, he knew this feeling. Cold fury was the bodyguard fear hired so it wouldn't have to show its face.

Because underneath the fury, in the dark where he kept it, the fear spoke plainly:

*They're still working up there. Ten years, and they're still working. Which means there's still something to work on. Which means there's still something to hide. Which means Tomás—*

He stopped the thought. He was good at stopping thoughts. He'd had a decade of practice stopping that one specifically, catching it mid-stride like a man grabbing a dog before it ran into traffic.

But the photograph sat on the table and would not be stopped. The red ink would not be unstared-at. Somewhere above the treeline, in a valley sealed by guns rather than law, men were doing something worth killing eleven people for—and worth keeping hidden for ten years after.

And Mara Voss, who had buried him with her testimony, had carried this image across borders to his door.

Why? Why bring it to the man she'd helped destroy? What did she know? What did she suspect? And the question that scraped worst of all, the one he'd been avoiding since she said *Ceniza* out loud:

How did a woman with no clearance, no company, and no reason get fresh aerial imagery of the most guarded drainage on the continent?

He looked at the photograph until the generator outside stuttered and the bulb dimmed and the valley in the picture seemed to shift, the shadows deepening, the glacier advancing a millimeter in the bad light, the way things in photographs do when you look too long.

Then he picked it up, meaning to burn it, meaning to prove to himself that his answer was still his answer.

He held it over the burner. The flame came up orange and hungry.

He did not let go.

After a long time he set it back on the table, face-up this time, and lay down on the cot with his boots on, staring at the ceiling, listening to the sea work the shore outside like a man worrying a knot.

Two days, Mara had said. She'd given him two weeks, but her eyes had said two days.

In the morning, he knew, Teo would find the photograph. Teo noticed everything—survey equipment, impossible stamps, red ink. Teo would hold it up to the light and start asking questions in that quiet engineer's voice, and the questions would spread to Ilsa, and then to Pico, who would look at Ansel with those open, unknowing eyes and ask the one question Ansel couldn't survive:

*Why won't we go?*

Ansel closed his own eyes. Behind them, uninvited, the valley glowed—green water, gray ice, a flashlight beam swinging through rain, a voice saying words he couldn't yet hear.

Thursday, she'd said. Room four.

He had three days to decide whether the man he'd built out of motion could afford to stand still long enough to go back.
