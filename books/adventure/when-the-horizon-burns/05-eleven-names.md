## 5. Eleven Names

Ten years is long enough to forget a phone number, the smell of your childhood kitchen, the name of the girl who taught you to dance at a wedding in Huaraz. It is not long enough to forget water.

Ansel Sandoval had learned this the hard way, in a hundred rented rooms across two continents, waking with his hands clawed around bed sheets as if they were rope, as if someone were still pulling him out. The dream never changed. The sound came first—a deep, arterial groan from the mountain's throat—and then the light: diesel lanterns swinging on the dam crest, orange and frantic against the black. And then Tomás's voice, though he could never hold onto it past waking. It dissolved like silt in current.

He was dreaming it now, or remembering it now; after ten years the border between the two had eroded down to nothing, and he lay in the dark of a room above a cantina in Puerto Ceniza and let the night take him back, because some nights he no longer had the strength to fight it.

* * *

It began, as most disasters do, with weather that should have been a warning.

The rains had come early that October—eleven years ago now, but who was counting; everyone was counting, always counting—and by the fourth day the Río Ceniza had gone the color of milk chocolate, swollen and muscular, hauling whole trees downstream like matchsticks. Ansel remembered standing on the loading dock below the camp and watching a dead cow spin past in the flood, legs rigid, eyes open, and thinking: *the river is taking inventory too.*

He'd been twenty-six. Tomás was thirty-one, five years older and forever acting like fifty, the responsible one, the one who'd stayed in the valley when Ansel went off to guide tourists up mountains that didn't matter. Tomás worked the dam—not for the consortium itself but for one of its hundred subcontractors, a maintenance crew that checked gauges and greased valves and lived in a row of tin-roofed houses the company called Camp Tres, which everyone called the Row because it was exactly that: one row of identical doors facing the water.

Their mother was buried on the hill above the Row. That was why Tomás stayed, he said. Someone had to keep her company.

The night of the failure, Ansel had come down from the high camps with a client party—two Germans and a Belgian, all of them exhausted, all of them alive, which was the job description entire—and he'd planned to sleep four hours in the Row, in Tomás's spare bunk, and catch the morning truck back to the city. He remembered that much with terrible clarity. He remembered the rain hammering the tin roof like applause that would not stop. He remembered Tomás cooking eggs at midnight because Ansel was always hungry off the mountain, and the two of them arguing about football, low-voiced, because the walls were thin and the neighbors' kids were sleeping.

He remembered Tomás saying, almost casually, turning the eggs with a spoon: "They've been running the spillgates wrong all week. I told Herrera. Nobody listens to the man who greases the valves."

And Ansel, mouth full, twenty-six years old and immortal, had said something stupid. Something like, *Then stop listening and start shouting,* or maybe just a grunt. He had spent ten years trying to reconstruct what he'd said in response to the last real sentence his brother ever spoke to him, and the cruelty was that he couldn't. The words had washed away with everything else. All he knew was that Tomás had looked at him then—there was a look, there had to have been a look—and whatever passed between them in that steamy little kitchen was the last uncorrupted thing Ansel owned, and even that was mostly lost.

After the eggs, memory became a country with no roads.

* * *

Here is what the official record said afterward, in the report that made Ansel Sandoval's name a curse in two countries:

At 2:47 a.m., during a declared hundred-year storm event, the Ceniza dam experienced an overtopping failure of its auxiliary embankment. Eleven persons perished, including six residents of Camp Tres, three workers on night shift at the intake gallery, and one Tomás Sandoval, maintenance technician. One Ansel Sandoval, visiting family, was recovered alive from the river approximately nine kilometers downstream, suffering hypothermia and head trauma. Mr. Sandoval's account of events prior to the failure could not be corroborated. Witnesses reported that Mr. Sandoval had been heard earlier that evening arguing with site management regarding drainage operations. A handwritten note in Mr. Sandoval's possession referenced unauthorized manipulation of spillgate controls.

A note. In his handwriting. In his jacket pocket, dry inside its plastic lining, which meant someone had put it there after the water—or before, expecting the water. He had no memory of writing it. He had no memory of the final hour at all. The last thing he carried from that night was sensory shrapnel: the taste of diesel in the floodwater, the impossible weight of wet wool, a flashlight beam swinging through rain like a searchlight looking for something worth saving, and a voice—calm, unhurried, wrong—saying words he could never quite surface. Two words, maybe three. They rose to the lip of his mind sometimes, in the hypoxic half-sleep of altitude, and sank again before he could read them.

Everything else—the inquiry, the testimony, the blacklist—was built on top of that absence like a road built over a sinkhole.

* * *

In the dream, he was in the water.

Not being pulled out. In it. The Ceniza at flood was not water so much as moving earth, a brown continent sliding downhill, and it took him the way an avalanche takes a climber—completely, politically, without negotiation. He remembered tumbling. He remembered his shoulder striking something that might have been a truck or a house and feeling it dislocate with a sound he felt more than heard. He remembered deciding, with strange bureaucratic calm, somewhere beneath the roar: *I am going to die, and this is the paperwork.*

And then—this was the part that had ruined him, the part no investigator ever asked about correctly—then there was light. Not lightning. Lantern light, close, deliberate, a beam held steady on his face while hands he never saw gripped his collar and hauled him against the current instead of with it, upstream somehow, or sideways, into an eddy behind a rock shelf where the world went suddenly quiet as a chapel. And the voice. Calm. Unhurried. Wrong.

*Sign it.*

He woke, as he always woke, with the two words dissolving on his tongue and his right hand aching, and he lay in the dark above the cantina and did the thing he had done ten thousand times: he counted the names.

Because that was the arithmetic nobody else did. The report had eleven names in a table, in a font, in an appendix. Ansel had them in his chest, eleven stones laid one atop another, sediment compressing into stone, and every year the strata got heavier.

Rosa Quispe, who ran the comedor at Camp Tres and had once fed both Sandoval boys for a week when their mother was sick, because that was what Rosa did. Her granddaughters had been visiting.

Ernesto Herrera, the shift supervisor Tomás had tried to warn. Dead at his post in the intake gallery, which meant he had gone down to check the gates himself rather than send anyone else. Ansel had hated him for years—for ignoring Tomás, for the shrug he'd given at the loading dock that afternoon—and then had spent more years learning that hatred needed a living target, and Herrera had died better than most men live.

The Vargas brothers, Julio and Marco, who worked the turbines and sang off-key on Fridays.

Doña Carmen Alvarez, Rosa's sister, visiting.

Six-year-old Flora Vargas. Julio's daughter. She had been asleep in the Row. Everyone in the Row had been asleep, because the company had decided, in its wisdom, that the storm did not meet the threshold for evacuation sirens. There was a memo. There was always a memo.

And Tomás. Tomás Sandoval, maintenance technician, thirty-one years old, who had known the gates were run wrong, who had shouted upward until shouting failed, and who had died—how? Ansel had never been able to make himself find out how. Drowned? Crushed? Taken by the same brown continent that had taken Ansel and then, inexplicably, spat him out nine kilometers downstream with a note in his pocket and a hole where an hour used to be?

Eleven names. He said them in order, oldest grievance first, the way you'd count beads. Some nights he got through all eleven before sleep took him. Tonight he got to Flora Vargas and stopped, because tonight—God help him—he kept seeing the photograph Mara Voss had left on his table, and the valley in it, and the survey equipment that shouldn't exist near a sealed grave, and a thought was forming in him that felt less like grief than like a blade being slowly forged.

*Someone has been working there recently.*

Working. At the source. Above the ruin. Where the water came from, and therefore—his mind turned it over with a guide's instinct for terrain—where the truth of the water also came from.

* * *

Morning came gray and dripping to Puerto Ceniza, and Ansel stood at the window with coffee he wasn't drinking and watched the river go by.

It was smaller now. Ten years of sediment behind the broken dam had settled into the delta, and the Ceniza here ran shallow and apologetic, braiding around gravel bars, carrying less than it used to. But he knew what it had been. He had been inside it. His body remembered the weight of it the way a bell remembers being struck.

Down at the dock, fishermen were laying out nets that would come up half empty, laced with the fine gray tailings dust that had never stopped leaching from the drowned workings. The company had called the fish die-off "natural fluctuation." The company had called everything natural. Storms, failures, deaths, disappearances—all of it weather, all of it geology, none of it anyone's decision. That was the genius of the cover-up, if you could call it a cover-up; it had never needed to hide anything. It had only needed to outlast attention. Grief, Ansel had learned, was the most perishable substance on earth. Ten years and the valley had already forgotten how to be angry. Only he hadn't. Only the families hadn't.

He thought of the widow in the downstream village whose husband had been on the intake shift. He thought of the small cemetery on the hill above where Camp Tres had been, eleven graves the company had paid for with stone so cheap the rain was already eating the letters off the markers. Flora Vargas's marker had lost its name within three years. A six-year-old girl, erased by weather, officially natural.

His hand ached. He looked down at it—at the right hand, the thumb, the ordinary thumb that had signed, according to the record, an acknowledgment of unauthorized gate manipulation, a confession he had no memory of making, on a night he had no memory of living. The investigators had shown him the document. His print sat there in blue ink, undeniable, a fact. Facts were heavy things. You could drown under them. He had, more or less, for ten years.

But facts had weight in another direction too, and lately—since the letter, since the photograph, since Teo's finger pointing at equipment that had no business existing near a sealed site—that weight had begun to pull differently. Not downward, into guilt. Upstream. Toward the source.

Ansel set down the untouched coffee. On the table, the photograph lay where he'd left it, face down, because he couldn't bear it face up and couldn't bear to throw it away either. He turned it over.

The valley looked back at him. Even in a photograph, even flat and silent, it had the presence of a place that had witnessed something. The glacier at the headwall, white and blind. The lake beneath it, the color of old bottle glass. And along the eastern shore, small as insects, the shapes Teo had found: tripods, shelters, machinery. Fresh tracks in the moraine. Work, at the grave of eleven people, conducted in secret by a company that officially did not exist.

Ten years he had believed—if believing was the word for carrying a thing you never chose to examine—that the past was settled. Sediment. Stone. You did not dig up stone; you built on it or you climbed away from it. That had been his whole doctrine, the rule beneath the rule of never stopping moving: *keep moving because the past can't follow you uphill.*

But the past, it turned out, had hired crews of its own. The past was working right now, upriver, in the forbidden valley, doing something to the evidence that needed doing in secret—and whatever it was, it was happening fast enough that someone had paid Mara Voss triple rates to go look.

Ansel picked up the photograph and held it a long moment, and the grief moved in him—not the drowning kind, not tonight. Tonight it was denser, quieter, compressed by ten years of pressure into something that could cut.

Eleven names. He said them once more, all of them, ending—as he always ended—with the one that mattered: *Tomás Sandoval.* And for the first time in ten years, the name didn't feel like a stone he was carrying.

It felt like instructions.

He reached for the phone to call his crew.
