## 6. What the Photograph Shows

Teo Quispe had a rule about photographs: the truth never lived in the center of the frame. It lived at the edges, in the things the photographer hadn't meant to capture. Ten years of forging documents had taught him that. A passport was just a photograph with a name attached, and every forgery he'd ever built or broken had been undone by some detail in the margins—a stamp slightly too fresh, a shadow that fell the wrong way, a reflection in a window that showed a room that shouldn't exist.

So when Mara Voss's photograph finally came off Ansel's table—when Ansel finally stopped looking at it like it was a grenade with the pin half-out and let it be passed around the crew's table at the back of Doña Rosa's cantina—Teo didn't look at the valley.

He looked at the edges.

The photograph itself was unremarkable at first glance, which was the first thing wrong with it. A high alpine valley, shot from a ridge, probably with a long lens from kilometers away. Gray moraine. A glacier tongue at the head of the valley, dirty with rockfall. The skeletal remains of the Ceniza dam visible as a pale scar across the river's throat, concrete the color of bone. Everything in it was dead, drained, abandoned. The kind of landscape that made you want to look away and check your watch.

Ansel had been looking at it for three days.

He wasn't looking at it now. He was drinking coffee at the end of the table with his back to the window, the way he always sat—never with light behind him, never with his face toward a door he wasn't watching. Teo had catalogued that habit years ago and never asked about it, because there were things you didn't ask Ansel Sandoval, and the disaster that had built his habits was the largest of them.

"Pass it here," Pico said, reaching across Ilsa's beer.

Ilsa held the photograph a moment longer, her thumb resting near the dam scar, then let it go. She'd been quiet all evening in the way that meant she was assembling something, and Teo had learned to let her assemble. The boy—Pico was twenty-two and would be a boy to Teo until he was forty—took the photo and turned it right side up with the exaggerated care of someone handling an artifact.

"That's it?" Pico said. "That's the whole forbidden valley? Looks like nothing."

"It's not nothing," Ansel said, without turning. "It's the reason I can't go back to two countries."

The table went quiet the way it did when Ansel mentioned the blacklist, which was rarely, and never with detail. Teo took the photograph from Pico's hand before the kid could smudge it further and tilted it toward the lamp.

And there it was. At the edge.

* * *

He didn't say anything at first. That was Teo's second rule: never announce what you've found until you're sure you've found it. He'd watched men die in border offices because they got excited too early, because their faces moved before their brains finished the arithmetic. So he sat with the photograph under the lamp and let his eyes do quiet work while the conversation flowed around him—Pico asking Ilsa if altitude sickness could kill a man his age, Ilsa answering with the flat precision she used when she was keeping herself from saying something else entirely.

The valley floor. Lower left quadrant, where the river emerged from the dam's ruins and spread into a braided wash of gray channels. Teo angled the photo. The light in the image was late afternoon, long shadows raking east, and in one of those shadows—half-swallowed by the shadow of the moraine itself—there was a rectangle.

A rectangle that was too straight.

Nature didn't do straight. Teo had spent enough years in the mountains to know that nature's geometry was all fracture and slump, everything rounded or shattered, everything leaning. Straight lines were human. And this rectangle was not only straight, it was *white*, and it sat on the valley floor like a tooth in a mouth of gravel.

He got Ilsa's hand lens from her kit—she kept it in the outer pocket of her medical bag, and she watched him take it with one raised eyebrow but didn't object—and held it over the rectangle.

A building. A prefabricated structure, the kind of modular shelter that mining companies and survey crews flew in by helicopter, aluminum-skinned, maybe eight meters long. And beside it, catching the low sun so that it threw a needle of glare across the moraine, a vertical line with a crosspiece.

Teo set the lens down. Picked it up again. Made himself look a third time, because the third look was the one that cost something.

A survey mast. A total station tripod beside it. And below the mast, arranged with the tidy spacing of people who expected to stay, a row of shapes he recognized from a decade of working around extractive projects: fuel drums. A generator housing. A snowmobile, or the shell of one.

"Ansel," he said.

Ansel didn't answer immediately. He was watching the door, or the window, or the street—Teo never knew which, only that Ansel was always watching something. Then he turned, and Teo held the photograph out to him and pointed with the lens.

"Tell me what this is."

Ansel took it. For a long moment he looked at the center of the frame—the dam, the glacier, the dead heart of the thing—and Teo watched him do it, watched him go to the wound first the way people always touch the sore tooth with their tongue. Then Teo tapped the lower left quadrant, and Ansel's eyes moved, and Teo saw the exact moment the photograph changed for him. It was like watching water find a new channel. His whole face reorganized around a different understanding.

"Where did you get this photo?" Ansel asked. His voice was level. Too level. Teo had heard that voice on the bridge job in April, when the cable anchor had sheared and the whole span had dropped forty centimeters with them on it. It was the voice of a man measuring the distance to the edge.

"Mara left it," Ilsa said. "You said she left it."

"She did." Ansel set the photo down flat, as if it might burn through the table. "She said it was recent. She didn't say how she got it."

"Long lens," Teo said. "From the eastern ridge, probably. The compression is wrong for a close shot. Whoever took it was standing somewhere they didn't want to be seen from." He turned the photo back toward the lamp. "Which raises the question of why they needed to hide to photograph a valley that's been sealed for ten years. Unless—"

"Unless it's not sealed," Pico said.

Everyone looked at him. Pico had the particular gift, common in young men and dangerous in expedition crews, of saying the obvious thing at exactly the wrong volume. But he was right, and the table knew he was right, and the knowing sat there among the beer bottles like a fifth person.

"Look at the mast," Teo said, and his voice came out lower than he intended, because now that he'd started he couldn't stop seeing it, the whole margin of the photograph unfolding like a confession. "That's a geodetic survey station. Total station, fixed mast, probably a GPS reference receiver on top. That's not exploration equipment. That's *monitoring* equipment. You put that up when you're tracking movement—glacier flow, subsidence, structural deformation." He paused. "Or demolition progress."

"Demolition," Ilsa repeated.

"The dam failed ten years ago," Teo said. "The ruins have been sitting there ever since. Nobody monitors ruins, Ilsa. You monitor things you're about to change."

* * *

Ansel stood up. It was a small movement—chair back, weight shift, coffee cup set down with a click that was too careful—but the whole cantina's ambient noise seemed to bend around it. Doña Rosa glanced over from the bar and then, wisely, found something to do with her hands.

"Teo." Ansel came around the table and stood over the photograph, arms folded, and Teo saw something in his face he'd only seen once before, in the whiteout on the Salkantay traverse, when the mountain had taken away every option except descent and Ansel had stood at the decision point for eleven full seconds, hating every one of them. "Say the rest of it."

"The rest of it is arithmetic." Teo spread his hands over the photo's edges. "The consortium that ran the Ceniza mine was dissolved on paper. I checked the registries when Mara's letter came—corporate death, assets liquidated, concessions reverted. There is no legal entity with any right to be in that valley. And yet." He tapped the prefab building. "Somebody flew in a structure that costs more than this whole crew makes in a year. Somebody is running power up there. Somebody is paying for survey-grade equipment and the technicians to run it, in a valley that the government closed, in a watershed that's supposedly patrolled to keep people *out*."

"Patrolled," Ilsa said. "You know this how?"

"I know how these things are guarded." Teo kept his voice even. "Private security, off the books, paid through layers of contractors so no name ever touches another name. I've built the paperwork for operations like that. I've built it, Ansel. I know the architecture because I used to sell the blueprints." He'd never said that part aloud to the crew—not so plainly—and saying it now felt like setting down a weight he'd carried so long his shoulders had grown around it. "Nobody hires guns to guard nothing."

The lamp buzzed. Somewhere in the kitchen a pan clattered. Pico had gone very still, the way he did when the adults' conversation crossed into the country where he hadn't been yet.

"So the company's still there," Pico said. "Under a different name. And they're doing something to the dam ruins."

"Or to what's under them," Ilsa said quietly.

Ansel looked at her. "What's under them is a dead river and eleven graves."

"Is it?" Ilsa asked. It wasn't cruelty in her voice. It was worse—it was clinical, the tone she used with a wound she suspected went deeper than the bleeding showed. "Eleven people died when that dam failed. The company was dissolved. The valley was sealed. And now, ten years later, someone is quietly working the site with monitoring equipment. Ansel, when does a company ever come back to the scene of an accident it paid to bury?"

"Never," Teo said, before Ansel could. "Unless there's something there it still needs to hide. Or destroy."

* * *

Ansel walked to the window. Teo watched his back—the set of the shoulders, the way his hand rose halfway to the glass and stopped. Outside, the border town did its evening business in the street: a truck grinding past with a bad alternator, dogs, the neon stutter of the pharmacy sign. A town where Ansel could be expelled from any day, where all of them were guests of nothing, tolerated by the accident of official inattention.

"You're all looking at this like a job," Ansel said, without turning around.

"It is a job," Pico said. "Mara's paying triple."

"Mara's paying triple because nobody else would take it, and nobody else would take it because it's a coffin with a paycheck stapled to it." Ansel turned. In the lamplight his face was all planes and shadow, ten years older than it had been at dinner. "You want to know what's in that valley? I'll tell you what I know. I know I woke up in a hospital in a country I'm banned from, with water in my lungs and a hole in my memory where my brother used to be. I know eleven families got settlements with confidentiality clauses, and I know mine got one too, and I know I signed something in that hospital that I don't remember signing. That's what the Ceniza is. That's what it does. It takes the last hour of you and keeps it."

"Then we get the hour back," Pico said.

Ansel looked at him, and something moved through his expression—too fast to name, gone before it landed. "You don't know what you're asking for."

"I know what I'm looking at." Pico tapped the photograph, right on the white rectangle, and his hand didn't shake. Teo felt an unexpected, complicated pride, the kind that arrives with a cost attached. "Somebody's up there doing something they'd kill to keep quiet. You told us when you hired us—when any of us—that we take the jobs nobody else will touch. This is that job. It's the most that-job a job has ever been."

"The rule was about dangerous work," Ansel said. "Not about—"

"Not about what? Going home?" Pico's voice cracked and he pushed through it. "None of us have a home. That's the whole crew. That's the *point* of the crew."

The silence that followed had weight. Ilsa broke it, gently, the way she did everything.

"Vote," she said. "That's how we decide. That's how we've always decided."

Ansel's jaw worked. "I'm the lead."

"You're the lead," Teo said, "and the lead gets a vote. One vote. You wrote that rule yourself, after the Huaylas bridge, when you tried to carry the whole decision alone and nearly drowned us all for it." He held Ansel's eyes. "You wrote it because you knew you couldn't trust yourself, on this one thing. On the thing where it's personal. You told me that. Drunk, in Puno, but you told me."

Something flickered behind Ansel's eyes—anger, and under the anger, gratitude, and under the gratitude, fear, the fear that Teo had finally learned to read after five years: not fear of the valley. Fear of what the valley knew about him.

"Fine," Ansel said. "Vote."

"All in favor of taking Mara Voss's contract—follow the Ceniza to its source, find out what the company is doing up there." Ilsa raised her hand first, clean and immediate, no hesitation, and Teo understood that she'd decided days ago, maybe the moment she'd seen the widow's name in Mara's file, if there had been a file, if there was anything about Mara Voss that wasn't a file. He raised his own hand next, and the raising of it felt like signing something, like the old days, except this time he was on the other side of the document.

Pico's hand went up so fast it nearly left his shoulder.

Three hands. Four people at the table. Teo looked at Ansel and waited, and the waiting was the hardest thing he'd done in months, harder than the bridge, harder than any border, because he was waiting to find out whether the man who'd built this crew out of the unbuildable would let them become what they were, or keep them as what he needed them to be: people who moved, and never stopped, and never looked back.

Ansel looked at the photograph a long time.

Then he looked at the white rectangle in the shadow, at the straight lines that had no business in a dead valley, and Teo watched him do the arithmetic he'd been refusing to do for three days—the arithmetic that said the company had never left, that the cover-up was not history but a *project*, ongoing, staffed, funded, and that every year he'd spent running had been a year they'd spent digging.

"No," Ansel said.

The word dropped into the room like a stone into a well.

"No, I don't vote for it." He straightened. "I vote against it. I think it's a mistake and I think it'll cost us. I think Mara Voss is using us and I think whatever she wants from that valley, she wants it more than she wants any of us alive." He picked up the photograph, folded it once along its crease, and put it in his jacket pocket, against his chest, where Teo could see he'd been keeping it all along. "But it's three to one, and I wrote the rule."

He looked around the table, at each of them, and for one unguarded second Teo saw what lived under all the watching and the moving and the never-stopping: a tired man carrying eleven names, one of them his own brother's, up a mountain he'd sworn never to climb again.

"So we go," Ansel said. "Downstream first. The river's mouth, the families, the ground truth. Nobody goes near that valley until we know what we're walking into." He drained his coffee and set the cup down. "We leave in two days. Pico, the boats. Teo, I want every scrap of paper that exists on the consortium and every shell company that ever touched it. Ilsa—"

"I know," Ilsa said. "Medical, for a fishing survey. I'll build the cover."

Ansel nodded once, and the meeting was over, because with Ansel a meeting was over when he turned toward the door.

Teo stayed at the table a moment longer, looking at the lamp, at the ring of beer moisture where the photograph had been. He thought about straight lines in dead places. He thought about the blueprints he used to sell, and the men who bought them, and how they had never, in all his years inside that world, spent money on a valley they intended to leave alone.

* * *

He caught up with Ansel in the street, under the broken pharmacy neon, and walked beside him without speaking for half a block, because that was their way—Teo talked when there was something to say, and Ansel tolerated silence better than any man Teo had known.

"You voted no," Teo said finally, "but you kept the photo."

Ansel didn't break stride. "Evidence."

"You've had it in your pocket for three days. That's not evidence, that's a compass."

Ansel stopped. The neon buzzed overhead, painting his face pink, then dark, then pink. When he spoke, his voice was quiet enough that Teo had to lean in to catch it.

"When I woke up in that hospital," Ansel said, "there was a man sitting in the corner of the room. Suit, briefcase, the whole costume. He watched me for a while and then he said, 'You were very lucky, Señor Sandoval. The river gave you back.'" Ansel's eyes came up to Teo's, and in them was the thing he never showed the crew, the thing under everything. "Ten years I've thought that was a memory of kindness. Tonight I'm looking at that photograph and I'm thinking the river doesn't give anything back. Rivers only ever do one thing."

"Carry things downstream," Teo said.

"Somebody put me in it," Ansel said. "And whoever's up in that valley right now, under that white roof—they're the reason I need to know why."

He walked on toward the rooms they rented above the chichería, and Teo stood a moment in the neon's stutter, feeling the reluctant weight settle onto his shoulders and finding, to his surprise, that it fit there like it had always belonged.

Two days. Downstream first.

And somewhere upstream, in a valley sealed by grief and guarded by guns, someone was watching the glacier move.
