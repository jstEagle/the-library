## 7. Downstream First

The Ceniza ended in a delta of gray water and old grief, and Ilsa had been counting the grief since dawn.

She stood on the mudflat where the river gave itself up to the sea, clipboard in hand, sun hat pulled low, playing the role they'd rehearsed for three days: a fisheries consultant from the coast, hired by an NGO that existed only as a letterhead Teo had designed at two in the morning. The clipboard was real. The survey forms were real. Even the little vials of river water she collected were real, because Ilsa could not do anything halfway, even a lie.

What was not real was the reason they were here.

*Eleven families,* Ansel had said the night before, spreading a hand-drawn map on the table of their rented room. *The flood took people from four settlements along the lower reach. We start at the mouth and work upstream. Nobody lies better than we do, because nobody has less to lose.*

He hadn't looked at anyone when he said it. He'd looked at the map, at the blue ink line of the river he'd once been carried down like driftwood, half-drowned, the only survivor the water returned.

Ilsa clicked her pen and approached the first house—a stilted shack with fishing nets drying on its railing, salt-crusted, mended so many times they looked like lace made by someone who refused to stop hoping.

An old woman sat on the porch shelling beans into a dented basin. She looked up, and Ilsa watched her perform the arithmetic that people in forgotten places performed automatically: outsider, clipboard, questions. Danger or opportunity.

"Buenos días," Ilsa said, switching easily into coastal Spanish. "We're doing a fish stock assessment on the Ceniza. The NGO is studying how the river's changed over the last decade." She let the number hang there. *A decade.* Ten years since the dam failed. Ten years since eleven people went into the water and did not come back.

The woman's hands kept moving, bean after bean, but something behind her eyes went still as a held breath.

"Fish," the woman repeated.

"Migration patterns, spawning grounds. We interview local families about what the river used to give them."

The woman set down a bean. "You want to know what the river used to give us." It wasn't a question. Her voice had a flint edge that Ilsa recognized from triage tents and waiting rooms—the voice of someone deciding whether to bleed in front of you or wait until you left.

"If you're willing. It takes twenty minutes."

"My husband fished this delta for thirty years." The woman resumed shelling, faster now. "Then your river took him, and now you want to know about *fish*."

Ilsa's pen hovered. This was the part they hadn't rehearsed—what to do when the cover story collided head-on with the truth it was meant to smuggle them past. "I'm sorry," she said, and meant it, which surprised her. She'd expected the lie to feel heavier than this. Instead it felt thin, translucent, like ice over black water.

"The company sent lawyers first," the woman said. "Then engineers with clipboards. Then journalists, until the journalists stopped coming. Now it's fish scientists." She looked up, and her eyes were dry and ancient. "Ask me whatever you came to ask. But don't insult me by pretending."

Ilsa lowered the clipboard. Down the mudflat, she could see Pico hauling the boat above the tideline, young and oblivious, and further off, a figure in a wide-brimmed hat standing at the water's edge with his back to everything: Ansel, watching the current come down out of the mountains like a man reading a letter he already knew by heart.

"I'm not with any company," Ilsa said quietly.

"No," the woman agreed. "Companies send people who can look at me without flinching."

* * *

They worked the delta for six days.

It went the way Ilsa had predicted it would and dreaded anyway: grief opened more readily to strangers than truth ever would. Families talked about the river because talking about the river was safe; talking about the night of the flood was not. But grief leaks. A man describing how the fishing had died after the disaster mentioned, in passing, that his cousin's body had been found forty kilometers downstream, wedged in a willow, and that the recovery crews had taken nine days to come. A teenage girl filling out a survey form about household diet wrote, in the margin, in careful schoolgirl script: *My grandmother says the water tasted wrong for a week before. Like metal. Like blood.* A grandfather showed Ilsa, unasked, a photograph of a boy in a soccer jersey, and then put it away before she could ask his name, because saying the name aloud was a door he'd nailed shut years ago.

Ilsa recorded all of it—not on the official forms but in her own notebook, in her own shorthand, medical shorthand, the language she thought in when things got bad. *Taste of water, metallic, pre-event, multiple independent reports. Delayed body recovery, 9 days, no state response. Child, male, name withheld by family.*

By day three she understood what Ansel had never said outright: they weren't gathering evidence yet. They were learning the shape of the hole. And the shape of the hole was wrong. Disasters made round holes—sudden, indiscriminate, complete. This hole had edges. Too many details that didn't fit a simple dam failure. The metal taste. The absence of warning sirens that everyone swore had been tested the month before. The way every family, independently, used the same word for the aftermath: *silencio.* Not silence as in quiet. Silence as in imposed. As in enforced.

On the fourth evening, back at the rented room, she laid her notebook beside Teo's laptop while Pico cooked rice and tinned fish in the corner.

"Look at this pattern," she said. "Every settlement downstream reported the water tasting wrong days before the failure. That's tailings leakage. That means the dam wasn't just structurally compromised—it was bleeding toxic waste into the river *before* it broke. Someone knew."

Teo scrolled through her notes, his forged passport lying on the table beside him like a prop he couldn't quite stop checking was still there. "Or the leak caused the failure. Erosion, pressure—"

"That's my point. Either way, it wasn't sudden. It was a process. And processes get observed." She tapped the page. "Somebody watched this happen and let it happen."

From the doorway, where he'd appeared without sound the way he always did, Ansel said nothing. He stood with his hat in his hands, looking older than his years, and Ilsa realized he'd been listening long enough to hear everything.

"You knew the families were downstream," she said. Not accusing. Just placing the fact on the table between them, where facts belonged.

"I knew everything was downstream," Ansel said. "That's how rivers work."

Pico looked up from the stove, sensing weather. Teo closed the laptop slowly.

"We start at the mouth tomorrow," Ansel said. "The Vargas family. They lost two sons." He turned the hat brim through his fingers once, twice. "Ilsa does the talking."

"Why you?" she asked, though she suspected she already knew.

"Because if they recognize me," he said, "it should be you standing closest. You're the one who can patch what gets torn."

* * *

The Vargas house sat where the delta began to climb toward the first foothills, whitewashed once, gray now, with a shrine to the Virgin in the yard whose flowers were plastic because nothing living stayed planted near the river anymore. Ilsa knocked. A woman in her sixties opened the door—small, upright, with the posture of someone who had spent ten years refusing to bend—and behind her shoulder Ilsa saw the room: two framed photographs side by side on the wall, two young men in matching smiles.

Señora Vargas heard the whole speech about fish stocks without interrupting, which Ilsa would learn later was worse than interruption. Then she stepped fully into the doorway, blocking it, and looked past Ilsa's shoulder to where Ansel waited by the gate, hat low, trying to be furniture.

The change in her face was total and instantaneous, like a storm front crossing water.

"*Tú*," she said.

Isla heard Ansel exhale behind her, one breath, controlled.

"You." Señora Vargas came down the steps and Ilsa moved to intercept—not physically, just positionally, the way you move toward a patient who's about to stand on a broken leg—but the woman flowed around her like water around a stone. She crossed the yard in six strides and stopped two meters from Ansel, and the whole street seemed to lean in. Doors opened. A dog stopped barking.

Ansel removed his hat. Of everything he might have done, Ilsa thought later, that was the most honest thing: he uncovered himself. Made himself visible. Gave her his face without defense.

"Señora Vargas," he said.

"Don't." Her voice cracked across the yard. "Don't say my name. Don't say *anything*. You have no right to words. You have no right to stand on this earth with your feet—" She stopped, shaking, and gathered herself the way people gather themselves when falling apart would be a luxury. When she spoke again her voice was low and terrible and precise. "Two sons. Two. And the whole country needed someone to blame, and they chose you, and you *let* them. You stood there at the hearing with your empty eyes and your empty story and you let them make it yours."

"I don't remember that night," Ansel said quietly. "I've never remembered it."

"Convenient," she spat. "The dead remember plenty. My sons remember. The water remembers. Only *you* forget, the one man whose forgetting was worth money to somebody." She stared at him, and Ilsa saw her searching his face for the lie, the tell, the crack—and finding, instead, something that seemed to enrage her more: sincerity. Or its convincing counterfeit. There was no way for her to know which.

"You were in that water," she said. "Tomás pulled you out. Everyone knows that. Tomás Sandoval went back into the flood for *you*, and you walked away and lived, and my boys—" Her voice finally broke, all the way through, and she let it, standing straight-backed, letting the grief show like a flag. "My boys are under that river right now. Under your river. And you come here with a *clipboard*."

Ilsa stepped forward then, gently, and said, "Señora—"

The woman turned and spat. Not at Ilsa. Past her. At Ansel's feet, a bright ugly arc in the dust, the oldest insult the Americas had, older than the conquistadors, older than language: *you are beneath me, you are dirt, I claim this ground you stand on.*

Ansel didn't move. Didn't wipe his boot. He stood in the small wet shadow of her contempt and received it, and Ilsa, watching, understood that some part of him had been standing in that exact spot for ten years.

"I'm sorry," he said.

"Sorry." She laughed, and it was the worst sound Ilsa had heard in a career full of bad sounds. "Keep your sorry. Bring me back my sons. Can your science do that? Can your *survey*?" She turned on her heel and went inside, and the door closed with a sound like a period at the end of a sentence.

The street watched. Ilsa felt their eyes on all three of them—on Ansel most of all—and understood suddenly that the whole delta knew who he was. Had known since the moment they arrived. Had been watching them work their way upstream for five days, house to house, letting them come, letting them knock, waiting to see what they wanted.

Word traveled faster than rivers here. Word had probably reached the upper valley before they'd launched their boats.

They walked back to the boat in silence. Halfway there, Pico, white-faced, said, "Boss, I didn't know—I mean, nobody told me why you—"

"Not now, Pico."

"But—"

"*Not now.*"

Ilsa got the boat ready while Ansel stood at the tideline again, hat in his hands, and she pretended to check the fuel line so she wouldn't have to decide whether to go to him. Shame, she thought, was supposed to be private. That was the whole architecture of it. But here it was public, physical, spat into the dust of a yard in front of witnesses, and she had watched him take it the way a wall takes weather.

When she finally did approach, he spoke first, without turning.

"She's right, you know. About the forgetting being worth money to somebody." His voice was flat, exhausted. "I've never let myself finish that thought. Because of where it goes."

"Where does it go?"

He looked upstream, toward the mountains, toward the source they were pretending not to be climbing toward. Somewhere up there, past the gorges and the checkpoints and the patrols they hadn't met yet, was a valley that eleven people had died to keep quiet.

"It goes to the question of whether I forgot," Ansel said, "or whether I was *made* to forget. And Ilsa—if it's the second one, then the woman who just spat at my feet is the least of what I owe."

He put his hat back on, and that was when Ilsa noticed, down the beach, the boy.

Maybe fourteen, barefoot, sitting on an overturned hull, watching them with the fixed attention of someone memorizing faces. Not a local kid—she knew the delta children by now, their games, their indifference. This one had clean clothes and city shoes caked with delta mud and a phone in his hand, held low, angled toward them.

Taking pictures.

The boy saw her see him. He stood, unhurried, and walked away up the beach—not running, not hiding, just leaving, the way a person leaves when they have exactly what they came for.

Ilsa touched Ansel's arm. "Don't turn around. There was a boy photographing us. City shoes. He's gone now, heading inland."

For a long moment Ansel was very still. Then he said, in a voice stripped of everything except arithmetic:

"He'll reach the checkpoint by dark. Which means whoever runs that valley knows we're coming." He swung his pack onto his shoulder. "Get Teo. Burn the survey forms—we're done pretending to count fish. We go upstream tonight."
