## 8. The Company That Isn't There

The records office in Puerto Ceniza smelled of mildew and diesel, and its one working fan stirred the heat without defeating it. Ilsa Vann stood at the counter for forty minutes while a clerk with reading glasses on a beaded chain hunted through ledgers that predated the flood by a decade.

"You said the consortium," the clerk said again. "Compañía Minera del Alto Ceniza."

"That's right. I'm doing background for a fisheries survey." Ilsa laid her forged credentials on the counter—Teo's work, good enough to survive a glance and a yawn. "We need to know who holds liability for the watershed. Contamination affects the fish stocks downstream. Our report has to name a responsible party."

The clerk looked at her over the glasses, then at the credentials, then at a computer so old its monitor sat like a fat gray toad on the desk. He typed with two fingers.

"Dissolved," he said. "Two thousand eleven. Liquidation order, court-supervised. Assets dispersed, charter revoked." He turned the screen toward her as if presenting a corpse. "There is no company."

"Then who owns the land?"

"The land reverted to—" He stopped. His fingers hovered over the keys. Something moved behind his face, quick as a fish under ice. "The state," he finished. "The land reverted to the state."

Ilsa had spent twelve years in emergency medicine learning to read faces—the flicker before a patient admitted how bad the pain was, the flatness of someone deciding what not to say. The clerk's face did all of it in the space of two seconds.

"And the security contractors?" she asked. "We've seen patrols on the upper river. Uniformed men. If the company doesn't exist, who pays them?"

The fan clicked, swept, clicked. Down the street a motorcycle buzzed past and faded.

"I couldn't say, doctora." He closed the ledger with exaggerated care, as if tucking in a child. "Perhaps you saw fishermen. We have many fishermen."

"We've seen armed men in matching uniforms running boat patrols at night."

"Then perhaps," the clerk said quietly, not looking at her, "your survey should stay downstream of the gorge." He slid her credentials back across the counter. "For the fish's sake."

* * *

She carried the word *downstream* back to the crew's rented house like a stone in her pocket, turning it over all afternoon. Ansel was out with Pico, buying fuel and lying about it to the fuel vendor. Teo sat on the back step with his laptop shielded from the light, pulling apart the consortium's corporate skeleton the way he'd once pulled apart engines—patiently, with dirty fingers, humming.

"Look at this," he said when she sat down beside him. He didn't look up. "You want to see something beautiful and disgusting?"

"Always."

He turned the screen. A corporate registry diagram bloomed across it, boxes and lines like a family tree drawn by someone drunk. "Compañía Minera del Alto Ceniza. Dissolved 2011, court-supervised liquidation, exactly like your clerk says. Except." He tapped a box near the top. "Its parent holding company, Vertiente Group, was dissolved six months *earlier*. And its parent, Andes Pacífico Capital, dissolved a year before that. They peeled the onion from the top down. Every entity terminated cleanly, assets transferred onward, paper trail ending in three shell companies registered in jurisdictions where 'registered' means a post office box and a lawyer's signature."

"So there's nothing left."

"There's nothing left *on paper*." Teo's hum had stopped. "But look at the dissolution filings. The liquidators were required to dispose of physical assets—the camp buildings, the vehicles, the equipment. Here's the disposal record." He scrolled. "Sold at auction."

"To whom?"

"To a company called Servicios Logísticos Apurímac. Which purchased the entire asset inventory of a dead mining operation—vehicles, generators, communications gear, a helicopter—for eleven thousand dollars." He let that sit. "Ilsa, the helicopter alone was worth two hundred thousand."

"Who is Servicios Logísticos Apurímac?"

"Registered the same month. Same address as one of the terminal shells. It has no employees, no website, no tax filings." He leaned back against the doorframe. "It exists to hold things. And every year since, it files an expense report claiming maintenance costs on equipment it supposedly owns in a valley everyone says is empty. Fuel. Rotor service. Ammunition."

Ilsa looked out at the brown water of the lower Ceniza sliding past the town, carrying silt from a place where, officially, nothing happened.

"They didn't dissolve the company," she said slowly. "They dissolved the *liability*. The company died so nobody could sue it. But the operation just kept going underneath, wearing different names."

"Now you're thinking like a fraud investigator instead of a medic."

"I'm thinking like someone whose last job ended because a hospital board decided the truth was more expensive than the lie." She said it evenly, without self-pity; that part of her life was packed away like everything else she owned, in one bag, ready to move. "Boards don't destroy evidence because they're evil. They do it because they've done the arithmetic and decided some number of people are worth less than the fine. Somebody upstream is still doing that arithmetic. Every day. With live ammunition."

Teo was quiet a moment. "Ansel knows these valleys better than anyone alive. If he says there are patrols, there are patrols."

"He hasn't said much of anything since Mara's letter."

"He will." Teo closed the laptop. "He always does. It's the silence before that scares me."

* * *

They went upriver two days later, ostensibly to sample fish populations at three stations along the middle Ceniza. In reality they were mapping: who lived along the banks, who would talk, who watched them pass. Ansel ran the boat with the economy of a man who'd done ten thousand river kilometers, reading the current the way Ilsa read a triage room—one glance, everything sorted. Pico rode the bow like a figurehead, young enough to enjoy the sun.

The settlements thinned as they climbed. Villages became clusters, clusters became single houses, houses became foundations with weeds growing through them. On the third day they passed a church with no roof and a cemetery whose headstones all faced the wrong way—away from the river, as if even the dead had been told to look elsewhere.

At the second sampling station, a boy was waiting for them on the bank.

He was maybe fourteen, barefoot, one arm cradled against his chest at a wrong angle, and he'd walked—or limped—kilometers down a trail Ilsa could barely see. He didn't wave. He just stood there, swaying slightly, gray around the lips, until Ansel cut the engine and the boat drifted in.

"¿Qué pasó?" Ansel called.

The boy spoke fast and low, eyes going past them to the treeline. "Trampa. En el sendero alto." A trap. On the high trail. "Fui a buscar cabras. Hay alambres. Con..." He made a vague gesture he couldn't finish because his arm wouldn't move.

Tripwire. Ilsa was moving before the boat touched gravel.

Up close he was worse than he'd pretended—shocky, clammy, pulse rapid and thin. She got him sitting on a rock in the shade, cut away his shirt sleeve with her trauma shears, and felt her stomach drop into the old cold professional place.

A snare wire, thin cable anchored to a spring pole, had caught him across the forearm when he stumbled off the trail. It hadn't amputated anything, but it had opened him from elbow to wrist in a long spiral, deep enough to show tendon, and he'd walked on it for hours with it bound in a rag that had gone stiff with dried blood.

"How long ago?" she asked in Spanish, already flushing the wound with saline.

"This morning. Early."

Hours. She worked. Pressure, irrigation, checking the tendons—intact, thank God—then the sutures, twenty-two of them, layered, while Pico held the boy's shoulders and talked to him about football in the relentless cheerful way Pico had. The boy's name was Ezequiel. He had a goat herd and a mother who thought he was checking traps of their own, legal ones, rabbit snares.

"It wasn't a rabbit snare," Ilsa said, when she'd tied off the last knot and was wrapping the forearm. "Those wires you hit. Who put them there?"

Ezequiel's jaw set. He was fourteen and trying to be brave and mostly managing it. "Los hombres," he said. "The patrol men. They string them all along the high trails now. Above the gorge. My cousin stepped on one last year—a pressure plate, bigger, it took his foot." He looked at his bandaged arm. "They say the trails are closed. They don't say why. There's nothing up there but rocks and goats."

"Nothing up there," Ansel repeated softly. He'd been standing apart the whole time, arms folded, looking upriver toward the wall of the gorge where the water went white and vanished between cliffs. Now he crouched to the boy's level. "Ezequiel. The men who patrol—who are they? Police? Army?"

The boy shook his head. "No uniforms we know. Green ones. New trucks. They pay for information, sometimes. Five hundred pesos if you tell them strangers came up the river." He hesitated, then, with the fatalism of children raised in hard country: "I'll tell them about you anyway. For five hundred. Not because I want to—it's because if I don't, and they find out I saw you and said nothing, they'll ask my mother questions. You understand?"

Ansel held his gaze for a moment. "I understand. Take the money. Tell them whatever keeps your mother safe." He glanced at Ilsa. "Just tell them we were fisheries people doing fish things, and that we turned around."

"Will you turn around?"

Ansel didn't answer that. He pressed a fold of bills into the boy's hand—more than five hundred—and said, "For the goats you didn't check today. Keep the bandage clean. Change it tomorrow. If it goes red or hot, come find the doctora at the estancia below the falls. She'll fix it free."

Ezequiel looked at the money, then at Ilsa, and something in his face cracked open—young fury, suddenly, the kind that comes when a child realizes adults have been lying to him his whole life. "My grandfather fished this river," he said. "Before. He said the water used to run green in summer, from the glacier, and the trout were this big." He spread his hands wider than any trout Ilsa had ever seen. "Now there are no trout above the falls. None. Everyone says it's natural. Everyone says everything is natural." He stood, cradling his bandaged arm. "Everyone says the company is dead too. But I've seen their trucks."

He limped off up the trail, and none of them spoke until the sound of his footsteps was gone.

* * *

That night they camped on a gravel bar below the falls, boats hauled clear of the waterline, fire small and screened. Ilsa restocked her kit by headlamp, counting sutures, and found Ansel sitting at the edge of the firelight with a map on his knees he wasn't reading.

"Talk to me," she said, sitting beside him.

He was quiet long enough that she thought he wouldn't. Then: "The tripwires are new. Ten years ago there were checkpoints, men at the gates, but nothing like this. Nothing strung through goat trails. You don't guard an empty valley like it's a prison."

"Unless it isn't empty."

"No." He turned the map over, decisively, as if closing a door. "Unless it isn't."

"Ansel." She kept her voice low, pitched under the river sound. "I treated that boy. Spiral laceration from a spring-pole snare on a public trail. His cousin lost a foot to a pressure plate. These aren't security measures—they're area-denial weapons aimed at civilians. Somewhere above us, people are paying for that hardware, deploying it, maintaining it, and filing the receipts under a company that legally does not exist. I've seen cover-ups before. Hospital ones. They're small. They end at a boardroom door. This one ends—" she gestured upriver, at the black gorge, at whatever burned beyond it, "—somewhere up there, and it's been running for a decade, and the only reason nobody's blown it open is that everyone downstream has learned to keep their heads down and call it natural."

"Which is exactly why we should sample our fish and go home."

"Is that what you believe?"

The fire popped. Across the bar, Pico was asleep in his bag with his boots on, the way he always did, as if readiness were a religion. Teo sat up with his laptop, its glow blue on his face, chasing shell companies through the dark.

Ansel looked at the fire for a long time. When he finally spoke, his voice had a strange flat quality, like a man reading coordinates.

"When the dam failed, the official story was seepage. Foundation failure. Act of God, basically. Eleven dead, mine among them. The inquiry took four months and concluded nobody was criminally negligent, which is what inquiries conclude when they're paid to. I was named in the report—guide error, route negligence. My license died. Then Peru's blacklist, then Chile's." He flexed his hands, scarred knuckles catching firelight. "I believed it. That's the worst part. I believed I'd killed my own brother, because believing it was simpler than the alternative, which was that I couldn't remember, and not remembering meant somebody had taken the memory, and somebody taking the memory meant somebody had something worth hiding."

"Do you remember now?"

"Pieces." His jaw tightened. "Water. A flashlight. Someone saying a word I can't make out. That's all. Like looking at the bottom of a lake."

Ilsa reached over and put her hand on his forearm—the same arm, roughly, where she'd stitched Ezequiel an hour ago. "In medicine we have a saying. If you hear hoofbeats, think horses, not zebras. But when every expert in the country tells you it's zebras, and the stable is locked, and there are armed men guarding the stable door—" she squeezed, gently, "—maybe stop doubting the horses."

Ansel looked at her hand, then at her face, and for a moment the practiced calm dropped entirely and she saw what lived underneath: not fear exactly. Hunger. Ten years of a man starving himself on guilt because guilt was at least a meal he could control.

"Ezequiel said they pay five hundred pesos for information," he said at last. "About strangers on the river."

"Yes."

"Then starting tomorrow, we're being paid for too. Everything we do from here gets reported within a day." He folded the map. "Which means we have exactly one night's lead on ourselves, forever, from here to the source."

"That's not a plan, Ansel. That's a countdown."

"Yeah," he said. "It is."

Across the fire, Teo suddenly sat up straight, laptop tilted toward him, and made a sound—not a word, just a short exhalation, the sound of a man finding a vein of ore.

"What?" Ilsa called.

Teo turned the screen toward them. On it was a scanned document, grainy, stamped, official-looking. "Servicios Logísticos Apurímac's annual expense filings. I cracked the shell's registration archive." His finger trembled slightly on the trackpad. "Ilsa, the maintenance contracts list a facility code. A fixed installation, staffed year-round, in the upper watershed. Fuel, food, medical supplies, ammunition—requisitioned monthly, for ten years, for a site that officially contains nothing." He looked up, and the firelight and the screen-glow met in his eyes. "And the requisition quantities doubled eighteen months ago. Doubled, and changed. Less food. More explosives."

Ansel rose slowly to his feet, and Ilsa watched ten years fall off him and ten years of weight settle onto him in the same breath.

"Get Mara on the radio," he said. "Tell her we're coming. And tell her—" he looked upriver, where the falls made their white noise in the dark, "—tell her to bring everything she knows about what's buried under that dam. Because whatever it is, they've started digging it out."
