## 9. Blacklist Arithmetic

Ansel did his best thinking with a pencil.

Not a pen—pens committed you. A pencil could be erased, and erasure was the closest thing to prayer he'd found in ten years of exile. He sat at the kitchen table in the crew house in Puerto Hondo with the lamp turned low and a sheet of paper ruled into two columns, the way he'd been doing it since Bolivia, since Chile, since every border that had closed behind him like water over a stone.

The left column he labeled *COSTS*. The right column stayed blank, because he had never once in his life been able to fill it honestly.

He sharpened the pencil with a knife, curling the shaving off in one long ribbon, and began.

*Cost one.* The job was in Peru. Not near Peru. *In* Peru—the Río Ceniza rose on the eastern shoulder of the Cordillera, inside a watershed that had been sealed by decree for a decade, sealed so thoroughly that even the maps sold in Cusco printed the valley as blank white space, as if the glacier had never existed, as if eleven people had never drowned beneath it. Ansel's name sat on a list in Lima. Not an arrest warrant—that would have been simple, legal, almost comfortable. A blacklist. A quiet administrative note passed between ministries and mining interests: *this man is not to be permitted entry, employment, or documentation.* He had seen the note once, years ago, in a border office in Desaguadero, glimpsed upside down across a clerk's desk before the clerk flipped the folder shut. His own name typed in a font no different from any other word. That was the thing about blacklists. They didn't shout. They just made you weather.

*If Mara Voss's job goes wrong,* he wrote, *I lose Peru permanently.*

He underlined *permanently* twice, then crossed out both lines because they felt theatrical.

*Cost two.* Chile. The only other country on earth still willing to stamp his passport. Chile had taken him in after the disaster the way a hospital takes in a man nobody will claim—without warmth, without questions, because its southern valleys always needed guides crazy enough to work them. But Chile and Peru shared more than a border. They shared contractors. The same security firms that patrolled the Ceniza watershed had offices in Santiago, and firms like those kept lists too, longer ones than governments, and they traded. If Ansel Sandoval was photographed standing at the source of the Ceniza, the photograph would travel south faster than he could. Within a season there would be nowhere on the continent that would let him off a plane.

He set the pencil down and looked at what he'd written. Two costs. Both of them the same cost, really: the last of the world, closing.

There were other costs that didn't fit columns. Teo's forged passport would not survive a Peruvian checkpoint interrogation—Ilsa had said as much, and Ilsa was right about most things she said quietly. Pico was twenty-three years old and had never been arrested anywhere, and there was a version of this job where that stopped being true. And Mara Voss was paying triple, and triple pay meant triple risk, and triple risk meant she already knew something about the valley that made her willing to spend other people's lives to learn the rest.

The house creaked around him. Rain on the tin roof, the sound he'd learned to sleep through in four countries. Somewhere down the hall Teo was still awake—he could hear the small rhythmic click of the engineer's slide rule, a habit from a university that had revoked his degree, working some problem he wouldn't talk about until it was solved.

Ansel picked up the pencil again.

*Cost three,* he wrote, and then couldn't finish it, because cost three wasn't money or borders. Cost three was the widow in the fishing village downstream who had spat at his feet, whose face he now carried in the same pocket of himself where he carried Tomás's voice saying *you go ahead, I'll check the gauge.* If he went back into that watershed, he would walk past more of those faces. Every one of them would see the man who walked away while their sons went under. Some of them might be right to.

He heard the click-click-click stop down the hall. Then start again. Teo solving, unsolving.

Ansel blew out the lamp.

* * *

He decided to refuse her properly in the morning, which meant he spent the night refusing her badly.

Sleep came in pieces, each one carrying a different border town, a different clerk sliding a folder shut. Toward dawn he dreamed of water—not the flood itself, which he had never once dreamed whole, but the sound of it, the pressure of it, the way a river the size of a falling building doesn't roar so much as erase every other sound including your own heartbeat. He woke with his hand fisted in the blanket and lay listening to the rain until it turned into ordinary rain again.

That was when he got up, dressed in the dark, and walked out to decide the thing for good.

Puerto Hondo at first light was all wet stone and woodsmoke, the river running brown with high-country melt, gulls arguing over the fish market. Ansel walked the length of the waterfront with his collar up, doing what he always did when a decision wouldn't come: he moved. Never stop moving—that was the rule, the only rule he'd ever managed to keep, the rule that had kept him alive through two expulsions and a decade of jobs no one else would touch. Movement as method. Movement as mercy. A man in motion couldn't be buried, couldn't be blamed, couldn't be made to stand still long enough for anyone to look too closely at what was behind his eyes.

And the arithmetic said go. That was the ugly truth of it. The columns balanced toward refusal. Triple pay was not worth two countries; nothing was worth two countries; he had spent ten years buying back inches of map with sweat and silence and he was not going to hand them both over for a woman's guilt and a photograph of a valley that had already eaten his brother.

So why, he asked himself, standing at the end of the pier with the brown river sliding past, had he not burned the second letter?

Because there hadn't been a second letter. Not yet. The first one he'd burned in the stove three nights ago, after reading it twice—*triple pay, unnamed objective, come alone to discuss*—and the burning had changed nothing, which is how he'd known he was in trouble. Fire was supposed to settle things. It had settled nothing. Her handwriting had stayed in his head all night, the sharp forward slant of it, the same slant it had in the field notebooks she'd kept at twenty-five, when she was the fastest route-finder he'd ever competed against and the only one who beat him cleanly, without luck, without excuse.

He had told her no to her face. She had put the photograph on his table and walked out into the rain, and he had let her go, and the photograph was still on his table because he hadn't been able to make himself throw it away either.

Movement, then. He'd move. He'd take the bridge contract in the south that Teo had flagged, the one paying half rates for a week of cold work, and by the time it was done Mara Voss would have hired some other crew of ghosts, and the valley would keep its secrets, and Ansel Sandoval would keep his one remaining country and his one remaining rule.

He turned back toward the house, and that was when he saw the envelope.

It was wedged in the doorframe of the crew house, dry despite the night's rain, which meant someone had placed it there recently—within the hour, maybe less. No stamp. No return address. His name on the front in that sharp forward slant.

He stood looking at it for a long moment. A gull screamed somewhere. The river kept up its brown argument with the sea.

He took it inside, set it on the table next to the photograph and the abandoned arithmetic, and made coffee first, because whatever was in the letter deserved a man who was fully awake to hate it.

Then he opened it.

* * *

*Ansel.*

*I'll be brief, because I know you don't read long letters and trust even fewer.*

*You said no. I expected you to say no. I want you to notice that I came anyway, and that I'm writing anyway, because I am not hiring a guide. Guides are replaceable. What I need is the only living person who has stood at the source of the Ceniza and walked away.*

*You're going to tell me you've never been there. Everyone says that. The official record says you were pulled from the river forty kilometers downstream, half-drowned, with no memory of the final hour. Concussion, they called it. Trauma. The report used the word "amnesia" the way reports use words—to close a door rather than open one.*

*But here is what the official record doesn't say, Ansel, and what I have spent ten years unable to stop asking:*

*Nobody swims out of a debris flood. You don't survive that water by swimming. You survive it because someone puts you in it—downstream, deliberately, alive—and I have never believed the river did that by accident.*

*Someone wanted you out of that valley breathing. Think about what that means. Think about who benefits from a witness who can't remember. Think about the incident report filed three days later with your thumbprint on it—you signed something, Ansel, or someone signed something with your hand, and that signature is the foundation your blacklist is built on, and I don't think you built it.*

*I testified against you. I won't insult us both by apologizing in a letter. The evidence pointed at you and I followed it, and I have followed it every year since, and every year it has pointed somewhere darker and further upstream, and last month it pointed at a survey team working a site that has been sealed by decree for a decade. Someone is spending millions guarding a ruin. Ruins don't need guards. Secrets do.*

*I know what I'm asking you to walk back into. I know what it costs. I'm not asking you to do it for me, or for the dead, or even for Tomás, though God knows he'd have gone without being asked twice.*

*I'm asking you to do it because the missing hour is the only thing in this world that can't be bought back once it's destroyed, and everything else about that valley is being destroyed right now, tonight, while you read this.*

*Come to the source. Remember what they paid you to forget. Or burn this letter too—but burn it knowing that I know, and that I have known for years, and that I am the only person alive who is asking the question instead of burying it.*

*—M.V.*

*P.S. Your brother cached supplies above the icefall. I have the manifest. Ask yourself how I got it.*

Ansel read it three times.

The first time, his hands were steady. The second time, they were not. By the third time he had put the page flat on the table and pressed it there with both palms, the way you press down on something that is trying to fly apart, and he was aware distantly of the coffee going cold beside him and of Teo's slide rule clicking down the hall and of the rain starting up again on the tin roof, and underneath all of it, rising like water in a gauge, the sound of the missing hour.

A flashlight. That was the oldest fragment, the one that surfaced sometimes at altitude, sometimes in fever—a flashlight held too close, swinging, blinding. And a voice. He had never been able to hold onto the voice; it dissolved every time like a fish through fingers. *Sign it,* the dream-voice said, or seemed to say, or maybe he had invented it over ten years of wanting there to be something to remember besides water.

Tomás shouting. That fragment was worse, because it came with feeling attached—urgency, fury, the specific anger of a younger brother who has caught you in something—and Ansel had spent a decade telling himself it was memory of an argument from days before the dam failed, some ordinary quarrel about rope or rations, anything but the last thing Tomás ever said to him.

Mara knew. Not guessed—knew. Knew about the amnesia, knew about the downstream recovery point, knew things about the incident report that weren't in any public record, and knew—or claimed to know—about a cache above the icefall that could only have come from one place: from Tomás himself, from the brother who had reached the source first and died trying to bring something out.

She had done what he had never dared. She had taken the missing hour seriously. She had treated it not as a wound but as evidence.

Ansel got up and stood at the window. The rain had thickened; the waterfront was dissolving into gray; a fishing boat was coming in with its lights on at midmorning, which meant the bar outside the harbor was rough, which meant the men on it were having a bad day, which meant nothing, which meant his mind was scrabbling for any thought that wasn't the letter.

The arithmetic was still on the table. Two countries. One rule. Ten years of movement, of never stopping long enough to be seen, of exile maintained like a garden—daily, carefully, at the cost of everything else growing.

And now a single line of new data, written in a rival's hand:

*Someone wanted you out of that valley breathing.*

If it was true, then his survival wasn't luck. His disgrace wasn't justice. His blacklist wasn't a verdict—it was a receipt, proof of purchase, the paper trail of a bargain struck over his unconscious body while his brother's river ran red with silt. And if that was true, then every border clerk who had slid a folder shut, every ministry that had typed his name into a font no different from any other word, every year he had spent moving because moving was the only mercy he could afford—all of it had been built on a lie he had swallowed without ever tasting.

They hadn't just taken Tomás. They had taken the story, and made Ansel the villain of it, and charged him ten years of exile for the privilege.

The pencil was still lying on the table. He picked it up. Under *COST ONE*, in the space where the right column had waited empty for a decade, he wrote a single word:

*Truth.*

Then he looked at it for a long time, hating it, because it wasn't a payment. It didn't balance anything. Truth didn't buy back a country, didn't un-drown eleven people, didn't fill a column so much as burn the whole ledger down. It was the one line item that made all the others irrelevant, and that was exactly why he had never allowed himself to write it.

Down the hall, the slide rule stopped.

Footsteps. Teo appeared in the doorway with his glasses pushed up into his hair and the look of a man who has been awake all night holding a solved problem he isn't sure he wants to say out loud.

"You didn't sleep," Teo said.

"No."

Teo came in, saw the letter, saw the photograph, saw the word at the bottom of the ledger. He didn't touch any of it. He pulled out the second chair and sat down heavily, the way he did when he'd decided something and was bracing the other person for it.

"I ran the numbers on the survey equipment," he said. "From the photograph. Theodolite model, the generator, the drill casings. Ansel—that gear isn't salvage work. It's demolition prep. Somebody is getting ready to take that dam ruin apart, and you don't demolish a ruin unless there's something under it you need gone."

Ansel looked at the window, at the gray rain, at the brown river running relentlessly toward the sea, carrying everything downstream the way it always had, the way it had carried him.

"They're destroying it," he said. "Right now."

"Every day we sit here." Teo folded his hands on the table, over the edge of the letter, careful not to cover Mara's name. "So I need to know. Are we voting on this? Because the others are awake, and they're waiting, and Pico's already packed."

Ansel looked down at the ledger. At the two columns. At the word he had written in the place where payment should go.

"Mara sent a second letter," he said.

"I gathered."

"She knows about the missing hour, Teo. She knows things about that night that aren't in any file I've ever seen. And she says—" His voice did something he didn't authorize. He steadied it. "She says my brother cached evidence above the icefall. Ten years ago. Before he died."

The rain filled the silence. Teo took off his glasses, cleaned them on his shirt, put them back on—the ritual he performed when the world needed reassembling.

"Then we're not voting on a job," Teo said quietly. "We're voting on whether the truth gets dug up or buried. And I already know how Ilsa votes. And Pico—" he almost smiled, "—Pico voted the day he met you."

Ansel stared at the ledger a moment longer. Then he picked up the pencil, turned the page over, and on the clean back wrote three words at the top, large enough that there would be no pretending later he hadn't meant them:

*Upstream. All of us.*

He was still looking at them when the knock came at the door—three knocks, unhurried, deliberate—and through the frosted glass of the pane, a silhouette he recognized, standing in the rain as if she had never left.
