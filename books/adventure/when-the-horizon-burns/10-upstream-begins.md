## 10. Upstream Begins

Pico had never seen a river look angry before.

He'd seen rivers that were fast, rivers that were brown with silt, rivers that ate a fence post whole during the spring melt back home. But the Ceniza at the mouth of the gorge was something else. It came out of the canyon walls like something being expelled—grey-green, muscular, its surface braided with standing waves and boils that opened and closed like breathing things. The sound of it filled the whole valley floor, a low continuous roar that Pico felt in his molars.

"Boats go in there," he said, not quite meaning it as a question.

"Boats go in there," Teo confirmed, cinching down a strap on the trailer rack. He said it the way he said everything, flat and certain, as if the universe had already agreed to his terms.

They'd left the trucks an hour before dawn in a gravel pit twelve kilometers downstream, tucked under netting and cut brush, paid for in cash by a man who didn't ask questions because Ansel paid him not to. Then they'd carried everything—the boats, the frames, the dry bags, the crates of food Ilsa had labeled in her small precise handwriting—down a goat track to the put-in. Pico's shoulders were already burning and they hadn't even touched water yet.

Ansel stood at the edge of the current doing what he always did before anything: nothing. Just looking. Pico had learned that about him early, back on the bridge job, when everyone else had been scrambling and shouting and Ansel had stood still for four full minutes while a cable groaned overhead. When he finally moved, every movement counted. It was like watching someone who'd already run the disaster once in his head and found the exits.

"You'll be with me," Ansel said now, without turning around. "Teo takes the second boat with Ilsa. Mara's gear goes split between both."

"And you?" Pico asked Mara.

She was crouched over a map she'd laminated herself, tracing the gorge with one finger. She didn't look up. "I know this river better from the wrong end," she said. "I'll ride sweep."

*The wrong end.* Pico turned that phrase over. There were a lot of phrases like that on this trip, words people used that had weight underneath them, sediment under clear water. *The source. The missing hour. Eleven.* Nobody explained them to him. He'd stopped asking somewhere around the border town, when he'd realized that asking made Ansel's face do something complicated and closed, like a door swinging shut on well-oiled hinges.

He didn't need to know everything. He needed to be useful. That was the deal he'd made with himself, and so far he'd kept it.

"Listen up." Ansel turned, and the crew gathered without being called, the way they always did. Even Mara, though she stayed a half-step outside the circle, which was her way of being inside it. "First day is easy water. Class III, maybe a hard IV at the narrows if the gauge is lying. We scout nothing today, we portage nothing today. We make kilometers." He held up two fingers. "Two rules. One: nobody ties into a boat. If you swim, you swim free. Two—" and here he paused, and Pico saw him glance upstream toward the grey wall of the gorge, where the canyon pinched down into shadow, "—if you see anything in the sky that isn't a bird, you get off the water. Fast. No discussion, no heroics. Undercut banks, willow cover, whatever's there. We move at night until we're past the lower gorge."

"Why?" Pico asked, and then heard how young his own voice sounded, and hated it.

Ansel looked at him for a moment. Not unkindly. "Because the river isn't the only thing watching this valley," he said. "Because we're ghosts from here on. Ghosts don't fly drones."

Nobody laughed. Pico hadn't really expected anyone to.

Ilsa broke the silence by slapping the hull of the lead boat twice, sharp, like a doctor starting a heart. "In," she said. "Daylight's spending itself while we stand here admiring the view."

* * *

The first hour was the best hour of Pico's life, and he knew even while living it that he would spend years trying to describe it and fail.

The Ceniza took them the way a big animal takes a rider—not gently, but without malice. The boat dropped into the first rapid and the world became noise and cold spray and Teo's voice behind them, high and delighted, whooping like a man half his age. Pico paddled until his arms screamed and then kept paddling because stopping felt like blasphemy. Water hit his face and tasted like rock, like minerals, like the mountain ground fine. Ahead of him Ansel read the current with tiny corrections, a blade of the paddle here, a lean there, and the boat went exactly where he wanted it as if the river had agreed in advance.

"Left channel!" Ansel shouted over the roar. "Hard left!"

Pico dug in. The bow swung. A hole the size of a dining table yawned where they would have been, and then they were past it, surfing the shoulder of a wave, and Pico was laughing, actually laughing, loud and stupid, and he saw the corner of Ansel's mouth move. On Ansel that was practically a parade.

Behind them, Mara ran sweep with a competence that made Pico's neck prickle. She didn't paddle so much as conduct, minimal strokes, maximum authority, her boat sliding through lines that looked impossible and weren't, apparently, if you'd spent enough years on enough water. Once, in a flat stretch between rapids, she pulled alongside and studied Pico for a long moment.

"You've got good instincts," she said. "Bad hands. You're muscling it."

"I'm—"

"Don't argue with me, boy, I've been running rivers since before you could spell *kayak*. Relax your grip. Let the boat tell you what it wants. You're not fighting the Ceniza. You're negotiating with it."

"Yes, ma'am."

Something flickered across her face at *ma'am*—annoyance or amusement, he couldn't tell—and then she dropped back and was gone behind them, watchful, always watchful, sweeping the sky as much as the water.

By midday they'd covered eleven kilometers and Pico's world had contracted to a simple, holy rhythm: read, paddle, breathe, repeat. His blisters had blistered. The sun came and went through cloud. The gorge walls rose around them, layered stone going from ochre to rust to a deep bruised purple, and the river narrowed and quickened, and the feeling of it—that feeling, the one Pico couldn't name yet, the one that felt like flying and falling at the same time—kept building.

That was why it took him too long to notice that Ansel had gone quiet.

Not quiet-quiet. Ansel was never chatty. But there was a specific quality to his silence, a coiled quality, and Pico had started learning to hear it. Now Ansel wasn't looking at the water ahead. He was looking up. At the canyon rim, three hundred meters of vertical rock above them, where the afternoon light lay long and gold.

"What is it?" Pico asked.

For a second he thought Ansel wouldn't answer. Then: "Bird count."

"Birds?"

"How many have you seen today?"

Pico thought back. The morning, the flats near the put-in: herons, a pair of condors riding the first thermals, some small darting swallows he couldn't name. Since entering the gorge proper—

"None," he said slowly. "No birds."

"No birds." Ansel's voice was level, but his knuckles had gone pale around the shaft of his paddle. "A gorge like this should be full of them. Cliff nesters, fish feeders. Nothing. That means somebody's been flying this canyon often enough that everything with wings has decided to live somewhere else."

The exhilaration that had been carrying Pico all day did something strange then. It didn't vanish. It curdled. It sat in his chest like milk turning, sweet becoming sour, and suddenly the same spray that had tasted like glory ten minutes ago just tasted cold.

Ahead, a whistle—two short notes. Teo's boat eddying out against the right bank, Ilsa already pointing upward. And there, finally, sound arriving late after sight: a thin mechanical buzz, insectile, wrong, echoing off the canyon walls so it seemed to come from everywhere.

"Bank! Now!" Ansel's voice cracked across the water like a whip.

* * *

They hauled the boats into a fold of undercut rock beneath a curtain of dead willows, and Pico lay on cold stone with river water soaking through his clothes and his heart slamming against his ribs, and watched a machine hunt them.

It was small—a quadcopter, maybe, or something built for survey work, matte grey, with a sensor pod underneath that caught the light once and went dark again. It flew the center of the canyon in slow, methodical legs, the way a man mows a lawn. Back. Turn. Forward. Back. Turn. It was maybe eight hundred meters upstream and closing, and the sound of it, that flat electric whine, was the most obscene noise Pico had ever heard in a wild place. It didn't belong here the way a gunshot doesn't belong in a cathedral.

"It's mapping," Teo whispered beside him. He'd produced a pair of binoculars from somewhere inside his jacket; of course he had. "Running a grid pattern. Thermal pod, looks like. If it's got a decent sensor, it reads body heat through light cover."

"Then we're cooked," Ilsa murmured. She had Teo's arm in her lap and was, absurdly, calmly, re-taping a bandage she'd put on him that morning. Her hands never stopped working. Pico suspected that was how she survived things.

"Wet rock," Ansel breathed. He was flattened against the stone, barely moving his lips. "Cold water kills thermal signature. Everyone in the shallows, boats between you and the open air. Slow movements. If it passes us, nobody exhales big."

Mara slid in last, silent as oil, and for a moment they were five shapes pressed into a seam of the earth, listening to the future come looking for them.

The drone grew louder. Pico discovered that terror has a texture—it's cold, it's mineral, it tastes like the water seeping past his lips. He thought, wildly, of his mother's kitchen, of the smell of frying plantains, of ordinary life happening in places where the sky was empty. He thought about how two days ago the worst thing in his world was a blister.

The whine crested directly overhead—or nearly. Through the willow screen he could see it: a dark cross against the pale slot of sky, hanging, tilting, its sensor pod rotating slowly like a blind eye trying to see.

Then it moved on. Upstream. Methodical. Patient. It did not come back.

They waited anyway. Twenty minutes. Thirty. Ansel counted it in silence, lips moving, and when he finally rose, mud to his eyebrows, he looked less like a legend and more like something the river had grown.

"That's a patrol loop," he said quietly. "Which means it's not random. Which means somebody has this canyon scheduled." He looked upstream, at the narrowing walls, at the water running fast and grey toward the sealed valley none of them were supposed to reach. "We're done traveling in daylight."

* * *

They made camp in a hollow above the river as the light died, no fire, cold food, and Pico learned that night what exhaustion does to fear—it doesn't erase it, it just makes room for both.

They launched again at 2 a.m., under a sky so thick with stars it looked like the black water had been flung upward. Running whitewater blind was its own kind of madness: Ansel in front reading the river by sound, by the pale seams of foam, by instinct so old it might as well have been genetic, calling commands in a low voice that somehow carried. Teo's boat a darker shadow behind. Mara sweeping the rear, and every few minutes, Pico noticed, her head tilted upward, tracking the sky.

At dawn they cached the boats in willows and slept in shifts, and at dusk they launched again, and the days folded into each other like wet paper. Pico stopped keeping count. His hands hardened. His eyes learned the night. Somewhere in there, without anyone announcing it, the trip changed species—from adventure to evasion—and the crew changed with it, conversations dropping to whispers, laughter rationed like fuel. Even Teo stopped narrating the geology.

On the fourth night, drifting a long flat stretch beneath walls that leaned together overhead until the stars were a jagged ribbon, Pico took his turn at watch while the others dozed in the boats, tied off in an eddy. The silence was enormous. The river talked to itself in the dark, patient, endless, going where it had always gone.

And then, far upstream—so far it might have been imagination—he heard it.

Not the drone. Something deeper. A rhythmic concussion rolling down the canyon, soft as a heartbeat heard through a wall. Boom. A pause long enough to doubt it. Boom.

Pico's skin crawled. He looked at Ansel, asleep sitting up against the gear pile, face slack, younger-looking, haunted even in sleep. He looked upstream, where the darkness was total.

The blasting had begun. Someone was erasing something, and the sound traveled down the water like a message, like a warning, like an invitation.

Boom.

Pico reached over and shook Ansel's shoulder, and Ansel came awake instantly, completely, eyes open and fixed on him in the starlight, and Pico said the only true thing he had:

"It's started. Whatever they're doing up there—it's started."
