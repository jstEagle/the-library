## 11. Rapids and Reckonings

Teo had rebuilt bridges in three countries and burned passports in two, and he had never been as afraid of anything as he was of the sound the Ceniza made around the bend.

It wasn't a roar. Roars were honest. This was a grinding, a chewing, a sound like the gorge itself working its jaw, and it came up through the aluminum floor of the boat and into the soles of his boots and settled in his teeth. He crouched behind the dry box with his arms wrapped around the gear straps and did the thing he did when fear arrived, which was to count. Eleven bags lashed. Two spare paddles. One satellite phone in a waterproof case that had already failed once. One guide standing in the stern like he was built there.

Ansel Sandoval didn't look at the water ahead of them. He looked at the water beside them—the speed of the foam sliding past the rocks, the angle of the current lines—and whatever he read there made him nod once, to himself, the way a man nods at a bill he'd been expecting.

"Pico!" he shouted over the noise. "When I say left, you're my left arm. Not my opinion. My arm. You feel the pull before you fight it. You hear me?"

Pico was in the bow, twenty years old and grinning like this was a festival, and Teo wanted to shake him. The kid had been grinning since dawn. Somewhere in the last two weeks Pico had decided that running rivers with Ansel was the best thing that had ever happened to him, and Teo understood it—God help him, he understood it, because he'd watched the man work a rope and a rapid and a map and had felt the same pull himself—but understanding didn't make the grin less terrifying. Grins like that were how young men ended up as stories other people told.

"He hears you," Teo called. "I'll make sure of it."

"Then hold on to something," Ansel said, "that isn't the boat."

The bend gave them the rapid all at once, the way a curtain gives you a stage.

Teo had seen the Ceniza angry. He'd seen it brown and swollen in the lower gorge, carrying whole trees like matchsticks. This was worse, because this was clear. The water came down the throat of the canyon in three channels split by black fangs of rock, and every channel ended the same way: in a hole, a reversal the color of hammered tin, where the river dropped into itself and ate whatever the river gave it. Above the holes the water stood up in waves taller than the boat. Spray hung in the air like weather.

"Class five," Ilsa said from the middle seat. She wasn't shouting. She'd gone quiet, which was how Ilsa did fear—she folded it small and put it away and her hands got very still. "Teo, is that class five?"

"That's class five," Teo said.

"Ansel." Her voice carried anyway. "The plan was to scout this."

"The plan was to scout this if there was an eddy to scout from." Ansel's voice was flat and even, a surveyor's voice. "There isn't. We're committed. Left channel. Pico—"

The boat hit the first wave before he finished the sentence.

Teo's whole world became water. It came over the bow like a door opening, solid and green and lit from inside, and then they were through it and dropping and the canyon walls were spinning up on either side and Ansel was shouting left, LEFT, and Pico dug in and the boat swung and missed the first fang by a distance Teo would measure later, in the dark, as roughly the width of a coffin.

The second wave hit them sideways.

He heard it happen more than felt it—the dry box tearing loose on one strap, Ilsa's hand clamping his shoulder, and then Pico, Pico going out of the bow like something the river had decided to take, no scream, just a flash of red jacket and gone, swallowed, the boat suddenly lighter and suddenly wrong.

Teo's mind did the arithmetic it always did. Pico in the water at the top of a Class V. Two holes below. Water temperature fed by glacier, which meant minutes, not hours, before the cold took his hands and then his will. A swimmer in water like this didn't drown all at once. He drowned in stages, and the first stage was the river taking his paddle, and the second was the river taking his orientation, and the third was the river holding him down in a hole and recirculating him until his lungs gave out, and the whole process was very efficient and had been running for millions of years and did not care that Pico was twenty and grinned too much.

Ansel moved.

Teo had watched Ansel do a hundred impossible things in the time he'd known him, and he'd catalogued them, because that was his nature, he kept ledgers. But he had never seen anything like the next four seconds. Ansel didn't grab a rope. There was no time for a rope. He put the boat into the current line behind Pico—one stroke, two, the stern swinging with a precision that shouldn't have been possible at that speed—and then he was out of the boat himself, over the side, into water that could crack a man's ribs, and Teo understood in one horrified instant what he was doing: he was closing the distance swimming because the boat couldn't hold the line, and he was doing it with no rope attached to anything, which meant that if he missed, the river would have both of them.

"Ansel!" Ilsa was on her knees now, hauling the boat around, and Teo grabbed the spare paddle and dug with everything his shoulders had, and the three of them—the boat, the doctor, the engineer—chased two men down the throat of the rapid.

He saw them once, clearly, in a gap between waves. Ansel had Pico by the collar of his life vest, one arm locked, and they were going down the face of the biggest wave together like a single animal, and Ansel's free hand was out, flat, reading the water, and at the bottom of the wave, where every instinct Teo owned screamed *away*, Ansel angled them *into* the hole.

It was the right call. Teo knew it was the right call even as his heart stopped. The hole was the only thing in the river moving slower than they were. A swimmer in the current was a leaf; a swimmer in the eddy behind the hole was a man in a room. Ansel drove them into the room and the river slammed shut behind them.

They came around the last fang and there was the eddy, a pocket of green calm against the canyon wall, and Ansel was in it, holding Pico's head above water, both of them breathing, both of them alive, and Teo discovered he was screaming and didn't remember starting.

Getting them into the boat took ten minutes and cost Teo the skin off both palms. Pico came over the gunwale coughing and shaking and alive, and Ilsa was on him instantly, checking pupils, pulse, the works, and Ansel climbed in last, unhurried, water sheeting off him, and picked up his paddle as if he'd stepped out to check the weather.

"Below this," he said, "it eases. We'll run the rest in shifts. Pico, you're in the middle seat until I say otherwise."

Pico, blue-lipped and trembling, managed something between a laugh and a sob. "You jumped in. You just—you jumped *in*."

"The boat couldn't hold the line."

"You jumped in a Class five with no rope."

Ansel looked at him then, and something crossed his face that Teo had never seen there before—something almost like confusion, like a man surprised by his own reflection. "You're alive," he said. "That's the whole report. Bailing position, everyone. We've got water in the floor."

They bailed. The river, true to his word, eased. The canyon opened by degrees, the black fangs giving way to gravel bars, the roar behind them fading into ordinary water-sound, and by late afternoon the terror had worn down into something like giddiness. Pico told the story twice, growing in the telling, and Ilsa let him, and Teo sat in the stern where Ansel usually stood and watched the man who had jumped.

That was the thing Teo couldn't stop turning over. Not the skill. The skill was real; he'd have staked his forged passport on it. It was the *arithmetic*. Ansel had gone over the side with no rope, no backup, no way back, in water that killed strong swimmers in minutes. If he'd missed—if the current had taken him a body-length to the left—there would have been three people in that boat watching two men drown, and one of them a doctor with no patient to save. The odds had worked. But Ansel hadn't seemed to consult them. He'd moved the way a man moves when something older than decision is driving him, and Teo, who had spent his adult life calculating loads and tolerances and margins, recognized the thing he'd seen in the guide's face at the top of the rapid and again when Pico thanked him.

Ansel Sandoval hadn't been thinking about the odds.

He'd been thinking about the river taking someone else. And Teo had a bad, cold feeling that he knew exactly which memory that was, and that it had nothing to do with courage, and everything to do with a night ten years ago that the man never talked about.

* * *

Camp that night was a gravel bar below a wall of granite, and the fire was small because everything they carried was counted. Ilsa dosed Pico with something warm and made him drink it and threatened him with a full examination in the morning, and Pico fell asleep mid-sentence, mid-story, his face slack and boyish and still faintly blue at the lips.

Teo waited until Ilsa turned in. Then he took the coffee pot and two cups and walked to the far end of the bar, where Ansel sat alone with the river, mending a strap that didn't need mending.

"Drink," Teo said, and put a cup in his hand, and sat down without being invited.

For a while neither of them spoke. The Ceniza ran past, quieter here, almost apologetic.

"That was the best boat handling I've ever seen," Teo said. "I want to say that first, so you know the rest isn't ingratitude."

Ansel's needle paused. "But."

"But I've been doing the math since the eddy." Teo turned his cup. He was not a man who enjoyed this—confrontation sat in his stomach like a swallowed bolt—but he'd joined this crew because Ansel was the only guide left who took jobs no one else would, and he'd stayed because Ansel had never once spent a life cheaply, and today he'd watched the man spend his own like it was pocket change, and the difference mattered. "No rope. No backup. If you'd missed the eddy line by two meters, we'd have watched you both recirculate in that hole until the river got bored. Ilsa can't swim in that. I can't. You'd have made us widows of a kind, and you'd have done it to save a kid you could've saved with a throw bag if you'd had one rigged. Which we didn't, because someone decided we didn't need one today."

"I misjudged the rapid."

"You didn't misjudge anything. That's what I'm telling you." Teo made himself look at him. In the firelight Ansel's face was all planes and shadow, ten years of hard country written on it. "You read that rapid in about four seconds and you read it right, and then you ignored the part of the reading that said *wait, rig a line, run it empty first*. You skipped your own playbook. And I've been asking myself all evening why a man as good as you would do that, and I keep coming back to the same answer, and I don't like it."

"Say it, then."

"Because when Pico went in the water, you weren't thinking about the odds. You were thinking about the last time someone went in the water and you didn't go in after them."

The silence that followed had weight. Down the bar, the fire popped. Ansel set down the strap and the needle with great care, the way he did everything, and Teo realized his hands weren't quite steady.

"You don't know what you're talking about," Ansel said.

"No," Teo agreed. "I don't. Nobody on this crew does. You carry it like a locked box and we've all agreed not to ask, because you're the guide and the guide's past is his own. But today the box opened for about four seconds, and I saw what was in it, and I'm telling you as your engineer: a man who runs rivers to settle a debt with his own past is a man who'll eventually spend someone else's life paying it. That's not courage. Courage calculates. What you did today didn't."

"You'd rather I'd stayed in the boat?"

"I'd rather you'd rigged the bag. I'd rather you'd been the guide instead of the ghost." Teo heard his own voice go hard and didn't soften it. "Pico worships you. He'll jump in after you the way you jumped in after him, and he'll do it with a quarter of your skill, and someday this crew is going to lose him because he learned from you that the calculation is optional. That's what I'm afraid of. Not the river. The example."

Ansel looked out at the water for a long time. When he spoke, his voice was low and stripped of its usual flat calm, and that, more than anything, told Teo he'd cut to the bone.

"You think I don't know the arithmetic?" Ansel said. "I know it better than you do. I've run it every night for ten years. Eleven people, Teo. I've run the numbers on eleven people so many times the numbers have grooves worn in them." He picked up a stone and turned it over and didn't throw it. "You want a promise. Fine. Here's the only one I can give you. If I ever start spending your lives to pay my debt, you mutiny. Tie me up, take the boats, leave me on the bar. I'll even tell you where I keep the spare keys to the dry boxes."

"That's not—"

"It's the only promise that's worth anything, because it's the only one that puts the check in your hands instead of mine." Ansel stood, and for a moment he looked older than the country around them. "Get some sleep. We run the gorge mouth tomorrow, and after that the water's done being our biggest problem."

He walked back up the bar toward his bedroll, and Teo sat alone with the cooling coffee and felt the strange, cracked shape of the thing between them: trust with a fracture line through it, still holding load, but mapped now, and unfixable with anything he carried.

He was almost asleep when he heard it—footsteps, light, and then Ilsa's voice, low, from the dark edge of camp.

"Teo. You're up. Good." She crouched beside him, and in the starlight her face was grim in a way that had nothing to do with rapids. "The boy I treated last week. The one with the tripwire cut. I asked around the fishing camp downstream today, quiet, like we planned." She glanced back toward the river, toward the country upstream, where the gorge narrowed and the maps went blank. "There's a village up there that isn't on any of them. Moved people. A whole village, moved. And nobody who talks about it will look you in the eye."
