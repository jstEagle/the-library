## 12. The Village That Was Moved

The village appeared out of the morning fog the way drowned things appear in lakes—suddenly, and all at once, and wrong.

Ilsa saw it first from the bow of the second boat, a scatter of tin roofs catching grey light on the left bank where the Ceniza widened into something almost gentle. She counted before she could stop herself: eleven structures, a chapel with a cross bent like a question mark, a water tank on legs, laundry strung between poles that snapped in the wind like signal flags.

"Resettlement," Teo said beside her, low enough that Pico at the tiller wouldn't hear the weight in it. "Company-built. See the survey stakes? They numbered the houses."

"They numbered the houses," Ilsa repeated, because saying it aloud made it worse instead of better.

Ansel raised a fist from the lead boat and they swung for the bank. The gravel shore took the hulls with a sound like teeth grinding. He stood in the shallows a long moment, looking up at the village, and Ilsa watched him do the thing he did—the flattening, the closing of the face, the man folding himself into a smaller shape so he'd fit through whatever came next.

"Fishing survey," he said. "Same story. We're two days downstream of where we're supposed to be, we need water, we need to ask about fish runs. Nothing else." His eyes moved across each of them. "Nobody asks about the flood. Nobody says the name Ceniza like it means anything."

"And if someone here wants to talk?" Pico asked.

Ansel looked up at the rooftops again. "Then we listen fast and leave faster."

* * *

The village was half empty in a way that had nothing to do with work or market day. Doors stood open on rooms stripped to concrete floors. A dog followed them for a block and then thought better of it, peeling away at an invisible boundary as though the air itself had been fenced. The laundry lines held clothes gone stiff with dust—shirts, a child's dress, trousers with the knees worn through—all of it hung weeks ago by people who had not come back for it.

Ilsa had worked refugee camps along three borders. She knew the taxonomy of emptied places: the ones evacuated in panic, the ones cleared by force, and the worst kind—the ones people left one family at a time, quietly, because staying had become a slow way of dying. This was the third kind. You could read it in the gardens. Some plots were gone to weed entirely, but others were still half-tended, beans climbing their strings with nobody to pick them, a row of maize hoed right up to the property line and then abandoned mid-row, as if the hoe had been set down one afternoon and never lifted again.

"There." Teo nodded toward the far end, where smoke rose thin and honest from a chimney behind the chapel.

The house was small, timber-framed, its wood silvered by weather. Fishing nets hung on one wall in careful repair, and a wooden rack held six fish split and drying, arranged with the precision of a man who still believed tomorrow would come. An old sat in the doorway on a stool, mending a net, his hands moving without his eyes' permission. His eyes were doing something else entirely. They were watching the street.

He saw them before they were ten meters out. Ilsa watched the calculation move across his face—not surprise, not fear exactly, but the weary triage of a man deciding how much of himself this visit was going to cost.

"Buenos días," Ansel called, hands open and visible. "We're running a fish survey on the river. Downstream cooperative. We got caught by the rain in the gorge and we're short on drinking water. We can pay."

The old man's hands kept mending. "There's no fish survey on this river," he said. "There hasn't been a fish survey since before you were born, probably." But his voice wasn't hostile. It was tired in the specific way of a man who has decided that being rude takes energy he needs elsewhere.

"The cooperative disagrees," Ansel said mildly. "They sent us with money and bad maps."

Something flickered then—the ghost of humor, quickly buried. The old man studied them one at a time. When his gaze reached Ilsa's medical bag, it stopped.

"You're not fishermen," he said.

"We're guides," Ilsa said. "River work. And I'm the medic."

His eyes came up to hers, and what she saw in them made her chest tighten: not hope—hope was too expensive—but the careful, rationed attention of a man checking whether a door he'd sealed years ago might open a crack.

"My hip is bad," he said. "And my wife's lungs fill with water at night. If your medicine is real, maybe we talk about fish."

* * *

His name was Eusebio Quispe, and he was seventy-one years old, and he had fished the Ceniza for fifty-three of those years. His wife, Rosa, lay inside on a bed near the window, propped on pillows, breathing in shallow wet sips. Ilsa listened to her chest and heard the crackle of fluid and something underneath it, older—a scarring she recognized from miners, from quarry workers, from anyone who had spent years breathing what the mountain exhaled.

"Dust," Rosa said, when Ilsa asked. "From the mine road. When the wind came down the valley it brought the road with it." She smiled, apologetic, as though her own lungs were a discourtesy. "It's better now. Fewer trucks."

Ilsa gave her a bronchodilator and antibiotics for the infection riding on top of everything else, and showed Eusebio how to use the inhaler, making him demonstrate twice. Then she treated the hip—an old fracture badly healed, arthritis laid over it like mortar—and while her hands worked, her ears did too.

Outside, she could hear Ansel and Teo talking with Eusebio's grandson, a boy of maybe nineteen named Marisol's-brother—no, Andrés—who had come around the corner of the house the moment the strangers appeared and now stood with his arms crossed in a posture copied from men much older than him. Pico hovered nearby, being seventeen and trying very hard to be invisible, which at seventeen is the most visible thing a person can be.

Ilsa kept her voice soft, her Spanish wrapped in the accent of the coast, unremarkable. "How long have you lived here?"

"Since the water took the other place." Rosa said it the way people say the names of the dead. "We lived at Quellomayo. On the river. There were forty families." Her eyes went to the window, to the fog. "This village was built for us. The company built it. They had a ceremony. There was a banner. *Un nuevo comienzo.*"

"A new beginning," Ilsa said.

"They gave every family a house with a number painted on it. Like the houses were also being resettled." Rosa laughed, and the laugh turned into coughing, and Ilsa held the cup while she rode it out. "Forty families came. You see how many roofs are smoking now."

"Where did they go?"

Rosa's hands folded together on the blanket. "Some went to the city. Some went to relatives. And some—" She stopped. Outside, Andrés's voice rose slightly, and fell, and Ilsa understood that the boy was listening for exactly this sentence. "Some just went. People don't ask here. Asking is how you get visits."

"Visits?"

"Men in trucks with no plates," Rosa said simply. "They drive through at night sometimes. Slow. So you hear them. They don't stop unless someone has asked a question." She looked at Ilsa then, directly, and her eyes were clear and terribly kind. "You should not have asked me a question, hija. But you fixed my lungs, so I will forgive it."

* * *

At midday they ate in Eusebio's kitchen—trout from the river, potatoes, a sauce that burned beautifully—and Ilsa catalogued the room the way she catalogued all rooms now: exits, sightlines, the shotgun above the door with dust on the stock (kept, not used), the photograph on the wall of a younger Eusebio holding an enormous fish, grinning, standing in water that Ilsa would have bet anything was the river at Quellomayo, before.

Ansel kept the conversation on fish. He was good at it—better than Ilsa had expected, asking about spawning runs and water temperatures with the fluency of a man who had actually guided these waters in another life. Eusebio answered in monosyllables at first, then in sentences, then, dangerously, in stories. Pico laughed at the right moments. Teo ate steadily and said little, but Ilsa caught him twice glancing at the window, at the road, counting the hours of daylight left.

It was during the second pot of tea that Eusebio said, without preamble, "You want to go upstream."

The kitchen went quiet. Even the fire seemed to hold its crackle.

"We want to find where the fish spawn," Ansel said.

Eusebio set down his cup with great care, as if it might detonate. "Fifty-three years," he said, "and no one has ever lied to me this politely in my own kitchen." He looked at Ansel for a long moment. "You have the face of a man who was here before. During. I don't know it, but I know the shape of it. Everyone who lost someone that year has the same face."

Ilsa felt Ansel go still beside her—not moving, but becoming motionless the way prey animals do, the whole body converting itself into silence.

"I'm not going to ask," Eusebio said. "Asking is expensive here. But I'll tell you what I told the last ones, because they paid me in medicine too, and because they're dead now, or gone, and dead men don't need my discretion." He pushed back his stool and crossed to a trunk in the corner, and from beneath folded clothing he drew a sheet of heavy paper, hand-drawn, dense with a fisherman's cartography—depths, eddies, gravel bars marked in a sure pencil line.

"The patrols," he said, laying it flat. "You've seen the drones. You'll see more. They fly the gorge at dawn and dusk, and there's a checkpoint below the falls where the canyon narrows—you cannot pass it by water, and the land route goes through their gate. But." His finger moved upstream along the penciled river. "Here. The old trail on the north wall. My father's trail, before there was a road. It climbs above the patrol road and drops back in past the falls. It is steep, and part of it fell in the quake two years ago, and if you break your leg on it, no one will come. But it is not watched, because the company believes no one remembers it exists."

Teo leaned over the map, and Ilsa watched his engineer's mind take possession of his face. "The switchbacks here—these are walkable?"

"With care. With light feet." Eusebio tapped a point beyond the falls, high on the page. "Above this, I cannot draw for you. Above this, I never went. The river comes out of the ice, and the ice comes out of a valley that used to belong to no one, and now belongs to them." He straightened, and his spine, bent with age, seemed for a moment to remember being straight. "Eleven families buried eleven of ours after the water came. Eleven. I count them every year on the day. My brother is one of them."

No one spoke. In the doorway, Andrés had gone white.

"So when you ask me why the village is empty," Eusebio continued, quieter now, "that is why. Not fear alone. Shame. We live on their charity, in their numbered houses, and every year fewer of us pretend it was an accident. The ones who couldn't pretend, left. The ones who stayed—" he spread his ruined hands, "we stay. We keep our heads down. We mend our nets. And once a decade, someone like you comes up the river, and we decide whether to help, knowing how it ended for the last ones."

"What happened to the last ones?" Pico asked. He was too young to leave the question unasked; Ilsa loved him a little for it and feared for him a lot.

Eusebio looked at the boy for a long time. "They went up in October," he said. "In March, the trucks came to the village and asked us if we knew them. We said no. It was true by then—we had learned to make it true." He rolled the map and held it against his chest. "Medicine first. Then the map. Those are my prices."

Ilsa went to the boat and came back with everything the kit could spare—antibiotics, the inhalers, analgesics, the good tape, a full course for Rosa's chest, a bottle of anti-inflammatories for the hip. She packed it into a plastic case and put it in Eusebio's hands, and showed him the labels one more time, and made him repeat the dosing schedule until she was satisfied. Rosa stood in the inner doorway watching, one hand pressed flat against the wall.

When Ilsa finished, Eusebio set the medicine down carefully and unrolled the map on the table, and with a fresh pencil he added the north-wall trail, the fallen section, the spring where the water ran clean, the notch in the ridge where the trail dropped back toward the river above the falls. His hand shook only a little.

"One more thing," he said, not looking up. "The men in the trucks. They pay some of the village boys to report who passes through. Not many. Two or three. They pay in fuel and phone credit, and the boys tell themselves it's nothing, that everyone does it, that if they didn't someone else would." His pencil paused. "That is how it works, señorita. Not with monsters. With neighbors. With small mercies and small betrayals until you cannot tell which one you performed today." He finished the last mark and straightened. "So. Tonight, after dark, someone will know strangers came to my house. I cannot stop that. What I can do is tell Andrés to say you were fish buyers from the coast, rude and boring, and gone by morning. Whether that story holds depends on how boring you were."

"We were extremely boring," Teo said solemnly, and Eusebio almost smiled.

* * *

They launched at dusk, in that blue hour when the fog came off the water and softened every edge. Ilsa took the bow of the second boat and looked back once. Eusebio stood on the bank with Rosa leaning on his arm, two small figures against the last light, and Andrés was already walking back up the street toward the numbered houses, already composing the boring truth he would sell tonight to whoever bought it.

She thought about the half-tended gardens. About the laundry stiffening on the line. About a man who had drawn them a map with the same hands that mended nets for a living, knowing the drawing might cost him everything, pricing it anyway in medicine because a fisherman understands that you trade what keeps people alive for what keeps people alive.

Complicity, she thought. That was the word the chapter of her own life had been written under—the malpractice scandal, the flight, the blacklist of her own that no newspaper had printed. She had told herself for three years that she'd run to protect patients from a system that would have eaten her. Sitting in Eusebio's kitchen, she had recognized the furniture of that story: the numbered houses, the banner reading *new beginning*, the careful not-asking. Survival dressed in respectable clothes. Everyone in this valley had signed something, one way or another. Most of them hadn't even been given a paper to sign.

"Hey." Pico, at the tiller, keeping his voice down. "You okay? You've got the look."

"What look?"

"The one Ansel gets. Like you're carrying something heavier than the bag says."

Isla turned forward. The gorge walls rose black on either side, and somewhere ahead, unseen, the falls thundered, and above the falls, invisible in the dark, a trail climbed a north wall that the company believed forgotten.

"Eusebio's brother was one of the eleven," she said.

Pico was quiet for a moment. The river talked underneath them, patient, going where it went.

"Then we'd better make the map worth it," he said.

Behind them, faint through the fog, an engine started on the village road. No headlights yet. Just the sound, idling, waiting—like the whole valley—for night to finish falling and decide what it was going to do about them.
