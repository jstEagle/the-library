## 13. Mara's Version

Mara saw the campfire before she saw the men around it—a smudge of orange in the black throat of the gorge, half a kilometer downstream, where no fire should be. She stood on the ledge above the river and let the cold work its way through her jacket, and for a long moment she simply watched, the way she'd watched a hundred ridgelines for a hundred clients who never knew how much of guiding was watching.

Ten years since she'd sat across a fire from Ansel Sandoval. Ten years since she'd stood up in a hearing room with fluorescent lights and said, *the evidence points to him*, in a voice that didn't shake because she'd trained it not to.

She descended the switchbacks without her headlamp, feeling for holds the way the old fisherman had taught her—no, that wasn't right, that had been Tomás, years ago, on a different river, laughing at her for trusting her eyes more than her boots. The memory arrived uninvited and she let it pass through her like weather.

The sentry heard her at thirty meters. Of course he did—the kid, Pico, moving out of the dark with a knife already open, all nerves and good instincts.

"Easy," she said. "I'm expected."

"You're not."

"Fair." She raised both hands, palms out, the universal grammar of *not tonight*. "Tell Ansel the diversion at the checkpoint worked, his people are through, and I'd like my tent back, because I lent it to a man who owes me money and I want it where I can see it."

Pico's eyes flicked toward the fire, then back to her. He was doing the arithmetic every young guide does: is this person a threat, an asset, or a story? She watched him decide she might be all three.

"He'll want to know how you got loose," Pico said.

"Then he'll ask." She smiled, and it cost her something. "Go on. I'll wait."

He went. She waited, standing in the cold, listening to the Ceniza grind itself against the canyon walls below. Upstream, somewhere past the icefall, was the valley. Somewhere under that water was everything they'd come to find, and everything she'd spent a decade pretending she hadn't lost.

Ansel came out of the firelight alone. That was deliberate, she knew—he'd have told the others to stay put, because Ansel Sandoval met his ghosts one at a time or not at all.

"Mara." Her name in his mouth sounded like a stone dropped down a well. No splash for a long time.

"Ansel."

"You were supposed to be detained."

"I was. For about six hours." She held up her wrists, showed him the raw skin where the zip ties had been. "Your checkpoint friends are thorough but not imaginative. And then a guard walked me out the back gate like we were going to get coffee."

"Which guard?"

"A kid named Delgado. Nineteen, maybe twenty. Nervous hands." She paused. "His father was a fisherman on the lower Ceniza. Died in the flood."

Something moved behind Ansel's eyes—not surprise, exactly. Recognition. The look of a man finding another stone turned over in a wall he thought he knew.

"They're everywhere," Mara said quietly. "The ones it happened to. They've been here the whole time, working for the people who did it, because there was nowhere else to work. You understand that better than anyone."

"Do I."

"Don't make me do this in front of your crew, Ansel. Don't make me say it loud enough for them to hear first."

He studied her for a long moment. Then he jerked his chin toward the rocks upstream, away from the fire, and led her along a game trail to a flat shelf above the water where the sound of the river would swallow whatever they said to each other. He sat on a boulder. He didn't offer her a seat. Some things you earned.

* * *

"Say it, then," he said.

She stayed standing. Her legs were still shaking from the escape and she refused to sit anyway; sitting would feel like surrender, and she had surrendered to this man once already, in a hearing room, in a voice that didn't shake.

"The night the dam failed," she began, and stopped, because the words had rusted shut. She pried them open one at a time. "You remember what they asked me. Not what happened—who did it. They weren't interested in the dam. They were interested in you."

"I remember what they printed."

"What I said," she corrected. "There's a difference. What they printed was a summary. What I said was—" She breathed. "I said the last confirmed sighting of you placed you at the intake structure forty minutes before the failure. I said your brother's logbook had your initials on three pages dated after you'd supposedly left the valley. I said when they pulled you out of the river you were carrying a company radio that was never issued to you."

"And?"

"And every word of it was true." Her voice cracked on the second word and she hated herself for it, hated the ten years of careful control splitting like river ice. "That's the part nobody understands, Ansel. That's the part *I* didn't understand for years. I didn't lie. God help me, I wish I had, because then I could take it back. Every fact I gave them was real. It was the shape of them that was wrong. Facts don't lie, but they can be arranged. Someone arranged them around you like a frame around a painting, and I—I stood in front of the room and described the frame and called it the picture."

The river roared below them. Ansel hadn't moved. His face in the starlight was the face of a man listening to a diagnosis.

"Who arranged them?" he asked.

"That's the question, isn't it. At the time I thought it was coincidence. Bad luck stacking up. You were there, you had access, you had debts—everyone knew about the debts—and your brother worked the intake shift. It fit. It *fit*, Ansel, and I was twenty-nine years old and I wanted the world to fit, because if it fit then it could be fixed, and if it could be fixed then maybe next time someone would listen to me early enough to save them." She wrapped her arms around herself. "Eleven people. I testified so it would mean something. So their deaths would buy a correction. Instead my testimony bought you a blacklist and bought the consortium a clean exit, and by the time I understood that, the paper trail had been dissolved into four shell companies and a holding firm in a country whose name I can't pronounce."

"So you doubted it."

"I *dismantled* it." She took a step closer. "Piece by piece, over ten years. The radio they said you carried—it was logged out to a survey team that afternoon, signed out by a man named Ochoa who doesn't exist in any personnel file before or after that month. Your initials in Tomás's logbook—Tomás initialed everything twice, once in pencil and once over it in pen, everyone on that crew knew it, and those pages only had pen. Someone added them after. And the sighting at the intake structure—" She stopped.

"Say it."

"The witness was a security contractor. Reyes's predecessor's man. He recanted privately, two years later, in a letter to the inquiry board. The letter was never entered into the record. I found it in 2019 in a mislabeled archive box in a regional courthouse that flooded the following spring. If I hadn't photocopied it that day, it would be underwater now with everything else."

Ansel's hands were flat on his knees. She could see the tendons standing out in them like bridge cables.

"Why are you telling me this now," he said, "and not ten years ago?"

"Because ten years ago I didn't know it yet. Because two years ago I still thought I was wrong about the details and right about the shape. And because eight months ago I found the surveys." She reached into her jacket and drew out a folded sheet, plastic-sleeved, soft at the creases from a decade of being taken out and looked at. "Teo will have told you about the hut documents by now. These are older. These are the originals the copies were made from. And Ansel—they show the overtopping was modeled. In advance. With margins. Somebody ran the numbers on how much water it would take, and how many hours of warning the downstream villages would lose, and they wrote it down in a report with a distribution list of eleven names."

"Eleven."

"Twelve, actually. One name was redacted from every copy. Black bar, professional job. Except one copy—the one in my hands—where the redaction slipped, and underneath the bar, faint as a watermark, you can read what it covered." She held the page out to him. "It covered your name, Ansel. The twelfth name on the distribution list. Whoever planned it wanted you to see the plan."

The wind came down the glacier and moved between them like a third person. Ansel took the page. He read it for a long time, or pretended to—she suspected he couldn't see a word of it, that his eyes had stopped working the way hers had stopped working the first time she'd understood what she was holding.

"They wanted me to know," he said finally. "And then they wanted me to forget."

"Yes."

"And I did." His voice was very quiet. "For ten years I built a life on top of the hole where that hour used to be, and I called the hole guilt, and I carried it, and I let it keep me moving because stopping meant looking down into it." He looked up at her. "You testified against me and spent ten years digging yourself out. I testified against myself without knowing it and spent ten years falling."

"Ansel—"

"No. Let me finish, because I've had this speech building for a decade and I only just got the last line." He stood. He was taller than she remembered, or the mountains had worn the rest of the world down around him. "I hated you. Properly, cleanly, the way you hate a thing that's simple. You were the reason I couldn't go home. And now you're standing here telling me you were the only person in that whole rotten machine who was trying to make the deaths mean something, and you did it with true facts in a false frame, and you've spent ten years paying for a mistake that was designed for you to make." He exhaled. "I don't know what to do with that. I've been carrying the hate so long I don't have anywhere to put my arms."

Mara laughed—a broken, wet sound, nothing like laughter, exactly matching the sound she'd made alone in a courthouse archive room in 2019.

"We're a matched set," she said. "You're the scapegoat and I'm the witness. Neither of us chose the role. Both of us performed it perfectly."

Below them, a light moved in the camp—Pico, checking the perimeter, young and dutiful and unaware that the two people on the shelf above him had just finished dismantling the story his whole expedition was built on.

"There's one more thing," Mara said. "And then I'll let you sleep, or whatever it is you do instead of sleeping."

"Say it."

"Delgado. The guard who walked me out. Before he opened the gate he asked me one question. He said: *the woman who testified, the one they blamed it on—is she coming to the source? Is she going to make them say the names?*" Mara's throat closed and she pushed through it. "They know, Ansel. The children of the dead, working the fences of the place that killed their parents—they know something's wrong, they've always known, and they're waiting for someone to stand up at the top of that valley and say it out loud. Delgado risked his life on a rumor that it might be you."

"It isn't me. It was never me. I'm the man they built the lie around."

"Then it's whoever's left." She stepped close enough that he had to look at her. "Ten years ago I pointed at you and the world agreed. Tomorrow we point at the truth and see who agrees. That's the only version of this that ends. You know it. I watched you know it, up on that ridge today, when you looked at the icefall like you'd seen it before."

Ansel was silent for a long time. When he spoke, his voice had changed—flattened, focused, the voice of a guide reading water he intended to run.

"The icefall," he said. "I need to tell you what I remember about the icefall. And Mara—you're not going to believe it, because I don't, and I was there."

Above the gorge, far upstream, the glacier caught the first gray suggestion of dawn and held it, burning faintly at the edges, like a horizon deciding whether to catch fire.

* * *

They came back into camp together, and the crew looked up—one firelit face after another, Teo with his bandaged arm cradled, Ilsa with a mug halfway to her lips, Pico standing too straight near the gear pile. Four people waiting to hear whether the woman who'd ruined their leader's life was friend or weather.

"She stays," Ansel said. "She knows things about the valley that none of us do, and she nearly died getting back to us. Give her the empty tent."

Nobody argued. But it was Pico who moved first—Pico who fetched her pack from where the guards had dumped it at the checkpoint, Pico who handed her a mug of the terrible boiled coffee without being asked, Pico who said, gruffly, in the tone of a boy defending a position he'd already decided on:

"Ilsa patched your wrists. She's good. Don't pick at it."

Mara took the mug. Her hands had finally stopped shaking.

"Thank you," she said, and meant the whole of it—the wrists, the mug, the tent, the strange grace of being allowed to sit inside a family she'd helped break.

Across the fire, Ansel was watching her over the flames. Not with hate anymore. Not yet with trust. With the expression of a man standing at the edge of a frozen lake, testing the ice with his weight, one slow step at a time.

She raised the mug to him, an inch, no more.

He didn't raise his back. But he didn't look away either, and after ten years, that was a country neither of them had ever been to, and it felt, improbably, like arriving.

Then Teo cleared his throat and held up a document in the firelight, and said, "Before anyone sleeps—there's something in these surveys you all need to see. Something about why *he* survived."
