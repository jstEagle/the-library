## 14. First Blood

The shot came out of the dark like a door slamming in another house.

Ilsa was kneeling over the cook fire with her back to the gorge wall when it happened—she heard the crack first, then the sound of the radio exploding off the rock where Pico had propped it, plastic and circuitry scattering into the embers. Then Teo made a noise she had never heard him make. Not a scream. Something shorter, more surprised, as if he'd been interrupted mid-sentence by the world.

"Down!" Ansel's voice, flat and absolute, from somewhere to her left.

She went down. Gravel bit her forearms. The fire hissed where shrapnel or stone had knocked dirt into it, and in the sudden half-dark she could see nothing but the orange afterimage of the flames burned onto her vision.

Second shot. Third. Both high, both wild—the sound of someone firing to pin them, not to kill them. Not yet.

"Teo." She said it once, quietly, the way you say a name across a ward at night. "Teo, talk to me."

"I'm—" His breath caught. "I'm hit. Left side. Ilsa, I'm hit."

She crawled toward his voice. Behind her, Ansel was moving low along the bank, and Pico had already killed the lantern with the practiced speed of a boy who'd grown up around men who shot at lights. The river filled the silence, loud and indifferent, the Ceniza talking to itself in the dark the way it had talked for ten thousand years while everything above it rotted.

Her hands found Teo's shoulder, then his jaw, then the wet heat spreading under his ribs. Too much wet. Far too much for a graze.

"Bullet's still in," he said through his teeth. He was trying to joke. She could hear the shape of the joke dying in his throat. "You always wanted an excuse to cut me open."

"Shut up." She got her headlamp on, thumbed it to red, and swept the beam down his flank. The entry wound sat just above his hip bone, ugly and small, the way they always were. Small going in. Everything else came later.

* * *

They dragged him forty meters upstream to a hollow beneath a leaning slab of granite—a place Ansel seemed to know without looking for it, the way he knew things about this river that he claimed not to know. Ilsa had stopped believing his claims of ignorance somewhere around day four. Nobody who didn't grow up on the Ceniza knew which side of every bend threw slack water in flood season.

The patrol didn't follow. That was the thing she kept turning over while she worked: they didn't follow. Three shots, one radio, one man bleeding, and then the night simply closed again like water over a dropped stone. Somewhere upstream a generator coughed to life and died. A drone passed overhead once, its running lights dark, visible only as a moving absence of stars.

"They're herding us," Ansel said. He crouched at the mouth of the hollow with his rifle across his knees—not aiming it, just holding it, the way a man holds a tool he resents needing. "They want us off the water. They're pushing us toward the east bank."

"You can't know that," Pico whispered.

"I can know it." Ansel's voice didn't rise. It never rose. That was the most frightening thing about him, Ilsa had long ago decided—that absolute level calm, the calm of a man who had already survived the worst night of his life and considered everything since negotiable. "Three rounds from a ridge position means they're not shooters, they're signallers. They've told whoever's above us exactly where we are. Now they wait and let the terrain do the work."

Ilsa wasn't listening anymore. She had opened Teo's shirt and was pressing gauze into the wound, and the blood came up between her fingers warm and shockingly fast, and all of her training narrowed to a single bright point: pressure, angle, count.

"Pulse is one-forty and thready," she said. "He's leaking fast. I need light. Real light."

"No real light," Ansel said.

"Then he dies in the dark, and you can explain to his mother why we respected tactical discipline at his funeral."

A pause. Then Ansel turned away from the hollow's mouth and nodded once, and Pico unclipped the big lamp and set it behind her shoulder, angled so the spill fell forward onto her hands and nowhere past the granite slab. It wasn't much. It was enough. It would have to be enough; that was the whole of field medicine, the whole of her life since San Rafael, since the inquiry, since the word *negligent* had been stapled to her name by men who had never held a clamp in their lives.

*Local anesthetic won't reach deep enough,* she thought, cataloguing. *He'll feel the probe. He'll feel the extraction. Lidocaine, ketamine, what's left of the ketamine—quarter dose, he's ninety kilos, hypovolemic, ketamine keeps the pressure up better than anything else I've got.*

"Teo." She took his face in both hands and made him look at her. His skin had gone the color of the river fog. "Listen to me. You're going to bleed less if your heart beats slower, and your heart will beat slower if you stop performing for the audience. So no jokes. No engineering commentary. Just breathe. In for four. Out for six. Can you do that?"

"That's—" breath, "—a very condescending bedside manner."

"In for four."

He did it. God help him, he did it, because underneath the sarcasm Teo was the most obedient patient she'd ever had, the kind of man who read the manual before assembling furniture and followed it even when the drawer went in backwards. She pushed the ketamine and watched his eyes lose their fear before they lost their focus, and she thought, as she always thought, that there should be a word for that moment—the exact second when terror leaves a face and trust arrives in its place. There wasn't one. Medicine was full of words for failure and almost empty of words for grace.

* * *

The bullet sat against his iliac crest, deflected, having spent its energy on the radio's battery pack in his jacket pocket before entering shallow. She found that out with two fingers and a steel probe, working by headlamp while Ansel knelt opposite her and pinned Teo's shoulders—not because the ketamine wasn't holding, but because Ilsa had asked him to, and because some part of her understood that a body needed weight on it the way a map needs stones at the corners.

"Forceps," she said, and Pico put them in her hand before she finished the word. The kid had appointed himself her scrub nurse without being asked, laying out instruments on the folded tarp in the order she'd taught him three nights ago around a fire, never guessing—or maybe guessing perfectly, being Pico—that the lesson would matter this soon.

The extraction took eleven minutes. She knew because she counted, the way she counted everything now, seconds and milliliters and heartbeats, arithmetic as armor. The bullet came out deformed into a gray mushroom, and she dropped it into a specimen jar without letting herself think about whose finger had pulled the trigger, because that thought could come later, in the daylight, where it couldn't shake her hands.

Then irrigation, then sutures, deep layer and superficial, and through all of it Teo murmured fragments of nonsense about load-bearing tolerances and the tensile strength of river gravel, and Ansel held him down with those enormous steady hands and said nothing at all, and Ilsa understood that the silence between the two men was its own kind of conversation—one she wasn't invited into and didn't need to be.

When it was done, she sat back on her heels and discovered her own hands were shaking, fine and fast, the delayed tremor arriving right on schedule like a train.

"Vitals?" Ansel asked quietly.

"Holding. He lost maybe eight hundred CCs. He needs fluids, warmth, and about a week of bed rest, and he'll get none of those things, so he'll get my worst temper instead." She stripped her gloves. "He lives. That part's settled. The rest is negotiation."

* * *

Pico took first watch, though watch was a generous word for a boy sitting in the black with a borrowed rifle and a heartbeat audible at ten paces. Ilsa checked Teo's dressing twice more than necessary, then a third time because checking was easier than thinking, and finally Ansel appeared beside her at the hollow's edge with two cups of something hot and terrible from the ration packs.

She drank it. It tasted like burnt hay and salt.

"Say it," he said.

"Say what?"

"Whatever you've been chewing on since the shooting stopped. You get a line between your eyebrows. It's been there for an hour."

She almost laughed. Almost. What came out instead was quieter and worse.

"They shot the radio first, Ansel."

He waited. He was good at waiting; it was probably how he'd survived everything.

"The radio. Not us. Their first round goes through our comms—our only link to Mara, to the checkpoint diversion, to anyone—and their next two go high and wide. If they wanted us dead, Teo's dead, I'm dead, Pico's dead, and it takes nine seconds." She turned the cup in her hands. "They're not hunting us. They're *managing* us. Cutting our line to the outside and steering us inland like you said. And that means—"

"It means they don't care if we see something," Ansel said. "As long as we can't tell anyone."

The sentence landed in the hollow between them and lay there.

Ilsa looked downstream, back the way they'd come, toward the resettlement village with its half-empty houses and its old fisherman who had drawn them a map with hands that shook until she gave him antibiotics for his granddaughter. She looked upstream, toward the black throat of the gorge and whatever waited above the treeline. And she thought about the tripwire boy, the one with the lacerated shin she'd sutured in his mother's kitchen while his family stood in the doorway like people posing for a photograph they hadn't agreed to. The patrols hadn't guarded the village. They'd patrolled *through* it, casually, routinely, the way a farmer walks his fence line.

Fences keep things out, she'd thought then.

But that was wrong. She saw it now, cold and complete, the way you see the answer to a differential diagnosis at three in the morning. Fences also keep things in. And some fences are built not around what's precious, but around what's being destroyed—so that no one watches it happen.

"They're not guarding the valley," she said slowly. "Ten years of patrols, tripwires, drones, contractors on a dissolved company's payroll—they're not protecting anything up there. They're guarding a demolition site. They're making sure nobody watches them erase it. The dam ruins, the tailings, whatever's buried under the floodplain—" Her voice steadied as it went, the way it always did when the clinical picture resolved. "Every person they've hurt, every fence they've strung, it's all one job. Covering the grave while they dig it deeper."

Ansel was very still. In the red glow of the distant lamp she could see only the outline of him, the set of his shoulders, and she realized she was watching a man hear his own suspicion confirmed by someone else's mouth—which was somehow heavier than suspecting it alone.

"My brother wrote me a letter," he said, "three weeks before the flood. Last one I ever got. He said the water tasted sweet near the source and wrong near the works. He said somebody should write down what the river knows." A long breath. "I thought it was Tomás being Tomás. Poetic. He was always poetic."

"And now?"

"Now I think he was filing a report." Ansel looked upstream, into the dark. "And I think the people who killed him have spent ten years making sure no one ever reads it."

* * *

Toward dawn, Teo surfaced from the ketamine haze enough to complain about the quality of his nursing, which was how Ilsa knew he'd live. She let him complain. Complaint was a vital sign.

Pico came to relieve her at the edge of sleep and found her cleaning instruments with a thoroughness that bordered on ritual.

"He's going to be okay," Pico said. It wasn't quite a question.

"He's going to be okay." She snapped the tray shut. "And Pico—when we move, you carry the documents. Not the tent, not the food. The documents. Whatever happens, whatever they throw at us, those papers leave this gorge even if we don't."

The boy looked at her, and she watched him understand—not the logistics, the meaning. Terror had been living in all of them since the shots, coiled under the sternum. But in Pico's face it did something she recognized from her own mirror, from the worst months after San Rafael: it sharpened. Fear, given a purpose, stops being weather and becomes fuel.

"Okay," Pico said simply.

Upstream, faint as a lie, the generators started again—and this time they didn't stop.
