## 15. The Missing Hour

The cold woke him before the dreams did.

That was the way of it at five thousand meters. The body surrendered its warmth to the thin air hour by hour, and somewhere past midnight the sleeping bag became a rumor and the tent a shell of nylon doing nothing against a wind that came off the glacier like something with intent. Ansel had slept in worse places—caves, truck beds, a drainage culvert in the Atacama during the year he didn't talk about—but this cold was different. This cold was patient.

He lay still and listened to the camp breathe. Teo's snore, rhythmic as a pump. Ilsa's silence, which was its own kind of sound; she slept the way she did everything else, efficiently, without wasted motion. Pico, on his third night at altitude, breathing in short shallow pulls, the boy's lungs still learning what thin air meant. And beyond the nylon, the Ceniza itself, muffled under new ice, muttering in its sleep.

*The river remembers where it's going,* Tomás used to say. *It's people who forget.*

Ansel closed his eyes again because there was nothing else to do. The headache sat behind them like a fist, the dull vise of altitude that no amount of water or coca tea fully loosened. He'd dosed Ilsa's acetazolamide down to half tablets for himself, hoarding the rest for the others, though he'd never told her that. She would have argued. She was good at arguing. It was one of the things he'd come to rely on.

Sleep took him the way it always did now—not gently, but like a hand closing over his face from behind.

---

The flashlight came first.

Not light exactly—the memory of it, the white punch of it through water, through darkness, through whatever he had been inside of in that final hour. A beam swinging across his vision in slow arcs, and beneath it the sound of water that wasn't water anymore but something heavier, something carrying silt and diesel and the taste of the mountain's own ground-up bones.

*Sign it.*

A voice. Calm. Almost gentle, the way men are gentle when they are certain of their power. Not shouting. That was the detail that made his stomach turn even inside the dream—that the voice never needed to shout.

*Sign it, and you can go home.*

Home. The word opened like a trapdoor. He was standing somewhere dry, which was wrong, because the last thing he remembered of that night was wet—being wet, being carried by wet, being *inside* wet—and here his boots were on concrete, or gravel, or the steel deck of something, and there were figures around him wearing shapes without faces, and one of them held a clipboard, and the flashlight was propped against a rock so both its hands were free.

His hands wouldn't work right. They were someone else's hands, heavy and slow, and there was a pen in them, or maybe just a thumb waiting for ink, and the paper made a sound when it turned over like a wing beating once.

*Your brother is already accounted for.*

The words went through him like a spike of ice through the roof of his mouth.

And then—Tomás. Not dead, not drowned, not *accounted for*, but alive in the way the dream allowed, alive and furious, his voice coming from everywhere and nowhere, from upstream, from under the water, from ten years away and three feet behind:

*"Ansel! Don't—"* 

The shout tore the dark open. *"Don't sign anything! ANSEL!"*

He tried to turn. In the dream his body moved like it was packed in sand, like the flood had followed him onto dry land and filled his limbs with riverbed. The flashlight beam swung toward the voice—

—and found nothing. Empty water. Empty dark. The beam swept left, swept right, and the calm voice said, patiently, *he can't help you now*, and the paper rustled again, and Ansel's own hand began to move toward it, dragging him along like a man wading into current, and he was screaming inside a throat that wouldn't open, screaming *no, no, Tomás, wait, wait for me, I'll find you, I'm coming, I'm coming upstream—*

---

He woke screaming out loud.

The sound ripped out of him raw and enormous, bouncing off the tent walls, and for two full seconds he didn't know where he was—didn't know the decade, the country, the name he went by. He knew only the flashlight and the paper and the voice saying *sign it*, and his hands were clawing at the sleeping bag as if it were the thing holding him under.

Then the zipper tore open and Ilsa was there, headlamp blazing straight into his eyes, and the white punch of it dropped him straight back into the dream for one horrible looping instant—*the flashlight, the flashlight*—before her hands found his shoulders and her voice cut through, flat and clinical and real.

"Ansel. Ansel. You're in the tent. You're at Camp Two. Look at me."

He looked at her. It took effort, like lifting something. Her face swam into focus: sharp, sleep-creased, unafraid. Behind her shoulder Pico's silhouette appeared in the gap of the flap, wide-eyed, half-dressed, a knife already in his hand—which told Ansel something about the boy's nights, about what he thought the world was made of, and something twisted in Ansel's chest that wasn't entirely the altitude.

"I'm fine," he said. His voice came out scraped. "Bad dream."

"You were screaming my name," Ilsa said. "You don't scream names in bad dreams. You scream them in night terrors." She kept her hands on his shoulders a moment longer, feeling, he realized, his pulse. Counting it. Of course she was. "Heart rate's coming down. Breathe slower than you want to."

Teo's head appeared beside hers, hair flattened on one side, glasses missing, squinting like a mole dragged into daylight. "Sirena," he mumbled. "You sounded like the emergency beacon."

"Go back to sleep."

"Nobody sleeps after that." But he retreated anyway, and Ansel heard him rummaging, and a moment later a thermos lid of tea appeared in front of him, steam curling in the headlamp beam. Teo's small ceremonies. The engineer believed that any problem could be reduced to something solvable if you first put something warm in the person's hands.

Ansel drank. The tea was terrible—overboiled, bitter, faintly of the fuel they melted snow with—and it was the best thing he had ever tasted, because it was *here*, it was *now*, it had no flashlight in it.

Pico hadn't moved. He stood at the edge of the lamplight with the knife still loose in his grip, watching Ansel with an expression the boy probably thought was neutral. It wasn't. It was the look of someone seeing a load-bearing wall crack.

"It's nothing," Ansel said to him. "Altitude does this. Everyone gets the dreams."

"That's true," Ilsa said, too quickly, too smoothly, and Ansel understood she was lying for him, and hated how easily she did it, and was grateful anyway.

Pico looked between them, filed something away behind those young eyes, and zipped himself back into the dark.

Ilsa stayed. She killed the headlamp so they sat in blackness and cold, which was a kindness—he couldn't be watched anymore—and after a while she said, quietly, "Third time this week."

"You've been counting."

"I count everything. It's the job." A pause. The glacier ticked and groaned outside, shifting its weight. "Do you want to tell me what's in them?"

"No."

"Do you want to tell me why we're really going up this valley?"

The question landed harder than the nightmare had. Ansel turned the thermos lid in his hands, feeling the heat die out of it. Above them, through the fabric, he could feel the direction of the source the way you feel a draft—a pressure, a pull, a place the whole expedition leaned toward whether anyone named it or not.

"Mara's paying us," he said.

"Mara's paying us triple for a job she won't describe, to a site that's been sealed for ten years, guarded by men with guns, and you flinch every time someone says the word *Ceniza*." Ilsa's voice stayed level, but there was iron under it. "I patch people up for a living, Ansel. I know what an old wound looks like when it's been reopened. I've been looking at yours since the bridge job."

He said nothing. The wind pressed against the tent like a palm laid flat.

"Fine," she said at last. "Keep it. But hear me: whatever happened to you up here—or downstream, wherever it was—it's not done with you. Dreams aren't ambushes, Ansel. They're messengers. They keep coming back until you read the letter." She rose, unzipped out into the cold, and paused. "Get some sleep. Real sleep. Tomorrow we portage."

The flap fell shut. *They keep coming back until you read the letter.* He sat alone in the dark with the dead tea and thought: *she has no idea how right she is.*

---

He didn't sleep. He tried, and the trying itself became its own country—a gray borderland where he lay awake enough to know he was lying in a tent and asleep enough for fragments to leak through anyway, and the fragments were worse for the knowing.

The flashlight again. Always the flashlight first, as if it were the key the memory turned on. Then the smell—diesel and wet rock and something chemical underneath, sweetish, wrong, a smell he hadn't been able to name for ten years and now, horribly, was beginning to: tailings. Processed ore. The mountain's guts, spilled.

Then faces assembling out of the dark the way faces do in the half-sleep, built from everyone he'd ever known. The calm voice wore Reyes's patience and Mara's certainty and, once, appallingly, his own. *Sign it.* The paper turned its single wingbeat. And Tomás—

Tomás shouting. That was the part he couldn't bear, the part that had woken him, because in the dream Tomás wasn't dead. In the dream Tomás was *upstream*. Shouting down through the dark: *don't sign it, Ansel, whatever they tell you, whatever they show you, don't—* and the sentence never finished. Ten years of dreams and the sentence had never once finished. As if the memory itself had been amputated at the exact point where the truth lived, cauterized clean, the way a company seals a mine.

*What did he say?*

He asked it aloud, into the empty tent, his breath smoking. *What did you say, Tomás? Finish the sentence.*

The glacier answered with a long cracking report, somewhere high on the icefall, ice calving in the dark. Like a door slamming. Like a vault locking.

Ansel lay back down and stared at the ceiling of nylon and did the arithmetic he'd been avoiding since Mara's photograph hit his table. Eleven dead. One survivor pulled from the water where no swimmer could have swum—Ilsa had said it once, casually, weeks ago, and he'd buried it, and now it surfaced again like a body coming up out of a reservoir when the level drops. *Downstream of where anyone could swim.* Someone had put him there. Someone had wanted him alive. And the calm voice, the paper, the thumbprint he could almost feel on his skin like a brand—

His blacklist wasn't a verdict. It was a receipt.

The thought arrived whole and cold and it changed the shape of everything, the way finding out a friend's death was murder changes the shape of grief. For ten years he had carried his disgrace like ballast, like penance, like the one honest thing he owned: *I was there, and it was partly my fault, and I don't remember how.* He had built a life on that foundation—built the crew, built the rule of never stopping, built the man who moves before anyone can look too closely. And if the foundation was false—if the guilt was manufactured, installed in him like a fixture—

Then who was he? What was left of the man when you took the guilt out?

He didn't know. And not-knowing, at five thousand meters, in the dark, with a demolition crew somewhere above them erasing the only witnesses, felt less like freedom than like free fall.

---

Dawn came gray and slow, the light rising through the tent fabric like development fluid bringing up a photograph. Ansel was already packed. He'd been packed for an hour, hands moving through the rituals—straps, buckles, load distribution—because rituals were what you did instead of thinking.

Outside, the world was white and brutal and beautiful, the way it always was up here, and he hated it a little less for the beauty. The icefall above camp caught the first sun and burned orange, then gold, and for a moment the whole upper mountain looked like the horizon on fire, and he had to look away from it.

Ilsa handed him tea without a word. Pico was checking the portage frames, cinching straps with quick competent hands, glancing over every few seconds and pretending not to. Teo stood at the edge of camp with binoculars, scanning the route ahead, and after a while he lowered them and frowned.

"Ansel. Come look at this."

Ansel crossed the moraine. Teo passed him the glasses and pointed up-valley, past the icefall, to where the gorge narrowed toward the high country—and there, tiny at this distance, unmistakable in the clean snow: tracks. Vehicle tracks. Fresh, cutting across terrain no vehicle should cross, running not toward the valley but *out* of it, in a hurry, in numbers.

"They're moving equipment," Teo said quietly. "Or moving evidence. Either way, they're ahead of schedule."

Ansel lowered the glasses. Somewhere above, under all that ice, his brother had shouted a sentence that had never finished. Somewhere above, men with a calm voice and a clipboard were finishing it for him.

"Then we move faster," Ansel said. "Break camp. We portage today."

And as the crew scattered to their loads, he stood a moment longer facing up-valley, into the wind, and said—to Tomás, to the glacier, to whoever was keeping the other end of that unfinished sentence—

*I'm coming. Tell me the rest.*
