## 16. Portage Through Hell

The river told them before anyone said a word.

Pico heard it first, actually—a change in the sound, the way the Ceniza's constant murmur deepened into something with bones in it. He was on rope detail that morning, coiling lines in the pre-dawn gray, and he stopped mid-coil because the noise coming up the gorge had dropped half an octave, like a voice clearing its throat before it shouted.

By full light they stood at the canyon mouth and understood. The river they'd been running for six days—violent but negotiable, a fight you could win—funneled here into a slot barely twenty meters wide between walls of black slate that rose sheer for two hundred. The water inside wasn't rapids. It was a blender. White chaos churned between unseen boulders, and the spray came out of the slot in gusts like breath from a wound.

"No," Teo said simply. He'd walked to the edge, looked down the throat of it, and walked back. "No boats. Not through that."

"Nobody's arguing," Ansel said.

Pico watched him study the canyon the way other men read documents—slowly, twice, looking for what wasn't written. Ansel had been quiet since the drone, quieter since the rapid that had nearly taken Pico's boat, and there was something new in his face that Pico couldn't name yet. Like a man listening to a radio frequency only he could receive.

"The old fisherman's map," Ilsa said. She had it out, the plastic sleeve fogging at her breath. "There's a trail. High line, left side. He called it *la escalera del diablo*—the devil's staircase. Said the miners used it before the valley closed."

"Miners' trails are goat logic," Teo muttered. "Goats don't carry three hundred kilos of gear."

"Then we become goats," Mara said.

That got the first almost-smile out of Ansel in two days. Almost. "Five days," he said, doing the arithmetic aloud, eyes moving across the scree fields climbing toward the ridgeline. "Maybe six if weather turns. We cache the boats, strip everything to essentials, and haul. Double carries. Nobody moves alone, nobody moves off-rope above the exposure." He turned, and his gaze snagged on Pico for half a second longer than necessary—the look of a man checking that something fragile was still intact—and then away. "Pico. You're on load distribution with Teo. Break the weight down so no single carry breaks a person."

"Yes, jefe."

"And Pico." Ansel's voice did the thing it did when he was about to say something he'd rehearsed. "The rapid. Two days ago. That wasn't your fault. The eddy moved."

"I know, jefe."

"Do you?" Ansel held his eyes. "Because I've carried blame that wasn't mine for ten years, and I'm telling you the current is a liar. Don't let it write your story."

Pico nodded, throat too tight for words, and Ansel clapped him once on the shoulder—brief, hard, gone in a second—and turned back to the canyon as if the conversation had never happened. But Pico carried the words with him all day, all week, all five days, the way you carry a stone in your pocket until it wears smooth.

* * *

The first day broke them in gently, which is to say it only made them bleed a little.

The trail existed, technically. A shelf cut into the canyon wall, wide enough for one loaded person, crumbling at the edges where a decade of freeze-thaw had been quietly eating it. Below them the river roared through its slot, loud enough that they stopped trying to talk by noon and developed a vocabulary of gestures: fist up for stop, flat palm for rest, two fingers pointed at eyes then ahead for *look at this*.

Pico learned the loads by heart. The satellite uplink equipment, broken into four sealed cases—Teo's obsession, guarded like relics. The medical kit, split into two packs so no single fall could take all of it. Rope, hardware, food compressed into bricks. His own body, which by evening felt like something he was renting rather than owning.

He also learned the crew.

Teo climbed like a machine with a grudge against gravity, precise and joyless, counting steps under his breath. Ilsa climbed like a nurse walking a ward—checking on everyone else's pace before admitting her own shoulders were screaming. Mara climbed like she was escaping something, which Pico supposed she was, though from what, none of them knew yet. And Ansel—

Ansel climbed like the mountain owed him money.

He took point every day, always, reading the rock three moves ahead, and where the trail died he built it again with cairns and cut steps, working with a fury that had nothing to do with efficiency. On the second day Pico finally understood: Ansel wasn't hauling gear up a canyon. He was hauling himself somewhere, and the gear was just ballast.

They camped that night on a ledge wide enough for four tents if the tents hated each other. The wind came up the gorge carrying river-spray that froze on the flysheets, and by morning everything wore a skin of ice that had to be cracked off like eggshell.

Day three, the trail ended.

Not faded—ended. A slope of scree the size of a cathedral rose where the map promised path, thousands of loose plates of slate stacked at the angle of repose, each one waiting for an excuse to move. Above it, the ridge. Below it, two hundred meters of nothing ending in white noise.

"We traverse," Ansel said. "Rope teams of two. Weight high on the shoulder, small steps, and if the slope starts talking—" he kicked a plate loose and they all listened to it rattle down, down, down into the roar, "—you don't wait to see what it's saying."

They crossed in pairs all morning. Pico went with Teo, hauling the heaviest case, and the work was simple and total: step, settle, breathe, step. The scree shifted underfoot like a living thing deciding whether to tolerate them. Twice the whole slope groaned, a long mineral moan, and both times they froze until it decided to keep their secret.

At midday, crossing with the last load, Pico saw it happen.

He was thirty meters behind Ansel, who had insisted on sweeping the final carry himself after everyone else was across. Ansel was moving fast—too fast, Pico thought, the way he'd been moving too fast since the checkpoint rumor, since whatever lived in his head had started whispering. Ansel's boots hit a plate the size of a door, and the plate said *no*.

It sheared inward. The whole section of slope beneath him unlocked—not a slide, a *collapse*, hundreds of plates letting go at once like a deck of cards fanned open—and Ansel went with them, arms wheeling, dropping toward the edge where the scree ended and the air began.

Pico didn't decide anything. Later he would search his memory for the decision and find it wasn't there, just motion, just his legs already driving uphill while everyone else's brains were still processing the sound. He reached the moving ground as it moved, dropped flat, and slammed his axe-pick into solid rock above the failure line. The rope snapped taut—he'd clipped the anchor without remembering clipping it, some lesson from a hundred drills surfacing like a diver breaking water—and the jolt nearly tore his shoulder from its socket.

Below him, Ansel slid. Ten meters, fifteen, boots plowing slate, hands finding nothing, the edge rushing up at him with the patience of geology.

"PICO—" Ansel's voice, and it wasn't an order. It was the first time Pico had ever heard anything in it except command.

The rope held. Pico held. His pick held, his boots braced against a boulder that bit through his trousers into his knees, and the whole system—boy, steel, mountain—held while Ansel swung on the line like a pendulum winding down, slate rattling past him into the abyss, until the movement stopped and he hung there over the drop, breathing.

Ilsa reached them first, then Teo, then Mara, hands hooking harnesses, hauling Ansel up onto the dead-stable ground above the scar. He came up over the lip coughing dust, and for a moment he knelt there on all fours, head down, a big man reduced to his smallest posture.

Pico stayed where he was, still anchored, still holding, because his hands wouldn't open. They'd locked around the rope and forgotten how to be hands.

Ansel crawled to him. Actually crawled, dust streaming off him, and took Pico's wrists and worked the fingers loose one at a time, gentle as a man defusing something.

"Hey. Hey. Look at me." Pico looked. Ansel's face was gray with dust and streaked with blood where a stone had opened his cheekbone, and his eyes were wet, and he didn't hide it. "You didn't think. You *moved*. Do you know how rare that is? How many people stand there and die politely?"

"You would've done it for me," Pico managed. His voice came out wrecked. "In the rapid. You already did."

"That was skill." Ansel shook his head slowly. "This was love. Different animal. Heavier." He squeezed Pico's freed hands once, hard, and stood, and became the guide again in the space of a breath—but not entirely, not all the way, something having permanently shifted behind his eyes. "Debt reversed," he said. "Officially. I hate owing."

Laughter came out of the crew then, shaky and real, the kind that only exists on the far side of almost. Even Teo laughed, one syllable, immediately denying it.

But that night, on watch together while the others slept, Ansel sat beside Pico at the fire and said, quietly, so the words wouldn't wake anyone: "Ten years ago someone pulled me out of a river and I've spent every day since wondering why. Why me. Why bother." He fed a stick to the flames. "Today I remembered what it feels like from the other side. Being the one who pulls." He looked at Pico, and the firelight did something complicated in his face. "Whatever we find up there—whatever happened to me, whatever happened to my brother—I want you to know the pulling was the truest thing I've ever been part of. Everything else is weather."

Pico didn't fully understand. He nodded anyway, because some things you agree to before you understand them, and trust is just understanding arriving late.

* * *

Days four and five blurred into pure labor. The ridge gained on them meter by meter, the canyon's roar fading below into a distant hiss, the air thinning until every step cost double. They cached loads in hollows marked with wands, doubled back, hauled again. Blisters opened and were taped. Shoulders rawed through jackets. Ilsa dosed them all with anti-inflammatories and pretended her own hands weren't shaking. Mara sang once—low, tuneless, an old mining song about women waiting at pit heads—and nobody asked her to stop, and nobody asked her what it meant, and she never sang again.

On the fifth evening they crested the ridge, and the wind hit them like a wall of glass, and below—

Below, the gorge opened into the upper valley, and the Ceniza ran free again, silver-gray with glacier milk, braiding across a floodplain under walls of ice. And beyond it, far upstream, half-hidden in the haze of its own spray: the dam ruin. A pale scar across the river's throat, broken in the middle like a snapped bone.

Nobody spoke. There was nothing to say that the view wasn't already saying louder.

Ansel stood longest at the edge. Pico watched him, and watched his jaw work, and watched him take one breath that seemed to come from somewhere deeper than lungs.

"Tomorrow," Ansel said finally. "We cross the floodplain at night. And Pico—" he turned, and in the last light his face looked carved, older than his years, younger than his scars, "—stay close to me tomorrow. Whatever's down there, whatever I become when I see it up close... stay close."

"I will, jefe."

And far downstream, faint as a memory, Pico thought he heard engines—patrol engines, hunting the darkening valley for people who refused to stop moving.
