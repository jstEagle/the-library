## 18. Ghosts in the Fog

The fog came off the river at dusk like a living thing, pouring over the canyon rim in slow gray tongues until the checkpoint's floodlights were nothing but smeared yellow wounds in the dark.

Ansel lay on his belly at the lip of the portage trail, a hundred meters above the water, and watched the lights blur. Below, the Ceniza narrowed to a chute barely wider than a truck bed, and on the gravel bar beside it the company had built its fortress: sandbag emplacements, a chain-link gate, two prefabricated barracks, a watchtower with a mounted lamp that swept the river in slow, patient arcs. Four men visible. Probably eight. Reyes's people didn't staff their outposts lightly.

"Tell me again why we're not going around," Pico whispered beside him. The kid's breath fogged and vanished.

"Because there is no around." Ansel pointed upstream, where the gorge walls pinched together into a canyon of polished black stone. "That's the Jaws. Class six, maybe worse. The river drops forty meters in three hundred. Nobody runs it. Nobody portages it on the south side—the wall's vertical for two kilometers. The only way past is this trail, right under their guns, and the only time it's passable is when they can't see it."

"And if they can see us?"

"Then we learn to swim very fast in very bad water."

Ilsa crouched behind them with the med kit strapped to her back like a shell. "Charming. Remind me why I left a warm clinic for this."

"Because the clinic had walls," Ansel said, "and walls have doors."

She didn't laugh. None of them did. Since Teo's blood had dried on the deck of the lead boat, humor had gone out of the crew like heat out of a dead engine.

They waited for Mara.

* * *

She'd left an hour before dark, walking down the portage trail with her hands visible and her old company credentials in her jacket pocket—the ones she'd told them about around the fire two nights ago, the ones that had gotten her arrested at this same checkpoint three years ago and released with an apology and a meal. *Someone up the chain wants me alive,* she'd said. *I intend to be expensive to kill.* Her plan was simple and insane: walk up to the gate, demand to speak to whoever was in charge, make enough noise about her "survey contract" and her lawyers that every light and every ear on that gravel bar turned toward her. Give the fog time to thicken. Give the others time to move.

"Thirty minutes," she'd said, shouldering her pack. "If I'm not making noise by then, assume they took the bait anyway and I'm just enjoying the conversation."

Ansel had almost told her not to go. He'd opened his mouth to say it—*we find another way, we turn back, we do anything*—and then he'd seen her face. Mara Voss, who had testified against him in a courtroom smelling of mildew and coffee, who had spent ten years carrying the same doubt he carried, was looking at him with an expression he recognized because he wore it himself every day: the face of someone who had already done the arithmetic and didn't want to be talked out of the answer.

"Thirty minutes," he'd said instead.

Now he checked his watch by feel, the lume dots on the dial glowing faint green. Twenty-two minutes since she'd disappeared down the trail.

The first sound reached them at twenty-six: her voice, carrying up the canyon with the strange clarity fog gives to sound, pitched high and indignant, the voice of a woman who had been wronged by bureaucracy and intended to document every instance. A man's voice answered, flat and bored. A door banged. A radio crackled. The watchtower's lamp stopped its sweep and held steady on the gate, on her, on the small theater she was staging at the mouth of their fortress.

"Now," Ansel breathed.

They moved the way he'd taught them, the way he'd taught a hundred clients on a hundred ridges: weight on the outside foot, steps placed not taken, packs cinched silent, no light, no voice. The portage trail was a miner's path from another century, cut into the north wall of the gorge, in places barely wide enough for the gear crates. They'd broken the loads down at dusk—boats lashed into A-frame bundles, the document tubes from the survey hut sealed in dry bags and distributed across three packs so no single capture could take them all. Teo should have been carrying the heaviest bundle. Teo was back at the high camp with fourteen stitches in his shoulder and a fever Ilsa didn't like, and the absence of him made every load heavier than its weight.

Pico went first, because the kid had the best night eyes and no fear yet—no, Ansel corrected himself, watching the boy edge along a ledge with the river roaring blind below, that wasn't true anymore. Pico had fear now. He'd earned it on the scree above the gorge, when the rockfall came down and he'd thrown himself sideways and hauled Ansel back by the strap of his pack with hands that shook for an hour afterward. He carried his fear now the way the old climbers carried theirs: quietly, and forward.

Ilsa came second, sure-footed and grim. Ansel took the rear, which was where he always put himself, and told himself it was tactics and not penance.

Below them, Mara's voice rose and fell. She was quoting contract clauses now. Somewhere a man laughed, and she let him, let him feel clever, and Ansel thought: *she's good at this, she's better than she needs to be, and that means she's done it before, more than once, and every time she walked into a place like this she was alone.*

The trail bent inland around a rib of rock, and for a few minutes the checkpoint vanished behind them, and the fog closed in until Ansel's world was three meters wide: stone, mist, the wet gleam of the trail, Pico's pale jacket ahead. The river's roar filled everything. It was the same roar, he thought, the same water, that had been in his ears the night Tomás died. Ten years, and the sound still reached into his chest and squeezed.

He was so busy hating the river that he almost missed it.

* * *

It happened the way these things happen—not gradually, but all at once, the way a trap springs.

The trail climbed a short pitch through a break in the rock, a chimney of stone with a view south across the gorge, and Ansel paused at the top of it to let Ilsa clear the next ledge. And because he paused, because for four or five seconds he was standing still in a gap in the fog, he looked up.

The ridge across the river rose maybe two hundred meters above the water, a dark spine of rock running up toward the glacier country. The fog lay on it in bands, moving, opening and closing like a curtain in a draft. And through one of those openings—for one of those seconds—he saw a figure.

A man, standing on the ridge crest, outlined against the last gray light. Not moving. Not crouched like a patrol scout or a spotter with a radio. Just standing, the way you stand when you're watching something you already understand. And he was wearing a jacket.

Ansel knew the jacket.

He knew the cut of it, the faded red of the shell fabric gone pink at the shoulders from a decade of sun, the way the left cuff hung loose because the elastic had died years ago and Tomás had never once thought to fix it, because Tomás fixed engines and bridges and other people's problems and never his own gear. Ansel had packed that jacket himself. Had zipped it into a bag with his brother's other things after the funeral that held an empty coffin because the river had never given the body back. Had kept the bag in a storage locker in Arequipa for six years and then, in one of the moves, one of the flights, one of the erasures that made up his life—lost it. Or left it. Or thrown it away. He'd never been able to remember which, and the not-remembering was its own small wound.

The figure stood on the ridge wearing his dead brother's jacket, and it was looking at him.

Not at the trail. Not at the boats. At *him*. He felt it the way you feel a hand on the back of your neck. Across two hundred meters of fog and roaring water, through a gap in the mist that was already closing, the figure stood and watched Ansel Sandoval, and did not move, and did not need to.

"Tomás," he said. Out loud. His own voice sounded like a stranger's.

The fog closed. The ridge was gone—just gray, just the memory of gray, the curtain drawn.

"Ansel." Ilsa's hand on his arm, low and urgent. "Move. Now. What is it?"

He didn't answer. His body answered for him, legs carrying him forward along the ledge, because the rule was the rule, *never stop moving*, and the rule had kept him alive through everything, including the things the rule had no name for. But his eyes went back to the ridge, again and again, dragging at each gap in the fog like a tongue to a broken tooth. The gaps showed him rock. Rock, and fog, and once, briefly, the distant yellow smear of the checkpoint lights below and Mara's voice still going, still going, bless her, still quoting clauses to men who had no idea what was sliding past them in the dark.

"Ansel." Ilsa again, sharper. "What did you see?"

"Nothing."

"You said a name."

He kept walking. "I said nothing."

* * *

They cleared the last ledge above the Jaws at midnight and stashed the boat bundles in a scree hollow a kilometer upstream, under a slab Teo would have called a textbook anchor and Pico called the Table. The plan was to come back for the boats at first light, run the easier water beyond the checkpoint, and put twenty river-kilometers between themselves and Reyes's outpost before the fog burned off. Ansel set the watch rotation: Pico first, Ilsa second, himself last, because he no longer trusted himself to sleep and didn't want them watching him not sleep.

But sleep took him anyway, the way it takes the drowning—suddenly, and without permission.

He was on the ridge. In the dream he was not below it, looking across; he was on it, standing where the figure had stood, and the fog moved around him in bands and the river roared below like a congregation. And Tomás was there, or the shape of him, wearing the jacket with the loose left cuff, and his face was the face from the photographs and also not, older, the way Tomás would have been older now if the river had let him.

"You kept moving," the shape said. It had Tomás's voice. Of course it did. Grief always steals the voices we love; it's how it gets in the door.

"I had to," Ansel said.

"You had to *run*," the shape said. "That's different. You signed the paper, and then you ran for ten years, and every year you ran, they wrote the story a little deeper. You let them write it, Ansel. You let them make you the ending."

"There was no paper. I don't remember any paper—"

"That's the point." The shape stepped closer, and the fog opened behind it, and behind the fog was the valley, the forbidden valley, the meltwater lake lying under the glacier like a coin at the bottom of a well. "You don't remember because they made sure. And you don't look because you're afraid of what's true. Eleven of us, Ansel. Eleven. And you're the only one who can say what happened, and you've spent ten years saying nothing."

"I'm going to say it," Ansel said. "I'm going up there. I'm going to the source."

"I know." And the shape's voice changed then, softened, became the voice from before, the voice from a kitchen in Huaraz a lifetime ago, Tomás laughing over a map, Tomás saying *you always were the careful one, hermano, somebody in this family has to be*—"I know you're coming. I've been waiting. But you're slower than you used to be, and they're faster than you think, and the thing you're looking for is buried under the thing they're burying it with. You have to go down to go up. You know that. You've always known that."

"Tomás—"

The fog came in like a wave.

He woke with his own hand clamped over his own mouth, which was how he'd learned to sleep in shared rooms years ago, and his heart going like a trip-hammer, and the fog around the bivouac so thick that Pico, on watch ten meters away, was a rumor of a shape.

"Everything okay?" Pico whispered.

"Fine. Dream."

"You yelled a name."

"Go check the boats."

The kid went, glad of the order, glad of anything. Ansel sat with his back against the slab and his knees drawn up and made himself do the thing he never did, the thing the rule existed to prevent: he stopped moving, and he thought.

The figure on the ridge. He turned it over the way he'd turn a suspect stone on a climb, looking underneath. Option one: a patrol scout, and the jacket a coincidence—some salvage, some secondhand shell, ten thousand identical jackets sold across the cordillera. Option two: a hallucination, altitude and exhaustion and a decade of guilt projecting his brother onto the weather. Option three—

Option three was the one that made his hands go cold. That someone knew. That someone knew exactly what Tomás's jacket looked like, and what Ansel's grief looked like, and had put the one on the ridge in front of the other like a lure in front of a fish. Reyes's people had dossiers; Reyes's people had been running this watershed for ten years; Reyes's people, Mara had said, knew things about her that only her dead husband should have known. A man who could be made to see his brother could be made to stop. Could be made to freeze on a ledge with his light in his eyes. Could be made to walk, alone, toward a figure on a ridge, straight into a pair of waiting hands.

*Grief is a lever,* his father used to say, back when his father had still said anything at all. *Anyone who knows where it's set can move you with it.*

So: it was probably a trick. Probably. He turned it over one more time and found the flaw in his own reasoning, the flaw that sat at the bottom of every version like a stone at the bottom of a clear pool. If it was a trick, it had been built by someone who had seen that jacket recently. Who had seen it, or handled it, or taken it off a body the river never surrendered. And if that was true, then the trick was built out of a truth, and the truth was that somewhere in these mountains, Tomás Sandoval's jacket was not in a lost storage bag in Arequipa. It was here. Upstream. In the country where he died.

And the figure had been standing at the head of a ridge that, if Ansel's memory of the old survey maps was worth anything, ran all the way up into the valley. Toward the source. Toward the dam ruin. Toward whatever the company was so busy destroying that it had hired a private army to keep the world away.

"Never stop moving," he said quietly to the fog. It was the rule, and it was true, and it had kept him alive. But for the first time in ten years, he heard what the rule had always really meant, what his brother's voice in the dream had stripped it down to: never stop moving *toward*. He had been using it as a way to run. Tomás had never once used it that way. Tomás had gone upstream when everyone else went down. Tomás had reached the source first—Ansel was sure of it now, sure the way you're sure of a load before you commit it to the anchor, some knowledge that lives below reasoning. His brother had gone up there with a camera or a recorder or whatever he'd carried, and he had died coming back down with it, and the river had kept him, and the company had kept the story, and Ansel had kept nothing but a jacket's ghost and an hour of missing time.

Dawn came gray and slow, and the fog began to lift off the river in rags.

They went back for the boats. Pico rigged the first bundle down the scree; Ilsa counted the document tubes twice and sealed them again; Ansel stood at the water's edge and looked south, at the ridge, at the place where the fog had opened.

The ridge was empty. Of course it was empty. Rock and light and the long morning shadow of the glacier country above.

But on a boulder at the crest—too far to be sure, too far for any sane person to claim it—he could see something red. A scrap of color, no bigger than a hand. Faded red, gone pink at the edges.

"Ansel," Ilsa called from the boats. "We're ready."

He looked at it for three more seconds. Then he turned and waded out to the lead boat, because the rule was the rule, and because upstream was the only direction left.

They ran the water beyond the checkpoint hard and clean, twenty river-kilometers by noon, and it wasn't until they made camp in a side canyon that the radio—Teo's spare, the one they'd kept wrapped and cold—crackled to life with Mara's voice, thin and stripped of all its theater.

"I'm in," she said. "They're taking me to someone named Reyes. Ansel—whatever happens, whatever they tell you—don't come for me. Go up. Go up and finish it."

Then static, and the fog coming down the canyon walls again, and somewhere above them, in the valley where the river was born, something boomed—once, deep, like a door closing underground.
