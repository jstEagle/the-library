## 19. Interrogation of One

They took her boots first.

That was the detail Mara Voss would remember afterward—not the zip ties biting her wrists, not the hood that smelled of diesel and old canvas, but the moment a young contractor knelt in the gravel of the checkpoint yard and unlaced her boots with careful, almost tender hands, then carried them away like something confiscated at a border. Barefoot on cold stone. It was such a small humiliation, so precisely calibrated, that she understood immediately she was dealing with professionals.

The checkpoint was bigger than it looked from the river. From the water they'd seen only the timber barricade and the watchtower, the kind of structure a road crew might throw up for a season. Inside, past the second gate, there were prefab buildings on concrete pads, a generator compound humming behind chain-link, satellite dishes bolted to a ridge like pale mushrooms. Forty men, maybe more. She counted what she could see while they marched her between two escorts who kept their muzzles angled down but their fingers indexed along the triggers—trained, disciplined, bored. Bored men with rifles were the most dangerous kind, because they were always looking for something to break the monotony.

They put her in a room with a table, two chairs, and a caged bulb overhead. The walls were plywood over steel studs. Someone had taped a calendar to the wall and crossed off days in red pen—thirty-one days marked, and today's square already slashed through. She stared at it while the zip ties came off and the door locked behind her, and she did the arithmetic she'd been doing since Ansel's boat slipped into the fog upstream without her.

Thirty-one days marked. A demolition schedule didn't run on thirty-one-day increments unless the end was close.

She sat down. Her bare feet were already going numb against the concrete, and she tucked them under the chair the way you do when you're trying not to shiver in front of someone. Then she waited, because waiting was a skill too, and she'd learned it in courtrooms and consulate offices and the long gray anterooms of companies that dissolved on paper but never in fact.

The door opened forty minutes later.

* * *

Reyes was not what she expected, which she suspected was deliberate. She'd imagined the security chief of a ghost consortium as a scarred ex-military type, all jaw and threat. Instead he was slight, graying at the temples, wearing a clean field jacket with no insignia and reading glasses pushed up onto his forehead like an accountant who'd wandered out of his office. He carried a thermos, two paper cups, and a thin folder held together with a rubber band.

"Señora Voss." He set the cups down, poured from the thermos—coffee, by the smell, real coffee, which meant he outranked everyone here—and sat across from her without hurrying. "You'll forgive the accommodations. We weren't expecting company this far up."

"You were expecting me," Mara said. "You released me from this checkpoint once before. Four years ago."

"I remember." He smiled faintly and slid one cup across the table. "You were looking for the same thing then that I suspect you're looking for now. You had better papers, though. And a better story."

"The story hasn't changed. Fishing survey downstream. My team got separated in the gorge. I came up here looking for them."

Reyes took off his glasses, polished them on his sleeve, and put them back on. The whole performance was unhurried, almost gentle, and every second of it told her he had nowhere else to be and nothing else to do until he decided otherwise. Somewhere above them, up in the valley where the Ceniza was born, machines were doing whatever machines did to a place that eleven people had died keeping quiet. She thought about the red slashes on the calendar and made herself breathe slowly.

"Señora Voss." He opened the folder. "Do you know what I admire about you? You've spent ten years asking questions nobody wants answered, and you've never once been arrested for it. Detained, questioned, escorted out—yes. But never charged. Never photographed for the newspapers. Do you ever wonder why?"

"I assumed I was boring."

"You are many things, but not that." He turned the folder around and pushed it toward her. Inside was a single photograph: Mara herself, four years younger, standing at this same checkpoint in a rain shell, arguing with a man whose face was turned away. "There is a person—a small number of persons—who find your work useful. They prefer that you stay just inside the line. Asking questions keeps the story alive. Getting answers would end it. So when you get close, we stop you. Gently. And someone, somewhere, makes a call, and you go home frustrated and alive, and the questions keep breathing."

Mara looked at the photograph for a long time. Her hands stayed flat on the table because she'd learned long ago that hands tell stories before mouths do.

"And if I cross the line?"

"Then the calls stop working." He said it without heat, the way a weatherman forecasts rain. "I want to be honest with you, señora, because I think honesty will save us both time. You are not my problem. Your friends on the river are my problem. There are four of them, they passed my checkpoint last night in the fog, and they are now inside an operational zone where my instructions regarding trespassers are—" he paused, choosing the word with care, "—flexible."

The cold in her feet climbed her ankles. She kept her face still. *Four.* Ansel, Teo, Ilsa, Pico. All through. All upstream. All inside the flexible zone.

"If they're just portaging past," she said, "they're not looking for trouble. They're avoiding it. That's all any of us have been doing—staying out of your way."

"That was true last week." Reyes closed the folder and laced his fingers on the table. "It stopped being true yesterday afternoon."

* * *

He stood, walked to the door, and spoke briefly to someone outside. When he came back he brought a second folder, thicker, and laid it down without opening it, the way a dealer lays down a card face-down.

"Do you know what's under the dam?" he asked.

"Ruin. Rubble. Eleven people's graves, if anyone had the decency to leave them be."

"Under the dam," Reyes said, "is the reason the dam exists. You've read the old permits—I know you have, you quoted them at a ministry hearing in 2019, I was in the room. The Cerro Blanco consortium built the Ceniza tailings dam to hold back the waste from three adits of ore body that tested rich in copper and catastrophically rich in arsenic. The tailings pond leaked from year one. Into the groundwater. Into the river. Into eleven villages' wells." He poured himself more coffee, unhurried. "The flood wasn't the disaster, señora. The flood was the cleanup."

Mara's mouth went dry despite the coffee. "Cleanup."

"A controlled overtopping. The records of the leakage—sampling data, internal memos, the medical surveys from the villages—were stored in the dam's lower gallery because it was the only reinforced structure on site. When the dam failed, those records went under sixty meters of water and rock. Eleven people died. The company declared force majeure, dissolved itself across four jurisdictions, and the arsenic numbers died with it." He said it all flatly, reciting inventory. "I'm telling you this because you already know most of it, and because I need you to understand that none of it is a secret to me. I've read everything you've read. I've read more."

"Then you know what you're guarding."

"I know exactly what I'm guarding." For the first time something moved behind his eyes—not guilt, she thought, or not only guilt. Fatigue. The deep, sedimented fatigue of a man who has done a job for years and finally looked up from it. "And right now, my orders are to make sure that in nine days, there is nothing left to guard. No ruin. No gallery. No records. The demolition charges are already placed. They're going to take the whole valley floor down to bedrock, bury the gallery under a hundred thousand tons of new rock, and regrade the watershed so no survey will ever find the seam again. After that, the patrols go home, the checkpoints come down, and the Río Ceniza becomes just a river."

Nine days. The number landed in her chest like a stone dropped down a well. She thought of Ansel somewhere in the dark above the treeline, hauling boats over scree, chasing a source that would be a crater by the time he reached it.

"You're telling me this," she said slowly, "because you want something."

"I'm telling you this," Reyes said, "because you're going to hear it eventually, and I'd rather you heard it from me than from the sound of the blast. And because I want to make you an offer—the same offer, more or less, that someone once made to a half-drowned guide downstream of this very checkpoint, ten years ago."

The room seemed to tilt. Mara gripped the edge of the table. "What did you say?"

Reyes watched her with those tired accountant's eyes, and she understood that he had said it deliberately—that the whole conversation had been built toward this sentence the way a river builds toward a drop.

"Ansel Sandoval," he said. "Your rival. Your expedition leader, I assume, since he's the only guide alive reckless enough to run the upper gorge at night. He survived the flood, señora. Did you ever wonder how? He was found six kilometers downstream, below a canyon no swimmer could live through, with barely a bruise. Somebody pulled him out. Somebody sat with him while he was half-conscious, and somebody needed a thumbprint on an incident report that blamed equipment failure and human error—his error, his brother's error—for a failure that was engineered in an office in Lima." He folded his hands. "He signed. He forgot, or was helped to forget. And the price of forgetting was that we let him walk away, and let him keep walking, country to country, for ten years. Every blacklist, every border that closed against him—that wasn't punishment. That was maintenance. Keeping him moving. A moving man doesn't dig."

The caged bulb buzzed. Somewhere outside, a generator changed pitch. Mara became aware that she was shaking—not with fear, she realized, but with a fury so old and so total that it had nowhere left to go except outward through her skin. Ten years she had testified against him. Ten years she had carried the certainty that Ansel Sandoval had signed his name to a lie that buried her cousin Elena among the eleven, and hated him for it cleanly, righteously, the way you hate when hating is easier than doubting.

"He didn't know," she said. Her voice came out low and cracked. "He genuinely didn't know."

"No. And now he's climbing toward a valley full of evidence that says he was the victim and not the cause, and my employers would very much prefer he never reads it." Reyes slid the thick folder across the table and tapped it once. "So. Here is the offer. Walk out of here within the hour, alone, downstream, and radio your team to turn back. Tell them the job is dead, the source is sealed, and there's nothing up there but men with rifles. Do that, and the four of them walk away too—we'll shadow them to the boundary and let them go. Everyone keeps breathing. The mountain keeps its secrets. And you—" he almost smiled, "—you get to keep asking questions for another ten years. Just inside the line. Where it's safe."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then in nine days the valley comes down, and anything inside it comes down with it. Including, if they're still up there, your friends." He stood, gathered the first folder, and paused at the door with his hand on the frame. "For what it's worth, señora Voss—I hope you refuse. I've been maintaining this lie for six years, and I am so very tired."

The door shut. The lock turned.

* * *

She didn't sleep. They left her in the room with the light burning and the calendar on the wall, and she sat with her numb feet tucked under her chair and did what she did best, which was work a problem from every side until it gave her an answer or bled her dry.

The offer was real—she believed that. Reyes wanted out of the room as much as she did; a man didn't confess the architecture of a cover-up to a prisoner unless part of him was begging to be contradicted. Which meant the offer was also a test, and possibly a trap, and possibly both. If she refused loudly, they might move the timeline up. If she accepted and radioed downstream, she might be signing her friends' death warrant with her own voice.

But the arithmetic underneath everything was simpler and colder than any of that. Nine days. Charges already placed. A valley floor scheduled to become a grave for the second time, deeper this time, permanent. Whatever Tomás Sandoval had gone up that river to find—whatever had gotten him killed—was sitting under sixty meters of rock with a fuse attached, and the clock wasn't ticking anymore. It was tolling.

She thought about Elena. Not the funeral, not the lawsuit, not the ten years of hearings where Mara had stood up with documents in her hand and been politely thanked and sent home. She thought about Elena at nineteen, sunburned, laughing on the riverbank, teaching Mara to skip stones across the Ceniza when the water ran clear in the dry season. Clear water. There had been clear water once.

Near dawn—she judged it by the thinning of the generator drone, the shift change she'd tracked all night through the wall—boots came down the corridor. Two of them, then a third, heavier, stopping at her door. The lock turned.

The guard who entered was young, maybe twenty-five, with a patrol rifle slung loose and a face she hadn't seen during the interrogation. He set something on the table without a word: her boots, laces tucked inside, and beside them a strip of dried meat and a canteen. Then he leaned close, so fast she flinched, and his voice came out barely louder than breath.

"The west fence line," he said. "Second tower. The sensor loop has a dead zone—forty meters, past the fuel drums. Shift changes at six. You have maybe ninety seconds of dark after that." His eyes, when they met hers, were wet-rimmed and hard as river stones. "My father fished that river. Before."

Then he was gone, and the lock clicked, and Mara sat staring at the boots on the table with her heart slamming against her ribs like something trying to get out.

Ninety seconds of dark. Nine days on the clock. And somewhere above her, in the fog and the scree, Ansel Sandoval was climbing toward the truth with no idea that the mountain was rigged to swallow it whole.

She picked up the boots and started pulling them on.
