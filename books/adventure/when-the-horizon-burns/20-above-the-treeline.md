## 20. Above the Treeline

The trees gave up before the river did.

Teo noticed it the way he noticed most things—gradually, then all at once. For two days they'd been climbing through stunted queñua forest, gnarled and wind-flattened, and now even those survivors had thinned to isolated sentinels, then to skeletons, then to nothing at all. The gorge walls fell away behind them and the world opened into a gray-brown amphitheater of scree and moraine, the glacier's old debris fields stacked like the leavings of some titanic argument between stone and ice.

Above it all, half-hidden in cloud, stood the wall.

"God," Pico said quietly. He'd said it three times in the last hour, each time as if for the first time.

The icefall was visible from here even through the weather—a staircase of shattered seracs climbing the flank of the mountain, blue-green in its depths where the light got in, white and blinding on its broken faces. It looked less like a route than a wound. Somewhere up there, Teo knew, was the source of the Río Ceniza. Somewhere up there was everything.

"Don't stare at it too long," Ansel said. He was breaking camp with the economy of long habit, his hands moving without his eyes. "It'll still be there tomorrow. So will we, if we're smart."

"We're always smart," Ilsa said. Her voice had a strange flatness to it that Teo didn't like.

He looked at her properly for the first time that morning and saw what he should have seen an hour ago: she was pale in a way that had nothing to do with cold, and she was walking carefully, deliberately, the way people walk when they're managing something rather than simply doing it. Her lips were a bad color.

"How long?" he asked, falling into step beside her.

"How long what?"

"Ilsa."

She exhaled through her nose. "Since yesterday afternoon. Headache. Nausea. I've been taking acetazolamide since base camp." She said it like a diagnosis delivered to a colleague, which, in a way, it was. "It's mild. I'm managing it."

"You're a medic. You know the word 'mild' is a lie people tell at altitude."

"I know exactly what it is, Teo." A flash of her usual sharpness, quickly banked. "And I know that we don't stop. Not here. Not on open ground with patrols below us and Mara still—" She stopped herself. Mara was four days behind them, detained or escaped or dead; no one knew which. The not-knowing sat on all of them like ballast.

Ansel had drifted close enough to hear. He studied Ilsa for a long moment with those gray eyes that seemed to take inventory of everything and commit to nothing.

"Can you make the ridge?" he asked.

"Yes."

"Liar," Teo said.

"Competent liar," Ilsa corrected. "There's a difference. Ask anyone who's ever been my patient."

They made the ridge by early afternoon, and it cost them.

The last four hundred meters were a scramble over blocky moraine where every step required a decision—where to put the foot, whether that boulder would hold, whether the one above it would come down. The air had gone thin enough that Teo could hear his own pulse in his ears, a metronome counting out how much he had left. Pico, twenty years old and built like a whip, moved ahead as if the altitude were a rumor. Ansel climbed the way water flows downhill: without apparent effort, without apparent thought. And Ilsa—

Ilsa stopped twice. The second time she didn't say anything at all. She just stood there with one hand braced against a boulder, head down, breathing in short controlled sips, and when Ansel touched her elbow she flinched hard enough that Teo's stomach dropped.

"Ataxia?" he asked quietly.

"No."

"That wasn't a question about your dignity."

"It's not ataxia." But she took his arm anyway, and let him steer her over the worst of the blocks, and that frightened Teo more than any symptom would have. Ilsa Reyes-Ortega did not take arms. Ilsa Reyes-Ortega had once stitched her own forearm by headlamp during a storm because, as she put it, "the alternative was waiting."

The ridge, when they crested it, gave them the valley beyond—and the hut.

It crouched on a shelf of flat ground maybe six hundred meters along the crest: a low rectangle of corrugated steel and stone, half-sunk into the moraine, its roof streaked with rust and old snow-stain. A survey hut. Teo recognized the type immediately—the mining consortium had dotted them across the whole watershed during the boom years, little eyes planted in the high country. This one had been abandoned so long that scree had begun to bury its southern wall, and yet someone had once cared for it: there were anchor bolts along the eaves, a lightning rod, a rain catchment gutter long since torn away.

"Company-built," Ansel said.

"Company-abandoned," Teo countered. "Different thing entirely."

They crossed to it fast, because the light was going gold and then copper and then gone, and because standing on a ridgeline at five thousand meters after dark was a way of volunteering for hypothermia. The door was a steel slab on hinges that screamed when Pico forced them. Inside: darkness, dust, and cold that felt geological.

Ansel got his headlamp on first. The beam swept across a room perhaps four meters by six—bunk frames bolted to the wall, their mattresses long since rotted or carried off; a wood stove with a cracked door; a table; shelves. And on the shelves, and on the table, and in a steel cabinet whose lock had been shot open rather than picked—

Paper. Hundreds upon hundreds of pages of it, curling with damp at the edges, furred with a decade of mold in places, but there. Folders. Binders. Rolled survey sheets bound with perished rubber bands. A cardboard box stamped with a logo Teo knew intimately, because he'd spent three weeks in a Lima archive hunting for any surviving trace of it: CONSÓRCIO MINERO CENIZA S.A.C.

Nobody spoke for a moment. Then Pico said, "They left?"

"They evacuated," Ansel said slowly. "When the valley was sealed. Somebody pulled the crews out fast—faster than they could pack. Or—" His jaw worked. "Or somebody decided this stuff wasn't worth coming back for. That nobody would ever climb this high looking for it."

"Eleven people died proving them right," Ilsa said from the doorway. Her voice wobbled on the last word, and Teo turned sharply—but it was just the altitude, just exhaustion, just a woman who had been holding herself together with professional discipline for eleven days finally letting one seam show.

* * *

They lit the stove. There was a cache of kindling beneath it—old crate slats, dry as bone—and Teo fed the fire with the reverence other men reserved for sacrament. Warmth in a steel box at five thousand meters was not comfort; it was infrastructure. It was the difference between a night and a death.

Ilsa lasted until the stove was hot before her body filed its formal complaint.

She was sitting on a bunk frame, cataloging symptoms aloud in the clipped tone she used for medical handovers—"headache seven out of ten, unresponsive to analgesia; nausea persistent; fine motor control degraded, watch me thread this needle"—when her hands simply stopped cooperating. She dropped the thermometer. Then she tried to pick it up and missed by ten centimeters, twice, and stared at her own fingers with an expression of pure betrayed fury, as though they were employees who'd gone rogue.

"Okay," Teo said. "That's enough."

"It is not—"

"You're ataxic. You've been ataxic since the ridge. You're describing your own HACE workup like it's somebody else's chart." He crouched in front of her, kept his voice level, kept his hands visible. Ten years of fieldwork had taught him that tone mattered more than words. "Dexamethasone. Now. And we consider descent."

"There is no descent." She said it flatly. "Not really. We go down two hundred meters into terrain we can't defend, with patrols in the gorge, with Mara missing, with a demolition clock running—we go down and we lose everything we climbed for. You know the arithmetic."

"I know dead medics can't treat anybody."

"And I know that dexamethasone buys time, and time is what we need, and I am the only person on this mountain who knows how to triage whatever we find in that cabinet." Her eyes were glassy but her will was intact, burning through the fog like a lamp through mist. "So dose me, strap me to a bunk, and put the documents in front of me, because I can read lying down."

Teo looked at Ansel. Ansel looked back, and for once the guide deferred—not to rank, but to the obvious. "She's right," he said. "Unfortunately."

"She's usually right. It's insufferable."

"DEX eight milligrams IV," Ilsa recited, already pulling her own kit with trembling hands. "Then oral maintenance. And oxygen if we have it."

"We have two bottles," Pico said. "Maybe three-quarters full."

"Then I get one, and nobody argues, and whoever needs it next gets the other." She held out her arm for the cannula with the grim courtesy of a woman offering a ticket-taker her stub. "Go on. Before I change my mind and start being brave instead of sensible."

* * *

By full dark the hut had become a small lit island in an ocean of black moraine. The storm arrived around nine—an altiplano front rolling up the valley, filling the air with spindrift and sound, hammering the corrugated roof with gusts that made the whole structure shudder like a struck drum. Outside, the temperature fell through freezing toward something worse. Inside, the stove ticked and breathed, and Teo sat at the table under a headlamp with a decade of buried paper spread before him, and began to read.

Ansel sorted. That was the only word for it—he moved through the folders with a strange, contained intensity, separating pages into piles with quick decisive motions, saying almost nothing. Pico had taken first watch at the window slit, though there was nothing to see but snow and darkness; he watched anyway, because watching was how Pico loved things. Ilsa lay dosed and oxygenated on the far bunk, propped against her pack, reading over Teo's shoulder whenever her vision cooperated.

The documents were a company's whole nervous system, abandoned mid-thought.

Survey reports. Core sample logs. Water chemistry analyses going back eight years before the disaster—eight years of readings showing contamination creeping up the watershed, arsenic and heavy metals leaching from the tailings impoundment into the very headwaters of the Ceniza. Teo found memos debating what to do about it and found, beneath the memos, the answer the company had chosen: not remediation. Erasure. A proposal, initialed in three places, titled *Plan de Contención Integral*—Comprehensive Containment Plan—which, read closely, was not about containing the tailings at all.

It was about containing the story.

"Look at the dates," Ilsa said. Her voice was slurred at the edges, the steroid haze and the altitude both pressing on her, but her mind was cutting clean. "The contamination reports accelerate through the final year. Then they stop. Not because the problem was solved—because reporting stopped being permitted. See this memo? 'All external analytical work suspended pending internal review.' Internal review that never concluded. Because eleven days after it was commissioned, the dam failed."

"The dam didn't fail," Ansel said quietly. He hadn't looked up from the folder in his hands. "We've known that since the hut in the gorge. This confirms the why." He turned the folder around and set it on the table. "They couldn't fix the leak without admitting it existed. Admitting it existed meant fines, closure, criminal liability. So they chose the other option. Drown the evidence. Drown the site. Call it an act of God, blame whoever's convenient, seal the valley, and wait for everyone to die or forget."

The stove popped. Wind screamed across the ridge and the hut groaned and held.

"Blame whoever's convenient," Pico repeated softly, from the window. He didn't look at Ansel. He was very careful not to look at Ansel. But the words hung in the warm air anyway, and Teo saw the guide's shoulders take the weight of them without complaint, the way a mule takes a load—by habit, by training, by having no better option offered in years.

"That stops now," Teo said. He heard his own voice come out harder than he'd intended. "That's why we're here. That's why we keep climbing. These pages are the confession they never wrote. Every signature, every initial, every date—it's all here. They were so sure no one would ever come this high that they couldn't be bothered to burn it."

"Someone should have burned it," Ansel said. "Someone will try again. Which means we copy everything tonight, we cache the originals somewhere Reyes can't blast them, and we carry duplicates out in three separate loads on three separate bodies." He finally looked up, and in the stove-light his face was older than Teo had ever seen it, carved into new lines. "Nothing important travels alone. Not anymore."

* * *

It was past midnight when Teo found the photograph.

He'd been working through a box of miscellany—supply requisitions, payroll stubs, the boring sediment of any enterprise—when a stiff envelope slid out from between two ledgers and spilled a sheaf of prints across the table. Site photographs. Aerials, mostly, of the upper valley: the dam ruin, the tailings impoundment, the glacier. Standard documentation.

Except the dates on the backs were wrong.

"These aren't pre-disaster," he said. His mouth had gone dry, and not from altitude. "Look. This aerial is dated fourteen months ago. This one—eight months. Someone has been flying survey runs over the sealed valley within the last year." He spread them out with careful hands. "And these aren't tourism shots. Look at the annotations. Grid references. Volume calculations. They're measuring the dam ruin. Measuring the tailings deposit. Calculating—" He stopped, and did the arithmetic he didn't want to do, and felt the cold slide up his spine independent of the storm outside. "Calculating how much explosive it would take to remove it."

Ilsa pushed herself up on one elbow, oxygen mask dangling. "Demolition surveys."

"Recent ones. Ongoing ones." Teo looked across the table at Ansel. "Mara told us they were erasing the site. We assumed she meant slowly, bureaucratically—permits, reclamation, plausible deniability. This isn't bureaucratic. This is an engineering schedule. They're not hiding the ruin anymore, Ansel. They're deleting it. Physically. Soon."

"How soon?" Pico asked.

Teo flipped through the annotated prints until he found the master sheet—a project timeline, typed, with phases and milestones. Phase One: access road rehabilitation. Phase Two: charge placement. Phase Three: controlled collapse and burial. At the bottom, a target window, expressed not as a date but as a season.

Early thaw.

"Weeks," Teo said. "Maybe less. Maybe they're already staging charges." He looked at the storm-lashed window, at the black wall of the icefall invisible beyond it, and felt hope and frostbite arrive in the same breath—because they had the proof, yes, the confession in paper form, but proof of a crime already confessed was worth less than proof of a crime about to be completed. If the company demolished the site before testimony went public, every document they carried would become history instead of evidence. *Allegations. Conspiracy theories. The fever dream of a disgraced guide.*

Unless they reached the source first. Unless they stood on the record itself.

Ansel gathered the aerials into a single stack, squared their edges with a precision that Teo recognized as the man's version of emotion, and said nothing for a long moment. The stove ticked. The wind tried the door in its frame like a patient burglar.

On the bunk, Ilsa began to laugh—a low, exhausted, unhysterical sound.

"What?" Pico asked.

"Fourteen months of surveys," she said, "and they left them in an unlocked cabinet in an unlocked hut." She settled back against her pack, eyes closing, the ghost of the smile still on her lips. "Arrogance is a filing system too."

Teo copied the timeline twice, by hand, in case the camera failed. Outside, the storm built toward its midnight crescendo, and somewhere above them, past the wind and the dark, the glacier waited with its buried valley and its buried dead—and, if Tomás Sandoval had been half the man his brother believed, with one more thing besides.

He was still writing when Ansel spoke, so quietly that Teo almost missed it beneath the storm.

"Thaw's early this year."
