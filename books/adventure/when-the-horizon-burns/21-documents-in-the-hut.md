## 21. Documents in the Hut

The hut had a smell Teo would never forget: rust, mouse droppings, and old paper going soft with damp—the particular rot of things that had been important once and then abandoned in a hurry.

He'd found it by accident, or as close to accident as his life allowed. Ilsa had gone down with altitude sickness an hour below the moraine saddle, gray-lipped and stumbling, and Ansel had made the call without hesitation—shelter, oxygen, rest—scanning the slope the way he scanned everything, like a man reading a page only he could see. Then he'd pointed at a dark rectangle half-swallowed by rock and ice fifty meters upslope.

"There," he'd said. "Survey hut."

"How do you know that?" Pico had asked.

"Because it's there," Ansel said, which was not an answer, but they were too tired and too scared to press him.

Now, six hours later, with Ilsa breathing slow and even under two sleeping bags and a chemical heat pack, Teo sat cross-legged on a rotten plywood floor with a headlamp clamped to his forehead and a steel document case open in front of him like a coffin at a wake.

The company had missed it. That was the thing that kept snagging in his mind, the splinter he couldn't stop touching. They'd demolished records, burned offices, dissolved a consortium into four shell entities scattered across three registries—he'd traced all of that himself from a cybercafé in Quito while pretending to research fishing licenses. They had been thorough everywhere except here, at five thousand two hundred meters, where the air itself argued against human presence. Someone had locked this hut, stacked these cases, and walked away assuming no one would ever climb high enough or care enough to come looking.

They had been right for ten years.

Teo lifted out the first folder. The paper was brittle at the edges, foxed with brown blooms of moisture, but the ink held. Engineering drawings, stamped and signed. He smoothed one flat on his knee and felt his stomach drop through the floor of the world.

It was the Ceniza dam. Not the public drawings—the ones from the tender documents, the ones any journalist could have pulled before the disaster scrubbed them from every archive on the continent. These were the working revisions. Redlines in different hands, dates climbing through eighteen months of quiet modification, and in the margins, in a cramped engineer's script, notes that read less like engineering and more like confession.

*Spillway capacity reduced per client directive. Flagged as non-standard. See memo 44-C.*

*Client insists final crest elevation -2.4m from approved design. Refused twice. Overruled.*

*This configuration cannot pass a flood event above 1-in-50 without overtopping. Documented objection attached. If this proceeds, my name is on record.*

There was a signature. Teo photographed it with shaking hands, then forced himself to keep reading, because the alternative was to put the folder down and scream into the thin cold air until his lungs bled.

* * *

Ansel came back inside just after midnight, bringing with him a blade of wind and the smell of snow. He crouched by Ilsa first—checked her pulse, her breathing, the color of her lips—with a gentleness that always surprised people who knew his reputation. Then he looked at Teo, at the folders spread across the floor, at whatever was showing on Teo's face.

"You've been reading," Ansel said.

"I haven't slept."

"I can see that." He lowered himself onto the floor across from the open case, folding his long frame down with the economy of a man who had spent decades making himself small in bad shelters. "How bad?"

Teo turned a drawing around and pushed it across the plywood. "You tell me. You grew up downstream of this thing. What does a crest two-point-four meters low mean?"

Ansel didn't touch the paper. He looked at it the way a man looks at an X-ray of his own fracture. "It means the dam dies in a big melt year," he said quietly. "It means the water goes over the top instead of through the spillway, and when water goes over an earthen crest—" He stopped. His jaw worked. "When water goes over an earthen crest, the crest erodes. Fast. From the top down. And then there is no more dam."

"And who ordered the crest lowered?"

The silence stretched. Outside, wind dragged its nails across the corrugated roof.

"The client," Ansel said finally. "The consortium."

"Which is strange," Teo said, "because you don't lower a dam's crest by accident. You don't do it to save money—it barely saves money. You do it—" He picked up the next folder, the one he'd saved, the one he'd been sitting with for two hours like a man holding a live grenade. "You do it if you need the dam to fail."

* * *

The tailings reports began forty pages deep into the third case, and once Teo understood what he was holding, he couldn't stop, couldn't even blink properly, his eyes burning in the headlamp beam as page after page assembled itself into a machine of deliberate harm.

The Ceniza mine wasn't gold. Not primarily. It was copper, and copper ore at that altitude and that grade required processing that left behind a slurry of ground rock and chemical residue—tailings—stored behind the dam in a pond the size of a lake. Standard practice. Ugly, but standard.

Except the liner had failed. Year three of operation, according to the monitoring logs: a tear in the geomembrane beneath the tailings pond, and heavy metals beginning their slow migration into the groundwater. Arsenic. Cadmium. Lead. Numbers circled in red by someone whose job was to catch exactly this, numbers that climbed month over month like a fever chart.

And then the reports changed character. The monitoring wells nearest the plume stopped reporting. A consultant's letter recommended remediation—cost estimate attached, a number with a lot of zeros. Below it, in different ink, a handwritten note:

*Remediation cost exceeds remaining mine-life revenue. Recommend containment strategy review.*

Containment strategy review. Teo said the words aloud, tasting them, because saying them made them real. Containment meant keeping the contamination in. But you couldn't contain a plume already moving through fractured bedrock toward the watershed—not really, not cheaply. Unless.

Unless the evidence stopped existing.

He spread the pages out in chronological order across the floor, a timeline twelve feet long, and stood over it with his heart hammering, and saw it whole for the first time: the failed liner, the rising costs, the decision that remediation was unaffordable, and then—beginning eight months later—a cascade of decisions that made no engineering sense and perfect criminal sense. The crest lowered. The spillway capacity cut. The emergency action plan revised to remove downstream evacuation triggers. Insurance quietly restructured so the dam itself sat outside the covered assets. And threaded through everything, memos about *records retention*, about *legacy documentation phase-out*, about a *final disposition schedule* for materials stored at the site.

Final disposition. Teo had seen that phrase before, in a completely different life, in the bankruptcy filings of a construction firm he'd helped bury in forged invoices. Final disposition was what you called it when you destroyed something and wanted the destruction itself documented as orderly.

"They weren't maintaining the dam," he said to the empty room. "They were scheduling its death."

* * *

Pico woke around three, or pretended to, and came and sat beside Teo without asking questions, which was one of the thousand things Teo loved about the kid. He just looked at the timeline of papers glowing under the headlamp, and at Teo's face, and waited.

"It was never an accident," Teo said. "The flood. The eleven people." He heard his own voice come out flat and precise and was distantly grateful for it, because the other voice, the one underneath, was not flat at all. "The company poisoned the groundwater and couldn't afford to fix it, so they decided the cheapest solution was to erase the proof. The dam, the tailings pond, the monitoring records—all of it, buried under a hundred million tons of water and mud. And the eleven people downstream—" His throat closed. He pushed through it. "The eleven people were in the way. They were collateral on a balance sheet. Cheaper dead than compensated."

Pico was very still. In the dim light his young face had gone old in some specific way, the way faces go when a person understands for the first time that the world contains men who will do arithmetic like that.

"Tomás," Pico said. "Ansel's brother. He was one of the eleven?"

"One of the eleven." Teo gathered the papers with exaggerated care, squaring their edges, because his hands needed a task or they were going to shake apart. "And now think about what we know. Think about who survived. One guide, pulled from the river with no memory of the final hour, carrying a false incident report with his thumbprint on it, blamed for everything, blacklisted from two countries, running for ten years." He looked up. "That's not what happens to a man who causes a disaster, Pico. That's what happens to a man who was supposed to be a scapegoat—and maybe more than a scapegoat. Maybe a witness they decided to keep alive because a live guilty man is more useful than a dead martyr. Dead martyrs get investigated. Guilty survivors get hated."

The words hung in the freezing air. Down the room, Ilsa stirred in her sleep and murmured something, and both of them went silent until she settled.

"We have to tell him," Pico said.

"He knows part of it. He remembers the thumbprint now—I heard him tell Mara on the portage." Teo stacked the last folder. "But he doesn't know this part. He doesn't know it was engineered from the start. He's spent ten years believing he killed his brother." Teo's voice cracked on the word *brother*, and he let it, because hiding it would cost more than showing it. "Ten years, Pico. Every border crossing, every job nobody else would take, every night he pretends he doesn't dream—that weight was manufactured. Stamped and signed, same as these drawings."

* * *

Ansel hadn't slept. Teo realized it when he looked over at four in the morning and found him sitting exactly where he'd been hours ago, motionless, staring at the middle distance with the expression of a man watching something happen far away. When Teo crossed the room and sat down beside him, Ansel spoke without turning his head.

"You've been reading for seven hours. You've taken four hundred photographs. You keep arranging the papers in order and then rearranging them, which is what you do when the answer is worse than the question." A ghost of the old dry humor, gone almost immediately. "So tell me. Don't manage me. Just tell me."

Teo told him. All of it—the liner failure, the remediation estimate, the phrase *containment strategy review*, the crest, the spillway, the insurance, the *final disposition schedule*. He told it in the order the documents demanded, chronologically, because lies need creativity and truth only needs sequence. Ansel listened without moving. Snow ticked against the window. Somewhere below, the Río Ceniza ran dark and constant, carrying its melted glacier toward the valley where eleven people had died for a line item.

When Teo finished, Ansel was quiet for a long time.

"I stood on that crest," he said at last. "Three weeks before. Tomás and I, we hiked up to look at it—you could walk right up to the fence line then, before the patrols. He was worried about something at work, wouldn't say what. He kept looking at the water level and frowning, and I teased him about it. Said he'd been an engineer too long, seeing problems in a pretty lake." Ansel's hands lay open on his knees, palms up, as if waiting to receive something. "He was seeing it. He was standing there doing the math, and I laughed at him."

"You didn't know."

"I know that. I've had ten years to know that." He turned his head, and in the headlamp light his eyes were wet and absolutely steady. "What I'm feeling right now isn't guilt, Teo. I want you to understand that, because you're the one who keeps the books of this crew and I need it entered correctly. What I'm feeling is that they took my brother, and then they took my grief and bent it into a weapon against me. They drowned eleven people to hide poison, and then they drowned me—my name, my career, every country that would have me—to make sure no one ever asked why the dam fell. And it worked. For ten years it worked perfectly." He exhaled, long and slow, the breath fogging and fading. "Ilsa asked me once, in the first week she was with us, why I only take jobs nobody else wants. I told her it was because nobody else wants them. That was true. But the real reason is that a man with nothing left to lose is the only kind of man who can stand on a glacier and read documents like these without flinching."

"So what do we do?" It was Pico, from the doorway, awake after all, holding three tin cups of snowmelt tea like an offering.

Ansel looked at the boy for a moment. Something passed over his face—too fast to name.

"Now?" He reached out and tapped the steel case with one finger, a small precise sound, a knell. "Now we carry every page of this off this mountain. Now we find Mara and Reyes's checkpoint and whatever's left of the ruin, because if these surveys are the skeleton, Tomás was after the organs—the recordings, the orders, the names of the men who signed them. My brother cached supplies somewhere between here and the source, and I intend to find them." He stood, unfolding to his full height in the low room, and for the first time since Teo had known him, Ansel Sandoval looked less like a fugitive than like a man arriving somewhere. "And then we make sure that everyone who did this arithmetic learns what it costs."

Teo began packing the cases with methodical hands, rage settling in his chest not as fire but as frost—cold, hard, exact, a load-bearing structure. He'd built bridges with less certainty than he felt about what came next.

Outside, the sky was turning the gray that precedes dawn, and far up the valley, faint but unmistakable, came a sound none of them had heard yet on this expedition: the flat, rolling boom of demolition charges.
