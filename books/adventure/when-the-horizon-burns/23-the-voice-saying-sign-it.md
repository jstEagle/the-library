## 23. The Voice Saying Sign It

Sleep at five thousand meters is not sleep. It is a negotiation the body keeps losing.

Ansel had learned this years ago on Aconcagua, where the thin air strips dreams down to their wire frame and leaves you staring at the inside of your own skull. He knew all the tricks—diamox timing, the slow ascent, the deliberate slowing of breath—but none of them worked tonight, because tonight his skull was not empty. Tonight it was filling up.

It began with water.

Not the memory of water—the sensation of it, cold climbing his shins in the survey hut's dark, which was absurd, because he was dry, wrapped in a bag rated to minus twenty, lying on a foam pad that smelled of mildew and old fuel. But the cold rose anyway, shin to knee to thigh, and with it came the river: brown, violent, full of trees and torn roofing and things he had spent ten years refusing to look at directly.

*The night of the flood.*

He was back in it before he understood he was anywhere. The roar so complete it wasn't sound anymore but pressure, a hand pressing him under. The taste of silt and diesel. His own arms doing something intelligent without him—rolling, reaching, catching a root mat that tore free and caught again. And through it all, threaded like wire through wool, Tomás's voice, shouting words the water chewed apart.

Ansel surfaced inside the dream gasping, and Ilsa's voice came from across the hut, low and professional: "Ansel. You're dreaming. You're safe."

"I'm fine," he said, because he always said that, and lay back down, and the water took him again.

This time it went further than it ever had.

* * *

He was on the bank. That much he had always known, or thought he'd known—that he'd crawled out somewhere downstream, half-drowned, concussed, and the next coherent thing was a hospital in Coyhaique and men from the ministry asking questions he couldn't answer, and then the report, the incident report with his name on it as site supervisor, the report that said negligence, that said Sandoval ordered the overtopping test against protocol, the report that made him the eleventh death's cause and ten lives' ruin. He had accepted it the way you accept weather. It had happened. He must have done it. The mind protects itself; everyone said so; Mara Voss said so most publicly, standing in front of cameras with the report held flat toward the lenses.

But now, in the dream, he was on the bank, and there were lights.

Not rescue lights. Not the sweeping beams of search teams working the debris field downstream, though those existed too, far off, small as fireflies. These lights were close. Two of them, handheld, coming down the bank toward him with the unhurried gait of men who are not searching for anything because they already know where everything is.

He tried to move. His body answered with static. Cold had gotten into the machinery of him somewhere below the skin and seized it. He could see his own hands in the beam spill—torn, blue-gray, shaking—and beyond them two pairs of boots stopping an arm's length away. Rubber boots. Company boots, the kind with the orange soles, the kind every man at the Ceniza project wore, the kind Tomás had hated because they marked you as management even when you weren't.

"Here," one of them said. Not alarmed. Almost bored. "Told you."

A crouch. A face resolving out of the dark behind a headlamp, and Ansel's waking mind scrabbled at it—*I know you, I know your face*—and got nothing, the way you get nothing when you try to read a word printed too close. Just a jaw, stubbled, and eyes that held no surprise at finding a drowned man breathing.

"He's got a concussion," the second voice said, from farther off. "Look at his pupils."

"So did the plan account for." The first man laughed at his own joke, or maybe at nothing. "Get the folder."

*Folder.* The word landed in the dream like a stone in still water, and the ripples went backward through ten years of Ansel's life, rearranging everything they touched.

Because folders meant paper, and paper meant someone had written something before the night ended, before the bodies were counted, before the water had even finished falling. Someone had written something while Ansel was still in the river.

Hands took his arms. Not gently. They dragged him up the bank above the flood line, into the lee of a boulder, and propped him sitting, and his head lolled, and through the curtain of his own hair he watched one of them kneel and open a document wallet and click a pen.

"Sandoval. Hey. Sandoval." Fingers tapped his cheek, hard enough to sting even through the numbness. "Stay with me. This won't take a minute."

"What—" His voice came out ruined, gravel and water. "Tomás. My brother's—"

"Your brother's accounted for." Flat. Final. A door closing on a room he wasn't allowed to enter yet. "Now listen. You're the site supervisor. You know that. You signed the overtopping authorization last Tuesday—you remember signing it?"

"No."

"You signed it last Tuesday." The same words, same tone, a recording played twice. "You ordered the test. Protocol deviation. Your call. That's what happened here, and that's what this says, and you're going to put your thumb right here at the bottom, because your hands are too cut up to hold a pen, poor bastard, and we're going to get you warm and get you to a doctor, and tomorrow this will all just be a bad day at work. Understand?"

Ansel, in the dream, in the memory, in whatever place this was where the two had finally become the same thing, understood with terrible clarity that he was being offered a trade. Not the one Reyes would offer him decades later on a moraine—though the shape of it was identical, the same architecture, the same company built to the same blueprint. This trade was simpler. Warmth and survival and a story in which he was guilty instead of convenient. Guilt was the price of the blanket. Guilt was what they wanted him to be, because a guilty man asks no questions about who else was on the bank that night, and who sent them, and why the folder was already written.

"I didn't order any test," he heard himself say. Slurred. Stubborn. Even half-dead, some part of him was still his father's son.

The man sighed, the sigh of a foreman dealing with a stubborn mule, and nodded to the other one, and something pressed over Ansel's mouth and nose—a cloth, chemical-sweet—and the dream went soft at the edges, and the last thing he saw clearly was the thumbprint card held steady in front of his face, and the last thing he felt was his own hand lifted like a dead man's, his own thumb pressed down into ink, onto paper, onto the rest of his life.

And underneath the sweetness, going down, he heard the first man say—not to him, to the other one, conversationally, the way men talk over a sleeping passenger:

"Voss girl's testimony will seal it. She saw him at the valve house. Everyone saw what they were told to see."

* * *

He woke with his fists closed so hard his nails had cut his palms.

The hut was gray-blue with pre-dawn. Frost furred the inside of the window glass. Somewhere outside, wind moved through the moraine with its long empty vowel sound, and Teo slept sitting up against the document crates with his arms crossed like a man guarding a vault even unconscious, and Ilsa was already awake, watching him from her bunk with the particular stillness she used for patients coming out of anesthesia.

"You screamed," she said quietly. "Twice. Then you talked."

"What did I say?"

"'I didn't sign anything.' Over and over." She paused. "Then you said a name. Reyes."

The name sat in the cold air between them. Ansel looked at his palms. Four crescents of blood, neat as stitching.

"It wasn't a dream," he said. His voice sounded foreign to him, scraped down to studs. "It was a memory. The whole hour. All of it." He flexed his hands open, closed. "Ilsa, I was on the bank. They pulled me out themselves. Company men. They drugged me and they put my thumbprint on a report that was written before I was out of the water."

Ilsa didn't move for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had the careful evenness of someone handling something that might detonate. "You're sure. Hypoxic dreams can feel exactly like—"

"They called me by name before I could talk. They knew my pupils were blown. One of them mentioned—" and here his own voice betrayed him, cracked down the middle, "—mentioned Mara. Said her testimony would seal it. Said everyone saw what they were told to see."

Outside, the wind rose and fell. In the corner, Teo stirred but didn't wake.

"Ten years," Ansel said. "Ten years I built my whole life on top of a lie I thought I'd written myself. Every border that closed, every job nobody else would take, every time Pico looks at me like I'm somebody worth becoming—I earned all of it on paper that was already dry before I ever touched it." He laughed, and it came out wrong, a sound with no humor in it, just pressure escaping. "They didn't just take the valley and the eleven. They took the truth and made me carry its corpse around the continent for a decade."

"Ansel." Ilsa swung her legs down, crossed to him, and did the thing she did better than anyone he'd ever known: she took his wrist, two fingers on the pulse, and simply stayed there, grounding him in the plain fact of a body still working. "Breathe. Then tell me exactly what you remembered. All of it. Details matter—faces, clothing, sequence. If we're putting this in the record, it goes in precise."

"The record." He almost smiled. "There's going to be a record?"

"There are four crates of surveys in this hut proving an engineered disaster, and a crew that voted to die on this mountain rather than walk away from it." Her grip tightened fractionally. "Yes. There's going to be a record. So start at the bank."

So he started at the bank, and told it all, and she wrote it in her medic's shorthand by headlamp, and the telling did what telling does—it made the thing realer and lighter at once, the way a wound hurts more after it's been cleaned. The rubber boots with orange soles. The folder. The cloth over his face. The sentence about Voss. The thumbprint, lifted and pressed like a stamp on a shipment.

When he finished, Ilsa sat back. "The man whose face you can't see. Think harder. Not the features—the context. Where would you have seen him before that night?"

Ansel closed his eyes and let the memory run again, forward this time instead of backward, past the thumbprint, past the darkness, to the hospital, to the ministry men, to the hearings—to the hearing where a young engineer named Mara Voss had stood at the witness table with her hands flat on the wood and testified, voice steady, that she'd seen Ansel Sandoval at the valve house in the hour before the failure. And seated behind her in the gallery, taking notes, wearing the same rubber-soled boots—

"There," he said. "He was at the inquiry. Taking notes for the consortium's legal team. He was never security. He was their cleaner from the start—he wrote the report, planted the witnesses' understanding, testified-adjacent, and then he's spent ten years making sure the site stays sealed." He opened his eyes. "He's not guarding the valley, Ilsa. He's guarding the version of events. And now they're demolishing the ruin because the ruin is the last physical witness left."

"And you," Ilsa said, "are the second-to-last."

* * *

Teo woke to the tail of it, and Teo, being Teo, went straight to arithmetic.

"Say it's true—and I believe you, for the record, I've believed you since the hut documents—but say it's true. Then your blacklist isn't shame. It's evidence." He turned his battered notebook to a fresh page, pen already moving. "A forged incident report, coerced under sedation, entered into official record. That's not just a scandal, that's criminal fraud by a corporate entity across three jurisdictions. The moment that thumbprint card surfaces—or even your testimony of it, corroborated—the warrants don't dissolve, they invert. You stop being the suspect. You become the surviving victim of a documented cover-up." He looked up, and behind the exhaustion his eyes had the bright hard gleam they got when a structure was load-bearing and holding. "Ansel. Do you understand what you are? You're not the man who caused it. You're the only living proof it was caused."

Ansel stood, because sitting still had become impossible, and went to the frosted window, and scraped a hole in the ice with his thumbnail, and looked out at the glacier wall glowing faintly under the last stars.

For ten years he had carried a specific weight, and he had assumed the weight was himself—his guilt, his failure, his one unforgivable hour. Men build their spines around a weight like that. They hunch. They stop moving toward things. They become excellent at leaving, because staying means being seen carrying it.

And now the weight had turned over in his hands and shown him its underside, and the underside said: *this was never yours.*

It should have felt like setting it down. Instead it felt like being handed something heavier. Because if the guilt was manufactured, then the grief was raw material—they had needed eleven dead to bury the tailings leak, and his brother had been not a casualty of accident but a line item, an inconvenience, a cost of doing business. And Ansel had spent a decade apologizing, in his whole flinching way of life, to men who should have been apologizing to him, on their knees, in court, in front of the eleven names.

Vindication, he discovered, does not arrive like sunlight. It arrives like a crate dropped on you from height. You stand there under it, stunned, checking whether anything broke.

"Teo," he said, without turning from the window. "The thumbprint card. The original report. If the consortium kept copies—and companies like this always keep copies, leverage dies otherwise—where would they be?"

Teo flipped pages. "Same place as everything else they're erasing. Under the dam."

"Then we're not finished." He turned around. In the gray light his crew saw something new in his face, and Pico—who had woken sometime during the talking and was sitting up in his bag, silent, wide-eyed—would remember it for the rest of his life: not the legendary guide, not the blacklisted ghost, but a man whose grief had just changed state, the way water changes state, from something that flows around obstacles into something that breaks them. "We came for the surveys. The surveys prove the how. But my brother cached something at the source—we haven't found it yet—and somewhere under that ruin is the original report with my forged print on it, and probably the overtopping order itself, signed by whoever actually gave it." He picked up his jacket. "Reyes thinks he's demolishing evidence. He's demolishing our evidence. We're going to go take theirs."

Pico scrambled up. Ilsa was already repacking the medical kit. Only Teo hesitated, pen hovering.

"And Mara?" he asked carefully. "She's still in Reyes's hands. Whatever we do next happens faster because she's giving them a reason to watch the checkpoint instead of the high routes. We can't leave her there."

Ansel looked at the window, at the glacier burning faintly in the pre-dawn, and thought of a younger Mara Voss with her hands flat on the witness table, saying words she'd been told to see, and spending ten years doubting them.

"We're not leaving anyone on this mountain," he said. "Gear up. We move in twenty minutes—and Teo, get me that radio schedule. If her guard contact has kept any channel open, I want to be listening when she calls."

As if the mountain had been waiting for the decision, the eastern horizon split, and the first light came down the icefall like something falling, and the day—the demolition day, the last day—began.
