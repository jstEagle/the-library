## 24. Rescue or Recapture

The cell smelled of diesel and cold concrete, and Mara Voss had been counting the hours by the sound of a generator that coughed every eleven minutes.

She'd been in worse places. A jail in Ushuaia with a flooded floor. A holding room in La Paz where they'd taken her boots and she'd spent two days learning to hate tile. But those had been ordinary detentions, the kind with paperwork at the end of them, a stamp, a ride to the border, a shrug. This was not that. This was a shipping container welded into a checkpoint at the throat of the Río Ceniza gorge, and the man who ran it—Reyes—had looked at her through the door slot with the flat patience of someone who had all the time in the world and no intention of using any of it.

"You've been here before," he'd said. "You were released before."

"I was released because I'd done nothing worth keeping."

"Then you have nothing to worry about." And he'd closed the slot like a man closing a ledger.

That had been thirty-one generator-coughs ago. Maybe nine hours. She sat on the steel bench bolted to the wall and did what she always did when the world narrowed: she made lists. Inventory of what she knew. Inventory of what she'd told them. Inventory of what Ansel's crew would be doing right now—portaging past the checkpoint in the fog while she stood in the open wearing her own name as bait, exactly as she'd proposed, exactly as she'd known she would regret.

It had worked. That was the bitter arithmetic of it. The patrols had converged on her, lights and shouting, and the others had slipped upriver through the white silence like ghosts paying a debt. She'd bought their passage with her body, and now the body was the only currency she had left, and Reyes hadn't even named a price yet.

Her shoulder ached where a contractor had wrenched it down onto gravel. Her ribs on the left side sang a low note every time she breathed deep. She catalogued those too. A medic's habit she'd picked up from Ilsa without ever admitting it.

The generator coughed. Eleven minutes.

Somewhere beyond the container wall, boots on gravel, a radio murmuring numbers. Then quieter. Then a different set of footsteps—heavier, slower, the walk of a man who wasn't in a hurry because he belonged here—and stopped outside her door.

Keys. Not many keys; one key, used once.

The lock turned and the door swung open on a young man in company gray, mid-twenties maybe, built like a fence post, with a face that seemed assembled out of other people's grief. He held a canteen in one hand and didn't look at her when he set it on the bench.

"Water," he said. "Reyes said you're to stay hydrated. He wants you coherent."

"Generous of him."

The young man's jaw tightened. He turned to go, and Mara, who had spent twenty years reading people in mountain light and storm dark, read him in the three seconds the lantern gave her: the way his eyes flicked to her left ribs, the way his hand stayed on the door frame a half-second too long, the way he breathed through his nose like a man carrying something heavy uphill.

"You knew Tomás Sandoval," she said.

He stopped.

"The guide. Ten years ago. He worked this valley."

"I don't know what you're talking about."

"Your face does," Mara said quietly. "I've seen it before. On men who lost someone up here."

For a long moment the only sound was the generator, coughing its eleven-minute cough somewhere in the dark. Then the young man stepped inside and pulled the door shut behind him—not locked, just closed—and stood there with his back against it, and Mara saw that his hands were shaking.

"My name is Ezequiel Barrientos," he said. "My father was Julián Barrientos. He fished the Ceniza below the dam. He was fifty-one years old, and he couldn't swim, and when the water came he climbed onto the roof of the pump house with six other men, and the roof held for four minutes." His voice didn't break. It just went very flat and very careful, the way voices do when breaking is not an affordable option. "Four minutes. I've done the math my whole life. Four minutes is nothing. Four minutes is how long it takes to boil water."

Mara said nothing. There was nothing to say that wouldn't be either a lie or a wound.

"They told us it was an accident," Ezequiel said. "Geological. Unforeseeable. They said it at the memorial, and they paid for the coffins, and my mother took the money because there were four children, and I grew up and I needed work, and the only work in this watershed is theirs. So I put on the gray uniform and I guard the place that killed my father, because the alternative is leaving, and everything we are is buried here." He looked at her then, and his eyes were dry and terrible. "Is it true? What they say about Ansel Sandoval? That he signed the report?"

"No," Mara said. "I testified that it was true. And I have spent ten years being wrong about it in ways I'm still finding out."

Ezequiel studied her the way she'd study a route—looking for the hold that would take weight and the hold that would shear off. Whatever he found, it decided him. He reached inside his jacket and brought out a ring of keys, and from it he took one, and set it on the bench beside the canteen.

"Generator cycle is eleven minutes," he said. "When it coughs, the perimeter cameras drop for six seconds. Old wiring, north corner, everyone knows and nobody fixes it. The night shift changes at two. Reyes sleeps in the operations trailer and he takes a sedative because the altitude gives him headaches—you didn't hear that from me." He was talking faster now, a man stepping off a cliff in words. "There's a supply trail on the east wall. It's steep but it bypasses the lower switchbacks. If you gain the moraine shelf by dawn you're above the patrol line. They don't look up. Nobody looks up; that's why the mountains keep killing us."

"Why?" Mara asked. Not why are you doing this—she understood why, she could feel it radiating off him like heat off stone—but the other why, the practical one. "Why me? Why now?"

Ezequiel's hand went to his chest, to a shape under the uniform shirt, a photograph in a plastic sleeve worn soft at the edges. "Because ten years ago a man came down this river with a broken arm and a bag full of papers, and he stopped at our house, and he gave my mother money he didn't have, and he said, 'Someone has to finish this.' And then he went back up." His voice finally cracked, one hairline fracture, immediately sealed. "Tomás Sandoval died trying to bring the truth out of this valley. You're going to finish it for him. That's the whole reason. There isn't another one."

* * *

She waited through forty-one coughs of the generator, which was five hours and change, which was the longest arithmetic of her life.

At two-ten the camp went quiet in the particular way camps do when the watch changes—the overlap of briefings, the thermos ritual, the small human slack in a machine. Mara stripped the blanket for warmth, wrapped it around her shoulders like a poncho, and listened until she could map the camp by sound: the generator shed, the ops trailer humming, two men talking by the vehicle pool, a third walking a slow circuit whose rhythm she counted until she owned it.

The lock opened without complaint. The container door moved on hinges someone had oiled recently, and she noted that too—someone had been maintaining this cell, keeping it functional, and the thought of who and why made her skin crawl.

Six seconds of camera darkness began with the generator's cough. She crossed the open ground between the containers in five, low and fast, the blanket swallowing her outline, and slid into the shadow of the fuel drums as the cameras woke to an empty corridor of gravel. Her ribs lit up white on the left side. She breathed through it. Pain was information; information was survivable.

The east wall trail started behind the maintenance shed, a scar of switchbacks stitched up a slope of talus and scrub. No lights marked it. Of course not—the company didn't want its own people wandering off the leash. Which meant the trail was hers alone, and the mountain was hers alone, and for the first time since the checkpoint lights had swept her down, Mara Voss felt something that wasn't fear.

It lasted until the third switchback, when the floodlight from the ops trailer swept the slope and she flattened herself against scree that bit through the blanket, and she heard, distantly, a voice raised in alarm—the discovery of the empty cell, the shout going up, radios waking across the camp like a nest of insects.

They would search the obvious routes first. The road. The riverbank. The lower gorge. Reyes was thorough but conventional; he thought in corridors. Mara thought in contours. She climbed.

The trail was worse than it looked, which was saying something, because it had looked like a goat's nightmare. In places it simply ceased, washed out by seasons of melt, and she went up on hands and knees over rock polished slick by frost. Twice the slope tried to give her back to the checkpoint below, and twice she refused, wedging herself into cracks, moving only when the mountain allowed. The blanket she abandoned at the fourth switchback—it snagged and she let it go rather than fight it, watching it fall away below her like a shed skin.

Altitude came for her around four thousand meters. The air thinned to a rumor and her lungs burned and her head pounded in time with her pulse, and still she climbed, because below her the flashlights were multiplying, sweeping the valley floor in patterns she could read like sentences: *we think she went down-river, we think she's still inside the wire, we think she is a problem with a known shape.* Let them think it. Every hour they spent searching the wrong elevation was an hour Ansel's crew gained toward the source.

Toward Tomás's truth. Toward whatever was buried under that dam ruin that Reyes wanted erased so badly he'd sealed a whole watershed with guns.

Near dawn the trail crested onto the moraine shelf, and Mara allowed herself ten minutes against a boulder the size of a chapel, shaking, drinking snowmelt from a hollow in the rock, eating the last protein bar from her jacket pocket—a habit of never letting her pockets go empty that had kept her alive on four continents. The sky in the east had gone the color of a healing bruise. Below and behind her, the checkpoint was a scatter of lights, still searching, still looking down.

Above her, the glacier wall rose into cloud, blue-white and indifferent, and somewhere beyond it was the source, and Ansel, and the end of a story that had begun with eleven names.

She got up. Her left side had gone from singing to screaming—she was fairly sure now it was more than bruised, possibly a cracked rib grinding with every deep breath—and her hands were cut to ribbons from the rock, and none of it mattered, because the high camp was close. Teo had described it to her in the planning sessions: the hollow below the icefall, sheltered from the katabatic wind, where the crew would stage for the final push. Two hours, maybe three, if the moraine held.

The moraine did not hold.

* * *

It happened on the descent into the hollow, a stretch of unstable rock frozen overnight and thawing now in the first sun, and it happened fast, the way the mountains always do—one moment her boot was on solid ground and the next the entire slope shrugged, a slab the size of a door releasing beneath her, and she went down with it, sliding, rolling, grabbing for anything, her shoulder slamming stone, her head ringing off something hard enough to fill her vision with static.

She stopped against a boulder field at the bottom, on her back, staring at a sky so blue it looked painted. For a long time she couldn't move. She took inventory instead, because that was what she did: ribs, bad. Shoulder, worse—she could feel the joint sitting wrong, a hot wire of pain from collarbone to elbow. Head, bleeding somewhere above the hairline, blood ticking warm down her temple. Legs, working. Will—

She tested the will. Still there. Stubborn as ever. Good.

She got up in stages, the way you get up at altitude, and she walked, lurching, following the sound of meltwater, because water runs downhill and downhill was where the camp was, and because if she stopped moving now the mountain would win by default.

She heard the camp before she saw it—the clink of a stove, a voice, Pico's laugh, young and bright and absurdly alive—and she came around a spur of rock with blood on her face and her arm cradled against her chest, and the laughter stopped.

"Mara." Ansel Sandoval was on his feet first, crossing the distance between them with that long guide's stride, and the last thing she saw before her knees decided the journey was over was his face—ten years of hatred and rivalry and grief rearranging itself, feature by feature, into something she had never once seen him wear.

Fear. For her.

Then the ground came up and Ilsa's hands were there before it arrived, and the sky tilted away, and the last thing Mara Voss thought, as the world folded down to fire and cold and the murmur of voices deciding what to do with her, was:

*We were enemies for ten years, and it took this valley to show us we were on the same side of the water all along.*

* * *

Pain brought her back, and with it Ilsa's voice, low and steady, narrating her own work the way she always did—"strap's tight, tell me if your fingers go numb"—and Mara opened her eyes to canvas and cook-smoke and the amber glow of a lantern inside a tent that smelled of altitude and human endurance.

"Welcome back." Ilsa knelt beside her, sleeves rolled, forearms streaked with iodine. "Cracked rib, dislocated shoulder—I put it back in while you were making extremely creative noises—concussion, assorted lacerations. You fell down a mountain and lived. Either you're lucky or the mountain's getting sloppy."

"How long?"

"Six hours. You slept like the dead. We nearly buried you out of habit." Ilsa pressed a cup of something hot and terrible into her good hand. "Ansel hasn't left the tent entrance since they carried you in. He's on his fourth circuit of blaming himself. I'd intervene, but honestly it's the most emotionally available he's been in years."

Mara drank. The broth scalded and soothed in equal measure. Through the tent flap she could see dusk settling over the moraine, the glacier wall above them catching the last light like a blade, and Ansel standing at the edge of the camp with his back to her, motionless, watching the icefall where the storm was already beginning to build—a smudge of white violence crawling over the col.

"Ezequiel," she said. "The guard who got me out. His father was Julián Barrientos. One of the eleven. He gave me the cameras, the shift change, Reyes's schedule. He gave me everything, knowing what it would cost him." She swallowed. "And Reyes will know it was him within a day. Sooner. We can't leave him behind in that camp, Ilsa. Whatever we do next—we do it knowing there's a man down there waiting to find out what happens to traitors."

Ilsa was quiet for a moment. "Ansel already knows. Teo told him while you were out. He's been standing there ever since."

Mara looked at the figure against the dying light—at the set of his shoulders, the stillness of a man who had spent a decade running from this valley and was now standing in its teeth, choosing.

"And?" she asked.

Ilsa followed her gaze upward, to the icefall, where the storm was gathering itself like something being born.

"He says we climb tonight," Ilsa said. "Before the storm closes the route. Before Reyes regroups. He says Tomás waited ten years, and he's done making anyone wait."
