## 25. The Glacier Wall

Pico had learned to read Ansel Sandoval the way sailors read weather—not by what he said, but by what changed in him. The set of his shoulders. The way his hands went quiet on a rope. And in the two days since they'd climbed out of the gorge onto the moraine, since Ilsa had gone gray and stumbling with altitude and Teo had come back to them with a bruise like a plum blooming across his ribs, Pico had been watching a change he didn't like at all.

Ansel was afraid.

Not the working fear Pico knew from rapids and rockfall, the fear that sharpened a man into something better. This was older. It sat in him like a stone in a boot, and it had gotten heavier with every kilometer of glacier they'd gained.

Now they stood at the base of the icefall, and Pico understood why.

It rose out of the white like a frozen explosion—a thousand tons of blue ice shattered mid-fall and locked there, seracs the size of houses leaning against each other in attitudes of imminent collapse, crevasses splitting the slope in black seams that swallowed the light you threw into them. Meltwater ticked somewhere deep inside it, a sound like a clock counting down in a language nobody spoke. Above it all, the storm was coming down off the summit ridge in a gray wall, dragging its skirt of spindrift behind it.

"We go around?" Ilsa asked. She was wrapped in every layer she owned, her frost-nipped fingers tucked into her armpits, her voice still rough from three days of coughing at altitude.

"There is no around," Ansel said. "The valley walls close to vertical on both sides past this point. The icefall is the door."

"Then we wait for the storm to pass."

"If we wait, we lose the night. And we don't have nights to spare." He said it flatly, without looking at any of them, the way a man reads a verdict he's already memorized. "Demolition day is tomorrow. Reyes thinks everything below is already buried. Everything above—" he nodded at the icefall, at whatever waited beyond it— "is on his schedule too. We climb now or we don't climb at all."

Teo shifted his weight and winced, one hand pressed to his ribs. "Roped travel through that, in worsening visibility, with four climbers and one of them half-sick." He wasn't arguing. He was counting, the way he counted everything, and Pico could see the arithmetic coming out bad. "Ansel. Be honest with us."

For a long moment Ansel didn't answer. He stared up at the icefall, and Pico watched something move behind his eyes—not calculation, not route-finding, but memory. It was the look of a man watching a film only he could see.

"I've been here before," Ansel said quietly.

The wind filled the silence. Ilsa looked at Teo. Teo looked at Pico.

"Ten years ago," Ansel went on. "The night Tomás died. I came up this way—or part of this way. Looking for him." His jaw worked. "I remember pieces. A serac field. A bridge of snow over a crevasse. The sound the ice makes when it's about to let go." He turned away from the wall as if it were a face he couldn't hold eye contact with. "I remember enough to know how to get through. That's the problem."

"That's the problem how?" Pico asked.

"Because the last time I was here, the mountain killed my brother." Ansel's voice didn't rise at all, which somehow made it worse. "And some part of me has spent ten years believing that if I ever came back, it would finish the job."

Nobody said anything for a moment. The storm dragged closer; the light went the color of dirty pewter.

Then Pico heard himself speak. "Then I lead."

* * *

They argued about it for twenty minutes, which in expedition time was an eternity, and Pico won by refusing to lose.

"You've never led ice," Ansel said. "Not like this."

"I've led everything you've taught me. You taught me everything." Pico kept his voice level, kept his eyes on Ansel's, because he'd learned that too—the younger version of himself would have looked down at his boots and lost the argument before it started. "You said once, on the Apurímac, that a guide who can't hand over the sharp end isn't a guide. He's a bottleneck. Your words."

"My words are going to get you killed."

"Your words kept me alive for six years. Let them keep working." He held out his hand for the rack of screws. "You know the route. Call it from behind. But your body doesn't believe in this place, Ansel. Mine does. Mine doesn't have anything here to be afraid of except falling—and I'm not planning to fall."

Ilsa checked his harness twice, her cold fingers efficient despite their tremor, and said nothing, which from Ilsa was a kind of blessing. Teo tightened the rope coils and murmured, "Four points of contact, thirty meters apart. If anyone shouts 'ice,' everyone drops and digs in. No heroics." Then, quieter, just for Pico: "Your father would have liked you."

"I don't have a father."

"You do. You've had one for six years. He's just too stupid to notice." Teo clipped him onto the line. "Go."

Ansel stood last in the rope order, and when Pico glanced back before stepping onto the ice, he saw the big man's face stripped of everything he usually wore—the competence, the wry distance, the armor of a man always halfway to somewhere else. What was left underneath was simpler and much younger: a boy from a mining town watching someone walk into the place where his brother died.

"Lead true," Ansel said. Just that.

Pico stepped onto the glacier.

* * *

The first hour was almost gentle, and Pico distrusted it completely.

The lower angle of the icefall was a maze of ridges and shallow moats, wind-carved into shapes like the ribs of some drowned animal, and he moved through it the way Ansel had drilled into him for six years: test, commit, move. Test, commit, move. Never stand still long enough for the mountain to take an interest in you. Behind him the rope paid out in soft arcs, and he could feel the others through it—not their weight exactly, but their rhythm, each of them a pulse traveling down the line. When he stopped, they stopped. When he flowed, they flowed.

That was the thing nobody told you about leading: you were never alone. Every decision traveled down the rope and became four people's reality. Pico had spent years being the last man on the line, the kid, the one carried. Now every placement of his boots wrote itself onto three other bodies, and the weight of it should have crushed him.

Instead it made him clear.

He found the first real crevasse forty minutes in—a black seam two meters wide cutting across their line, its lips undercut into fragile flutes of hollow ice. He probed it with his axe, listening, feeling the mountain talk back through the shaft. Solid on the far lip. He swung across on his tools, planted a screw anchor, and brought them over one at a time, Ilsa crossing with her teeth bared and her breath tearing in ragged white flags, Teo moving carefully, protecting his ribs, Ansel last and fluid as water, his body remembering even while his mind fought it.

"Good line," Ansel said as Pico hauled him over the lip. Two words. From Ansel, a decoration ceremony.

The storm hit them at the top of the lower section.

It didn't arrive so much as descend—one moment the world was gray and the next it was nothing, a whiteout so total that Pico's own axes vanished when he raised them, his boots ghosts beneath him, the rope ahead and behind dissolving into the void. The wind came in pulses now, hard enough to lean on, carrying ice crystals that stung like thrown sand. Somewhere above them, deep in the icefall, something boomed—a serac calving, the sound arriving through the ice itself, felt in the teeth more than heard.

"Hold!" Pico shouted, and dropped to his knees, driving both tools in, and felt the three impacts behind him as the team anchored.

In the white blindness, crouched on a slope of living ice with the wind trying to peel him off the mountain, Pico waited for the fear to come.

It didn't. What came instead was Ansel's voice, six years old in his memory, on a riverbank in the rain: *The river doesn't care how brave you are. It cares whether you're paying attention. So pay attention, and you'll live forever.*

"Pico." Ansel's voice through the storm, close, muffled. "Talk to me. Where's the line?"

"Right side bends left around the serac—there's a snow bridge, I marked it on the way up." He realized he was shouting the map from memory, drawing the invisible route in the air. "Thirty meters, then the angle steepens. Follow my axe strikes—you'll hear them."

And then Pico did the thing that six years ago would have been unthinkable: he moved out blind, into the white, striking his tools into ice he couldn't see, trusting the route he'd read on the approach, trusting his own memory of it, trusting the taut line behind him that meant three lives riding on his judgment.

He heard Ansel's axe strikes following his own, echo for echo, like a heartbeat learning a new rhythm.

* * *

The snow bridge nearly killed them all.

It appeared out of the whiteout exactly where Pico remembered it—a sagging lens of packed snow spanning a crevasse wider than the road into town, its surface sculpted by wind into a smooth gray hump. Too smooth. Pico froze at its edge, probing, and the probe sank forty centimeters into something with no spine.

"It's rotten," he called back. "Thermal melt inside. It might hold one at a time, it might not hold anyone."

"Options?" Teo shouted over the wind.

Pico looked left. The crevasse ran on into the murk. Right, same. Down—the way they'd come, hours of it, back into the teeth of the schedule they couldn't afford. Up—

Up. There. Where the bridge sagged lowest, a serac leaned over the gap from the upper side, its base forming an overhanging wall of dense blue ice. If he could climb it—fifteen meters of steep, aerated ice, in a whiteout, at five thousand meters with a storm chewing on them—it would bypass the bridge entirely. Descend the other side onto solid ground.

"It's steep," he said. "Vertical-ish. I'd be climbing above the crevasse on bad protection. If I fall, I fall into the gap."

Silence on the rope. Then Ansel's voice, and Pico heard something in it he'd never heard before: not command, not caution. Trust, offered raw and unarmored, like a man handing over a weapon he'd carried so long he'd forgotten how to put it down.

"Your call, Pico. You're the leader. Whatever you decide, we're with you."

Later, Pico would try to explain to himself why those words did what they did—why they landed in his chest like a lit match. All he understood in the moment was that Ansel Sandoval, the man whose approval he'd chased for six years like a dog chasing a truck, had just handed him the wheel in the worst hour of the worst day, and meant it.

"Climbing," Pico said.

He swung across the gap on his tools, hooked the serac's base, and began to climb.

The ice was terrible—hollow in places, his picks tearing free with sounds like ripping cloth, the aerated texture offering no honest purchase. He placed a screw, weighted it gently, watched it creak. Placed another higher. The wind gusted and tried to swing him off the wall like a coat off a hook, and he hung there by two picks and his fingertips, breathing, paying attention, alive forever. Below and behind him the crevasse breathed its cold breath upward, patient as gravity.

Fifteen meters took forty minutes. When he finally rolled over the serac's crest and found solid ground beyond, he lay on the ice gasping, laughing, crying, all three tangled together, while he built an anchor and brought his family across the void one at a time—Ilsa swearing steadily in German under her breath the whole way, Teo grunting with pain on every pull, and Ansel last, rising over the crest like something surfacing from deep water.

Ansel knelt beside him in the storm, gripped the back of his neck the way you grip a son, and pressed his forehead briefly against Pico's.

"Where did you learn to lead like that?" he asked.

"You know where."

* * *

Above the serac, the icefall relented. The angle eased, the crevasses grew polite, and within the hour they crawled out of the storm's lower teeth onto a shoulder of wind-scoured névé with the sky beginning, impossibly, to thin. Through a tear in the clouds, the light of late afternoon fell gold across the upper glacier, and there—beyond a final rise, guarded by a wall of ice that burned like stained glass—lay the pass to the source valley.

They stopped. They had to. Ilsa's oxygen-starved lungs needed the rest, and Teo needed to re-tape his ribs, and all of them needed to simply exist for ten minutes without the mountain trying to remove them from it.

Pico sat with his back against his pack, watching Ansel.

The big man stood apart, facing the ice wall at the head of the glacier, and the low sun set it alight behind him so that he stood silhouetted in fire. He was looking at the pass the way a man looks at a door he buried someone behind. His hand had drifted unconsciously to his chest, pressing flat, the way he sometimes did in his sleep when the dreams came—the missing hour knocking.

"Hey." Pico got up, crossed the snow, stood beside him. "What's up there? Really?"

Ansel was quiet a long time. The wind had dropped to a whisper; the glacier ticked and settled beneath them like a house at night.

"When I was sixteen," he said finally, "Tomás took me up onto a glacier near our town. Smaller than this. He taught me to read the ice—the blue veins, the air bubbles, the layers. He said the glacier keeps everything. Every year, every storm, every disaster. It writes it all down and never loses a page." His thumb moved against his chest, against the memory under it. "He believed that. That nothing true gets lost. It just waits."

"And you didn't believe him."

"I wanted to." Ansel's voice was very quiet. "But I remembered the wrong things for ten years. I remembered guilt because guilt was easier than the truth—that maybe I saw something that night and let it be taken from me. Maybe I signed something. Maybe—" He stopped. Shook his head. "If the glacier keeps everything, then it kept Tomás. And it kept whatever happened to me. And I have spent a decade afraid to open the book."

Pico looked at the burning wall of ice, at the pass beyond it where the forbidden valley waited with its demolition crews and its buried dead and its half-buried cache of evidence.

"So open it," he said.

Ansel looked down at him then, and something in his face shifted—the stone in the boot finally shifting loose after ten years of walking on it. He put his arm around Pico's shoulders, heavy as a beam, and for a moment neither of them said anything, and nothing needed saying.

Then Ansel stiffened.

His arm tightened—not around Pico, but pulling him down, turning him, and Pico followed his gaze across the valley floor below the pass, toward the dark scree slopes descending toward the source.

Lights. Moving lights, three of them, sweeping in disciplined arcs across the lower slopes. Headlamps of a sweep team, methodical as lighthouse beams, working their way along the base of the glacier wall.

Between the crew and Tomás's cache, between the crew and everything.

"They're not patrolling," Ansel breathed. "They're searching. Something spooked them." He turned, and all the softness had gone out of his face, replaced by the flat calm Pico had seen only twice before—once before a Class V rapid, once facing down a patrol. "Storm's clearing. We move now, tonight, over the top while they're looking low. Pico—"

"Yeah?"

"Still feel like leading?"

Pico looked at the lights below, at the burning wall above, at the three faces of his family turning toward him out of the fading gold.

"Every step," he said.
