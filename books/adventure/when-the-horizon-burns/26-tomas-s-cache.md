## 26. Tomás's Cache

The crevasse opened in the glacier like a mouth that had been waiting ten years to speak.

Pico found it first—of course he did, the boy had a nose for weakness in ice the way some men had a nose for money—and by the time I reached him he was already lying flat at the lip, headlamp cutting down into blue dark, saying nothing, which from Pico was louder than shouting.

"Talk to me," I said.

"You need to see this."

I dropped to my belly beside him and looked down into the throat of the glacier, and what I saw made the cold of the moraine feel like nothing at all.

There was a floor to it—not far down, maybe four meters, a shelf where the ice had pinched and healed over some older fracture—and on that shelf sat things that did not belong to any glacier. A rope coil, bleached gray with age but still coiled with the particular discipline of a man who'd been taught that sloppy ropes kill. A red dry bag, sun-faded to the color of old blood. And propped against the wall of ice behind them, catching Pico's light and throwing it back in a dull orange gleam, an aluminum crate stenciled with letters I could read even from above, even through ten years of frost-scour:

CENIZA CONSOLIDADA — SURVEY DIVISION.

My hands went numb before the rest of me did.

"That's company issue," Teo said quietly. He'd come up behind us without sound, the way he always did, forged passport folded into his jacket like a second heartbeat. "That stencil. That's pre-collapse inventory marking."

"I know what it is," I said.

I knew more than what it was. I knew whose knot was holding that rope coil together—a bowline on a bight finished with a half-hitch, the way our father taught us both on the Río Mantaro when we were small enough that the river seemed like a giant and we were its two fleas. I knew whose handwriting would be on whatever tag hung from that dry bag, because Tomás Sandoval labeled everything, obsessively, because he said a man who couldn't find his own gear in the dark deserved to die in the dark.

I knew, and I could not say it yet, because saying it would make it true, and there is a kind of truth that lands on you like a rockfall—you don't get to decide whether it hits, only whether you're under it or beside it when it comes loose.

* * *

We rigged the extraction properly because I insisted, and Ilsa watched me do it with her medic's eyes and said nothing, which was its own kind of diagnosis. Fixed line off Teo's anchor, backed up twice, because if the shelf collapsed under me I wanted the glacier to have to work for it. Storm light was failing above the icefall—we had perhaps three hours of usable weather, and Reyes's sweep teams were somewhere below us in the fog, and every hour we stood still was an hour the demolition crews gained—but I made them wait while I checked the anchor a third time.

"You're stalling," Ilsa said finally. Not unkindly. Ilsa never said anything unkindly; she just said things exactly as they were, which is sometimes worse.

"I'm being thorough."

"You're being a man looking down a well." She crouched next to me at the lip. "Ansel. Whatever's down there, it's been down there for ten years. It can wait another five minutes for you to be ready."

"It can't," I heard myself say. "It already waited too long."

She didn't ask what I meant. That was the thing about Ilsa—she read people the way Teo read load calculations, and she knew when the structure couldn't take more weight.

I went over the edge into the blue.

The cold inside the crevasse was a different animal than the cold outside. Outside, the wind took your heat by force. In here the cold simply was, patient and total, the stored winter of a decade, and my breath came out in slow ghosts that rose past my face and died against the ceiling of ice. My boots found the shelf. It held. The aluminum crate stood at arm's reach, and beside it the dry bag, and beside those—

A jacket. Folded neatly, because of course he would have folded it, laid flat on the ice like a man making his bed before a long sleep. Canvas, olive-drab, patched at the left elbow where he'd torn it on barbed wire outside Juliaca the year before the dam failed, patched with thread that didn't match because our mother had died before she could teach him to care about such things and I hadn't either.

Tomás's jacket.

Not the one I'd seen on the ridge in the fog—that one lived in my head, a ghost, a grief hallucination, something I still hadn't told the others about. This one was real. This one had weight. I picked it up and it was stiff with cold and light as ash and it smelled, impossibly, faintly, of engine oil and river water, the two smells of my brother, and I knelt on the ice of a glacier at five thousand meters and pressed my forehead against it and let the shaking come.

I don't know how long. Long enough that Pico called down once, voice thin with echo: "Ansel? You good?"

"No," I said. "But I'm coming up."

* * *

We opened the crate in the survey hut, because the hut had walls and a door that latched and, after Teo swept it again for transmitters, the illusion of privacy. The storm had arrived while we were hauling the cache up—Reyes's weather or God's, no way to tell anymore—and the wind was doing its best to peel the corrugated roof off, so that we worked inside a drumbeat, a war drum, the same rhythm I imagined driving the demolition crews in the valley below.

Inside the crate: notebooks. Dozens of them, wrapped in plastic, sealed with tape, labeled in Tomás's blocky engineer's hand. Water samples with dates. Tailings seepage measurements going back eighteen months before the failure, each page showing contamination numbers climbing like a fever chart. Photographs in waterproof sleeves—the tailings pond, the cracked containment berm, the pipe discharging gray slurry into a side drainage that fed, eventually, into villages downstream. Villages. People.

And beneath the notebooks, wrapped separately, a hard case containing a digital recorder and three memory cards, each labeled in the same hand:

PROOF — DO NOT TRUST THE COMPANY WITH THIS.
PROOF — TELL ANSEL.
PROOF — HE'LL KNOW WHAT TO DO.

He'll know what to do.

I set that card down on the table very carefully, the way you set down something explosive, and Mara—who had reached high camp two days earlier, wounded, half-frozen, carried in on Pico's back and refusing to discuss how—looked at the label and then looked at me, and I watched her arrive at the same arithmetic I was drowning in.

"He got here first," she said. "Ten years ago. Before the investigation, before the hearings, before—" She stopped. Started again. "Before me. Before any of us. Tomás came up here alone and documented the whole crime and cached the evidence and started carrying it out." Her voice did something I'd never heard it do in twenty years of rivalry: it broke. "And they killed him for it."

"Not here," I said. My own voice sounded like someone else's, scraped raw. "He didn't die here. The flood took him at the dam. At the toe of the ruin, downstream side. They recovered—" I stopped, because I'd never let myself finish sentences about that night, not once in ten years, and now the sentence was coming whether I allowed it or not. "They recovered eleven bodies. His was the eleventh. Found three days later, wedged in the debris fan. He was carrying a sample case. It was empty."

The hut went quiet except for the wind.

"The case was empty," Teo repeated slowly. He was turning one of the notebooks in his hands, reading dates. "Because he'd already brought the first load partway down. And cached the rest." He looked up. "Ansel. Look at the date on the last entry."

I looked. The last notebook entry was dated nine days before the dam failed.

Nine days. My brother had stood at the source of the Ceniza nine days before the company drowned eleven people to hide what he was holding, and then he'd gone back down into the valley, into the teeth of it, to bring the evidence out himself rather than trust it to anyone—to anyone except, apparently, me, according to a memory card label written in ice-cold block letters a decade ago.

"Why didn't he send it out?" Pico asked. He was young enough to ask the honest questions. "Mail it. Give it to a journalist. Why carry it yourself?"

"Because mail gets intercepted," Mara said. "Because journalists get bought. Because in 2014, in that watershed, every road out passed through a company checkpoint, and Tomás knew it." She was staring at the photographs, at the gray water pouring from the pipe. "So he walked it out. Through the gorge. Alone."

"And they were watching him," I said. The thought arrived whole, terrible, fully formed, the way the worst ones do. "He documents everything for months. He caches the bulk of it up here where no patrol will ever look. Then he starts moving samples down in pieces, testing the route. And the company notices. Their own survey division's numbers are walking out of the valley in a fisherman's pack, and they know exactly who's carrying them, and they have a problem they've been trying to solve anyway, because those tailings numbers mean fines and lawsuits and prison for somebody senior." I touched the empty air above the photographs, not quite touching them. "So they stop solving the problem the expensive way. They solve it the cheap way. They break the dam."

"Deliberately," Teo said. Not a question. We'd proven it in the documents from the other hut, the surveys the company missed—the engineered overtopping, the falsified inspection records. But hearing it now, attached to a name, to *my* name, to a man who labeled his gear so he could find it in the dark—it landed differently. It landed like the rockfall.

"They killed him to stop the evidence," I said, "and the evidence is sitting right here, and it waited ten years, and it waited for me."

Nobody spoke. Outside, the storm hammered the roof, and somewhere below us in the dark, machines were eating the ruin of the dam, and somewhere below that, Reyes was sleeping in a heated tent with a radio at his elbow, and none of it mattered as much as the weight of my brother's handwriting in my hands.

* * *

I didn't sleep. I sat by the hut's single window with the recorder in my lap and the memory cards in my breast pocket, against my skin, like they were warm, like they were alive, and I listened to the storm and I did the thing I had spent ten years training myself never to do.

I remembered.

Not the missing hour—I wasn't ready for that, and it didn't come, that hole in me stayed a hole. What came was everything around the hole. Tomás at nineteen, teaching himself hydrology from a stolen textbook because the company wouldn't promote local boys past laborer. Tomás at twenty-four, standing in our mother's kitchen, saying: *they're poisoning the water, Ansel, and everyone downstream drinks it, and nobody upstairs cares because the people downstairs are poor.* Me, at twenty-four, telling him to be careful. Me, at twenty-five, taking the guide contract from the company because the pay was triple and I told myself it was just money, just a season, just a job leading their survey crews up trails I already knew.

Me, on the night of the failure, on the dam access road, on the company payroll, while my brother was somewhere below me in the dark with a sample case full of proof.

The guilt I had carried for ten years—the guilt that had become my spine, my blacklist, my name in three countries' worth of banned-person files—had been built on the belief that I might have caused it. That my hands, my silence, my signature on some report I couldn't remember signing, had helped kill him. I had organized my entire life around that possibility. Never stop moving. Never be known. Never be close enough to anyone for the truth to catch up.

But Tomás hadn't died of my guilt. Tomás had died of their arithmetic. Eleven people inconvenient, one witness dangerous, one dam cheaper broken than fixed. And the man they'd made me believe I was—the coward, the accomplice, the survivor who signed—he was their invention. Twice manufactured: once with a thumbprint I couldn't remember giving, once with a blacklist that turned my shame into a weapon they could aim at anyone who asked questions.

He was ahead of me all along. Ten years ahead. He'd seen the crime coming, climbed past the patrols, crossed the icefall alone with no partner on the rope, cached the truth in the one place on earth colder and more permanent than the company's lies—and then he'd gone back down to fetch it, because he trusted exactly one person to finish what he'd started, and that person had spent a decade running from a ghost story written by the men who killed him.

I put the recorder on the table. I put the cards beside it. I looked at Mara across the dark hut, at her bandaged shoulder, at the bruise still yellowing along her jaw from Reyes's interrogation, and I said the words out loud because saying them made them real and I was done living in the unreal:

"We play it tomorrow. All of it. And then we take everything he carried down off this ice ourselves—every notebook, every card, every photograph—and we finish the walk he started."

Mara nodded once. Teo was already checking batteries. Ilsa began repacking the medical kit for a descent.

Only Pico hesitated. He was standing at the window, looking out into the storm-dark toward the glacier, toward the source, toward the lake and the demolition lights flickering far below like a city of insects.

"Ansel," he said. "The storm breaks at dawn. And look."

He stepped aside from the glass. Far down the valley, through a tear in the cloud, the work lights of the demolition crews had come on early—all of them, the whole ring of the forbidden shore burning white against the black water.

They weren't lighting up to work.

They were lighting up to search.
