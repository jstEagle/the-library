## 27. The Source

They came over the last shoulder of moraine at first light, and the valley opened beneath them like a wound that had learned to hold water.

Ansel stopped walking. Behind him, Pico's boots crunched to a halt; Ilsa's breath rasped in the thin air; Teo set down the pack with the documents and said nothing at all, which from Teo was louder than shouting.

The glacier hung above the valley's headwall like a frozen wave about to break—a wall of blue-green ice two hundred meters high, veined with ash from some eruption a century dead. At its foot, where the ice wept in a hundred silver threads, lay the lake.

The source of the Río Ceniza.

It should have been beautiful. It was beautiful, and that was the obscenity of it. The water was that impossible glacial turquoise that looked lit from within, so still it held the sky perfectly, as if the mountain had kept one honest mirror all these years for no one to look into. Mist moved across its surface in slow ribbons. On the far shore, the ruins of the old dam—Tomás's dam, the dam they'd blamed on Tomás—rose out of the shallows like the ribs of something drowned.

And around the ribs, the vultures were already working.

"Madre de Dios," Pico whispered.

Three excavators. Two rock trucks. A crane barge sitting flat and patient on the lake itself, its cable dipping into the water again and again, hauling up slabs of shattered concrete and swinging them onto a growing mound on the eastern shore. Diesel exhaust hung in a brown smear above the work site, flattening the morning light. Somewhere below the mist, a compressor throbbed like a second heartbeat, out of rhythm with everything alive.

"They're not guarding it," Ilsa said quietly. "They're erasing it."

Ansel had known. He'd known since the hut, since the documents, since Reyes's patrols started making sense. Knowing a thing and seeing it were different animals. Seeing it put claws in you.

Ten years ago this valley had been sealed by men with rifles and a press release: *structural failure, tragic loss of life, site closed pending investigation.* Pending forever. And now, when the world had finally stopped looking, they'd come back not to investigate but to delete—to chew the ruin into gravel and bury whatever the flood hadn't already taken, until even the shape of the lie dissolved.

Eleven people. Eleven names ground under caterpillar treads.

His hands were shaking. He made fists of them.

* * *

They watched from the ridge for an hour while the light strengthened, mapping what could not be unmapped. Teo counted vehicles with binoculars and murmured numbers. Fourteen workers visible, maybe twenty total. One guard post near the access road on the south rim, two pickups, an antenna mast. No dogs. Small mercy.

"The cache," Ansel said finally. His voice came out sanded flat. "Show me again."

Teo unfolded the map they'd drawn from the survey hut documents—the ones the company had missed, the ones that proved the dam hadn't failed. It had been *opened*. Deliberately overtopped, spillway gates jammed, eleven people drowned so the records of toxic tailings leakage would drown with them. The surveys showed the original intake tunnel, the valve house, the coordinates where a 2014 inspection team—Tomás's team—had flagged contamination readings so severe the company had ordered the report destroyed and the inspectors silenced.

One inspector had gotten out with copies. One had cached them somewhere in this valley before he died trying to carry more evidence downriver.

Tomás.

Ansel had spent ten days hauling himself up a river he'd sworn never to look at again because his brother had walked ahead of him the whole way, leaving bread crumbs in the dark. The crevasse shelter three days ago—the supplies, the sealed tubes, the note in Tomás's cramped hand that Ansel had read forty times and still couldn't read without his vision blurring—

*If someone finds this, I couldn't finish. The rest is at the source. Under the valve house. A. — if it's you, I'm sorry. Finish it.*

Under the valve house. Which was, according to the demolition schedule Teo had photographed off a clipboard through the guard post window, scheduled to be dynamited into the lake tomorrow afternoon.

"You're sure it's still there," Ilsa said. Not a question really. A prayer wearing a question's clothes.

"No," Ansel said. "But Tomás hid things the way he did everything else. Thoroughly. If anyone could keep a secret safe from excavators, it's him." He paused. "He kept me a secret from myself for ten years."

Nobody laughed. Nobody was supposed to.

* * *

They descended along the western rim while the sun climbed, moving through scree fields the color of old bone, keeping the ridge between themselves and the guard post. Ansel led. His body knew this terrain—not this exact slope, but this kind: the particular grammar of broken rock at altitude, the way a foothold tells you before your eyes do whether it will hold. Ten years of guiding other people's escapes had built him into an instrument, and the instrument did its work while the rest of him stood inside the memory of this valley like a man standing inside a burned house.

He'd been here before. Not here-here—but downstream, that night, in the black water. His body remembered the cold even now. Sometimes, at altitude, when the air went thin and silver, he could feel the river again: the tumbling, the silence between waves, the moment he'd stopped fighting and let it take him because fighting meant knowing, and knowing meant—

No. That wasn't the missing hour anymore. He'd found the missing hour in the hut documents and the cave of his own sleep. Company men. A flashlight. *Sign it.* His thumbprint on their false report, wet with riverwater, his mind still half-drowned. They'd pulled him from the water not to save him but to use him—the survivor who couldn't remember, whose signature would seal the story. *Operator error. Guide negligence. Case closed.*

They'd built his disgrace out of his own broken body. And he'd carried it faithfully for a decade, like a good mule.

"Ansel." Pico's hand on his arm. "You stopped walking."

He hadn't noticed. He was standing on a shelf of scree staring at the lake, and the lake stared back, and for a moment he was twenty-nine years old and drowning in it.

"I'm fine," he said, and heard how much Pico didn't believe him, and kept walking anyway.

* * *

By midday they'd reached the shoreline of the western arm, a kilometer upstream of the demolition work, crouched behind a rampart of blast rock thrown there by some earlier charge. Close enough now to hear voices on the wind. Close enough to smell diesel and blasted stone—that raw mineral stink of freshly fractured rock, like a bone broken through skin.

The valve house stood in the shallows two hundred meters away.

It had been a squat concrete block once, functional, ugly, part of the dam's plumbing. Now it was a ruin listing into the water, its roof gone, its walls furred with ten years of mineral stain, orange and white streaks running down like candle wax. Half of it had already been torn open—one wall collapsed outward, concrete slabs stacked beside it waiting for the trucks. A yellow excavator sat parked nearby, bucket resting on the ground like a sleeping animal.

And around its base, half-burying it, was fresh debris. Blast rock. Gray, sharp-edged, dusted with powder, thrown there within the last day or two. They were burying the structure before destroying it—smothering it in its own pulverized body so that when the final charges came, there would be nothing left to identify, nothing left to measure, nothing left to prove anything had ever stood here at all except a hole in the water.

"They're entombing it," Teo breathed. "That's—God. That's not demolition protocol. That's a burial."

"It's a confession," Ilsa said. "You only bury what you're afraid someone will dig up."

Ansel lay on his stomach in the shadow of the rock rampart, cheek against stone still cold from the night, and looked at the valve house until his eyes burned.

Tomás had been here. After the flood, after everyone else was dead or scattered or bought—Tomás had come back into this sealed valley, past the guns, past the patrols, alone, and hidden the truth under the bones of the thing that killed him. And then he'd died trying to bring the rest of it out. Downstream. In the water.

In the same water where they'd found Ansel.

The thought arrived with its full weight for the first time, and Ansel had to close his eyes. Tomás hadn't just been his brother. Tomás had spent his last months trying to reach him. *A.—if it's you, I'm sorry. Finish it.* Sorry for dying. Sorry for leaving it unfinished. Sorry for the decade Ansel had spent believing himself a monster when he'd actually been a witness—twice victimized, twice silenced, once by force and once by his own grief.

"We can't go in daylight," Mara said. She'd come up beside him without a sound; she still moved like the climber she'd been at twenty-five, all economy, no wasted motion. Her face was drawn, the bruise from the checkpoint gone yellow-green along her jaw. "There'll be night workers, but fewer. Maybe four. The excavators stop at dusk—they don't run lights over the water."

"And the charges?" Ansel asked.

Teo checked the photographed schedule again, though he knew it by heart. "Demolition sequence begins tomorrow, oh-eight-hundred. Valve house first. Then the intake gallery, then the main abutment." He swallowed. "We have tonight. One night."

One night. Ansel looked at the water between them and the valve house—two hundred meters of open shallows, no cover, moonless after midnight but starlit before it. Looked at the guard post's antenna mast catching the sun. Looked at the excavator sleeping beside the ruin like a predator digesting.

"One night," he said. "Then we do what my brother asked."

* * *

They waited out the afternoon in a hollow behind the moraine, melting snow on Ilsa's stove, eating in silence, checking gear with the ritual economy of people who have done this too many times to waste motion. Pico slept. Ilsa pretended to check her medical kit for the fourth time. Teo sat with the documents spread on his knees, reading them yet again, as if repetition could make them heavier than paper—as if weight were what they needed.

Ansel didn't sleep. He sat at the mouth of the hollow and watched the light die on the glacier.

It was the most beautiful thing he had ever seen and he hated it. That was the truth he kept circling, unable to land. The wall of ice caught the sunset and turned the color of embers, gold bleeding into rose bleeding into ash, and the lake below took the fire into itself and held it, and the whole valley burned silently, gorgeously, like the horizon on fire—and underneath the beauty, the diesel stink, the compressor's throbbing, the mound of shattered concrete growing on the shore like a grave mound for eleven people who had never been given graves.

His brother had loved this valley. Ansel remembered that suddenly, whole and specific: Tomás at nineteen, home from his first season on the survey crew, hands cracked from the cold, saying *you should see it, Ansel, you should see where the river is born, it's like the mountain keeps a piece of the sky.* He'd promised to take him someday. They'd never gone. There had always been another season, another job, another year.

Then the company had shown them the valley in the worst possible way, and one of them had never left it.

"I used to think the river was guilty," Ansel said quietly. Mara glanced over. "Every town downstream, every family—I couldn't drink from it. Couldn't guide on it. Felt like it had Tomás in its teeth." He shook his head slowly. "Rivers don't kill people. Valves do. Orders do. Men with clipboards do."

"And guides who sign false reports," Mara said, gently, watching him.

"Guides who are forced to," he corrected. "There's a difference. I spent ten years not being allowed to know that." He looked at her. "You testified against me."

"I did."

"Why are you here?"

Mara was quiet long enough that the compressor's throb filled the space between them. "Because I've spent ten years wondering why the math never worked. Why a careful guide with your record ran a trip into a storm window every other outfit had refused. Because the evidence pointed at you and I followed the evidence, and then the evidence kept pointing at things that didn't exist—records that vanished, witnesses who recanted and then went silent, a company that dissolved on paper but never in the flesh." She looked out at the darkening water. "I followed the evidence right off a cliff, Ansel. It just took me a decade to hit bottom. You hit it faster. You just kept falling in place."

"That's almost an apology."

"Don't get used to it."

Almost, he smiled. First time in days. It felt strange on his face, like a boot worn on the wrong foot.

* * *

Full dark came at seven. By nine the work lights on the far shore had shrunk to a cluster of sodium-orange stars, and the night shift showed itself: four figures around a fire near the guard road, one walking a slow circuit of the demolition zone with a flashlight he swept more out of habit than attention.

Ansel, Mara, and Pico stripped to base layers and drysuits. Ilsa dressed Teo's rechecked bandages one last time and said nothing, which cost her visibly. The plan was simple because simple plans survive contact with reality: swim the western shallows wide of the work zone, cross the exposed mudflat during the patrol's blind arc, reach the valve house, find the cache, come back. Ninety minutes if everything went right. Ansel had stopped believing in everything going right somewhere around the age of thirty, but he'd learned to move anyway. That was the whole trick of his life. Move anyway.

At the waterline, Ansel paused.

The lake touched his boots and it was cold—not river-cold, not drowning-cold, but close enough that his pulse jumped sideways. Above them the glacier loomed invisible, a presence more than a shape, breathing its slow cold down the valley. Somewhere under that ice, the meltwater gathered that became the Ceniza, that became the flood, that became everything.

"Hey," Pico said softly. The kid's face was pale in the starlight, young and scared and absolutely steady. "Whatever's down there—we're getting it. Both of us. That's the deal."

"When did you start making deals with me?"

"The day you pulled me out of the rapid and didn't even yell at me after." Pico grinned. "Figured a man like that keeps his word."

Ansel looked at him—at the boy who idolized him without knowing why, who'd saved his life on the scree without ever asking what ghosts he was carrying—and felt something in his chest crack open, pain and warmth in the same fracture.

"Stay on my left," he said. "Kick slow. We're not swimming, we're sneaking."

They slid into the water together.

It was warmer than the flood had been. He told himself that. He told himself that the whole way across, stroke by slow stroke, while the stars wheeled overhead and the valve house grew out of the darkness ahead of them, black against black, its broken silhouette rising from the shallows like a headstone somebody had tried to knock flat and failed.

They reached the mudflat as the patrol's flashlight swung away. They crossed low and fast, feet sinking in cold silt, breath held, hearts loud. At the base of the ruined wall, Ansel put his palm against the concrete—his brother's concrete, the company's concrete, the lie's foundation—and felt the mineral fur, the old cold, the deep hum of the compressor transmitted through stone.

Somewhere under this, wrapped and sealed and hidden by a dead man who had trusted him with the truth, was the end of ten years of running.

Ansel took out the crowbar Pico handed him, set the blade into the first seam of the collapsed wall, and began to dig his brother out of the earth.

Behind them, across the dark water, a work light flicked on early—and a voice, amplified and bored, rolled across the valley:

*"Perimeter check. All units, perimeter check."*

The night had just gotten shorter.
