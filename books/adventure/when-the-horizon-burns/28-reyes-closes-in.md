## 28. Reyes Closes In

Teo heard the helicopters before he saw them—a flat, rhythmic thudding rolling down the valley like a fist knocking on the world's door. He was elbow-deep in the cache they'd dug out of the blast debris, hands black with ten-year-old mud and glacier silt, when the sound arrived. His whole body went still in a way that had nothing to do with calm.

"Ansel." He said it quietly first, then again, louder. "Ansel. Choppers."

The lake lay below them, gray-green and glassy under the glacier's shoulder, ringed by the machinery of erasure: excavators folded like dead insects where the crews had left them at dusk, blast scars fresh on the moraine, the shattered spine of the old dam ruin jutting from the shoreline like a rib cage someone had tried to bury and failed. They'd reached it an hour before sundown, giddy, half-mad with altitude, and had spent the last of the light digging Tomás's second cache—half-crushed by a charge that had gone off close enough to crack the case but not enough to kill what was inside.

Now the sky was answering back.

Ansel came up the slope fast, low, moving the way he always moved when things went wrong—like water finding the downhill line. Pico was behind him, then Ilsa, her face already stripped of everything except calculation. Mara wasn't there. Mara was two valleys east, running her own war, and Teo found himself thinking about that with a cold, arithmetic clarity: whatever happened next, they were going to have to solve it without her.

"Count," Ansel said.

Teo counted. Two helicopters, working the lower gorge in a sweep pattern—searchlight beams raking the canyon walls even though full dark hadn't come yet, the light bouncing off ice and making the whole valley flicker like something breathing. "Sweep pattern," he said. "Grid search. They're not looking for a camp. They're looking for tracks."

"They'll find ours." Ilsa crouched beside him, pulling the document case out of the debris pit and wrapping it in the spare rope tarp with quick, economical motions. "We came up the moraine in daylight. There's a trail a blind man could follow."

"So we don't be here when they finish following it." Ansel turned, and in the last gray light his face looked carved out of something older than the rock around them. Teo had watched this man make a hundred decisions on a hundred bad days—the bridge at Quebrada Honda, the whiteout on the Salkantay traverse, the night the porters' bus went off the road—and he had never once seen him hesitate. But he saw something now that wasn't hesitation. It was the opposite. It was a man who had already decided and was only choosing how to say it.

"The documents go west," Ansel said. "Tonight. The ridge station is fourteen kilometers as the condor flies, twenty-two the way we have to walk. The uplink window Teo found opens at 0600 and closes at 0900—that's satellite handover, and after that we're blind for thirty hours we don't have."

"I can hit the window if I leave now," Teo said. "With one other person. The station's a climb, not a walk—I built half the relay hardware they're still using up there, I know the approach."

"You're not going alone."

"Pico comes with me." Teo said it before Ansel could assign anyone else, and saw the boy's head come up in the dark like a struck match. "He's the strongest climber we've got besides you, and you—" He stopped. Made himself say it plainly, because plainness was the only currency that spent at this altitude. "You're the one they want. Reyes has been chasing your name for ten years. If you walk toward that ridge station, you lead every gun in this valley straight to the only thing that matters."

Ansel was quiet for a long moment. Below them, the searchlights swept the lower gorge, patient as tide.

"And you?" Ilsa asked. "What do you do while we play courier?"

"What I'm good at," Ansel said. "I run. Loudly, visibly, in the wrong direction. I've got ten years of practice being the thing people chase. Tonight I finally use it."

* * *

They divided the evidence on a flat rock like men dividing a card deck, and Teo hated every second of it—not because the division was wrong but because it felt like a funeral. The core documents went into the tarp-wrapped case: the survey reports from the hut, the tailings contamination data, the overtopping calculations that proved the flood had been engineered, page by page, number by number, eleven names' worth of proof. That was the payload. That was the reason any of them were still breathing this thin air.

Tomás's recorder—the one from the crevasse cache, the one with the footage Ansel had described in four broken sentences and then stopped talking about entirely—went into a waterproof bag that Ilsa slung across her chest. She'd volunteered to carry it, and no one had argued, because Ilsa carried things the way she treated wounds: completely, without flinching, and without ever letting you see how much it cost her.

"If we get separated," Ansel said, "the case goes to the ridge station or it gets burned. It does not get surrendered, traded, or buried for later. Later doesn't exist anymore. We watched them prove that today." He nodded down at the demolition scars ringing the lake. "They're not cleaning up a crime scene. They're destroying the crime. Tomorrow there won't be a ruin to investigate. If we're not done by tomorrow, we never were here."

"Cheerful," Pico said, and his voice cracked on the second syllable, and nobody laughed, because it wasn't a joke, it was just true.

They checked each other's gear in silence. Teo re-taped Pico's boot seam. Ilsa pressed a blister kit and a strip of coagulant into his chest pocket without asking. Ansel stood apart, looking down at the lake, and Teo watched him do it—the thing he'd seen him do exactly twice in six years, once at Tomás's grave outside Huaraz and once on the bridge at Quebrada Honda when they'd all nearly died and Ansel had thought none of them were watching.

He was saying goodbye to something. Teo didn't know what. He suspected Ansel didn't either.

"Ansel." He kept his voice level. "Come back to the ridge station. Not somewhere else. Not some heroic detour. The station. We hold the uplink until you're there, and you testify on camera yourself, in your own voice, with your own name. That was the whole point. That was why you wouldn't let any of us carry this part."

"I know what the point is, Teo."

"Then promise me."

The searchlights swept higher up the gorge. Somewhere below, faint on the wind, came the grind of an engine—an excavator being woken for the night shift, or a vehicle on the valley track, impossible to tell. Time had stopped being a resource and become a wall, and they were all leaning against it.

"I promise," Ansel said, and it was the first time in six years Teo had heard him say anything he didn't mean, and both of them knew it, and neither of them said so.

* * *

They split at the outlet stream, where the meltwater left the lake in a braided silver rush. Ansel shook Teo's hand once, hard, the way he did at the start of jobs instead of the end, as if refusing to admit endings existed. Then he took the eastern route along the shoreline, deliberately exposed, deliberately loud, kicking scree as he went, a man composing himself into a target.

Pico and Teo went west, into the boulder field, climbing.

For the first hour the going was brutal but simple: hands and boots, headlamps hooded to slits, the glacier groaning somewhere above them in voices like furniture being dragged across eternity. Teo navigated off memory and a compass bearing he'd drawn three days earlier from the fisherman's map, and Pico carried the case strapped to his back like it weighed nothing, which it did not—it weighed eleven people, and both of them knew it, and neither of them mentioned it, because at eight thousand meters you spend words like money.

Then the flare went up.

It rose from the direction of the lake—a red star climbing, hanging, dying—and Teo threw himself flat behind a boulder and pulled Pico down with him, and for ten seconds the whole valley was lit the color of an open wound.

"There," Pico breathed. "That's near the outlet. Near where—"

"He led them right to himself." Teo's mouth had gone dry. "That's the plan. That's the plan working."

But even as he said it, he was counting lights. Two helicopters had become three. The third had been silent, parked somewhere out of earshot, and now it lifted from behind the eastern ridge with its searchlight already burning, and it did not go toward the outlet stream where Ansel was making his noise.

It went west. Toward the boulder field. Toward them.

"They split the grid," Teo whispered. "Reyes isn't stupid. One runner means two routes. He's covering both."

The helicopter passed a kilometer south of them, close enough that the rotor wash stirred the scree dust, and Teo lay against the frozen stone with Pico's wrist in his grip and the boy's heartbeat hammering through his fingers like a second pulse, and he thought about forged passports and bribed border clerks and every careful, invisible life he'd built since the day his real name became a liability, and he understood with total clarity that none of it had ever been safe. Safety had been a story he told himself. The story was over.

The light moved on. They climbed.

* * *

At midnight the weather turned, which saved them and doomed them in the same breath. Cloud poured over the western ridge like smoke filling a room, killing the moon, killing visibility, killing the helicopter's advantage—but also killing their line to the ridge station. Teo navigated by compass and counting paces, calling turns to Pico in a whisper, and twice they stopped and shut everything off and listened to nothing, absolutely nothing, which was worse than any sound.

Near two in the morning, on a shelf of rock above the cloud layer, they found the radio.

Not theirs. A company set—military-grade, tripod-mounted, abandoned or hidden, its frequency log still glowing faintly on the little screen. Teo should have walked past it. Every instinct honed over fifteen years of staying alive said walk past it, touch nothing, want nothing.

Instead he knelt and read the log, because Teo had never in his life met a machine he could resist understanding.

And what he read made the cold go through him like water through cloth.

The traffic was routine—patrol check-ins, weather reports, fuel counts. Routine except for one repeated order, timestamped every ninety minutes for the past six hours, addressed to all units:

**SWEEP PRIORITY REVISED. INTERCEPT COURIER TEAM BEFORE RIDGE STATION. DEMOLITION ADVANCED TO 1400. NOTHING ABOVE THE LAKE SURVIVES THE DAY.**

"Teo." Pico's voice, very small. "What's 'nothing above the lake survives the day'?"

Teo stared at the screen. Fourteen hundred hours. They'd planned on tomorrow. They'd built the whole fragile architecture of the escape on tomorrow—on time to reach the station, time to test the link, time for Ansel to arrive and stand in front of a camera and give the dead their names. Reyes had just taken tomorrow away. The man wasn't sweeping the valley to find them. He was sweeping it to make sure the valley itself would swallow the evidence of everyone who'd ever come looking.

"We tell the others," Teo said. He was already repacking the radio's frequencies into his head, already calculating whether the handheld unit Ilsa carried could reach Mara's channel this far west. Probably not. Probably nothing worked, the way nothing ever worked. "We move faster, and we tell the others."

"How? Ansel's drawing patrols east, Ilsa's got the recorder and no radio range, and we—" Pico stopped. In the hooded glow of the headlamp his face was young and terrified and absolutely unwilling to quit, and Teo thought: *this is what Ansel sees when he looks at him. This is why he can't stop protecting him.* "How do we reach anybody?"

Teo looked at the company radio. Looked at the frequency log. And felt the plan—the whole desperate, fraying, beautiful plan—teeter on a single ugly idea.

"Reyes wants to talk to his own people," he said slowly. "So we let him. We get on this net, we spoof a patrol check-in, and we feed his intercept teams a false position—pull them east, away from the station." He was already unscrewing the handset, hands steady, mind running cold and bright. "It buys us the window. It might buy Ansel the same."

"And if they trace the transmission?"

Teo clipped the handset to his pack and started climbing, faster now, into the dark, into the wind, toward a ridge station that had to be reached in five hours or never.

"Then they'll know exactly where we are," he said. "So let's not be here."

Behind them, far below the cloud, the valley glowed with work lights as the machines woke to bury the truth—and somewhere in that glare, a man who had promised to come back was running through the dark with every gun in the watershed turning toward the sound of his name.
