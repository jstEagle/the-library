## 29. Ambush at the Moraine

The moraine was a spine of broken stone running east from the glacier's shoulder, and it looked on the map like salvation—a high road above the valley floor where a man carrying documents could move fast and stay hidden. On the ground it was something else: a kilometer of shattered rock the size of coffins, frost-heaved and unstable, every step announcing itself to anyone listening.

Ansel crouched behind a boulder streaked with glacial flour and listened. The wind came down off the icefield in long, steady pulses, and beneath it—faint, rhythmic, wrong for this place—he could hear engines. Not one. Several. They moved along the valley floor where the demolition crews worked, but they were not working engines. They were searching ones.

"They're sweeping the lower moraine," Teo whispered beside him. He had the documents wrapped in waterproof bags strapped across his chest like a bandolier, and he kept one hand pressed flat against them as if they might try to escape. "Reyes isn't stupid. He knows we split. He knows we have to cross open ground somewhere."

"He knows more than that," Ilsa said. She was flat on her belly in the scree, binoculars up, her breath fogging the lenses. "Look at the ridge line. West side. Those aren't survey teams."

Ansel took the glasses from her. Through the heat-shimmer of cold air he counted six figures moving along the western ridge in a skirmish line, spaced wide, walking with the deliberate patience of men who were being paid by the hour to be thorough. Behind them, half-hidden in a rock notch, a drone sat on its launch rail like a hooded bird.

Six on the ridge. Engines below. And somewhere out of sight, Reyes himself, who had spent two days demonstrating that he never committed all of his men to any single guess.

"We go through the middle," Pico said.

They all turned to look at him. He was twenty-three years old and had frostbite scars on his cheeks from the portage through hell, and he said it the way he said everything now—not bravado, just arithmetic. "The ridge is covered. The valley floor is covered. Nobody covers the middle because nobody's crazy enough to cross the moraine crest in this wind. So we're crazy enough."

Ansel studied the boy—the young man; when had that happened?—and felt the old familiar weight settle onto his shoulders, heavier than any pack. It was the weight of knowing exactly how much of Pico's confidence was borrowed from him, and what it would cost if he spent it badly.

"The middle it is," he said. "Teo in center with the documents. Ilsa on his left, Pico right, me on point. We move on the wind gusts—when the wind roars, we run; when it drops, we freeze. Nobody helps anybody up. If you fall, you get up alone or you stay down until the next gust. Understood?"

"Nobody helps anybody," Pico repeated, and there was something in his voice that suggested he intended to break that rule at the first opportunity.

They crossed the first four hundred meters cleanly. The wind cooperated, roaring down the icefield in thirty-second bursts, and they ran bent-double between the boulders like thieves, freezing into statues whenever it died. Ansel felt the old joy flicker somewhere under the fear—that animal pleasure of moving well through bad terrain, body and mountain arguing in a language only the two of them spoke. He had missed it. He hated that he had missed it.

Then the wind shifted.

It came around from the south instead of the north, and it carried with it the sound of the drone launching—a thin electric whine that climbed and climbed and did not fade.

"Down!" Ansel hissed. "Rock, now!"

They scattered. The drone came over the moraine crest low and fast, a gray wedge against the gray sky, and swept the length of the spine in eight seconds. Ansel pressed himself into the shadow of a fractured slab and watched its camera gimbal rotate, methodical as a lighthouse beam. It passed over Ilsa without pausing. It passed over Pico. It hung above Teo.

It hung.

Three seconds. Five. Then it banked hard and dropped back toward the valley, and Ansel knew—with the sick certainty of a man who has watched avalanches start before the sound arrives—that the sweep had found what it was looking for.

* * *

They came up the moraine from both ends at once.

Ansel saw them materialize out of the broken ground the way soldiers do—not appearing so much as resolving, gray figures becoming men with rifles becoming a coordinated net closing along the crest. Six from the west. Four from the east. The engines below had gone silent, which meant the vehicles were positioning at every descent route off the moraine's northern flank.

"Split," Ilsa breathed. "Ansel, take the documents—"

"No." His voice came out flat and final, the voice he used on clients who wanted to argue about turnaround times on a burning mountainside. "Teo keeps them. You three go north, down the gully, into the boulder field. I draw them east."

"You can't—" Pico started.

"I've been running from these people for ten years. Nobody on that ridge knows my face better than I do. Move."

For one suspended second none of them moved. Then Teo did something Ansel would remember for the rest of his life, however long that turned out to be. He unslung the document bandolier, held it out—and then, instead of handing it over, he turned and *ran*, straight up the crest, straight into the teeth of the western team, shouting words in Spanish that were mostly profanity and partly a dare.

"*¡Aquí! ¡Están aquí!*"

He was buying them the east flank. He was spending himself as bait, the engineer with the forged passport who had once told Ansel, over cheap whiskey in a border bar, that the whole secret of survival was to never be the most interesting thing in the room.

"TEO!" Ansel was up and moving before he'd decided to, scrambling over the frost-heaved blocks, and behind him he heard Ilsa grab Pico's jacket and haul him north, and he heard Pico fighting her, and then the wind swallowed everything.

The western team converged on Teo at the crest's highest point, where a fin of bedrock stood up out of the scree like a ship's prow. Ansel was fifty meters away when they took him down—no shots, no drama, just four men folding around him with practiced economy, and then Teo was face-down on the stone with a knee between his shoulder blades and the document bandolier being cut away from his chest with a knife.

Ansel dropped behind a boulder. His heart was slamming. Every instinct he owned screamed the same lesson it had been screaming for a decade: *run, survive, live to move again.* Never stop moving. The rule that kept them alive.

But the rule had just failed, hadn't it? The rule had gotten Teo's face pressed into the frozen rock while men in company jackets rifled through ten years of buried truth.

Through the gap between two stones he watched them stand Teo up. One of them radioed something. And then the tallest of them—broad-shouldered, silver at the temples, moving with the unhurried authority of ownership rather than employment—walked up the crest and stopped in front of Teo and looked at him for a long moment before saying something that made the others laugh.

Reyes. In the flesh, at last. Ansel had imagined him a hundred ways since the checkpoint, since Mara's interrogation, since the name had begun threading through everything like a wire through meat. He had imagined a brute. What stood on the moraine was worse: a man who looked like a regional manager, patient and reasonable, the kind of face that signed things.

Reyes said something else. Teo spat at his feet.

And then Reyes turned his head—slowly, deliberately, as if he had all the time the glacier had—and looked directly at the boulder where Ansel was hiding.

He raised one hand. Not toward the rifleman nearest him. Just raised it, palm out, a gesture meant to be seen.

*I know you're there. Stay there.*

* * *

They let the others go.

That was the part Ansel kept turning over during the hour that followed, as he tracked Ilsa and Pico's descent route from a distance, confirming they'd made the boulder field clean, confirming the patrol vehicles on the north flank rolled past their hiding spot without slowing. The company had Teo, the documents, and a clear shot at capturing all four of them. Instead the net had opened on one side like a hand releasing a bird.

Because they didn't want the others. They wanted *him*. And they wanted him to come in on his own feet, understanding the terms, the way a man comes in to sign something.

The rendezvous was a meltwater pool in a shallow depression below the eastern moraine, a lens of green water cupped in stone, ringed by a single figure. Reyes stood alone at its edge, hands visible, an offered openness that was itself a kind of weapon. Two riflemen waited far enough back to be scenery rather than threat, which was its own message: *you are not a prisoner yet; prisoners don't need to be persuaded.*

Ansel came down the slope with his hands empty and his mind running the old arithmetic, the blacklist arithmetic, the arithmetic of a man who has always known the exact price of every exit.

"Mister Sandoval." Reyes's voice was mild, almost warm, pitched to carry across the water without effort. "You look better than your file photographs. Thinner, though. The altitude?"

"What do you want."

"Honesty. Refreshing." Reyes crouched, touched two fingers to the surface of the meltwater pool, and watched the ripples. "Do you know what this is? This pond. It's the Ceniza. First free-standing water in the whole watershed. Everything above here is ice. Everything below is river. Right here—" he tapped the surface, and the ripples spread to the edges and died— "is where the river decides what it's going to be."

"You have my engineer and my documents."

"I have your engineer." Reyes straightened. "The documents are copies. Good ones—I'll grant your brother's craftsmanship was excellent—but copies. The originals, the recordings, the master files, are inside the glacier, and by tomorrow night the glacier will have eaten them. That's not a threat, Sandoval. That's a schedule. Demolition doesn't wait for weather and it certainly doesn't wait for grief."

Ansel said nothing. Saying nothing was the oldest tool he had.

"So here is the trade, and I'll make it only once, because unlike some people I respect the value of time." Reyes folded his hands behind his back, a lecturer's posture, a man presenting terms to a board. "Your engineer walks free. Unharmed, delivered to the coast, passport intact—which, between us, is more than his government would offer. Your medic and your boy are left alone permanently; they're not in our files, they were never here, and they stay that way. In exchange: the remaining copies, your silence, and your departure from this country within seventy-two hours. You will never be permitted back. But you were never permitted anywhere, were you? What's one more door?"

The wind moved across the pool. Somewhere above, the glacier ticked and groaned, settling its ancient weight, and Ansel thought: *it sounds almost like a man clearing his throat before speaking.*

"You're offering me the same bargain," Ansel said.

"I'm offering you a bargain. Whether it's the same one depends entirely on you."

"It's the same one." His voice came out low and even, and he was surprised by how little anger was in it. What was in it instead was something colder and more useful: recognition. "Ten years ago a man stood in a hospital corridor in Cajamarca with my brother's blood still on someone's boots, and he offered me a signature and a bus ticket and a story to tell myself. *You were swept downstream. You remember nothing. Sign here.* And I signed, because the alternative was a cell and a trial and eleven funerals I couldn't face. And I have spent ten years telling myself I was half-dead, that I didn't know what I was doing, that the man who signed wasn't really me."

"And was he?"

"Yes." Ansel looked at the pool, at the small green eye of the river deciding what it would become. "He was me. That's the part I finally understand. I knew. Some part of me knew exactly what I was signing, and I signed anyway, because surviving quietly was easier than being right loudly. So no, Mister Reyes. I'm not going to mistake you for a new offer. You're the same offer. You're the corridor in Cajamarca with better weather."

Something flickered behind Reyes's eyes—not anger. Recalculation. A man discovering that the file he'd been given on Ansel Sandoval, the broken blacklisted guide, the man who runs, had been compiled from the wrong decade.

"Sandoval." Reyes's voice lost its warmth entirely, and what remained was almost respectful, the way a chess player acknowledges an unexpected move. "You should understand what refusal costs. Not you—you've already paid everything a man can pay, that's why you're worth so little to us alive and so much quiet. I mean the boy. The engineer. People keep choosing to stand near you, and people near you have historically drowned. Eleven of them, if we're counting. Shall we count?"

The words landed exactly where they were aimed. Ansel felt the old guilt rise in him like floodwater, the sediment of a decade, and he let it rise, and he watched it, and he noticed—for the first time—that it no longer reached his throat. There was higher ground in him now. Tomás had put it there, in a crevasse, in a recording, in a dead man's voice saying *I trusted you to finish it.*

"My brother counted," Ansel said. "He counted all the way to the source and cached the evidence and died trying to bring it out. You're standing on his river, Reyes. You're demolishing his grave tomorrow. And you think the thing I'll trade for is my own silence." He shook his head slowly. "I already gave you that. Ten years ago. It's the only thing I ever stole from those eleven people, and I've come four hundred kilometers upstream to give it back."

Reyes exhaled through his nose. Then he turned and nodded once toward the ridge, and the two riflemen began walking forward—not rushing, not aiming, just closing, patient as the demolition schedule itself.

"For the record," Reyes said, turning back, "the trade remains open until sundown. Men change their minds at altitude. It's the thin air." He paused, and for just a moment the managerial mask slipped and something older showed underneath—something tired. "My father fished the Ceniza, you know. Before. I was nine when the dam went. So believe me when I say I take no pleasure in any of this. Believe me when I say that the men who ordered it are far above me, and that burying them is the only career I have left."

"That's the difference between us," Ansel said. "I had a career left too. I'm spending it."

He turned and walked—not ran, walked—back up the slope toward the moraine, hands loose at his sides, and the riflemen parted around him without touching, because the trade was open until sundown, because a man who might still sign was worth more walking than bleeding.

Behind him, Reyes called out one last thing, and the wind carried it clearly across the green water:

"The engineer asks for you, Sandoval! He wants to know if you'll trade! Sundown!"

Ansel did not turn around. He climbed. Above him the glacier burned white in the late light, and inside it, sealed in blue ice, his brother's voice was waiting, and tomorrow the charges would fall, and between this sunset and that destruction there was exactly one night—one night, one crew, one plan fraying at every edge—and Ansel Sandoval, the man who had spent ten years never stopping moving, finally understood where he was moving *to*.

He keyed the battered satellite messenger twice. Once to Ilsa and Pico: *I'm coming to you. Bring everything.*

And once to a number Mara Voss had given him at the checkpoint, a number attached to a guard whose father had drowned in the flood, a number that had gone silent since her escape.

*Mara. It's sundown soon. Tell me your friend is still ours.*
