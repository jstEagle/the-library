## 30. The Bargain Refused

The moraine held its breath.

Ansel stood with Reyes's offer hanging in the thin air between them—Teo for the documents, silence for silence—and felt the old machinery of himself begin to turn: the calculation of exits, the weighing of legs and distance, the cold arithmetic of who could be saved and who couldn't. It was the arithmetic that had ruled his life for ten years. Run first. Grieve later. Forget fastest.

He let the arithmetic run its course, watched it lay out its answers like cards on a table, and then he did something he had never once done in a decade of running.

He folded the hand.

"No," Ansel said.

Reyes tilted his head, unhurried, a man who had heard no before and buried it. Behind him, two contractors stood at the edge of the blast-scarred terrace with their rifles slung loose, theatrical in their ease. Below them, the meltwater lake lay flat and gray-green beneath the glacier, ringed by the skeleton of the dam ruin and the yellow machines that were eating it. Somewhere down-valley, Teo was zip-tied in the back of a patrol truck, and Ilsa and Pico were moving through the boulder fields with the core evidence strapped to Pico's back, and every second Ansel kept this man talking was a second they gained.

He had learned that much from Mara. Talk is terrain. Every word you make an enemy climb is ground he doesn't take from you.

"Say it again," Reyes said. "Slower. I want to enjoy it."

"I said no." Ansel spread his empty hands. "You want the documents. You want us gone quiet, like last time. And you're offering me a man's life to buy it." He shook his head slowly, letting confusion show on his face—not all of it feigned; some of it was real, the genuine bewilderment of a man watching his own history try to rerun itself. "Here's what I don't understand, señor. You already believe we have nothing worth trading. That's why your men are sweeping the valley instead of shooting me where I stand. So why negotiate at all?"

Something flickered behind Reyes's eyes—the small, involuntary twitch of a man whose assumption has been touched. "Because you do have something," he said. "Your people ran west with packs heavy enough to matter. My sweep teams will find them by dark regardless. The trade saves everyone unpleasantness."

"Then it costs you nothing to give me until dawn."

The words were out before Ansel had fully decided to say them, which was how his best decisions had always arrived—sideways, on instinct, the way water finds a crack. But the moment they landed he understood their shape, and he leaned into it.

"Dawn," Reyes repeated.

"You're demolishing tomorrow anyway. Everything goes under." Ansel gestured at the ruin, the terraces of shattered concrete, the machines parked for the night along the lake shore. "So the documents' location stops mattering the moment the charges fire. Which means between now and then, the only thing you actually need from me is certainty that my people won't come back with more copies, more testimony, more noise. Killing Teo buys you nothing but a martyr. Letting me talk to him buys you a chance I'll convince him to disappear."

"And if I don't believe you'd convince anyone of anything?"

"Then shoot me now and find out how loud a dead guide's friends can get." Ansel held the man's gaze and did not blink, and inside, where no one could see, his heart was going like a trip-hammer and some older, smaller part of him was screaming *run, run, you fool, you always run*—and he told that part of himself, quietly, with something close to tenderness, *not today.*

* * *

They gave him a tent.

Not kindness—logistics. Reyes wanted him contained, visible, and plausibly cooperative, because Reyes had made the same mistake the company had made ten years ago: he believed Ansel Sandoval was a man who could be bought, because he had been bought once, and men who sell once will sell again. It was the single most useful lie Ansel had ever lived inside, and tonight he intended to live in it all the way to morning.

The tent sat at the edge of the contractor camp, forty meters from the comms shack, close enough to hear the radio traffic crackling faintly through canvas. They took his pack, his ice axe, his boots—they left him his socks, a courtesy that told him exactly how seriously they took his flight risk—and posted one guard, a young man with wind-chapped cheeks who wouldn't meet his eyes.

Ansel sat on the cot and listened.

Not to the guard. To the radios. Ten years of guiding had taught him to hold whole maps in his head—contour lines, hazard zones, escape routes—and tonight he built a new map out of sound. Sweep Team Two, reporting negative contact, grid reference called out in clipped numbers: west ridge clear. Sweep Team Three, same, farther south. The uplink ridge—his ridge, the one Ilsa and Pico were climbing toward—was not yet reported clear, which meant either they hadn't reached it or the sweep hadn't. Either way: time. Not much. Some.

And underneath the tactical chatter, another frequency, lower, half-garbled: the demolition schedule. Charges laid at the dam's remaining abutments. Firing circuit test at 0500. Main sequence at 0600.

Dawn wasn't a metaphor. Dawn was a fuse.

He closed his eyes and let himself see the board as Teo would see it—Teo, who thought in systems, who drew diagrams in the dirt when he was scared. Assets: documents, moving west with Pico and Ilsa. Liability: Teo, captured, in the truck depot below the camp. Unknowns: Mara. Always Mara.

Mara, who had walked into that checkpoint wearing her own name like a flare, who had been arrested there before and released, which meant someone above Reyes wanted her alive or wanted her watched or wanted her—Ansel turned it over—wanted her *reachable*. She had known that when she offered herself. She had stood in the fog at the checkpoint and said *use me* with the same flat calm she'd used to testify against him a decade ago, and he had hated her for it then and understood her for it now, and both of those things could be true about a person, he was learning, late.

She would be held somewhere. Not the truck depot—that was operational space. Somewhere separate. His eyes moved across the camp layout he'd memorized during the walk in: the comms shack, the fuel bladders, the mess tent, and beyond them, apart, a hard-sided shelter with its own generator hum and a single guard walking a short, bored perimeter.

A detention box. For a prisoner someone considered valuable.

*Okay,* he thought. *Now the plan.*

* * *

The guard came in at 2300 with water and a ration pack, and Ansel performed the first act of the night: he broke.

Not convincingly—he didn't need convincing, he needed *legible*. He needed the boy to see exactly what Reyes expected to see: a broken guide, ten years of guilt finally arriving, a man bargaining with himself. Ansel sat hunched over the untouched food, hands shaking—he didn't have to fake that; his body had been running on adrenaline and altitude for thirty hours—and when the guard turned to leave, Ansel spoke to the floor.

"The woman they're holding. Voss."

The guard stopped.

"She testified against me once," Ansel said. "Ruined my life. And I still—" He let it die. Let the silence do the work. Grief is a language every human speaks fluently, and this boy, whoever he was, patrolling a demolition site in the mountains for money, had grief of his own somewhere. Everyone in this valley did. That was the company's whole architecture: build your walls out of other people's losses.

"They're not supposed to talk about prisoners," the guard said.

"I'm not asking you to talk. I'm asking—" Ansel looked up then, and let the boy see everything: the red eyes, the wrecked face, ten years of it. "Is she alive? That's all. I signed their paper once. I know what these people do to loose ends. If she's a loose end, I want to know before I sign anything again."

The guard's jaw worked. Wind-chapped, twenty-five maybe, standing in a valley where eleven people had drowned so a company could bury its own poison. "She's alive," he said finally. "The boss needs her talking. There's questions about who she's been working with." A pause, and then, quieter, almost ashamed: "Nobody's coming for her, though. If that's what you're hoping."

"Someone came for me once," Ansel said. "Worst thing that ever happened to me."

The guard left. Ansel counted to three hundred, listening to the footsteps fade around the shelter, and then he got to work.

* * *

There was a seam in the tent wall where two fabric panels overlapped, stitched but not taped—a manufacturer's shortcut, the kind of detail Teo would have found in four seconds and Ansel found in twenty minutes of patient fingernail work. Through the gap: the camp, the lake, the glacier wall burning faint blue under a rising moon, and the hard-sided shelter with its lone walker.

He watched the perimeter. Counted the loop. Ninety seconds around, forty seconds out of sight behind the shelter's blind corner on the generator side. Forty seconds. It wasn't enough to cross open ground, get Mara out, and get back. It was enough to reach her window—if the shelter had a window—and put his mouth near it.

It had a window. Small, frosted, wired. Of course it did.

Which meant the plan couldn't be rescue. Not tonight, not with one guard's patrol rhythm and a demolition clock ticking toward 0600. The plan had to be smaller and stranger: it had to be a signal.

Ansel lay back on the cot and assembled it piece by piece, the way he'd assemble a rope team before a bad pitch—checking each knot twice, because on the mountain, the knot you trust is the one that holds. Element one: Reyes expected a broken man. Element two: the broken man had asked about Mara, which was plausible and also true, and truth was the best camouflage he owned. Element three: the guard would report the conversation, because guards report everything, and Reyes would hear *Sandoval asked about Voss* and file it under sentiment, under weakness, under the exact category of thing that made a man sign papers.

Element four: at 0400, when the firing-circuit crews began their pre-dawn checks and every light in the camp swung toward the dam abutments, the perimeter guard would be pulled to assist—because Reyes was thorough about explosives and careless about prisoners, and thorough men are predictable in their thoroughness.

Element five: Ansel would be at that window, and he would say five sentences to Mara Voss, and those five sentences had to carry everything—Teo's location, the evidence route, the uplink ridge, the 0600 deadline, and the one thing he had never said to her in ten years of rivalry and one week of alliance:

*I'm not running this time. Stand with me.*

* * *

At 0340, the camp woke into controlled chaos. Floodlamps bloomed along the dam abutments; generators surged; men shouted numbers at each other across the ice. The perimeter guard on the shelter got a wave and a shout—*"Reyes wants hands on the circuit check!"*—and went, leaving the blind corner blind.

Ansel was already out of the tent. He crossed the forty meters low and fast, boots in socks, breath held, the way he'd taught a hundred clients to cross avalanche-runout zones: commit fully, hesitate never. The frost bit through his socks. The generator covered his footfalls. He reached the wall below the window and pressed his back to the cold metal and looked up at the frosted glass, and for one absurd second he thought of Tomás—of tapping on his brother's bedroom window at fourteen, midnight, snow falling, *come on, Tomás, get dressed, the reservoir's frozen over*—and he put the thought away carefully, like a tool he'd need again soon.

He knocked. Three soft taps. Pause. Two more. Their old signal, from a lifetime ago, repurposed for a woman who'd never known it—but she'd know *a* signal when she heard one, and she was Mara Voss, and Mara Voss missed nothing.

Shuffling inside. Then her voice, low and rough with sleeplessness: "If that's Reyes, I've already told him everything I intend to tell him, which is nothing, creatively phrased."

"It's Ansel."

Silence. Then, closer, right against the glass: "You're supposed to be signing things."

"I'm done signing things." He kept his voice at a thread's width. "Listen fast. Teo's alive, in the truck depot below the camp, west end, guarded light—Reyes is holding him as trade bait for the documents. Pico and Ilsa are moving for the uplink ridge with the core evidence. Demolition fires at 0600. Everything after that is ash, including us if we're still in this valley."

"Mara," he said, and stopped, because the next part wasn't tactics and tactics were easier. "Ten years ago I took their bargain because I was drowning and they handed me a rope and I didn't ask what it was tied to. I've spent a decade being the man who runs. Tonight I'm standing. I need to know you're standing too."

Through the frosted glass, a sound that might have been a laugh, bitten in half. "I walked into a checkpoint using my own name as bait, Sandoval. What exactly did you think I was doing?"

"There's a guard here—young, chapped cheeks, does the night perimeter. He talked to me. He knows things he shouldn't be comfortable knowing. Find him. He's the crack in their wall."

"He's mine," Mara said, and there was something in her voice Ansel had never heard from her—not triumph, not irony, but a fierce, banked heat, ten years of doubt finally given somewhere to go. "His name is Delgado. His father drove a supply truck on the Ceniza project. Go, before the guard comes back. And Ansel—"

The floodlights swept. Footsteps returning, early, someone jogging. Ansel pressed flat against the shelter wall as the beam passed over him, counting heartbeats, and Mara's voice came through the glass one last time, so quiet he had to lean in to catch it:

"When this is over, you're buying the drinks. Ten years of interest."

He was back in his tent before the perimeter guard rounded the corner, lying on the cot, breathing hard, socks soaked, hands steady for the first time in days.

* * *

Reyes came at first gray light.

He ducked into the tent uninvited, flanked by the two contractors, and studied Ansel with the flat patience of a man checking a trapline. Outside, the demolition crews were making their final calls; the firing circuit test had passed; the sky over the glacier had begun, obscenely, beautifully, to turn gold at its eastern edge.

"My people tell me you had a conversation last night," Reyes said. "About the woman."

"I asked if she was alive."

"And are you ready to conclude our business? The documents for your engineer. One call from you, one walk west, and everyone lives to be anonymous." He held out a satellite phone. "This is the rerun, Sandoval. You've seen this movie. You know how it ends—for you, it ended fine. A decade of work, a career, a life. All it cost was a signature."

Ansel looked at the phone. He let the silence stretch long enough that Reyes would read it as temptation—which it was, God help him, it was; Teo was down in that depot with a split lip and a busted rib and Ansel would have traded his own pulse for him, and Reyes knew it, and that was precisely why the bargain was poison, because it was built from the truest thing Ansel felt.

Then he stood up, slow, in his sock feet, and looked Reyes full in the face.

"Here's what your file on me gets wrong," Ansel said. "Ten years ago, a man signed a paper in a place like this, and you've been spending that signature ever since—my blacklist, my exile, my silence, all of it drawn on that account. But accounts run dry." He stepped past the phone without taking it. "I remember the flashlight now. I remember the voice saying *sign it*. I remember my brother shouting. And I remember being dumped in the river downstream of anywhere a swimmer could swim, because you people didn't want a witness—you wanted a scapegoat who'd stay bought." He saw it land, the flicker, the recalculation, the moment Reyes understood that the broken man in the tent had spent the night building something. "So no. No trade, no call, no silence. You want to know where the documents are? Ask the river. Ask the glacier. Ask the eleven people you buried under that dam—except you can't, because as of about an hour ago, they're being dug back up."

Reyes's face went very still. "What did you do?"

"What you should have done ten years ago," Ansel said. "Told the truth to someone who could use it."

Outside, far to the west, on the black spine of the uplink ridge, a light blinked once—twice—three times. Steady. Deliberate. Mara's signal, relayed from Delgado's radio, or Ilsa's hands, or Pico's stubborn young courage; it didn't matter whose. It meant: *transmission beginning.*

Reyes spun toward the window, shouting orders, and the camp erupted around them—men running, engines turning over, the whole machine of the cover-up lurching into motion ten minutes too late.

Ansel stood in the doorway of his tent in his wet socks and watched the sunrise set the glacier burning gold, and for the first time in ten years, he did not look for the way out.

He looked for the way forward—and saw Mara Voss cresting the moraine with a stolen rifle and a patrol guard at her side, running straight at the guns.
