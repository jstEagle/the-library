## 31. Prisoner Exchange

Mara had learned to measure time in the small sounds of a place that wanted her dead.

The drip of meltwater through the corrugated roof of the detention hut. The scrape of a boot on gravel outside—the sentry walking his circuit, forty paces out and back, every eleven minutes. The creak of the cot when she shifted her weight, which she did only when she had to, because even small movements cost warmth, and warmth was currency up here at four thousand meters where the sun dropped behind the ridge like a coin into a well and the cold came up out of the ground with teeth.

Three days since Reyes had put her in this room. Three days of questions delivered in a voice so reasonable it was almost gentle, the way a scalpel is gentle right up until it isn't. *Tell me about Voss Expeditions. Tell me about your permits. Tell me who else is on the mountain.* She had given him nothing but weather reports and deadpan lies, and he had given her tea, and between them they had conducted a conversation that was really a siege.

But it was the fourth thing she listened for that mattered: the generator.

It ran from dusk until roughly ten, then cut out, and in the silence after she could hear the patrol compound breathing—the murmur of men in the mess tent, the clink of dishes, once, memorably, someone singing badly. And beneath all of it, faint as a pulse under skin, a second engine. Smaller. Idling at the north end of the camp where the vehicles were parked, running long past the hour any sane mechanic would shut it down.

Someone was keeping an engine warm.

She'd noticed it the first night and told herself it meant nothing. The second night she'd timed it. The third night, when the guard brought her ration tin through the door—Ezequiel, broad-faced, slow-moving, eyes that never quite met hers—she had said, quietly, without looking up from the tin:

"Your truck's going to flood its carburetor, idling that long."

He had frozen in the doorway for exactly one heartbeat too long. Then he'd set the tin down and left without a word, and Mara had lain in the dark and felt something she hadn't allowed herself to feel since the checkpoint: hope, sharp and dangerous as a fresh-edged tool.

Because she knew why Ezequiel Ordoñez was here. She'd known since the second interrogation, when Reyes had made the mistake of letting her see the duty roster pinned inside his folder—a name, a home village two valleys east of the Ceniza, and a date of hire that fell eight months after the flood. She'd done the arithmetic lying awake that same night. A man from those villages, hired onto the patrol that guarded the water that killed his people? Men didn't take those jobs by accident. They took them the way you press on a bruise—to keep yourself from forgetting it's there.

And yesterday, when he'd brought her water, she had said one more thing. Just one. She'd kept her eyes on the cup and her voice below the hum of the generator.

"My father fished the Ceniza before the company came. I was seven when the trucks arrived." A pause. "I'm sorry about your father."

Ezequiel had set the cup down with exaggerated care, the way a man does when his hands want to do something else entirely, and walked out, and the door had not locked behind him. She'd heard the bolt slide—but heard, too, the rattle of it settling loose in its frame, half-thrown, a lock that could be argued with.

Now it was the fourth night, and the generator died at ten o'clock exactly, and the small engine at the north end of the camp was still running.

Mara sat up in the dark and began, methodically, to retie her boots.

* * *

The plan, such as it was, had been built out of almost nothing, passed in fragments over three days of ration tins and water cups. Ezequiel would not—or could not—say much. He spoke the way men speak when they are committing treason in their own heads and haven't fully signed the paperwork: sideways, in single sentences, always looking at the middle distance.

But the shape of it had come clear enough. The armory tent stood between the mess and the vehicle park, and its padlock was old, and Ezequiel had the key because armory duty rotated and tonight it was his. The rest of the patrol—eleven men, minus two on ridge watch and one asleep off-duty with a fever—would be in the mess tent until midnight, playing cards by lantern light, their rifles racked outside the flap because Reyes's standing order forbade loaded weapons near the stove. Reyes himself slept in the command trailer at the south end with a radio and a pistol and, Mara suspected, very little actual sleep.

The exchange point was the old survey bridge across the outlet channel, a kilometer downstream, where the terrain funneled anyone moving toward the high camps into a single visible line. That was where they were holding Teo—not in the compound itself but in a gear cache beside the bridge, a steel box of a building with a hasp and a padlock and, presumably, a man or two watching it. Reyes hadn't killed him. That fact told Mara more than any interrogation had: Teo was leverage now, a card to play against Ansel, and Reyes would keep the card intact until he'd played it.

*Get to the bridge,* Ezequiel had said, the only full sentence he'd ever given her, spoken into the space between them while he refilled her cup. *Midnight. When the ridge watch changes. There will be noise. Use the noise.*

She'd asked him what noise, and he'd looked at her then—really looked, for the first time—and there had been something in his face that frightened her more than Reyes ever had. Not anger. Grief, held the way a man holds a lit match: carefully, and knowing it will cost him.

"The kind I make," he'd said.

* * *

The bolt gave on the third try, just as she'd hoped—half-seated, all show. She eased the door open onto a night so clear it felt like falling. Stars in brutal abundance, the glacier above the valley catching their light and throwing back a faint grey luminescence, the whole basin visible in negative: black ridgelines, blacker trees, the pale scar of the moraine trail.

No moon. Ezequiel had chosen the night for that too.

She moved along the wall of the detention hut, then cut north through the vehicle park, keeping the idling truck between herself and the mess tent. Up close she could see why the engine ran: a man sat in the cab, and the cab's heater ticked, and the man was Ezequiel, and laid across the seat beside him, wrapped in a canvas rag, were three rifles and a bandolier of magazines that gleamed dully when the starlight caught the brass.

He rolled the window down as she approached. His face in the dark was unreadable, carved out of the same material as the mountains.

"You're early," he said.

"I'm never early."

A sound that might have been a laugh, quickly strangled. He handed the rifles out through the window, one at a time, and then a canvas satchel that clinked. "Flares. Two. And this." A pistol, compact, cold. "Don't miss."

"I don't shoot," Mara said, taking it anyway. "I negotiate. But I've been told my negotiating has gotten more physical lately."

Something flickered across his face—disapproval, or maybe its opposite. "You should know what happens tonight," he said. "Before you're part of it. So you can't say you didn't choose it with your eyes open."

"I've been choosing things with my eyes open for thirty years, Ezequiel. It's the closing them that's dangerous."

He looked at her for a long moment. Then he reached beneath the seat and brought out a photograph, creased soft with handling, and held it where she could see it in the dashboard's faint glow. A younger version of himself, in a fishing hat, kneeling on a bank beside an older man holding a fish the size of an arm. Behind them, water. Clear water. The Ceniza before.

"He took me there every Sunday," Ezequiel said. "Eleven years old, first time I hooked something bigger than me, he didn't grab the rod. He just talked me through it. Two hours, he talked me through it, and I landed it myself." He put the photograph away with the same care he'd used on everything. "Reyes thinks we guard this valley because we're paid. Some of us guard it because it's a grave and somebody has to stand watch until it's dug up."

"And tonight you dig?"

"Tonight I make noise." He turned the key and the engine rose from idle to a growl. "The mess will empty. Men run toward trouble; that's what they're trained for. While they run at my truck, you run at the bridge. The cache lock is a Master padlock, cheap, the shackle gives if you twist with the rifle barrel. There's one guard, maybe two. They'll be watching the road, not the river side." He paused. "After you have your engineer, go up the moraine trail, west fork, past the cairn with the red paint. Don't stop for anything. Don't wait for me."

Mara looked at him. "That's a strange sentence, Ezequiel."

"It's a practical sentence." But his hands stayed on the wheel, gripping it, and she understood suddenly and completely what he was not saying, and her chest went tight in a way that had nothing to do with altitude.

"There are other ways—"

"There aren't." Flat. Final. A man closing a ledger. "Eleven people, señora. My father makes twelve. I've spent eight years pressing on the bruise. Tonight I press hard." He put the truck in gear. "Midnight. The ridge changes watch at midnight, and for ninety seconds nobody official is looking anywhere. Be at the bridge."

He drove. Not toward the gate—toward the fuel dump at the compound's edge, where the drums stood ranked in their earthen berm, and Mara watched the taillights shrink and understood, with a lurch, exactly what kind of noise he intended to make.

* * *

She ran the kilometer to the bridge in nineteen minutes, which would have been respectable at sea level and felt, at this altitude, like dying politely. The rifle bounced against her spine with every stride. Twice she went down on scree and got up with her palms burning. The outlet channel roared alongside her, glacial water hammering through its rock throat, covering whatever sound she made and half of what she thought.

The bridge appeared as a darker darkness against the sky—old timber on steel pilings, the survey crew's crossing, sagging in the middle. Beside it squatted the gear cache, a steel container with a single door facing the road, a lantern burning dim inside the wire mesh of its window slit. One silhouette moved behind the mesh. One. Thank God and Ezequiel both.

She circled wide, down to the channel bank, and came at the cache from the river side exactly as instructed, boots in cold water, breath tearing. Through the mesh she could hear the guard humming. Actually humming. A young man, bored, alone, counting the minutes until relief.

At the corner of the container she stopped, pressed her back to the freezing steel, and waited for midnight.

It came not as a clock struck but as a light.

The fuel dump bloomed orange at the far end of the valley—a whump she felt through the ground before she heard it, then a column of flame standing up against the stars like something planted and growing fast. Three seconds later the sound arrived, rolling across the basin, and with it every dog, every man, every alarm the compound possessed. Voices erupted. The mess tent emptied in a spill of lantern light and shouting. Somewhere a whistle shrieked three short blasts—fire protocol—and men began running toward the blaze exactly as Ezequiel had promised they would, trained animals all, running toward the trouble he had made.

The guard at the cache stepped out of the door to look.

Mara came around the corner and put the barrel of the rifle across the back of his knees and her hand over his mouth and lowered him to the ground in one motion that surprised her with its own competence. He was nineteen, maybe twenty. His eyes were enormous.

"Don't," she whispered. "You saw nothing. You were checking the fire. Go."

He went. She watched him sprint toward the flames and felt no pride in it, only a cold arithmetic: everyone in this valley was being spent tonight, one way or another.

The padlock fought her for thirty seconds—rifle barrel through the shackle, twist, the cheap metal groaning—and then gave with a snap that sounded like a shot. She hauled the door open.

Teo lay on a foam pad in the dark, wrists zip-tied to a pipe rack, one eye swollen shut, lip split. Even in the starlight spilling through the door she could see how bad the last three days had been. But his good eye found her and focused, and the ghost of his crooked grin surfaced through the damage.

"Took you long enough," he rasped. "I was starting to think I'd have to engineer my own rescue."

"Shut up and give me your hands." She cut the ties with the knife from her boot cuff. Blood rushed back into his fingers and he hissed. "Can you walk?"

"Can I walk. Ilsa would have a field day with that question." He got an arm over her shoulder and stood, swaying, and she felt how much weight he'd lost, how much of him the days had eaten. "Ansel—"

"Is drawing the pursuit, like planned. Pico and Ilsa have the documents on the west fork. We join them past the painted cairn."

"Then let's not keep the family waiting."

They made it fifty meters onto the bridge.

* * *

The flare went up from the direction of the compound—red, stuttering, hanging over the burning fuel dump like a wound in the sky—and in its light Mara saw the road fill with headlights.

Two vehicles, coming fast from the compound. Not toward the fire. Toward the bridge.

"They know," Teo breathed. "Reyes knows."

Of course he knew. Fire at the fuel dump, prisoner gone, guard missing—it wasn't a puzzle, it was a signature. Mara shoved Teo behind the bridge's mid-span piling and pulled the pistol Ezequiel had given her, and the lead vehicle slewed to a stop at the near end of the bridge and men spilled out of it, four of them, five, rifles up, and a voice she knew called across the water.

"Voss!" Reyes. Amplified by the canyon, calm as ever, that terrible reasonableness carrying over the roar of the channel. "You have my engineer. I have your mountain. Let's not make this complicated."

She pressed her eye to a gap in the planking. Five men at the bridgehead, spread in a firing line. More coming. The fire glowed behind them, and against that glow, at the compound's edge, a single figure stood beside a burning drum bay with a rifle in his hands, and even at this distance she knew the breadth of those shoulders, the unhurried stance.

Ezequiel hadn't run with the others toward the fire. He'd stayed at its edge, and he was shooting—not at men, at vehicles, at tires, at the headlight of the second truck, which died in a spray of glass. Deliberate. Loud. Drawing every eye, every muzzle, making himself the loudest thing in the valley.

*Use the noise,* he'd said. He'd meant this. He'd meant all of it.

"Last chance, Voss!" Reyes shouted. "Or we come across and take everything—him, you, and whatever your friends are carrying up that moraine!"

Mara looked at Teo. Teo looked at her, and read her face the way engineers read load-bearing walls.

"No," he said.

"We can't outrun them on this span. It's open timber, we're silhouetted, and they'll shoot us off it like—" She stopped. Recalculated. The river side. The channel bank. Fifty meters of boulder field between the bridge's far footing and the treeline, all of it in shadow, none of it covered by the men at the road end, because they were watching the bridge, because everyone watches the obvious line.

"Ezequiel's still shooting," she said. "Every second he keeps them looking backward is a second we don't have to spend. Down the footing, into the rocks, west. Can you move fast for two hundred meters?"

Teo's grin came back, bloodied and magnificent. "I can move fast for anything if the alternative is Reyes."

They went down the far footing hand over hand, dropped the last six feet into the boulder field, and ran—crouched, stumbling, Teo's breath sawing beside her—while behind them the firefight at the compound swelled and scattered. She heard Reyes shout orders, heard boots on the bridge timbers overhead, heard a burst of automatic fire rip the night and knew it wasn't aimed at them.

It was aimed at the fuel dump. At the man standing in front of it.

Mara skidded to a stop on a flat boulder and turned, and in the firelight she saw it happen the way you see things in nightmares: clearly, slowly, completely.

Ezequiel Ordoñez had taken cover behind the last standing drum rack, and three of Reyes's men had flanked it from the vehicle park, and there was nowhere for the flanking fire to miss. She saw him turn toward them. She saw him keep shooting anyway—covering the bridge, covering the boulder field, covering her and Teo with every round he had left, because that had been the plan, the whole plan, the plan he had chosen with his eyes open. She saw him stagger. Saw the drum rack behind him erupt. Saw him go down among the flames he'd made, and stay down.

The sound Mara made then was not a word in any language. Teo caught her arm and hauled her onward, and she went, legs moving on their own, grief and fury and the obscene mechanics of survival all fused into one burning wire.

They reached the treeline. Shadow swallowed them. Behind them the fire roared, the channel roared, and somewhere above on the moraine a flashlight blinked twice—Pico, at the cairn, alive, waiting.

They climbed.

* * *

They found the others at the red-painted cairn just before two in the morning: Ilsa with the document satchel strapped across her chest like armor, Pico pacing, and Ansel standing at the edge of the overlook with his back to them, looking down into the valley at the fire.

When Mara came up over the last rise, he turned. His face in the dark was older than it had been a week ago, aged by something heavier than distance.

"Mara. You made it. Where's—"

"Gone," she said.

The word landed among all of them. Ilsa closed her eyes. Pico stopped pacing.

"He bought us the bridge," she said. Her voice came out level, which felt like a betrayal of everything underneath it, but level was all she had. "He burned the fuel dump, he held the flank, and he knew—he chose it, Ansel, he told me beforehand, he made me promise not to wait for him. Eight years he stood watch over his father's grave, and tonight he decided the watch was over." She looked down at the fire, at the tiny figures moving around its edges, at the smoke climbing toward the indifferent stars. "His name was Ezequiel Ordoñez. His father fished the Ceniza. He deserves to be in whatever we build out of this. Whatever testimony, whatever broadcast, whatever reckoning—his name goes in it. First, if I have anything to say about it."

Ansel was quiet for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, the way men nod at graves.

"He's in it," he said. "All of them are. That's the whole point now."

Teo sank down against the cairn, gray-faced, and Ilsa was already at his side with her hands on his wrist, counting, muttering, already working. Pico stood watch with the rifle, young and straight-backed, staring down at the fire with an expression Mara recognized because she'd worn it herself at twenty-three: the exact moment childhood ends.

It was Ansel who broke the silence, and when he spoke, his voice had a sound she'd never heard from him—not the guide's calm, not the fugitive's wariness. Something harder and brighter, forged in the last hour.

"Reyes thinks he knows where we are and what we have. He doesn't. And he just lost a fuel dump, a prisoner, and a night of sleep—which means tomorrow he stops being careful." Ansel turned to face them all, and the firelight from the valley below caught the edge of his jaw. "Which means we have one night. Maybe less. Before he comes up this mountain with everything he's got to bury us along with the evidence."

Above them, impossibly high, the glacier caught the first grey suggestion of dawn, and Mara thought: *one night*, and held the name *Ezequiel* in her mouth like a coal she refused to drop.
