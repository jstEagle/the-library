## 32. Demolition Day

Teo heard the first blast at dawn, and it was wrong.

Not the sound—though the sound was wrong too, a flat concussive thump that rolled down the valley walls like a boulder dropped into a dry cistern. It was the *timing*. Demolition crews didn't work at dawn. They worked mid-morning, after inspections, after the fog burned off, because nobody with a permit and a schedule blew rock into cloud cover they couldn't see through. Dawn charges meant one of two things: either the crew above them was sloppy, or they were in a hurry.

He lay in the shelter of a split boulder above the meltwater lake, binoculars pressed to his eyes, and counted. Four minutes between the first blast and the second. Then three. Then two and a half. The intervals were shrinking like a heartbeat accelerating.

"Teo." Ilsa's voice, low, from behind him. "You're seeing it too."

"I'm seeing it."

"They're not following a demolition sequence anymore. That's not how you take down a dam ruin—that's not even how you quarry. They're just breaking everything as fast as the drills cycle." She crouched beside him, her breath steaming, the stitched line of her jaw pale with cold. "Which means they're not trying to do it right. They're trying to do it *fast*."

Teo lowered the binoculars. Across the lake, maybe two kilometers distant, the ruins of the Ceniza dam stood in the gray light like the ribs of something enormous that had died on its side—and around those ribs, tiny with distance but unmistakable, orange machines crawled. Excavators. Drill rigs. A line of haul trucks moving spoil toward the new scar where the eastern abutment had already been chewed away.

And beyond them, on the access road that switchbacked down from the checkpoint, dust plumes. Vehicles coming up. More than yesterday.

"They know," he said. "Reyes knows we're in the valley. Maybe he's known since the moraine. And instead of hunting us the slow way—" he tapped the binoculars against his palm, thinking out loud, the way he did when the numbers frightened him, "—he's going to bury the whole question. The evidence, the ruin, the cave, and us. One operation. Tomorrow night, this valley is a crater with a lake in it, and whatever story gets told about four missing foreigners will be told by the only people left alive to tell it."

Ilsa said nothing for a moment. Below them, at the edge of the moraine where they'd cached the bulk of their gear under a tarp of frost-stiffened netting, Pico was repacking ropes with quick, angry hands, pretending not to listen, failing completely.

"You're assuming they can actually finish," Ilsa said. "That's a lot of tonnage."

"It's not tonnage, it's *will*." Teo handed her the binoculars. "Look at the eastern face. They've already dropped two-thirds of it since we got here. That's not a salvage job, that's an erasure, and erasure has a budget that doesn't care about engineering tolerances." He pulled his notebook from inside his jacket—a habit so old the notebook was more callus than paper now—and began writing figures in the margin, small and precise: blast intervals, truck cycle times, the visible number of rigs, the estimated charge sizes from the delay between flash and report. Numbers were the only language in which terror became useful. "By my count they'll have enough placed by tomorrow evening to bring the whole remaining structure and half the valley wall down into the lake basin. Everything Tomás hid in that cave goes under fifty meters of rubble and meltwater. Everything *we* found in the hut becomes unverifiable—their lawyers will say the site was unstable, that no independent survey could ever be confirmed against ground truth, because there won't be any ground left."

"So we move tonight."

"We move tonight." He looked at her. Her face was drawn, the altitude sitting on her like a debt collector, but her eyes were steady, and Teo thought—not for the first time—that of all of them, Ilsa had the most dangerous kind of courage, the kind that had already survived its own worst day and refused to be impressed by anything after. "The uplink station is fourteen hours' hard travel from here, over the saddle and down the far moraine. Ansel thinks ten if we push. We push."

"And Reyes?"

Teo turned back to the valley. On the far shore, near the demolition staging area, he could just make out a cluster of figures around a white vehicle, and even at this distance, even without resolution, he knew which one was Reyes, because the others orbited him. Men don't stand that way around a colleague. They stand that way around weather.

"He's done waiting," Teo said. "That's what the dawn blasts are really saying. Not to us—to his own crews. *We are past caution.* Which means tonight isn't just our window. It's our last one."

* * *

They gathered at noon in the lee of the boulder field, all five of them—Ansel, Mara, Ilsa, Pico, himself—with the hut documents spread inside waterproof bags and the core evidence divided across four packs so that no single capture could kill it. Ansel stood at the center with the glacier wall rising behind him, white and blue and indifferent, and Teo watched him the way he always watched Ansel when decisions were being made: looking for the fracture lines.

There weren't any. That was the strange thing. Ten days ago the man had been held together with guilt and habit, a guide whose entire genius was motion, who treated stillness like drowning. Now he stood planted like a man who had finally decided where north was.

"They've accelerated," Ansel said. "Teo's read is right. Demolition completes tomorrow night. Which means Reyes doesn't need to find us anymore—he needs to make sure nothing leaves this valley that can contradict the official record. We are now, officially, part of what gets buried." He paused. "I want to be honest about what that means. Until yesterday, the worst case was capture. Interrogation, deportation, prison, maybe worse, but *worse* was a possibility, not a plan. As of this morning, worse is the plan. If they take us, we don't come back. Anybody who wants to run downstream and take their chances with the patrols instead—I'll draw the route myself. No shame in it."

Nobody moved. Pico actually laughed, one short bark, and then looked embarrassed.

"All right," Ansel said, and something in his voice eased, though his face didn't. "Then here's the shape of it. We leave at dusk, while the shift change slows their observation. Two teams. Team one—Mara, Ilsa, Pico—carries the primary evidence set over the saddle to the uplink station. Fast movement, minimal gear, you travel light and you don't stop for anything short of a broken leg. Team two—Teo and I—carry the duplicate set along the high traverse west. Slower, harder ground, but it puts us in position to watch the demolition and gives Reyes two problems instead of one. Whichever team reaches the ridge station first transmits. The other covers."

"Why split the evidence at all?" Pico asked. "Why not all together, all speed?"

"Because together we're one target," Mara said. She'd been quiet all morning, her wounded shoulder strapped tight, her color bad but her mind running cold and clear. "Reyes has maybe thirty men and a valley to search. Split, we're two signatures he has to choose between. And if he chooses wrong—" she let it hang, "—the other team walks the truth off this mountain."

"And if he splits too?" Ilsa asked.

"Then it comes down to legs," Teo said. "And I like our legs."

Ansel caught his eye. Something passed between them that needed no translation—ten years of Teo forging passports and Ansel burning bridges had built a shorthand deeper than speech. Teo nodded once. *I know why you picked me for your team. You think I'm the one who can't afford to die. You're wrong, but I appreciate the sentiment.*

"One more thing," Ansel said, and now his voice changed, dropped into a register Teo had only heard once before, on the river, the day he'd pulled Pico out of the hole in the Class V rapid. "Tonight, nobody plays hero. Not for documents, not for each other. The evidence matters because we deliver it. Dead couriers carry nothing. If contact happens, you break away, you run, you live. Am I understood?"

"Understood," Mara said.

"Understood," said Ilsa.

Pico hesitated. Teo saw it happen in the boy's face—the flicker of the question he'd been carrying since the moraine, since the ambush, since the cave, the question of what you owed the dead versus what you owed the living. Then Pico said, "Understood," and Teo believed him, mostly.

The sun crossed the col and the temperature fell like a verdict. They spent the afternoon checking gear in silence broken only by the distant rhythm of the drills—thump, thump-thump, pause, thump—a pulse counting down. Teo catalogued the duplicate document set twice, sealed it, weighed both packs until they matched within half a kilo, and then sat with his back against stone and did the thing he did instead of praying: he ran calculations.

Fourteen hours to the station, loaded, at altitude, in the dark. Call it sixteen with the traverse. Demolition complete tomorrow night—say thirty hours from now, plus or minus Reyes's patience. That gave them a margin of fourteen hours, minus whatever Reyes did tonight to spend it for them.

The math was fine. The math was *fine*. He wrote it in the notebook anyway, three times, because the hand wanted something to hold.

* * *

Dusk came up the valley like water filling a bowl, purple first, then iron, then black. Above the demolition site, floodlights snapped on in a chain, turning the dam ruin into a stage set for something obscene—machines silhouetted against glare, exhaust plumes lit gold, the whole works humming with diesel and intent. From the high traverse, Teo and Ansel could see all of it laid out below like a diagram of the crime itself.

They'd been climbing for two hours. The traverse was everything Teo had promised and less than he'd feared—narrow, loose, exposed to the drop into the lake basin, but passable at a steady pace, and Ansel moved through it with the economy of a man walking his own hallway. Twice they froze as patrol lights swept the lower slopes; twice the lights moved on, occupied with the perimeter below, not the sky above.

It was during the second freeze, lying flat against scree with his cheek against stone that still held the day's last heat, that Teo saw the convoy.

Headlights on the access road. Six vehicles, maybe seven, moving up from the checkpoint in tight formation—not the casual spacing of supply runs. This was a deployment. The lead vehicle carried a light bar, unlit, and the trucks behind it were the canvas-backed kind, the kind that carried men.

"Ansel." He kept his voice below the wind. "Road. Count the lights."

Ansel counted. Teo watched him count, watched the stillness come over him—not fear, exactly. Recalculation. The look of a man doing arithmetic with lives in the columns.

"That's not a perimeter sweep," Ansel said finally. "That's a sweep of the *high country*. They're going camp to camp, cache to cache, working every place we might have slept." He was quiet a moment. "They found the hut."

"The hut's empty. We stripped it."

"They'll find our fire rings. Our track. And then they'll find the bivouac we used two nights ago, and they'll follow our line backward or forward, and forward leads—" he stopped.

"To the saddle," Teo finished. "To Mara's team."

Below them, the convoy reached the switchback below the moraine and halted. Doors opened. Lights spilled out, a dozen handheld beams fanning across the talus in practiced sweeps. Somewhere a radio crackled, thin and metallic on the wind.

Ansel's jaw worked. Teo knew what he was watching: the old Ansel, the runner, calculating the vector that saved his own skin, and the new Ansel arriving a half-second later to overrule him. The struggle lasted three seconds. Then Ansel keyed the radio—short range, encrypted, Mara's frequency—and spoke five words.

"Mara. Convoy moving north. Move."

Static. A breath. Then Mara's voice, calm as a weather report: "Copy. Already moving. Don't be late, Sandoval."

Ansel exhaled. But Teo was watching the valley floor, and what he saw made his stomach drop through the scree and keep falling.

The convoy wasn't continuing north.

The vehicles had stopped, and the men were deploying—spreading out in a line across the base of the moraine, headlights doused one by one until only three remained, positioned like the points of a triangle. And at the center of the triangle, a figure stood beside the white vehicle with a megaphone, though at this distance the voice that came up the valley was just a shapeless amplification, words without edges.

Except the last sentence had edges. The last sentence carried.

"—or be buried with the rest of it!"

Silence took the valley back. The floodlights at the dam flared brighter, as if in answer, and the drills resumed their countdown rhythm—thump, thump-thump—faster now, faster, a drumbeat with no more rests in it.

Ansel lay very still. When he spoke, his voice had gone somewhere Teo hadn't heard before: past fear, past fury, into a flat cold country where decisions get made.

"He's giving us till dawn," Ansel said. "Surrender, or the demolition takes us with the evidence." He turned his head, and in the dark his eyes caught the distant floodlight glow, two embers in a hollow face. "Teo. How fast can you build me a transmitter?"

Teo stared at him. "What?"

"The uplink station needs line of sight and power, and we have neither. But you built a field radio out of a truck alternator outside Iquique when the border police had our real one. So I'm asking." Ansel's hand closed on his shoulder, grip like a carabiner. "If we can't outrun the deadline—we break it. Tonight. From right here."

Teo looked down the slope: at the triangle of lights, at the crawling machines, at the dam ruin standing its last night against the stars. Then at his pack, where the duplicate documents rode against his spine, and at the wrecked patrol radio they'd hauled out of the ambush site ten days ago because Teo never threw away a component that could carry current.

His mind was already building it—antenna length, ground plane, battery draw, the cold-crushed limits of every part he owned. It would be ugly. It would barely work. It would take all night.

The drumbeat of the drills filled the valley, faster and faster, and Teo heard it for what it was now: not a countdown.

A starting gun.

"Give me two hours," he said, "and a reason for them not to shoot at the light."
