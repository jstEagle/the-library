## 33. Into the Ice Cave

The demolition schedule was written on a clipboard in Reyes's abandoned forward camp, and it was the most honest document Ansel had read in ten years.

*Phase Three: Controlled collapse of dam remnant and associated ice structures. Detonation window: 0600 tomorrow.*

He stood in the wrecked tent with the clipboard in his hands while wind worried the canvas overhead like an animal trying to get at what was inside. Outside, the light was going the color of old bone. Somewhere below, across the valley floor, the meltwater lake lay flat and gray beneath the glacier's snout, and above it rose the ruin—what remained of the Ceniza dam after ten years of frost and neglect and now, apparently, deliberate erasure. A broken wall of concrete and steel jutting from the ice like a rib from a butchered animal.

Teo came through the tent flap behind him, breathing hard from the climb up the moraine, his wounded arm strapped tight against his chest. He'd escaped Reyes's men two hours ago with rope burns on both wrists and a look in his eyes that Ansel hadn't seen before—not fear exactly. Calculation.

"You found something," Teo said.

Ansel handed him the clipboard without a word.

Teo read fast. His lips moved over *Phase Three* twice. "They're not just demolishing the dam ruins. They're bringing down the whole ice face." He looked up. "Why? What's under there that's worth this?"

And Ansel felt it happen—the thing he'd been dreading since the moraine, since the survey hut, since the moment he'd understood that his brother had walked ahead of him for a decade without ever once looking back. The memory didn't come as fragments anymore. It came whole, arriving like water finding a crack, filling a space he hadn't known was empty.

"The cave," he said.

"What cave?"

"Under the dam. Before the failure, there was a drainage gallery—a maintenance tunnel cut into the bedrock, and where the tunnel met the glacier toe, the melt had hollowed out an ice cave. Tomás worked the site. He showed me once, when I visited him that summer. He said—" Ansel stopped. Swallowed. "He said it was the coldest quiet place he'd ever stood. He liked it because nothing could hear you in there."

Teo waited. He was good at waiting. It was one of the things that made him an engineer.

"He cached things," Ansel said. "In the hut we found his field notes—the ones you translated. Page forty-one. He wrote about moving the originals somewhere the company would never search. I thought he meant a cache on the mountain somewhere. A crevasse, a cairn. But he wasn't talking about hiding them from searchers." He looked at the clipboard again. "He was talking about hiding them from time. From weather. From fire. You hide evidence in the one place nobody can burn it down."

"Ice," Teo said slowly.

"A glacier preserves everything. Ten years, fifty years—the recordings stay exactly as he left them. And nobody goes into a cave sealed under a disaster site. Nobody except the people who built the disaster, and they never wanted to look at what they'd done." Ansel's voice came out flat, which was how he knew how bad it was. When it shook, he was still performing. When it went flat, it was true. "But now they're demolishing it. Tomorrow morning, six o'clock, they bring the whole face down and whatever's inside the ice goes under a hundred thousand tons of rubble, and no one will ever dig it out. Not in our lifetime. Maybe not ever."

Teo set the clipboard down on the camp table with exaggerated care, the way he did when he was angry enough to want to throw it. "So we tell Mara and Ilsa and Pico. We go in together tonight, we get the recordings, we're back out by midnight."

"No."

"No?" Teo's eyebrows went up. "You want to explain that word to me, or are we just doing monosyllables now?"

"It's a single-file route. The gallery tunnel is collapsed partway—I remember that much, even if I don't remember the rest. One person can chimney through the break. Two people can't. And the ice above the cave has been taking charges all week—they've been test-firing, listening to the echoes. Every hour we're in there, the roof is less roof." Ansel met his eyes. "One person goes. Fast. In and out before the light dies."

"And that person is you, obviously."

"That person is me."

"Because you're the strongest climber? Because you know the route?" Teo stepped closer, and despite the strapped arm he somehow managed to make himself take up space. "Or because your brother is in there? Because some part of you thinks if you crawl deep enough into that ice, you'll find the last hour of your own life waiting for you, and you'd rather die trying than live another day not knowing?"

The wind filled the silence between them. Out past the tent, the first stars were coming up over the western ridge, cold and indifferent.

"Yes," Ansel said finally. "All of it. And it doesn't change the answer."

"It changes the risk."

"The risk was always mine. That was the deal when any of you signed on. I lead; I go first; I go deepest. That's not heroism, Teo. That's arithmetic. If I don't come back, you still have the documents from the hut, you have Mara's testimony, you have Pico who knows the uplink route. The mission survives me. It doesn't survive losing anyone else."

Teo stared at him for a long moment. Then he did something unexpected—he laughed, short and bitter, wincing around his injured arm. "Ilsa said you'd do this. She called it. She said, 'Watch, he'll find a reason to go alone.' She said men who carry guilt long enough start treating it like a load-bearing wall. Take it out and the whole building comes down."

"She's not wrong about buildings," Ansel said. "She's wrong about this one. This isn't guilt. This is the only door left, and I'm the only one small enough—or stupid enough—to fit through it."

They argued for another twenty minutes. Teo lost, which he acknowledged by sitting down heavily on a supply crate and putting his head in his good hand. But he won something too: Ansel agreed to carry a radio, agreed to a hard turnaround time—if he wasn't out by 2300, they'd assume the worst and move to the uplink without him—and agreed, though it cost him more than he expected to say it, to let Pico rig the anchor line at the gallery mouth so that someone else's hands, not his own, were responsible for pulling him back if the ice decided otherwise.

When Teo left to brief the others, Ansel stayed alone in the tent and took inventory, the way he always did before moving. Rope, sixty meters. Headlamp plus backup, plus the chemical light sticks Ilsa pressed on him against his better judgment. Ice screws, four. The piton hammer that had been Tomás's—he'd carried it since the hut, unable to leave it among the company's abandoned papers. And at the bottom of his pack, wrapped in a plastic bag against sweat, the photograph Mara had left on his table a lifetime ago, in a border town he could be expelled from any day. The valley from above. The glacier. The ruin. He'd hated her for leaving it. He'd looked at it every night since.

He put it back. Some weights you carried because putting them down meant admitting you'd been carrying them.

Outside, the others waited at the edge of the moraine where the glacier's lateral moraine dropped toward the valley floor. Mara stood with her arms crossed, silhouetted against the last gray light, and she didn't argue at all. She'd already spent her argument days ago, at the checkpoint, offering her own name as bait. There was nothing left to trade that she wasn't already spending.

"Pico rigged the line," she said. "Double anchors, backed up. He says if you die in there he's going to be very angry with you, and you should think about that."

"Tell him I'm terrified of his anger."

"I'll tell him you're a liar." Her voice softened by a degree, which for Mara was a confession. "Tomás went in there first, didn't he. Ten years ago."

"He went everywhere first. That was the problem with Tomás. He never believed the mountain could beat him, and one day he was going to be right or dead, and I always figured it was a coin flip." Ansel checked his harness buckles, one by one, feeling for the click. "Turns out it was neither. Turns out he was right, and it killed him anyway, because being right doesn't matter when the other side owns the guns."

Mara caught his arm as he turned to go. Her grip was stronger than it looked. "Come back out," she said. "Not for the evidence. For the reading of the names. Somebody has to say his name out loud, and I think it has to be you, and I refuse to read eleven names alone."

Ansel looked at her hand on his arm. Then he nodded, once, and stepped off the moraine onto the ice.

* * *

The descent to the dam ruin took forty minutes and cost him more than he wanted to count.

The glacier's surface here was shattered—seracs leaning at drunk angles, crevasses opening under the headlamp beam like black mouths, the whole lower tongue groaning and ticking as the night cold locked it solid. He moved the way he'd taught a hundred clients to move: probe, weight, commit. Probe, weight, commit. The rhythm of it emptied his mind of everything except the next step, which was the mercy of mountains. They didn't care what you remembered. They only cared where you put your feet.

The ruin appeared out of the dark all at once, the way large things do.

Concrete. Ten years of frost had chewed it, but the scale of it still landed in his chest like a blow—the spillway section standing thirty meters high, rebar hanging from the broken crest like exposed nerves, the whole structure tilted slightly downstream as if it had tried to follow the water it failed to hold. He'd seen photographs. Photographs were nothing. Standing beneath it, hearing the wind play across the broken edges like breath across a bottle, he understood that he had spent a decade remembering a disaster and never once reckoning with its body.

*Eleven people,* he thought. *This is what eleven people looks like.*

The gallery entrance was where he remembered it—or where the memory said it should be, which these days amounted to the same thing. A square-cut portal in the rock abutment at the dam's north end, half-choked with ice, the old steel door long since stripped for salvage. Above it, fresh in the beam, he saw the marks he'd feared: drill scars, orange survey paint, a coil of detonation cord staked along the crest of the ruin with the casual precision of professionals. They'd wired the whole face. Tomorrow morning it would come down, and the portal would be buried under a hundred thousand tons of deliberately arranged history.

Pico's anchor line ran taut from a flake of rock beside the portal back up the slope. Good hands. He touched it once in thanks and ducked inside.

The tunnel swallowed the wind immediately, and the quiet arrived like water over his head.

It was exactly as Tomás had described it, ten years ago, laughing, young, invincible: the coldest quiet place. The drainage gallery ran straight into the mountain for perhaps eighty meters, its concrete walls furred with frost, and the air was so still that his own breathing sounded like a stranger's. His headlamp made a small bright room that traveled with him, and beyond its edge the darkness was total and ancient.

At thirty meters, the collapse.

The tunnel was blocked floor to ceiling by a jumble of rock and ice, but a gap remained near the top—a slot maybe forty centimeters wide, polished smooth by meltwater, angling upward into blackness. He remembered it. God help him, he remembered climbing it, though the memory had no faces attached, no words, just the physical truth of pressing his chest against wet stone while the mountain pressed back.

He went up the way water goes up a crack: slowly, by persuasion. The pack scraped. The piton hammer dug into his hip. Twice the slot narrowed and he had to exhale everything and force himself through on sheer refusal, and both times a voice in his skull—the sensible one, the guide's voice, the one that kept crews alive—said *this is where you turn around, this is where bodies happen.* And both times another voice answered, younger, hoarse, familiar: *keep coming, Ansel. I left the light on for you.*

He told himself he imagined it. He kept climbing.

The slot opened into the cave, and he hung there a moment at the lip, letting the headlamp find the walls, because some places deserve a held breath.

The meltwater had carved a cathedral. The chamber was larger than the gallery, larger than anything the tunnel had promised—a vault of blue-white ice rising into darkness, its walls scalloped into flowing forms, lit from within by his beam so that the whole space glowed like something alive. Meltwater dripped somewhere far off, each drop distinct as a struck bell. The floor was bare ice, swept clean, and against the far wall—

Against the far wall, a dry-stone cairn. Human-built. Deliberate. With a strip of survey tape fluttering faintly in a draft he couldn't feel, faded almost white, but still legible where the marker pen had held:

*T.S.*

His brother's initials. His brother's handwriting. He knew it the way he knew his own pulse—the backward S, the heavy crossbar.

Ansel crossed the cave floor on his knees, because standing suddenly seemed like too much to ask of his body. He pulled the cairn down stone by stone, carefully, the way you uncover a sleeping thing, and underneath, sealed in a waterproof case crusted with a decade of frost, he found what Tomás had died carrying toward the light.

A recorder. Field model, ruggedized, the kind survey crews used. Beside it, three data drives in anti-static bags, labeled in that same backward-S hand: *ORDER / PAYMENT / NAMES*. And beneath the case, folded and frozen stiff, a single sheet of paper that he thawed against his chest with shaking hands until the ink swam into meaning.

*If you're reading this, then either the company found it—in which case, burn it and run, little brother—or you finally stopped running. I bet on the second one. I always bet on you, even when you wouldn't bet on yourself. Everything's on the recorder. Play track one first. And Ansel—whatever you hear, whatever they made you forget: it was never your fault. It was never your fault. Come home.*

The paper blurred. He let it. There was no one in the dark to see, and the cold took the tears before they could fall, and he knelt in his brother's cathedral with his brother's letter against his heart and grieved for ten years in the space of sixty seconds—grieved cleanly, for the first time, because grief needs a grave and he had finally found one.

Then the mountain cleared its throat.

It started as a sound he felt rather than heard—a deep, structural groan rolling through the ice above, followed by a crack like a rifle shot, sharp and close. Frost sifted down through his headlamp beam. Somewhere overhead, a serac the size of a house shifted its weight and settled with a grinding moan that went on and on.

The test charges. They were firing the test charges early—or the day's blasting had destabilized more than their engineers predicted, and the ice above the dam was beginning to answer questions no one had asked it yet.

Another crack, closer. A hairline fracture raced across the cave ceiling and stopped directly above him, glowing faintly blue in the lamplight like a vein.

Ansel looked at the recorder in his hands. At the drives. At the exit slot, forty centimeters wide, forty meters away.

*Play track one first,* the letter said.

There was no time for both. There might not be time for either.

He jammed the drives and the letter into his jacket, seized the recorder, and thumbed the power switch as the second serac began to let go.
