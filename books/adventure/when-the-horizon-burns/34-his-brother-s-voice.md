## 34. His Brother's Voice

The cold in the ice cave was not the cold of altitude. It was older than that, patient as stone, and it pressed against Ansel's skin like a hand laid flat over his chest—checking his heartbeat, deciding whether to keep it.

He had left his pack at the mouth of the cave. Everything but the headlamp and the pistol he'd taken from a patrolman at the moraine and never once wanted to use. The lamp's beam cut a white cone through air so still it felt held, the way a room holds its breath when someone is about to say something irreversible.

Behind him, maybe four hundred meters back through the blue dark, the dam ruin sat under its net of demolition charges. Above him, the glacier groaned in a language he'd spent ten years pretending not to understand. And somewhere ahead of him—Tomás had said so, in a message recorded a decade ago and hidden where only a madman or a brother would look—was the rest of it. The original recordings. The thing worth killing eleven people to bury and worth demolishing a valley to keep buried.

Ansel walked into the cold and let it have him.

* * *

He'd found the entrance the way Tomás must have found it: by accident, by almost dying. A crevasse bridge near the old dam's eastern abutment, snow-bridged over a gap that dropped into the glacier's gut. He'd probed it with an axe shaft on instinct, the way you check a floorboard in a house you don't trust, and the shaft had gone through into hollow space—and beneath the hollow, faint as a coin at the bottom of a well, a smell that didn't belong. Diesel. Old canvas. Human habitation, sealed in ice.

Tomás had been here. Not after the flood. Before it.

That was the part that kept Ansel moving when every nerve in his body screamed to turn around. His brother hadn't hidden this evidence while fleeing for his life. He'd cached supplies here first—rope, fuel, food with expiration dates that predated the disaster by months—which meant Tomás had come up the Ceniza before the dam failed, which meant he'd known something was coming, which meant—

Which meant everything. Which meant the last hour of Ansel's missing memory might not be the worst thing buried in this ice.

The cave opened around him in chambers, melt-carved, the walls scalloped like the inside of a shell. His breath hung in the beam and drifted sideways, following a current of air that moved through the glacier the way blood moves through a body. He followed the current. Somewhere in the back of his mind, Pico's voice: *You go in alone, we pull you out alone, tío. That's the deal.* And Ilsa's voice, flat with fear she wouldn't perform: *Two hours. If you're not back in two hours, we're coming in after you, and then we all die in here together, which is a stupid plan, so don't make me use it.*

"Two hours," Ansel said aloud, and the cave took the words and gave nothing back.

* * *

He found the cache chamber forty minutes in.

He knew it before the light reached it, because the air changed—warmer by a degree, carrying that diesel-and-canvas smell stronger now, and underneath it something else. Something domestic. Coffee gone stale in a tin. The ghost of a man who had lived here long enough to make it a place.

The beam swept across a shelf of natural ice, level as if cut by a mason, and on it: two aluminum boxes, a coil of rope wrapped in plastic, a camp stove, a sleeping bag compressed into its sack. And beside them, weighted down with a carabiner against the draft, a notebook with a rubber band around it, and a small hard case the size of a brick.

Ansel stood there for a long time without moving.

Ten years ago he had stood at a funeral in a town that no longer existed, watching them lower an empty coffin, because they had never recovered Tomás's body from the flood. He had put a hand on the coffin lid and promised his brother, silently, in the language they'd invented as boys on their mother's porch—a language of tapped knuckles and whistled notes—that he would find out what happened. Then he'd lost the memory of the night itself, lost his reputation, lost two countries, and told himself the promise had dissolved like everything else.

His brother had beaten him here by a decade. Had climbed into this ice with a recorder and a notebook and whatever terrible thing he'd learned, and had died trying to carry it out.

"I'm late," Ansel said. His voice cracked in the middle of the word and he let it. "I know. I'm sorry. I'm—"

He couldn't finish it. He crouched instead, and picked up the case, and his hands were shaking so badly he had to brace it against his knee to work the latches.

* * *

Inside the case, nested in foam: a ruggedized field camera, older model, scuffed at the corners the way equipment gets scuffed when someone uses it every day and loves it carelessly. A battery pack, still holding charge after ten years in the cold—Tomás had wrapped it in chemical warmers, the clever bastard, the same trick their father used for the truck battery in July frosts. And a folded sheet of paper, typed, with a line across the top that made Ansel's vision swim:

**MEMORANDUM — INTERNAL — DO NOT DUPLICATE — CONSOLIDADA ANDINA S.A.**

Consolidada Andina. The consortium that had dissolved itself on paper the week after eleven people drowned, leaving behind a security apparatus that patrolled the watershed like a private army. The name hit him like cold water in the face. He'd spent years telling himself the company was a shapeless villain, a fog. Here was its name in his brother's handwriting in the margin: *They knew. Get it out.*

Below the memo were three pages of Tomás's own notes, cramped, urgent, the handwriting deteriorating page by page as though the writer were running out of time even as he wrote:

*Overtopping scheduled. Not maintenance — they're going to open the gates past capacity during the storm and blame the weather. The tailings pond is leaking into the aquifer; the test data proves it and the data has to disappear. The data is IN THE DAM. Records vault, sublevel. Eleven of us are supposed to be downstream when it goes.*

*Eleven of us.*

*I got a copy of the order. I'm putting it somewhere they'll never look because they think this valley will be sealed forever. If anyone finds this — find Ansel Sandoval. My brother. He doesn't remember yet but he will. He's the only one stubborn enough to finish it.*

Ansel read the last line four times. The beam trembled. In the ice around him, the glacier ticked and settled, and somewhere far above, muffled by meters of frozen water, came a sound he refused to name—the deep, structural cough of a mountain being prepared for demolition.

*He doesn't remember yet but he will.*

"You knew," Ansel whispered. "You knew what they did to me."

Because that was the shape of it now, unavoidable, lit up in his brother's cramped hand: Tomás had known about the missing hour. Had known Ansel was pulled from the water downstream of anywhere a swimmer could reach, dumped like cargo, thumbprinted onto a false report while half-conscious. Had known and had written it down and had trusted—had *trusted*—that the truth would surface in his brother eventually, the way bodies surface.

Ansel set the notes down carefully, the way you set down something sacred, and turned on the camera.

* * *

The screen glowed to life with a battery warning and then, mercifully, held. The footage was date-stamped nine days before the flood.

Grainy color, handheld shake. A corridor Ansel recognized with a jolt that went through him like a fall—the records vault sublevel of the Ceniza dam, where he and Tomás had both worked the season before, where Tomás had shown him how to bypass the card reader with a bent shim, laughing, saying *don't tell anyone your brother's a criminal, Anselito.*

On screen, Tomás set the camera on a shelf, angled it, and stepped back into frame. He looked younger. Thinner. There was a bruise along his jaw that Ansel didn't remember ever seeing, and his eyes had the specific shine of a man who hasn't slept in days and has decided sleep is a luxury for people with longer futures.

"This is Tomás Sandoval," he said to the lens, "and if you're watching this, then either I got it out—" a crooked smile, there and gone— "or I didn't, and you're the one I trusted to come get it."

Ansel lowered himself to sit cross-legged on the ice, the way you sit at a graveside, and watched his brother talk to him across ten years of dead air.

Tomás held documents up to the camera one by one. A signed authorization for emergency drawdown testing—dated for the night of the storm, authorized by a name Ansel recognized from the blacklist hearings, a vice-president of Consolidada Andina whose signature had been conspicuously absent from every inquiry. Test results from an independent lab showing arsenic and heavy metals in monitoring wells at concentrations that would have shuttered the mine. A schedule of payments to a security contractor—the same contractor whose descendants still patrolled these slopes—with a line item marked *site sterilization, contingency.*

"Sterilization," Tomás repeated on screen, and laughed without any humor in it at all. "That's the word they used. For us. For the valley. For the water." He set the papers down and leaned close to the lens, and his voice dropped, went rough. "There's a meeting tomorrow. I'm going to record it if I can. Reyes runs security now—he's the one who signs off on everything, the one who talks about the river like it's a problem with a budget. If anything happens to me, it's him. Whatever story they tell, it's him."

Reyes. The name landed in Ansel's chest like a dropped axe. The man who had interrogated Mara at the checkpoint. The man whose crews were sweeping this valley right now, tonight, hunting them.

On screen, Tomás glanced off-camera—someone coming?—and when he looked back his face had changed, hurried, stripped to essentials.

"Last thing. Ansel—if it's you, if you're the one sitting there—" he exhaled, and for a moment the mask of purpose fell away and he was just a scared young man talking to his big brother, "—I need you to know I'm not doing this to be brave. I'm doing it because Mom raised us to leave places better than we found them, and I can't leave this one like this. And because you always said the mountain tells you the truth if you shut up long enough to listen. Well. I listened. It's bad, hermano. It's worse than bad."

A sound off-screen. Tomás killed the recording mid-motion, and the screen went to static, and the static went to black, and Ansel sat in the dark behind his own dimming headlamp with his brother's last words cooling in the air between them.

Then, because the camera held more than one file, he scrolled.

The next file was dated the day before the flood. Forty seconds long. Audio mostly, camera pointed at the floor of a vehicle. Two men talking in the front seat, voices half-lost to engine noise—but clear enough, clear enough forever:

*"—Sandoval's brother's been asking questions. He's got copies of something."*

*"Reyes knows. After the drawdown, the brother handles it. The other one—the guide—they'll collect him out of the water. He signs the incident report and everyone forgets there ever was a vault."*

*"And if he doesn't sign?"*

A pause. Then, mild as weather: *"Then the river keeps him too."*

* * *

Ansel did not remember standing up. He became aware of himself already upright, already moving, the camera clutched to his chest with both arms like something rescued from a fire, and he was making a sound he didn't recognize as coming from him—low, continuous, animal. They had planned it. All of it. His brother's death, his own drowning, his disgrace, the blacklist that had shaped every year of his life since—all of it drafted in the front seat of a truck in voices as casual as men discussing freight rates.

And Tomás had heard enough of that planning to hide the proof, and had died anyway, and had still believed—written it down in freezing ink, *he's the only one stubborn enough*—that his brother would come.

"I'm here," Ansel said to the black screen. "I'm here, Tomás. I've got it. I've got all of it."

The glacier answered.

Not with a groan this time. With a crack—a single, enormous, percussive crack somewhere above and behind, followed by a hiss of settling powder that came sifting down through the cave in the lamplight like the world's slowest snowfall. Then another crack, deeper, and a vibration Ansel felt in his molars, in the bones of his feet, in the fluid of his eyes.

Demolition charges. Testing sequence. Or worse—an early firing, accelerated, because Reyes had decided witnesses were cheaper to bury than to chase.

The current of air moving through the cave stuttered and reversed. Cold pressure pushed against him from the depths, the glacier exhaling through its wounds, and far below—farther than the lamp could reach—came a sound like a door closing: a mass of ice shifting somewhere in the glacier's interior, blocking a passage, rerouting a century of slow plumbing.

Ansel shoved the camera, the memo, and the notebook into his jacket, zipped it to his chin, and ran.

* * *

The way back was trying to become no way at all.

The first chamber he'd crossed was still passable, barely—the ceiling had dropped a fan of fresh shards that crunched under his boots like glass. The second was flooding. Meltwater poured from a split in the wall in a rope as thick as his arm, pooling fast across the floor, and the pool was rising as he watched, fed by something opening up above. He waded it hip-deep, gasping at the cold that clamped his muscles, holding the camera case above the waterline with both hands like an offering.

At the crevasse exit, the rope he'd fixed hung slack. The anchor above—good ice an hour ago—had calved away with the shaking, and the rope's upper end dangled free, useless, swaying in the venting cold air.

Ansel stood in the rising water and did the arithmetic climbers do, fast and merciless. One route out, gone. Backup route: the long gallery east, unexplored, the direction the air current had originally flowed—toward the dam ruin, toward the charges, toward the sound of the glacier being murdered from above. Toward the crew, if any of them were close enough to hear the cracks and come looking. Ilsa had promised they'd come in after two hours. He'd been inside perhaps ninety minutes.

The east gallery it was. Toward the noise. Toward the men with the detonators.

He clipped the camera case to his harness, checked the knot twice with numb fingers, and started walking into the dark with the whole mountain groaning overhead like a ship going down in very slow motion.

Somewhere above him, muffled and rhythmic, came the thud of charges being placed rather than fired—work continuing, the deadline accelerating—and threaded through it, thin and impossible and unmistakable, a human voice calling his name.

Pico. Young, cracking with echo, terrified and refusing to stop.

Ansel turned toward the sound, water streaming off him, his brother's recordings hard against his heart, and answered with everything his lungs had left.
