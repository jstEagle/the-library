## 35. Collapse

Pico had learned to read Ansel's silences the way other people read weather. There was the silence before a rapid, all coiled attention, and there was the silence after a nightmare, hollow as a burned-out stove. But this silence—the one that had swallowed Ansel whole when he'd announced he was going into the ice cave alone—was neither of those. It was something older. Something like a man standing at the door of his own grave and finding it already open.

"You're not going in there by yourself," Ilsa had said.

"I am," Ansel had answered, and he'd said it the way he said everything that mattered: quietly, finally, like a stone set down on a table.

Now Pico crouched at the mouth of the cave with the wind screaming off the glacier above them, watching the blue throat of the ice swallow the last of Ansel's headlamp glow, and he understood that he had just watched a man walk into ten years of his own past. The demolition charges were somewhere below them, wired into the ruin of the dam, timed for dawn. The company's crews would be here within hours. And Ansel Sandoval had gone down alone into the one place on earth where his brother still spoke.

"Rope," Pico said. His voice came out steadier than he felt. "We run a line in behind him."

Ilsa looked at him—looked *at* him, really looked, the way she did when she was deciding whether a wound needed stitching or just pressure—and then she nodded once and started uncoiling rope with her frost-stiffened hands.

"He'll hate it," she said.

"He'd do it for us."

"That's what scares me about him." She fed the line out through her gloved fingers, fast and precise, the medic's economy of motion even now. "Anchor here. That serac's been standing a thousand years; it can stand another hour."

Pico drove the pickets while Ilsa rigged the anchor, and together they paid out sixty meters of line into the dark, tied off at intervals so they could feel him—feel *something*—moving below. The rope hummed faintly against the ice lip each time it shifted. Pico pressed his palm against it and tried to read Ansel in the vibrations, the way you could read water through a paddle shaft.

Above them, the storm was building. It had come down off the summit ridge an hour ago, fat gray clouds dragging their bellies along the icefall, and every few minutes the glacier spoke—a deep internal groan, like a ship settling—or cracked sharp as a rifle shot. Somewhere under all that ice, Tomás Sandoval's recordings waited in a crevasse shelter that had held for ten years and might not hold for ten more minutes.

"We should have gone with him," Pico said.

Ilsa didn't answer right away. She was watching the sky, counting the gaps between gusts. "He ordered us not to. And you saw his face when he said it." She pulled her balaclava down tighter. "Some doors you have to walk through alone or they don't count."

The rope went slack.

Pico felt it happen before he understood it—the sudden absence of tension, the line drifting loose against the ice lip like a fishing rod with nothing on the hook. Sixty meters of rope and no weight on the end of it.

"No," he said. "No, no, no—"

He hauled. The rope came up too easily, hand over hand, his arms burning, until the last of it slithered over the lip and lay dead in the snow, cleanly severed. The cut was smooth. Melted, almost. A falling block of ice, sharp as glass, or a serac calving somewhere in the dark above the passage.

The wind filled the silence where Ansel's signal calls should have been.

Ilsa was already moving. She clipped into the anchor, threw the spare line over her shoulder, and looked back at Pico with an expression he'd seen her wear over dying men: total clarity, zero hesitation.

"We go in," she said. "Both of us. On belay. If the cave goes, we're no good to him standing out here being safe."

* * *

The inside of the glacier was a cathedral built by something that hated cathedrals. Blue light leaked through the walls in veins, and the passage sloped down and inward, following the old meltwater channel, the ceiling dripping a slow cold arithmetic. Their headlamps swung shadows across walls scarred by decades of floodwater, and everywhere the ice groaned around them, a sound Pico felt in his sternum more than heard.

They found the cut rope's end dangling from a shattered ledge forty meters in. Below it, the floor dropped away into blackness—a crevasse, wide as a road, its far wall lost beyond the reach of their lamps.

"Ansel!" Pico's voice came back at him doubled and tripled, the glacier throwing it around like a rumor.

Nothing. Then—faint, ragged, unmistakable—a cough. Echoing up from the dark.

Ilsa went to the lip and played her light down. The beam found ice walls streaked with ancient dirt, found a shelf of debris, found—

"There." Her voice cracked on it. "Bottom left. He's moving."

Ansel hung maybe fifteen meters down, tangled in the severed rope, one arm hooked through his own harness, his lamp pointing uselessly at the ceiling. As they watched, he shifted, and the shift became a slide, and the slide stopped only because his boot jammed against a protruding blade of ice. Below him the crevasse went deeper than light. Debris trickled past his shoulder and fell without sound, or with a sound that arrived seconds later, wrong, delayed, like thunder out of a clear sky.

"Ansel!" Pico screamed down. "Don't move! We're coming!"

The helmet turned. The lamp found them. Even from up there, even across that distance, Pico could see the blood on his face, black in the lamplight, and the way his free hand rose—not waving for help, but holding something up. Clutching it to his chest like a coal he couldn't let fall into the water at the bottom of the world.

He had it. Whatever Tomás had left him, he had it, and he was hanging over a bottomless dark refusing to drop it.

"Ilsa—" Pico started.

"I know." She was already rigging the descent, hands flying. "You're lighter. You go down. I anchor and haul. We've done this drill a hundred times in practice; we do it once for real."

She clipped him in, checked his figure-eight twice, and gripped his shoulder hard enough to bruise. "Whatever happens down there—you climb, you don't think. Thinking is my job right now. Go get our guide."

Pico went over the edge.

* * *

The crevasse swallowed him by degrees. First the lamplight above shrank to a coin, then a star, then nothing, and there was only his own beam swinging across walls of ice so old and compressed they were the color of deep water. The cold down here was different—not the wind's cold but the glacier's own, patient and total, the cold of a place that kept things. He thought of Tomás's cache in the shelter higher up, supplies wrapped and waiting a decade. The glacier kept things. It kept bodies, too. He made himself stop thinking about that.

"Ansel."

"Here." The voice was wet and thin. "Careful left. Ice bridge is rotten. Everything's rotten. Whole place is—"

A crack ran through the dark above them both, long and splintering, and a rain of ice chips pattered down past Pico's lamp.

"—coming down," Ansel finished.

Pico rappelled the last meters and landed on the debris shelf beside him. Up close Ansel was worse: a gash over his eyebrow painting half his face red, his left arm hanging wrong, breath whistling. But his right hand was locked around a waterproof case, knuckles white, and his eyes—his eyes were burning.

"You cut the rope," Pico said stupidly.

"Falling ice cut the rope." Ansel coughed. "Get me out of here, kid. Fast. I heard the charges' timers when I passed the dam gallery. They moved them up. Dawn's not the deadline anymore."

"How long?"

"If Reyes accelerated like I think he did?" Ansel looked up, toward the distant star of the entrance. "Hours. Maybe less. Maybe a lot less."

Pico got the sling under Ansel's good arm and clipped him to the haul line, then keyed the radio twice—their signal for *haul*. Above, invisible, Ilsa would be leaning into the anchor with everything she had. The rope came taut. Ansel rose off the shelf, swinging gently, and Pico climbed beside him, matching his pace, one hand fisted in the older man's harness because trust was a thing you could also hold onto literally.

Halfway up, the glacier decided otherwise.

It began as a sound Pico had no name for—a vast exhalation, a sigh from the belly of the ice—and then the whole crevasse shuddered like a struck bell. Somewhere above and behind them, a section of the roof let go. He heard it falling before he saw it, a rushing white roar, and he did the only thing there was to do: he shoved Ansel sideways across the shaft, toward the far wall, and pressed himself flat against the near one as the block came down between them.

It missed them by the width of a prayer. It hit the debris shelf where they'd stood and kept going, punching through into the depths, and the shockwave of its landing came up the shaft like a hammer blow through the rope itself. Pico's grip tore loose. For one endless second he was falling—weightless, silent, his stomach rising into his throat—and then the rope snapped taut and the world jerked white with pain and he was swinging in the dark, alive, screaming, alive.

"Pico!" Ansel's voice, raw. "PICO!"

"Here!" His own voice surprised him. "I'm here! I'm—rope held—I'm here!"

Above, Ilsa's voice came down the shaft, stripped to its wires: "CLIMB. BOTH OF YOU. NOW. IT'S ALL MOVING."

They climbed. Pico didn't remember most of it—just the rhythm, boot, tool, boot, tool, Ansel gasping beside him, the ice shivering under every placement, small avalanches of crystals hissing past their shoulders. Once the whole wall flexed and a fissure opened a hand's width from Ansel's face and closed again, like the glacier breathing. Once Pico's axe punched through into nothing and he hung by the other for three heartbeats that lasted a year.

Then light. Real light, gray and furious with storm, and Ilsa's hands hauling him over the lip, and then both of them dragging Ansel out onto the surface like fishermen landing something miraculous and half-drowned.

For a moment none of them moved. They lay in the snow, three people breathing in ragged unison, while behind and below them the glacier went on collapsing—deep, rolling detonations of ice, the mountain clearing its throat.

Then Ansel sat up.

Blood sheeted down from his brow. His arm was wrong. He was fifty years old and wrecked and he had never looked more alive, and he was clutching the case to his chest, and he was laughing—a low, disbelieving, broken laugh that cracked apart halfway through and turned into something else entirely.

"It's real," he said. "It's all real. Ten years, and it was in the ice the whole time, waiting."

"Later," Ilsa said, but her voice shook, and her hand stayed on his shoulder a beat longer than triage required. "Can you walk?"

"I can run if I have to."

"You'll have to." She pointed east, down-glacier, where the storm was tearing open just enough to show the moraine, the route down, the long night between them and the ridge station and the uplink. And then she pointed west, back toward the dam ruin—and Pico's stomach dropped.

Lights. Dozens of them, strung along the valley rim like a carnival coming to town. Engine noise carried thin and distorted on the wind. Floodlamps bloomed one by one across the face of the old dam, and tiny figures swarmed its spine, and even from this distance Pico could hear the first generator spooling up.

"They're early," Ilsa breathed. "Reyes moved the whole schedule up. They're going to blow it tonight."

Ansel got to his feet. It took him two tries, and Pico caught his elbow on the second, and this time Ansel let him.

"Then we move faster than grief does," Ansel said. "All of it comes out tonight. Every page, every recording, every name. Or we die trying, and it comes out anyway, because I mailed copies." He almost smiled. "Tomás taught me redundancy. He just never got to use his."

They ran. Down the moraine, three small figures against the enormous dark, the case strapped to Ansel's chest like a heartbeat, the storm at their backs and the lights of the demolition crews burning brighter behind them. Pico ran point because he was youngest and his lungs were newest, breaking trail through the fresh snow, and every time he glanced back he checked for two things: that Ansel was still upright, and that the dam was still standing.

At the first ridge they paused, chests heaving, and looked back one last time.

The ruin of the Ceniza dam stood black against the floodlights, broken-toothed, the wound in the valley that had defined all their lives. Ansel stared at it for a long moment—the place his brother died, the place his memory was stolen, the place he'd just crawled out of twice.

"Burn," he said softly. Not a curse. Almost a blessing. "Take the lies down with you."

Then the horizon behind the dam flickered orange—too early, far too early, the first test charges or the first act of panic—and the ground shivered under their boots, and there was no more time to look at anything except the path down.

Pico turned and led them into the storm, toward the ridge station, carrying the truth downhill as fast as three human beings could move it.
