## 36. The Uplink

The ridge station had been a weather observatory once, before the company bought the sky along with the valley. Now it was a rusted dome and a concrete bunker crouched on the spine of the mountain like something that had crawled up there to die. The satellite dish on its roof was furred with lichen, tilted at an angle that suggested it had given up on the heavens years ago.

Ilsa Vinter had patched worse.

She knelt in the station's equipment room with her med kit open beside her—not for patients this time, but for machines. Teo lay on his side on the cold floor, one wrist still zip-tied to a pipe stanchion, his face gray with pain and two days of captivity, watching her work with the feverish intensity of a man who could not do the thing himself and was finding it unbearable.

"The coupler's cracked," he said. "Not broken. Cracked. If you brace it—"

"I know how to brace things, Teo."

"You brace *bones*. This is fiber-optic alignment. It's a different—" He hissed through his teeth as he shifted. His ribs were bad; she'd taped them in the truck they'd stolen from the checkpoint, during the twenty minutes of grace Reyes's men had allowed them before realizing their prisoner exchange had been a diversion inside a diversion. "The alignment has to be within a millimeter or the uplink drops every time the dish moves."

"Then hold still," Ilsa said, "and let me have your hands."

She cut the zip tie first. His wrist beneath was raw, the skin abraded to weeping pink where he'd tested the restraint for hours. She cleaned it, wrapped it, and then pressed the cracked coupler into his palms—the small brass-and-carbon fitting that was now, absurdly, the most important object on the continent—and watched him cradle it like a wounded bird.

Outside, the wind came off the glacier in long white gusts. Pico was on the roof with binoculars, lying flat behind the parapet, counting headlights on the switchback road far below. Ansel was in the corner of the room with Mara's laptop open, reading his own testimony aloud under his breath, over and over, the way a man rehearses a confession he intends never to retract.

*My name is Ansel Sandoval. On the night of March ninth, eleven people died at the Ceniza dam because the consortium ordered the spillway gates opened against every safety protocol, to destroy evidence of tailings leakage that would have ended their operating license. I survived because they needed a witness who would forget. I did not forget. I was made to carry the forgetting like a stone.*

Ilsa had heard him read it perhaps forty times since dawn. It never got easier to listen to. That, she understood, was the point. A truth that became easy was a truth becoming furniture.

"Signal strength?" she asked.

Teo had the portable spectrum analyzer balanced on his knee, its screen the only blue light in the room. "Once you get me that coupler seated? Ninety-four percent. Enough." He looked up at her, and for a moment the engineer fell away and what was left was just a frightened man with taped ribs and a forged passport in his pocket. "Ilsa. Once we send this, it doesn't come back. There's no recall button. Every court, every newsroom, every prosecutor on three continents gets it in the same minute. And so does everyone who wants us dead."

"That's rather the design," she said.

"I'm serious."

"So am I." She took his hands, guided the coupler toward the junction box, held it steady while he seated it with the delicacy of a surgeon reducing a fracture. The connection clicked home. "I spent six years being careful, Teo. Careful got my license suspended and my name dragged through a hearing where nobody listened to the actual patient. Careful is what they want from us. Careful is how eleven people stay a statistic instead of eleven people." She tightened the last screw. "Send it."

He exhaled. Nodded. Turned to the console.

* * *

They gathered around the terminal the way people gather around a grave: too close, not touching, each privately unsure what the proper posture was. Ansel stood at the center with the laptop connected by cable to the station's ancient transmitter, and Mara stood at his shoulder, and Pico came down from the roof long enough to stand in the doorway with his rifle slung and his young face trying very hard to be old.

"Files are staged," Teo said quietly. "Tomás's recordings. The survey documents from the hut. The demolition orders Reyes signed. Your testimony, Ansel—video and sworn text. Distribution list is four hundred and twelve addresses. Press bureaus in nine countries. The international criminal tribunal's public intake. Three ministries. Two universities that keep investigative archives." He paused. "And one address Mara gave me that she wouldn't explain."

"Leave it," Mara said.

"One press send, on your mark," Teo said.

Ansel looked at the screen for a long moment. In the monitor's glow Ilsa saw his face doing something complicated—grief and terror and a kind of terrible relief all fighting for the same territory. Ten years of running compressed into a cursor blinking in an empty field.

"Tomás reached this valley first," Ansel said. Not to any of them; to the room, to the mountain, maybe to the brother whose voice was sealed in those files. "He carried it out alone and died in the river trying. All I'm doing is finishing the walk." He lifted his head. "Send it."

Teo pressed the key.

For three seconds nothing happened except a rising hum from the transmitter and the flicker of a progress bar. Then the bar filled, and the console printed a single line of confirmation—a timestamp, a checksum, a word: **DELIVERED**—and Ilsa felt the moment pass through her body like a change in air pressure.

It was done. It was out of their hands. It was in four hundred and twelve inboxes and archives and intake queues, multiplying already as journalists forwarded it to editors and editors to lawyers and lawyers to whoever would listen. No explosion, no sound at all. Just a small machine on a cold ridge, talking patiently to satellites, and somewhere below them men with guns who no longer had anything left to protect.

Pico laughed—one sharp, disbelieving bark. Mara closed her eyes. Ansel put his hand flat on the console as if steadying himself against a deck in heavy seas.

"It's gone," Ilsa said. "You can't take it back."

"No," Ansel said. "I can't." He said it the way a man says *I can't swim* while standing at the edge—naming the fact that had defined him, finally, without flinching from it.

That was when Pico, back at the doorway with the binoculars, went rigid.

"Headlights," he said. "Four vehicles. Coming fast. They're not even using blackout discipline anymore."

* * *

They had planned for this hour the way they'd planned everything since the checkpoint: badly, quickly, with whatever the mountain offered. The plan, such as it was, lived in three sentences. One: the broadcast takes minutes and the trucks take forty, so the truth leaves first. Two: after that, survival is optional but useful. Three: the station's back wall faces a scree chute that drops into cloud—if they must run, they run down, into the white, and let the mountain argue with the bullets.

Ilsa's job in the plan was the med kit and the rear guard, which were the same job. She'd learned that about herself somewhere between the tripwire boy and Teo's ribs: she was the person who stayed with the wounded while the world decided whether the wounded would become casualties. She hated it and was good at it, which was, she suspected, the definition of vocation.

Now she moved through the bunker collecting what mattered. Water. The remaining morphine. Teo's documents case. Ansel stood at the window slit with binoculars, and she heard him counting under his breath.

"Two hundred meters. They're dismounting early—they don't want the trucks shot at. Eight men visible. Maybe more behind the dome." He lowered the glasses. "Reyes isn't with them. He's holding back. He wants deniability even now."

"He doesn't have deniability," Mara said. "We mailed it to the Hague."

"We mailed it somewhere. Whether anyone opens the envelope in time is a different question." Ansel turned from the window. In the dimness his face was calm in a way Ilsa had never seen on him—not the practiced calm of a guide managing clients, but the settled calm of a man who had already survived the worst thing that could happen to him and found it survivable. "New math. We don't need to escape. We need to exist. Every hour we're alive up here, alive and identifiable, is an hour they can't bury this. Dead anonymous witnesses are a cleanup problem. Dead famous ones are a scandal. So if it comes to shooting—we make sure they know exactly who they're shooting."

"You want us to stand in the open?" Pico said.

"I want us to be *undeniable*."

The first bullet came through the window slit before anyone answered.

It punched a hole in the opposite wall and buried itself in the document shelf with a crack like ice calving. Then the world outside resolved into muzzle flashes among the rocks below the dome—short, disciplined bursts, professionals working a suppression pattern—and the bunker became a place of plaster dust and shouting.

"Down! Down, all of you!" Ilsa hauled Teo behind the transmitter housing, the one piece of equipment thick enough to stop a round, and pressed him flat while splinters rained across her back. Pico returned fire through the doorway, three aimed shots, unhurried, then rolled to cover. Mara was already moving along the wall toward the back hatch, laying down covering bursts from a contractor's rifle she'd taken off a man at the checkpoint whose loyalties had been for sale.

Ansel didn't shoot. Ansel stood up.

Ilsa saw it happen and her heart stopped in her chest. He rose into the open beside the window slit, arms wide, empty-handed, silhouetted against the gray glare of the glacier, and he shouted into the storm of gunfire with a voice that came from somewhere below his lungs, some cellar of the self ten years deep:

"MY NAME IS ANSEL SANDOVAL! THE EVIDENCE IS ALREADY PUBLIC! EVERYTHING YOU'RE DYING TO HIDE IS ON ITS WAY TO EVERY COURT IN THE WORLD—AND YOUR ORDERS ARE TOO!"

The firing stuttered.

Not stopped—stuttered, the way a machine stutters, one gun falling silent and then another, because contractors are professionals and professionals listen. Somewhere in the rocks below, a voice Ilsa couldn't see called out a question she couldn't hear, and another voice answered it, and the volume of fire dropped by half.

"They're checking their phones," Teo breathed beside her. "Oh God. They're actually checking their phones."

Of course they were. Ilsa almost laughed. Four hundred and twelve addresses. Men crouched behind rocks in the high Andes, reading on cracked screens the sworn testimony of the man standing above them with his arms spread, learning in real time that the thing they were killing to prevent had already happened, that their own signed orders were attached, that their names might be in the metadata. Bullets could be denied. Screenshots could not.

But half the guns were still firing, and half was plenty.

A round clipped the transmitter housing an inch above Teo's head. Another found the fuel canister by the generator, and the bunker filled suddenly with the reek of diesel gouting across the floor. Fire, Ilsa thought distantly. Of course. If they couldn't erase the evidence they'd erase the scene, burn the station with the witnesses in it, and call the ashes ambiguous.

"Back hatch!" Mara screamed. "Now! Move!"

Ilsa shoved Teo upright, got his arm over her shoulders, and ran bent double through the smoke toward the hatch where Pico held the door. Behind her she heard Ansel still shouting his name into the din—not fleeing yet, making himself undeniable to the last second—and then his footsteps hammering after hers, and Mara slamming the hatch, and then they were out.

* * *

The scree chute swallowed them into cloud.

It was less running than controlled falling—boots skidding on loose shale, hands out, the world reduced to gray vapor and the crash and slide of their own bodies and, above and behind, the orange pulse of the station beginning to burn. The diesel had caught. Smoke boiled off the dome into the fog, and through it the satellite dish on the roof stood black and skeletal, still pointed faithfully at the heavens, still warm with the memory of having spoken.

Ilsa fell twice. The second time she landed on Teo, which was worse for him, and they lay tangled in the scree gasping while the others caught up—Mara first, then Pico dragging the document case, then Ansel, bloodied at the temple where a fragment of something had kissed him, alive, all of them alive, which felt less like victory than like a temporary condition.

They regrouped in a fold of the moraine where the fog pooled thickest, fifty meters down and invisible. Above them the station burned with a soft orange glow diffused through the mist, and below them the valley dropped away into darkness, and somewhere in that darkness was the new lake, and the ruin, and Reyes.

"Confirmations," Teo rasped. He had the spectrum analyzer out, shielding its screen with his body. "First responses are hitting. A bureau in Santiago auto-acknowledged. The tribunal intake sent a receipt number. Someone at a wire service in Lima is—wait." His thumb moved. He stared. "Someone's already publishing. There's a headline. It's—it's live, Ilsa, it's *live*, they're running it with the recordings embedded—"

The words came out of him in pieces, half sob, half laugh. Ilsa took the analyzer from his shaking hands and read the headline herself, there in the fog, by the light of a burning building:

**THE CENIZA PAPERS: ENGINEERED FLOOD KILLED ELEVEN, RECORDINGS SHOW.**

Eleven names underneath it. All of them. Including Tomás Sandoval.

She read them twice. Around her the others went quiet, listening to Teo read them aloud, each name landing in the fog like a stone dropped into deep water. When he reached Tomás, Ansel made a sound Ilsa had never heard from him—not grief exactly, and not relief, but the collision of the two, the sound of a man setting down a weight and discovering his body no longer knows how to stand without it.

"It's out," Pico said softly. "It's actually out. It's everywhere. They can't—there's no version of tomorrow where they can pretend—"

"There's a version," Mara said. Her voice was flat and hard and aimed downhill, toward the valley, toward the lake. "The version where we die up here tonight and the story becomes *four foreign trespassers killed in an accident*, and the papers spend a week wondering before the company's lawyers drown it. Reyes knows it too. He has nothing left to protect and one night to protect it in." She checked her rifle. "He'll come up here with everything he has. Not to capture. To erase."

"Then we make ourselves expensive," Ansel said.

Ilsa looked at him—at the blood on his temple, the burned station glowing behind him, the fog beading in his beard—and understood that the man who had once taken money to forget was gone, and the man kneeling in the scree checking his rifle with steady hands was what ten years of running had been building toward without either of them knowing it.

Below them, far down the valley, lights began to move. Vehicles, sweeping up the moraine road. More than four this time. More than eight.

Reyes was coming, and he was bringing the whole night with him.
