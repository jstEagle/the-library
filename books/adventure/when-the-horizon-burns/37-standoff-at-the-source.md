## 37. Standoff at the Source

The broadcast went out at 4:17 in the morning, and for eleven minutes the world was allowed to know.

Ansel stood at the ridge station's window and watched the progress bar crawl—ninety-one percent, ninety-four—while below them the demolition crews' floodlights swept the valley like searchlights hunting a fugitive that was already gone. The truth had left their hands. It was in Quito now, in Santiago, in a server farm in Virginia he would never see. Ilsa had routed it to forty-seven press outlets, three courts, and one very persistent freelance journalist who had been asking questions about the Ceniza watershed since before Tomás died.

"Confirmed," Teo said quietly. He was still gray-faced from the ambush, his arm bound tight against his ribs, but his voice was steady as bedrock. "Delivery receipts from thirty-one of the addresses. The journalist's server pinged back twice."

Ninety-eight percent. Ninety-nine.

The bar filled. Somewhere in the dark above the glacier, a machine no bigger than a suitcase finished the work that eleven dead people had started a decade ago.

"It's done," Pico breathed. He said it like a prayer, or maybe an apology.

Ansel didn't answer. Because through the window, down in the valley of the source, the floodlights had stopped sweeping.

They had stopped, all of them, at once.

* * *

They heard the trucks before they saw them—engines climbing the moraine road that the company had built for its own machines, grinding up through the cold with the particular patience of men who believed they had all night. Reyes had lost the documents. That much was obvious from the radio traffic Ilsa had been monitoring on the patrol band: clipped, furious Spanish, the word *estación* repeated like a curse. But Reyes had not lost his temper, and he had not lost his men, and there were more of both than Ansel wanted to count.

"Thirty, maybe thirty-five," Mara said, flat on her belly at the ridge's edge, binoculars pressed to her eyes. Her shoulder wound had been dressed twice since she'd crawled into camp two nights ago, and she'd refused morphine both times because, she said, she wanted her hands steady. "Two squads moving on foot along the east lateral moraine. Vehicles holding at the blast zone. And Reyes himself is coming up the middle."

"He wants the station," Teo said. "And everyone in it."

"He wants us gone before the sun comes up and someone in an office somewhere starts asking what happened to his demolition schedule." Mara lowered the binoculars. In the pre-dawn gloom her face looked carved out of something older than stone. "We have maybe twenty minutes."

Ansel crouched beside them and did the arithmetic he'd been doing since they'd fled the lake shore—the same grim ledger he'd kept his whole life, except this time the columns wouldn't balance. Four of them, one wounded. One rifle, taken off a patrolman at the checkpoint; one pistol Mara had carried out of detention inside her boot; a flare gun, climbing rope, and a satellite terminal worth more than all of them combined. Against thirty-five professionals whose orders, until ninety minutes ago, had been secret.

"We can't hold this ridge," he said. "There's no cover on the descent, and if we run for the icefall they'll pick us off in the open like it's practice."

"So we don't run." Mara said it without looking at him. "And we don't hide. We stand."

Pico made a sound that might have been a laugh. "Stand against thirty-five guys with automatic weapons. Sure. Great plan. Love it."

"You're missing the point," Mara said, and now she did turn, and her eyes found Ansel's in the dark. "Reyes doesn't know the broadcast succeeded. He thinks he's cleaning up a mess before anyone sees it. But his men—" she tapped the radio Ilsa had left between them, still murmuring with patrol chatter, "—his men are about to find out otherwise. All we have to do is keep breathing long enough for them to hear it too."

* * *

They chose the ground the way Ansel had once chosen campsites: with a professional's eye for what kills people. A shallow depression behind the ridge crest, rock on three sides, the terminal's case dragged into the deepest shadow. Ilsa rigged the flare gun where she could reach it. Teo, who could barely lift his arm, insisted on manning the radio anyway, because—as he put it, white-lipped and immovable—if the company jammed the band, someone needed to know the moment it happened.

Ansel took the rifle and lay at the lip of the depression with the barrel resting on stone, and found that his hands were not shaking.

That surprised him. Ten years ago, on the night that had ended his life, his hands had shaken so badly he couldn't sign his own name—which was convenient, he understood now, for the men who wanted only his thumbprint. Every disaster since, every narrow escape, every midnight departure from towns that had grown tired of him, his body had done what bodies do: flooded, trembled, begged him to move. Fear as fuel. Fear as the engine of never stopping.

But lying here above the source of the Río Ceniza, waiting for the men who had killed his brother's memory to climb within rifle range, he felt something he had no name for. Not calm exactly. More like the moment on a rope when the load comes taut and you stop being a person and become part of the system—anchor, rope, climber, mountain, all one held breath.

Tomás, he thought. I'm standing where you stood.

The first flare went up at 5:02, fired by nobody, it seemed—just a red star blooming over the eastern moraine, and under its light the shapes of the advancing squad froze like animals caught mid-stride. Then the loudspeaker on the lead truck crackled to life, and Reyes's voice rolled up the valley, amplified and patient, the voice of a man reading terms to a debtor.

"Sandoval. You have nothing. Paper burns, servers crash, journalists forget. Come down, put your hands where my men can see them, and you walk out of this valley alive—all four of you. I give you my word."

"His word," Pico whispered. "That's rich."

"His word is actually worth something," Mara said grimly. "It's the only currency he has. Which means he'll honor it right up until the moment honoring it costs him more than breaking it."

Ansel keyed the handheld they'd salvaged from the patrol gear—a different frequency than the one Reyes was broadcasting on, but the same band, and every contractor on that mountain was carrying a radio tuned to it. He thought about what to say. He had spent ten years learning how not to speak: how to deflect questions with weather, how to make his silences so complete that people stopped asking. Now, with everything, he found the words came easily. They had been waiting a decade for the door to open.

"This is Ansel Sandoval," he said. "For the men listening on channel six. Your boss just offered me a deal, and I want you to understand what kind of man is making it. Ten years ago his employers drowned eleven people to bury a leak they'd known about for two years. They forged a report. They blacklisted the witness. And tonight they were going to blow the last of the evidence into the lake and call it erosion." He paused. Below, the floodlights had begun to move again, searching. "At 4:17 this morning, the surveys, the sabotage order, and my sworn testimony went to thirty-one newsrooms and three courts. Check your phones. Check the terminals in your trucks. It's already out. Nothing you do to us in the next hour changes that. The only thing still undecided is whether you spend the rest of your lives as the men who guarded it—or the men who ended it."

He let go of the key. The silence on the band lasted four seconds, five, six—and then it broke, not with gunfire but with voices. Low, overlapping, disbelieving. Someone repeating *cuatro diecisiete* like a man checking a lottery number. Someone else saying, clearly, in accented English: *"Confirm? Anyone confirm this?"*

"They're checking," Ilsa breathed. She had her phone out, screen dimmed to nothing, watching signal bars like a pulse. "God, look at them all checking."

Below, the squads had stopped. The floodlights wavered, swung, held. And Reyes's amplified voice came again, tighter now, the patience stretched thin as old rope: "Sandoval. Last chance. Come down or we come up."

Ansel looked at Mara. She looked back, and something passed between them that ten years of hatred had somehow failed to kill—the old guide's shorthand, rival to rival, *I see you, I know what you're carrying.* She nodded once.

Then the shooting started.

* * *

It was not, in the end, much of a battle. Later, when reporters asked him—he learned to dread the word *later*, the way it assumed a future—Ansel would struggle to describe those eighteen minutes as anything but a slow-motion argument. Reyes sent one squad up the east moraine to flank, and the flanking squad got two hundred meters and simply stopped, radios buzzing with men demanding confirmation of the broadcast, of the orders, of the legal status of what they'd been doing in this valley for a month. Reyes sent the second squad straight up the middle, and they came firing—wild, high, more warning than intent, because the men pulling those triggers had also checked their phones, and their phones told them the world was already watching.

Ansel fired exactly three shots. None to kill. One to knock the muzzle of the nearest rifleman skyward, one to drop a floodlight, one—this one deliberate, almost ceremonial—into the loudspeaker on the lead truck, which died mid-sentence with a squawk like a stepped-on bird.

Mara held the flare gun and lit the ridge red whenever the line wavered, and the light itself became a weapon, because light meant witnesses, and witnesses were the one thing Reyes could no longer afford.

And through it all, on channel six, the argument grew. Ansel lay with the radio against his ear and listened to a private army come apart in real time—not in blood but in doubt. A sergeant named Ochoa, voice cracking, asking his commander directly whether the demolition order included the survey hut, whether it included personnel, whether *personnel* meant what he thought it meant. A man on the west perimeter saying, quietly, that his father had fished the Ceniza below the dam, that his father had been one of the names, and that he would like someone to tell him why he was still holding a weapon for these people.

That voice, Ansel thought. He knew that voice. The guard from the checkpoint—the one who had walked Mara out of her cell and turned his patrol's guns around, the one who hadn't made it off the moraine. His son. His brother. Somebody's blood answering somebody's blood across ten years of silence.

"Reyes is falling back to the trucks," Mara reported. There was something in her voice Ansel had never heard there before—not triumph. Relief, maybe, or its exhausted cousin. "He's ordering a withdrawal. Nobody's following the order."

Then, over the channel, a new voice. Cold, official, unfamiliar: *"This is Contreras, site security supervisor. Effective immediately, all personnel stand down. Weapons safe. Mr. Reyes, please report to the command vehicle."*

A pause, long enough to hold a whole history.

*"Mr. Reyes. That was not a request."*

* * *

They came up the moraine at dawn, and they came without weapons.

Not all of them—Ochoa's squad stayed below, and a handful of Reyes's originals kept their distance like dogs unsure of a changed master—but eight of them climbed the ridge in the grey-gold first light, rifles slung, hands visible, led by the flat-voiced Contreras and flanking a ninth man whose wrists were already bound with his own flex-cuffs.

Reyes looked smaller in daylight. Ansel had imagined him for years without knowing it—in the missing hour, in nightmares, in the shapeless dread that had worn his life hollow—and the man his imagination had built was enormous, inevitable, a force of geology. This one was fifty-ish and heavyset, with sweat-stained collar and the reddened eyes of a man who had not slept, and when his gaze found Ansel across the rocks it held not menace but a kind of furious bewilderment, as if Ansel had cheated at a game whose rules Reyes had written himself.

"You had the deal," Reyes said. His voice, unamplified, was almost ordinary. "You had the same deal you took ten years ago. Money. Freedom. A clean slate. Any sane man takes it twice."

"That's the thing," Ansel said. He came up out of the depression slowly, hands open, and stood in the growing light where everyone could see him. "Ten years ago I wasn't sane. I was half-drowned and half-broken and they put a paper in front of me and a thumbprint next to it, and I spent a decade telling myself that was survival." He looked past Reyes, down the valley, to where the meltwater lake lay still and pale beneath the glacier, ringed by the machinery of erasure. "It wasn't survival. It was the last piece of the cover-up. You didn't buy my silence. You bought the story. And stories are the only thing that ever really gets buried."

"The broadcast changes nothing," Reyes said—but he said it to Contreras, not to Ansel, and Contreras was not looking at him. None of them were. They were looking at their phones, at the small bright screens where, somewhere in the last hour, the Ceniza had stopped being a rumor and become a headline.

"Señor Reyes," Contreras said quietly. "The order to demolish with personnel on site. Did you issue it verbally?"

Reyes's jaw worked. "You're a security contractor, not a lawyer."

"I'm a man whose name is now attached to yours in thirty-one newsrooms." Contreras turned and gestured, and two of his own men took Reyes by the arms—not roughly, which somehow made it worse, the way you handle equipment you intend to return intact. "The lawyers can sort out what you issued. My job is making sure you're standing in front of someone official when they start asking."

They walked him down the moraine, and the contractors parted to let them pass, and not one of them met his eyes.

* * *

Afterward there was nothing to do, which was its own kind of shock.

Ansel sat on the ridge crest with his back against stone and watched the sun clear the eastern icefields and set the glacier burning—gold, then rose, then a white so fierce it hurt—and the valley below filled with light the way it must have filled with light every morning for ten thousand years before any of them were born, indifferent and total. The demolition machines stood silent in the blast zone, abandoned mid-task, cranes frozen over the ruin they would never finish erasing. The lake lay mirror-still. Somewhere beneath it, under silt and a decade of cold water, the bones of a dam rested alongside the bones of eleven people, and today, for the first time, the water above them belonged to the truth.

Pico sat down beside him without asking, the way he'd learned to. For a while neither spoke.

"I keep waiting to feel different," Pico said finally. "Like there should be a bell, or something. Ten years of running and it just—ends on a Tuesday morning, and the sun comes up anyway."

"The sun always comes up anyway." Ansel turned the rifle over in his hands, then set it down, away from himself, deliberately, like closing an account. "That's the part nobody tells you. The world doesn't pause for your worst night or your best one. You just wake up on the other side of it and find out what's left."

"What's left?"

Ansel considered the question honestly, which was new. Down the slope, Ilsa was repacking the medical kit with the unhurried precision of a woman who had finally stopped expecting to need it in the next five minutes. Teo sat propped against the terminal case, asleep sitting up, his bound arm rising and falling with his breath. Mara stood apart at the ridge's edge, phone to her ear, speaking rapidly in the clipped cadence of a woman giving an interview she'd rehearsed for a decade—and catching sight of Ansel, she paused, and lifted two fingers off the phone in something that was not quite a salute and not quite forgiveness, and was somehow both.

"Names," Ansel said. "Eleven of them. And a river that runs clear, if we're lucky. And whatever comes after." He looked at Pico—young, scarred, alive, looking back at him with that open, unguarded faith that used to terrify him more than any patrol. "What's left is finding out who I am when I'm not running."

Pico grinned. "Scary."

"Terrifying," Ansel agreed.

His own phone buzzed against his chest—an actual signal, up here, relayed through the station's booster. Unknown number. He answered, and a voice he didn't know, formal and distant, identified itself as a legal attaché from an embassy he'd spent years avoiding, and informed him that certain warrants had been flagged for review, that certain testimony received overnight was, quote, *materially exculpatory*, and that Mr. Sandoval should expect to be contacted regarding formal proceedings.

Formal proceedings. Courtrooms. Microphones. His name spoken aloud in rooms full of strangers, on the record, permanent.

"Mr. Sandoval?" the voice said. "Are you still there?"

Ansel watched the light come down the valley, touching the lake, the ruin, the graves no map had ever marked. Eleven names. One of them his brother's. All of them, by tonight, on every screen in the hemisphere—and tomorrow, if he could make himself do it, spoken by his own mouth in a room he couldn't walk out of.

"Yeah," he said. "I'm here. Tell them I'll testify."

He hung up, and sat a moment longer in the burning gold, and then went down to tell the others that the hardest part—the part with no ropes, no rifles, no route to run—was just beginning.
