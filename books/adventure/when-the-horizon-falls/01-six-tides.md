## 1. Six Tides

The river was dropping by the hour, and Jude Lockridge could taste it.

Not in any way a landsman would understand — not literally, though after twenty years on the water she supposed she *did* taste it, the green silt-sweetness gone thin and mineral, the smell of exposed mud flats rising like a confession from the banks. She tasted it the way she tasted weather before a squall, or rot in a plank before the caulking failed: somewhere below language, in the body's older country. The Kestrel River was falling out of its banks and into its bones, and every pilot who worked her knew what that meant. Six tides, maybe seven if the rains inland surprised everyone, before the channel at Widow's Narrows silted shut and the whole upper river became a rumor instead of a road.

Jude stood at the wheel of the *Marrow*, one hand resting on the spokes without gripping them, and read the water ahead the way other people read letters from home — closely, hungrily, with an eye for bad news.

Low water told the truth. That was the thing about it. At flood stage the river lied generously; it hid its snags and sandbars under a brown smile and let you believe in your own luck. But in the dry season the water pulled back its skirts and showed you everything — the drowned fence post, the sunken skiff, the old pilings of a dock no one remembered building. The river at low water was honest the way grief was honest. You just had to be able to stand looking at it.

"Sandbar off the left bank," Tam called up through the engine-room hatch, his voice half-swallowed by the throb of the motor. "I can feel her dragging."

"You can't feel anything but your own engine," Jude said, but she eased the wheel two degrees starboard anyway, because Tam had been feeling that engine for eleven years and he wasn't often wrong about drag. The *Marrow* was sixty feet of pre-war riverboat that had been hauled out, patched, burned, sunk, raised, and patched again more times than Jude cared to count. Her hull was a quilt of steel plates and salvaged planking. Her engine had been built to power a cannery and now pushed a boat that carried whatever paid: freight, mail, occasionally passengers who wanted to get somewhere the roads didn't go. She drew four feet empty and six loaded, and today she was light, riding high, which was the only reason they were running the upper channel at all this late in the season.

The left bank slid past, all bleached roots and cracked clay, the waterline dropped three feet down the mud like a bathtub ring. A heron stood in the shallows with the patience of something that had never once been in a hurry. Jude envied it briefly, the way she always did, and then let the envy go. Envy of still things was a habit she'd quit years ago, mostly.

*Mostly* was the word that had been following her around all season.

She was forty-one years old and she owned her boat outright, owed nothing to any bank or family or man, ran six months of charters a year and spent the other six hauling freight and patching rust, and by any arithmetic she'd ever learned, she was content. Contentment was a thing she'd built plank by plank, the way Tam rebuilt the engine — deliberately, over years, out of parts that didn't obviously fit together until you made them. Rules were part of the construction. Jude had rules the way other boats had keels. Never run a channel you haven't sounded. Never carry cargo you can't look at straight. Never take a passenger whose trouble might follow them aboard. Never, ever, ask the river for more than it's offering.

The rules kept the boat alive. The rules kept her alive. And lately — this season, this month, this slow golden afternoon with the light coming long across the water — the rules had started to feel less like a keel and more like a cage, and Jude did not know what to do with that information except refuse to have it.

Restless contentment, Marisol called it, when Jude drank too much coffee on the stern deck and watched the miles go by without seeing them. *You're happy the way a caged bird is happy, capitana. Happy there's no cat.* Jude had thrown a dish towel at her.

Ahead, the channel bent east around a bluff the color of dried blood, and the late sun came off the water in a long hammered sheet, and for a moment — just a moment — Jude forgot the low water and the season's end and the strange itch under her ribs, and simply flew. This was the part no one who lived ashore would ever understand. The boat beneath her feet became an extension of her spine; the wheel spoke to her hands in pressures and vibrations; the current talked to the hull in a grammar of push and pull that she'd been learning for twenty years and would never finish learning. There was no past and no future at the wheel. There was only the next hundred yards of water, forever.

That was the whole trick of her life, she thought. Live at the wheel. Stay in the next hundred yards. Miles, not years.

Then the engine note changed — a stutter, a cough — and Tam's voice came up the hatch again, and the spell broke the way it always broke, into ordinary time.

"She's running hot, captain. I want to stop and let her breathe before we hit the narrows."

"There's a slough two miles up, past the dead willow," Jude said. "We'll anchor there tonight." She glanced at the sky, reading it out of habit: high haze, no wind, the kind of evening that came down cool and still as poured glass. Good anchoring weather. Good dying weather, some old-timers said, though they said it about everything.

Two miles later she brought the *Marrow* into the slough with the easy authority of long practice — a hard turn at the willow snag, a glide, the anchor going off the bow with a splash that rang in the quiet like a struck bell. The boat swung, settled, went still. Somewhere back in the reeds a bittern made its pump-handle sound, once, twice. The first mosquitoes found them within a minute, because the river gave nothing for free.

Marisol appeared on deck with a plate of fried plantains and salt fish, and the smell of it drifted aft where Jude was coiling line, and for a few minutes the three of them existed in the small warm economy of a boat at rest: Tam up top cleaning the condenser coil and swearing at it conversationally, Marisol humming something in a language she'd never explained, Jude working rope and watching the light die on the water.

It was during that light — the last of it, the hour when the river turned from hammered gold to bruised pewter — that Jude saw the man on the bank.

He came out of the tree line like something the forest had decided to give back. Not walking so much as falling forward in installments, catching himself on trunks and roots, a pack on his back that looked older than he was. He was maybe sixty, maybe seventy; the dry season had gotten into him the way it got into everything, shrunk him, cracked him. He reached the mud flat at the mouth of the slough, saw the boat, and stopped. Even at two hundred yards Jude could see him gathering himself, deciding, spending something precious just to raise his arm.

He waved. Then he shouted — a cracked, carrying sound — "*Passage!*" And then, when no answer came fast enough, he waded.

The mud took him to the knee. He went down once, got up streaming black water, and kept coming, and Jude stood at the rail with her arms crossed and her rule-book open in her chest — *never take a passenger whose trouble might follow them aboard* — watching him come, and hating herself a little for how long she watched before she moved.

By the time she'd dropped the boarding ladder he was at the hull, hanging onto the lowest rung with both hands, his head bowed against the planking like a man at prayer. Up close he smelled of woodsmoke and sickness and old sweat, and his eyes, when he lifted them, were yellow as old ivory.

"Please," he said. His voice was gravel dragged over gravel. "Downriver. I'll pay. I've got—" He fumbled at his coat and produced, absurdly, a leather pouch that clinked faintly, and a folded paper packet that he held the way a drowning man holds driftwood. "I've got business downriver. I'm out of time."

"Out of time how?" Jude asked. It wasn't kindness. It was triage; you asked the question the same way you checked a hull for holes.

The man smiled, and the smile was worse than the yellow eyes. "How do you think, captain?"

Tam had come down from the engine room and stood at the edge of the deck light, wiping his hands on a rag, saying nothing. Marisol had come out too, silent, dish towel over her shoulder. The boat waited. The river waited. Somewhere in the reeds the bittern pumped its lonely handle.

"I don't take sick men," Jude said. "Rule."

"I know your rules." He said it strangely — not bitter, not pleading, almost gentle, as if he'd studied her from a distance the way she studied channels. "Everyone on this stretch knows the *Marrow*'s rules. That's why I came to you and not the others. The others would rob me and dump me at the first landing. You'd at least put me off where there's a doctor." He coughed then, a deep wet tearing sound, and had to hold the ladder with his whole body while it passed, and when it was done he stayed bent over the rung, breathing in small careful sips, like a man counting coins he was afraid he'd already spent.

Jude looked at Tam. Tam looked at the man, then at Jude, and shrugged one shoulder — *your call, captain, it's always your call* — which was its own kind of loyalty and its own kind of burden.

"What's your name?" she asked the man.

"Elias Vane."

"What's the business?"

Vane lifted his head. In the last of the light his face was all planes and hollows, a map of a country Jude didn't want to visit, and his yellow eyes fixed on hers with a sudden ferocious clarity, the fever burning through the exhaustion for one bright second, like a lamp flaring before the oil's gone.

"The business is a debt," he said. "One I mean to pay before the river collects me. And you're the only boat on this water that runs at night, captain Lockridge, and the only one whose captain I'd trust with it."

The name landed on the deck between them like a dropped tool.

Jude hadn't used the name Lockridge in nineteen years. She was Jude Marsh at the fuel docks, Jude the pilot on the manifests, *capitana* to Marisol, *captain* to everyone else. The name Lockridge belonged to another life, a shore life, a life she had walked away from one night with smoke behind her and ash in her pockets, and she had buried it so thoroughly that sometimes she almost believed it had never been hers.

And this dying stranger, this ghost waded out of the treeline, had just spoken it aloud on her own deck.

"How do you know my name?" she said quietly.

Vane smiled again, and this time there was something almost apologetic in it, and something else underneath — something that looked, God help her, like relief. Like a man setting down a weight he'd carried so long his shoulders had grown around it.

"I know a great many things I wish I didn't," he said. "Let me aboard, captain. Let me buy passage downriver, and by the time we reach the delta, you'll understand why it had to be you." His grip slipped on the rung; Tam moved toward the ladder without being told. "I'm out of time," Vane whispered, "and the tide's going out, and I swear to you on whatever you hold holy — I swear on the river itself — what I'm carrying is worth more than gold, and it belongs to someone else, and I am the last man alive who can deliver it."

The mosquitoes whined. The engine ticked as it cooled. Downriver, invisible beyond the bend, the tide was indeed turning, beginning its long slow withdrawal toward the sea, taking the day's depth with it, and Jude felt — with the same below-language certainty with which she tasted falling water — that something in her life had just begun to drain as well, pulling back its skirts, getting ready to show her the truth.

She stepped aside from the ladder.

"One night," she said. "You ride to the delta, you keep to the stern cabin, and you tell me everything before we're under way. Those are my terms."

"Captain's terms," Vane agreed, and began, hand over hand, to climb.

* * *

They got him into the stern cabin and Marisol fed him broth he could barely swallow, and by full dark Elias Vane lay on the narrow bunk looking smaller than he had on the bank, as if the act of being rescued had finally permitted him to stop holding himself together. Tam went back to his engine. Marisol washed dishes and said nothing, which from Marisol was a sermon.

Jude sat on the bunk's edge, because the man had asked her to stay a moment, and there was a quality to his asking — stripped of all performance, all strength — that made refusal impossible even for a woman with rules.

"In the morning," Vane said. His eyes were closed. "In the morning I'll show you what I carried out of the hills, and you'll want to throw me off the boat, and I'll beg, and you'll hate yourself, and then you'll say yes. I know the shape of you, captain. I've watched this river a long time." A breath, thin as thread. "But tonight — tonight I just need to hear the water. Do you mind? Twenty years I listened for it from the wrong side."

Jude stood to go. At the door she paused, because the fever had a rhythm and she wanted to mark it, and in the pause Vane spoke once more, so softly she nearly missed it.

"They'll come for it," he murmured, mostly asleep already, drifting. "Hale's people. They can smell paper the way sharks smell blood. Don't let them photograph your boat, captain. Don't let them learn your face."

The lamp guttered. Out on the dark river the tide turned, and turned, and went out.

Jude stood a long time in the companionway, listening to a dying man listen to the water, and understood — with the calm, terrible clarity of a pilot reading a channel at low water — that her next hundred yards had just changed forever.
