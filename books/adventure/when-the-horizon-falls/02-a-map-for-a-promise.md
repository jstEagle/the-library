## 2. A Map for a Promise

The fever came on with the dark, the way fevers do on the river — sudden and total, as if the sickness had been waiting all day for permission.

Jude heard it first through the deck planks: a shuffling, then a heavy, graceless fall against the rail of the guest bunk in the wheelhouse's shadow. She climbed down from the pilot house, lamp in hand, and found Elias Vane curled on his side like a man trying to make himself small enough to disappear. His skin had gone the color of old tallow, slick with sweat that steamed faintly in the lamplight. His lips moved around words that weren't words.

"Marisol," Jude called up the companionway. "Bring the willow bark and the good blanket."

Marisol appeared in her nightdress, hair bound in a scarf, and took one look at the prospector before her face settled into the particular grimness she reserved for lost causes. "That's not river fever," she said quietly. "That's been in him a while. We're just where he decided to stop."

"Then we stop with him."

Tam came down last, drawn by the light, and stood at the foot of the bunk with his arms crossed over his barrel chest, watching Vane the way he watched a failing engine — diagnosing, measuring, deciding whether it was worth parts. "He's going to die on my boat," Tam said. It wasn't cruelty. It was arithmetic.

"He's going to die somewhere," Jude said. "Better here than in the reeds."

She spent the night doing what little there was to do. Cool cloths wrung from river water. Willow bark steeped bitter and spooned between cracked lips. Marisol sat with him through the middle hours, humming something low and tuneless from her childhood, an old habit she claimed kept the dying company. Tam went back to the engine room, because grief and fear made him restless and engines were where his hands knew what to do.

Toward the black hour before dawn — the hour rivermen called the dead tide, when the current seemed to forget itself — Vane surfaced from the fever long enough to know where he was.

His hand shot out and caught Jude's wrist with a strength that surprised them both.

"You stayed." His voice was gravel dragged through water. His eyes were bright and wrong, burning with the fever, but behind the burn was something lucid and desperate. "You could've put me off at the bend. Pilots do. Cheaper than a burial."

"I don't put people off," Jude said. "It's a rule."

"Rules." He almost laughed; it turned into a cough that bent him double, and when he straightened there was blood on his lip, a thin rust thread against the gray of his beard. He wiped it with the back of his hand, matter-of-fact, a man who had stopped being shocked by his own body some miles back. "Good. A woman with rules. I need a woman with rules, because I'm about to ask you to break every one you've got."

"You're about to sleep."

"I'm about to die." He said it plainly, without drama, the way he might have reported the weather or the depth of a channel. "I've known since the cough started in the spring. I've had time to make arrangements. Most of them fell through." His grip tightened on her wrist. "One remains."

* * *

He made her sit. He would not begin until she sat, and she did, because there are arguments even a stubborn captain loses to a dying man.

From inside his coat — the coat stiff with dried mud and older sweat — he drew a bundle wrapped in oilcloth, tied with cord gone soft as string cheese from handling. He worked the knot with fingers that shook, cursed softly when they failed, tried again. Jude reached out once to help and he flinched away from her hand so sharply that she understood: whatever this was, no one else's fingers were to touch it. Not yet. Maybe not ever.

The oilcloth opened around a map.

Not a printed chart, nothing from any survey office. This was hand-drawn on heavy paper gone amber with age, inked in a careful, patient hand — soundings marked in fathoms, bearings noted in a cramped script, channels and shoals rendered in cross-hatching fine as fabric. And along the margins, in the same hand but different ink, newer annotations: dates, depths, corrections. Someone had been refining this map for years. Decades, maybe.

At its center, folded into the crease, was a wax seal the color of dried blood, stamped with a device Jude didn't recognize — a starburst, six points, pressed deep into wax that had cracked with age but never broken.

"What is it?" she asked.

"It's a map," Vane said. "And it's a debt." He laid it flat on the blanket between them, smoothing it with both palms like a man soothing an animal. "Thirty-one years ago I helped sink something in these waters. You understand me? Helped. There were four of us, and what we did we did for money, and the money was good, and we told ourselves the man who paid us was only a businessman and businessmen are allowed their sins because everyone has sins." His breath whistled. "The thing we sank holds papers. Papers that say who really owns what in this delta. Papers that would hang a very great man if they ever saw daylight."

Jude looked at the map. At the seal. At the fever-bright eyes fixed on her face, reading her the way she read water — for the hidden rock, the false channel, the lie beneath the surface.

"Hale," she said.

Something flickered across his face — surprise, then something like relief, then something darker. "You know the name."

"Everyone knows the name."

"Everyone knows the empire. Nobody knows the foundation." He tapped the map with one yellowed fingernail. "This is the foundation. The Meridian Star. Salvage vessel, forty-two tons, officially lost with all hands in the flood of 'sixty-one. Unofficially scuttled in disputed water by four men who were paid to make sure certain documents never surfaced again. Three of those men are dead. Drank themselves into the ground, mostly. Guilt is thirsty work." He smiled without humor. "I'm the fourth. And I've spent thirty-one years diving for it, mapping the approach, learning the currents, buying off officials, lying to everyone who ever loved me—" his voice caught, just slightly, on a word that wasn't *lying* at all, though he covered it fast, "—and now I'm out of tides. Literally. The dry season closes that channel in weeks, and after that the water drops and the wreck sits exposed to anyone Hale sends, and Hale has been sending people for years. He knows someone's been looking. He doesn't know how close I've come."

"Why tell me?"

"Because you're the only boat on this river that didn't stop when I asked." He laughed, wet and ragged. "Do you know how many boats passed me at that landing? Eleven. Eleven boats, and ten of them saw a dying man and calculated what a corpse costs versus what a passenger pays, and cast off. You stopped. That's either stupidity or principle, and I'm gambling it's principle."

"It might be stupidity."

"Then I'm gambling on that too." He pulled himself up against the bulkhead, and for a moment the fever receded and Jude saw the man he must have been — broad-shouldered, hard-eyed, a voice built for shouting over wind. What remained was a ruin wearing that man's ghost. "Here's the trade. The map is yours. Free and clear, no payment asked, because I can't pay and won't insult you by pretending otherwise. In exchange, one promise. One. Find my daughter. Her name is Ada. Give her what's hers."

"What's hers is a map to a sunken ship full of damning paperwork?"

"What's hers is the truth about her father." His voice cracked clean through on the word *father*, and for a moment he was just an old man in pain, and Jude had to look away. "I left her when she was three. Left her with the sisters at the mission school upriver, told myself I'd come back rich enough to make it right, and then — well. Then I spent thirty years chasing a wreck instead of a child, because the wreck couldn't look at me the way she'd look at me." He swallowed. "You have children, Captain?"

"No."

"Then you can't understand what I'm asking. Good. Better you do it blind than do it understanding, because understanding is where mercy dies." He pushed the map toward her. "Promise me."

"I don't make promises I don't understand."

"Then understand this." He leaned close, and she smelled the sickness on him, sweet and rotten, like fruit past its time. "Hale's people are already on the river. They bought a diver out of Port Cane two months ago. They're asking questions at every fuel dock between here and the delta. When I die, this map becomes the most dangerous piece of paper on this water, and whoever holds it becomes a target. If you refuse, I'll give it to the river, and Ada gets nothing, and Hale wins by default. Is that what your rules want?"

Jude sat with it. The lamp hissed. Somewhere below, Tam's wrench rang against iron, steady as a heartbeat. Outside, the river moved in the dark, indifferent, carrying everything toward the sea and nothing back.

She thought about rules. She thought about how a person builds a life out of them — keep to the channel, pay your debts, never carry what you can't deliver, never promise what you can't perform — and how the whole structure holds only until something like this climbs aboard at dusk and asks you to choose between the rule and the thing the rule was protecting.

"If I take it," she said slowly, "I decide how it's delivered. Not you. Me."

"Agreed."

"And I don't sell it. To anyone. Including you, including Hale, including God."

"Agreed."

"And if delivering it puts my crew in danger—"

"It will," Vane said simply. "It already has. The moment I stepped aboard, Captain, you were in it. The only question left is whether you're in it holding the map or empty-handed."

* * *

He died at dawn.

It happened the way the old rivermen said it should happen, which almost never happens: cleanly, between one breath and the next, while the eastern sky was still the color of unpolished pewter. Marisol was sitting with him. She called Jude's name once, softly, and by the time Jude reached the bunk it was done — Vane's chest stilled, his eyes half-open and aimed at the porthole, at the first thin light coming up the river.

They buried him at the first suitable bank, in ground high enough to stay dry, under a cottonwood whose roots would hold the earth against the rains. Tam dug. Marisol wrapped him in canvas and sewed it shut with sail twine, quick and competent, and said a few words in Spanish that Jude didn't understand but didn't need to. Jude read the service from the dog-eared book in the wheelhouse drawer, the one that had come with the boat from some previous owner, and her voice held steady except on the last line, which no one remarked upon.

*Man that is born of woman hath but a short time to live, and is full of misery.*

The river took the sound of it and carried it off downstream, the way it took everything.

* * *

It was that evening, alone in the wheelhouse with the map spread on the chart table, that Jude found the second document.

She'd been studying the map for hours — memorizing it, because a map can be taken from you but a memorized channel cannot — tracing the annotated approach to the waypoint reef, the bearing lines converging on a stretch of disputed water where three boundaries met and none agreed. The paper was thick, old-fashioned, layered. And near the fold, where decades of handling had loosened the backing, a corner lifted.

She should have left it. Some part of her knew that — knew that a sealed thing is sealed for reasons, that Vane had given her the map and the promise and nothing more, that prying into the backing was a violation of a dead man's intent.

But she was a captain, and captains inspect their cargo.

She peeled the backing back carefully, working it loose with a thumbnail, and beneath it, glued flat against the stiff linen, was a second sheet. Smaller. Thinner paper, the kind used for carbon copies, typed rather than handwritten, and folded twice into a rectangle no bigger than a playing card.

She unfolded it under the lamp.

Names. A column of names, typed in a fading ribbon: *E. VANE. T. OKAFOR. S. MERCER. R. HALE.* Dates beside each — 1961, 1961, 1963, and beside Hale's name nothing, just a dash, as if the date hadn't come due yet. Below the names, amounts. Below the amounts, a line of text that made the wheelhouse feel suddenly colder:

*Payment in full for services rendered, Meridian Star salvage operation, per agreement of record. Balance of claim transfers to surviving principals upon recovery of cargo.*

And beneath that, in Vane's handwriting, pressed so hard the pen had torn the paper:

*Meridian. He never paid the fourth share. He thinks the paper died with the ship. Make him wrong.*

Jude sat back. The lamp hissed. The river ran.

So it wasn't just proof of fraud. It was a ledger — a bill of sale for a crime, with Hale's own name on it, and a debt outstanding. Vane hadn't just wanted redemption. He'd wanted *payment*. The fourth share, withheld for thirty-one years, accruing interest in the form of leverage. The map wasn't a gift. It was a weapon, and he'd handed it to a stranger because his own hands were too busy dying.

And somewhere in the arithmetic of it, threaded through names she didn't recognize — Okafor, Mercer — was a daughter named Ada who had been left poor on purpose, or by accident, or by some mixture of the two that only the dead man could have explained and hadn't.

Jude refolded the document. Slid it back beneath the backing. Pressed the linen down until it lay flat, innocent, indistinguishable from the map itself.

Then she stood at the window a long time, watching the dark water slide past, and felt the shape of the thing settle onto her shoulders like a yoke. She had promised a dying man she'd find his daughter and deliver what was hers. She had promised herself she wouldn't sell. Both promises had just gotten heavier, and neither had gotten simpler.

Below, she could hear Marisol washing dishes, and Tam muttering at the engine, and the small domestic sounds of the only family she had. Above, the first stars were coming out over the treeline, indifferent as always.

Somewhere upriver was a girl named Ada who didn't know her father was dead. Somewhere downstream, men were asking questions at fuel docks. And somewhere in the disputed water where three boundaries failed to meet, a ship full of lies waited under the silt for someone brave enough or foolish enough to come digging.

Jude rolled the map, sealed it in the oilcloth, and stowed it in the strongbox beneath the chart table — the same box, she realized with a dry twist of humor, that Vane's cargo had once filled.

Tomorrow they'd run for the mission school. Tonight she'd stand her watch, as always, and let the river decide how much trouble a promise could hold.

She just wished — and she was honest enough with herself to admit it, alone in the wheelhouse with the lamp hissing and the miles ticking past — that the trouble didn't feel so much like it had been waiting for her. Like the current itself had bent, all these years, toward this one stretch of water, this one dying man, this one promise.

Like the river had finally come to collect.
