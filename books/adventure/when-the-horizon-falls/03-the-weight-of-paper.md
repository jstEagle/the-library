## 3. The Weight of Paper

Tam Okafor had a theory about engines and people: both ran on what you fed them, and both would tell you the truth eventually, usually at the worst possible moment. He'd spent thirty years listening to machines confess. The *Kestrel*'s engine was an old diesel from a shipyard that no longer existed, and it told him everything — every bad fuel batch, every grain of silt in the coolant, every night Jude pushed her harder than she should. It never lied.

People were another matter.

He was elbow-deep in the engine bay at dawn, three days out from where they'd taken Elias Vane aboard, when he heard Marisol's boots on the deck plates above him. She stopped directly over his head, which meant she wanted him to hear what came next.

"She hasn't slept," Marisol said, to no one, to the river, to him through the floor.

Tam wiped his hands on a rag that was more grease than cloth and climbed up into the grey morning light. The river lay flat as poured tin, mist peeling off it in slow ribbons. At the wheel, Jude stood the way she always stood — one hand resting on the spokes like she was holding a bird she didn't want to startle — but there was something new in her shoulders. A set. Like a woman carrying something heavy in a bag she refused to open.

The map was in the chart drawer beneath the wheel. Tam knew because he'd looked. He wasn't proud of it, but he'd looked.

"Morning," he said.

Jude nodded without turning. "Current's lazy today. We'll make the fuel dock by noon."

"That all?"

"That's all."

Marisol caught Tam's eye across the deck and rolled hers toward the sky. Then she went aft to start breakfast, banging the galley door harder than any door deserved.

* * *

The fuel dock at Cordero Bend was a floating platform of gray planking and rusted drums, run by a man named Ferro who had one glass eye and two opinions about everything. The *Kestrel* took on diesel while Tam haggled over the price of a new impeller and Marisol went for provisions, and it was there — in the shade of Ferro's awning, waiting for the pump gauge to climb — that Tam heard the name for the first time.

Two men stood at the far end of the dock, dressed in the kind of clothes that tried hard not to look expensive. One held a tablet; the other did the talking. Their launch, tied alongside, was white fiberglass with a company crest on the bow: a black star split by a vertical line.

"—paying cash," the talker was saying to Ferro, loud enough to carry. "Salvage rights survey. Wreck of the *Meridian Star*, lost '09, disputed waters below the Serrano line. Anyone with charts, dive logs, witness accounts. We're paying cash, Ferro. Good cash."

Ferro spat over the rail with the precision of long practice. "Nobody dives down there. Water's a graveyard."

"Somebody knows something." The man smiled the way a hook smiles. "Somebody always does."

Tam kept his eyes on the impeller box and his ears wide open. The *Meridian Star*. He turned the name over the way he'd turn an unfamiliar bolt, feeling for threads. He found none. But he watched Jude, up on the boat, watching them — and what he saw in her face made his stomach drop half an inch, the way it did when the depth sounder lied.

She knew the name too.

The second man raised something small and dark. Not a weapon. A camera, old-fashioned, with a lens that caught the light. He aimed it casually along the dock — at the moored boats, at the water, at nothing — and then, unhurried, at the *Kestrel*. Held there. Clicked twice.

Tam was moving before he decided to move. He crossed the planking with his best mechanic's amble, hands loose, face arranged into the mild stupidity that had gotten him out of trouble on four rivers and into it on two.

"Careful with that thing," he said pleasantly. "Boats photograph badly. All our rust shows."

The man lowered the camera. Up close he had the smooth, unlined face of someone whose work never involved weather. "Just documenting traffic."

"Traffic." Tam tasted the word. "River's full of it."

"It is." The smile again. "Funny thing about this stretch, though. Most boats running low water are headed downstream, light and fast. Yours is working upstream, heavy, against the season. That's either a mistake or a mission." He glanced past Tam's shoulder at the *Kestrel*, reading her the way Tam read an engine — hull draft, cargo weight, the wear pattern on her lines. "I'd love to know which."

"You'd be bored inside a minute," Tam said. "We haul machine parts and cookware. My captain won't even tell me what's for dinner."

"No?" The man's eyes flicked back to him, quick and precise as a torque wrench. "And what's your captain's name?"

"Ferro charges double for questions," Tam said, jerking a thumb at the fuel office. "You want answers, buy diesel."

He walked away before the man could decide whether to laugh. Behind him he heard the talker murmur something to the photographer, low, and then the launch's engine coughed awake. When Tam glanced back, the white boat was already sliding out into the current, and the man with the camera was watching the *Kestrel* over his shoulder until the bend took them both.

* * *

That evening, anchored in a willow-choked cut with the fireflies stitching the dark together, Tam laid it out on the deck plates between the three of them the way he'd lay out parts before a rebuild: everything, in order, so everyone could see how it fit.

"The name's Meridian Star. Hale's salvage fleet is paying cash for information on her. And somebody photographed our boat today like we were a criminal record." He looked at Jude. "So I'll ask plain. What's in the chart drawer?"

The lantern swung gently above them. Moths ticked against its glass. Jude sat with her forearms on her knees, and for a long moment she said nothing at all, and Tam felt the silence do what silence does — widen, harden, become a wall.

"A dead man's business," she said finally.

"Elias Vane's business," Marisol said. Not a question.

Jude's jaw tightened. "You went through my things too?"

"I went through his things," Marisol said evenly. "He died in my galley, Jude. I washed his body. I closed his eyes. You think I didn't have a right to know what he carried?" She kept her voice low, but there was iron under it. "A wax-sealed map. Old paper. And whatever else you pulled out of it that you haven't shown us."

Tam watched Jude's hands. They were steady — her hands were always steady, pilot's hands — but the knuckles had gone pale around nothing at all.

"He made me promise," Jude said. "One promise. Find his daughter. Give her what's hers. That's it. That's the whole of it."

"And the Meridian Star?" Tam asked. "Is that part of what's hers?"

Something moved behind Jude's eyes — a calculation, quick and private, the kind of look Tam had seen on men deciding whether to tell a customer the truth about their engine or just take the money. He hated seeing it on her.

"The less you know," she said, "the safer you are."

Marisol laughed once, short and sharp, with no humor in it anywhere. "Safer. Mm. You know what I fled, Jude? You know why a woman who can't stand the smell of money ends up cooking beans on a rust bucket for a captain who pays herself last?" She leaned forward into the lantern light. "I know exactly how safe I'm not. Safe was never on the menu. What I signed up for was *honest*. You tell me the truth or you don't, but don't dress a lie up as protection and hand it to me like a gift."

The river lapped at the hull. Somewhere in the willows a night bird called, three notes, like a question asked twice and then given up.

"We burn it," Tam said quietly.

Both women looked at him.

"The map. Whatever's under the map. We burn it tonight, scatter the ash, and tomorrow we're just a boat hauling cookware upstream because we're idiots. Hale's fleet buys information. Fine. Information we won't have." He spread his scarred hands. "I've spent my life keeping things running by knowing what to leave alone. Some machinery you don't open, Jude. You open it, you can't ever close it again."

"It's not ours to burn," Jude said.

"Whose is it, then? A dead man's? His daughter we've never met?" Tam shook his head. "Paper's a funny kind of cargo, captain. It weighs nothing and it sinks boats."

For a moment — just a moment — Tam thought she might tell them. Her mouth opened. Her shoulders dropped a quarter inch, the way a lock begins to give. And then the moment passed, and the wall came back up, troweled smooth, and Jude Lockridge looked at the two people who had shared her deck for six years and said, in a voice flat as the dawn river:

"When it's done, I'll tell you what matters. Until then, trust me or don't. Those are the choices."

Marisol stood, collected her plate, and walked into the galley. The door shut behind her with a click that was somehow louder than a slam.

Tam stayed. He looked at his captain — at the woman who had pulled him out of a flooded engine room in Vellacruz with the water at his chin and never once let go of his collar — and he felt the loyalty in his chest pull against the doubt like two currents meeting, choppy water, dangerous water.

"I've followed you into weather most captains wouldn't radio about," he said. "I'd follow you into worse. But a crew runs on what you feed it, same as an engine. Starve it long enough, doesn't matter how good the machinery is. It stops."

"I know how engines work, Tam."

"Yeah," he said, getting up, joints cracking. "But you don't listen to them till they're already knocking."

He went below to check the bilge he'd already checked, because checking things was what he had instead of sleep. Through the hull he could hear the river moving, patient and enormous, carrying everything toward the sea and keeping most of what it took.

Before he turned in, he did one more thing he wasn't proud of. He opened the chart drawer — just an inch, just enough — and looked at the corner of oilcloth showing there, and at the edge of paper beneath it where a single word was visible in faded ink, written in a dying man's careful hand:

*Meridian.*

He closed the drawer. Above him, through the deck plates, he heard Jude take the wheel watch alone, and the *Kestrel* creaked at her anchor like a held breath.

Two days later, at the next fuel stop, a boy on the dock handed Tam a folded newspaper and pointed silently at the white launch tied up downstream. On the front page, above the fold, was a photograph of the *Kestrel* — their boat, their rust, their home — and beneath it, a headline:

**HALE FLEET EXPANDS DELTA SURVEY — SALVAGE MAGNATE OFFERS REWARD FOR INFORMATION ON LOST WRECK.**

And stapled to the newspaper, like a fly in amber, was a smaller clipping, older, yellowed at the edges. A society page. A wedding announcement from twenty years ago.

Tam read the bride's family name twice, because the first time he was sure his eyes had slipped a gear.

Then he folded it all away, climbed aboard, and went to find out who Jude Lockridge really was — before Brannock's men came asking the same question.
