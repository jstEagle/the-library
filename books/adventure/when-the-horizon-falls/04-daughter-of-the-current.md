## 4. Daughter of the Current

The river narrowed as they climbed north, and with it the world seemed to narrow too — the banks drawing in like a hand closing around a fistful of water. Jude had run this stretch twice before, years ago, when the fuel was cheaper and her hair still held its color at the temples. It hadn't changed. The jungle leaned over both shores until the sky became a green seam stitched with white birds, and the current ran brown and muscular against the hull, carrying silt from mountains she would never see.

She stood at the wheel through the morning, one hand on the brass rail worn smooth by a decade of other hands — hers, mostly — and let the boat tell her what it needed. The *Cormorant* spoke in vibrations. A shudder through the deck plates meant Tam was fighting the injector pump again; a certain hollow note in the superstructure meant the cargo hatch wanted cinching; and a heaviness in the way the bow shouldered the current meant the river was dropping faster than it should for the season.

Dry season arithmetic. She didn't let herself do that math yet. Not with Elias Vane's map folded into the oilskin packet beneath her chart table, and not with the promise folded inside herself, heavier than any cargo she'd ever carried.

*Find my daughter. Give her what's hers.*

Vane had said it with his eyes already going glassy, his hand around Jude's wrist like a man gripping a rail in a storm. He'd died at dawn two days later with the fever burning him down to nothing, and Jude had buried him on the bluff above Miller's Bend because the ground there was soft and because no one else would. She'd carved his name into a piece of driftwood with her belt knife: ELIAS VANE, and then, after a long moment, FATHER. It seemed like the kind of thing a daughter might want to know he'd been, even if he'd been bad at it.

"Captain." Marisol appeared at the wheelhouse door with coffee in an enamel mug, steam curling off it into the humid air. "You've been standing there since before light. The river isn't going anywhere you're not."

"The river's going somewhere I don't want to be," Jude said. "It's getting shallow."

Marisol set the mug in her hand. The cook was a small woman with iron-gray braids and forearms like dock rope, and she had a way of looking at Jude that made lying feel like a waste of good breath. "We passed the third marker Vane wrote on that map an hour ago. You haven't told us where we're going. Only that we're going."

"I told you. Upriver."

"You told us *north*. North is a direction, mija. It is not a reason." Marisol leaned against the doorframe, arms crossed. Behind her, through the galley window, Tam could be seen elbow-deep in the engine housing, swearing at it with the tenderness of long marriage. "Tam thinks we're running from something. I think we're running toward it. Either way, this crew deserves to know which."

Jude drank the coffee. It was bitter and perfect. "There's a girl," she said finally. "Up past San Rafael. Vane's daughter. He made me promise."

"A promise," Marisol repeated, tasting the word. "And how much does a promise weigh, Captain? In tons?"

"It weighs whatever it costs you to keep it." Jude turned back to the wheel. "That's the only unit of measure I know."

Marisol studied her a moment longer, then snorted softly and pushed off from the doorframe. "Then we'd better hope this girl is worth the freight." She paused at the stairs. "For what it's worth — my mother made me promise things once. On her deathbed. Half of them were for her sake, not mine. Be careful whose promise you're keeping."

She went below, and Jude was alone again with the river and the wheel and the slow, grinding weight of a dead man's last wish.

* * *

San Rafael announced itself by smell before sight: woodsmoke, wet clay, ripening plantains, and under all of it the particular green rot of a mission town built too close to the waterline. The school sat on a low bluff above the landing — a whitewashed building gone gray at the corners, a chapel with a bell tower leaning like a tired man against a wall, and a yard of red dirt where a dozen children in mismatched uniforms chased a rag ball with shrieks that carried all the way down to the water.

Jue took the skiff in alone. She'd left the *Cormorant* anchored mid-channel with Tam and Marisol aboard, partly because the landing looked soft enough to swallow a hull, and partly — though she wouldn't have admitted it aloud — because she wasn't sure yet whether she wanted witnesses to what she was about to do.

The headmaster met her at the gate. He was a narrow man with spectacles and a smile that arrived a half-second after the rest of his face, the kind of clergyman who'd learned to shake hands like a merchant counting change. Father Ambrose, he said, welcome, welcome, and what business brought a riverboat captain to his humble school?

"Looking for a student," Jude said. "Ada Vane. Her father sent word he'd be coming for her. I'm here in his place."

Something flickered behind the spectacles — surprise, or its well-rehearsed cousin. "Ah. The Vane girl." He led her across the yard, children scattering like minnows. "A difficult case. Bright, certainly. Too bright for her own comfort, perhaps. Her father's remittances stopped arriving some years ago, and yet—" he spread his hands, "—she remains. We keep her on as a helper to the younger classes. The sisters have grown fond of her, in the way one grows fond of weather."

"What does that mean?"

"It means," said Father Ambrose mildly, "that she is remarkable and exhausting, and we have never once considered turning her out."

He left her at the door of the laundry shed behind the chapel, where steam billowed out into the afternoon heat and a young woman stood over a boiling copper with a wooden paddle, wrestling sheets the size of sails.

Jude knew her at once, which surprised her. She'd expected a stranger. But there was Vane in the set of the shoulders — that same stubborn forward lean, like a man walking uphill against wind — and something else, something that made Jude's chest tighten in a way she couldn't name. The girl straightened when she heard the footsteps, and her face was Vane's face thirty years younger, minus the rivers of drink and regret: sharp-boned, sun-browned, with eyes the flat gray-green of storm water.

"You're not a sister," the girl said. "Sisters knock like they're apologizing."

"I'm looking for Ada Vane."

"You found her. What did he do?" The paddle came to rest across the copper's rim. No fear in the voice. Just a flat, practiced readiness, the tone of someone who had spent years braced for bad news and had gotten very efficient at receiving it.

"He died," Jude said. "Eleven days ago. Fever. I buried him above Miller's Bend."

The steam rose between them. For a moment Ada Vane didn't move at all — didn't blink, didn't breathe, so far as Jude could tell — and Jude understood that she was watching a person decide, in real time, what to do with a feeling too large for the room.

Then the girl laughed. One short, ugly syllable of it, bitten off at the teeth.

"Of course he did." She turned back to the copper and drove the paddle into the sheets as if they owed her money. "Eleven days ago. And it took you eleven days and a riverboat to come tell me. Was he sober at the end? Don't answer. He was never sober at the end of anything."

"He asked me to find you."

"Oh, *he asked*." The paddle stopped. She turned, and now the storm-water eyes were bright and wet and furious, and she didn't wipe them, didn't give them the dignity of acknowledgment. "Let me guess how it went. He was dying, so suddenly everything mattered. Suddenly there were words. *My daughter, my girl, my Ada.* Did he cry? They always cry. It's the crying that's supposed to fix it. Twenty years of nothing and then five minutes of tears and somehow that's supposed to count as a father."

"He gave me something for you," Jude said quietly. "A map. And a message."

"I don't want his map." Ada pulled off one glove, then the other, finger by finger, with terrible precision. "I want the nineteen years. You can't ferry those upriver, Captain — what's your name?"

"Lockridge. Jude Lockridge."

"Well, Captain Lockridge, you can't deliver the birthdays. You can't deliver the Christmas he didn't come, or the year the fever came through and I nearly died and he was — where? Somewhere north of everywhere, chasing whatever men like him chase." She stepped closer, and Jude saw that her hands were shaking, and that she hated them for it. "So you can take his map and his message and his apology, if there is one, and you can put them exactly where you put him. In the ground."

"I could," Jude agreed. "Or you could hear him out. It takes ten minutes. Then you can still hate him, and you'll hate him informed."

That got her. Something shifted in the girl's face — not softening exactly; more like a door opening onto a darker room. "Informed," she repeated. "Fine. Ten minutes. Say it out here. The sisters gossip through that wall like it's made of paper."

Jude reached into her jacket and drew out the oilskin packet. She unwrapped it carefully, the wax seal already broken from her own reading, and unfolded the map on top of a stack of clean sheets that would now need rewashing. The ink lines caught the light from the doorway — the river system drawn in Vane's cramped, confident hand, waypoints marked, depths noted, and at the center of it all a small, precise star with a name beside it.

Ada looked down at the map.

Jude watched her look. Watched the fury hold for three seconds, five, seven — and then crack, not into grief but into something hungrier. The girl's finger rose and hovered over the star without touching it, the way you hover a hand over a stove to test whether it's still hot.

"The Meridian Star," Ada said. Her voice had changed entirely. All the anger was still in it, but now it was aimed somewhere else, channeled, weaponized. "He used to talk about it. When I was small. Before the letters stopped." Her jaw tightened. "He talked about it the way other fathers talked about God. Like it had done him a personal favor once and he'd spent his whole life trying to get back on its good side."

"Do you know what it is?"

"I know it's a wreck. I know it's in disputed water, which means it's in water where men shoot first and file papers later. And I know—" she stopped. Drew a breath. When she spoke again the voice was lower, controlled, almost gentle, which was somehow worse. "I know that whatever's on that map killed him. Not the fever. The fever just finished the job. He'd been killing himself over that star for twenty years, and now some stranger brings me his ghost in an oilskin wrapper and asks if I'd like to hear him out."

"That's about the size of it."

Ada Vane looked at the map a long time. The laundry steam curled around her; somewhere beyond the wall, the chapel bell began to ring the hour, each stroke falling into the yard like a stone into still water. And Jude, watching her, felt the strange vertigo of recognition — because she knew that look. She had worn that look, nineteen years old and furious, the night she'd stood in her father's study among the ledgers and the brandy glasses and understood exactly what her family's fortune was floating on. She remembered the door. She remembered the road to the river. She remembered deciding, with the same terrible precision this girl was using on a pair of laundry gloves, that she would rather have nothing than have *that*.

The difference — and it cut her to see it — was that Ada Vane had actually been left with nothing. Jude had walked away from a fortune. This girl had never been given the choice.

"Ten minutes," Ada said at last. "Start talking."

So Jude talked. She gave it to her straight, because anything else would have been an insult: the promise, the death, the second document hidden in the map's backing — the names, the dates, the word *Meridian*, the paper trail that pointed at Corvus Hale's shipping empire like a compass needle pointing at a storm. She watched the girl absorb it all without flinching, filing each fact away behind those storm-water eyes, and she thought: *she's smarter than me.* Not cleverer. Smarter. The kind of smart that comes from having had to be.

When Jude finished, Ada was quiet for a long moment.

"Hale," she said. "Corvus Hale. His barges pass this school every month. The sisters make the children wave at them." Her mouth twisted. "He endowed our library. There's a plaque."

"There usually is."

"And you're going after it. Alone. With two crew and a boat old enough to vote." Ada peeled the map off the linens, folded it along Vane's original creases with unsettling exactness, and held it out. "Take me with you."

"No."

"I wasn't asking."

"I noticed." Jude took the map anyway. "It's not a question of asking. It's a question of what's sane. That water is disputed, the dry season's closing the channels, and there are men paid to kill people who go looking for what's on that paper. You're nineteen. You have a life here. Classes. Sisters who are fond of you, in the way one grows fond of weather."

"Don't quote him at me."

"I'm not quoting anyone. I'm telling you the truth, which is that this is no place for—"

"For what?" Ada stepped in close, and up close Jude could see the fine tremor still living in her hands, mastered but not gone. "Say it. Say *a girl*. Go on. My father said it too, the last time I saw him. He said, *it's no place for a girl,* and then he got on a boat and vanished for six years, and I suppose the place was fine for a man." Her chin came up. "Here's what I know, Captain Lockridge. That map is the only true thing my father ever gave me. Not money, not letters, not himself — this. Whatever's at the end of it, it's mine. Mine by every right there is, including the right nobody writes down, which is that I have spent nineteen years being the daughter of a ghost and I am *done* being left on the bank."

The bell finished ringing. In the ringing silence, Jude looked at her — really looked — and saw the whole impossible arithmetic of it: the fury and the hunger and the bone-deep stubbornness, all of it bent on the horizon like a compass needle. Like a young captain who hadn't found her boat yet.

"You'll work," Jude said. It wasn't a question either. "Galley duty, line handling, watches. You'll do what Marisol and Tam say, when they say it. And the first time you endanger my boat or my crew, I put you ashore wherever we happen to be, and I don't look back. Those are the terms."

Ada's eyes narrowed. "Those are terrible terms."

"They're the only ones on offer."

A pause. Then, unexpectedly, the girl smiled — a quick, crooked, dangerous thing, Vane's grin resurrected without the ruin in it — and stuck out a hand calloused from laundry work.

"Ada Vane," she said. "But you knew that. And you're going to regret this, Captain, because I read charts better than anyone on this river, and I intend to prove it before we're done."

Jude shook the hand. It gripped like a deckhand's.

They were halfway across the yard when Father Ambrose intercepted them, spectacles flashing, smile arriving late as ever. "Leaving us already, Miss Vane? And without notice? The sisters will be heartbroken."

"Leave the notice to me, Father," Ada said sweetly. "I'll write it myself. I'm told I'm remarkable at difficult letters."

Jude kept her face neutral, but something in the exchange snagged — the headmaster's gaze lingering on the oilskin packet under her arm a half-second too long, the smile holding steady while the eyes behind the spectacles did their own private arithmetic. She filed it away, the way she filed sandbars and snags and the smell of a storm before it broke.

The skiff carried them back through the gold-and-copper light of late afternoon. At the rail of the *Cormorant*, Marisol took one look at the girl climbing aboard and said, "Ah," in the tone of a woman watching weather roll in. Tam emerged from the engine room wiping grease on a rag, assessed Ada Vane in approximately four seconds, and muttered, "We're all going to die," with what Jude chose to interpret as approval.

Ada stood at the bow as the anchor came up, the river opening ahead of them, and did not look back at the school.

Neither, Jude noticed, did she ask where they were going next. She simply planted her feet the way river people do, and watched the water, and waited for the current to tell her.

Which was, Jude thought, turning the wheel toward the deep channel, exactly what a born pilot looked like — and exactly what Jude had been at her age, the night she burned her name and walked to the river.

She just hoped the girl's ending would be a kinder one than the map promised.

By full dark they were ten miles downstream, running dark themselves with lamps doused, and it was Ada — squinting at the black water from the bow — who called back, calm as Sunday:

"Captain. There's a boat behind us. Running without lights. It's been there since the bend."
