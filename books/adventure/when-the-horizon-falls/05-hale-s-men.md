## 5. Hale's Men

The riverport of Calloway's Bend smelled of tar and overripe mangoes and the particular sweetness of fish left too long in the sun. Ada Vane had been aboard the *Kestrel* for six days, long enough to learn that Tam grunted instead of speaking before his second cup of coffee, that Marisol sang off-key on purpose to annoy him, and that Captain Jude Lockridge slept maybe four hours a night and stood her watches like a woman counting down to something.

Long enough, too, to learn that no one had told her anything about where they were going or why.

She trailed Jude through the market with her arms crossed, watching the captain move through the crowd the way water moves around stones — never touching anyone, never stopping, always finding the open channel. Jude wore a canvas jacket gone soft as cloth could get without falling apart, and a hat pulled low, and she walked like she was trying not to be remembered by the place itself.

"You're doing it again," Ada said.

"Doing what."

"Being mysterious. It's insufferable. You know that, right?"

Jude didn't break stride. "Buy fruit if you're going to complain. Marisol wants limes."

"I don't have any money."

"I know." A folded bill appeared between two fingers without Jude so much as glancing at her, the way a magician produces coins. "Don't spend it all on nonsense."

Ada took the bill and hated how grateful she was, and hated more that Jude seemed to know exactly how much gratitude cost a person like her — a person who'd spent three years at the mission school learning to need nothing from anybody, because needing things was how you got hurt, and wanting things was how you got left.

Her father had wanted things. Look where that had gotten everyone.

The market sprawled along the levee in a chaos of striped awnings and hanging lanterns not yet lit against the afternoon. Boats crowded the fuel dock downstream — shrimp trawlers, a mail packet, two sleek white-hulled cruisers that looked like they'd been born somewhere money grew. Ada noticed them because everything about them was wrong for this river: too clean, too new, too deliberate. They sat at the dock like knives laid out on a table.

Jude noticed them too. Ada saw it happen — saw the captain's eyes flick once toward the cruisers, cataloguing, and then away again, her face going smooth as poured wax.

*She's done this before,* Ada thought. *Whatever this is. She's been hunted before.*

They bought limes from a woman with hennaed hands who pinched Ada's cheek and called her *chiquita*, which Ada tolerated because the limes were free with the cheek-pinching. They bought rope. They bought a coil of copper wire that Tam had scrawled on a list in handwriting like a spider drowned in ink. And then, at the mouth of the alley behind the chandlery, where the crowd thinned and the shadows went long and cool, a man stepped out of a doorway and said:

"Captain Lockridge. What a coincidence."

Ada felt it before she understood it — a change in the air pressure of Jude's body beside her, subtle as a rudder shifting. The captain stopped. Turned. Smiled.

It was the smile that frightened Ada. Not because it was unfriendly, but because it was *perfect*. Warm at the corners, easy, a man-of-the-world's smile, and Ada realized in one cold drop of understanding that she had never once seen Jude Lockridge actually smile in six days aboard the *Kestrel*. This one was manufactured. This one was armor.

The man in the doorway was big without being soft about it — heavy through the shoulders, gray at the temples, dressed in a linen suit that probably cost more than the *Kestrel*'s engine. He had a face like a door someone had tried to kick in and mostly failed: dented, but still shut. His hands were folded in front of him like a deacon's.

"Mr. Brannock," Jude said pleasantly. "I don't believe we've met."

"No, we haven't." He extended a hand. Jude shook it. Ada watched the handshake the way you watch two dogs decide something. "Corvus Hale sends his regards. He's an admirer of independent captains. Thinks there are too few of them left."

"He'd be right about that."

"And yet here you are." Brannock gestured down the levee with one hand, taking in the boats, the river, the whole sunstruck sprawl of the Bend. "Independent. Running light water in the dry season, which takes either desperation or skill. I'm told your boat draws eighteen inches. That's remarkable craftsmanship. Old Lockridge yards built hulls like that."

The name landed in the alley like a dropped glass.

Ada watched Jude's face and saw — nothing. Not a flicker. Not a twitch. The word *Lockridge* passed across it and left no more trace than wind across water, and Ada thought: *she heard it, she knew exactly what he meant, and she gave him back a blank wall.* It was the smoothest thing Ada had ever seen a human being do, and it made her stomach turn over, because she couldn't tell whether she was watching mastery or something colder.

"Just good luck with the yard work," Jude said. "My engineer keeps her floating."

"Your engineer." Brannock smiled. It did not reach anywhere near his eyes. "May I be direct, Captain? Mr. Hale is assembling a consortium. Salvage interests. There's a wreck in these waters — old wreck, deep water, historically significant — and Mr. Hale believes certain documents relating to it may have come into circulation recently. Documents of, ah, genealogical interest." His gaze slid to Ada, lingered just long enough to be rude, and returned. "He's authorized me to make a standing offer. Fifty thousand dollars for information leading to their recovery. Seventy-five if the information proves actionable within the month."

The number hung in the air. Ada did the arithmetic without meaning to — seventy-five thousand dollars was eleven years of the mission school's entire budget, was every debt her father's ghost had ever left her, was a life. She looked at Jude expecting — what? She wasn't sure. Hesitation. At least hesitation.

Jude laughed. Actually laughed, a short warm sound, and shook her head like Brannock had told a joke at a party.

"Genealogical documents," she said. "Mister — Brannock, was it? — I haul diesel and dried fish up this river. Last week my cargo manifest was forty chickens and a church organ. If I'm sitting on somebody's family tree, nobody told me."

"Of course." Brannock's voice stayed pleasant. "But rumors travel faster than chickens. And rumor says a prospector named Elias Vane came down from the high country in bad shape last month, and rumor says he died, and rumor says he passed something along before he did." He paused, delicately. "Rumor also says the *Kestrel* took on a passenger at Miller's Landing and hasn't posted a cargo manifest since."

"That would be the girl," Jude said, and jerked her thumb at Ada with such casual indifference that Ada's ears burned. "Vane's daughter. She needed a ride to the mission school at Saint Ambrose. We're dropping her when it's convenient. As for her father—" Jude shrugged elaborately. "Man was dying. Talked a lot. Fever does that. I was mostly trying to keep him comfortable."

Every word of it was true, and every word of it was a lie, and Ada stood there holding a bag of limes while this stranger dismantled her father's death into small talk, and she could not decide whether to admire Jude or be terrified of her. Both, she decided. Both at once. That was apparently what knowing Jude Lockridge meant.

Brannock studied Jude for a long moment. Somewhere behind him a cart rattled past; a vendor shouted prices; the river went on glittering beyond the levee as though none of this were happening.

"Fever," Brannock said. "Yes. They talk, the dying ones." He reached into his breast pocket and Ada flinched — but it was only a card, cream-colored, heavy stock, embossed with a black starburst she recognized without knowing why until she did: a compass rose with eight points, and beneath it, in engraved letters, *Hale Maritime Concern*. "Seventy-five thousand. The offer doesn't expire, but the season does. Dry water closes channels, Captain, and closed channels close opportunities. When you reconsider — and people in your position almost always reconsider — you can find me on the white cruiser at the fuel dock. The one named *Providence*."

"Providence," Jude repeated. "That's a funny name for a ship belonging to a man who buys dead men's secrets."

Something moved behind Brannock's eyes — the first honest thing his face had done. "Mr. Hale would say Providence belongs to whoever can afford the crew." He tipped his hat to Ada, a mockery of courtesy. "Miss Vane. My condolences on your loss. Your father was a complicated man."

"You didn't even know him," Ada said.

"No," Brannock agreed. "But I've read his file."

And then he was walking away, unhurried, linen suit catching the light, and the alley swallowed him, and Ada discovered her hands were shaking around the bag of limes.

* * *

Jude didn't speak until they were three streets from the levee, cutting through the back lanes where the houses leaned together overhead and the smell shifted from fish to woodsmoke to jasmine growing wild through broken fences.

"You lied," Ada said finally.

"I misdirected. It's different."

"It isn't. You told him I was cargo."

"You *were* cargo. Best cover I could give you in ten seconds." Jude stopped at a corner, checked the street both ways with a habit so ingrained it looked like breathing, and turned toward the docks. "He knows about Vane. Which means someone talked at Miller's Landing, or someone followed us, or both. Either way, from today forward, you're a schoolgirl going home and I'm a freight captain with a soft heart. That story keeps you safe. Don't poke holes in it."

"By pretending my father never existed?"

"By keeping him alive in the only way that matters right now." Jude's voice didn't rise, but something under it did, a current under calm water. "That man works for someone who has ruined better families than yours, Ada. He offered me seventy-five thousand dollars in an alley because he already knows I have something worth seventy-five thousand dollars. Do you understand what that means? It means the polite part is finished. Everything after this is the part where people stop being polite."

Ada hugged the limes to her chest. "You knew that name he said. Lockridge. You went still."

"I go still when men in expensive suits say things they shouldn't know. It's called caution."

"It's called something else." Ada planted herself in the middle of the lane, jasmine petals drifting down around her like slow snow, and forced herself to hold the captain's eyes. "There were Lockridges in the history books at school. Shipping family. Founding family, the book said. Then there was a scandal, and the line just — ends. Like a sentence that stops mid-word. Hale's fleet bought most of their routes after. Everybody knows that story. So when a man working for Corvus Hale says *Lockridge yards* to you, and you smile at him like he's complimenting your engine—"

"Ada."

"—then either it's a coincidence, which I don't believe in anymore, or you are the most dangerous kind of liar there is. The kind who's good at it."

For a moment — just a moment, half a breath — something cracked in Jude Lockridge's face. Not much. A hairline fracture, the kind you see in glaze on old pottery, running under the surface where you can't follow it. Then it sealed over, smooth as ever, and Ada understood that whatever lived behind that composure had been buried a long time ago and buried deep, and that Jude would dig her own grave before she let anyone else excavate it.

"Elias Vane asked me for a promise," Jude said quietly. "I keep my promises. That's the whole story you need. Come on — Tam will send a search party if we're late, and it'll be a search party with opinions."

She walked on. Ada stood in the jasmine shade a moment longer, heart hammering, and thought about the way Jude had said *the girl* — flat, dismissive, perfect — and the way her hand had found the small of Ada's back, steering her out of Brannock's sightline, gentle as anything, at exactly the same time.

Protection and deception, wearing the same gloves.

* * *

The *Kestrel* sat low and patient at the commercial moorings, well away from the white cruisers, and Tam met them at the gangplank with a face like a storm front.

"We've got company," he said, before Jude's feet touched deck. "Fella been photographing the boat since noon. Stood on the seawall with a camera on a tripod, real professional about it. Took her picture, took our registration numbers, took Marisol coming back from the market. Left twenty minutes ago on a motorcycle heading south."

"South toward the narrows?" Jude asked.

"Toward the narrows."

Jude set down the rope and copper wire and stood very still, looking south down the river, where the water ran gold with late light and the channel bent away toward reefs and shoals and, somewhere far along it, whatever Elias Vane had died pointing at. Ada watched her captain's shoulders and saw the weight settle onto them, ton by ton, like cargo being loaded by invisible hands.

"Seventy-five thousand dollars," Marisol said from the galley doorway, wiping her hands on her apron. "I heard it from the fruit woman before you two were even out of the market. Whole levee's heard it by now, I expect. Half of them are doing sums."

"Let them do sums." Jude picked up the wire. "Tam, double the anchor watch tonight. Marisol, we provision and take on fuel first thing — full tanks, extra drums. We leave on the morning tide."

"The morning tide runs shallow past the point," Tam said. "You know that."

"I know that." Jude's jaw tightened. "We'll run it anyway. Because by tomorrow evening, every informant on this river will know exactly what the *Kestrel* carries and exactly what she's worth, and I would rather scrape my keel on sand than sit in one place waiting for Brannock to come back with a better argument than money."

Ada climbed aboard and stood at the rail, watching the white cruiser *Providence* gleam at the fuel dock, watching a figure in linen stand motionless on its aft deck, watching her — watching them all, patient as tide.

Her father had traded something to a dying stranger. That stranger had traded it to a woman who lied like breathing. And now the whole river was doing arithmetic, and somewhere south, in the narrowing channels, the numbers were already turning against them.

"What aren't you telling me?" Ada asked.

Jue coiled the copper wire slowly, loop over loop, and didn't look up.

"When you've earned the answer," she said, "you won't have to ask."

The evening bell rang from the mission across the water, and Ada thought of her father's file, and the word *complicated*, and wondered which of the two women on this boat — the one who lied or the one who kept promises — was the greater danger to her heart.
