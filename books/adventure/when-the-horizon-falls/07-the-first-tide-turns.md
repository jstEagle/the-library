## 7. The First Tide Turns

The reef announced itself before Jude saw it — a long seam of white water stitching across the channel where the current tore itself open on submerged stone. She cut the engine to half and stood at the wheel with her weight forward, reading the water the way other people read letters: the boil over the outer bar, the slick green tongue of deep channel threading between two brown smears of shoal, the way the current leaned east as if whispering directions.

"Starboard a point," she said, though her hands were already doing it.

Tam's voice came up through the deck plates from the engine room, muffled and constant as a heartbeat. "She's running warm, Captain. Whatever you're planning, plan it quick."

"I'm always planning it quick."

"That's what worries me."

Marisol appeared at the companionway with three tin mugs balanced in one hand and the particular expression she wore when she disapproved of geography. "There's nothing out here but rocks and birds," she said, handing the mugs down — one to Jude, one to the deck for Tam to collect later, one pressed into Ada Vane's hands where the girl sat on the cargo hatch with the map spread across her knees.

"There's a reef," Jude said.

"I can *see* there's a reef. I'm asking why we love it."

Ada didn't look up. Four days since the mission school, and the girl had barely spoken except to ask questions about soundings and channels and the meaning of the faded ink notations along the map's western margin — questions sharp enough that Jude had caught herself answering them fully, honestly, which was more than she gave most adults. The girl had a way of studying water that made Jude uneasy in a way she couldn't name. It was like watching her own reflection move independently.

"The first waypoint," Ada said now, without being asked. Her finger rested on a symbol like a fishhook drawn in Vane's cramped hand, beside a notation: *where the river forgets its name.* "He wrote it twice. Underlined."

"He underlined everything," Jude said.

"He didn't underline anything else." Ada looked up, and her eyes were Vane's eyes — gray-green, river-colored, stubborn as silt. "You've known that for four days. You just don't want to say it out loud because saying it makes it real."

Jude drank her coffee instead of answering. It was scorched and bitter and perfect. Marisol had a genius for coffee that tasted like it had opinions about you.

*Where the river forgets its name.* She'd turned the phrase over all night while anchored in the willows, listening to the boat tick and settle around her like an old animal dreaming. Rivers forgot their names in only a few ways: where they braided into a hundred threads and none was the river anymore; where they emptied and lost themselves in tide; or where they ran so fast and broken over stone that the water stopped being water and became noise. A reef, then. A reef where the charted name of the channel dissolved into rapids, and no pilot bothered to remember which thread was which.

Ahead, the white water stretched bank to bank, and beyond it the river bent south into country the charts marked with a single dismissive word: *BAD.*

"Tam," Jude called down the hatch. "How warm?"

"Warm enough to have feelings about it."

"Give me ten minutes of your best behavior."

"I *always* give you my best behavior. It's the engine that sulks."

Jue put the wheel over and took them at the slick green tongue, full throttle, the hull rising until the plates sang. The white water came on like a wall deciding whether to be merciful. She felt it in her boots before she saw it — the deep channel's pull, the river grabbing the keel and *choosing* them — and then they were through, the roar falling away astern, the boat settling into the sudden hush of the bend below, where the water ran dark and slow and deep as held breath.

Behind them, the reef kept its white seam stitched shut. Ahead, the river forgot its name.

* * *

They found the marker on the falling tide.

It was Ada who spotted it — of course it was Ada — a pale sliver snagged in the roots of a drowned tree at the edge of the slack water behind the reef's downstream shoulder. Jude brought the boat in slow, bumping gently against the sunken branches, and Marisol hooked the thing aboard with the boathook before anyone could tell her it might be dangerous.

It was a diver's marker. A slate, lead-edged, the kind salvage men weighted to the bottom to mark a working site, its line rotted clean away. Someone had scratched into the slate face with a knife:

**VANE — 40 FT — SILT — COME BACK AT DRY**

And beneath that, newer, deeper, gouged by a different hand:

**TOO LATE, OLD MAN**

Nobody spoke for a moment. The river slapped against the hull. Somewhere in the drowned trees a heron lifted off on prehistoric wings and beat away south.

"Dry season," Ada said finally. Her voice was very steady, which is how Jude knew it wasn't. "*Come back at dry.* He meant this year. He meant—" She stopped. Her jaw worked. "He was dying and he knew he'd never come back himself, so he sent me. Sent *us.*"

"He sent the map," Jude said. "You were the address."

"That's worse." Ada set the slate down on the hatch cover with exaggerated care, the way people handle things when they want to throw them. "That's so much worse."

Jude crouched and studied the slate herself. The second inscription bothered her more than she let show. *Too late, old man* — gouged hard, angry or triumphant, impossible to tell. But the slate hadn't been down there rotting since Vane's last expedition. The line attachment was bright where the knife work was old. The slate's edge bore scratches too fresh to have come from the river.

Someone had been here within days. Maybe hours.

"Marisol," Jude said quietly. "Get the glasses."

Marisol fetched the binoculars from the wheelhouse, and Jude climbed to the roof of the cabin and swept the banks. The bluff above the reef. The willow tangle downstream. The far shore, where a fisherman's drying rack stood abandoned and crooked. Nothing moved but the water and the birds.

Then, downstream, where the unnamed river widened toward the delta country — a smudge of smoke. Not cooking smoke. Engine smoke, low and flat, hugging the water the way boats did when they didn't want to be seen.

Jude lowered the glasses slowly.

"Captain?" Marisol said.

"Nothing." She handed the glasses back. "We dive on the next slack. Tam, I need the compressor and both tanks checked. And I need you to look at that smoke without making it obvious that you're looking at it."

Tam looked. Tam was many things, but subtle was not among them; his head swiveled like a lighthouse. "Smoke," he reported. "Downstream. Moving upstream. Slow."

"Thank you, Tam."

"Boats that sneak," Tam said, "are boats with reasons."

"Yes."

"And we are currently a boat with reasons."

"*Yes,* Tam."

Ada had gone still on the hatch, the slate in her lap again, her thumb moving over the gouged words as if she could read the anger out of them. "Brannock," she said.

"You don't know that."

"I know Hale's fleet buys information at every fuel dock between here and the sea. I know a stranger photographed our boat at Calloway's Landing while pretending to check his watch. I know Papa—" she caught herself on the word, flinched, drove past it, "—Vane said they'd come. He told me once, before I stopped speaking to him, that if he ever found what he was looking for, men would die over it. I thought he was being dramatic. He was dying of river fever and drinking turpentine-laced whiskey; I thought everything he said was dramatic." She looked up, and there was something new in her face — not fear exactly, but fear's older sibling. "Was he dramatic, Captain?"

Jude thought of Elias Vane in her bunk, fever-bright, trading a wax-sealed map for a promise with the calm of a man settling accounts. *No,* she almost said. *He was the only honest man in the room.*

"No," she said. "He wasn't."

* * *

The slack tide came at midafternoon, the current dying to a mutter, the whole river holding its breath between exhales. Jude dove alone. That wasn't negotiable, and she didn't negotiate it; the water below the reef was forty feet of cold brown murk where the current funneled through gaps in the rock, and she'd seen what the river did to divers who trusted it. She wasn't going to ask Tam or Marisol to do it, and Ada —

Ada had tried. Ada had stood on the hatch in a borrowed diving jacket two sizes too big and informed Jude that she was coming, that it was her father's wreck, her father's marker, her right.

"It's my air hose," Jude had said, "and my funeral. Stay on the boat."

"You can't just—"

"Ada." Jude had held her gaze until the girl's chin dropped a grudging inch. "On this boat, the river decides who dives, and the river has decided it's me. Argue with the river."

"I intend to argue with *you* for years."

"Get in line."

Now she went down the anchor line into the green-black cold, equalizing her ears, the world narrowing to the circle of her mask and the rope in her fist. At twenty feet the light died to a bruise. At thirty-five her boots found bottom — hard clay ribbed with current-scour, littered with rubble fallen from the bluff above. Visibility was an arm's length of drifting sediment, the water thick as tea, and she moved by feel and compass and the memory of the marker's position, sweeping her lamp through clouds of silt that lit up like galaxies and settled like snow.

Forty feet. The depth Vane had scratched. She swam a slow expanding spiral, one hand trailing the clay, feeling for anything that wasn't river.

She found the first sign at the edge of her third circle: a trench. A long, straight excavation in the clay, crisp-edged, recent — recent enough that the current hadn't yet softened it. Silt had begun to drift back into it, but the walls still stood. Someone had worked this bottom with a dredge or a powerful pump, methodically, along a search grid.

And beside the trench, half-buried: a second marker. Newer than Vane's. Plastic, not slate. A commercial salvage tag, stenciled with a registration number and a company name she couldn't read through the fouling — but she knew the stencil font. Everyone on the river knew it. It was the font on every crate, every barge, every bill of lading that came out of the Hale shipping offices.

Her chest went tight around the regulator. She hung in the cold brown water, lamp beam trembling slightly, and made herself think. They hadn't found it. If they'd found the Meridian Star, there'd be no reason to keep shadowing her — they'd simply take the wreck and everything in it. The trench was a search grid, not an excavation. They were looking. Which meant they knew roughly where, but not precisely — which meant somewhere out there was information they didn't have, and the information was folded in oilcloth inside her cabin, sleeping under Ada's pillow.

Or they were close. Days close. Tides close.

Jude finished her spiral anyway, thorough as her father had taught her to be thorough — the thought arrived uninvited and she let it pass through her like cold water — and on the fifth circle her glove knocked against something solid. An iron ring, set into a boulder. Old iron, crusted with rust, but the rust over the ring's inner curve was worn bright. Recently used. And tied off to it, rotted to a black fringe, the ghost of a mooring line.

Vane's. He'd been here. He'd worked this exact spot, ring to boulder, and the boulder sat at the head of a scour channel where the current had dug the clay down another six feet into a pocket of deeper quiet.

She didn't have the air or the light or the nerve to go into that pocket today, with a gunboat smoking somewhere downstream and her compressor wheezing like Tam's conscience. She committed the boulder to memory — its shape, its relationship to the reef's downstream shoulder, the bearing back to the drowned tree — the way her grandfather had taught her to commit channels to memory, back when she'd been someone else.

Then she kicked for the surface, and the river closed the trench behind her like a mouth.

* * *

She came up the ladder and Ada was waiting with a towel and a question mark all over her face, and Marisol was waiting with soup, and Tam was waiting at the rail pointing downstream with the grim satisfaction of a man whose predictions keep coming true.

"Still there," Tam said. "Closer. And it's stopped sneaking."

Jude took the glasses. The smoke had resolved into a boat — a gunboat, proper, steel-hulled, with a wheelhouse built heavy and a mount on the foredeck covered by a tarp that fooled nobody. It sat a mile downstream, dead in the water, at the edge of the channel. Waiting. Not approaching. Not hiding. Just *there*, the way a statement is there.

A flag snapped at its masthead. Even at this distance, even through heat shimmer and river haze, Jude could read the device: a black bird, wings spread, on a field of white.

Corvus. The crow.

"They're not attacking," Marisol said. She'd come up beside Jude, arms crossed, flour still on her wrists. "Why aren't they attacking? We're a wooden boat with a dying engine and a crew of four. They could take us with a rowboat and bad manners."

"Because taking us costs them something," Jude said. "Wreckage floats. Wreckage gets found. Wreckage gets *talked about.*" She lowered the glasses. "Hale doesn't want a story. He wants silence. So they'll follow us. Herd us. Wait for us to either find what we're looking for — and then take it — or turn back — and then let us go."

"And if we do neither?"

"Then they get creative."

Ada stood at the rail with the wind pulling her hair sideways, staring at the distant crow flag, and Jude watched her swallow whatever was rising in her throat. Nineteen years old. Four days off a mission school where the biggest danger had been the headmaster's temper and the porridge. Now a gunboat with her father's ghosts aboard it was sitting in the current like a held breath.

"We could turn back," Ada said. It cost her something to say it; Jude watched the words come out sideways, dragged. "You could drop me at the next landing and turn back, and they'd let you go. That's — that's the smart play. That's what a sensible captain does."

Jude looked at her. The girl's knuckles were white on the rail. Her jaw was set like the reef upstream, like something the river broke against rather than moved.

"Are you asking me to?" Jude said.

"No," Ada said immediately, and then, quieter, furious at herself: "I don't know. Are you going to?"

Jude turned back to the wheelhouse. Below the waterline, the river ran south toward the delta, toward the second waypoint, toward a pocket of deep quiet under a boulder where an honest thief had moored his hope to iron. Behind her, a mile of brown water and a crow on a white field. Above her, the dry-season sky, hard and blue and pitiless, already beginning to starve the tributaries that fed this river. Six good tides, maybe. Fewer, if the wind turned.

She thought about turning back. She thought about it honestly, the way she tried to do everything — weighed it, priced it, imagined the landfall, the anonymous life, the miles without passengers. It would be easy. It would be safe. It would be exactly what Elias Vane had expected of everyone he'd ever met, including, probably, himself.

"No," Jude said.

Ada exhaled. Marisol, who missed nothing, smiled faintly at the deck and went below to make the soup into dinner.

"But hear me," Jude said, and her voice came out lower than she intended, roughened by cold water and cold arithmetic. "From here on, nobody's a passenger. Not you, not any of us. We watch watches. We run dark at night, no lights, no lamps, no stove smoke after dusk. We speak to no one at any landing. And when I say dive, hide, or run — you don't debate, you *move.* Can you live like that?"

Ada looked at the gunboat a long moment. Then at Jude.

"My father chose the river over me," she said. "I'm starting to understand why."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I've got." She lifted her chin. "Yes, Captain. I can live like that."

Jude rang the engine telegraph to slow ahead, and the old boat answered with a shudder and a cough, and the unnamed river carried them south into the falling light — and a mile astern, unhurried as fate, the crow spread its painted wings and followed.

That night, anchored in the willows with lights doused and the crew whispering over cold food, Jude lay in her bunk and listened to the boat tick around her, and did her own arithmetic in the dark. One waypoint found. One pocket of deep water unfound. One gunboat between them and the sea.

And six tides left — though the river, she suspected, was already counting fewer.
