## 8. Dry Season Arithmetic

Tam Okafor had never trusted a number he hadn't checked twice, and never loved a number he hadn't checked three times. Numbers were the only language that never lied to him — not like people, not like rivers, which lied constantly and beautifully and with total commitment. A number simply sat there on the page and let you interrogate it until it confessed.

So at four in the morning, with the *Kestrel* tied up to a rotting pilaster of an abandoned customs dock and the whole river holding its breath before the dry season, Tam sat in the wheelhouse with a grease pencil, an almanac older than he was, and a sheet of butcher paper, and he did what he always did when the world got complicated.

He counted.

The tide tables were the easy part. The river here was tidal for another forty miles upstream — a slow, brackish pulse that pushed and pulled beneath the current like a heartbeat under a blanket. High water came twice a day, and each high water was a door. Through the door, the channel over Sable Bar ran four feet deep, maybe five on a spring tide. Below it, at low water, the bar went dry enough to walk across in places, the riverbed exposed like the ribcage of something enormous and long-dead.

The hard part was the other number. The one the almanac didn't print.

Tam had been reading gauges all his working life — boiler gauges, fuel gauges, the brass depth gauge bolted to the *Kestrel*'s hull that everyone else ignored and Tam checked the way other men checked their pulse. And the gauges were telling him a story he didn't like. The river had dropped nine inches in four days. Nine inches in four days meant a foot and a half by the end of the week, meant two feet inside a fortnight, meant the channel at Sable Bar — the only deep passage through the reef country ahead — would close like a fist.

He did the arithmetic again, because he didn't trust it the first time, and didn't love it the second.

Six usable tides. Maybe seven, if the wind came out of the southeast and stacked water against the coast, but you didn't plan a voyage on the kindness of wind. Six tides before the channel closed until the wet season returned, and the wet season was four months away — four months of sitting in some backwater slough with a dying engine and a hold full of questions, while Corvus Hale's fleet picked the delta clean like gulls on a drowned thing.

Six tides. Tam wrote the number large on the butcher paper, circled it twice, and stared at it until the grease pencil left a black smudge on the side of his hand.

Behind him, the wheelhouse door creaked.

He didn't have to turn around. He knew the footsteps. Marisol walked like the deck owed her money. Jude walked like the deck was an old argument they'd agreed to stop having. And this — light, quick, half-hesitant, the sound of someone still learning where the boards groaned — this was the girl. Ada Vane. Four days aboard and her footsteps still sounded like an apology.

"You're up early," Tam said, not turning.

"I never went down." She came to stand beside him at the window. The river was a sheet of hammered pewter, the first grey light just beginning to bleed into it from the east. "I kept thinking about the map. The way the channels were drawn — the old man drew them from memory, you know. No survey instruments. Just memory."

"Mm."

"Except he didn't. Draw them from memory, I mean. Look at the notations." She set a folded page on the chart table — she'd copied it, Tam realized, copied the map's marginalia in her own small, slanted hand, because Jude kept the original locked in the cabin and Ada had apparently decided that what Jude locked away, she would simply reproduce from memory. "Depth soundings to the inch. Compass bearings corrected for annual drift. Nobody does that from memory. He had instruments. He had *training*."

Tam looked at her. In the half-dark her face was all sharp angles and sleepless eyes, nineteen years old and furious at the world in a way he recognized from the inside, having once been furious at it himself.

"You've been up all night reading tide tables," he said.

"Someone has to." She said it without apology. "Jude's been sleeping three hours a night. Marisol's been feeding everyone like grief can be buttered. And you—" she nodded at the butcher paper, at the circled number, "—you've been doing arithmetic. So I read. What's the number?"

"Six."

"Six what?"

"Usable tides through Sable Bar. Then the channel closes until the rains." He tapped the almanac. "Spring high water's in three days. That's our best door. After that, each tide's shallower than the last."

Ada was quiet for a moment. Then she pulled the grease pencil out of Tam's hand — took it right out of his fingers, casual as breathing, and Tam let her, which surprised him — and bent over the chart.

"Sable Bar's not the problem," she said. "Sable Bar's just the gate. The problem is the reach between the second and third waypoints. Look." She drew a line with her thumb along the river's course. "The old man's notes say the channel hugs the north bank there. North bank, dry season, falling water — that's where the silt goes. The river dumps its sediment on the inside of every bend when it's low. His soundings are a year old. A year of dry-season silt on the inside of a bend, that's—" she paused, lips moving, doing sums in her head faster than Tam could do them on paper, "—eighteen inches, maybe two feet. Your six tides just became four."

Tam stared at the chart. Then at her. Then at the chart again, because he did not trust a number he hadn't checked twice, and this girl had just checked his work in her head, overnight, without instruments.

"Where did you learn that?" he asked.

"Where does anyone learn anything worth knowing?" She shrugged. "I read. The mission school had a library with three books and a tide almanac, and I read all of them. Twice." A ghost of a smile, there and gone. "The headmaster said a girl didn't need navigation. So I learned it just to spite him."

Tam said nothing. But something in his chest shifted a quarter-inch, the way a bolt shifts when the metal around it finally warms.

* * *

By full morning, the whole crew had gathered around the chart table, and Tam laid out his arithmetic the way he laid out everything — flatly, completely, without decoration.

"Six tides," he said. "Call it four, if Ada's right about the silt on the north-bank reach. Spring high water in three days is our widest door. We run the bar on that tide or we don't run it at all. Fuel's the second problem. We've got enough for sixty miles at cruising, forty if we push the engine, and the third waypoint is seventy-two miles upchannel. We need to find fuel between here and there, and every fuel dock on this river is a place where somebody might be selling our names to Hale's people." He looked at Jude. "So. Captain. What's the call?"

Jude stood at the wheel, one hand resting on the spokes the way a rider rests a hand on a horse's neck. She'd been quiet through the whole briefing, watching Ada with an expression Tam couldn't file anywhere.

"The call is that we run the bar on the spring tide," Jude said. "And we don't stop at any fuel dock between here and the second waypoint. We'll stretch what we have."

"Forty miles of fuel for seventy-two miles of river," Tam said. "That's not stretching. That's praying."

"Then we'll pray efficiently." Jude's mouth twitched — the closest thing to a joke she'd made in four days, and even that had a wire of tension in it. "Tam, you know this engine better than its maker did. I'm asking you to make forty miles behave like seventy."

"I'll make it behave like seventy," Tam said. "It'll hate every mile."

"Good. Hate it with me." Jude turned to Ada. "And you. The north-bank reach. Walk me through it."

And Tam watched, leaning against the engine-room hatch with his arms crossed, as the girl who'd been aboard four days — the girl who'd stowed her fury like contraband and her grief like ballast — stood at the chart and *taught the river* to a captain with thirty years on the water.

"His soundings are stale by a year," Ada said, tapping the map. "So we don't trust the numbers. We trust the *signs*. Falling water, you read the surface. Silt on the inside of a bend makes the current go glassy and slow — the fast water pushes to the outside where the channel's scoured deep. So we run the outside of every bend, even when the map says inside. Even when it looks wrong." She traced the reach. "Here, and here, and here. Three places where the map and the river are going to disagree. And when they disagree—" she looked up at Jude, and there was no apology in her voice at all now, "—the river wins. Every time. The map's just an old letter from someone who used to know her."

The wheelhouse went quiet. Marisol, who had come up with a pot of coffee and stayed for the lecture, set the pot down very slowly, the way you set down something you don't want to break.

Jude looked at Ada for a long moment. "Where did you learn to read water?"

"I told you. Books."

"Books don't teach you to read current." Jude's voice was careful. "That's boat-time. That's years."

Ada's jaw tightened. "My father left me on the mission school steps when I was six," she said. "The school sat on the river. I sat on the river too, every day for thirteen years, because there was nothing else to sit on. You learn things when you're bored and angry and the water's right there." She shrugged, and the shrug didn't quite hide the wound under it. "I didn't have a boat. So I had the water."

Tam saw it land — saw Jude take that sentence somewhere private and turn it over, saw something move behind her eyes like a fish under ice. The captain said only: "Outside of every bend. Even when it looks wrong."

"Even when it looks wrong."

"All right." Jude turned back to the wheel. "Tam, get below. I want every ounce that engine has, and I want it without a breakdown. Marisol, we travel light and eat faster. We run the bar on the spring tide." A pause. "Two days."

* * *

Tam spent the two days below decks, and he was glad of it, because below decks was the only place where the world still made sense. Above decks there were tides that closed like doors and salvage fleets that bought names and a dead man's map and a dead man's daughter with a dead man's eyes — but below decks there were only pressures and tolerances, valves and clearances, a language of metal that never pretended to be anything but what it was.

He stripped the fuel system and cleaned it with the patience of a monk. He re-timed the injectors to squeeze a half-mile more from every gallon. He wrapped the cooling lines where they'd chafe, shimmed the mounts where they'd rattle, and talked to the engine the whole time, low and constant, the way you'd talk to a horse you were about to ask for something cruel.

"Seventy miles out of forty," he told it. "I know. I know. But the girl did the sums, and the sums say we've got no choice, and I've learned to trust a girl who does sums at four in the morning."

The engine, being an honest machine, did not answer. But it idled a little smoother after that, and Tam chose to believe it was agreement.

He came up on the evening of the second day, hands black with grease, and found the wheelhouse lit by a single lamp, and found Ada Vane standing at the chart table with Jude, and found the two of them bent over the same chart like a pair of surgeons over the same patient — and found, to his quiet astonishment, that Jude was *listening*. Not the polite listening of a captain tolerating a passenger. The real kind. The kind where your assumptions get corrected and you let them.

"Say that again," Jude was saying.

"The reef country above Sable Bar — Vane's marks are good, they're *very* good, but they're all referenced to a channel buoy that isn't there anymore." Ada's finger moved across the chart. "The buoy's gone, but the rock it marked isn't. So I re-referenced everything to the bluff on the north bank — you can see that bluff from everywhere in the reach, it's the only fixed thing. Every one of his waypoints, shifted to the bluff. It's all here." She tapped a page covered in her small slanted hand — columns of corrected bearings, worked and reworked, the margins dense with her arithmetic. "I checked every calculation twice."

"Twice," Tam said from the doorway.

"Three times," Ada said, without turning around. "I'm told that's the standard."

Tam looked at Jude. Jude looked at Tam. And something passed between them that had no words in it — the look of two people who had spent years being the only careful ones on the river, suddenly discovering they were three.

"Where'd you learn to double-check?" Tam asked.

"Where'd you learn to strip an injector in the dark?" she shot back.

"Fair," Tam said.

* * *

The spring tide came in the small hours of the third day, and it came the way the almanac promised: fat and high and slow, the river swelling against its banks like a chest filling with breath. Tam stood at the engine throttle with the hatch cracked so he could hear the wheelhouse, and at 3:40 in the morning Jude's voice came down the tube, calm and flat as a sounding line.

"Stand by, Tam."

"Standing by."

The *Kestrel* crept off the customs dock into the dark, her running lights burning low, and the river took her the way it took everything — without comment. Tam watched his gauges. The depth gauge under the hull read nine feet, then eight and a half, then eight, as they ran down the black reach toward Sable Bar, and Tam did the arithmetic in his head without wanting to, because that was the curse of his life: the numbers never stopped.

Eight feet of water. Six and a half feet of boat, keel to wheelhouse rail. Two feet of margin, on a bar that was dropping nine inches a day.

Above him, through the deck, he could hear the wheelhouse conversation in fragments. Jude's voice. Marisol's, low, at the rail, calling soundings by lamplight off the bow. And Ada's — clear, certain, coming faster now as the bar rose up under them.

"Glassy water to starboard — that's the silt shelf, don't trust it. Fast water port side, off the point. Come left. *Come left.*"

A pause. The engine note changed as Jude answered the wheel.

"Steady. There's a deeper cut along the outside, just past the dead tree on the bar — I can see the current seam. Follow the seam."

Another pause, longer. Then Jude's voice, and Tam would have sworn on his mother's grave that there was something in it he hadn't heard in years — something that in a less careful man he would have called wonder.

"I see it."

The depth gauge read seven feet. Six. Five and a half. The *Kestrel* trembled — Tam felt it come up through the deck plates, through his boots, into his teeth — the hull kissing silt, the river reaching up to touch its belly, and then the seam of deep water took them and the gauge climbed: six, seven, eight, and the bar was under them, behind them, and they were through.

Tam let out a breath he'd been holding for three days.

Above, he heard Marisol laugh — a short, disbelieving bark of a laugh. And then Jude's voice again, and this time Tam heard it clearly, and he never forgot it:

"Where the hell did you learn to see a current seam at night?"

And Ada's answer, quiet and fierce as a struck match:

"I told you. I sat on the water for thirteen years. You think I was just *sitting*?"

* * *

They made the north-bank reach by midmorning, and Ada's arithmetic proved out to the inch. The map said hug the north bank; the river said otherwise; they ran the outside of the bend, through fast brown water, and passed over the silt shelf close enough to read it in the glassy swirl off the starboard rail. Twice more that day Ada called the river against the map, and twice more the river did exactly what she said it would.

By evening, Tam noticed, nobody said "the girl" anymore. Marisol called her *la piloto*. Jude simply called her name, the way you call a member of a crew — short, unadorned, assumed.

And Tam, who trusted nothing he hadn't checked three times, found that he had started checking his own arithmetic against hers — and that when the two agreed, he believed the number all the way down.

That was the thing about competence, he thought, watching her at the chart in the last light, sleeves rolled, grease pencil behind her ear like a carpenter's nail. It wasn't a gift you gave someone. It was a door they opened themselves. All you could do was notice it was open, and walk through, and stand beside them in the room.

He was still thinking about that — about rooms and doors and the strange new shape of his crew — when Ada went very still over the chart table.

"Tam," she said. Her voice had changed. All the certainty had gone out of it, and what was left was quiet and cold. "Come look at this."

He came. She was pointing at the margin of the reach chart, at a notation in Vane's cramped hand that they'd all read a dozen times and never once understood — a string of figures, a bearing, and a single word.

The word was *Lockridge*.

"Depth soundings," Ada said slowly. "A bearing off the north bluff. And a name." She looked up at Tam, and her eyes were the eyes of a navigator whose instruments had just told her something impossible. "This isn't a waypoint. It's a *dedication*. He wrote someone's name into the map. Someone who knew these waters." Her voice dropped. "Tam — who is Lockridge?"

And Tam, who had spent four days watching Jude Lockridge flinch, very slightly, every time the dead man's past came up — who had watched her change the subject, and check the locks, and sleep three hours a night with her back against the cabin door — Tam looked at the name on the chart, and then down the deck to where the captain stood alone at the wheel in the falling dark, and said nothing at all.

But he had been an engineer long enough to know what it meant when a machine ran smooth right up until the moment it didn't.

The *Kestrel* ran smooth.

The channel ahead was black, and the tide was falling, and somewhere behind them, low and patient, a gunboat's searchlight swept the water like a slow eye opening.
