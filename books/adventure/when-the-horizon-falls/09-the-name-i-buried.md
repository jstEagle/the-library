## 9. The Name I Buried

The fire had burned down to a red heart by the time Tam banked it for the night, and the boat settled into its creaking rhythm — hull against current, rope against cleat, the small music of a vessel at rest. Marisol had gone below with a lamp and a book she'd read four times. Ada sat on the deck crate near the wheelhouse, knees drawn up, turning the hidden documents over in her hands like they might bite.

Jude stood at the rail and watched the river go black.

She could feel the girl's eyes on her back. Ada had been watching her all evening, in that way she had — not shy, not subtle, but measuring, the way you measured a stranger's boat before deciding whether to trust it with your life. And Jude knew what question was building behind those eyes, because it was the same question everyone eventually asked, the one she'd spent eleven years making sure no one would ever have reason to ask.

*Who are you, really?*

Brannock had said *Lockridge* like he was laying a card face-up on a table. He'd watched her when he said it. Watched for the flinch.

There hadn't been a flinch. There never was. But there had been something else, something old and cold uncoiling in her chest, and Ada — sharp as a fishhook, that girl — had seen it anyway.

"You knew," Ada said now, quietly. "When Brannock said the name. You knew it meant something."

The river slid past, carrying someone else's lamplight downstream. Jude didn't answer right away. She'd learned long ago that silence was a kind of currency, and she'd hoarded it the way misers hoarded gold. But hoarding was just another word for drowning, if you thought about it too long.

"Go get the coffee," she said. "It's still warm."

Ada went. When she came back, she poured two cups without asking whether Jude wanted one, which was answer enough about who she was becoming out here. She handed one over and sat down on the deck, back against the rail, and waited. Nineteen years old and patient as a heron. Elias Vane's blood showing through after all.

Jude looked into her cup. The coffee was dark as the water below.

"My father," she said, "owned nine steamboats and half the warehouses on the upper delta. When I was born, there were bells rung in three towns." She laughed, short and airless. "They rang bells, Ada. For me."

* * *

She had been Marguerite Lockridge then. That was the name on the christening record, the name stitched into the collar of every dress she owned until she was seventeen: *Marguerite*, after her grandmother, a woman she remembered only as a smell of lavender and disapproval. Her father, Aldous Lockridge, was a big man made bigger by money — broad-shouldered, silver at the temples by forty, a laugh that filled ballrooms and boardrooms alike. He lifted her onto his shoulders at the dock openings so she could see over the crowd. He called her his *flagship*. He told her, more times than she could count, that one day all of it would be hers: the boats, the warehouses, the name painted in gold leaf on nine hulls.

*Lockridge & Sons*, though there were no sons. Just her. Her father had amended the letterhead himself, in ink, and framed the first page of the new printing, and hung it in the front hall where she passed it every morning of her childhood. *Lockridge & Daughter.* She had loved that frame more than anything else in the house. She had loved it the way you love a promise.

What she hadn't understood — couldn't have understood, at eight, at ten, at fourteen — was what the name was standing on.

The summer she turned sixteen, a young man came to dinner. Corvus Hale. Thirty years old, hungry-eyed, charming the way weather is charming right before it turns. He'd built something small and fast out of nothing — two tugs and a contract — and he wanted what the Lockridges had: the delta routes, the warehouse leases, the goodwill of every merchant from the headwaters to the sea. Her father received him like an equal, which should have been the first warning. Aldous Lockridge did not receive many people like equals.

Jude served the coffee that night, because that was what daughters of shipping lines did, and she remembered Hale looking at her — not the way men usually looked, appraising, but the way a cartographer looks at coastline. Measuring. Deciding where the deep water ran.

"Your father tells me you can read a tide table better than most of his captains," Hale said.

"She reads everything," her father said, proud and easy. "My flagship."

"Then she'll understand this someday," Hale said, smiling at her across the rim of his cup, "when the arithmetic comes due."

She hadn't understood. Not then.

The arithmetic came due the winter she was seventeen, and it came due in ledgers.

She found them by accident — or by instinct; she'd never decided which. Her father kept a private office above the counting-house, locked, and she'd been raised with a key ring and a sailor's habit of opening things. She'd gone in looking for the winter freight schedules, because her father had promised her the northern run for her birthday, her first command of anything larger than a skiff, and she wanted to study the manifests ahead of time.

The schedules were in the second drawer. Everything else was in the third.

Bills of sale with dates that didn't match their own ink. Deeds to delta channels signed by men who, she knew from the dock gossip she'd absorbed like river water, had died or gone broke years before the signatures were dated. Transfer papers routing Lockridge cargo through shell companies whose names she'd never seen on any wharf. And threaded through all of it, again and again, the same counter-signature, elegant and cold as a blade:

*C. Hale.*

She sat on the floor of her father's office until the lamps burned low, and she did the thing she was best at, the thing that would later keep her alive in channels no pilot had survived: she read everything, twice, and made the numbers tell her the truth.

The truth was simple. The Lockridge line — the boats, the warehouses, the gold-leafed name, the bells — rested on routes that had never honestly belonged to them. Her father hadn't built the empire. He'd helped steal it, a signature at a time, in partnership with a man young enough and ruthless enough to be doing it on purpose while Aldous did it for the comfort of staying rich. And the paper trail — the proof, the whole rotten foundation — wasn't destroyed. It was *kept*. Filed. Preserved in that locked office like a relic, because her father was too much a creature of paperwork to burn the only copies of his own sin.

She confronted him. That part she remembered in fragments — the way you remember a storm you survived rather than witnessed. She remembered spreading the ledgers across his desk. She remembered his face going through surprise, then something worse than anger: *pity*, as if she were a child crying over a dead bird. She remembered him saying, in the reasonable voice he used on creditors, that this was how rivers worked, how trade had always worked, that the strong took the channels and wrote the laws afterward and the world called it progress. That she would understand when the line was hers. That a name like theirs was worth more than the manner of its keeping.

"And if it isn't?" she'd asked.

He'd smiled at her, fond and terrible. "It is. You'll see."

She hadn't seen. She'd done something else instead.

* * *

The night she left, it rained — a hard spring rain, the kind that made the gutters run brown and the river rise fat and impatient against its banks. She was eighteen by then. She'd spent a year pretending, eating at her father's table, standing beside him at the dock openings, letting him introduce her as his flagship while the ledgers sat in her memory like swallowed glass. A year of learning the other lesson, the one nobody teaches heirs: that shame, carried long enough, stops feeling like guilt and starts feeling like armor. You wear it so long you forget it isn't yours to keep.

But armor is heavy. And she was tired.

She went to the office one last time. She didn't take the ledgers — she'd thought about it, God knew she'd thought about it, taking the proof to a magistrate, to a newspaper, to anyone. But she was eighteen and she knew exactly what would happen: her father's lawyers would bury the story, Hale's money would bury the lawyers, and the name on nine hulls would come out of it polished. Power like that didn't lose to a girl with a satchel of paper. It lost to time, or it didn't lose at all.

So she did the smaller, stupider, more honest thing. She took the documents her father treasured most — the founding charters, the amended letterhead, the framed *Lockridge & Daughter* page off the front hall wall — and she carried them down through the rain to the coal furnace behind the counting-house, and she fed them in one page at a time.

The charter burned green at the edges first. The letterhead curled like a living thing. The frame she smashed against the brick and threw the pieces in after, glass and gilt and all, and watched her own name — *Marguerite*, *daughter*, *heir* — go up in a breath of orange light.

Then she walked to the river.

Not to a boat. Not yet. Just to the water, to the mud stairs behind the fish market where the working people launched their skiffs, and she stood there in the rain with her good shoes ruined and her hair plastered flat, and she let the river look at her. It didn't care about her name. That was the whole of it, the revelation that reorganized her entire life: the river did not care. It would take her or drown her on identical terms, and either way it would never once ask whether she was a Lockridge.

She hired on as a deckhand under a name she invented on the spot — *Jude*, because it was plain, because it fit in a mouth like a work glove. She learned engines from a one-armed mechanic named Ossie who never asked questions because he had his own. She learned channels the way you learn a language, by immersion, by nearly dying in a few of them. She saved for six years, bought a rotten-hulled boat everyone said was a coffin, and rebuilt it plank by plank with her own hands until it became the *Vespera*, and the *Vespera* became the only true thing she owned, because she had built it out of nothing but labor and low water and luck.

Her father died when she was twenty-six. She read it in a shipping gazette, three weeks late, over eggs in a dockside café. Nine steamboats sold at auction. The obituary called him *a titan of the delta trade*. She finished her eggs, paid her bill, and walked back down to the water, and she did not cry, and she has wondered ever since whether that was strength or just scar tissue doing its work.

* * *

"I never went back," Jude said. The coffee had gone cold in her hands. She drank it anyway. "Eleven years. I built a whole life out of not going back."

Ada hadn't moved. The lamp in the wheelhouse threw a bar of amber light across her face, catching the stubborn set of her jaw — Vane's jaw, Jude realized, the same bones, the same refusal to look away.

"So Brannock wasn't lying," Ada said. "You're one of *those* Lockridges."

"I'm the one who burned it," Jude said. "Which apparently counts for less than I hoped."

"And the documents." Ada lifted the oilcloth packet slightly, the hidden papers they'd pulled from the map's backing, the ones with names and dates and the word *Meridian* running through them like a vein of ore. "Tam thinks they prove Hale forged his claim to the delta routes. Marisol thinks they're worth a fortune. Nobody's asked what they prove about anyone else." Her eyes were steady and merciless. "Do your family's signatures appear?"

Jude looked at the river. The river offered no help. It never had, really — that had always been the beautiful lie at the center of her escape. The river didn't care about names, but names followed you down it anyway, swimming alongside like sharks, patient, waiting for the water to shallow.

"Aldous Lockridge," she said. "Counter-signed on eleven of the transfer deeds. My father's hand is all through them. Which means the proof that breaks Hale also—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "Also hangs my family next to him on the same gallows. Every document that ruins him vindicates no one. It just spreads the rot around evenly. That's what fraud looks like from underneath, Ada. It doesn't have villains and victims. It has partners and people who looked away, and my father was both, and some part of me—" Her jaw tightened. "Some part of me signed those papers too. Not in ink. In silence. A year of sitting at that table knowing, and saying nothing, because the bells were nice and the name was mine."

The words hung in the cold air between them. Saying them aloud felt like opening a sealed hold that had been shut for eleven years — and finding, instead of treasure or ruin, just the ordinary smell of old wood and damp. Smaller than she'd feared. Still hers.

Ada was quiet for a long moment. Then, in the flat practical tone that was becoming her trademark aboard the *Vespera*: "So if we sell the box to Hale, we sell your father's crimes along with his. And if we give it to a commission, your family goes down in the record books next to his."

"That's the arithmetic."

"Marisol will want to burn it."

"Marisol always wants to burn things. It's why I keep her."

"And you?" Ada set the packet down between them, deliberate as a judge laying a case on the bench. "Captain. What do *you* want? Because I've watched you since the mission school, and you lie beautifully and you steer beautifully and you don't tell anybody anything. But tomorrow we start running waypoints, and Brannock's gunboat is somewhere behind us, and I need to know what my captain does when the choice gets expensive. I've had one adult already sell me out for quiet. I don't have room for another."

The question landed harder than Brannock's had. Brannock had offered money; Ada was offering something Jude had refused for eleven years — the chance to be known and stay aboard anyway.

Jude crouched, so they were eye to eye, and picked up the packet. Heavy, for paper. Light, for everything it carried.

"I want to finish what Vane started," she said. "I promised a dying man I'd deliver his findings to his daughter, and I'm looking at her, and I intend to keep that promise even if the findings are ugly. Especially then. Ugly truth delivered is worth more than pretty lies kept." She turned the packet over in her hands. "And I want — God help me — I want to see Hale's face when the arithmetic comes due for *him*. I've waited eleven years for somebody's arithmetic to come due that wasn't mine."

Ada almost smiled. Almost. "And if it costs you the name you buried?"

"The name's already buried, Ada. I dug the hole myself." Jude stood, joints complaining, and nodded toward the dark channel ahead, where the first waypoint reef waited somewhere beyond the bend, marked on a dead man's wax-sealed map. "What I haven't decided is what we do with what's in the box. That's not my call alone anymore. I learned that tonight."

From below decks came the clank of Tam stoking the boiler for the pre-dawn run, and the engine coughed, caught, settled into its heartbeat. The tide would turn within the hour. Jude could feel it in the mooring lines, the slow lean of the river changing its mind.

Ada gathered the packet and hugged it to her chest like a child, or a weapon.

"One more thing," she said, pausing at the wheelhouse door. "Marguerite. Is that really your name?"

"It was."

"And now?"

Jude put her hand on the wheel, worn smooth by ten thousand miles of her own palms, and felt the *Vespera* answer the turning tide beneath her.

"Now I'm whoever the river says," she said. "Come dawn, we find out what it says about reefs."
