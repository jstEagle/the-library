## 10. Smoke on the Water

Ada Vane had learned to read men the way other girls learned to read novels — by the shape of their silences, the grammar of their hands. So when the gunboat came up the channel at midmorning, gray and patient as a shark, she was already reading Brannock before he ever set foot on the deck of the *Kestrel's Wake*.

He stood at the rail of his own vessel with his hands folded behind him, a broad man gone soft at the edges but not in the eyes, and he did not shout across the water the way rivermen did. He simply waited. That was the first thing she noted: patience, in a man like that, was not a virtue. It was a tool.

"They're coming alongside," Tam said from the engine housing, wiping his hands on a rag that had long since surrendered to grease. His voice was carefully flat, the way it went whenever he was frightened and didn't want the fear to spread.

"I can see that," Jude said.

She stood at the wheel with one hand resting on the brass rim, and Ada watched her — watched the way her jaw set, the way her thumb moved once along the wheel's edge, a small unconscious motion, like a woman touching a talisman. Jude Lockridge did not look afraid. She looked *arranged*, the way a room looks after someone has tidied it for company.

"Inspection," Marisol muttered from the galley doorway, arms crossed, flour still dusting her forearms. "In the middle of nowhere. On a channel so shallow even the herons have to walk."

"Customs boats have been thinner on excuses," Tam said.

The gunboat swung wide, its wake slapping against the *Wake*'s hull in slow, insolent shoves, and then the fenders were out and lines were thrown and Brannock stepped aboard as though he owned the planks beneath his boots. Two of his men stayed at the rail. One of them carried a rifle slung loose across his back — loose enough to be casual, tight enough to be reached.

"Captain Lockridge." Brannock's voice was warm gravel. He pronounced the name correctly, every syllable, and Ada saw something flicker behind Jude's eyes at the sound of it — there and gone, quick as a fish turning under the surface. "Corvus Hale sends his regards to all vessels traversing the upper delta this season. There have been... incidents. Salvage disputes. Piracy, some say." He smiled without warmth. "A routine inspection. Papers, cargo, safety equipment. You understand how it is."

"I understand exactly how it is," Jude said.

* * *

Ada had hidden things before. She had hidden a stolen orange in her bodice at the mission school and eaten it in the privy, heart hammering, certain God could see straight through wool and skin alike. She had hidden letters from her father — the few, brief, guilt-stained notes that arrived some years and not others — inside the lining of her mattress until Sister Agnes found them and burned them in front of the whole dormitory, saying that lies kept in the dark grew mold.

So when Brannock's men began their slow, insulting circuit of the boat — lifting tarpaulins, poking through the fuel drums, flipping open the hatch to the hold where the bilge water breathed its cold iron smell — Ada moved without deciding to move.

The map was in the chart drawer beneath the wheel, wrapped in oilcloth, sealed in wax that had cracked with age and handling. Elias Vane's wax. Her father's wax. She had touched that seal a hundred times since they'd pulled it from his dying hands, tracing the impression of the stamp — a star, six-pointed, pressed crooked into the red — wondering what kind of man chose a star for a life spent digging in mud.

The stovepipe rose from the galley stove through the deck above, a black iron throat two hands wide, and where it passed through the ceiling of the galley there was a collar plate held by four screws. Tam had loosened those screws a month ago chasing an exhaust leak, and Ada had watched him do it, because she watched everything now, because watching was the only inheritance her father had actually left her.

She took the screws out with the tip of Marisol's paring knife while the galley filled with the smell of the stew Marisol had abandoned halfway through making. The map, rolled tight, slid into the gap between pipe and collar with half an inch to spare. She seated the plate back over it, screwed it down finger-tight, and stood back just as boots sounded on the deck above.

Her hands were steady. That surprised her. Inside, she was the privy-orange girl again, nine years old, certain of discovery, certain of fire.

Brannock came down the galley ladder without hurrying. He had to duck; the ceiling made him stoop, and Ada took a small fierce pleasure in seeing a man like that made smaller by anything, even architecture.

"Cook's off duty?" he asked, nodding at the cold stove.

"She's topside," Ada said. "I'm minding the pot."

"You're the new hand. Picked up at the mission landing." He said it as fact, not question, and Ada felt the floor tilt a degree beneath her. He knew about the mission. He knew about her. "Young for river work."

"I'm old enough to know when a man's fishing," she said.

His smile widened a fraction. "Fishing?"

"For something to bite. You've turned over every crate on this boat twice and you keep looking at the captain instead of the cargo. Whatever you're inspecting for, it isn't safety equipment."

For a moment the warmth drained out of his face entirely, and what remained underneath was the actual man — patient, thorough, and utterly without mercy, a dredge in a suit. Then the warmth came back, painted on, and he tipped an imaginary hat.

"Your father would be proud of your eye," he said softly, and climbed the ladder, and left Ada standing in the galley with the stew pot ticking as it cooled and the word *father* ringing in her skull like a struck bell.

He knew. Not everything — not the map, or he'd never have left — but enough. Enough to make the name a hook and wait to see what it caught.

* * *

On deck, the inspection ended the way theater ends, with everyone pretending it had meant something. Brannock shook Jude's hand — Ada watched her take it, watched her grip like a man's, watched Brannock hold on one beat too long — and said, "Safe travels, Captain. These channels get treacherous past the second waypoint. Shifting bottom. A man could lose a whole operation down there." And then he was over the side, and the gunboat was backing water, and the gray hull slid away downstream with the unhurried confidence of something that knew exactly where it was going.

They watched it go. Nobody spoke until it rounded the bluff at the channel bend and vanished.

Then Tam exhaled like a man surfacing. "Stovepipe," he said, looking at Ada. "You put it in the stovepipe."

"It was the only place he wouldn't look. Men like that don't look at kitchens. They think kitchens are for women and nothing else."

Marisol laughed, short and sharp, and clapped Ada on the shoulder hard enough to stagger her. "The girl hides papers in my stove and insults the man with the rifle. I like her."

But Jude wasn't laughing. Jude was standing at the wheel with her back to all of them, looking downriver, toward the second waypoint, and Ada realized with a cold settling feeling that the divers were probably already in the water there — that the inspection had been a screen, a magician's flourish, while Hale's men probed the reef with grappling hooks and air hoses. Brannock hadn't come for the map. He'd come to see if they'd panic. To count their faces. To learn who they were.

"He knows my name," Ada said. "He said — he said my father would be proud of my eye. How does he know who I am? How does he know I'm aboard?"

Jude turned. Her face was doing the thing it did — closed, arranged, the room tidied for company.

"Because Hale has been looking for Vane's daughter almost as long as he's been looking for Vane's map," she said. "Vane was his partner once. Everyone in the delta trade knows the story, or a version of it. Brannock put it together the moment he saw you. It wasn't hard."

"And you didn't tell me that." Not a question. An accusation laid down gently, like a knife on a table.

"There wasn't time."

"There's always time. You just decide what people can bear to know." Ada heard her own voice rise and couldn't stop it, twenty years of mission-school silence coming up all at once. "You decide what Tam gets told, and what Marisol gets told, and what I get told, which is nothing, which is whatever's left over after you've decided I'm too young or too new or too much my father's daughter to handle it—"

"That's not what this is."

"Then what is it?" Ada stepped closer. The deck rocked under them both. "Because here's what I saw today, Captain. I saw you shake that man's hand like you were glad to. I saw you lie to him with your whole face — smooth as glass, no crack anywhere — and I thought, *she's done this before.* I thought, *she's lied her whole life, she lies the way I breathe.* And I thought—" Her voice caught. She hated that it caught. "I thought, when it finally comes to the narrows, when Brannock's guns are close enough to touch, she won't fight. She'll run. She'll point this boat somewhere none of us can follow and she'll run, because that's what she does. It's what she did to her family, and it's what she'll do to us."

The words landed. Ada watched them land, one by one, in Jude's face — and for the first time since the mission landing, since the dock, since the dying man and the promise, she saw the arrangement fail. Saw the room untidy itself. Something raw moved behind Jude's eyes, and her hand came off the wheel and hung at her side, empty.

"You don't know what you're talking about," Jude said quietly.

"Don't I?" Ada laughed, and it came out bitter. "My father ran too. Did you know that? Everyone says he abandoned me, but nobody says *where he went*, and I've had nineteen years to wonder about it. Running is a thing I know the shape of, Captain. I've lived inside the hole it leaves."

"*Enough.*" It wasn't a shout. It was worse than a shout — low, and cracked down the middle. Jude turned away, both hands gripping the wheel now, knuckles pale, staring downriver at the water that had taken everything from her and given her this life and was now, tide by tide, taking it back.

Tam looked at Marisol. Marisol looked at Tam. Neither of them moved. The engine ticked as it cooled. Somewhere upstream a bird cried out, three notes, and got no answer.

"I'm not running," Jude said at last, to the river more than to Ada. "You want the truth? I don't know how to prove it to you. I don't know how to prove anything to anyone anymore. Every time I open my mouth around that man, I lie, because lying is the only weapon I have that he doesn't outrank. But I will tell you this." She turned her head, just enough. "When the last tide comes, I'll be at the wheel. Wherever this boat goes, I go with it. That's the only kind of promise I know how to keep — the kind you steer."

Ada wanted to believe her. She stood there wanting it so badly it felt like hunger, like the ache after the fever broke, and that wanting frightened her more than Brannock had. Because believing someone was how they got inside. Because her father had believed in redemption and died face-down in the mud with a map in his fist and a daughter who'd stopped waiting for him.

"The stew's ruined," Marisol announced into the silence, practical as a plank. "Somebody eats beans tonight and it isn't going to be me."

It broke something loose. Tam snorted. Even Ada felt the corner of her mouth twitch against her will.

Jude let go of the wheel. She crossed the deck to Ada, and for a moment Ada thought she was going to reach out — and she braced for it, the touch she hadn't felt from anyone since Sister Agnes retired — but Jude stopped short, hands at her sides, and just looked at her. Close up, in the flat afternoon light, there were lines around Jude's eyes that Ada had never noticed, deep ones, the kind carved by squinting into weather for years.

"When we make the second waypoint," Jude said, "you'll stand beside me at the wheel. You'll see every decision before I make it. No more leftovers. Can you live with that?"

"No," Ada said. "But I can work with it."

Something passed between them then — not forgiveness, not yet, but the ground forgiveness grows in. Jude nodded once and turned back to the wheel, and Ada went below to rescue what she could of dinner, and to check, with two fingers, that the screws on the collar plate were still finger-tight.

They were. Through the iron she could feel the faint warmth of the pipe, and beyond it, rolled tight as a secret, her father's star pressed crooked into red wax.

That night, anchored in the lee of the bluff, Ada took the first watch alone. The river ran silver-black under a rising moon, and far downstream — so far it might have been a trick of the light, except that Ada had stopped trusting tricks of the light — a lantern blinked twice, paused, and blinked twice again.

Not a fisherman's rhythm. Fishermen were careless with light.

Someone was signaling someone, and the second waypoint lay between them and morning.
