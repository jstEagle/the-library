## 11. Three Tides Left

The tide chart lay open on the chart table like a confession.

Jude had been staring at it for an hour, long enough that the lantern oil burned low and the numbers had begun to swim. Three tides. That was what Tam's arithmetic came down to, and Tam was never wrong about water. Three usable tides before the dry season dropped the channel below the *Vela's* draft, before the sandbars stitched themselves shut until next year, before the river itself closed the door on the Meridian Star for another twelve months.

Three tides, and Brannock's gunboat sitting fat and patient in the lower reach, its running lights burning every night like a promise.

She heard Marisol come up the companionway — the particular soft tread of her, the creak of the third stair that she'd never fixed because it told her when someone was coming. Marisol set a mug of coffee on the chart table without being asked, then stood with her arms folded, looking at the same numbers Jude was looking at.

"You haven't slept," Marisol said.

"I slept."

"Liar. You slept forty minutes and you dreamed loud enough I heard you through the bulkhead." Marisol pulled out the opposite bench and sat, which she almost never did at the chart table; it was Jude's territory, the way the galley was hers. "Talk to me."

Jude turned the mug in her hands. The coffee smelled of chicory and scorched tin, the way it always did, the way that meant home more than any harbor did. Outside, the river moved in the dark with that low, endless whisper, and somewhere downstream the gunboat's lights made a smear against the stars that didn't belong there.

"Brannock knows we're here," Jude said. "He doesn't know where we're going. Not exactly. He has the second waypoint, and he has divers on it right now, and they'll find what Vane left them, which is nothing but a marker and a hole in the silt. But he knows we're ahead of him, and he knows the channel is closing, and he can afford to wait us out. We can't."

"So we run tonight."

"On this tide? He'd have us in the narrows inside two hours. His boat draws less than ours, his engines are twice ours, and he has guns." Jude said it flatly, because saying it any other way would make it worse. "We run when he runs, or not at all."

"Then we're dead in the water."

"Not necessarily." And there it was, the thought she'd been circling all night like a bird over carrion, the thought that had come to her sometime around three in the morning, fully formed and terrible, wearing the face of common sense. She looked at the chart again. At the false channel she'd drawn years ago, back when she was young and mean and liked to watch strangers' boats grind their props to splinters on the oyster bar below Widow's Reach. It was still inked faintly under her corrections, a ghost line, a lie in her own hand.

Marisol saw her looking. Marisol had known her eleven years, and she could read a chart the way she read bread dough — by feel, by what rose and what didn't.

"No," Marisol said.

"You don't even know—"

"I know that look. That's the look of a woman about to do something clever and rotten." Marisol leaned forward. "Jude. Whatever it is. No."

Jude took out a fresh sheet of paper anyway. Her hands were steady, which surprised her; she'd expected them to shake. She wrote coordinates in a careful, slightly cramped hand — not her own handwriting, something older-fashioned, the way Vane might have written, feverish and hurried. She invented a waypoint in the drowned forest above Serpent Bend, where the water was deep enough to sound honest and the snags were thick enough to tear the bottom out of anything that went looking. Then she folded the paper once.

"If Brannock thinks we've found the wreck," she said, "and thinks we're anchored on it waiting for slack water to dive — he'll move. Tonight. He'll pull his divers off our wake and run for those coordinates, and while he's doing it, the narrows are empty for one tide. One. We go through on the ebb and we're gone."

"And if he finds nothing at Serpent Bend?"

"Then he comes back angry, and we're already past the third reef, and by then it won't matter."

Marisol was quiet for a long moment. The lantern hissed. Somewhere aft, Tam's engine ticked as it cooled, a sound like a slow heart.

"And the other part," Marisol said quietly. "Say it out loud. The part you're not saying."

Jude looked at the chart. At the drowned forest. At the honest-sounding deep water full of snags.

"Anyone who follows those coordinates," she said, "hits the bar. Anyone who's watching us, anyone who's bought information, anyone who thinks they're cleverer than Hale's fleet — they see us heading upriver, they work out the general bearing, they go looking near Serpent Bend, and they find the bottom with their hull." She set the folded paper down between them. "It's a trap. Not just a decoy. A trap."

"For Brannock."

"For whoever comes."

Marisol stood up so fast the bench scraped. "That's not survival, that's murder with extra steps. There are other captains on this river, Jude. Half-starved men running cargo on boats older than this one, trying to feed their kids the same way we do. You'd sink one of them to buy six hours."

"I'd sink nobody," Jude said, and heard how cold her own voice sounded, how reasonable. "I'd put a lie on the water. What they do with it is theirs."

"That is exactly what your father would have said."

The words landed like a hook behind the ribs. Jude didn't flinch — she'd had practice at not flinching — but something in her chest went tight and small. Marisol knew it too; she saw it land, and her face softened even as she held her ground.

"I'm sorry," Marisol said. "But you needed to hear it."

"I needed to hear it nine years ago," Jude said. "I walked away from all of it. The name, the money, the whole rotten ledger. I've spent my whole life since being honest in ways that cost me. Every mile I've ever steered has been paid for honestly." She picked up the folded paper. It weighed nothing. It weighed everything. "And now I'm three tides from losing the only chance that girl will ever have to know what her father really was, and the only chance I'll ever have to finish what I promised a dying man. So yes. I'll spend a lie to buy a tide. Call it betrayal if you want. I call it arithmetic."

"It's not arithmetic," Marisol said. "Arithmetic doesn't keep you up at night."

* * *

She went ashore alone, which was the first dishonest thing, because she told Tam she was checking the mooring lines and Tam, bless him, believed her, or pretended to.

The riverport at night was a different animal than by day — quieter, meaner, lit by oil lamps that turned every face the color of old bone. The tavern where Brannock's men drank was the one with the new paint, the one whose owner had suddenly stopped asking questions. Jude had watched it for two days. She knew the rhythm: the enforcer himself never came before ten, and he always sat with his back to the wall, facing the door, facing the street, because men like Brannock trusted no room they couldn't see all of.

She waited across the street in the shadow of the ice house until quarter past ten, and then she walked in.

The noise dipped when she entered — she was known here, the woman off the old riverboat, and known faces were interesting faces. She crossed to the bar, ordered nothing, and let Brannock come to her, which took four minutes and cost her more composure than she'd expected. Up close he smelled of cigar smoke and machine oil, and his eyes were patient the way a fisherman's are patient.

"Captain Lockridge." He used the name deliberately, letting it sit in the air between them, watching for the flinch. She gave him none. "Or should I say—"

"Lockridge is fine," she said. "I have something for you."

His eyebrows rose, genuinely, briefly. Men like Brannock were rarely surprised, and she banked the surprise like fuel.

"I'm listening."

"A location." She set the folded paper on the bar between them, under her palm. "The Meridian Star. Where she lies. Where she's lain for thirty years."

"And why would you sell it to me instead of just diving it yourself?"

"Because I've seen your divers work," Jude said, and let a little weariness into her voice, a little of the broke-and-beaten captain she was pretending to be. "Because I'm one tide from a closed channel and a dead season, and because Corvus Hale pays better than a dead prospector's ghost does. I want five hundred now and five hundred when you raise whatever's in her. And I want you to forget my face and my boat after that."

Brannock picked up the paper without touching her hand. He read it slowly. She watched him not react, which was its own kind of performance, and she understood suddenly, viscerally, why her father had been good at this — why the lying had come easy to the Lockridges, generation after generation, like a family trade passed down with the silverware. It felt awful. It felt easy. Those two things together frightened her more than Brannock's gunboat ever had.

"Serpent Bend," Brannock said.

"The drowned forest. Deep water under the snags — that's why nobody found her. That's why Vane's map was worth killing for." She met his eyes and lied straight through them. "Slack water tomorrow morning. If I were you, I'd be there before dawn."

"And your boat? Where will your boat be?"

"Gone. South, on the ebb, while you're busy. That's the deal — you get the wreck, I get the river." She pushed the paper toward him. "Five hundred."

Brannock smiled. It was not a warm smile, but it was an efficient one. He counted bills onto the bar — five hundreds, crisp, smelling of some other city's ink — and slid them over with two fingers.

"Pleasure doing business, Captain." He paused, and the patience in his eyes sharpened into something finer. "One thing. The map Vane carried — the original. Hale wants it. Paper burns, memory fades, but an original map is proof. You understand."

"I understand," Jude said, which was true, and also a warning she was handing him for free, and she hated herself a little for how smoothly it came.

She walked out with the money in her coat and the lie loose in the world, working its way downstream toward Serpent Bend like poison through a current.

* * *

Tam met her at the gangplank, and one look at his face told her the night had gone sideways while she was ashore.

"He asked me directly," Tam said, keeping his voice low as they walked back aboard. "Ada. Whether we'd found anything. She told him no — flat, fast, better than I'd have managed. But afterward she went quiet. She's in the galley with Marisol, and Marisol looks like she's been crying or arguing or both."

"What happened?"

"She figured out the chart," Tam said. "Your corrections. The false channel under Widow's Reach. She worked out what it was for before I even saw it, and then she worked out what you'd use it for, and then—" He stopped at the companionway, and in the lamplight his face was older than his years. "She's nineteen, Captain. She's asking herself what kind of people she signed on with."

Jude climbed down into the galley carrying the five hundred dollars and the weight of the evening, and the conversation stopped when she entered, the way conversations stop around a verdict.

Ada sat at the little table with her arms wrapped around her knees, and she looked at Jude with an expression Jude recognized from mirrors, from years ago, from the girl she'd been before the fire and the river: the look of someone watching a person they admired become smaller.

"You sold him a location," Ada said.

"I sold him a fiction."

"You sold him a *trap*." Ada unfolded herself and stood, and she had her father's height and none of his caution. "Marisol told me. Snags under deep water. Any boat that goes looking hits the bar. You put a lie on the river that could kill somebody, and you got *paid* for it." Her voice cracked on the last word, and she pointed at the money in Jude's coat pocket like it was a snake. "Is that what my father's promise is worth? Five hundred dollars and a drowned stranger?"

"Nobody is drowned," Jude said. "Brannock's divers are professionals. They'll pick their way in slow, and they'll find nothing, and they'll lose a tide doing it. That's the whole design. The danger is to anyone *else* who follows the bearing — some independent, some rival—"

"Some captain like you," Ada said. "Some broke, desperate, half-starved captain chasing the same rumor, the same reward, trying to feed a crew. Somebody's daughter's father." She was shaking now, and Jude saw that it wasn't entirely anger. "My father abandoned me to keep me safe, and I didn't even know that until yesterday, and now you're telling me safety is something you can buy with other people's lives. Is that the lesson? Is that what the river teaches?"

The galley was very quiet. Marisol stood by the stove with her back to all of them, shoulders rigid, saying nothing, which was its own judgment. Tam hovered at the ladder, unwilling to descend into it, unable to leave.

Jude set the five hundred dollars on the table between them.

"Half of that buys our fuel back," she said. "The other half buys the tide we need to reach the wreck before the channel closes. That's what it buys. Not comfort. Not profit. Time." She held Ada's gaze. "You think I don't hate it? You think I slept? I have spent nine years building a life where every mile is paid for clean, and last night I sat at my own chart table and drew a lie with my own hand, and it felt *easy*, Ada. That's what frightens me. Not Brannock. Not Hale. How easy it was."

"Then don't do it."

"It's done."

"Then undo it! Send word—"

"To whom? There's no sending word. There's no taking back a thing once it's on the water." Jude heard her own voice harden and couldn't stop it, the old Lockridge steel coming up through the cracks like rust through paint. "This is what survival costs on this river. This is the price of the tide. You want your father's truth? This is the road to it. It isn't clean. It was never going to be clean."

Ada stared at her for a long moment, and then something in her face closed, the way water closes over a stone.

"My father traded everything to unmake what he helped build," she said quietly. "He starved. He lost me. He died in the mud begging passage from a stranger. And you're telling me the only way to honor that is to become a little more like the men he ruined himself fighting." She stepped back toward the ladder, toward the stern cabin she'd taken over, and paused there with her hand on the rail. "Marisol's right. It's betrayal. Maybe not of us. But of something. Of the person you were before tonight."

She went below. The curtain swung shut behind her.

Marisol finally turned from the stove. Her eyes were red-rimmed but steady.

"Well?" she said. "Say it. Say it's survival. Say it out loud so we all know the words."

Jude looked at the money on the table. At the chart room above, where the false channel waited under her corrections like a buried thing. At the black window, beyond which the river ran south toward Serpent Bend, carrying her lie to whoever was foolish enough to believe it.

"It's survival," she said.

Her voice was steady. That was the worst part. It came out steady as a chart line, steady as a course plotted by a good captain in bad water, and it sounded, in the little iron galley, almost exactly like her father's voice, and she stood there in the silence afterward and let the crew hear it too.

Above them, through the deck plates, came the far-off change in the river's note that Tam would read as surely as a heartbeat: the ebb beginning, the first of the last three tides, sliding out toward the sea.

And downstream, faint but unmistakable, the gunboat's engines woke — turning, as she'd planned, toward Serpent Bend, toward the drowned forest, toward the lie — leaving the narrows empty and black and open for exactly as long as it would take the *Vela* to run them.

Jude picked up the money and went up to take the wheel.

The channel was open. The clock was running. And somewhere behind her in the dark, on some other stretch of this same hungry river, another captain might already be reading the same false bearing, pointing a bow toward Widow's Reach, trusting the water to be honest.

Jude rang for slow ahead, and tried not to hear it as a countdown.
