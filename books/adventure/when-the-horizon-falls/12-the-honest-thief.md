## 12. The Honest Thief

Tam Okafor had spent thirty years listening to engines, and in that time he had learned a truth that most people never understood: machines were honest. An engine could not lie. When it was failing, it told you — in the rattle of a bearing gone dry, in the hiccup of fuel pressure, in the way the temperature needle climbed like a liar's pulse. People were different. People could smile at you across a fuel dock and empty your tanks while they shook your hand.

He thought about this a great deal on the morning after the false coordinates.

The *Kestrel* lay tied up at Cutter's Bend, a fuel stop so small it barely qualified as a town — a corrugated shed, two pumps gone green with rust, and a dock of blackened pilings that groaned whenever the current leaned against them. The plan had worked. That was the thing Tam kept turning over in his mind like a coin he suspected was counterfeit. It had *worked*. Brannock's gunboat had taken the bait, peeling away downstream toward the coordinates Jude had fed them through the radio man at Marrow's Landing, chasing a phantom waypoint into water too shallow for their draft. They had bought themselves a tide — maybe two — and Tam should have been celebrating.

Instead he stood on the engine room ladder with his head and shoulders above deck, watching the headmaster of the St. Brendan's Mission School count money into a leather satchel.

Well. He didn't know it was the headmaster yet. What he saw, from forty yards across the oily morning water, was a tall man in a grey coat talking to one of Hale's dock agents — a soft-bellied fellow named Quill whom Tam had seen before at other landings, always with clean hands and a ledger under his arm. The grey-coated man handed over something flat and folded. Quill handed back an envelope. The transaction took less than a minute. Then the grey coat turned, and the morning light caught his face, and Tam felt something cold slide down his spine and pool in his boots.

Reverend Aldous Crane. The headmaster himself. The man who had signed Ada Vane's school papers, who had pressed her hand between both of his own when she'd left with them and said, *Go with God, child, and write to us.* The man whose mission school sat three days' travel upriver, full of girls like Ada who had nowhere else in the world to go.

Quill tucked the envelope away and strolled off toward the gunboat's tender, whistling. Crane stood alone on the dock for a moment, looking out at the water with the expression of a man doing arithmetic he didn't enjoy. Then he walked up the plank road toward the mission launch moored at the far end, and he was gone.

Tam stayed where he was for a long moment, one hand on the warm rail of the ladder, the diesel smell rising around him like incense. He thought about going after the man. He thought about shouting. He thought about all the things a younger Tam would have done, back when he still believed that catching a thief red-handed meant something would be done about it.

Then he went below and started checking their fuel lines, because a suspicion had begun to itch at him, and Tam Okafor had learned long ago that suspicions about fuel were never wrong twice.

* * *

The *Kestrel*'s main tank held four hundred gallons when full. They had filled her at Marrow's Landing two days ago — 380 gallons, paid for with nearly everything Jude had left in the strongbox under her bunk that wasn't maps or letters or the weight of dead men's promises. At Marrow's, Tam had personally watched the gauge, had dipped the tank with his own calibrated rod, because he trusted no pump man farther than he could throw him. Three hundred and eighty gallons. Enough to run the river for six days at cruising speed, eight if they babied her.

This morning, dipping the rod, he came up wet to the two-hundred-gallon mark.

He did it again. Two hundred. He checked the bilge for leaks — dry as a sermon. He checked the return lines, the seals, the drain cock at the tank's lowest point. All tight. He stood in the engine room's amber gloom with the dip rod dripping in his fist and let the truth arrive the way it always arrived: quietly, completely, without any possibility of argument.

Someone had siphoned them. Not last night — the night before, while they'd slept at the mooring downstream of Marrow's, before the false coordinates had even been delivered. Which meant this hadn't been a response to Jude's trick. This had been arranged *in advance*. Someone had known where they would stop, how long they would stay, and exactly what would hurt them most to lose. Someone had done the same arithmetic Tam did every morning — tide windows, fuel burn, distance to the next reliable pump — and had subtracted accordingly.

Two hundred gallons wouldn't get them to the Meridian waypoint and back. It wouldn't even get them to the narrows. And there was no fuel at Cutter's Bend worth buying — Tam walked up to the shed to confirm it and found the tanks near-empty, the pump man shrugging with magnificent indifference. *Dry season, friend. Barge is due Thursday. Maybe.*

Thursday was four days past the last usable tide.

He stood in the doorway of the fuel shed and looked down at the *Kestrel*, small and stubborn against the brown water, and he thought: *They're not trying to catch us. They're trying to strand us.* Brannock's gunboat couldn't follow them into the shallow water, so Hale's people had simply reached ahead of them instead, quiet as rust, and taken the one thing no cleverness could replace.

And then, unbidden, the image returned: the grey coat. The envelope. Reverend Aldous Crane, counting out the location of a nineteen-year-old girl's trust like so many coins.

* * *

He found Jude in the wheelhouse, chart spread across the desk, a pencil behind her ear and another in her hand, working the channel numbers the way she always did — lips moving slightly, as though she were arguing with the river and losing gracefully. She looked up when his shadow crossed the chart.

"Talk to me," she said. She always said that. No preamble, no pleasantries. In three years aboard the *Kestrel*, Tam had come to understand that this was Jude Lockridge's version of tenderness: she wanted your worst news first, straight, undiluted.

"We've got two hundred gallons," he said. "Should be three-eighty."

The pencil stopped moving. He watched her not react — watched her hold it in, the way she held everything in, a whole ocean behind a locked door — and then she set the pencil down very carefully, as if it might go off.

"Leak?"

"Checked everything twice. Tank's sound. Somebody siphoned us at the Marrow's mooring. Night before we ran the ruse." He paused. "Which means they knew our route before you ever fed Brannock those coordinates. The lie bought us a tide, but somebody had already sold us out."

Jude was quiet for a moment. Outside, a kingfisher hit the water and came up flashing. "Who?"

"That's the part you're really not going to like." Tam told him then — Crane, the grey coat, the envelope, the arithmetic of it. "Ada's headmaster. The man who raised her half her life. He sold our position to Hale's people, and I'd bet my tools he's the one who tipped them about the fuel too. He knew we'd stop at Marrow's. She probably told him herself — wrote it in a letter home, trusting as sunrise."

Jude closed her eyes. For a moment she looked older than the river, older than the boat, older than the drowned hills. "She'll have to hear it."

"She'll have to hear it from one of us," Tam said. "Not from finding out. There's a difference."

"I know there's a difference." Jude opened her eyes, and they were flat and bright and terrible. "I've been on the wrong side of it."

* * *

They told her at noon, in the galley, with Marisol's rice and beans going cold in the pot because nobody touched them.

Marisol had insisted on feeding everyone first — *bad news rides better on a full stomach* — but the food sat untouched, steam curling off it and dying, while Tam laid it out plain: the fuel, the numbers, the grey coat on the dock. He watched Ada's face while he spoke, and it was like watching frost cross a window. She started composed. She ended somewhere else entirely.

"No," she said. It wasn't loud. That was the worst of it — how quiet she made it. "No, you're wrong. You have to be wrong. He—" Her hands were flat on the table, pressing down as though the boat might buck. "He kept me. Eleven years. When my father didn't come back, when nobody wanted a prospector's brat, *he* took me in. He taught me navigation off old charts. He's the reason I can read water at all. Why would—"

"Because Hale paid him," Tam said gently. "Because the mission runs on donations, and Hale's foundation is the biggest donor on the upper river. Because good men do arithmetic too, Ada, and sometimes the sum comes out ugly."

"You don't know that." Her voice cracked now, finally, the quiet splitting along its seams. "You saw a man on a dock. Anybody wears a grey coat. Maybe it was—"

"It was him," Tam said. "I'm sorry. I've known his face since you showed us his photograph in your letters. I don't make mistakes about faces. Only about people."

That landed hard — he heard it land — and Ada pushed back from the table and stood there shaking, and Marisol rose to go to her, and Ada stepped away from her too, which Tam had never seen her do, not once, not in storms, not in fear. She stood against the galley bulkhead with her arms wrapped around herself like someone holding a hull together.

"Every single one," she said, to no one, to the bulkhead, to the river beyond it. "My father sells fraud papers and leaves me poor on purpose. My headmaster sells me for an envelope. Hale buys children's futures the way he buys fuel. Is there *one* adult in this world who doesn't—" She stopped. Swallowed. Started again, smaller. "Is there one?"

The silence that followed had weight. Tam felt Marisol looking at him across the galley — a look he knew well, the look that said *your turn, engineer, fix this* — and he wished, briefly and sincerely, that fixing people worked anything like fixing engines. With an engine you replaced the broken part. With a person you just had to sit beside the brokenness until it remembered it wasn't alone.

So he did the only thing he knew how to do. He stayed.

"Come look at something," he said.

Ada didn't move. "I don't want to look at anything."

"Come look anyway." He held the engine room hatch open. "It won't take long, and then you can go back to hating everybody with a clear conscience."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth — not quite a smile, more like the memory of where a smile used to be kept. She followed him down.

* * *

The engine room was Tam's church, and he gave her the tour the way he'd give it to a doubter: honestly, without ornament. He showed her the main bearing he'd replaced with a piece scavenged from a wrecked tug, machined down by hand over nine days because they couldn't afford a new one. He showed her the fuel filter he cleaned every Sunday whether it needed it or not, because *trust is a filter, girl — you maintain it before it fails, not after.* He showed her the hairline crack in the block they'd sealed with epoxy and prayer three seasons ago, still holding, still leaking a drop a day into a coffee can he emptied like a ritual.

"This engine," he said, "is a liar's dream. Half these parts are stolen, salvaged, or borrowed. Every bolt in here has a criminal history. If honesty were a requirement for running, we'd have sunk years ago." He tapped the cracked block. "But look. It holds. Do you know why?"

Ada wiped her face with her sleeve. "Why?"

"Because somebody keeps showing up for it. Every day. Even when it's cracked. Especially when it's cracked." He sat down on his stool by the workbench, knees complaining, and patted the crate across from him until she sat too, in the amber light, among the smell of diesel and hot metal and thirty years of small repairs. "You asked if there's one adult in the world who doesn't sell children. I can't answer for the world. But I can tell you what I know. I know a woman who burned her own inheritance rather than touch dirty money, and hasn't told us why yet, but will. I know a cook who fled a family so powerful she changed her name twice and still sends half her wages to a sister she hasn't seen in fifteen years. And I know myself — a man who stole his first engine part at eleven and has been an honest thief ever since, which is to say, a man who takes only what he needs and gives back more than he took, whenever the river allows it."

Ada was staring at him. "Honest thief."

"It's a real trade," Tam said solemnly. "Very old. Very small membership. Requirements are strict: you must steal nothing that breathes, nothing that belongs to someone poorer than you, and nothing you can't live without giving back." He let that sit, then nodded upward, toward the deck, toward the wheelhouse where Jude was still bent over her charts. "That woman up there? She stole a promise from a dying man. That's all. And she's going to keep it if it kills every one of us, which, frankly, is a real possibility given the fuel situation."

And then — finally, blessedly — Ada laughed. It came out ragged and wet and surprised her as much as anyone, but it was a laugh, and in the engine room's dim gold light it sounded like a bearing seating itself true.

"The fuel situation," she repeated. "We have two hundred gallons and six tides and a headmaster who sold us, and you're making jokes."

"Honey," Tam said, "if I couldn't make jokes at two hundred gallons, I'd have drowned of seriousness decades ago." He stood, joints cracking like kindling, and put a hand on her shoulder — brief, rough, real. "Listen to me now. Adults will fail you. I'm sorry; it's true; I'd sooner tell you than let the river tell you. But failing isn't the same as leaving. Your father failed you and left. Crane failed you and cashed the check. We fail you — and we will, count on it — but we don't leave. That's the whole difference. That's the entire religion."

Ada looked down at her hands for a long time. When she looked up, her eyes were red-rimmed but level, and there was something in them Tam recognized from the navigation lessons she'd been giving them all week — that fierce, hungry competence, the light of a person who has decided to be useful instead of destroyed.

"Then teach me the fuel math," she said. "All of it. Burn rates, tide windows, what we can reach and what we can't. If we're going to be stranded, I want to be the one who figures out how not to be."

Tam grinned, and it felt strange on his face, like sun after rain. "Now *that's* the honest thief spirit."

* * *

They worked through the afternoon, the three of them — Tam, Ada, and eventually Jude, drawn down by the smell of calculations — hunched over the workbench with grease pencils and the backs of old charts. The numbers were ugly and they all knew it. Two hundred gallons, conservatively burned, got them to the second waypoint and maybe twenty miles past. The Meridian lay beyond the narrows, past the last fuel of any kind, in water that would be gone within the week. To make the round trip they needed ninety gallons minimum that they did not have.

It was Ada, bent over the chart, who found it first.

"There," she said, tapping a smudged notation in the margin of Vane's map — a symbol Tam had taken for a surveyor's mark. "That's not a reef marker. That's the old standard sign for a cached cache — prospectors used it. Supply drops. My father taught me the symbol when I was twelve." Her finger trembled slightly, but her voice didn't. "Elias Vane ran this route before. Twice, according to his notes. A man that careful doesn't run a route twice without caching fuel along it."

Jude went very still. "If it's still there."

"If it's still there," Ada agreed. "But he hid it from Hale's people the first time. And whatever Elias Vane hid well enough to survive ten years..." She looked up, and the frost was gone from her face now, burned away by purpose. "...is hidden better than anything Crane could ever find."

Tam looked from the girl to the captain and back, and felt the old engine-room warmth settle in his chest — the feeling that came, rarely, when all the parts of a machine were turning true at once.

Outside, the light was going copper, and downriver, faint as a rumor, came the low thrum of a heavy engine running against the current.

Somebody was coming up to find them.
