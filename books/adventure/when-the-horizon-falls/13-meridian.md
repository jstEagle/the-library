## 13. Meridian

The fourth tide came in gray and breathless, the water so still it looked poured rather than risen. Jude stood at the wheel with her hands light on the spokes, feeling the *Kestrel* answer her the way an old horse answers a familiar knee — not because it must, but because it has forgotten how to do anything else.

Behind her, Tam crouched over the depth sounder with a pencil behind his ear and a look on his face like a man doing arithmetic he didn't trust.

"Bottom's coming up," he said. "Forty feet. Thirty-five. There's your bluff."

Jude saw it before the sounder confirmed it — a dark shoulder of stone sliding beneath the surface off the port bow, the collapsed remnant of what had once been a headland tall enough to appear on Vane's map as a drawn cliff with a cross beside it. The rest of it lay in the river now, tumbled into the channel in some long-ago wet season, and the river had spent decades doing what rivers do: burying what it breaks.

"Vane called it the Sleeping Man," Ada said from the rail. She had the map open across her knees despite the damp, one hand pinning the corner flat. Her voice was careful, level, the way it had been for two days now — ever since the stovepipe, ever since the argument Jude kept replaying at the edges of sleep. "He drew it like a profile. See? The nose there, the chin where it broke off." She held the paper up against the horizon, and for a moment the silhouette matched, stone against ink, and something cold walked down Jude's spine.

"He was here," Marisol said quietly. She'd come up from the galley without being called; she always seemed to know when a moment needed witnesses. "Before he got sick. He dove this."

"And someone else did too," Tam said. He pointed with his pencil. Along the bluff's drowned shoulder, half-submerged in the shallows, sat a rusted tripod of pipe and chain — a diver's hoist, old but not ancient, its winch drum furred with orange corrosion. Beside it, a coil of rotted air hose lay on the rocks like something shed.

"That's not Vane's," Jude said. "His gear was older than that."

"No," Tam agreed. "That's maybe five years old. Maybe less."

Nobody said what they were all thinking. Brannock's gunboat had fallen back after the narrows, after the false coordinates Jude had fed them — a debt she hadn't let herself count yet — but falling back wasn't leaving. Somewhere downstream, Hale's divers were either chasing numbers that led to empty water or working their way back toward the truth. Either way, the clock was the clock. Three usable tides after this one. Then the dry season would drop the channel until next year, and whatever lay under the silt here would sleep another twelve months behind thirty feet of drying mud.

Jude turned the wheel. "We anchor off the chin. Tam, get the compressor running. Marisol—" She hesitated, because the order she was about to give went against every rule she'd built her life on, the rules about never showing anyone where you keep things, never diving with an audience, never letting the river see you want something. "Marisol, I want you topside the whole time. Not cooking. Watching."

Marisol met her eyes, and something passed between them that didn't need language — the memory of an oxbow slough, a storm, two women trading the stories they'd buried. "Watching," Marisol repeated. "For boats?"

"For anything."

* * *

The visibility underwater was a rumor.

Jude had dove in silt before — every river pilot who worked salvage had — but this was different. This was diving inside milk, inside smoke, inside the idea of water rather than water itself. Ten feet down her own hands vanished. Fifteen feet down the world reduced itself to the tug of the current on her tether line, the pressure in her ears, and the pale ghost-glow of the surface somewhere above her like a ceiling seen through fogged glass.

She followed the shot line Tam had dropped off the bluff's broken face, hand over hand, counting. At forty feet her boots found bottom, and the bottom was soft — not sand but silt, fine as flour, decades of the river's patience settled into a layer that swallowed her ankles and breathed up around her in slow clouds whenever she moved. She froze and waited for it to clear, knowing it wouldn't, knowing clearing was a thing this water had forgotten how to do.

So she learned to read with her hands instead of her eyes.

She swept her arms in arcs, low, feeling for structure. The first thing she found was timber — a beam as thick as her waist, crusted with mussel shell and black with age, running at an angle that no natural log would run. Then another, parallel. Then iron: a deadeye, a length of chain fused into a single rusted sculpture, and beneath her palm, unmistakable even through the glove, the curve of a hull rib rising out of the muck like the skeleton of something enormous that had lain down here to die.

The *Meridian Star*.

Even blind, even deaf in the press of forty feet of brown silence, Jude felt the awe hit her like cold water in the ears. This was a ship. This had been a ship — a real one, a deepwater hull that had come up the delta when the delta was young, carrying whatever cargo men like Elias Vane and Corvus Hale had thought worth risking a bar crossing for. And then the bluff had come down, or the channel had shifted, or both, and the river had taken her the way the river took everything, without malice and without mercy, and folded her into the mud like a letter into an envelope.

And the envelope had been waiting all these years for someone to break the seal.

Jude moved along the rib line, hand over hand along the dead ship's spine, and the silt rose around her in halos with every motion until she was working inside a cloud of her own making, blind in a blindness she was creating. It should have terrified her. Part of it did. But underneath the fear ran something else, something older and hungrier — the pilot's instinct, the wreck-raider's pulse, the feeling she'd chased since she was nineteen years old and first understood that rivers keep things. That everything lost is only misplaced. That the water remembers.

Her left hand crossed a shape that was not timber and not iron.

It was wood, but dressed wood — planed, joined, edged in metal. A chest. A strongbox, bolted straps and all, sitting proud of the silt exactly where it had come to rest when the world ended for the *Meridian Star*, as if it had been set down carefully by someone who meant to come back.

Jude's heart slammed against her ribs hard enough that she felt it in her ears, in her mask, in the regulator's rhythm. She knelt in the muck beside it. The box was maybe two feet long, iron-banded, its lock plate green with bronze disease, its lid sealed — actually sealed, she realized, running her fingers along the joint — with a bead of what felt like old wax, hardened to stone, laid across lid and body both.

Vane's wax. The same seal that had closed the map.

He'd sealed it shut. Down here or before, somehow, he'd made sure that whatever was inside would know it had been promised to someone — that opening it was a vow, not a theft.

Jude got her hands under the box and lifted, and the silt gave it up grudgingly, sucking at it, the river reluctant to surrender a thing it had held for so long. It was heavier than it looked. Of course it was. Paper was heavy when there was enough of it — deeds, bills of sale, registry copies, the whole paper skeleton of an empire. She clipped the lifting line to the box's iron handle, checked it twice, three times, and then pulled the signal cord twice: *found. haul away.*

The line went taut above her. The box rose out of her hands into the brown dark, and Jude stayed kneeling on the wreck of the *Meridian Star* for one more minute, alone in the blind water, and let herself feel it — the whole weight of the thing arriving at once. Forty feet down, in zero visibility, on a dying tide, she had done what no one else alive knew how to do. She had found it.

Then her right hand, braced on the silt where the box had rested, touched paper.

* * *

It was a packet, oilcloth-wrapped, tied with tarred twine, wedged into the hollow the strongbox had left behind — deliberately hidden, deliberately separate. Someone had wanted this part to survive even if the box was taken. Even if the box was never opened.

Jude stuffed it inside her suit against her chest and kicked for the line.

The surface took her back like a birth. Gray sky, gray water, the *Kestrel*'s rail swinging close, and Tam's hands hauling her over the side while Marisol wrapped a blanket around her shoulders before she'd finished coughing. Ada stood a little apart, coiled rope in her hands, watching the strongbox swing inboard on the davit with an expression Jude couldn't read — hunger and dread sharing the same face.

"You found it," Ada said. Not a question. Her voice shook anyway.

"I found it."

They laid the box on the deck planking amidships, and for a long moment nobody touched it. The tide murmured against the hull. Somewhere downstream a bird cried, high and lonely, and the wind carried the smell of drying mud — the dry season announcing itself, patient as a creditor.

Tam broke first, the way he always broke first, practicality outrunning reverence. "We can't open that here. Open water, mid-channel — if Brannock's glass is anywhere on this river—"

"We open it below," Jude said. "In the engine room. Steel walls, steel deck, one door." She looked at Marisol. "You watch the water."

But it was the oilcloth packet that stopped her. She'd brought it up against her chest, and now she unwound the tarred twine with fingers that wouldn't quite steady, and unfolded the oilcloth, and inside was a letter — a real letter, written in a cramped, failing hand she recognized from the map's margins, the handwriting of a man writing against his own death.

Elias Vane's last words. Not to his daughter. To whoever found this.

Jude read it standing in the open air with her crew around her, and she read it aloud, because some things are too heavy to carry silently.

*"To the one who keeps my promise —*

*"You have the box, so you have already gone further than I managed, and further than I deserved. So hear the truth while you can still put it down and walk away.*

*"I was not the Meridian Star's victim. I was her partner. Corvus Hale and I built the fraud together — the forged registries, the phantom salvage claims, the papers in that box that stole the delta routes from the families who held them honest. My share bought this boat you're standing on. My share bought twenty good years I had no right to. And when I finally couldn't stomach it anymore, I sold my portion back to Hale and used the money to search for the proof I helped bury — thinking that finding it would make me clean.*

*"It doesn't. I know that now. Proof doesn't clean a man. It only proves him.*

*"Give the box to my daughter, if she'll have it. But tell her the truth first, all of it, because a legacy built on a lie is just a lie with better furniture. And ask yourself, keeper — whoever you are — what your own hands have held. Because the Meridian's papers don't just name Corvus Hale. They name everyone who ever signed beside him.*

*"Check the third page. Check the signatures.*

*"I'm sorry. For all of it. For her most of all.*

*"— E.V."*

The wind moved across the deck. Nobody spoke.

Jude heard her own breathing. She heard the tide. And underneath both, faint and terrible, she heard her father's voice saying the thing he'd said the night she burned her inheritance — *someone has to hold the pen, Judith, or someone else will* — and she understood, with a vertigo that had nothing to do with heights or depths, that the third page of those documents might carry a signature she already knew by heart.

A Lockridge signature.

Ada reached for the box's seal first, but Jude caught her wrist — gently, and yet Ada flinched as if scalded.

"Not yet," Jude said. Her voice came out lower than she intended. "Not until we're all ready for what's in it."

"Ready?" Ada's laugh cracked in the middle. "My father just told us he built the lie we've been bleeding for. What could possibly be in that box that I'm not ready for?"

Jude looked at the wax seal, unbroken, patient, holding its secret the way the river held the wreck — and said nothing, because the honest answer was: *a name. Mine.*
