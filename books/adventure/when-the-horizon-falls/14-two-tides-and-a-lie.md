## 14. Two Tides and a Lie

The letter was where her father had always hidden things: inside something else.

Ada found it folded into the lining of the strongbox's lid, sewn there with waxed thread so fine she only felt it because the tip of her thumbnail caught on a ridge no wider than a hair. She had been running her fingers along the seams for an hour, alone in the engine room while Tam slept and Marisol kept the night watch and Jude stood at the wheel pretending not to watch the water as if it might confess something back to her. The boat rocked in its anchorage off the drowned bluff, riding the long slow swell of the fourth tide going slack, and the lamp above Ada's head swung its small yellow circle across the iron walls like a pendulum counting down to something.

She hadn't meant to find it. That was the lie she would tell herself later, in the years when lying to herself became easier — that she had simply been curious, that any daughter would have looked. But she had gone looking. She had gone looking because Jude had come up from the dive with the strongbox cradled against her chest like an infant, and because she had set it down on the deck plates and put her hand flat on top of it and said nothing for a full minute, and because Ada had watched her father's ghost move across Jude Lockridge's face and needed to know what he had said to put it there.

So: the letter. Sewn into the lid. Her father's handwriting, cramped and slanting, the hand of a man who had learned to write by candlelight in bad weather.

*My Ada.*

She read it twice before she understood it. Then she read it a third time, slowly, the way you pick apart a knot, and the engine room went very cold around her, or perhaps it was only her own blood deciding to go elsewhere.

*If you are reading this, then Elias Vane is dead, and good riddance, and I hope whoever carried this box to you made him say your name at least once before the end.*

*You will have been told I abandoned you. You will have been told worse than that — children invent their own cruelties when adults leave them none, and I left you nothing but the invention. So let me give you the truth instead, though it will sit in your stomach like river stone:*

*I did not lose you. I spent you.*

Ada stopped. The lamp swung. Somewhere above her, through two decks of old wood, the river breathed against the hull, patient as everything else that intended to outlast her.

She read on.

*When I was young I was partner to a man named Corvus Hale. Not his servant, not his clerk — his partner. The Meridian Star was as much mine as his, and the papers in this box prove what we did together: how we forged the route claims, how we sold the delta twice over, how we built an empire on paper that never touched water until men like your captain started floating on it. I signed my name beside his. I took my share. I bought your mother a house with money that belonged to other people, and when she died I told myself God had taken her rather than let her see what I was.*

*Then I tried to leave, and learned what leaving costs. Hale does not forgive debts; he collects them. So I paid the only way he could not follow: I sold him my share of the fraud itself. My silence, my signature, my claim — all of it, signed away for less than it was worth, because the price was never the point. The price was that Hale would believe me finished. A man who has sold everything has nothing left worth hunting.*

*And you — Ada, my Ada — I left you poor on purpose.*

Her hands were shaking. She pressed the page flat against her knee to still it, and the paper crackled like ice on a winter channel.

*A poor girl is invisible. A poor girl is beneath the interest of men like Hale, who audit the world the way bankers audit ledgers and skip every column that doesn't sum to something they want. If I had left you money, you would be leverage. If I had left you my name spoken aloud, you would be a target. I left you a mission school and a hard mouth and nothing else, because nothing else was the only inheritance I could give you that wouldn't get you killed.*

*But I could not leave you nothing forever. So I spent eleven years diving wrecks and walking reefs and trading favors to men who spat when they said my name, buying back, piece by piece, the proof of what I helped build — not to sell it, though God knows I was offered enough, but to hold it over Hale's head from the grave. Dead men make excellent creditors. He cannot buy off a letter.*

*Give it to no one who wants it. That is all. Anyone who wants this box wants it for themselves, and everyone who wants it for themselves will tell you it is for you.*

*I loved you the whole time. I know that reads like the smallest coin in the purse. Spend it however you like.*

*Your father,*
*Elias Vane*
*who was also Elias Vane, which is the trouble*

* * *

The lamp oil burned low. Ada sat with the letter in her lap and listened to the boat settle around her — the tick of cooling pipe, the groan of the anchor line, Tam snoring somewhere aft with the untroubled rhythm of a man whose conscience fit inside his skull.

He didn't abandon me by accident.

That sentence should have been a mercy. She turned it over and over, looking for the mercy in it, the way she used to turn river stones looking for agate, and found instead that it cut deeper than abandonment ever had. Because abandonment was simple. Abandonment was a man who chose the horizon over his daughter, and a daughter could hate that cleanly, could build a whole self out of hating it, sharp and load-bearing, the way she had. *Furious at her father's ghost*, Jude had called her once, not unkindly, and Ada had worn the phrase like armor because it was true and truth was the only thing she owned.

But this — this was worse. This was a man who had looked at his daughter and seen a liability to be managed. Who had weighed her life against Hale's attention and chosen poverty for her the way you'd choose a dull coat: practical. Protective. *Deliberate.* Every hungry winter at the mission school, every secondhand dress, every Christmas when the other girls' families sent parcels and hers sent nothing — none of it had been neglect. It had been strategy. Her whole childhood of want had been a maneuver in a war she'd never known she was fighting, executed without her consent, without even her knowledge, by a man who signed his love in the negative space of what he refused to give her.

*I loved you the whole time.*

She almost laughed. The sound came out of her as something else — a short wet breath that echoed off the iron — and she clamped her jaw shut because the walls were thin and grief carries.

Because here was the thing, the thing she would carry downriver and upriver and through every chapter of whatever came next: he was right. That was the unbearable part. Lying awake in the dormitory at the mission school, listening to the rich girls' fathers' carriages on the gravel road, she had been invisible, and invisibility had kept her alive. Hale's men had walked through that school twice in her memory — she remembered now, remembered Brannock's kind of stillness in a doorway, remembered the headmaster bowing — and both times they had looked past her as if she were furniture. Poor Ada Vane, the orphan with the chip on her shoulder and holes in her boots. Not worth the ledger ink.

Her poverty had been a wall, and her father had built it with his absence, brick by brick, year by year, and she had lived inside it cursing the mason.

And now the wall was coming down. Now she was aboard this boat, holding proof worth an empire, and every eye on the river had found her. Brannock knew her face. Hale would know her name within the week. The letter had said *give it to no one who wants it*, and everyone wanted it — Jude wanted it, or wanted what it could do, wanted her family's name scrubbed clean of complicity; Hale wanted it buried; half the salvage fleet on the delta wanted it for the bounty — and her father had known, had known from beyond the grave, that the moment this box surfaced his daughter would stop being safe.

He had bought her nineteen years of safety and spent them all in one tide.

* * *

She heard footsteps on the deck above and went still, folding the letter into her shirt before she'd decided to. Old habit, she told herself. New habit, said something quieter underneath.

It was Jude. Of course it was Jude. The woman slept the way cats do, in shallow installments, one ear always on the water. Ada heard her cross to the rail, heard the creak of her weight settling, and after a while — because the engine room hatch stood open to the night air, because sound travels down through old boats like water through cloth — she heard Jude speak, low, to no one, the way solitary people pray.

"Two tides," Jude said. "Two tides and I'm done with you, Vane."

Ada closed her eyes. Two tides. She'd heard the arithmetic at supper — Tam chalked it on the bulkhead like a man writing his own epitaph: falling water, closing channel, six tides become four become two. And she had watched Jude say almost nothing through that whole meal, watched her weigh the strongbox's existence against the crew's lives with the transparent arithmetic of a woman who believed she hid things well.

Jude was going to sink it. Ada had known since the bluff, since the moment Jude came up from the black water with the box and wouldn't meet anyone's eyes. She was going to drop her father's life's work — eleven years of reefs and rot and debt — into deep water past any diver's reach, and call it keeping a promise, and row away clean.

And the terrible thing, the thing Ada sat with in the dark while the lamp guttered out and the river breathed against the hull, was that some part of her understood. Some part of her, the part raised on nothing, the part that knew exactly what wealth does to the people who orbit it, thought: *burn it, drown it, let it feed the fish.* Proof is a weapon, and weapons only change hands; they don't end wars. Her father had held it over Hale from the grave, and look where holding it had gotten everyone — a gunboat in the narrows, a fuel cache in ashes, a headmaster's smile that had never once been honest.

But another part of her — the part that had memorized documents by lamplight since she was twelve, the part that understood that the only thing more dangerous than evidence is evidence destroyed — another part was already moving. While Jude read the river, Ada had read the box. Three nights ago, in the oxbow slough, while Marisol told her story and the rain came down, Ada had sat with the deeds and the forged charts and the letters of transfer, and she had done what she always did with anything that mattered: she had put it inside herself, word for word, date for date, seal for seal. Names, amounts, witnesses, the lot — filed away behind her eyes where fire couldn't reach and water couldn't touch and no enforcer's hands could ever search.

Hale could sink the paper. He could not sink her.

She didn't know yet what she would do with it. Testify, maybe, someday, to some commission that didn't exist yet, on terms she hadn't invented yet. Or nothing. Or wait. Her father had taught her that much without meaning to: the most powerful thing a person can hold is a secret nobody knows they're holding, and the second most powerful is patience.

What she knew, lying in the dark with her father's letter against her sternum like a coal, was that she would not tell Jude. Not about the sale, not about the deliberate poverty, not about the memorized pages sleeping behind her eyes. Jude had her own buried name, her own burned papers, her own reasons for wanting this finished — and Ada had watched her too closely these last weeks, watched her lie to Brannock smooth as poured oil, watched her calculate tides and betrayals with the same steady hands. Jude Lockridge would do what Jude Lockridge thought was right, and what she thought was right would drown the last true thing Elias Vane ever made.

Let her think the secret sank with the box. Let her sleep easy on open water, believing the past was past.

Ada lay back against the cold iron and listened to the tide turn. Below the keel, the current reversed itself without a sound, the whole river quietly changing its mind, and above her, faint through the deck planks, she heard Jude push off the rail and walk back toward the wheelhouse — two soft footfalls, a pause, two more — a woman measuring her remaining miles.

Two tides, Jude had said.

Ada pressed her palm flat over the letter, over the words her father had sewn into iron for her alone, and began, silently, to count.
