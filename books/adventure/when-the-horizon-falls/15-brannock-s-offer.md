## 15. Brannock's Offer

The narrows came at them the way all bad news comes — slowly enough to see it coming, fast enough that there was no time left to do anything but meet it.

Jude had run the narrows a dozen times in her life. It was a throat of rock where the river squeezed itself down to less than half its width, where the current went from walking pace to something like fury, and where the channel — never wider than the *Kestrel's* hull and a prayer on either side — threaded between two walls of black stone streaked white with old bird droppings. In high water it was a thrill. In falling water it was arithmetic done with your stomach. And today, with the dry season eating the river a hand's depth every day, it was the last door between them and the deep water beyond.

She had planned to run it at first light, on the back of the morning tide, when the current would be least hostile. She had planned to be through before anyone could think to look for her there.

But Brannock had not needed to look. He had only needed to understand her, and he did — that was the thing she kept having to admit about the man, the thing that made him more dangerous than Hale's whole fleet. Brannock understood her the way a good pilot understands a river: by reading what was visible and inferring everything underneath.

So his gunboat sat in the wide water below the narrows, engine idling, positioned exactly where any boat coming out of the throat would have to pass within thirty feet of it. Two of Hale's salvage barges flanked it like a pair of cupped hands. On the gunboat's foredeck, a mounted machine gun sat under a canvas cover that had been pulled back just far enough to make its point without making a scene.

"He's been waiting since before dawn," Tam said from the wheelhouse doorway. He had the field glasses in one hand and a wrench in the other, as if he hadn't yet decided which problem he intended to solve. "I counted eight men I can see. There'll be more below decks."

"Of course there will." Jude stood at the helm with both hands on the wheel out of habit, though they were dead in the water, holding position against the current with short bursts of the engine. The *Kestrel* trembled under her like an animal that could smell what she could smell. "He didn't want to chase us through the narrows. Too much risk of wrecking whatever we're carrying."

"Or us," Marisol said quietly, from behind him.

"Or us," Jude agreed.

Ada came up last, moving carefully, the way she'd learned to move over the past weeks — reading the deck, reading the faces. She was nineteen years old and she had learned more about boats in six weeks than some men learned in a lifetime, and she had learned something else too, something Jude wished she could unteach her: how to read the silence of adults who are deciding your future without you.

"They're not shooting," Ada said. "Why aren't they shooting?"

"Because bullets don't buy anything back," Jude said. "A sunk strongbox is worth nothing to him. A sunk crew is worth nothing either. He wants a conversation."

"That's worse," Ada said.

"Yes," Jude said. "It is."

* * *

They tied off at the base of the narrows because there was nowhere else to go. Running the passage under the guns would have been suicide dressed up as courage, and Jude Lockridge had never confused the two. She brought the *Kestrel* alongside the rock shelf where the water ran slowest, dropped anchor fore and aft, and told Tam to keep the engine warm and Marisol to keep everyone else below.

"I'm coming with you," Ada said.

"No."

"You promised Vane you'd find me. You found me. Whatever happens next happens to both of us, and I'm done being cargo." Her chin was up, her arms crossed, and she had her father's stubbornness written all over her face — which was, Jude thought, exactly the wrong inheritance for this particular morning. "You can't leave me below decks wondering. I've spent my whole life below decks wondering."

Jude looked at her for a long moment. The girl had mud on her boots and salt in her hair and a jaw set like poured concrete, and standing there in the gray dawn light she looked so much like someone Jude used to know — someone who had once stood on a different deck with the same expression, refusing to be sent below by people who loved her — that it hurt somewhere under the ribs.

"Stay behind me," Jude said finally. "And if I say run, you run. Not toward the boat. Toward the rocks. There's a goat path up the north wall. Marisol knows it. You go up and you don't come down until one of us comes for you."

"And if no one comes?"

"Then you take the *Kestrel* and you go home."

"This is home," Ada said, and then seemed surprised at herself for saying it, and turned away quickly, as if the words might be taken back if she didn't let them stand in the open air.

Brannock came across alone.

That was the performance of it, of course — the single man in the skiff, unarmed, hands visible, wearing his weathered canvas coat with the collar up against the morning chill. He looked less like an enforcer than like a fisherman who'd lost his way, except that Jude knew the two men remaining in the skiff were not fishermen, and knew that the rifle lying flat under a tarp in the bow was not a fishing rod, and knew above all that Brannock himself was the weapon, the same way a river is the weapon and the floodplain is only where it does its work.

"Captain Lockridge." He stepped aboard without asking, which was its own kind of message, and nodded past her at Ada with something almost like courtesy. "Miss Vane. You've grown since the school photographs."

Ada went very still beside her.

"You keep photographs of children?" Jude said.

"Hale keeps files. I keep the files honest." Brannock smiled without warmth, the smile of a man doing paperwork. "May I?" He gestured at the wheelhouse bench, and when neither woman answered, he sat anyway, settling in like a man prepared to stay until the tide changed or the argument did. "I'll be brief, because I respect your time and because the tide won't wait for either of us. Mr. Hale has reconsidered his terms."

"His last terms involved divers following us upriver and an inspection that wasn't an inspection."

"His last terms were made by a man who believed you might still be persuaded to sell. His new terms are made by a man who understands you might not be." Brannock laced his fingers over one knee. "So here is what they are. The strongbox, delivered intact, and in exchange: every soul on this boat walks away free. No charges, no questions, no follow-up. The gunboats turn around this morning. And—" he paused, and Jude watched him choose to say the next part, watched him aim it, "—Miss Vane receives a settlement. Five thousand now, held in trust, plus an annuity for life. Education paid, wherever she wants to study. Her father's debts, such as they are, settled. Hale's word on it, and Hale's word, whatever else may be said of him, has never once been broken."

The wheelhouse was very quiet. Somewhere below, Marisol's stove ticked as it cooled. The river muttered against the rocks.

"And me?" Jude said. "What do I get besides my life? Because you didn't come all this way to offer pocket change to a girl and freedom to a captain. That's not worth two gunboats and a season."

Brannock looked at her then — really looked, the way he had at the market, the way he always did, with that terrible patient attention — and Jude felt the trap close around her before he even spoke.

"You get your name back," he said.

The words landed in her chest like ballast dropped from height.

"Elias Vane carried documents proving the Meridian Star's cargo was real — deeds, ledgers, correspondence. But documents cut both ways, Captain. The same papers that prove Hale forged his claim also prove who helped him forge it. And they prove, in black ink, in a lawyer's hand, dated and signed, that certain members of the Lockridge family acted under duress. Coerced. Threatened. Their signatures obtained by means the courts of that era would call fraud and ours would call criminal." He leaned forward slightly. "Your grandfather didn't sign those routes away because he wanted to, Captain. He signed because Hale had something on him, and the papers prove it. Every history book, every ledger line, every sneer at the name Lockridge — the box can burn all of it clean. Your family ruined, your father disgraced, yourself driven onto this river — all of it rewritten in a single afternoon, once the commission sees what's in that strongbox. Hale doesn't just want the evidence destroyed. He wants it destroyed *because* it acquits your bloodline. Because a cleared Lockridge is a Lockridge with standing, and a Lockridge with standing is a Lockridge who could contest his claim in court someday. He'd rather sink the whole truth than let half of it float free."

Jude heard herself breathing. She heard the river. She heard, distantly, the tick of the cooling stove, and beneath all of it, steady as a keel, the sound of her own heartbeat telling her something she did not want to hear.

Because here was the truth she had buried nine years ago on the night she burned her inheritance papers in a copper washbasin and walked down to the water with nothing but a duffel bag and a hatred she mistook for freedom: she had never stopped wanting it. Not the money — she'd proven that, lived lean and proud on river wages while her schoolmates bought ships. But the *name*. The right to walk into a room and be Jude Lockridge instead of nobody, instead of the daughter of the man who signed, instead of a rumor with a riverboat. She had told herself for nine years that she'd chosen the river. And she had. But she had chosen it the way you choose exile, and exiles dream of home, and she had dreamed of it every night whether she admitted it or not.

And now a man in a canvas coat was offering it back to her. Washed clean. Certified. With interest.

"The box could clear my family," she said slowly, testing the weight of the words in her own mouth. "Even handed over publicly. Even testified to. The truth doesn't have to die to set my name free."

"No," Brannock agreed pleasantly. "It doesn't. Which is precisely why Hale wants it at the bottom of the sea rather than in your hands. And why he's authorized me to say this: deliver it sealed and unopened, and he will not merely spare you. He will fund the restoration of the Lockridge house on Harbor Row. He will see your father's grave marked properly. He will—" and here Brannock's voice softened into something almost gentle, which was the most frightening register it owned, "—he will ensure that when Miss Vane testifies to the river commission someday, if she ever chooses to, the record shows the Lockridges as victims, not collaborators. He'll write your family back into the world, Captain. All he asks is that the full truth stays drowned."

"And the parts of the truth that implicate him?"

"Drowned with the rest."

"And the parts that indict my father's *choices* — not just his coercion? Because I've read what Vane collected, Brannock. My grandfather took payments after the signing. Years of them. Duress doesn't explain the deposits."

Brannock spread his hands. "Hale is prepared to be generous about the details."

"There it is," Jude said softly. "There's the price. The details get generous. The record gets edited. My family gets laundered along with his crime, and twenty years from now some historian writes a tidy paragraph about the poor wronged Lockridges, and nobody ever knows what my grandfather actually did, and nobody ever knows what Hale actually did, and Ada's testimony gets — what did you call it — *generous about the details*."

"Captain—"

"Get off my boat."

Brannock didn't move. That was the thing about him; he never moved until the mathematics told him to. He sat there with his hands folded and his eyes on hers, and he said, very quietly, "You're going to want to think about this longer than you're allowing yourself. I can see it. I've watched you weigh it since I stepped aboard. Captain — you have spent nine years paying for a crime you didn't commit. This box buys that debt back. Not for Hale. For *you*. Think about what you're refusing, and who you're refusing it for, and whether the river has ever once given you anything it didn't take back with interest." He rose, unhurried, and straightened his coat. "The offer stands until the tide turns. After that, the narrows close for the season, my orders change, and everyone's options become considerably worse. I'll be in the wide water. Come find me before the water drops another foot."

He stepped down into the skiff. The tarp stayed over the rifle. The gunboat's engine note rose and fell and settled back into its idle, patient as a held breath.

* * *

Afterward, Jude stood at the rail of the *Kestrel* and watched the tide mark creep down the black stone of the narrows wall, measuring the hours the way she measured everything, in feet and fathoms instead of days.

Tam came up and stood beside her without speaking for a long time. Then: "Five thousand and an annuity isn't nothing. For the girl, I mean. She's got nothing else. No family, no trade, no—"

"She's got us."

"She's got us," Tam agreed. "But us is a boat that needs a new shaft bearing and a fuel bill we can't pay and a channel that closes in three tides. Love doesn't moor a vessel, Captain. I'm just saying the offer isn't stupid. Parts of it aren't."

"I know which parts aren't," Jude said. "That's the problem."

Marisol appeared with coffee, three cups, handing them out like communion. She looked at Jude with those level dark eyes that had seen her through storms and mutinies and one memorable night in a jail in Puerto Oscuro, and said only: "Whatever you decide, decide it as the woman who burned the papers. Not the girl who signed nothing."

"That's the trouble, Mari. The girl who burned the papers burned them because she couldn't bear what her family had become. If I hand that box to Hale, I finish the job. I make the lie permanent. My grandfather becomes a victim forever, officially, in the record, and I live in the house on Harbor Row on top of it." Jude wrapped her hands around the cup and felt the heat bite. "But if I give it to the commission — if I testify — I clear the coercion and expose the deposits in the same breath. I clear my family's name and hang my family's shame on the same hook, in public, forever. There's no version where I get the name back clean. Brannock knows that. It's why he offered the dirty version first."

"So the honest version costs you everything you've wanted for nine years," Marisol said.

"The honest version costs me the *wanting*," Jude said. "Which might be the same thing."

Below them, faintly, through the deck planks, they heard Ada's voice in the galley, talking to herself the way she did when she was working through something — running tide calculations aloud, or reciting the contents of documents she had memorized against fire and water, letter by letter, date by date. Jude had heard her do it at night sometimes, a low litany through the bulkhead, and had never asked about it, and had never quite stopped listening.

"She'd give it all up for you," Tam said. "You know that. The annuity, the schooling, all of it. If you asked her to burn the box tomorrow, she'd strike the match."

"I know," Jude said. "That's exactly why I can't ask."

* * *

Night came down on the narrows like a lid. Up on the rock shelf, the goat path was a pale thread in the starlight, and the gunboat in the wide water showed a single riding light, green, patient, keeping its station. The tide turned at 2:14. Jude knew it without checking, the way she knew her own pulse.

She was at the chart table when she heard the step behind her — soft, barefoot, deliberate. She didn't turn. She knew the weight of everyone aboard by their footsteps now, the way you learn the creaks of a boat you love.

"How long were you standing there?" she said.

Ada came around into the lamplight. Her face was composed, but her eyes weren't; they were wet and furious and trying very hard to be neither. "Long enough," she said. "Since 'get off my boat.' I was on the ladder. You closed the hatch, but the hatch doesn't latch anymore, remember? Tam keeps meaning to fix it."

"Ada—"

"You were going to say yes." The words came out flat and precise, each one placed like a stone in a wall. "Maybe not today. Maybe not this hour. But I watched your face when he said your name back to you. *Lockridge.* Nobody's said it to you in nine years, and you — you *lit up*, Jude. Like a lamp. Like a harbor light. Don't tell me you were going to refuse him. I know what refusing looks like on you. I've seen it twice. That wasn't it."

Jude set down the dividers she'd been holding. Her hands were not entirely steady, and she hated that, and hated more that Ada saw it.

"What do you want me to say?" she asked. "That I'm not tempted? I am. God help me, I am. That box is the only thing in the world that could give me back the name I burned, and I have wanted it back every single day for nine years, and I have lied to myself about that every single day for nine years, and today a man put it on the table in front of me with a ribbon on it and I—" She stopped. Breathed. "Yes. I nearly accepted. I stood there and I built the case for it, plank by plank, the way you build anything you already want. Clearing my family. Protecting you. Being practical. And every plank was true, Ada. That's the horror of it. Every single reason I gave myself was *true*, and stacked together they made a ladder straight down to selling the truth to the man who forged it."

Ada stood very still in the lamplight. When she spoke, her voice cracked in the middle and she pushed through it anyway. "My father sold me too. Did you know that? Sold my future to buy his redemption, and called it protection. And now you're standing here telling me you nearly sold the truth to buy back your name, and calling it protecting *me*." Her jaw trembled and set. "Everyone keeps deciding what's best for me inside rooms I'm not allowed in. Everyone keeps trading pieces of my life across tables I never sit at. So here's what I need you to tell me, Captain Lockridge, and I need you to tell me the truth, because it's the only currency anyone on this boat claims to have left."

She leaned across the chart table, close enough that Jude could see the tide charts reflected in her eyes.

"When the moment comes — and it's coming, tomorrow, at slack water, one way or another — whose side of the table are you going to be sitting on? Mine? Or theirs?"

Outside, far off in the dark, the green riding light of the gunboat burned steady over the falling water. And Jude opened her mouth to answer, and found — for the first time in nine years, for the first time since the washbasin and the fire and the long walk down to the river — that she did not know what the truth was going to be.
