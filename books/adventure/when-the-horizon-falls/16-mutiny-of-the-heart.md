## 16. Mutiny of the Heart

Marisol had learned long ago that a boat tells the truth before its people do. A hull creaks differently when the crew is angry. The galley smells wrong when someone is afraid. And that evening, moored in the shadowed bend below the narrows, the *Kestrel's Wake* smelled of gun oil and river rot and something else — something sour and human, like milk just beginning to turn.

She had cooked nothing. That was how she told them.

For eleven years she had fed this crew, and in eleven years she had never once let an argument reach the table unfed. Hunger made men stupid; fullness made them honest. But tonight she had set out only bread, hard cheese, and four cups of coffee gone cold in their tin mugs, because tonight the truth needed to be hungry.

The strongbox sat on the table between them.

It was smaller than Marisol had imagined, when Tam had hauled it dripping from Jude's dive and set it down like a sleeping animal. Smaller than all the trouble it carried. Salt-scabbed iron, hinges furred with rust, Vane's initials scratched into the lid in a hand that shook. Inside it: deeds, ledgers, letters — paper that could buy a fleet or burn an empire, depending on whose hands held it and how long they held on.

Tam sat across from it with his arms folded, jaw working at nothing. Ada sat beside him, her father's letter folded and refolded so many times in her pocket that Marisol could hear the creases weakening every time the girl shifted. And Jude stood at the far rail with her back to all of them, watching the dark water slide past as if the river might carry an answer up to her if she waited long enough.

"So," Marisol said, and pulled out the chair at the head of the table, which had been hers by unspoken right since the day she'd boarded, and sat down in it. "Somebody start talking."

Nobody did. The engine ticked as it cooled. Somewhere upstream a night bird called twice and thought better of it.

"Three days ago we were a crew," Marisol went on, when the silence had grown teeth. "Now we're three people and a girl, sitting around a box, each of us doing arithmetic we don't want anyone else to see." She turned her mug a slow quarter-turn on the table. "I can smell it. Can't you?"

"Smell what?" Tam said, too fast.

"Fear. Greed. Take your pick — they cook down to the same thing."

That got him. He unfolded his arms and leaned forward, and Marisol saw that he'd already decided, sometime in the last day, and that deciding had not made him lighter.

"Fine," he said. "You want it said out loud? I'll say it out loud. We sell it."

Ada's head came up like a struck animal's.

"Brannock offered us enough to refit this boat twice over," Tam went on, his voice flat and reasonable, the voice he used for engine parts and other facts. "Enough that none of us ever has to run fuel through a dying channel again. Enough that *she*—" he tipped his chin toward the rail, toward Jude's back— "never has to take another contract hauling cargo she won't name for people she doesn't trust. I've spent six years keeping this engine alive with baling wire and prayer. I'm tired, Mari. I'd like to be rich and tired instead of just tired."

"You'd sell a dead man's promise," Ada said.

"I'd sell *paper*," Tam said. "Paper doesn't bleed. Paper doesn't get people shot at narrows. You know what that box is worth in lives? Brannock's already tried to board us once. Next time he won't knock."

"He'll get the money from Hale either way," Ada said, and her voice cracked on it, young and furious. "Don't you understand? The money isn't the point. The *proof* is the point. My father spent twenty years and everything he had digging this out of the water, and if we sell it, Hale buys back his own crime and burns it, and no one will ever—"

"No one will ever what?" Tam asked. "Be saved? Be sorry? You think Corvus Hale stands in front of a river commission and weeps?"

"I think it gets *said*," Ada said. "Out loud. In a room where it can't be unsaid. I think his name gets nailed to what he did, in public, where my father's name can—" She stopped. Swallowed. Marisol watched the rest of the sentence go down her throat like a stone.

"Can what?" Marisol asked quietly.

"Nothing." Ada looked at the table. "Can be next to it. Where it belongs."

*There it is,* Marisol thought. *The letter.* She had seen the girl reading it alone in the bow two nights past, seen her face do something complicated and then go carefully blank, and Marisol had said nothing, because some doors you have to let people open themselves. But she knew a hidden thing when she saw one. She'd been one herself, once.

"And me?" Marisol said. "Since we're being honest. I don't want to sell it." She looked around the table, at all of them, and found she wasn't afraid of what she was about to say, only of how long she'd waited to say it. "I want to burn it."

"Burn it?" Tam stared. "Mari—"

"Every word of it. Tonight. Feed it to the stove page by page and watch the smoke go up the pipe and out over the water, and tomorrow morning Hale's empire is still standing but nobody owns the proof of it, and nobody ever will." She spread her hands. "You want to know why? Because I grew up in a house like Hale's. Not as big. Big enough. And I will tell you what happens to documents like these, even the ones that win. They get used. Somebody trades them, somebody leverages them, somebody's granddaughter inherits them like a disease. Power doesn't get defeated by paper. It just changes pockets."

"That's cowardice," Ada whispered.

"That's *experience*," Marisol said. "There's a difference, and the difference is about thirty years."

"And you, Captain?" Tam said, louder now, turning in his chair toward the rail. "We're saying it all out loud, right? That was the rule. So what does the box mean to you? Because you've been standing back there breathing like a woman at a graveside, and I've known you six years and you have never once kept a secret this heavy without it leaking somewhere."

Jude didn't turn around.

"Jude." Marisol put the word down gently, like setting a full cup on a moving deck.

For a long moment there was only the river, slapping the hull in its patient way, the way water reminds you it has all the time in the world and you don't. Then Jude pushed off the rail and came to the table, and she didn't sit — she stood over the strongbox with her hands flat on either side of it, caging it, or maybe herself.

"You want to know what it means to me," she said. Her voice was low and even, the voice she used in bad channels. "It means my family helped build the thing it proves."

The words dropped into the cabin like an anchor through black water.

"My name isn't Lockridge because I picked it," Jude went on, and now she did sit, slowly, across from Tam, and Marisol saw that her hands were not quite steady. "Lockridge is the name I ran from. My grandfather owned half the delta trade before Hale did. My father signed the papers that let Hale take it — signed them knowing what they were, took his cut, and called it partnership. The Meridian Star's ledgers would prove it. Would prove *us*. Every mile I've run since I was nineteen years old, every rule I've made, every time I've told you I don't talk about my past — I wasn't being private. I was being *ashamed*."

"Jude—" Tam started.

"Let her finish," Marisol said softly. "This is the first honest thing this boat's heard in a week."

"The box could clear us," Jude said. "That's the truth too. That's the part I couldn't say in front of Brannock, and the part I couldn't say in front of any of you, because if I said it, I'd have to admit how badly I wanted it. Hand it over, take Hale's price, walk back into the world with my name washed clean and money under it. One signature. One conversation. Everything I burned the night I left home, handed back to me gift-wrapped." She laughed, and there was nothing warm in it. "And that's exactly what my father would have done. That's exactly the arithmetic he taught me. Everything has a price; everything is a trade. I swore on a riverbank at nineteen that I would never make that trade again, and last week, standing on this deck, I caught myself making it in my head. Twice."

The engine ticked. The lamp hissed. Ada had gone very still, the way the young go still when the adults finally crack and show the machinery underneath.

"So there it is," Jude said. "All out loud. Tam wants to sell. Marisol wants to burn. Ada wants justice. And I want—" She stopped, and Marisol watched her look down at the strongbox, at Vane's shaking initials, and something moved through her face like weather. "I don't know what I want. That's the honest answer. I've been steering by dead reckoning since Vane died, and I don't know where I'm headed anymore."

"Yes, you do," Marisol said.

All three of them looked at her.

"You've known since the fuel dock," Marisol said. "Maybe since the mission school. You just haven't let yourself say it, because saying it means the others get a vote." She leaned forward and laid her hand flat on the cool iron lid, feeling the river's chill still living in the metal. "So here's what we're going to do. Nobody leaves this table until everyone has said the truest thing they've got — not the smart thing, not the safe thing, the *truest* thing. And then we decide together. All four of us. Because that's what this boat is, whether you all remember it or not. It's not a captain and her hands. It's a crew. And a crew sinks together or it doesn't sink at all."

* * *

They talked until the lamp burned low.

Not arguing — arguing had been the easy part, earlier, the part with shapes and edges. What came after was stranger and slower. Tam told them about the year his daughter got sick, the year he'd have sold anything, sold himself, to pay a doctor in the city, and how the doctor had taken what little he had anyway and the girl had died in the spring. "Rich didn't save her," he said, staring into his cold coffee. "But poor is what killed her. Don't tell me money doesn't matter. Money is the difference between begging and choosing. That's all I know for certain." And Marisol heard, underneath it, that he wasn't greedy — he was frightened, and grief had taught him that fear was rational.

Ada went last of the three. She pulled her father's letter from her pocket, and her hands shook, and she didn't read it aloud — she just held it, pressed flat between both palms like something that might fly away.

"He planned it," she said. "All of it. Being poor. Leaving me. He *calculated* me, same as Hale calculates everything, and he did it because he loved me, and I don't—" Her breath hitched. "I don't know yet if that makes it better or worse. But I know this. If we sell the box, then everything he did meant nothing. If we burn it, then it meant nothing *quietly*. I can live with a lot of things. I can't live with quiet."

When it was Marisol's turn, she almost didn't. But a rule is a rule, and she had made it, and the table was listening.

"I fled a house like yours," she told Jude. "You knew that much. What you don't know is that I had a brother who stayed. He stayed and managed the estate and told himself he was protecting the workers, softening the master's hand, and by the end he *was* the master's hand. That's what staying inside the machine does, even with good intentions. That's why I say burn it. Not because I'm a coward. Because I've watched what happens to people who think they can hold power's leash without it learning their scent." She looked at Jude, steadily. "Your turn. Truest thing. Not the smart thing."

Jude was quiet for a long time. When she spoke, her voice had changed — stripped down, no channel-voice in it at all.

"I don't want to be my father," she said. "That's the whole of it. Every choice I've made since Vane died, I've checked against one question: what would he do? And twice the answer scared me. So the truest thing I have is this — whatever we do with that box, I need to do it in a way my father wouldn't have. Even if it costs us. *Especially* if it costs us."

Marisol nodded slowly, and felt something in the room shift — not resolve, exactly, not yet, but the ground firming under all of them, the way mud feels after rain.

"Then we decide," she said. "Now. Together. One voice at a time, and nobody moves until we all agree, because a split crew is worth more to Brannock than a map."

They voted with words, not hands. Tam spoke first, and surprised everyone including himself: "Sell it — no. Not to Hale. Never to Hale. But I can't burn it either, not after hearing the girl. There's a third door. We just have to find it." Ada said, "Then we don't sell and we don't burn — we *use* it. Publicly. On our terms, when we choose, not when Brannock corners us." Marisol gave her vote last, and it cost her: "Burn it. I still say burn it. But I'll hold my fire if we agree — *agree*, all of us — that it never gets traded. Not for money, not for names, not for safety. The moment it becomes currency, we burn it that same hour. Swear it."

They swore it. Four voices in a lamplit cabin, over a dead man's iron box, while the river ran dark and patient outside.

Jude rose last. She picked up the strongbox, and Marisol watched her weigh it — not the metal, but the meaning — and then carry it to the stern and lower it into the bilge locker beneath the floorboards, deep in the heart of the boat, where the whole hull would have to come apart before anyone reached it.

"It stays with the ship," Jude said. "And the ship stays with us."

* * *

Marisol was scrubbing the mugs when the sound came.

A light, far-off across the water — engines, more than one, running without lights. She froze with her hands in the dishwater and listened the way her mother had taught her to listen in another country, another life: not to the sound, but to the space between sounds.

Two engines. Maybe three. Slow, disciplined, holding formation. Nobody ran the night river in formation except a fleet.

She dried her hands and climbed quietly to the deck, where Jude was already standing at the wheel, motionless, a silhouette against the last gray band of sky. Ada and Tam came up behind her, and no one spoke, because there was nothing to say that the darkness hadn't already said.

"They found the narrows empty," Jude murmured at last. "Brannock knows we lied to him. And he knows we're the only ones left who know where it is."

"How long?" Ada whispered.

Jude's eyes went downstream, toward the black seam where the river met the coming dawn, and Marisol saw her captain do the arithmetic she'd been born doing — miles against hours, water against weight, courage against the falling tide.

"One tide," Jude said. "Maybe less. Get some sleep. Tomorrow we run the channel no pilot survives."

Marisol stayed on deck a moment longer than the others. Below her feet, beneath the floorboards, the strongbox rode in the dark like a heart in a chest. Above her, the first wind of the dry season moved down the valley, flattening the water, smelling of dust and endings.

She touched the rail once, lightly, the way she touched the stove each morning to know its temper, and went below to wake the stove herself. Whatever came at dawn, they would meet it fed.
