## 17. The Last Tide Begins

The tide turned at 4:17 in the morning, and Jude Lockridge felt it before she saw it — a long, low shudder through the hull of the *Kestrel*, like the river clearing its throat.

She stood alone at the wheel in the grey half-dark, hands resting on the spokes the way other people rested their hands on the shoulders of old friends. Below her feet, the engine ticked and settled as Tam let it cool for the last time. Behind her, downriver, the running lights of Brannock's gunboat hung in the murk like a string of yellow teeth.

Six tides had become five. Five had become three. And now there was this one — a window of ninety minutes, maybe less, when the water would rise high enough over the Serpent's Neck to float a boat of their draft across the bar, before the dry season pulled the channel shut like a fist closing.

No pilot had ever run the Neck at low water. The charts said so plainly, in the flat bureaucratic language of men who drew lines on paper: *Not navigable below 1.2 meters. Losses recorded: three vessels, eleven souls.* The charts did not say what Jude knew, which was that two of those losses had been pilot error and the third had been bad luck, and that the difference between the two was everything.

"Captain." Tam's voice from the engine hatch, hoarse with oil and sleeplessness. "She'll do it. But I can't promise she'll do it twice."

"She only has to do it once."

"That's what worries me."

Jude almost smiled. Tam had been saying variations of that sentence since the fuel dock at Calloway Bend, and every time, it had been true in a new way. She turned from the wheel and looked aft, where Marisol was lashing down the galley stores with the grim efficiency of a woman who had packed up her whole life more than once and intended never to be surprised by it again. Ada crouched beside the strongbox, one hand flat on its iron lid, the way you'd rest a hand on a sleeping animal you didn't entirely trust.

The box. Everything came back to the box. Vane's confession, Hale's forged deeds, the proof that an empire had been built on paper that lied — all of it riding in an iron coffin on the deck of a dying boat, while the man paid to keep it buried closed the distance behind them at eighteen knots.

"Ada," Jude said. "Below deck. Strap yourself into the pilot house bench. If we hit, I want you somewhere the river can't take you without taking the whole boat first."

"And if we don't hit?"

"Then you'll have earned the right to gloat at breakfast."

Ada held her gaze a moment longer — that level, unblinking stare she'd learned from no one and everyone, the stare of a girl who'd been raised by institutions instead of parents and had decided early not to flinch. Then she went below without another word, and Jude heard the bench straps buckle, one, two, three, deliberate as a heartbeat.

* * *

The Serpent's Neck was not a channel so much as an argument the river was having with itself. For six miles the current split around a spine of black rock and braided itself into four threads, each one lying to the charts in a different way. In flood season, a pilot could take any of them drunk and blind. In dry season, the sandbars rose like the backs of sleeping animals, shifting every night, rearranging themselves according to logic only the water understood.

Jude had spent twenty-two years learning that logic. She'd learned it the way you learn anything that matters: by being wrong, and paying for it, and going back out the next morning to be wrong differently.

Now she stood at the wheel with the chart rolled open at her elbow — not reading it, because the chart was already a lie, but holding it the way you'd hold a grudge — and watched the water.

Water told you things if you spoke its language. A smooth green tongue of current meant depth. A wrinkled seam where two flows met meant a bar underneath, growing or dying depending on the angle of the wrinkle. A boil of sediment rising from the bottom was the river digging; a glassy stillness was the river filling in. Birds told you things too — where the terns fed, the channel ran deep enough for fish, and therefore for keels. Driftwood rode the deepest thread. A single leaf, turning in place, marked an eddy behind a snag that could tear out a hull.

And the moon, low and white over the eastern bluff, told her the one thing she needed most: the tide had begun to come in, and it would come in angry, pushing against the outgoing current until the whole Neck stood up in standing waves.

They would run the slack between the tides. Fifteen minutes, maybe twenty, when the water forgot which way it was going and lay flat enough to forgive a mistake.

"Tam," she called. "Give me everything she has. Not redline — past it. I don't care what she costs us. She's costing us nothing tomorrow."

A pause. Then, from the hatch, a sound that might have been laughter or might have been a man making peace with his own sins: "You heard the captain, old girl. One last lie about your age."

The engine caught, coughed, caught again, and settled into a note Jude had never heard from it — higher, rougher, a sound like a saw biting into wet wood. The deck began to tremble. Somewhere below, something metal rattled in protest and was silenced by a hammer blow.

Marisol came forward and took her station at the bow rail, coil of line over her shoulder, sounding pole in hand. Her knuckles were white on the pole and her voice, when she spoke, was perfectly calm. "I've run rivers in three countries," she said. "None of them scared me like your face does right now."

"Good," Jude said. "Be scared. Scared pilots live."

"And what are you?"

Jude looked at the water. The first grey light was coming up over the bluffs, and the Neck lay ahead of them like a road made of mercury and broken glass.

"I'm the one who knows the way," she said.

* * *

They entered the Neck at full throttle, and the river tried to kill them inside the first minute.

It happened the way it always happened — fast, and then faster. The entrance thread looked wide and honest in the pre-dawn light, and it was a lie, because forty meters in the bottom rose to meet them and Jude hauled the wheel hard to starboard and the *Kestrel* heeled over so far that the strongbox slid the length of the deck and struck the rail with a boom that Jude felt in her teeth. Ada's voice came up through the deck boards, one word, furious and clear: *"Again?"*

"Again," Jude answered, and threaded the gap between the bar and the black rock with less than a boat-length to spare on either side. The rock passed close enough that she could see the old scar in it, pale stone where some earlier hull had ground its dying mark. She put the *Kestrel*'s bow exactly where that scar wasn't.

"Deeper!" Marisol shouted from the bow, pole thrust down. "Starboard side, deeper — no — it shoals — port! PORT!"

Jue threw the wheel. The engine screamed past redline, a sound like tearing metal, and Tam's voice rose beneath it, wordless, coaxing, begging, threatening — a man talking an animal off a ledge. The *Kestrel* came around with her stern slewing, took a wave broadside that sent green water across the deck and ripped the canvas cover off the strongbox, and then found the deep thread and ran down it like a fox going to ground.

For ninety seconds, they flew.

That was the thing nobody who hadn't done it could understand — that there were moments in the worst water, in the very throat of the danger, when the terror burned off like fog and what was left underneath was clean and bright and simple. The wheel in her hands. The water telling her its story. Her body answering before thought, the way a body answers a language learned young. Every sandbar trick she owned came up out of her one after another: reading the boils, splitting the seams, using the eddy behind the mid-channel snag as a slingshot, letting the incoming tide lift her over a bar she could never have crossed against gravity alone. She wasn't fighting the river anymore. She was dancing with something that wanted to eat her, and both of them knew the steps.

"Bar ahead!" Marisol's voice, cracking now. "Big one — moving — it's MOVING—"

A sandbar, on the make, building itself in real time as the tide pushed silt up from the bottom — a wall of boiling brown water where ten minutes ago there had been a channel. Jude's mind went cold and quick. There was no way through. There was only around, and around meant the eastern thread, the one the charts marked with a skull-and-crossbones and the words *unnavigable*, the one no pilot had taken in living memory because it narrowed to a boat's width and bent twice around standing rock.

The eastern thread or the end of the run.

She took it.

The *Kestrel* went in at a speed that made the rigging howl. The walls of the thread closed in — rock on the left, a drowned tree on the right, water the color of hammered tin — and Jude steered by instinct and by the white scars on the stones, the marks left by every flood since the beginning of the world, keeping her keel in the flood-line because the flood always knew best. First bend. She counted it in heartbeats. Second bend — too tight, too tight, the stern swinging wide, and she kicked the rudder against the swing and felt the hull kiss stone along her entire starboard side, a long grinding shudder like a tooth being pulled slow—

—and then they were through, and the Neck opened up ahead of them into the lower reach, wide and brown and empty, and the tide was coming in behind them like a hand at their backs.

Jude became aware, gradually, of sounds. The engine, ragged but alive. Water sloshing in the bilge. Tam laughing — actually laughing, a cracked, disbelieving sound from the engine hatch. And from below, muffled but unmistakable, Ada's voice: "Is it over? Is somebody going to tell me if it's over?"

"It's over," Jude called down, and her voice came out steadier than her hands.

Marisol leaned on the sounding pole at the bow rail, breathing hard, and looked back at her captain with an expression Jude couldn't read — awe and accusation in equal measure.

"You kissed the rock," Marisol said.

"I know."

"The whole side."

"I know."

"You're bleeding."

Jude looked down. Her palms were striped red where the wheel spokes had bitten in during the second bend. She hadn't noticed. She noticed now, and found that she didn't much care. She looked astern instead, back toward the Neck, where the morning light was coming up gold on the black rock — and there, far downstream, framed in the mouth of the channel they had just survived, the gunboat's searchlight swept once across the water and went dark.

Brannock had reached the entrance. And stopped.

Jude understood why, and the understanding settled over her cold and complete. He wouldn't run the Neck. Not at low water, not with a fleet vessel, not with Hale aboard watching. He would go around — the long way, the sea way, twelve hours lost — or he would wait at the channel mouth for whatever came out the other side.

Which meant they had bought time. Hours of it. Maybe enough.

Maybe.

"Tam," she said quietly. "How is she?"

A long silence from the hatch. When Tam finally answered, the laughter was gone from his voice, replaced by something older and wearier.

"She's alive, Captain. But she's got a knock in her I don't like, and she's drinking fuel like it's the last drink on earth." A pause. "Ask me again in an hour and I'll give you a different answer."

"Then don't ask me to ask you." Jude turned the wheel gently, bringing the *Kestrel* around the last bend toward open delta water, and the wind came up fresh off the sea flats and filled the pilothouse with the smell of salt and distance. Ahead, the horizon lay unbroken — grey water, grey sky, and between them a thin bright line that might have been the edge of the world.

Behind them, faint as a memory, she heard the gunboat's engines turn, and turn away.

They had beaten the Neck. They had beaten the tide. She allowed herself exactly ten seconds of that knowledge — ten seconds, no more, counting them off against the wheel spokes like a debt being paid — and then she looked down at the strongbox, sitting bare on the scoured deck with the morning sun glinting off its iron lid, and at the thin ribbon of smoke beginning to rise, very faintly, from the engine hatch.

The horizon was close now. So was everything else.

"Hold on," she said, to no one and everyone. "It isn't finished yet."
