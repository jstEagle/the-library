## 19. What Power Wants

The water came aboard like a guest who had stopped pretending to be polite.

It slid across the deck in a thin bright sheet, found every low place, pooled around Jude's boots, and kept rising with the patience of something that had all the time in the world and knew she didn't. The *Kestrel* lay canted against the channel mouth's last sandbar, her stern half-swallowed by the flood current that had carried them through the collapsing channel — through it, barely, gloriously — before Brannock's gunboat had put his iron bow into her hull just aft of the wheelhouse.

Tam was below, or had been. Marisol had Ada by the arm at the rail. And Corvus Hale stood on the foredeck of his seaplane tender not ten yards away, hands clasped behind his back like a man admiring a garden, while his crew trained rifles on everything Jude loved.

"Captain Lockridge," Hale said.

The name went through her like a fishhook finding old scar tissue. She hadn't heard it spoken aloud in eleven years. She'd said it herself only once since — to her own crew, in the lamplight of the galley, three nights ago, when Marisol made them all say their truths out loud. It still didn't fit in her mouth. In Hale's mouth it fit perfectly, the way a key fits a lock it was cut for.

"Not Lockridge," she said. "Just the captain."

Hale smiled. He was smaller than she'd imagined from the stories, and older — silver at the temples, a face built for boardrooms rather than gunboats, soft-handed and precise. But the eyes were exactly as advertised. They were eyes that had never once been told *no* by anything they couldn't buy, drown, or bury.

"The *Kestrel*, though." He tilted his head, reading her boat the way Tam read an engine — with a mechanic's frank appraisal. "Lockridge hull design. Third generation. Your father's yard built forty-one of them before the banks took it all away. I bought the molds myself, afterward. Sentimental, I suppose." He stepped to the rail of his tender, unhurried, as the flood current dragged both vessels slowly toward open delta water. "You're taking on water faster than you're taking on sense, Captain. Let's talk."

"There's nothing to talk about."

"There's always something to talk about. That's the first lesson of shipping." He gestured, and one of his men passed him a leather case, which he opened on the rail like a man showing cards he already knew would win. "Your engineer is bailing with a bucket. Your cook is holding your pilot's daughter—" he paused delicately, "—your *passenger*, rather, quite close to a rail she could be removed from. Your hull has perhaps twenty minutes. And I have a seaplane, two gunboats, and thirty years of patience. So let us skip the part where you pretend you have leverage."

The current shouldered the *Kestrel*. Jude felt it in her knees, in the wheelhouse behind her, in the groan of the deck plates. Water sloshed over her boot tops, warm and brown and indifferent.

"You've read the papers," Hale said. "Or your girl has. Don't look surprised — Vane was my partner once; I know how he thinks, even dead. Deeds, manifests, letters of incorporation. Proof that the Meridian routes were mine before they were ever 'granted,' proof that the grants were purchased, not earned. Proof that certain other families helped me purchase them." His voice did something almost gentle. "Including yours."

"I know what's in the box."

"Do you?" He studied her. "Then you know it doesn't just hang me, Captain. It hangs the whole founding generation — the banks, the commissioners, the Lockridges. Your father signed those documents with the same pen I did. Burn the box and you burn your own name with mine. Hand it to me, and—" he turned the leather case toward her, and inside, bank drafts sat in neat clipped rows, figures with so many zeros they looked less like money than like architecture, "—and no one ever has to know what's in it at all. Not the commissions. Not the newspapers. Not the girl."

"She's not for sale."

"Everyone is for sale. Some people are simply waiting for the right currency." He said it without cruelty, which was somehow worse. "I'm offering you more than money, Captain. I'm offering you the name back. Lockridge Shipping, rechartered, your signature on the letterhead where it always belonged. The house your grandfather built. The yard your father lost. Everything the river took from you, returned — if you hand me a box of rotting paper and let me sink it quietly where no commission will ever dredge it up. You lose nothing but weight. I lose nothing but fear. Everyone sails home rich."

The water was at her shins now. Behind her, she heard Tam come up the companionway, coughing bilge stink, and stop when he saw the rifles. She heard Marisol say something low to Ada. She didn't turn around. Turning around would have meant looking at Ada, and she couldn't do that yet, because she knew — with the terrible clarity of a pilot reading water she'd sworn she knew — that some small drowned part of her was listening to Hale the way a starving man listens to a dinner bell.

*The name back.*

She was nine years old the first time she signed her name to company stationery, standing on a chair in her father's office so she could reach the desk, her father's hand warm on her shoulder, his voice full of a pride she'd spent her whole childhood trying to deserve. *One day this will all be yours, Jude.* She had believed, then, that the name was a thing of substance — oak and iron and river-right-of-way. It had taken her until the night she burned the inheritance papers to understand that the name was also a ledger, and that every entry in it had been written in someone else's ink.

Her father had stood in rooms like this one. Not with guns — he'd never needed guns; he'd had contracts — but with the same soft certainty, the same arithmetic of everyone-has-a-price. He'd sold the delta routes knowing what the sale meant for the pilots who worked them. He'd signed the documents in the strongbox with a steady hand and slept soundly after. And when Jude had asked him why, once, only once, he'd looked at her with those patient boardroom eyes and said: *Because power isn't taken, Judith. It's maintained. Someone has to keep the river running.*

He'd genuinely believed it. That was the horror of it. Not a monster — a maintainer. A man who confused ownership with stewardship right up until the day the banks maintained *him* straight out of everything.

And here was Corvus Hale, offering her the same trade her father had made, dressed in kinder language, and here was the starving part of her, the nine-year-old on the chair, actually *listening*.

"Captain," Hale said softly. "You're thinking about it. Good. Honest women think about it. Only liars refuse instantly."

"I'm not honest," Jude said. "Ask anyone."

But she looked down at the water sliding over her deck, and she thought about miles. She had measured her whole life in miles because miles asked nothing of you except that you keep moving through them. Miles didn't care what your grandfather built. A mile was the same for a Lockridge as for a nobody; the river ran under every hull equally, and it was the only judge she'd ever trusted. Every mile she'd piloted since the fire had been a mile walked away from that chair, that desk, that warm hand on her shoulder. Eleven years of them. Thousands.

And now a man with soft hands wanted to sell her the whole distance back in exchange for one box.

"No," Jude said.

Hale sighed, genuinely regretful, a man disappointed in a fine vintage. "Captain—"

"You want to know what I see when I look at you?" She stepped forward, water streaming off her trousers, and the nearest rifleman shifted, and she ignored him, because the *Kestrel* was sinking under her feet and there wasn't time left in the world to be careful. "I see my father. Same arithmetic. Same patient voice. Same belief that everything floats and everything's for sale and nobody ever really drowns if they can afford the salvage." She shook her head. "My father offered me this exact deal once. Not in words. In a whole life of words. And I burned it. I burned it in a coal scuttle in his own office, and I walked to the river, and I have never once regretted the walk."

"The name is not a burden, Captain. It's an inheritance."

"It's a debt," Jude said. "And I paid it off in miles."

Something moved behind Hale's eyes — not anger. Calculation. The recalculation of a man who has just learned the price was wrong and is already drafting the next offer. And Jude understood, with a coldness that had nothing to do with the water, that he would never stop. Not today, not with the box, not ever. Men like Hale didn't take *no*; they filed it. He would find Ada in a year, or five. He would find the mission school records, the headmaster's receipts, the loose thread of a nineteen-year-old girl who'd memorized a fraud. He would offer her scholarships, then salaries, then silence. The box could go to the bottom of the world and the arithmetic would go on, because the arithmetic was the man, and the man was still breathing.

Unless—

The idea arrived the way the best ideas always arrived for her: not as a thought but as a reading. The way you read a channel at flood stage, all at once, the whole shape of it, sandbar and chute and the single thread of deep water — and you either commit or you die. She looked at the strongbox sitting lashed to the mast base, seawater breaking white around it. She looked at the seaplane bobbing on its tender. She looked at Hale's face, wearing its patience like paint over the machinery underneath.

And she saw the one thing he could not buy back.

"Last chance, Captain," Hale said. "Name. Fortune. The girl provided for — my word on it, and my word, whatever else you believe, is iron."

"That's the problem," Jude said. "It is."

She crossed the deck. Three steps through shin-deep water, rifles tracking her, Marisol calling her name, Ada's voice cracking on it — *Jude, don't* — and she knelt at the mast base and got her fingers under the strongbox's wet lashings.

Hale's smile flickered. For the first time, uncertainty crossed it, quick as a bird's shadow. "What are you doing?"

"What my father never had the nerve to do," Jude said.

She hauled the box up. It was heavier than sin and colder than the bottom of the river, and the flood current grabbed it the moment it cleared the deck, tugging at it like the delta itself reaching up to take what had always belonged to the water. Beyond the channel mouth, past the bar, the deep water waited — sixty fathoms of black, cold, tide-scoured nothing, deeper than any diver Hale could hire, deeper than any salvage crane his fleet could float, deeper than money.

Brannock shouted an order. Rifles swung. Ada screamed.

And Jude Lockridge — heir to nothing, captain of a sinking boat, keeper of a dead man's promise — lifted the entire fraudulent history of the Hale empire above her head, and held it there, trembling, over the rail, over the current, over the only honest judge left on the river.

"Shoot," she said, "and we all find out what's in it when the commission drags it up anyway. Or watch."

Corvus Hale went very still, and for the first time in thirty years, the most powerful man on the delta looked at open water and saw something he couldn't own.

The box swayed in her grip. Her arms burned. The *Kestrel* groaned beneath her, settling, giving up.

And Jude smiled at him — the same smile her father used to give losing opponents across a negotiating table — and opened her fingers.
