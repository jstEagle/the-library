## 20. Sunk Proofs

The channel mouth opened before them like a held breath let go.

Jude stood at the wheel with her hands aching from the run — the collapsing channel behind them still shuddering in her bones, the memory of sandbars sliding under the keel like animals fleeing a fire. The *Kestrel* had taken the last tide and won it by the width of a prayer. Ahead, the water widened into the lower delta, where the river forgot itself and became sea, and beyond that, gray-green to the edge of sight, open water over the deep trench the charts called the Hollows.

Brannock's gunboat came out of the smoke astern, listing slightly, its bow scarred white where it had struck them at the channel mouth. Behind it, two more of Hale's fleet crawled out of the haze like wolves deciding whether the wounded thing ahead was worth the cost.

But the *Kestrel* was not wounded. Not yet. And Jude had one thing left to do.

"Tam," she said. "Full ahead. The Hollows."

Tam looked at her from the engine hatch, grease to his elbows, his face streaked with sweat and soot and something older than either. He didn't ask why. He'd been reading documents for two weeks; he knew what rode in that strongbox better than she did. He just nodded once and disappeared below, and a moment later the old engine gathered itself and answered, hammering out its rhythm like a heart that had decided, finally, what it wanted.

Ada stood at the rail beside the strongbox. She had carried it through the collision, through the chaos, through Brannock's boarding hooks and Marisol's thrown lantern and the terrible arithmetic of the last three hours, and now she stood with both hands flat on its lid as if it were a coffin and she was keeping vigil.

"You're really going to do it," Ada said. It wasn't a question. Her voice had gone quiet in the way water goes quiet before it drops over a falls.

Jude kept her eyes on the channel ahead. "You could stop me."

"I know." A long pause. Wind pulled at Ada's hair, tore loose strands across her face. "I memorized everything, Jude. Every name. Every date. Every forged signature. It's all up here." She tapped her temple once, hard, like knocking on a door. "So sink it. I don't need paper."

"I know you don't." Jude glanced at her then — just once — and found the girl looking not at her but at the horizon, jaw set, eyes bright and dry in the way that meant she had done her crying somewhere private and wasn't going to offer anyone the evidence. Nineteen years old and already harder than Jude had been at thirty. Elias Vane's blood, no mistake about it. "That's why I can."

Marisol came up from below with the medical kit she hadn't needed and set it down anyway, because Marisol always brought things whether they were needed or not — extra rice, spare fuses, second chances. She looked at the strongbox, then at Jude, and said nothing, which from Marisol was a whole sermon.

The delta opened around them. Mudbanks gave way to reed islands, reed islands to open braided water, the channels splitting and merging like arguments, until there was no bank close enough to touch and the sky came down to meet the water in a seam of pale silver. The wind arrived with the dry season the way it always did this time of year — not gradually, but all at once, a wall of hot air off the interior flattening the river's surface into long dull sheets, erasing wakes, erasing tracks, erasing everything soft.

Behind them, the gunboat's engines changed pitch. Gaining.

"Hold course," Jude said, though no one had suggested otherwise.

* * *

The Hollows showed on the chart as a smudge of blue so dark the cartographer's ink had nearly bled through the paper. Sixty fathoms, the old survey claimed, though the survey was forty years old and honest men rarely surveyed places like that twice. Cold water down there. Black water. Water where nothing rusted slowly enough to be found and everything sank past the reach of rope and light and greed.

Jude had grown up on charts. She had learned to read them before she learned to read scripture, and her father had laughed at that and said, *Good girl, the river will save you faster than God.* She had believed him. Then she had read the ledgers in his study at seventeen — the ones with Hale's early signatures in them, the ones that showed the Lockridge line buying the delta routes with money that was never earned and deeds that were never true — and she had understood that her father had never meant the river would save her. He had meant it would carry her, the way it carried everything: cargo, lies, bodies, all the same freight, all the same fare.

She cut the engine to half. The gunboat was closing — a mile, maybe less — and through the dry-season glare she could see figures on its deck, and at the bow, motionless as a fence post, the shape of Brannock with his field glasses raised.

"He'll ram us again," Tam said, coming up wiping his hands. "We're slower loaded. He knows it."

"He won't ram us here." Jude nodded at the water ahead, at the place where the color changed from green to a blue so deep it looked like a hole in the world. "Nobody rams anybody over the Hollows. One wrong move and everybody's cargo."

"That's supposed to make me feel better?"

"It's supposed to make you hold the rail."

Ada laughed — short, sharp, surprised out of her — and the sound did something to Jude's chest that she chose not to examine. There would be time for examining later, or there wouldn't. Out here there was only the next minute, and the next, and the box.

Hale's seaplane came in low an hour ago — no. That was before the channel. Before the run. But even now, circling high above the delta mouth, she could see it: a silver speck riding the thermals, watching, too far to land, too proud to leave. Corvus Hale himself, come to see his empire's proof delivered or destroyed, reduced to a man in a window watching a woman in a wheelhouse make a decision he couldn't buy.

The gunboat slowed. Held off at four hundred yards, engines idling, a bull bellowing without sound across the water. Through the glass Jude could see Brannock lift something — not a weapon. A speaking trumpet.

*"Lockridge!"* The voice came flattened and strange across the wind. *"Last offer stands! Whatever you think you're doing out there — it changes nothing! Paper sinks, names don't!"*

Jude took the trumpet from its bracket by the wheel. Her hands were steady. That surprised her most of all — after the channel, after the collision, after everything, her hands had gone quiet as tide pools.

"You're right about one thing, Brannock," she called back. "Paper sinks."

She turned to the crew.

Tam stood with his arms crossed, soot to the shoulder, watching her with the expression of a man who has already argued every side of a thing and arrived at peace with none of them. Marisol had her rosary out, though she wasn't counting it; she was just holding it, letting the beads run through her fingers like water. Ada had both hands on the strongbox lid and her eyes closed, lips moving — not prayer, Jude thought. Recitation. Names. Dates. Signatures. Her father's whole confession, filed away in the vault behind her ribs where no salvage fleet would ever dive.

"Last chance," Jude said. "Anybody. Once it goes, it goes. No selling it, no trading it, no using it. Not for the Lockridge name. Not for anything."

"The name was never yours to sell," Marisol said softly. "It was theirs. You gave it back years ago, capitana. This is just the receipt."

Tam grunted — his version of agreement, of blessing, of goodbye to a thing. "Do it before I talk myself into being sensible."

Ada opened her eyes. She looked at the box for a long moment, and then she stepped back from it, and her hands, when they left the lid, left marks in the dust.

"Do it," she said. "For him. For whatever he was trying to be at the end." Her voice cracked on the last word and she let it, and did not look away from Jude while it happened, and Jude loved her for that — for the refusal to hide, which was the one inheritance Elias Vane had actually managed to deliver.

Jude bent and lifted the strongbox.

It was heavier than iron had any right to be. It was heavy with more than metal — with deeds and dates and the forged signatures of two empires, with her father's handwriting and Hale's ambition and Elias Vane's guilt, with nineteen years of a girl's abandonment and sixty years of a river's patience. It was heavy the way the past is heavy, which is to say: impossibly, and only for as long as you insist on carrying it.

She carried it to the stern rail.

The gunboat surged forward — she heard the engines bite, saw the bow wave stand up white — and Brannock's voice cracked across the water again, no longer offering, now threatening, and somewhere above the seaplane tilted and began to descend, too late, always too late, Hale arriving at the end of every story just in time to watch it finish without him.

"Jude—" Tam said.

"I know."

"Jude, the tide—"

"I know, Tam."

She swung the box once, twice, feeling its weight write itself into her shoulders, her back, the whole long ledger of her life — and on the third swing she let it go.

It turned over in the air. For one suspended moment the morning light caught the brass fittings and flared, and the strongbox hung against the pale sky like a small dark sun, and Jude thought, absurdly, of her father's study, of the ledgers lying open on the desk, of the smell of pipe smoke and varnish and money, of the girl she had been standing in that doorway with a candle, deciding.

Then the water took it.

The entry was clean — barely a splash, a white ring expanding outward and gone, swallowed by the flat bronze sheet of the Hollows. Sixty fathoms. Maybe more. Down there the cold was absolute and the dark was permanent and nothing that sank ever came back up, not for love, not for money, not for Hale's entire fleet working till the end of the age. The deeds were beyond salvage. The fraud was beyond proof. The name Lockridge was beyond restoration, and the name Vane beyond vindication, and every fortune those papers could have built or burned or bought was now the property of the black water, which had never once, in all the history of the world, spent a dollar.

Jude gripped the rail and watched the ring fade and felt —

Nothing. Then everything. Then nothing again, the way a bell rings after the strike.

It was not relief. Relief was lighter than this. It was closer to amputation — the sudden absence of a weight she had carried so long she'd forgotten it was attached to her. The documents were gone. The proof was gone. The chance to clear her family's name, to restore the Lockridges to honor with the same paper that had damned them — gone, sunk past any diver's depth, past any price, past any argument she might have made to herself on some weaker morning. She had done the one thing that could not be undone, and she stood at the rail of her own boat and felt the terrifying, bottomless freedom of it, like stepping off a ledge and finding out the air holds.

"We're going to have to run," Tam said quietly. "They're coming about."

"No, they're not."

And they weren't. The gunboat had stopped — dead in the water, four hundred yards off, its bow wave dying into ripples. On its deck, figures clustered at the rail, looking down at the empty water, at the ring already vanished, at the place where an empire's founding document had just become fish habitat. Nobody shouted. Nobody fired. What was there to shoot? You cannot threaten a woman who has already thrown away the thing you want. You cannot bargain with someone who has removed the bargaining table from the face of the earth.

Above, the seaplane leveled off, circled once, twice — and then turned, slow and deliberate as a closing book, and climbed away north toward the city and the towers and the boardrooms where men like Corvus Hale would spend the rest of their lives wondering what had been in that box, and never knowing, and never being able to prove anything, ever again.

Because that was the thing Jude understood, standing there with the wind flattening the world behind her: doubt was worse than scandal. Proof could be fought. Proof could be bought, buried, spun, survived. But the possibility — the endless, unprovable possibility that the box had held everything, that it was down there still, sixty fathoms down, waiting — that would sit in Hale's chest like a hook for the rest of his days. She hadn't destroyed his empire. She had done something far crueler. She had made him uncertain of it forever.

"Captain," Marisol said, and there was something in her voice Jude had never heard before — not respect exactly, something older, something from the deep water of things. "The wind's turning. We should run for the estuary before it flattens the channel."

Jude looked astern. She was right — the dry season wind was building, driving long ragged lines of whitecap down from the northwest, and the shallow channel they'd nearly died running that morning was already beginning to smooth over, silt moving, bars shifting, the river rearranging its furniture for the season. In an hour it would be unrecognizable. In a day, impassable. The river was closing its own account, erasing the whole chase — the markers, the waypoints, the wreck of the Meridian Star under its collapsed bluff — folding it all away into mud and murk until next year's flood, when the water would rise and remember nothing.

Behind them, Hale's gunboats were turning, one by one, like weather vanes surrendering to the same wind. Retreating. Not beaten — Hale's people were never beaten, they were merely redirected — but finished here, finished with this stretch of water, this season, this woman who had chosen a riverboat over an empire and made the choice stick with sixty fathoms of cold black silence.

"They're going," Ada said. She sounded young suddenly. Younger than nineteen. "They're actually going."

"They were always going to," Jude said. "Men like that don't fight for things. They acquire them. And you can't acquire the bottom of the Hollows."

Tam laughed — a real laugh, rusty, startled out of him like a bird from cover. Marisol crossed herself, kissed her beads, and went below to put water on for coffee, because in Marisol's theology every miracle deserved a pot of coffee afterward, and this, whatever else it was, qualified.

Jude stayed at the wheel.

The wind came down the delta in earnest now, hot and dry and smelling of dust and distant grass, pressing the river flat, pressing the whole day flat, smoothing away wakes and tracks and the white ring where a strongbox had gone down. She felt it on her face and in her chest, and underneath the exhaustion, underneath the ache in her shoulders where the box's weight had written itself and was now, stroke by stroke, being erased — underneath all of it, she felt something she had not felt since the night she burned her inheritance papers and walked down to the water with nothing but a duffel bag and her father's fury ringing in her ears.

She felt finished. Not ended — finished. The way a sentence is finished. The way a promise is finished.

Elias Vane had asked her for one thing: find my daughter, give her what's hers. He had probably meant the map. Probably meant the proof, the justice, the reckoning — the letter of it, the paper of it. Jude had given her something else instead. She had given Ada the truth, memorized and untouchable, locked in the one vault no fleet could raid. She had given her a boat, a crew, a place at the rail. She had given her the sight of an old captain choosing the water over the gold, so that whenever Ada faced her own version of that choice — and she would, soon, with her testimony and the commission and all the slow dangerous work of telling truth on her own terms — she would know it could be done. That someone had done it. That the air holds.

Not the promise kept to the letter. Kept to the spirit. Which was, Jude suspected, the only way promises to dying men ever really get kept.

"Course, Captain?" Tam called up from the hatch, hand on the throttle, ready.

Jude looked ahead. The delta spread out before them, braided and silver and wide, and beyond it the estuary, and beyond that the open coast where the river finally admitted it had been the sea all along. The dry season wind ran down all of it in long flat sheets, erasing, smoothing, beginning the year's great forgetting. Somewhere behind them the Meridian Star slept under its bluff. Somewhere below them the strongbox settled into the cold. Somewhere ahead — always ahead — the horizon.

"Nor'east," Jude said. "Main channel. Let's see how many miles we've got left in this old girl before the season shuts us down."

The engine answered. The *Kestrel* leaned into it, and the river took them, and the miles began.

At the rail, quiet as a new crew member, Ada Vane watched the water slide by — and then reached into her coat pocket and drew out a folded scrap of paper, creased and worn, and smoothed it once against her knee, and did not show it to anyone.

Jude saw. Jude said nothing.

Some cargo, she had learned, you carry without asking what it weighs.
