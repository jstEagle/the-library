## 21. Ada's Inheritance

The morning after the sinking, the river looked like it had swallowed a secret and was pretending to digest it.

Ada stood at the rail of the *Kestrel's Grace* — Jude's boat, though Jude never called it hers, only "the boat," as if naming it would be a kind of ownership the river hadn't authorized — and watched the brown water slide past the hull. Somewhere behind them, miles back and fathoms down, lay a strongbox full of paper that could have bought a fleet, or toppled one. Corvus Hale's seaplane had lifted off at dawn, a silver insect climbing into the haze, and the gunboats had turned like dogs called home. The channel was theirs again. The channel had always been theirs; they had only borrowed their fear of it.

Her hands still smelled of salt and rope. She had held that box over open water with both arms shaking and her voice steady, and she had meant every word of the threat, and she had never once intended to drop it — because dropping it would have been a waste, and Ada Vane did not waste things. Not water, not words, not the truth.

Not anymore.

"You're up early." Marisol came up beside her with two tin mugs, steam curling off them like small ghosts. She handed one over without asking, the way she did everything — as if belonging were not something you requested but something you simply assumed.

"I didn't sleep much."

"Nobody did." Marisol leaned on the rail. "Tam's already got the engine apart. He talks to it when he thinks no one's listening. Told it it did good."

"Did it?"

"It did enough." Marisol sipped her coffee. "That's the whole philosophy of this boat, girl. Enough. You'll learn it."

Ada wrapped her hands around the mug. The heat bit pleasantly at her fingers. "Marisol. Can I ask you something?"

"You're going to anyway."

"When you left your family. When you ran." She had heard the story in the oxbow slough, in the storm, wrapped in tarps and lamplight — the estate, the name that opened doors and closed mouths, the night Marisol had walked out with one bag and never looked back. "Did you ever want to go back? Not to them. Just — to what you'd have had."

Marisol was quiet for a long moment. The river made its endless commentary beneath them, water worrying at the hull, the creak of timber like an old man settling into a chair.

"I wanted the *not-having* to stop hurting," she said at last. "That's different. The wanting-to-go-back wears off. The not-having takes longer." She looked at Ada sideways, and her eyes were kind and unsentimental at once. "You're not them, you know. Whatever's in your blood. You're what you did yesterday. And yesterday you stood on a sinking deck and out-stubborned Corvus Hale."

"He had a seaplane."

"You had a strongbox and a river. I know which one I'd bet on." Marisol drained her mug and set it on the rail. "Jude's in the wheelhouse. Been there since before light. Go on. She's been circling you like a gull round a fish since we cleared the narrows."

* * *

The wheelhouse smelled of engine oil and old coffee and, faintly, of the river itself — that green, mineral breath that got into everything aboard and never left. Jude stood at the wheel with her hands resting on the spokes, not steering so much as conversing with the water, small corrections, small concessions. She didn't turn when Ada came in. Jude never seemed to need to turn; she tracked people by sound, by the shift of weight on the deck plates, the way a pilot tracks a sandbar by the color of the current over it.

"You held the box," Jude said. "Over the rail. With the flood current under it."

"Yes."

"You could have dropped it. It would have been over. Hale would have had nothing to chase and nothing to fear."

"I know."

"And you didn't."

Ada set her mug on the chart table. "No. I didn't."

Jude did turn then, and her eyes in the grey morning light were the color of the channel at low water — patient, unreadable, and underneath, something moving. "Why?"

"Because everyone keeps deciding what the truth is worth," Ada said. "Hale thinks it's worth a fortune. Brannock thinks it's worth our lives. My father thought it was worth —" her voice caught, and she let it, because she was done swallowing things — "he thought it was worth my whole childhood. And you thought it was worth nothing, so you threw it away." She took a breath. "You all decided. Every one of you. And nobody asked the truth what *it* wanted. Nobody asked the girl whose father wrote the letter."

The wheelhouse was very quiet. Below them, Tam's voice rose faintly from the engine room, murmuring encouragement to machinery.

"Say what you mean, Ada."

"I mean this." She reached into her jacket — the same jacket she'd hidden the map inside the stovepipe with, the jacket that had survived an inspection, a ramming, a sinking's worth of spray — and drew out a small oilcloth packet, tied with waxed twine. She set it on the chart table between them like a card played face-up. "I memorized it. All of it. The night you found the documents in the map's backing, before we ever read them together — I read them first, alone, by lamp, and I put every name, every date, every figure into my head. The forged survey. The transfer dates. The signatures that don't match. The way Hale's claim to the delta routes rests on a deed signed by a man who was dead eleven days before the ink was dry. All of it. I can recite it. I've been reciting it in my sleep for weeks."

Jude stared at the packet. "You never said."

"You never asked what I was carrying. You asked what the *box* was carrying. Everyone did. That was the whole problem with this river, Jude — everyone kept looking at the paper." She tapped her own temple. "It's in here. Fire can't have it. Water can't have it. Brannock can't search for it, because there's nothing to find. I am the strongbox now. I'm the one thing Hale can't buy, can't sink, can't threaten — because you can't take a memory out of a girl's head without making her a martyr, and martyrs testify louder than witnesses."

Something moved through Jude's face — not surprise exactly; recognition. The look of a woman seeing her own younger self and being glad, this once, of the resemblance.

"You could sell it," Jude said quietly. "What you know. Hale would pay more than the box was worth, to be sure."

"I could. And then I'd be my father, trading my daughter's future for a clean conscience." Ada shook her head. "Or I could blackmail him, and I'd be Brannock with a better vocabulary. Or I could publish it tomorrow, and it would be a scandal for a season, and Hale's lawyers would drown it in procedure, and nothing would change except that I'd have spent my one shot." She looked out the wheelhouse window at the brown water, the far green blur of the delta willows. "There's a river commission sitting in the capital this winter. Hearing on delta trade routes. Public testimony, under oath, on the record. I've read about it in every riverport paper since Port Meridian. I'm nineteen. I'm nobody. And I know things that could unmake the largest shipping concern on this water — not as gossip, but as *evidence*, sworn and dated and mine."

"And when you're ready," Jude said. It wasn't a question. It was the closest thing to approval Ada had ever heard her give.

"When I'm ready. On my terms. With a lawyer I trust and a transcript I've read and a life that doesn't depend on the outcome." She turned back. "That's the difference between me and my father, and me and Hale, and — I'm sorry, Jude — me and you. They all spent other people's futures buying their own peace. I'm going to spend mine *deliberately*. That's my inheritance. Not the money he never sent. Not the name I was born with. This." She tapped her temple again. "A voice. Held until it can actually be heard."

* * *

They anchored that evening in a cut behind a willow island, the first safe water in two days, and Jude did something she almost never did: she broke out the good lamp, the brass one with the cracked chimney, and set it on the deck table. Marisol cooked the rice she saved for occasions, heavy with pepper and the last of the dried fish, and Tam came up from the engine room with grease to his elbows and an expression of cautious festivity, like a man who suspected the celebration might be premature but was willing to be wrong.

They ate. And somewhere in the middle of the meal, in the lamplight with the moths ticking softly against the glass, Ada told them the rest.

Not the documents. They'd heard enough of the documents. She told them about the letter — Vane's final letter, the one she had read alone in the hold while the *Kestrel's Grace* took on water, the one she had told no one about. How her father hadn't abandoned her by accident. How he had sold his share of the fraud — his dirty, complicit, profitable share — and used every coin of it to fund his search for the proof that would undo it. How he had left her poor *on purpose*, because a poor, forgotten daughter was a daughter Hale would never bother to find, never bother to fear, never bother to make disappear.

"He traded me for my safety," Ada said. "He decided my whole life for me, to protect it. Same as Hale decides. Same as —" she glanced at Jude, and Jude held her gaze, and didn't flinch, and Ada loved her a little for that — "same as anyone with power does, when they forget that the people they're protecting get a vote."

The lamp hissed. The river muttered against the hull.

"I hated him for years," Ada went on. "Every mission-school supper, every Christmas with no parcel, every time the headmaster said *your father will provide* and I knew he was lying — I hated him. And now I know he was down in the silt and the dark, diving on a wreck for twenty years to buy back one bad choice, and he was keeping me poor to keep me *alive*." She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, angry at the tears and refusing to apologize for them. "It doesn't make it all right. It doesn't give him back the birthdays. But I understand it. And I forgive him. Not because he deserves it — because *I* do. Because I can't testify clean with hate in my mouth. They'd hear it. They'd use it. And I want —" her voice steadied, found its footing like a boot finding a gunwale — "I want to walk into that commission room with nothing in me but the facts. That's the only way it works. That's the only way *I* work."

Marisol reached across the table and took her hand, and said nothing, because Marisol understood that some silences are the loudest thing you can give a person.

Tam cleared his throat, awkward as a man in a borrowed coat. "For what it's worth," he said, "anyone who can hold a whole fraud in her head and a whole grudge in her heart and keep 'em both sorted — well. Boats need people like that."

"Is that an offer?" Ada said. "Because I'm about to make one."

She stood. The lamp threw her shadow tall against the wheelhouse glass, out over the water, and she let it.

"I've got nowhere to go that isn't behind me. The school's done — the headmaster sold us to Hale for a purse of coin, and I'll deal with that too, in time, on the record. My father's dead. My name is either worthless or dangerous, depending on who's saying it. And this boat —" she looked around the deck, at the scarred timber and the good lamp and the three faces that had become, without her permission and against every plan she'd had, the only home she had left — "this boat is the first place anyone ever asked me what *I* wanted. So. I'm asking to stay. Not as cargo. Not as a promise someone's keeping for a dead man. As crew. I can read water — Jude's seen it, I ran the channel soundings through the narrows and she didn't correct me once. I can cook worse than Marisol and better than Tam, which is a low bar but I'll clear it. And I can hold a secret like a river holds silt — quietly, and forever, until it's time to let it go downstream."

She looked at Jude. "Captain. Your boat. Your call."

Jude was silent for a long moment. Ada watched her — watched the woman who had sunk an empire's proof rather than sell it, who had run the collapsing channel when every pilot said it couldn't be run, who measured her life in miles and had just, perhaps, run out of reasons to measure it alone.

"The pay's terrible," Jude said at last.

"I know."

"The hours are worse."

"I've seen them."

"And someday soon, you're going to stand up in a hearing room in the capital and say my family's name out loud in front of lawyers, and it's going to cost me something, and I'm going to let you."

"I know," Ada said softly. "That's why it has to be you who says yes."

Jude looked at her crew. Tam grinned around his pipe. Marisol raised her coffee mug in something that was not quite a toast and entirely was.

Jude extended her hand across the table, rough-palmed, river-calloused, and Ada took it.

"Welcome aboard, Miss Vane," Jude said. "Crew signs on for the season. We'll see about the rest."

"The rest," Ada said, "is mine to decide."

* * *

Later — much later, when the lamp was out and Tam snored in the forecastle and the willow island breathed its green breath over the water — Ada stood alone at the stern rail and let the river talk to her.

She thought about inheritance. Everyone she'd ever met thought it was a thing you received — money, name, debt, ruin, passed hand to hand like a disease or a blessing. Her father had inherited guilt. Hale had inherited nothing and stolen everything. Jude had inherited a name she'd had to burn to survive.

But standing there, with the current pulling steady and patient toward the delta, toward the capital, toward a hearing room where a nineteen-year-old girl would one day stand up with a fraud held in her head like a lamp held high — standing there, Ada understood that inheritance was not a thing you received.

It was a thing you *decided*.

You looked at everything the dead and the powerful had left you — the maps and the forgeries, the money and the absence, the names that opened doors and the names that burned — and you chose which of it to carry, and which to sink, and which to speak aloud, and when, and to whom, and in what voice.

Her father had left her a letter. Hale had left her a target. The river had left her a crew.

She would take all three, and she would decide.

Downstream, far off where the willows broke, the first grey light of the wet-season dawn was coming up the water, and with it — small, patient, unmistakable — the sound of engines. Not gunboats. Something slower, heavier, official. A commission steamer, flying survey pennants, working its way up the delta with the season's first high water.

Coming toward her. Toward the truth in her head. Toward everything she had chosen to keep, and everything she had chosen, at last, to say.

Ada Vane watched it come, and did not look away, and began, quietly, to practice.
