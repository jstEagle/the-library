## 1. The River Keeps Its Receipts

The Serpent's Reach in low-water season was a liar's river—wide and calm on the surface, all its secrets held close beneath the skin of it. Paloma Fairweather knew this the way she knew the scar on her left palm: not as information but as history, earned one mistake at a time.

She stood at the wheel of the *Magpie Jay* with her boots bare against the deck planking, feeling the boat's mood come up through the soles of her feet before it ever reached the gauges. That was the first thing they never taught you at the maritime academy in Corvane Harbor—that a riverboat was an animal with opinions, and that the captain's job was less steering than listening. The Jay was telling her the channel had shifted again overnight. She could feel it in the way the hull wanted to drift left, a half-degree of reluctance that no instrument would register but that meant the sandbar below Gullwing Point had grown another arm.

"Third shoal this week," she murmured to the wheel, to the river, to nobody. "You're hoarding your silt like a miser."

The Reach answered with its usual indifference. Morning fog lay along the water in long gray ribbons, and through them the drowned forest on the eastern bank rose like the ribs of something enormous that had lain down to die a thousand years ago and was in no hurry to finish. The water was the color of strong tea, tannin-dark and opaque, and somewhere under it the old channel markers were buried to their collars. Low water stripped the river down to its arithmetic: every inch of draft mattered, every bend had teeth, and the difference between a good season and a dead one was measured in centimeters of mud.

Paloma loved it anyway. That was the trouble with her, Old Wen liked to say. She loved the river the way gamblers love cards—not wisely, not profitably, but with the whole of herself.

"Captain." Bao's voice came up the engine tube, tinny and resigned. "Number two injector's running hot again. I can baby her through the week if you keep us under eight knots."

"You always say that."

"And you always run her at nine."

"That's because you always fix her better than you promise." She leaned toward the brass speaking tube. "How bad is she really?"

A pause. In the pause she heard the engine itself, the deep uneven heartbeat of the port-side diesel, sixty years old and older than Bao by three decades, coughing on its own carbon. "Bad enough," Bao said finally. "We need a new injector pump. Real one, not salvage. And the shaft bearing's singing."

"Singing or screaming?"

"Singing. For now. Bearings scream right before they quit, and then we're a very expensive houseboat."

Paloma did the math without wanting to. A new injector pump from the yard at Vell's Landing: four hundred marks, minimum, plus the bearing if she was honest with herself, which she tried not to be before breakfast. Against that, the *Magpie Jay*'s account book, which currently showed a balance best described as aspirational. Two charter cancellations in the last month—the timber survey that had gone to a government steamer instead, the honeymooners who'd read about bandit activity upstream and decided to love each other somewhere with paved roads. The fuel bill alone was eating her alive.

"I hear you," she told the tube. "Keep her singing. Don't let her start screaming."

"Aye, Captain." A beat. "Also, we're out of the good coffee. Wen's making the chicory stuff."

"Now you're just trying to ruin my morning."

"It was already ruined. I'm just being accurate about it."

Paloma smiled despite herself and turned back to the river. Through the thinning fog she could see the Gullwing shallows ahead, a braided scatter of bars and channels that changed their minds weekly, and she brought the Jay's speed down and took them into the maze by feel and memory, threading water the color of old glass. This was the part of the work that made everything else worth it—the pure animal pleasure of reading a living river, of knowing that the third bar from the eastern bank had a spine of hardpan under its soft crown, that the cut between the second and third was lying to anyone who trusted last year's chart. The Reach kept its receipts. Every flood, every drought, every fallen tree on some nameless tributary got written down eventually, in silt and scour, and a captain who didn't keep reading would end up written down herself.

They came through clean. Of course they came through clean. Eleven years on this water.

* * *

Breakfast was chicory coffee, fried plantain, and dried fish that Suri had salted with what could only be described as vengeance. They ate in the wheelhouse because the morning air still had a chill to it and because the wheelhouse was where Paloma did her real thinking, which was to say her worrying, dressed up as navigation.

Suri sat cross-legged on the chart bench, seventeen years old and built like a whip, mending a net with quick vicious knots. She'd come aboard two years ago off a clinic barge at Saltmere, all elbows and silence, and had turned out to be the best deckhand Paloma had ever employed—which was to say the only one who'd stayed longer than a season. Her mother ran the clinic. The clinic was drowning in debt the way the Jay was drowning in deferred maintenance, and Paloma had stopped pretending she didn't know that Suri sent half her pay downstream in letters marked with a pharmacy's stamp.

Old Wen occupied the corner by the window, as he occupied every corner, permanently and with dignity. He was seventy-something, possibly eighty, possibly older; he claimed to have navigated the Reach before the last great flood redrew a third of it, and while Paloma doubted the specifics, she'd stopped doubting the general claim. He'd been a cook on cargo boats for forty years and a navigator for thirty before that, and now he was both, because the *Magpie Jay* couldn't afford to be particular about job titles. He ate his plantain in small precise bites and watched the banks go by with eyes gone pale as river fog.

"Birds are wrong today," he said.

Paloma looked up. "Wrong how?"

"Gone quiet past the Point. Usually there's herons working the shallows by now. Kingfishers on the dead snags." He nodded at the window, at the drowned forest sliding past, silent as a held breath. "Nothing. Not even the little brown ones that never shut up."

"They'll be nesting inland."

"Mm." Wen said nothing else, which from Wen was a paragraph. He went back to his plantain.

Bao came up from the engine room wiping his hands on a rag that had once been white, a broad, slow-moving man with grease worked so deep into his knuckles it looked like tattooing. He poured himself chicory, tasted it, and grimaced with the expression of a man being personally insulted by a beverage. "We're going to have to stop at Vell's Landing regardless," he said. "I can stretch the lubricant, but the filters are done. And Captain—" he lowered himself onto the bench with a groan that had become structural over the years, "—we're going to have to talk about money. Real talk. Not the kind where you say 'we're fine' and I nod."

"We're fine."

"Paloma."

"Bao."

He set down his cup. "The account's got enough for fuel to Vell's and back if the price holds. It does not have enough for an injector pump. It does not have enough for Suri's mother's clinic, which I know you know about, and which I also know you've been quietly shorting your own share to help cover, which is noble and stupid and I'd thank you to stop, because if the Jay goes down we all go down together, and then nobody's mother gets anything."

Silence in the wheelhouse. Suri's knots had stopped. Wen chewed.

"I know the numbers," Paloma said at last.

"Knowing them isn't the same as facing them."

"I've been facing them since the timber contract fell through." She stood and paced the narrow space, three steps and turn, three steps and turn, the rhythm of every hard decision she'd ever made. "There's a salvage job out of Corvane—a dredge lost off the Oxbow, insurance work, boring but paid. If I can get the broker to—"

The courier boat appeared out of the fog then, and Paloma would remember afterward how precisely it interrupted her, as though the river itself had decided the conversation needed ending.

It came upstream fast for a government hull—white, sharp-bowed, flying the Ministry pennant, the twin diesels running with the arrogant smoothness of machinery that had never once been repaired with salvaged parts. It swung alongside the *Magpie Jay* with a wash that rocked them both, and a man in a gray uniform stepped onto Paloma's deck without asking permission, which told her more about him than his face did.

He was young for the rank tabs he wore, dry-skinned and city-pale, with the look of someone who spent his days carrying things rather than deciding about them. But his eyes moved over the wheelhouse—the charts, the crew, the rust-streaked funnel—and she saw him do the same math she did, except in reverse, pricing her boat as an asset rather than a life.

"Captain Fairweather?" He had a document case chained to his wrist. Actually chained. That was new.

"Depends who's asking and why."

"Courier Second-Class Dren, Office of Territorial Survey." He produced a sealed folio, heavy paper, wax stamped with the ministry's coiled-serpent seal. "You're flagged in the registry as available for emergency charter."

"I'm flagged as *existing*. Availability depends on terms."

"The terms are generous." He said it flatly, rehearsed, but his gaze flicked to Bao's grease-black hands, to the patched canvas dodger, to the chicory pot, and Paloma watched him revise his estimate of her desperation downward, which annoyed her more than it should have. "Triple standard rate. Hazard supplement. Half upfront, wired on acceptance."

Triple rate. The number landed in the wheelhouse like a dropped anchor. Paloma felt everyone react to it—Bao going carefully still, Suri's head lifting, even Old Wen pausing mid-chew—and hated that she felt it too, a bright hot pull behind the sternum, the gambler's itch.

"What's the job?"

"Recovery operation. Survey vessel *Kestrel*, government contract, overdue at its check-in. We require a vessel with shallow draft and a master with Reach certification above Kilometer Sixty." He held out the folio. "Full particulars in the contract."

"Where's the *Kestrel* overdue?"

"Upper Reach. The flooded caves country."

Old Wen set down his plate. Very quietly, without any drama at all, he said: "Nobody runs charter above Sixty in low water. Channels aren't surveyed. Bars move daily."

"The Ministry is aware of the conditions," Dren said. "Hence the hazard supplement."

"And hence the secrecy clauses," Paloma said, and watched him blink for the first time. "You don't chain documents to your wrist and fly hazard pay for a routine recovery, Courier. What happened to the *Kestrel*?"

"That's in the contract, Captain. Along with the penalties." He offered the folio again, patient as a trap. "You'll want to read it before you answer. The Ministry requires a decision within the hour—we have other vessels to approach."

"There are no other vessels," Wen said mildly. "Not certified above Sixty. Not anymore."

Dren's composure flickered, just slightly, and Paloma understood suddenly that the young courier knew exactly how thin his options were, and that the generosity in those terms wasn't generosity at all—it was scarcity wearing a uniform.

She took the folio. It was heavier than paper should be, dense with pages, and the wax seal was unbroken and official and cold against her thumb. Triple pay. Half upfront. Enough for the injector pump, the bearing, the filters, a month of fuel, a wire to a clinic at Saltmere with real medicine in it. Enough to stop hearing Bao's careful voice in her sleep saying *bad enough*.

Enough, said a quieter voice, to make a person sign things they shouldn't.

"I'll read it," she said.

"Of course." Dren stepped back to his boat, then paused, one hand on the rail, and gave her a look that was almost apologetic. "Captain. One piece of advice, free of charge, outside my duties." His eyes went upstream, toward the fog and the drowned forest and the silent, birdless miles beyond. "Whatever you find in those pages—read the penalty schedule twice. Most people only read it once."

Then he was gone, white hull shrinking back into the gray, and the *Magpie Jay* rocked in his wake, and the river lay around them dark and close-lipped, keeping its receipts as always.

Paloma broke the seal.

* * *

She read for a long time. The others pretended not to watch her read, which fooled no one. The fog burned off. The sun climbed and laid a hard glitter across the water, and somewhere downstream a heron finally called, one harsh note, like a question.

Paloma reached the last page, set the folio down on the chart table, and looked at her crew—her strange, stubborn, unpaid-in-spirit family—and found she couldn't yet make her mouth form any of the words she'd read.

"Well," said Old Wen, into the silence. "How bad is it?"

Worse than the courier's face had promised. Better than triple pay. Both at once, which was exactly how the river liked its traps built.

"Bad enough," Paloma said, using Bao's words, meaning far more than an engine. "Let me tell you what we'd be signing."
