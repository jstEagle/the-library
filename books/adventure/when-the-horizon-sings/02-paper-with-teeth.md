## 2. Paper with Teeth

The courier's case was the color of dried blood, and it opened with a sound like a knuckle cracking.

Paloma Fairweather sat in her cabin with the lamp turned low and the contract spread across the chart table, and for a long while she did nothing but look at it. The paper was heavy stock, cream-colored, the kind that didn't come from any print shop on the Reach. It had traveled a long way to find her, and it carried its distance like a smell—ink from some government office three hundred miles downstream, stamped and sealed by hands that had never felt river mud between their toes.

Outside, the Magpie Jay creaked against her moorings at Tallow Bend, settling into the low-water season like an old woman into a chair. The river was down to its bones this time of year. Sandbars she knew by name stood exposed and pale as scars; the channel markers leaned at angles that lied about where deep water ran. Somewhere aft, Bao was arguing with his engine in the low, patient voice he used when he thought something was listening.

Paloma read the first page twice.

*Contract of Charter: Salvage and Rescue Operations, Vessel Kestrel.*

The language was clean. That was the first thing wrong with it. Government documents were usually bloated, hedged, padded with so many qualifiers they read like apologies. This one was lean as a knife. It said what it wanted in short sentences and then moved on before she could ask questions.

*The charterer agrees to proceed upstream to the last known coordinates of the survey vessel Kestrel, effect rescue or recovery of personnel and equipment, and return said personnel and equipment to the designated authority at Port Meridian.*

*Recovery of equipment is to be considered equal in priority to recovery of personnel.*

She read that line a third time. *Equal in priority.* Not secondary. Not *if practicable*. Equal. She'd run rescues before—two drownings, one fire, a party of lost geologists who'd wandered off a ridge trail chasing a mineral seam—and every contract she'd ever seen put people first, unambiguously, because that was how you kept the paperwork honest and the crews alive. Equipment could be replaced. The government knew that better than anyone; they signed off on replacing it all the time.

Equal priority meant they thought whatever was on the Kestrel couldn't be replaced.

She turned the page and found the secrecy clauses, and there they were, dense as a flock of starlings.

*The charterer shall not disclose, discuss, or otherwise communicate the nature, location, findings, or circumstances of the operation to any party not named herein, including but not limited to: family members, port authorities, other vessel operators, members of the press, or officers of any court.*

*Violation shall incur liquidated damages of forty thousand sovereigns per instance.*

Paloma set the page down flat, because her hand wanted to shake it.

Forty thousand. She looked around her cabin as if the number might be written somewhere in the woodwork—the water stain shaped like a running dog above her bunk, the brass compass her father had left her, the stack of unpaid chits rubber-banded to the lamp base. Forty thousand sovereigns was more than the Magpie Jay had grossed in the last two years combined. It was more than the boat was worth, hull, engine, and soul together. One careless sentence over drinks at the Salt Loom, one letter to a sister, one complaint filed with the harbormaster, and she wouldn't be ruined. She'd be *erased*. They'd take the boat, and then they'd take the debt the boat couldn't cover, and then they'd take the rest of her working life.

And the triple pay—the number the courier had dangled at the dock like bait on a line—suddenly looked less like generosity and more like a down payment on her silence. You didn't pay a woman three times her rate to do a job you expected her to talk about afterward.

She heard boots on the deck plates, and then Bao filled the doorway without knocking, which was his way. He was a broad, slow-moving man who took up space the way furniture did, and his face was doing something complicated.

"You've been reading it," he said.

"I've been reading it."

"And?"

"Come see for yourself."

He came in and bent over the table, reading the way he read everything—thoroughly, silently, with his lips barely moving. Paloma watched him reach the secrecy clauses and saw his jaw tighten. He reached the penalty schedule and made a sound low in his chest, like a pump starting to lose prime.

"This isn't a charter," he said. "This is a leash."

"It's a contract."

"It's a contract with teeth." He tapped the penalty page with one thick finger. "Look at the structure of it, Captain. Everything we're paid up front—half now, half on delivery. But everything we can lose comes after. They've built it so the money runs out before the danger does. We do the job, we keep our mouths shut, maybe we break even. We say one word wrong, we owe them more than we own." He straightened up and rubbed the back of his neck. "I've rebuilt engines my whole life. I know a machine designed to fail in one direction when I see one. This thing only fails toward us."

"The courier said the survey team's been dark eleven days."

"I heard."

"Eleven days, Bao. If there are people up there—" She stopped, because the sentence finished itself in a place she didn't want to go. Eleven days on the upper Reach in the dry season, with the nights already turning cold and no word sent down. People could survive eleven days. People could also stop being people in eleven days, quietly, without witnesses, and the river would keep its receipts and say nothing.

Bao was quiet for a moment. Then he said, in a different tone, "Suri's mother's clinic closes at the end of the month."

Paloma closed her eyes. Of course he'd say it. Of course he'd get there before she did, because Bao always found the soft point in any structure—it was the same instinct that let him hear a bad bearing in an engine before it screamed.

"I know," she said.

"Half up front is four months of medicine and a new roof. Suri told me yesterday, when you were ashore negotiating the mooring fees. She didn't tell you because she doesn't want it to matter." He paused. "It matters."

Everything mattered. That was the trouble with a crew—they weren't employees, they were a body, and pain traveled through a body whether you permitted it or not. Old Wen cooked their meals and read the water like scripture. Suri handled deck and lines with a grace that made the work look easier than it was, and sent half her pay downstream to a clinic in Marsh Crossing where her mother treated river fever for fishermen who paid in fish. And Bao kept a thirty-year-old engine alive with wire and stubbornness and prayers to no god in particular. Paloma held the debt of the boat alone, officially. Unofficially, they all held each other, and the interest compounded daily.

If she refused this contract, the clinic closed. If she signed it and broke silence once, the boat died. Those were the choices as they lay on the table under the lamplight, and neither of them was a choice at all, really. That was what made her certain the document had been drafted by someone who understood exactly how a crew like hers worked—who understood them the way a trapper understands a snare, by studying where the weight falls.

There was a knock—two light raps—and Suri came in without waiting, which she never did, which meant she'd been standing outside the door long enough to decide she was coming in regardless. She was nineteen and quick-eyed, with rope-callused hands she hid inside her sleeves when she was nervous, and she was nervous now.

"Is it true?" she asked. "Triple rate?"

"It's true."

"For a rescue." Her eyes went to the contract, to the government seal, back to Paloma. "For a *government* rescue. Nobody pays triple for a rescue. Rescues are charity work. Everyone knows that."

"Everyone knows that," Paloma agreed.

"So what are they paying for?" Suri asked, and the question hung there, sharp and young and exactly right, and none of the three of them answered it.

The lamp hissed. Out on the water, a night heron gave its one dry note, like a stick breaking.

"We don't have to sign," Bao said at last. "We can run cargo this season. We can be poor the honest way."

"My mother's coughing again," Suri said, very evenly, the way you carry something hot across a room. "Dr. Amma says if the clinic closes she'll keep treating people out of our kitchen, and then it'll be me nursing her instead of the other way around, and the money will run out anyway, just slower and worse." She looked at Paloma, and her chin came up. "I'm not asking you to sign for me. I'm telling you why I want you to. Those are different things, and I know the difference."

Paloma looked at her for a long moment. Nineteen years old and already fluent in the grammar of pride and need. She'd been the same at that age, standing in her father's wheelhouse, learning that the river gave nothing and the shore gave less.

"Nobody signs tonight," she said. "Sleep on it. All of us. Wen too—I want his eyes on the route terms before anything else." She gathered the pages and squared them. "First light, we talk again. And if anyone's heart isn't in it, we walk away and I'll sell the compass before I sell any of you. Understood?"

They nodded, and left, and Paloma sat alone with the contract and the lamp and the sound of the river moving in the dark, patient as ever, keeping its own counsel.

* * *

Old Wen came to her near midnight, which meant he'd waited until the others were asleep, which meant he had something to say that wasn't for the crew.

He didn't knock. He never knocked; doors were a formality the young bothered with. He folded himself onto the bench across from her, small and brown and wrinkled as a walnut, and accepted the tea she poured without thanks, because gratitude between them had been settled decades ago into something quieter.

"You've read it," he said.

"All of it."

"Then you know the strangest part isn't the penalties." He sipped. "It's the geography."

Paloma frowned. "The route terms? They're standard. Last known coordinates, salvage rights along the corridor—"

"They specify the corridor." Wen set down his cup and looked at her, and his eyes in the lamplight were the pale amber of river water at dawn. "Forty years I've run this Reach, girl. I have drowned men's names for every bend. And the corridor they've drawn—" he touched the map annex, the neat printed line running upriver past the fishing camps, past the confluence, into the high country where the Serpent narrowed and the caves began— "it avoids the old channels. Deliberately. There are stretches in here where the marked route adds hours, adds risk, runs us through water I wouldn't pole a skiff through in the dry season. Unless whoever drew this line wanted us nowhere near certain places."

"Maybe the surveyor didn't know the river."

"Government surveys know rivers fine. They buy their charts from people like me." He tapped the line once more. "This isn't ignorance, Paloma. This is avoidance. Somebody drew a fence on the water and hired you to walk inside it."

She stared at the map until the lines seemed to move. A fence on the water. She thought of the clause about equipment equal to lives. She thought of forty thousand sovereigns per word spoken. She thought of the courier's face at the dock—young, correct, sweating slightly in the heat, relieved to be handing the case over and leaving, the relief of a man passing a lit lantern down a line and walking away before it catches.

"Wen," she said slowly, "if we sign this, and we go up there, and we find out something they don't want known—what happens to us?"

"Nothing," Wen said. "As long as we find exactly what they expect to find, and nothing more, and say exactly nothing either way." He smiled, and it was an old man's smile, tired and fond and entirely without comfort. "The river keeps its receipts, child. So do governments. The difference is the river lets you read them."

* * *

They signed at first light, all four of them, because there was never really any doubt, and everyone had known it since the courier stepped aboard, and pretending otherwise for one night had been a kindness they'd allowed themselves.

Paloma signed last. Bao pressed his thumb below his mark and said nothing, but he looked at the engine hatch the way a man looks at a friend he's about to ask something hard of. Suri signed quickly, before her resolve could cool, and then went aft to check the lines twice, which was what she did with feelings too large to hold. Old Wen signed with a brush-stroke flourish that was nearly a signature and nearly a curse, and muttered something in the old river dialect that Paloma chose not to translate even to herself.

The courier met them at the dock as the mist burned off, took the countersigned copy, and paid the first half in crisp notes that smelled of ink and nothing else. He was already turning away when Paloma called after him.

"The Kestrel's crew. What are their names?"

He stopped. For just a moment his shoulders did something small and unhappy. "That information wasn't included in your packet," he said.

"I'm going to pull them out of the river. I'd like to call them something besides 'personnel.'"

The courier looked at her then, and behind the official blankness she saw a flicker of something almost human—pity, maybe, or its cousin. "Dr. Elias Voss is the senior scientist," he said. "Geologist. The rest are in the annex." He hesitated. "Captain Fairweather. The authority wants this resolved quickly and quietly. Everyone involved would prefer it that way."

"Everyone involved," Paloma repeated. "That's a lot of people for a missing survey boat."

The courier didn't answer. He went up the dock steps with the blood-colored case under his arm, and the mist took him, and Paloma stood on her deck with money in her pocket that felt heavier than any debt she'd ever carried.

Behind her, Bao fired the engine, and the Magpie Jay shuddered awake beneath her feet—a good sound, a trusted sound, the heartbeat of the only home she'd ever owned. Suri cast off the bow line. Old Wen took the wheel without being asked and turned the boat's nose upstream, toward the high country, toward the quiet villages and the missing birds and the caves nobody had paid her to think about.

The current took them. The banks began to slide past, green and indifferent.

Paloma stood at the rail with the contract's weight still in her mind and watched the Reach open ahead of them, and tried to name the feeling sitting in her chest. It wasn't fear, exactly. Fear was simple. This was the feeling of having signed something whose full text hadn't been printed yet—of knowing, with a captain's certainty about weather she couldn't yet see, that somewhere upstream the second half of the agreement was waiting for her, written in something older than ink.

"Captain," Wen said mildly, without turning from the wheel. "The birds have stopped."

She looked up. The reeds along the bank stood silent. No herons, no whistling kites, no darting swallows over the shallows. Just the water, moving fast and smooth, and a silence ahead of them that stretched upstream like a held breath.
