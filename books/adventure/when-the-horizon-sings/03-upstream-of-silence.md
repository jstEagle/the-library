## 3. Upstream of Silence

Suri learned the river from the birds before she ever learned it from charts.

Her mother had taught her that, back when Suri was small enough to ride in a woven basket lashed to the stern rail, back when her mother still had steady hands and a clinic with four walls and a tin roof that didn't leak. *The birds are the river's ledger,* she'd said, pointing at the white egrets standing their patient vigil in the shallows. *Where they gather, the water is honest. Where they won't go, you shouldn't either.*

Suri had thought of that saying all morning without knowing why, and by midday she couldn't stop thinking of it at all.

She stood at the bow rail with a coil of line in her hands she wasn't actually coiling, watching the Serpent's Reach unspool ahead of them like something being slowly poured out of the world's throat. The low-water season had stripped the river down to its bones. Sandbars showed themselves shamelessly, long pale spines of them, and the red clay banks stood exposed in terraces like the cut flesh of some enormous fruit. In the shallows, dead trees rose out of the water where last year they'd been drowned entirely, their branches bare and gray, holding nothing.

That was the first wrongness, though it took her another hour to name it: the trees were holding nothing.

On any other stretch of the Reach — on every other stretch, in three years of running this water with Paloma Fairweather — those drowned snags would be crowded. Cormorants drying their wings in black rows like laundry. Herons hunched over the current with the patience of monks. The small brown swallows that nested in the dead limbs by the hundreds and stitched the air above the water into seams. Bao called them the river's welcome committee. Old Wen called them dinner bells, because where the birds gathered, the fish gathered, and where the fish gathered, Wen's galley smelled like heaven by nightfall.

Today the snags stood empty. Every one of them. Gray branches against gray sky, holding nothing but light.

Suri told herself it was the season. Birds moved. Birds were fickle. She coiled her line and uncoiled it and coiled it again, and told herself that.

* * *

They passed the first fishing camp an hour after noon.

It was the Ondra family camp, or had been for as long as anyone could remember — a cluster of stilt houses on the eastern bank where the river bent slow and deep, with drying racks strung between the pilings and a smokehouse that perfumed the water for half a mile in either direction. Paloma always slowed there to trade. Mrs. Ondra smoked a river eel so good it had nearly caused a mutiny once, in the early days, when Old Wen caught Bao hoarding a whole one in the engine room.

The camp was empty.

Not abandoned-empty, which the river knew well enough — abandoned meant collapsed racks, torn nets, doors gone missing to salvage. This was different. The drying racks were full. Fish hung in neat rows, split and salted, cured stiff as shingles. A cooking pot sat on a cold hearth. Nets hung folded over rails in the way working people fold nets when they mean to come back tomorrow. Laundry — actual laundry, shirts and a child's dress — hung motionless on a line between two stilts, dry as paper in the windless heat.

Nobody was there.

"Paloma." Suri heard her own voice come out smaller than she intended. "Paloma, look."

Paloma came out of the wheelhouse wiping diesel off her hands, took one look at the camp, and brought the Magpie Jay around in a slow, wide arc that Suri recognized as the captain's version of caution. They drifted along the bank at mooring distance. Paloma cupped her hands and called out — the polite hail, the one that said *friends on the water* — and the word went out across the silent water and came back unchanged.

No dogs. That was what got Suri, later. Fishing camps always had dogs, and dogs announced boats from a mile off, and even if the people had gone somewhere, the dogs would have stayed and shouted at them. There were no dogs. There weren't even dog tracks in the soft bank mud below the ladder stile.

There were footprints, though. Dozens of them, all leading away from the camp — not toward the jungle road, not toward the neighboring bend where the Tovan camp lay, but straight down into the water. Into the river itself, as if forty people had simply walked in and kept walking.

"They left in a hurry," Bao said, coming up beside her. He'd shut down half his engine room to come look, which told Suri how much it bothered him; Bao did not abandon his engines for anything short of fire. He squinted at the bank through the rail. "But not panicked. Look — they took the boats? No. No boats gone. Canoes are still beached there under that tarp."

"So they walked," Suri said.

"People don't walk into a river, Suri."

"I know that, Bao."

He looked at her then, really looked, and whatever he saw in her face made him put his hand briefly on her shoulder — heavy, warm, smelling of grease and machine oil — before he went back below.

Old Wen didn't come look at the camp at all. When Suri found him afterward, he was in the galley standing very still in front of his spice shelf, and he wasn't touching anything, just looking at it, the way a man looks at his own hands when they've started to shake.

"Wen-ge?"

"The birds," he said.

"What about them?"

He turned. His face was doing something complicated, folding itself around a thought too large for it. "You're river-raised. Your mother kept the ledger, yes? So count it with me. From the second bend to here — how far?"

"Eleven miles."

"Eleven miles of the best feeding water on the whole Reach, and I haven't seen one egret. Not one. Not a cormorant, not a kingfisher, not a single swallow." He held up one finger. "In thirty-one years on this river, I have never seen eleven miles without birds. Not in flood, not in drought, not in the year of the fish kill. Birds starve before they leave water like this. So either they are all dead—"

"Or they're avoiding something," Suri finished.

Old Wen nodded slowly, and then, because he was Old Wen and grief and dread both made him cook, he turned back to his cutting board and began slicing ginger with terrible precision, and the smell filled the galley, and somehow that made everything worse instead of better.

* * *

The second village was worse than the first, because it was bigger, and because Suri knew its name.

Ket-Solong sat where the Reach met the mouth of a tributary, forty houses and a market hall and a school with a bell, a place that threw a lantern festival every spring that drew boats from sixty miles downstream. Suri had danced there once, years ago, with a boy who'd been training as a pilot himself, and she remembered the whole shoreline lit up like a spilled jewelry box.

They came around the bend at dusk and Ket-Solong did not light up.

Not one lamp. Not one cooking fire. Forty houses' worth of evening life, and nothing. The market hall stood open-fronted and dark, its stalls stacked with goods nobody had bothered to cover against the rain that hadn't come yet. The school bell hung silent in its frame. Somewhere a rooster crowed at the fading light, absurd and alone, the only voice in the whole village, and the sound of it raised the hair on Suri's arms.

Paloma didn't even slow down this time. She stood in the wheelhouse window with her jaw set in the particular way Suri had learned meant the captain was afraid and converting the fear into speed.

"We should check," Suri said. "There could be sick people. People who couldn't travel."

"With what?" Paloma didn't turn from the window. Her voice was flat and careful, a deck plate walked on gently. "Forty houses, Suri. If everyone's gone, we learn nothing except that we're alone out here. If someone's there and doesn't want to be found—" She stopped herself. Started again. "We're a charter boat with a government seal on our manifest and a deadline. We're not rescue. We keep moving."

It was reasonable. It was even probably right. Suri hated it anyway, hated the way the village slid past the rail in the blue dark, doorways gaping like open mouths, and hated more the small traitorous part of her that was relieved not to stop, not to step onto that silent shore and find out what silence meant.

She took the middle watch that night, which was a mistake, because the middle watch was when the river talked.

Normally it talked plenty. Water over gravel bars, the slap of fish, wind in the bamboo on the banks, the far-off conversation of frogs in choruses so loud the surveyors' instruments supposedly picked them up as weather. Suri loved the middle watch for exactly that reason — the river's crowd noise, proof you were never alone out here no matter how black the night got.

Tonight the river whispered.

That was the only word she could find for it, standing alone at the bow with the running lights dimmed, listening. The water made sound, yes — the mechanical sounds, the necessary ones. But underneath them, in the gaps, there was a hush that had weight to it. The frogs had stopped. Not thinned out — stopped, all of them, as if someone had drawn a line across the map behind the boat and told everything living not to cross it. The bamboo didn't rustle. Even the water seemed to move carefully, sliding past the hull with a smoothness that felt less like current and more like discretion.

And beneath the whisper, so faint she could convince herself she'd imagined it, there was a hum.

Not engine noise. Bao's engines had a signature she knew in her teeth, a two-stroke heartbeat with its own harmonics. This was lower. Slower. It didn't come from anywhere she could point to — behind them, ahead, below — it came from everywhere, or from inside the water itself, a vibration she felt in the soles of her feet through the deck plating more than heard. It rose and fell so gradually it might have been her own blood in her ears.

Except her blood didn't change pitch when she leaned over the rail and put her hand in the river.

The water was warmer than it should have been. Warm as bathwater, warm as something alive, and the hum strengthened against her palm like a cat pressing into a hand, and for one vertiginous moment Suri had the absolute certainty — irrational, total, embarrassing — that the river had noticed her. That something vast and patient on the bottom of the world had felt her fingers and turned its attention, slowly, the way a sleeping animal turns toward warmth.

She snatched her hand back. Wiped it on her trousers. Stood there with her heart slamming while the boat carried her upstream into the dark, and told herself: fever. Fatigue. Imagination. Her mother would have laughed at her, kindly, and checked her pulse anyway.

The hum faded as the night wore on, or she stopped being able to hear it, which amounted to the same thing. But her hand, when she finally slept, she slept on like a stolen thing.

* * *

Morning brought fog, and the fog brought the third sign, and the third sign broke something loose in Suri that she suspected would never quite set again.

They were creeping through it — Paloma at the wheel with Old Wen beside her calling depths from memory, because the fog on the Reach ate radar returns and laughed at sonar, and in low water the sandbars moved like slow animals — when the birds came back.

All at once. Everywhere at once.

They burst out of the fog in a panic, hundreds of them, thousands — egrets and cormorants and ducks and the brown swallows in shrieking clouds, a whole season's worth of missing birds materializing around the boat in a storm of wings. They streamed past the Magpie Jay downstream, every single one of them flying the same direction, away from upstream, away from wherever they'd been refusing to go. They beat past so close Suri felt the wind of them. One cormorant struck the wheelhouse roof with a crack like a thrown stone and tumbled, righted itself, and fled on without looking back.

Bao came up the companionway two steps at a time. "What in the—"

"Count them," Old Wen said quietly. He was standing at the rail with his face tipped up, and Suri saw that his eyes were wet, and that frightened her more than the empty villages had. "Go on. Count them, girl. Thirty-one years, and I have seen a flock flee a brushfire, and I have seen a flock flee a flood. I have never seen a river empty itself backward."

"Fleeing what?" Bao asked. "There's nothing up there. It's jungle and karst and the old caves. There's nothing for fifty miles except—"

He stopped. They all stopped, in the way of people arriving at the same door at the same moment.

The Kestrel. The survey team. The government contract folded in Paloma's cabin drawer with its secrecy clauses and its penalties, triple pay for salvage and rescue, upstream of everything, upstream of the empty camps and the silent village and the eleven birdless miles.

"You don't suppose," Bao began.

"Suppose what?" Paloma's voice came from the wheelhouse doorway. She'd heard everything; Suri could tell by the stillness of her. "Say it."

"That they didn't disappear," Bao said slowly. "That they ran. Like the birds. Like the Ondras. Whatever they found up there—" he gestured vaguely upstream, at the fog, at the whole white blindness of it, "—maybe they found it, and it found them back, and everybody who could run, ran."

Nobody answered. The last of the birds went over them in ragged skeins, dwindling downstream, and the fog closed again behind them like a curtain drawn on an empty stage.

Suri stood at the bow with her arms wrapped around herself, and thought about her mother's ledger. *Where the birds gather, the water is honest.* And the corollary her mother had never needed to say aloud, because no one had ever needed it said: where the birds will not go, something is waiting that even instinct cannot bear to name.

Three days upstream, the survey team had stopped answering their radio. Two days upstream, forty people had walked into the river rather than stay on its banks. One day upstream, the birds had broken and run.

And the Magpie Jay, triple-paid and contract-bound, was steaming steadily in the opposite direction from every living thing that could choose otherwise.

Ahead, through a thinning in the fog, Suri saw the water change color — a strange, deep, glassy green where the chart said it should be brown — and beneath her feet, faint and unmistakable now, the hum began again, rising like a held breath.

"Captain," she said, and her voice didn't shake, which surprised her. "I think we should talk about turning around."

Paloma looked at her through the wheelhouse glass for a long moment. Then she looked upstream, at the green water, and Suri watched her captain weigh the debt against the dread and watch the debt win, and hated her a little for it, and understood her completely.

"Hold your course," Paloma said. "We'll talk tonight."

Behind them, far downstream, the first thunder of the coming monsoon rolled along the horizon like something clearing its throat. Ahead, the river waited, warm and humming, patient as a trap that had never once needed to close.

The birds, Suri thought, had tried to tell them.

Nobody had listened.
