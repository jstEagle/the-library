## 4. Gear Without Ghosts

The Kestrel appeared out of the morning haze the way a lie appears in a conversation—suddenly, and with the sense that it had been waiting for you all along.

Paloma saw her first as a shape, a gray suggestion against the gray-green wall of the far bank, and for three full minutes she said nothing, because saying something would make it real, and if it wasn't real then the contract wasn't real either, and she could still turn the Magpie Jay around and go home to her debts like an honest woman.

"Bank side, port quarter," Suri said from the bow rail, where she'd been standing with her elbows on the metal for the better part of an hour, watching the water the way you watch a sleeping animal. "There. Under the dead fig."

"I see her," Paloma said.

Old Wen came up from the galley wiping his hands on his apron, which he only did when something was wrong, because when things were right he stayed below with his rice and his radio dramas and let the river do its work. He looked at the moored vessel for a long moment, then at Paloma.

"That's a government hull," he said.

"That's a government hull," Paloma agreed.

"She's sitting right."

That was the thing that put the cold in Paloma's stomach, more than anything else. The Kestrel was sitting *right*. Not aground, not listing, not hung up on a snag with her stern half-drowned the way boats die on the Reach. She was moored bow and stern to two living fig trees, lines tensioned properly, fenders out, riding easy on the current like a woman asleep who has simply decided not to wake up.

Boats in trouble look like trouble. This one looked like a decision.

"Bao," Paloma called down the engine hatch. "Kill us to a drift. I want quiet coming in."

The diesel coughed twice and died, and suddenly the Reach was all there was—the slap of water against the hull, the creak of the mooring lines somewhere ahead, the enormous wet breathing of the jungle on both banks. Somewhere upstream, so faint Paloma might have imagined it, there was a sound she had no name for. A hum, almost. Like a tuning fork touched to stone and then taken away. She filed it away with the other wrongnesses: the empty fishing camps, the missing birds, Old Wen's silence over breakfast when she'd asked him what he made of it.

They drifted in on the current, and the Kestrel grew until she was undeniable. Forty feet of survey vessel, steel-hulled, government gray, her crane folded neatly against the deckhouse. Her name was stenciled on the bow in flaking white paint, and beneath it, smaller, the registry number that matched the one now sitting in Paloma's chart drawer under a contract with teeth.

"No movement," Suri said. She'd gone very still at the bow, one hand flat on the rail. "No smoke. No light. But—"

"But she's not abandoned," Paloma finished. "I know. I see it too."

Because that was the second cold thing. Abandoned boats rot fast on the Reach. Within a month the rain gets into everything, the rust blooms orange at every seam, the gulls claim the rails, and the mud daubers build their cities in the wheelhouse. The Kestrel had been sitting here—how long? Two weeks since the last check-in, per the courier's schedule. Maybe three since the team went missing. And she looked like she'd been tied off yesterday. Lines coiled and hung. Canvas lashed over the forward hatch. Even the brass on the wheelhouse door had been wiped down; it caught the morning light without a spot of green on it.

Somebody had loved this boat on their way out the door.

"Come alongside starboard," Paloma said quietly. "Suri, take the line. Bao, you're with me. Wen—" she hesitated. Old Wen hadn't held a rifle since the border troubles, and everyone knew it, and nobody mentioned it. "Wen, keep the boat ready to move. If we come back running, I want us already running."

Old Wen looked at her for a moment longer than necessary, and something passed between them that had no words in any of their languages—the old man's knowledge that he was being managed, and his willingness, this once, to allow it. Then he nodded and took the wheel, and his hands settled onto the spokes like they were coming home.

* * *

The Kestrel's deck was dry.

That shouldn't have mattered. It was the kind of detail that mattered anyway. It had rained two nights ago—a hard, brief squall that had them all out on the Magpie Jay checking lashings—and yet the Kestrel's deck planking was dry underfoot, sheltered by nothing, as though the rain had simply declined to fall on her. Paloma knelt and pressed her palm to the boards. Damp, maybe, at the very grain. Nothing more.

Beside the deckhouse, stacked with a neatness that made her skin crawl, were the supplies.

Crates of ration tins, labels facing out. Jerricans of water, capped. A medical kit with its white case unmarked. Tool rolls tied with their ties tucked in. A surveyor's theodolite in its fitted case, standing upright like a small obedient soldier. Everything arranged in rows, everything squared to the deckhouse wall, everything exactly where a careful person would put it if they intended to come back.

Or exactly where eight careful people would put it if they wanted whoever came looking to find nothing disturbed.

"Captain." Bao stood at the open engine hatch, and his voice had a strange flat quality she'd learned to distrust, because it was the voice he used when the news was bad enough that drama would be a waste of breath. "You want to see this."

She went. The engine room smelled of clean steel and cold oil. The Kestrel's twin diesels sat in their beds like sleeping animals, and when Paloma laid her hand on the block it was cool—not river-cool, not morning-cool, but *cool*, the temperature of metal that hasn't turned over in weeks. She checked the fuel gauges out of habit. Three-quarters. More than enough to run anywhere.

"They didn't run out of anything," Bao said. "No breakdown. No fire. No flooding—I've been in the bilge, it's dry as a sermon. Captain, these engines are fine. These engines are *better* than fine. Somebody's been maintaining them. Look." He pointed to the oil filter housing, where a wrench mark gleamed fresh and bright against the older grime. "That's recent work. That's somebody keeping her ready."

"Ready for what?"

Bao didn't answer, and neither did she, because they both heard Suri's voice from above, calling down the hatch, and the tone of it pulled them up the ladder faster than any order could have.

* * *

Suri was standing at the gangway, at the top of the steps down to the mud bank, and she was not moving.

"Suri?" Paloma said.

"There are no tracks," Suri said. "Captain, there are no tracks."

Paloma came down beside her and looked, and the world rearranged itself into something worse.

The bank below the Kestrel was the soft gray-brown silt of the upper Reach, the kind of mud that recorded everything. On the Magpie Jay's home stretch, that mud kept receipts, as Paloma's father used to say—you could read a week of comings and goings in it like a ledger. Heron prints like calligraphy. The slash-marks of monitor lizard tails. The deep crescent boot-prints of fishermen hauling skiffs.

Here there was nothing. No bird tracks. No lizard. No sign of the capybara that should have been working this bank at dawn. Just smooth, unbroken silt, stretching away toward the treeline, drying at its edges into a skin of cracked clay.

Except.

One set of tracks. Leading *down*.

Eight sets of boot prints, close together, spaced like people walking deliberately, unhurried, side by side rather than single file—all pointing from the gangway down across the mud, straight into the water's edge. Where they ended. No turning prints. No second journey back up. Just eight lines of evidence walking into the brown water and stopping, as if the river had reached up and licked the page clean after the final entry.

"They walked in," Suri whispered. "All of them. Together. They walked into the water."

"Or something walked them in," Bao said grimly, "and the mud's had time to forget."

But Paloma was shaking her head slowly, and the understanding was arriving in her the way floodwater arrives in a low field—quietly at the edges first, then all at once everywhere. Because she had grown up reading this mud, and the mud was telling her something specific, and it was not violence.

Violence leaves drag marks. Violence leaves scuffs, churned ground, dropped things, a story told in panic. This mud told a story told in *order*. Eight people had walked down those steps, arranged themselves shoulder to shoulder, and waded into the current at a walk. Nobody had run. Nobody had fought. And before they went, someone had wiped down the brass, tensioned the mooring lines, covered the hatch, stacked the supplies in rows, and maintained engines they weren't going to use.

"You don't tidy up if you're being dragged," Paloma said aloud. "You don't coil the lines. You don't leave rations stacked and theodolites standing up straight." She crouched at the bottom of the gangway, just short of the first print, and looked at it closely. A survey-issue boot, size ten or so, pressed deep and even, weight balanced. Beside it, a smaller print, lighter, toes angled slightly outward—the walk of someone carrying something. "This is a departure. This is eight people leaving on purpose, and leaving the way you leave when you don't want to alarm anybody who comes after you."

"Leaving to where?" Bao demanded. "Into the river? People don't—"

"People also don't vanish off a moored boat with full fuel tanks," Paloma said. She stood. Her knees ached, and she realized she'd been clenching her jaw since the haze lifted. "They went somewhere. Upstream. Into the caves, maybe—that's what the survey was for, the flooded system past the bend. The map fragment showed chambers under the ridge." She looked at the water at the foot of the gangway, brown and slow and utterly unreadable. "And they went in quietly, and they left the boat looking lived-in, because they knew someone would come looking. They knew a charter crew would stand exactly here, exactly where we're standing, and they wanted us to find a boat that said *nothing happened here.*"

"It says plenty happened here," Suri said. "It says too much. Why wipe the brass but leave the tracks? Why cover the hatch but leave the log—"

She stopped.

"The log," Paloma said.

They looked at each other, and then all three of them moved for the wheelhouse at once.

* * *

The wheelhouse door was unlocked. Of course it was. Locked doors say *keep out*; this boat said *come in, sit down, nothing to see.*

Inside, everything waited for them with the patience of a stage set. Charts rolled and sleeved in their rack. Coffee mugs washed and inverted on the shelf—one, two, three, four, five, six, seven, eight. Eight mugs. Paloma counted them twice. In the corner, a jacket hung on its hook, still holding the shape of the shoulders that had left it there. Survey-issue, government gray, name tape stitched above the pocket: **E. VOSS**.

On the chart table, weighted down with a brass compass whose glass had been polished until it burned, lay the Kestrel's logbook. Closed. Square. Centered.

Suri reached for it and Paloma caught her wrist—not hard, just enough. "Gloves first," she said. "If this goes where I think it goes, we touch it like lawyers, not like tourists."

Bao handed up the cotton work gloves from the tool roll, and Paloma opened the book herself.

The entries were routine, meticulous, and stopped like a sentence cut off mid-word. Soundings. Water temperatures. Mineral content readings that climbed steadily through the last week—numbers annotated in a cramped, urgent hand, some circled twice, one underlined so hard the pen had torn the paper. The final entry was dated eleven days ago. It was four lines long:

*Chamber seven confirmed. Readings exceed projection by an order of magnitude. Voss says the instruments aren't wrong—he says the water is doing this on purpose. Team vote unanimous. We go at first light. Leave her shipshape for whoever follows.*

*If whoever follows reads this: it was worth it.*

Below that, nothing. No signature. No closing time. The ink of the last word still sharp-edged, as if the pen had been set down mid-thought and never picked back up.

Paloma read it three times. Around her, the wheelhouse held its breath. Out on the water, that faint hum came again—there and gone, like a note remembered rather than heard—and this time Old Wen's head came up at the wheel of the Magpie Jay, and this time none of them pretended not to hear it.

"'It was worth it,'" Suri repeated softly. "Captain, what was worth it? What did they find?"

"The water's doing this on purpose," Bao read, and laughed once, without any humor in it at all. "A geologist writes that in an official log, and then votes with seven other people to walk into a flooded cave system, and leaves his jacket hanging on the hook like he's stepping out for cigarettes." He shook his head. "Captain, either these people found God, or they found something the government is going to want very badly buried. And either way—"

"Either way we're being paid triple to find them," Paloma finished, "and secrecy clauses or no secrecy clauses, I need to know which."

She closed the logbook gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping child, and looked out the wheelhouse windows at the Reach. The light had thickened toward noon. Upstream, around the bend, the ridge stood dark against the sky, and somewhere beneath it, chamber seven waited with its water that moved against current, and its order-of-magnitude readings, and its eight sets of bootprints walking calmly in.

The contract in her chart drawer promised salvage and rescue. It had not promised this. Rescue meant people who wanted finding. What waited upstream, if the logbook was honest, was eight people who had chosen the water over the shore—and a discovery that someone with a government seal had already tried, in advance, to buy her silence about.

"Wen!" she called across the gap between the boats. "Get steam up. We're pushing to the bend."

Old Wen's voice drifted back across the water, dry as the cracked clay on the bank. "I figured," he said. "Birds went quiet past the bend, girl. Been trying to tell you since breakfast."

Paloma took the wheel of the Kestrel long enough to tear the map fragment free of the chart rack—the one showing the cave entrances beneath the ridge—and fold it into her shirt pocket, over her heart, where the important things rode.

Then she stepped back aboard the Magpie Jay, and behind them the Kestrel settled again into her patient, terrible stillness, moored and maintained and waiting, like a receipt the river was keeping until someone came back to settle the account.
