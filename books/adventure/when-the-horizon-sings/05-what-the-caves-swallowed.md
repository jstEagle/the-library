## 5. What the Caves Swallowed

Suri found the map fragment in Old Wen's tea tin, of all places, folded into a square no bigger than a playing card and tucked beneath a nest of dried chrysanthemum buds he used for his evening brew. She hadn't been snooping. She'd been looking for the tin itself, because the galley's sugar had run out two days ago and Old Wen hoarded his sweetening like a dragon with a grudge.

The paper was heavy stock, water-stained at one edge, torn raggedly along the other—as if someone had ripped it from a larger chart in a hurry, or in anger, or both. Half a coastline. A stretch of the Serpent's Reach rendered in the confident ink of a government surveyor, all clean contour lines and numbered soundings. And there, in the upper left where the tear began, a hand-drawn annotation in pencil that had been half-erased: *chambers below — DO NOT—*

The rest was gone.

She carried it to the wheelhouse without deciding to. Her feet made the decision for her, the way they always did when something felt wrong aboard the Magpie Jay, which was more often lately than she cared to admit.

Paloma read it twice. Then she held it up to the gray light slanting through the rain-scratched window, as if the paper might confess something under better illumination.

"Where."

"Old Wen's tea tin." Suri watched her captain's face do the thing it did when she was rearranging the world inside her head—jaw tightening on the left side, eyes going still as moored water. "He didn't say anything about it?"

"He doesn't say anything about anything. That's his whole philosophy." Paloma turned the fragment over. On the reverse, faint as breath on glass: grid coordinates, and beside them a date from eleven days ago—the day before the Kestrel's last scheduled check-in. "Eleven days," she murmured. "Whoever drew this knew where they were going before they went dark."

"Maybe that's why they went dark."

Paloma looked at her then, really looked, and Suri realized she'd said the thought out loud without meaning to. It kept happening since they'd passed the last fishing camp three days back—the empty drying racks, the nets hung up like shed skins, the silence that pressed against the hull. Words she didn't intend came out of her mouth like fish rising to a lure.

"Maybe," Paloma said, and clipped the fragment to the chart board with a brass clip that clicked like a small bone breaking.

* * *

They anchored at the mouth of the side-channel just after dawn on the fourth day, in a stretch of river so narrow the jungle leaned over them from both banks, breathing its green rot across the deck. The coordinates put the cave entrance somewhere behind a curtain of hanging vine and limestone slicked black with water that never stopped moving, even here, even in low season, when the rest of the Reach ran shallow and sluggish as an old man's blood.

Old Wen stood at the rail with his hands folded into his sleeves, watching the cliff face the way he watched everything: without expression, without comment, without any sign at all that he was watching. But Suri had learned to read him anyway, in the small grammar of his body. The set of his shoulders said *I have seen this place before.* The angle of his chin said *and I did not like it.*

"You've been here," she said quietly, coming to stand beside him.

The old man was silent long enough that a kingfisher called somewhere upstream—a single bright note, the first bird sound Suri had heard in two days—and then he said, in his rust-and-river voice: "Before your time. Before the war, maybe. Fishermen wouldn't camp here. Said the water sang at night." He unfolded one hand and gestured at the black seam in the rock where the entrance hid. "Not birdsong. Not wind. Singing."

"Superstition."

"Mm." Old Wen smiled without warmth. "So is money. So is government. People believe in worse things than singing water." He turned and shuffled back toward the galley, and over his shoulder, almost too soft to catch: "Wear the life vest. Even inside."

Bao heard the story secondhand and laughed it off, but Suri noticed he checked the generator housing twice that morning and said nothing about singing.

* * *

The entrance swallowed their dinghy the way a throat swallows a pill—smoothly, completely, without any sense of the thing once it was done.

Inside, the world changed registers. The air went cool and mineral, tasting of wet stone and something older underneath, something like iron and something like flowers, which made no sense and unsettled her more than the darkness did. Their headlamps carved cones out of the black, and the black took them back. Water stretched ahead of them, flat as poured glass, and their light lay down on its surface and could not seem to reach bottom. There was no bottom visible. There was only depth going down like a held breath.

"Holy mother of engines," Bao whispered. His voice came back at them wrong—too fast, too close, as though the chamber were small when everything about it said vast. "Echoes are broken in here."

"The rock's porous," Voss would have said, if Voss were with them. But Voss was three days downstream in their stern cabin, locked in by Paloma's order and his own terror, and so the explanation died unspoken, and the wrongness of the echo stayed wrong.

Paloma killed the outboard and let them drift. "Soundings," she said, and her voice, too, behaved strangely—flattened, as if the air itself were pressing it down. "Suri, take the lead line."

Suri swung the weighted line out and let it drop.

It went down, and down, and down, and the coil spun off her palm smooth as silk, and then—it stopped. Not struck bottom. Stopped. The line hung slack in the water at forty feet, neither sinking nor rising, as though the weight had been set down gently on a shelf that their lights could not find.

"That's not how water works," Bao said.

"No," Suri agreed. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears. "It isn't."

And then she heard it.

At first she thought it was the generator, or Bao's stomach, or her own pulse climbing into her ears with fear. But it was none of those things. It was a hum—low, layered, patient—rising not from around them but from below, from the water itself, vibrating up through the hull of the dinghy and into the bones of her pelvis, her spine, the small bones of her inner ear. It wasn't loud. It was *everywhere.* It was the way she imagined the inside of a bell felt while being struck.

"There it is," Paloma breathed. "The surveyor's note. *Chambers below.*" She swept her lamp across the far wall, and the beam caught striations in the limestone—bands of pale and dark stone laid down in rings, like the growth lines of some impossible shell. "This whole cliff is hollow. This whole Reach might be sitting on top of—" She stopped herself. Paloma stopped herself often now, Suri had noticed, editing out conclusions the way you'd spit out river water.

The current, when Suri dipped her hand to test it, moved against them.

Gently. Barely a finger's width of drift against the direction the river above ran—but definite, insistent, the water sliding past her fingers toward some interior destination with the quiet purpose of a creature going home. She should have pulled her hand back then. Some part of her, some old animal ledger deep in her blood, told her to pull her hand back.

She didn't. Curiosity had always been her worst virtue, the one her mother scolded and her teachers despaired of and Old Wen, in his wordless way, seemed to approve of. She let her fingers trail. The water was warm. Warmer than the air. Warm as skin.

And the hum—

The hum changed.

It was subtle, the way a change in wind is subtle until the sail snaps full. The layered tone shifted pitch, settled, steadied—and Suri felt it *answer,* felt the vibration climb her arm like something climbing, and for one vertiginous half-second she was certain—not afraid, not imagining, *certain*—that something on the other side of that warm black water had noticed her hand, and was curious back.

"Suri." Paloma's voice, sharp. "Hand out of the water. Now."

She pulled back. Her fingertips were pink and tingling, pricked with a sensation like a thousand tiny warm needles, and the feeling did not fade the way numbness fades. It spread. Up her wrist, into her forearm, a slow tide of prickling heat following the path of her veins as though the hum had gotten into her blood and was mapping her from the inside.

"I'm fine," she said, because that was what you said.

"You're not." Paloma's face in the lamplight had gone the color of the limestone. "We're done here. Bao, turn us around."

But Bao was already pulling the starter cord, and the engine caught, and caught again, and died, and in the sudden silence the hum rose up around them like water rising in a lock, and Suri felt it press against her eardrums, against her teeth, against the backs of her closed eyes, and the last thing she saw clearly was Paloma reaching for her across the gunwale, mouth shaping words the hum was swallowing—

—and then the chamber tilted, or she did, and the warm black water came up to meet her like an open hand.

* * *

Fever has a geography. That was the first thing Suri understood, lying in the stern cabin under three blankets while the monsoon clouds stacked themselves over the horizon like slate plates, waiting. Fever is not heat. Fever is a country. You cross into it through a border you don't see, and the rules there are different, and the inhabitants know your name.

She drifted. In the drifting, the hum came with her.

It wasn't frightening anymore, in the fever-country. It was *articulate.* It moved in layers the way a river moves in layers—surface current and undertow and the slow deep drag of the channel bed—and each layer meant something, and the meanings braided together into sentences too large for her skull, so that she understood them the way you understand weather: wholly, bodily, without translation.

*You touched us,* the hum said, or meant, or was. *Few touch us. Fewer return.*

*I didn't mean to,* she answered, in whatever language answering happened in. *We were looking for people.*

*We know. We counted them. Eleven came. Eleven left. They ran because they listened too well.*

*Listened to what?*

And the hum showed her—or the fever showed her using the hum's voice—an image that would stay burned behind her eyes for the rest of her life: the aquifer entire, the flooded chambers running for miles beneath the Reach like the waterways of some drowned cathedral, and threaded through every channel of it, a living mesh. Fine as silk. Ancient as stone. A network in the water itself, filament touching filament, signal touching signal, older than any nation's borders, older than any map, drinking the seismic whisper of the continent and passing it along, node to node, the way the river people passed news from boat to boat.

The water was alive. Not metaphor-alive. Alive.

And the government's engineers, with their concrete and their diversion charts, planned to dam the throat of it and bleed it off into irrigation canals for plantations that did not yet exist, and the network would die the way coral dies when the reef is dredged—slowly, silently, and everything that depended on its breathing would follow.

*Now you carry it too,* the hum said, almost gently. *That is what touching costs.*

Somewhere far away, above the fever-country, she heard Paloma's voice arguing with Bao about turning back versus pushing on, and Old Wen's silence sitting in the middle of the argument like a stone. Somewhere far away, a thermometer clinked against a cup. Somewhere far away, her mother's clinic needed money, and she had signed a contract with teeth to get it, and the teeth had finally closed.

But mostly there was the hum, and the knowledge settling into her like silt into a harbor, and one more thing—one detail the fever delivered last, the way rivers deliver their rarest fish to the patient net:

The eleven who left had scattered on purpose. She knew it the way she now knew the hidden channels of the Reach, knew it in her marrow: they hadn't fled the caves. They'd been *sent out.* Carriers. Each one carrying a piece of the truth downstream, the way the network carried its signals, so that no single seizure could stop them all.

One of them was locked in their stern cabin, three doors down, pretending to be mad.

Suri surfaced from the fever on the third night, drenched, shaking, clear-eyed, and found Paloma asleep in the chair beside her bunk with the map fragment still clipped to her belt like a weapon.

"Captain," Suri whispered.

Paloma woke instantly, the way captains do. "You're back. Good. You gave us four bad days." Her voice was rough with sleeplessness. "Talk to me. What do you remember?"

Suri looked at her—at this woman who'd been coerced onto this river by a contract with penalties like manacles, who was already carrying more truth than she wanted—and made the choice that the fever-country had made for her.

"I remember what the water said," Suri said. "And Captain—you need to hear it before we decide whether Voss is our prisoner or our proof."

Paloma went very still. Outside, the first fat drops of the early monsoon began ticking against the cabin roof, faster and faster, like fingers drumming on a door.

"Start talking," Paloma said.
