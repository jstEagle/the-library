## 6. The Survivor Who Isn't Grateful

Bao heard the man before he saw him—a cough, dry and ragged, threading through the green hush of the bank like a wire pulled taut. He froze with the wrench still in his hand, half-crouched in the engine room's doorway, and listened the way his grandmother had taught him to listen to engines: not for the sound itself, but for what the sound was trying to hide.

The Magpie Jay was tied up at a sliver of shingle beach below a collapsed bluff, taking on water for the night. Paloma had ordered cold engines and no lights. Three days upstream of silence, with the fishing camps empty and the birds gone from the water, nobody argued about caution anymore. Even Suri, fever-flushed and sleeping in the cabin since the caves, hadn't argued.

The cough came again, closer now, from the tangle of deadfall at the top of the shingle.

"Paloma," Bao said quietly, and she was beside him in an instant—she always moved like that, like the boat herself, all ballast and sudden current. Old Wen appeared at the galley door with a cleaver in one hand and a lantern in the other, which was Old Wen's way of negotiating.

"Friend or trouble?" Paloma murmured.

"Coughing trouble," Bao said. "Trouble that's been coughing a long time."

They found him at dawn's grey edge, or rather he let them find him, because Bao understood quickly that this was a man who had chosen every position he'd taken for weeks. He sat propped against a fallen strangler-fig with a rifle across his knees—not aimed, but *present*, the way a knife is present on a table during a negotiation—and he was so thin that his cheekbones looked like they were trying to escape his face. His survey jacket had been mended with fishing line. His boots had been mended with bark. His eyes, sunken in a face gone the color of old tarpaulin, moved across each of them in turn with the flat, cataloguing attention of a man counting exits.

"You're not military," he said. His voice sounded like gravel poured slowly.

"We're a charter," Paloma said. "Magpie Jay, out of Corvane Landing. We're looking for the Kestrel."

Something flickered behind those eyes—recognition, then something colder underneath it, something that might have been grief wearing armor. "You found her."

"Moored and tidy," Paloma said. "Supplies stacked. Engines cold. No bodies." She took a step forward, hands loose and visible. "No tracks out of the mud except into the water. You want to tell me where your team went, Dr.—?"

"Voss." He said it like he was paying off a debt. "Elias Voss. Geologist." A pause, and then, with terrible clarity: "Turn around."

Bao watched Paloma's face do the thing it did when she was being offered less than she knew existed—the small tightening at the corners of her mouth, the stillness of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't intend to share. "We have a contract," she said.

"Whatever they're paying you, it isn't enough." Voss shifted, and Bao tracked the rifle barrel as it swung a lazy few degrees away from them, deliberately, a gesture of control rather than trust. "Whatever they told you, it isn't true. Take your boat and go back downriver while the water's still low enough to run it."

"The monsoon's coming," Old Wen rumbled from behind, cleaver resting on his shoulder like a philosopher's staff. "Nine days, maybe ten. Everyone knows it."

"Then you have nine days to be somewhere else." Voss laughed, and it was the worst sound Bao had heard in weeks—dry, cracked, with nothing in it but exhaustion. "I've been eating river snails and raw fish heads for nineteen days so I could be here when someone came. Do you understand? I *waited*. I could have walked out. I stayed so the first boat up this stretch of river would hear it from me and not from a grave." He pushed himself upright against the fig, swaying, and Bao saw how much the motion cost him—one hand white-knuckled on the root, the rifle suddenly an anchor rather than a weapon. "So hear it. Turn back. Burn your contract. Go home."

"And your team?" Paloma asked. "Six people. Where are they?"

"Scattered." The word came out of him like a splinter. "Safe, if they're smart. Gone to ground. That's all you get."

"That's not all we get," Paloma said, very quietly. "We've got a paper with teeth in it. Penalties that would sink us. And one of my crew—" her voice caught, just slightly, on the word crew, and Bao felt it land in his own chest like a dropped tool, "—is sick since she went near your caves. So you'll give us more, Dr. Voss, or you'll come aboard and explain yourself to the river until you do."

For a long moment the only sound was the Reach moving past the shingle, that vast brown patience, and Voss looking at Paloma with an expression Bao couldn't read—something between contempt and desperate hope, the look of a drowning man being offered a rope he suspected was attached to an anchor.

"I'm coming aboard anyway," Voss said finally. "Because you won't turn back, and because someone has to slow you down." He shouldered the rifle—not offering it, not surrendering it—and took two steps toward the waterline before his legs betrayed him. Bao caught him under the arm without deciding to; the man weighed almost nothing, like carrying a bundle of dried reeds.

"Easy," Bao said.

"Don't call me easy," Voss muttered. "I signed the plan. Whatever happens to you is on my signature too."

* * *

They put him in the aft cabin, the small one with the porthole that stuck, and Suri's fever kept her in the forward bunk, so the boat held its sick at opposite ends like ballast. Bao brought Voss broth—Old Wen's broth, which had revived deckhands and once, allegedly, a drowned cat—and sat on the deck plate across from him while the man ate with the controlled desperation of someone who knew exactly how fast a starved stomach could kill its owner.

"You waited nineteen days," Bao said. "To warn strangers."

"To warn *anyone*." Voss set down the bowl. His hands had a fine tremor that he visibly hated. "There's a difference between you and the people who'll come next. You're poor. Poor people can still be scared. Rich people can only be paid."

"That's cynical for a government man."

"I stopped being a government man somewhere around day four." He looked at the porthole, at the brown slide of the river beyond the salt-stained glass. "You want to know why I won't tell you what's up there. It's not because I don't trust you. It's because the moment you know, you can't unknow it, and then you have to decide what to do about it, and every decision after that costs something you can't afford." He turned back, and his eyes were terribly steady. "I've watched six good people make that decision. Ask me how many of them are sitting at this table."

"None," Bao said.

"None," Voss agreed. "Now ask me if I regret it."

Bao didn't ask. He gathered the bowl instead, and at the door he paused. "Suri's sick. Fever, chills, talking in her sleep. It started when she touched the water in the caves."

Something moved through Voss's face then that Bao had never seen on a living man—guilt so old it had fossilized into architecture. "I know what started it," he said. "That's part of what I can't say." And then, quieter, almost to himself: "Is she getting worse?"

"Worse and stranger both."

"Keep her away from the water," Voss said sharply, and then seemed surprised by his own sharpness. "Please. Keep her away from the water."

* * *

Bao woke in the black hour before dawn to a sound he knew too well: the particular click-and-hum of the radio cabinet opening. Not the transmit switch—that had a heavier throw, a sound like a knuckle cracking. This was the lighter scrape of the access panel, the one that hid the tuning capacitors and the wiring loom, the guts of the thing.

He lay still in his bunk and counted. One: whoever it was knew the panel existed. Two: whoever it was knew it opened without tools. Three: the Magpie Jay carried five souls, and two of them were himself and Old Wen, who was currently snoring like a landslide two feet away.

Which left either Paloma doing something strange in the dark, or their guest.

Bao rose without a sound—he'd spent thirty years in engine rooms; silence was his mother tongue—and crossed the deck under a sky the color of wet slate. The radio shack's door stood open an inch. Inside, by the light of a shielded penlamp clenched in his teeth, Dr. Elias Voss was elbow-deep in the radio's interior with a scavenged fishhook in one hand, delicately, precisely, unspooling the connection between the transmitter and its antenna feed.

He was good at it. That was the thing that made Bao's chest go cold. This wasn't panic; this was method. The fishhook lifted a soldered lead with the tenderness of a surgeon, and Voss's ruined hands were steady now, steadier than they'd been all week, because apparently sabotage was the one thing that still gave him peace.

"Careful with the blue wire," Bao said from the doorway. "It feeds the receiver too. Break that and you're deaf as well as dumb."

Voss didn't jump. He closed his eyes for a moment, the way a man does when the last card he was holding turns out to be marked, and then set down the fishhook with exaggerated care. "How long have you been standing there?"

"Long enough to admire the work."

"It would have looked like weather damage." Voss lowered his arms, and the penlamp rolled across the desk and guttered out, leaving them in the grey pre-dawn gloom. "A week from now, halfway downstream, you'd have blamed the humidity. You'd have cursed me for a hundred other things instead."

"Why?" Bao asked. It wasn't anger in his voice; he found he genuinely wanted to know. Thirty years of machinery had taught him that everything broken tells you why, if you're patient. "You waited nineteen days to flag down the first boat on this river. You nearly killed yourself staying. And the first thing you do aboard is cut our throat to the outside world?"

"Because the first boat on this river wasn't supposed to be *you*." Voss's voice dropped low, urgent, stripped of all its earlier armor. "Listen to me, engineer. There's a schedule. Check-ins. Your charter didn't come up this river on its own initiative—it came because somebody upstream missed a check-in, and somebody downstream noticed. Which means the same notice reached other ears. Better-equipped ears. Within days there will be another vessel on this water, and it will not be flying a survey pennant, and the men aboard will not be geologists." He leaned close, and Bao could smell the river-snail starvation on him, could see the pulse jumping in his throat. "When they come, they will ask your captain one question: did you make contact with anyone? And if your radio works—if anyone on this coast has heard a voice from the Magpie Jay—they will already know the answer, and the question will be academic, and so will all of you."

"So break the radio and we're safe?"

"No. Breaking the radio buys time. Time is the only currency left on this river." Voss straightened, wincing. "I told you to turn back. You wouldn't. Fine. Then understand what you've signed. You aren't on a rescue anymore. Rescues end. This doesn't end. The only way off this river clean is to be worth more silent than spoken."

"You could have told Paloma this at the shingle."

"She'd have done something noble." Voss said the word like it was a diagnosis. "Noble gets people killed faster than cowardice ever did. Sabotage is quiet. Sabotage keeps boats floating." He paused, and for the first time since they'd dragged him aboard, something young and wrecked surfaced in his face. "Your sick girl. Suri. In the caves—did she touch the water directly?"

"Bare hands. Pulled her sleeve up."

Voss exhaled through his teeth. "Then she's already past the point where silence helps her. Which means—" he stopped himself, jaw working, and Bao watched a man physically swallow a sentence whole.

"Means what?"

"Means the clock you're worried about is wrong." Voss picked up the fishhook, turned it over in his fingers, and handed it back to Bao handle-first, a surrender of sorts. "Fix the radio or don't. But know this much: whatever you've calculated about the monsoon, about check-ins, about how long you have—cut every number in half. And then cut it again."

Bao took the hook. Outside, the eastern sky had begun to bruise toward grey-green, and the river ran past the hull with its endless low whisper, and somewhere below decks Suri turned over in her fever and spoke a word in a language Bao didn't recognize—though later, much later, he would realize the river did.

He looked at Voss, half-starved and armed and lying about everything except the fear in his eyes, and made the engineer's judgment he'd been making his whole life: this machine was damaged, yes—but it was damaged *on purpose*, by someone trying to keep it running.

"Paloma decides," Bao said. "Not me. But I'll tell you one thing for free, doctor." He nodded at the gutted radio, at the blue wire hanging loose as a snapped tendon. "Next time you want to cripple my ship, ask me first. I'd have done it cleaner, and I'd have left us a listening ear."

Voss stared at him. And then, horribly, he laughed—the second laugh Bao had heard from him, and this one had something almost human in it, rusty and disbelieving, like an engine turning over after years in the flood.

"A listening ear," Voss repeated softly. "Yes. That's exactly what we need." He looked out the porthole at the brightening water, and his face went grim and far away. "Because they're coming, engineer. Sooner than you think. And when that boat rounds the bend, the only question that matters is whether we hear it before it hears us."

Upstream, faint as a memory, the river hummed.
