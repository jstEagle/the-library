## 7. Monsoon Math

Old Wen did his best thinking with a cup of tea going cold beside him and a pencil behind each ear. He had three pencils tonight, which meant the problem was serious.

Paloma found him in the galley after midnight, the chart of the upper Reach spread across the table and weighted down with a tin of salted plums, a coil of copper wire, and Bao's spare wrench. The lamp swung gently overhead with the river's breathing, throwing Wen's shadow long across the paper like a second man leaning over the same calculations.

"You should be sleeping," she said.

"You should be too." He didn't look up. His finger traced the river's course northward, past the Kestrel's mooring, past the drowned camps, into the blue smear of the high country where the mapmakers had given up on detail and simply written *gorges* in a hand that looked tired. "Sit down, Captain. I've been doing arithmetic you won't like."

Paloma sat. The tea was jasmine, the cheap kind Suri bought in bulk at the delta markets, and it had gone bitter in the cup. She drank it anyway. On the Magpie Jay you learned to take what the night gave you.

"Talk."

Wen turned the chart toward her. He'd drawn two lines on it in red grease-pencil — one crawling up from the south, one descending from the north — and where they would meet he'd drawn a small, neat X, the way a man marks a grave.

"The southern line is the monsoon." He tapped it. "Every year it comes up the coast and turns inland here—" his pencil followed the coastal bend, "—and here, and then it climbs the Reach. First rain falls in the lowlands within six days, maybe seven if we're lucky. But that's not what matters. What matters is the headwaters."

He moved his hand to the top of the chart, to the mountains rendered as hatched triangles. "Snowmelt starts early this year. I can feel it in the water. The river's been rising a finger-width a day since we passed the Kestrel, and it'll accelerate. When the meltwater meets the monsoon in the gorges—" he tapped the X, "—the whole upper Reach becomes a single moving throat. Nine days. Ten if the sky is merciful, and the sky has never once been merciful to me personally."

Paloma studied the X. Somewhere under that grease-pencil mark were the caves, the humming chambers, the water that moved against current. Somewhere up there was whatever had made Dr. Elias Voss half-starved and armed and unwilling to say why.

"And the other line?"

Wen's face did something complicated. Old Wen had cooked for her father before her, had run rivers back when the Reach was called by its older name, and his face could hold more weather than most men's whole lives. Right now it held something close to apology.

"That's the government," he said. "I went through the courier's packet while you were sleeping. There's a check-in schedule sewn into the charter folder — page nine, very fine print, the kind of print lawyers use when they want you to feel clever for finding it and stupid for reading it."

"I read page nine."

"Then you know the schedule says the Kestrel reports every seventy-two hours, and the last report was four days before we signed." He let that sit. "Which means the next scheduled check-in — the one that will tell whoever is listening that something has gone wrong up here, or that someone unauthorized is asking questions up here — comes due in less than two days. And when it comes due and nobody answers, they won't send another polite courier with triple pay. They'll send boats."

The lamp swung. Outside, the river murmured against the hull in its endless patient voice, and somewhere upstream a night bird called once and got no answer. The birds had been getting no answer for two days now. Paloma had stopped mentioning it, because saying it aloud made it heavier.

"So our real number isn't nine days," she said.

"Our real number," Wen said, "is however long it takes us to find what the survey team found, decide what to do about it, and get downstream ahead of both the water and the government. And those two clocks are running at different speeds, Captain, and only one of them cares whether we live."

* * *

She took the calculation topside, because she didn't trust herself to make the decision sitting down.

The Magpie Jay lay moored in a cutbank eddy below a stand of ironwood trees, running dark except for the anchor light, which was itself a calculated risk — dark enough to hide from anything coming upstream, visible enough to keep log rafts from finding them by accident. Bao had the engine hatch open even at this hour, because Bao treated sleep as a rumor spread by people who had never heard an engine misfire. Suri stood at the rail wrapped in a blanket, looking north.

Paloma joined her. For a while neither spoke. The river slid past, black and muscular, carrying the smell of wet stone and distant vegetation and, underneath it all, something faintly mineral that hadn't been there a week ago. Like struck flint. Like the inside of the earth remembering itself.

"Suri." She kept her voice low. "How's your arm?"

Suri pushed back her sleeve. The skin where the cave water had touched her — just a splash, just a moment, knee-deep in a chamber that shouldn't exist — had healed wrong and then healed right again, leaving a pale tracery like frost ferns across her forearm. It didn't hurt anymore. That was somehow worse than if it had.

"It sings sometimes," Suri said. "At night. Not out loud. More like — you know when a tuning fork is near another tuning fork? Here." She pressed the tracery lightly. "It hums when the river changes direction. I think it knows things about the channels. I think—" She stopped, jaw tightening, embarrassed by the sentence before she finished it. "I think it's trying to tell me where the safe water is."

Paloma had watched this girl grow from a dock rat who stole engine parts into the steadiest deckhand on the Reach. She had watched her come out of those caves shivering with a fever that broke in twelve hours and left her different — calmer in some ways, stranger in others, standing watches she didn't need to stand, staring north. Paloma believed her. That was the part that frightened her most. Six months ago she'd have laughed. Now she believed her, because the river itself had started behaving like something with an opinion.

"If I ask you," Paloma said slowly, "whether we go deeper — what does your gut say?"

Suri was quiet long enough that the anchor light swung through a full arc.

"My gut says run," she said finally. "My gut says take the pay, take the story, take ourselves, and get downstream before anyone can stop us. But my mother's clinic is funded by the advance on this charter, and there are eleven people missing up that river who walked into the water on purpose, and there's something in those caves that the government wants badly enough to write contracts with teeth in them." She turned, and in the dimness her eyes caught the anchor light like an animal's. "And my arm says go north. My gut and my arm aren't speaking to each other, Captain. You're going to have to pick which one we listen to."

* * *

Bao wiped his hands on a rag that had been black longer than some nations had existed, and gave his opinion the way he gave all his opinions: while lying under machinery, so that disagreement felt like talking to a wall.

"Contract penalties," he said, voice echoing off the hull plates. "Page eleven. If we return without locating the survey team or recovering survey materials, we forfeit the advance *and* owe damages equal to ninety days' operating cost. Ninety days, Paloma. That's not a penalty, that's a foreclosure with extra steps. Whoever wrote this contract wanted us unable to walk away."

"They wanted us committed," Paloma said.

"They wanted us *owned*. There's a difference, and the difference is about to matter." He rolled out, sat up, and looked at her with grease still smudged along his jaw. In the work-lamp's glow his face was all planes and worry. "Here's my math, and I'm better at math than Wen pretends to be. Two days until their check-in window closes. Say one day after that before someone decides silence is suspicious and launches a response. Call it three days before government boats start working this stretch of river. We need — conservatively — two days to reach the cave system properly, a day to find whatever there is to find, and four days minimum to run downstream ahead of the flood crest through water I've never seen at monsoon levels." He counted it off on greasy fingers. "That's ten days of need against nine days of supply, and I haven't factored in Renn."

"We haven't met Renn."

"No. But the contract names a 'security liaison' attached to the expedition, and governments don't attach security liaisons to rescue operations. They attach them to problems." Bao stood, joints cracking like small-arms fire. "So the honest answer is: the numbers don't work. Not cleanly. We're choosing between losing the boat to the river or losing everything to the men who wrote the contract, and the only version where we win is the version where we find something worth more than both."

Paloma leaned against the bulkhead. Through the steel she could feel the engine's residual warmth fading, the river's cold seeping in, the whole vessel settling into the night like a tired animal. Her father had bought the Magpie Jay with salvage money and stubbornness. She had grown up in these decks the way other children grew up in houses. Every plank knew her weight.

And the contract, if they failed, would take her. Not the boat first — her. Penalties, liens, the courts in the delta city that existed to strip river people of river boats. She thought of the courier's smile when she'd signed. Paper with teeth. She'd known. She'd signed anyway, because Suri needed the money and pride doesn't pay interest.

"What's worth more than both?" she asked quietly.

Bao shrugged, but it wasn't a shrug of ignorance. It was a shrug of a man declining to say the thing out loud because saying it would make it real. Whatever Voss had fled from, whatever the survey team had walked away from their own supplies to escape — it was worth enough to ruin careers over. Worth enough to write teeth into contracts. Worth enough, maybe, to kill for.

"I'm going to bed," Bao said. "Wake me when you've decided. Or wake me when the decision decides you. On this river it's usually the second one."

* * *

Dawn came gray and slow, the way it did in the weeks before the rains, the sky a lid pressing down on the water. Paloma stood at the wheel with the chart folded under her arm and made herself look north for a long time.

The upper Reach narrowed ahead into the first of the true gorges, walls of black stone rising sheer from the current, mist coiling in their shadows. Somewhere beyond the third bend lay the caves. Somewhere beyond the caves lay the answer to a question she couldn't yet frame — why eleven trained people had abandoned a fully supplied vessel and walked into flooded rock, why the birds avoided fifty miles of living water, why the government's paperwork wanted them silent rather than saved.

Old Wen came up the companionway with fresh tea, two cups, and handed her one without being asked. He stood beside her at the wheel and they drank in silence while the gorge drifted closer.

"Nine days," he said at last. "Give or take."

"Less, really."

"Less, really." He sipped. "You know, when your father ran this river, he had a rule. Never make a decision at night that you can put off until dawn, and never make a decision at dawn that the river hasn't already answered." He nodded north, at the mist, at the black stone, at whatever waited in the flooded dark. "River's already answered, I think. It's been answering since the birds left. Question is whether you're brave enough to hear it."

Paloma set down her cup. Her hands were steady, which surprised her; she'd expected them to shake.

"Spin the wheel," she said. "We go north. Full daylight running, double watch at night, and we're through the cave country and turning for home inside five days — because I am not negotiating with either clock, Wen. I'm beating both of them."

Old Wen smiled, slow and creased, and rang the bell twice to wake the boat.

They took the first bend at speed, and the gorge swallowed them, and that was when Suri came up the ladder two steps at a time with her blanket still around her shoulders and her face drained white as river foam.

"Paloma." Her voice cracked. "There's smoke. Behind us. Coming up fast — and it's not woodsmoke. It's diesel. Somebody's burning fuel to catch us, and they're not even trying to hide the trail."

Paloma turned. Far downstream, threading the gap they'd just left, a gray shape moved against the gray water, and the morning wind carried the sound of an engine being driven harder than any engine should be driven.

The clock hadn't been waiting for her decision after all.
