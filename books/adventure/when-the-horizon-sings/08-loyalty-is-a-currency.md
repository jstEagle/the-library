## 8. Loyalty Is a Currency

Bao heard the second boat before anyone saw it—a wrongness in the water's voice, a deeper thrum beneath the Magpie Jay's idle, the way a man knows another heartbeat in a dark room before he sees the shape of it.

He was elbow-deep in the engine compartment at dawn, third morning anchored below the caves, chasing a fuel-line hiccup that had been threatening to become something worse since the Kestrel. His hands knew engines the way Old Wen's hands knew channels: without asking permission, without needing light. So when the rhythm of the river changed—when the current against the hull picked up a stutter it shouldn't have, a wash pushed sideways from somewhere upstream—he closed the panel and stood up out of the bilge with grease to his wrists and his heart already sinking.

"Paloma," he called up through the deck plates. "We've got company."

* * *

They came around the bend of the Reach like a rumor made steel.

The boat was government gray, but not survey gray—not the faded, sun-bleached utility of the Kestrel with its peeling registration numbers and its coffee-stained charts. This was a newer vessel, low-slung, matte-finished, twin diesels tuned so clean Bao could hear the balance in them from two hundred meters and hated how much he admired them. A gun mount sat forward under a canvas shroud that fooled no one. Along the rail stood six men in river-fatigues without insignia, which was its own kind of signature. Men who wore no name on their chest were men whose names were kept somewhere else, in files, in ledgers, in places you didn't want your own name written.

"They're flying no colors," Suri said from the bow, binoculars steady in her hands. She'd been feverish for two days and still moved like her body belonged to someone else, but her eyes through the glass were clear and cold. "No flag, no pennant. Just a hull number."

"What hull number?" Paloma asked. She stood at the wheel with her arms folded, watching the gray boat come down the channel with the particular stillness she got when she was doing arithmetic she didn't like.

Suri read it out. Paloma was quiet a moment. "That's not a survey prefix," she said finally. "That's an asset-protection prefix."

Bao didn't know what that meant, exactly, but he knew what it sounded like, and what it sounded like was *property*.

Old Wen came up from the galley wiping his hands on his apron, took one look at the approaching boat, and said nothing at all—which from Old Wen was louder than any warning he'd ever given. He simply went to the stern rail and stood there, very straight, the way old men stand when they have seen a thing before, decades ago, on a different river, and hoped never to see it again.

The gray boat slowed. Held station fifty meters off their starboard side, close enough to read faces, far enough to be a statement rather than a greeting. A loudhailer crackled.

"Magpie Jay, this is support element attached to the Kestrel recovery operation. We're here to assist. Please confirm your status and remain where you are."

*Assist.* The word arrived wrapped in cotton over iron. Bao looked at Paloma. Paloma looked at the boat. And then, from the shadow of the cabin where Voss had spent three days hiding like a man who'd learned that visibility was the same as vulnerability, the geologist emerged onto the deck—and stopped dead.

Bao watched Voss's face do something complicated and terrible. It wasn't fear exactly. It was recognition—the specific, exhausted recognition of a man who has been running from something and has just watched it round the bend.

"Oh," Voss said softly. "Oh, they sent him."

"You know them?" Paloma asked, not turning her head.

"I know the boat." Voss's voice had gone flat and careful, a man walking on ice he'd fallen through once. "I don't know who's commanding it. But I know what that hull number means." He swallowed. "That's not a rescue designation. That's a containment designation."

The loudhailer crackled again, polite as a bank notice. "Magpie Jay, please confirm status. Over."

Paloma lifted her hand to the hailer mounted by the wheel. Bao watched her take one breath—one long, slow breath, the kind she took before lying to inspectors or arguing with loan officers, the kind that meant the lie or the argument was going to be good.

"Support element, this is Magpie Jay," she said, warm as fresh bread. "We've located the Kestrel, secured the site, recovered one survivor, Dr. Elias Voss. All quiet here. Glad to see friendly faces this far up. Over."

A pause on the other end. Long enough to count. Then: "Copy, Magpie Jay. We'll come alongside. Have your crew stand ready to receive. Over."

*Have your crew stand ready to receive.* Not *we'll dock alongside and share a meal.* Not *prepare to transfer the survivor.* Receive. As if the Magpie Jay were a port. As if everything aboard—including, Bao understood with a slow cold turn of his stomach, themselves—were cargo awaiting inspection.

Paloma lowered the hailer. Her knuckles were white around the wheel spoke, though her face gave away nothing.

"Bao," she said quietly, without looking at him. "Go make sure the logbook is where we agreed it would be."

He went.

* * *

The logbook lived now inside the water heater.

It had been Bao's idea, and he was quietly proud of it. The Magpie Jay's ancient calorifier was a rusted cylinder bolted behind the engine room bulkhead, drained last season and never refilled because Bao hadn't gotten around to replacing the heating element and Paloma hadn't gotten around to yelling at him about it. Its access plate opened with a screwdriver. Inside: dry, dark, warm-smelling with old scale and iron, and utterly unremarkable to anyone who didn't know. He'd sealed the pages in oilcloth first, then in a ration tin, then wedged the tin into the sediment of years at the bottom of the tank. Three layers between Renn's men—if that was who these men were—and the only document on the Serpent's Reach that explained why six surveyors had walked into the water rather than wait for their government to find them.

He checked it now, screwdriver quick in his fingers, tin solid, oilcloth tight. Closed the plate. Wiped his prints off the screws with his shirttail. Stood a moment in the dimness of the engine room listening to the gray boat's diesels grow louder, thinking about machines.

That was the thing about Bao—he thought in machines. People confused him, mostly; motives slid past him like oil on water. But a machine told you the truth if you listened. Every machine had a purpose built into it, visible in its bones. You could look at a pump and know it was made to move water. You could look at a gun and know it was made to stop hearts. Purpose was honest. Purpose didn't lie.

And purpose was why, when he climbed back up on deck and got his first real look at the men coming across on the boarding line, he felt sick instead of afraid.

Because he could see what they were built for.

* * *

The man in charge introduced himself as Renn.

He came over the rail lightly for a big man, landing on the Magpie Jay's scarred deck like he'd done it a hundred times on a hundred boats, and Bao watched his boots find their footing automatically, watched his eyes do a full sweep of the vessel in four seconds flat—wheel, radio, companionway, fuel drums, crew. Not sightseeing. Inventorying. Bao had seen buyers do that at salvage auctions, pricing a wreck before the auctioneer finished talking. Renn priced the Magpie Jay the same way, except that what he was pricing wasn't quite the boat.

"Captain Fairweather." Renn's handshake was firm, brief, calibrated. He had a face like a well-kept tool—nothing wasted, nothing soft, a small pale scar through one eyebrow that might have been a childhood fall or might have been a story he'd tell differently depending on the listener. Behind him his five men spread along the rail with a discipline that pretended to be casual. Two of them drifted, without appearing to drift, toward the companionway. One stopped near the radio. One stayed on the boarding line, facing the water, watching the jungle.

"Mr. Renn." Paloma matched his handshake exactly. Bao had seen her do this with port authorities and customs men—give them back their own pressure, their own duration, so the measure of each other canceled out. "You came fast. The Reach isn't an easy run this time of year."

"We have good boats." Renn smiled. It reached his eyes the way frost reaches a window—all the way across, touching everything, warming nothing. "And the department was concerned. Six missing personnel, a civilian charter involved—we wanted to make sure you had everything you needed."

"We've managed."

"So I see." Renn turned, surveying the deck, and his gaze passed over Voss standing by the cabin door—passed over him, held on him, came back. "Dr. Voss. You're a difficult man to find."

"The Reach is a difficult place," Voss said. His voice was steady. His hands, at his sides, were not.

"That they tell me." Renn's attention moved on, smooth as a trolley changing tracks, and Bao understood that the pause had been deliberate—that Renn had wanted Voss to feel seen, weighed, filed. "Your team, Doctor. Six colleagues unaccounted for. You must know something about where they went."

"I know they left the Kestrel," Voss said. "I don't know why. I was separated from them in the caves. I've told Captain Fairweather everything I remember."

"Everything?"

"Everything I remember," Voss repeated, and put a hair of space between the words that Bao, who missed most human subtleties, nonetheless caught clearly, because it was the same space a bearing leaves when it's about to seize.

Renn let it go with a nod, and turned to business. "Here's the situation, Captain. The department wants the site secured, the Kestrel documented, and Dr. Voss transported downstream for medical evaluation and debriefing. Your contract stands—you'll be paid in full, plus a completion bonus for locating the survivor. Outstanding work, truly." He produced a folder from his jacket, thick, official, corners sharp enough to draw blood. "There are some addenda. Standard for operations at this classification level. Communications protocol, primarily. From this point forward, all transmissions from this vessel route through our relay. Routine security."

"Routine security," Paloma said.

"For everyone's protection." Renn handed her the folder. "I'll need your radio operator to sign the acknowledgment. And I'll need to inspect your vessel. Standard procedure when integrating a civilian asset into an ongoing operation."

*Asset.* There it was again. Bao felt the word settle on the deck of the Magpie Jay like ballast, shifting her whole center of gravity. Asset. Cargo. Property. The gray boat at the rail, the men without names, the folder full of teeth—they hadn't come upstream to help find anything. They'd come because something had been found, and found things needed collecting.

Paloma took the folder. Didn't open it. "My engineer handles my radio," she said. "He'll review your addendum. My cook is ill"—a small gesture toward Suri, who was leaning against the rail with convincing grayness—"so inspections will have to wait until he's back on his feet. And my survivor needs food and sleep before he goes anywhere near a debriefing room. We'll run downstream together tomorrow, after the fog lifts. The channel above the Split is suicide in fog; you know that or you wouldn't have waited for daylight to come alongside."

Something flickered in Renn's face at that—quick, quickly buried. Respect, maybe. Or recalculation. "Tomorrow, then," he said pleasantly. "We'll anchor astern. If you need anything, hail us." He paused at the rail on his way to the boarding line, and looked back at Paloma with the smile again, the frost-window smile. "Captain. One thing. The department takes confidentiality seriously. Very seriously. I'm sure your contract made that clear. But contracts are paper. Out here, discretion is a practice. I'd hate to see a fine crew pay penalties for a lapse in practice."

"We're very practiced," Paloma said.

Renn laughed—a real laugh, short and genuine, and somehow that was worse than the smile. "I believe you," he said, and went over the side.

* * *

They watched the gray boat ease back and drop anchor a hundred meters astern, close enough that its running lights would sit in the corner of their vision all night like a patient animal.

Then Paloma said, very quietly, "Everyone below. Now."

They crowded into the galley, the four of them, and Paloma shut the door and stood with her back against it, and for the first time since Bao had known her—eight years, two monsoons, one fire, one bankruptcy, one funeral—she looked like a woman doing math she couldn't make come out.

"He inspected us," Bao said. "Not the boat. Us. Did you see? He counted us. Four. He knows there are four of us."

"I saw."

"And those two who drifted toward the companionway. They weren't looking for the head. They were measuring the door width. For stretchers, maybe. Or for carrying someone out." He rubbed his greasy hands on his trousers, restless. "Paloma, I fix engines. I know when a machine is built for one job and labeled as another. That boat is labeled *support*. It is built for *control*. Twin diesels balanced for pursuit load, gun mount under canvas, boarding team with door-width eyes. That's not a rescue configuration. That's a retrieval configuration."

Nobody spoke. Down in the engine room of the world, Bao thought, the truth hummed plain as a shaft alignment, and everybody in this little galley could feel it through the deck.

Voss sat at the galley table with his head in his hands. When he looked up, he looked ten years older than he had that morning. "His name is Renn," he said. "Asset Protection Division. They're the people who arrive after the survey is done, to make sure the survey stays done. I've seen them at three sites. There's always a village that stops being on the maps afterward." He exhaled. "When I scattered my team, I told them: whatever happens, don't be found by the gray boat. Be found by anyone else. Fishermen, smugglers, missionaries, anyone. Because the gray boat doesn't ask questions. It collects answers."

"And now it's collected us," Suri said. Her fever had burned her voice down to embers, but the words came out level. "Anchored a hundred meters behind us. Watching our lights all night."

"Which is why," Paloma said slowly, "tomorrow we run downstream together, in convoy, like good little assets. And somewhere between here and the Split—" she looked around the galley, at each of them in turn, and Bao saw the captain surface in her face like a fish coming up through dark water, "—somewhere in that fog, this convoy is going to have an accident."

Old Wen, who had been silent at the end of the bench, poured tea for everyone without being asked, which was his way of voting yes.

* * *

Later, near midnight, Bao couldn't sleep. He went up on deck to check the anchor watch and found Voss at the stern rail, staring back at the gray boat's lights.

Bao stood beside him. For a while neither of them said anything. The river talked to itself in the dark, the way it always did, sliding secrets downstream.

"My grandmother ran a tea house on the delta," Bao said at last, because the silence was heavier than he liked. "During the bad years. Soldiers came sometimes. Government ones, then other government ones. She always fed them. Always smiled. I asked her once, when I was small, why she was nice to men she was afraid of. She said: *because loyalty is a currency, little one, and you must always know who you are paying, and what they think they are buying.*" He shrugged, embarrassed. "I don't know why I remembered that just now."

"Because it's true," Voss said. "And because you can feel the transaction happening, even if you can't stop it." He turned. In the dark his face was mostly shadow, but his eyes caught the distant running lights. "Bao. Can I tell you something? Since you understand currencies."

Bao nodded.

"Renn thinks he's here to collect a survivor and secure a site. That's what the paperwork says. But I've watched him today, the way you watched his boat, and I think the paperwork is already out of date." Voss's voice dropped. "Six of us went into those caves. Six of us came out, scattered, sworn to silence. Renn was sent to retrieve one geologist and a set of findings. Instead he's going to find out there are six witnesses loose on this river, and a civilian crew that's already heard too much, and a logbook that may or may not still exist." He paused. "People like Renn don't have orders for that situation. They have methods."

"You think he'll hurt us."

"I think," Voss said, "that to a man like that, the difference between an asset and a liability is one signature on one form. And I think he's already drafting it." He looked back at the lights astern. "They're not here to rescue anyone, Bao. They never were. Rescue is what they call it when the collecting goes smoothly."

Bao stood with that a long moment, feeling it settle into his bones the way cold settles into a mooring chain—slowly, and then all at once.

Below decks, Suri slept with her fever finally breaking. At the galley table, Paloma sat awake over Renn's folder, reading every clause twice. And astern, a hundred meters back, the gray boat rode its anchor light, patient as interest accruing.

Tomorrow there would be fog on the water above the Split. Tomorrow there would be an accident.

Bao went below to check the fuel lines one more time, and to make sure the water heater's screws were tight, and to start, quietly, in the back of his machine-shaped mind, planning what kind of accident an engine could have that would leave four people alive and one convoy broken.

The river carried the answer downstream before he had it, whispering to itself in the dark, keeping its receipts.
