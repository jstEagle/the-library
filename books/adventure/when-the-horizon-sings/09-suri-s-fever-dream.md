## 9. Suri's Fever Dream

The fever came in like weather.

One hour Suri was hauling the anchor line on the Magpie Jay's bow, the river sliding green and patient beneath her, and the next her hands wouldn't close around the rope. The world tilted. The rope went soft as kelp. She heard herself say something—she thought it was Bao's name, though it came out as a sound like water finding a crack in stone—and then the deck rose up and met her, kind and cool, and she was grateful to it in a way she would be ashamed of later, grateful the way you are grateful to anything when you can no longer stand.

They carried her below. She knew this because she felt the hatch light strobe across her eyelids, gold, gold, gold, and because she heard Old Wen say, in his flat cook's voice that never once in all her years on the river had sounded afraid, *put her in my bunk, the engine heat will keep her warm.* And then she heard Paloma say, quieter, the way you say things you don't want the river to overhear: *She touched it. She put her whole arm in.*

*It* was the water in the caves. Suri remembered that much, or thought she remembered it, though memory by then was already becoming an unreliable crew member, showing up late, telling stories. She remembered the chamber where the current ran backward. She remembered the hum—not a sound exactly, more a pressure, a tuning fork struck inside her sternum. She remembered reaching in because she was Suri, because she had been reaching into rivers since before she could swim, because her mother had taught her that you test water with your hand before you trust it with your boat.

She remembered the water holding on.

Not grabbing. Holding. The way an old woman holds your wrist in a market to tell you something important. And then—

Then the fever took the rest and rearranged it, and Suri went down into the dark where the river kept everything.

* * *

She dreamed she was small, small as a fish egg, and the water was not around her but *through* her, and it was thinking.

That was the part she would never be able to explain properly, not to Paloma, not to the inquiry officers later, not to herself on cold nights: that the water thought. Not the way a machine thinks. Not the way a person thinks, in sentences, in wants. It thought the way a delta thinks—branching, patient, everything arriving eventually at everything else. It had no name for itself. It had a million names for itself, one for every spring and seep and flooded chamber, and none of them mattered to it, the way your own name doesn't matter to you at three in the morning when you are the only one awake.

It was old. That was the second thing. Old the way stone is old, but stone doesn't know it's old, and this did. It had been in the mountain before the mountain had a river on top of it. It had been in the limestone when the limestone was the floor of a sea. It had watched the Serpent's Reach get carved, drop by patient drop, the way a person watches a child grow and cannot see it happening and yet knows it is happening all the time. It had no word for *government*. It had no word for *diversion*. But it had felt the survey team's instruments the way you feel a mosquito on the back of your neck, and it had felt the plans in their papers—not read them, nothing so crude—had *tasted* the intention in the vibrations of their generators, in the rhythm of their footsteps on its stone, and it had understood, the way a body understands a splinter, that something meant to change the shape of it.

And it had done what anything old does when it is threatened.

It had gone quiet. And the birds, who had been listening to it longer than any human tribe had lived on the Reach, had gone quiet with it.

*You felt that,* the water said to her, or did not say, because saying requires a mouth and it had none, but the meaning arrived in her the way warmth arrives through a wall. *You put your hand in and you felt the shape of the thought.*

*I felt something hum,* Suri answered, in the dream, in whatever language dreams use, which is no language at all and therefore all of them. *It hurt.*

*It always hurts the first time,* the water said. *You are a small thing and I am a large thing and the truth is heavy no matter how gently it is set down.*

And then it showed her.

* * *

It showed her the channels.

Not the ones on Old Wen's charts, the ones inked in careful faded lines with soundings in fathoms and names in three languages. The *real* ones. The ones the river kept the way a miser keeps coins, hidden, counted, never spent. It showed her the channel behind the Singing Bluff that only ran in the wet months and was dry stone now but would be deep water again in nine days—*nine,* the water said, and the number landed in her chest like a stone dropped down a well, and she knew it was true the way she knew her own heartbeat. It showed her the false channel that every chart in every pilot house on the Reach marked as the main passage, the one that had wrecked the *Cormorant* eleven years ago and drowned four people, because the river had moved its deep water in a single flood season and let the maps go on lying. It showed her the throat of rock below Kestrel Shoals where the channel narrowed to less than a boat's width and the current ran at eight knots and there was a seam—there was always a seam—where the two currents leaned against each other and made a lane of almost-calm, a hand's width of mercy, if you knew where it was.

She knew where it was.

She knew it the way she knew the scar on her left knee. Not learned. *Had.* The knowledge was in her body, in her balance, in the soles of her feet, and it was the most terrible gift she had ever been given, because it was not hers. It belonged to the water. She was only holding it, the way you hold a bird—lightly, knowing it can leave, knowing it was never yours.

*Why me,* she asked the dark. *Why my hand. There were six of us at the cave mouth.*

*You asked,* the water said. *The others looked. You asked. You put your hand in the way your mother taught you, to test before you trust, and asking is a door, and I have been alone a very long time, and a door is a door.*

*What do you want?*

The water was quiet for a long time. In the dream, quiet lasted years. She grew old in the quiet. She watched the mountain rise and wear down and rise again.

*Nothing,* it said at last, and the word was so vast and so tired that she wept, or would have wept if she had had eyes in that place. *Nothing. That is what I am. The nothing under everything. I want only to continue, the way rain wants to fall and stone wants to be still. But your kind has drawn a line on paper that will cut me in half, and I cannot draw lines on paper, and so I have been waiting for someone to come who could. You are not the first. There was a man—*

And the dream shuddered, and the water's voice went strange, and Suri felt it holding something back the way a river holds back behind a weir, all its weight leaning on a thin bright wall.

*There was a man who put his whole self in,* the water said. *Not his hand. His self. And he came out of the water and he ran and he has been running ever since, and he is starving, and he is afraid, and he is the only one of his kind who knows what you now know, and he is close to you, closer than you think, and he does not trust you, and he is right not to trust you, because you have not yet been tested.*

*Tested by what?*

*By the ones who drew the line,* the water said. *They are coming upstream. They are coming with guns and paper and a boat that runs quiet, and they will arrive when the birds return, and the birds will return when I tell them, and I am telling them now, because you asked, and asking is a door, and doors open both ways.*

And Suri understood, in the way you understand things in fevers, completely and uselessly, that she had not been chosen. She had been *used*—gently, even kindly, but used. A messenger. A door. And the ones coming upstream would want to close her the way you close a door that lets in a draft.

*Am I going to die?* she asked.

*Everyone is going to die,* the water said, with the terrible patience of geology. *The question the river asks is not whether. It is what shape the water takes while it is passing through. You are passing through now. Pay attention.*

* * *

She surfaced once, briefly, into the bunk in Old Wen's cabin, into the smell of diesel and camphor and the cook's spare blanket, rough wool that smelled of engine oil and star anise. Bao was there, sitting on the deck plate with his back against the bulkhead, his huge engineer's hands wrapped around a mug of something he wasn't drinking. The lantern swung with the river's motion and his shadow swung with the lantern, and his face was doing something she had never seen it do, which was nothing—absolutely nothing, all the worry held so tight behind it that the surface had gone still, the way the Reach went still above the deep holes.

"You're awake," he said, and his voice cracked on the second word like green wood in a fire.

"Water," she said. Her throat was a dry channel. "The water thinks, Bao."

"Shh." He set the mug down and pressed the back of his hand to her forehead, and his hand was cool and calloused and shaking, which frightened her more than the fever had, because Bao's hands never shook, Bao's hands held a boat together with wire and stubbornness through two monsoons and a grounding on the Devil's Teeth. "Don't talk. Paloma says fever talk doesn't count. Fever talk is just the engine running hot."

"It's not fever talk." She gripped his wrist. She was surprised by her own strength, or by how little of it there was. "The birds. Ask Old Wen about the birds. Ask him why they left the water between the Bluff and the Shoals. The water told them to go. It's been telling them things longer than we've had boats. And there's a seam below Kestrel Shoals, on the east side where the current leans—"

"Stop." But his hand had gone still on her forehead, and his eyes had gone somewhere else, somewhere behind his face, and she knew that look. It was the look of an engineer hearing a sound in an engine he has just told himself is nothing. "Stop, Suri. Sleep."

"Nine days," she said. "The monsoon comes in nine days. And something else comes before that."

He pulled the blanket up over her shoulders with hands that were no longer shaking, which meant they had passed through shaking and come out the other side, and she let the dark take her back, because the water was waiting there, and the water had more to show her.

* * *

The second dream was not kind.

In the second dream the water showed her the line on the paper. She saw it as the water saw it—not ink, not words, but a *wound in advance*, a scar scheduled. A dam at the upper Reach, a diversion channel cut through limestone to feed fields and furnaces two hundred miles away, and the flooded caves drained to a dead trickle, and the aquifer—the living, thinking, ancient aquifer—cut in half like a snake with a shovel. And downstream of the cut, every spring that fed every village from the Bluff to the sea would fail within a season. She saw the wells going dry in the villages she had grown up trading with. She saw the fishing camps emptying. She saw her mother's clinic in the lowland town, the clinic that needed money for medicine and a roof, standing in a district where the taps ran brown and then ran nothing.

*This is what the paper wants,* the water said. *The man who ran—the man who is starving and afraid—he saw this too. He helped draw it. That is why he cannot sleep. That is why he tried to break your radio. He was not trying to keep you from calling for help. He was trying to keep you from calling* them.

And Suri, in the dream, understood Dr. Elias Voss—not his name, she didn't have his name yet, but his shape, his weight, the particular gravity of a man carrying a thing too heavy to put down and too terrible to hold. She understood him the way the water understood him, which was without mercy and without judgment, the way water understands everything it carries.

*What do I do?* she asked.

*You already know the channels,* the water said. *That is not the question I gave you the channels to answer.*

*Then what is?*

*The question,* said the water, and its voice was everywhere now, in the hull of her, in the timbers of the boat, in the rain beginning far upstream, *is who the water belongs to. The government thinks it belongs to the paper. The man thinks it belongs to no one, and that no one can save it. And you—you have not decided yet. Decide soon. The rain is deciding for you.*

* * *

She woke at dawn, or what she took for dawn, in a cabin full of grey light and the smell of rain on hot iron. The fever had broken in the night; she knew it the way you know a storm has passed, by the strange washed cleanliness of everything, by the ache in her body like a shoreline after a surge. Old Wen was gone from his bunk. From somewhere above her came the clatter of the galley, the good sound of the wok, the ordinary music of a boat being a boat.

She climbed the ladder on legs that felt borrowed. The morning was low and silver, mist lying on the river in long unbroken sheets, and the Reach was so still it looked painted. Paloma stood at the wheel, one hand on the spoke, watching the water with the expression she wore when the river was telling her something she didn't like. Bao was at the rail with the engine room's grease still on his forearms, and when he saw Suri he came to her in three fast strides and took her elbow as if she might blow overboard.

"You look terrible," he said, which from Bao meant *I have not slept and I have been afraid.*

"I feel," Suri said, and stopped, because the feeling was too large for the sentence. She looked out at the mist, at the water sliding past the hull, and the knowledge rose in her like a fish rising to take a fly—unbidden, effortless, hers and not hers. "There's a snag off the port bow," she said. "Fifty meters. Old teak, half-submerged, been there since the flood two years ago. The charts don't have it. Paloma, come left ten degrees. Now. Before the mist lifts and you can see it."

Paloma turned from the wheel and looked at her for a long moment, and Suri watched her do the arithmetic—fever, delirium, impossible knowledge, the cost of being wrong at this speed in this water.

"Come left ten degrees," Paloma said quietly, and turned the wheel.

The Magpie Jay leaned. The mist slid past. And fifty meters down the old track, exactly where Suri's bones said it would be, the black shoulder of a drowned teak log broke the surface and slid by close enough to touch, streaming, silent, patient as everything else the river kept.

Nobody spoke. The wok clatter in the galley had stopped. Old Wen stood in the companionway with a spatula in his hand, looking at Suri as if seeing her for the first time, and on his old flat cook's face was the expression of a man watching a chart redraw itself.

Then the mist ahead thinned, just for a moment, just enough—and Suri, whose eyes were now the river's eyes whether she wanted them or not, saw the shape in the fog downstream and felt her stomach drop through the deck plates.

"Paloma," she said. "There's a boat ahead of us. Running without lights. It's been following the same channels I just told you about."
