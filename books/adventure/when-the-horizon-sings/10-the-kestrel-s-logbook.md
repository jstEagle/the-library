## 10. The Kestrel's Logbook

Bao had never trusted a lock he couldn't hear.

Every machine spoke, if you listened long enough. The Magpie Jay's engine told him when her bearings were tired before the vibration reached the deck. The generator hummed a half-tone flat on humid nights. Even the river had a voice—a low conversation of silt and current that changed pitch around sandbars and drowned trees. So when he knelt in the Kestrel's chart room with a pry bar and a flashlight clamped between his teeth, staring at the steel document case bolted beneath the captain's desk, he listened first.

The lock said nothing.

That was the problem. Good locks were supposed to whisper their resistance—pins grinding, tumblers sulking. This one was silent as a stone, and silence in Bao's experience meant either very cheap or very expensive, and government hardware was never cheap. He set down the pry bar and took out his own tools instead: a jeweler's screwdriver worn to a nub, a length of piano wire he'd straightened against his knee, and a small tin of graphite that smelled like pencil shavings and old promises.

Behind him, the Kestrel breathed.

He'd been alone aboard for an hour while Paloma and Suri slept—or tried to—on the Magpie Jay, moored hull-to-hull in the shadow of the survey vessel's stern. Old Wen's absence sat in the boat like ballast nobody had asked for. Nobody said his name. They didn't have to; it was there in the untouched fourth bowl at dinner, in the way Suri still turned toward the wheel when something needed navigating, finding only Paloma's hands where Wen's should have been.

Bao worked because work was the only prayer he knew.

The wire found the first pin. He closed his eyes and let his fingers do the listening now, reading the lock through the tension in the wire the way Old Wen used to read weather through the ache in his hip. Second pin. Third. The fourth fought him—stubborn, oiled recently, maintained by someone who cared—and Bao felt something almost like respect. Whoever had sealed this log had wanted it hard to open. Which meant whoever had sealed it had known exactly how much it was worth.

*Paper with teeth,* Paloma had called the contract. Well. Here were the teeth themselves, all grown into one small steel jaw.

The fifth pin gave with a click so soft he felt it more than heard it, and the dial spun free.

He sat back on his heels and wiped his palms on his coveralls. Through the porthole, the night over the Serpent's Reach was the color of wet slate, the monsoon clouds stacked up in the northwest like cargo nobody would claim. Nine days, Old Wen had said. Maybe eight now. The air tasted like iron and green rot, the smell of a sky getting ready to break.

Bao lifted the lid.

Inside: a ledger bound in oilcloth, its spine cracked from use rather than neglect. A bundle of survey charts tied with waxed cord. And beneath them, wrapped separately in plastic sheeting, a second notebook—smaller, private, the handwriting cramped and hurried, nothing like the measured print of official documents. Two logs, then. The one they were meant to keep, and the one someone had kept anyway.

He started with the official ledger, because that was what he'd come for, and because starting with the other one felt like reading someone's letters.

The early entries were dull as dredging reports. Depth soundings. Water chemistry. Core samples logged by station number. Dr. Elias Voss's initials appeared on every third page, neat and authoritative, a man signing off on a world he thought he understood. Bao skimmed forward, past the routine, past the equipment notes, until the dates grew close together and the handwriting began to change—not Voss's anymore but someone else's, a hydrologist named Imara Chen whose script slanted hard left, as if she were writing while walking away from something.

*Station 14. The aquifer is not a pocket. It is a system. Repeating: SYSTEM. Extent exceeds all projections. Flow rates suggest recharge zones far outside the surveyed basin.*

*Station 17. Acoustic anomaly persists. Chen insists the water is "communicating." I've asked her to keep observations within parameters. —V.*

There it was. Bao read the line three times. *Keep observations within parameters.* The phrase of a man building a wall out of vocabulary. He'd heard engineers talk like that his whole career—men who believed that if you named a thing narrowly enough, it couldn't grow teeth.

But Chen's entries kept growing teeth.

*They've stopped asking us to map the system. Now they're asking us to model it under diversion scenarios. Diversion. As if you could divert a living thing and call it engineering.*

Bao untied the bundle of charts with fingers that had gone strangely cold. He spread the topmost sheet across the desk, weighting the corners with the flashlight, a spool of solder, his tin of graphite.

It took him a full minute to understand what he was looking at, and when he understood it, he wished he hadn't.

The aquifer was drawn in blue—the whole vast hidden watershed of the Reach, branching underground like the root system of some impossible tree, feeding springs and seeps and the slow blind lakes beneath the karst. And over it, in red, precise as surgery, the government's plan: a dam line across the upper gorge. A diversion tunnel bored through the ridge. Intake stations marked at every point where the blue veins ran shallowest.

And downstream of the dam line, in the fine print of the legend, a table of projected impacts. *Reduced dry-season flow: 71%. Wetland loss: total. Settlements affected.*

He counted the settlements twice because the first time he was sure he'd misread.

Eleven. Eleven named villages and fishing camps, the ones they'd passed all week going quiet, the ones whose boats they hadn't seen on the water, whose birds had vanished from the reeds. Not abandoned yet. Not yet. But every one of them sat on a stretch of river that the red lines would strangle. The flooded caves weren't the danger. The flooded caves were the *symptom*. The danger was coming downstream, wearing a government seal, scheduled and budgeted and signed off by men who would never smell this water.

*They're not going to find the villages,* Bao thought. *They're going to replace them. On paper first. Then with water—or the lack of it.*

His hands were shaking. He noticed it the way you notice rain starting—one drop, then the sound of all of them.

He opened the private notebook.

The handwriting here was Voss's, but a different Voss—unhinged, urgent, the letters of a man writing fast in the dark. The earliest entries matched the official log, professional and dry. Then, dated six days before the Kestrel was found empty:

*I went down into Station 19 myself today. I don't have language for what the water did. Chen was right. It responds. Not randomly—I've been telling myself randomly for two weeks—but deliberately. Patterns in the current that repeat when we repeat our signals. It has been listening to our instruments this entire time. We are not surveying a resource. We are shouting in the house of something old.*

*The diversion models are wrong. Not inaccurate—wrong. They treat the aquifer as static volume. If you dam the upper gorge, the pressure redistribution will do things no model predicts. The system will reroute. It will drown chambers that have been dry since before there were names for dry. And the lower Reach—*

The entry stopped mid-sentence. The next page resumed, later, the ink smeared as if written with wet hands:

*I pulled the final impact assessment from the secure file. Full inundation modeling for the lower basin under failure scenario D-4. They've run the numbers on the dam failing. They know the diversion raises catastrophic flood risk downstream by a factor of six. Six. And the assessment concludes, in the language of these things, that "residual risk falls within acceptable tolerances for the affected population."*

*Affected population. That's what we are to them. A tolerance.*

*I can't sign the certification. If I refuse alone, they replace me and sign it anyway. So we made another plan. Seven of us. We scatter. We take copies. We go dark until the monsoon, and then we surface in different ports with the same story, and no one man can be discredited or disappeared. Renn's people think we're lost. Let them keep thinking it.*

*If anyone finds this log: the team did not vanish. We left. Every supply stack, every cold engine, every trackless mudbank—we arranged it to look like accident because accident gets searched for and desertion gets shot at. We are not lost. We are witnesses, dispersed.*

*God forgive me for the years I spent measuring this river without once asking what it wanted.*

Bao sat very still.

The flashlight beam trembled slightly on the page, and he realized the tremor was in his hand, in his arm, in his chest—a slow earthquake with nowhere to put itself. He thought of Suri burning with fever after touching that water, speaking channels she'd never sounded, and the fever breaking into knowledge. He thought of Voss half-starved in the jungle, begging them to turn back, sabotaging the radio—not madness then, or not only madness. Arithmetic. One radio transmitting from the upper Reach might bring Renn's people running before the truth could get downstream.

He thought of Old Wen, who had piloted this river for forty years, who had died at the wheel of a boat he loved, shielding a girl who carried the river's own song in her blood. Wen had never needed a logbook to know the Reach was alive. He'd have laughed—that low, wheezing laugh—at the idea that a government could divert it like plumbing. *The river keeps its receipts,* he always said. Bao had thought it was just an old man's saying about debts and honesty.

It was a warning. It had always been a warning.

Footsteps on the deck above. Soft, bare feet—he knew Suri's walk, the careful way she moved even now, weeks after her fever, as though the deck might be water. Then Paloma's boots behind her, heavier, and the low murmur of voices he couldn't make out.

Bao looked at the two logs lying open under the light. The official record and the true one. The wall of vocabulary and the confession that broke through it. Somewhere downstream, eleven settlements were sleeping tonight in houses that existed only until a signature took effect. Somewhere upstream, six scattered witnesses were hiding in the green dark, waiting for the monsoon to cover their surfacing. And somewhere closer than any of that, Renn's patrol boat was out there in the black water, hunting the same paper Bao held in his shaking hands.

He understood now why the contract had been built like a trap. Why the secrecy clauses had penalties sized to ruin them. It wasn't to protect a discovery.

It was to protect a decision already made.

Paloma's boots came down the companionway ladder. She filled the doorway of the chart room, hair loose, eyes rimmed red with a tiredness that wasn't from sleeplessness alone. Suri crowded behind her, thin still, her mother's clinic and her mother's village both sitting somewhere in her face.

"You found it," Paloma said. Not a question. She'd seen his face.

"I found it." Bao's voice came out rougher than he intended. He turned the chart around so the red lines faced them, and put his finger on the dam site, and dragged it slowly downstream, over the blue veins, over the printed names. "I need you to look at this. All of it. Because I've read it three times and I keep hoping I'm wrong, and I'm not wrong."

Suri leaned in. He watched her read the legend, watch her lips move over *settlements affected*, watched her stop breathing.

"That's my mother's camp," she whispered. "That's—" Her finger touched the name and stayed there, pressing into the paper as if she could hold the place on the map the way you'd hold a wound closed.

"Read Voss's notebook," Bao said quietly. "Both of them. Then tell me whose side the river is on."

Paloma took the notebook. She read standing, fast, her jaw tightening page by page, and when she reached the entry about the seven of them scattering, she looked up at Bao with an expression he'd seen maybe twice in ten years on the water—the expression of a woman recalculating everything, course and cargo and crew, all at once.

"They didn't disappear," she said.

"No."

"They *scattered*. Like seed."

"Like witnesses," Bao said. "Like people who figured out that staying together just makes you easier to bury."

Outside, far off in the northwest, thunder rolled along the cloud-stacks—long and low, almost harmonic, almost like the hum that lived in the flooded caves. Suri lifted her head at the sound, and Bao saw her listen to it the way he listened to engines: knowing it, answering it.

Then, from the direction of the river, cutting across the storm-mumble, came a sound that froze all three of them where they stood.

An engine. Deep-throated, diesel-heavy, running without lights.

Coming upstream.

* * *

Paloma killed the lantern with the flat of her hand and the chart room went black except for the gray breath of the porthole. In the dark, Bao heard her breathing change—slow, deliberate, the rhythm of a woman putting her fear somewhere it wouldn't interfere with her hands.

"Renn," Suri breathed.

"Maybe." Paloma's voice was barely air. "Or maybe his friends. Either way, nobody runs an engine dark upriver at midnight unless they already know where they're going." A pause. "Which means somebody told them where we are."

Bao's stomach dropped through the hull. He thought of the relay tower on the ridge, of Voss's scattered teammates, of the words *no one man can be discredited or disappeared*—and how easily a plan built on seven people could be undone by one of them wanting to go home.

"The logbook," he whispered. "We can't let it be aboard if they board us."

Paloma was already moving in the dark, a shape and a purpose. "Bao, wrap it. Oilcloth, plastic, double-bagged—make it survive anything short of fire. Suri, wake Voss. Tell him if this is a betrayal, I want to know which of his precious witnesses sold us before I decide whether to believe anything else he's ever said."

"And you?" Suri asked.

Paloma stood a moment in the porthole's gray light, and Bao saw her look down at the chart spread on the desk—at the eleven names, at the red lines, at the river rendered as a thing to be spent.

"I'm going to get us ready to run," she said. "Because whatever's coming up that channel—it's coming for this paper. And as of tonight, this paper is the only voice those villages have left."

Out on the black water, the engine note deepened, rounded the bend, and began to grow.
