## 11. Renn's Offer

The rain came at dusk the way it always did in the upper Reach—not falling so much as arriving, a gray curtain drawn across the world from one bank to the other. Paloma stood under the Magpie Jay's awning and watched it stitch itself into the river, ten thousand small punctures that healed instantly, and she thought about how a man could stand in weather like this and still feel watched.

Renn had been aboard for six hours. He'd come alongside in a black-hulled patrol boat with no markings worth reading, four men who moved like they'd been poured into their gear rather than dressed in it, and a smile he wore the way other men wore sidearms—visible, holstered, meant to be noticed without being drawn. He'd drunk her coffee. He'd complimented Bao's engine work through the open hatch, loudly enough to be heard over the generator. He'd asked Suri how her fever was coming along with a gentleness that made Paloma's skin crawl, because it was the gentleness of a man checking inventory.

Now the rain had driven everyone below or under canvas, and Renn found her alone at the stern rail, watching the water go silver-dark.

"Captain Fairweather." He settled beside her, close enough to share warmth, far enough to be polite. He was good at distances. "Mind if I ask you something honest?"

"You can ask," Paloma said. "Honesty's a different transaction."

That earned her a real laugh, short and surprised, and for half a second she saw the man underneath—the one who'd probably been decent once, before whatever machine had gotten hold of him. Then the smile reassembled itself and the contractor came back.

"Fair enough." He turned his face to the rain. "You've been on this river your whole life. That right? Your mother before you?"

"Her mother before her."

"And you know every channel from the delta to the headwaters better than any survey map the government ever printed."

"I know where the maps are wrong," Paloma said. "Which is most of them."

"Mm." He nodded slowly, as if she'd confirmed something he'd already paid to learn. "Here's my problem, Captain. I have a job. The job is: recover government property, secure government personnel, and ensure the integrity of an ongoing hydrological assessment. That's what my contract says, word for word—I can recite it in my sleep, and some nights I do." A pause. The rain filled it. "But contracts are paper, and rivers are rivers. And I look at you, and I see a woman who understands the difference."

Paloma said nothing. She'd learned young that silence was the cheapest bait on the Reach, and the fish that mattered always bit hardest when you didn't tug the line.

Renn reached inside his coat—not fast, deliberately slow, showing her his hand the whole way—and brought out a folded document, waterproof stock, heavy as a promise. He didn't offer it. He just held it against the rail, letting the rain drum its plastic sleeve.

"The Kestrel's crew scattered," he said. "Eight people walked away from a fully provisioned survey vessel and vanished into jungle that would kill a trained soldier in three days. Except they're not dead—we both know that, because Dr. Voss is sitting in your galley eating Old Wen's rice like a man who knows exactly where his next meal is coming from." His voice stayed level, almost bored. "And somewhere in this boat is a logbook that eight professionals decided was worth ruining their careers, their pensions, and quite possibly their lives to keep out of official hands."

"You assume I have it."

"I assume nothing. I pay for certainty; assumption is for amateurs." He finally turned to look at her, and his eyes were patient and gray and utterly without warmth. "So here's the honest question I promised you. What would it take—for you, personally, Captain Fairweather—to deliver Dr. Voss and any documents recovered from the Kestrel downstream to the district office at Port Callow, intact and unexamined, and to remember this trip afterward as a routine charter that ended in disappointment?"

Paloma looked at the river. The rain had thickened until the far bank was a rumor. Somewhere upstream, past the black shoulder of the ridge, the caves were doing whatever it was they did—that hum Suri swore she could still feel through the hull even now, a note too low for ears, felt in the teeth, in the ribs, in the old wood of the boat herself. *Water that moves against current.* She hadn't told Renn about that. She wondered if he knew. She suspected there was very little he didn't know, and that the things he didn't know were the only things worth wanting.

"How much?" she said, because not asking would tell him more than asking did.

Renn unfolded the document. "Base figure: two hundred thousand, certified draft, payable on delivery at Port Callow. Plus debt forgiveness." He glanced at her, quick and precise. "Yes, we know about the debt. The engine loan against this vessel, held by Meridian Trade Consortium, called due in eleven weeks. One hundred forty thousand outstanding. It goes away. Clean slate. You own your boat free and clear for the first time since—" he checked a card in his breast pocket, though Paloma was certain he didn't need to— "since your father died and you refinanced to bury him."

The rain kept falling. Paloma kept her hands loose on the rail and her breathing even, and hated him a little for knowing, and hated herself more for the way the number landed anyway—two hundred thousand plus one hundred forty, three hundred and forty thousand reasons, laid out neat as cargo on a manifest. She thought of the Magpie Jay's engine, the way it coughed on cold starts now, Bao nursing it with wire and prayer and parts cannibalized from two dead boats. She thought of Suri's mother's clinic with its leaking roof and its medicine cabinet that held more hope than inventory. She thought of her own berth, and the ledger she kept in a oilcloth packet under it, and the column of red that had been growing for six years like mold up a wall.

She thought of Old Wen saying, three nights ago, his pipe glowing in the dark of the wheelhouse: *A river doesn't care what you owe, girl. It only cares what you carry.*

"That's a lot of money," Paloma said.

"It's a fair price for a fair service."

"It's a lot of money for delivering one half-starved geologist and a book."

"It's a lot of money," Renn agreed, "for delivering them *quietly*. The fee isn't for the cargo, Captain. The fee is for the discretion. Anyone can run a boat downriver. Not everyone can run a boat downriver and arrive having forgotten what they saw." He tapped the document once. "There's a non-disclosure rider. Standard. You'll never speak of the aquifer, the caves, the survey, or this conversation. Not to port authorities, not to newsrooms, not to your crew—though frankly, your crew signing the same rider would simplify everyone's evening."

"And if I don't sign?"

Renn sighed, and it was a good sigh, well-rehearsed, regretful. He let the rain fill another pause, and when he spoke again his voice had dropped into a register that was almost kind, which was somehow worse than everything that had come before.

"Then I proceed under my original authority. Which includes salvage rights." He nodded forward, toward the bow, toward the whole wet dark length of her. "This vessel is operating under an emergency charter with lapsed check-ins, carrying restricted government material and an unsecured person of interest, in a flood zone, without valid transit documentation—I've read your papers, Captain, and your transit stamp expired at Kilometer Post 300, which was four days ago. In the event of non-cooperation, maritime law gives me a great deal of latitude. I could impound the Magpie Jay tonight. Chain her to my tender, put a prize crew aboard, and tow her to Port Callow as salvage, where she'd be auctioned to cover 'operational costs.' Your crew would be put ashore wherever it was convenient. Dr. Voss would come with me regardless—he comes with me either way, Captain, that part isn't negotiable, and I'd rather do it with your help than over your deck."

He said it all gently. That was the thing she'd remember. Not the threat but the tenderness of it, the care he took, like a surgeon explaining an amputation.

"But," Renn went on, "I'd genuinely prefer not to. Salvage is paperwork. Auctions are paperwork. And honestly—" and here something flickered across his face that might, in a better man, have been fatigue— "I've seen what happens to boats that get taken as salvage on this river. They come apart. Bad moorings, bad weather, bad luck. A boat like yours deserves better than a chain and an auction block. So does her captain."

Paloma turned the document over in her hands without opening it. Waterproof stock. Certified draft numbers already printed in the payment line, which meant the money existed, which meant this wasn't a bluff, which meant somewhere in a government office a man with a clean desk had signed off on buying a riverboat captain's silence for less than the cost of one dam turbine blade. That detail frightened her more than Renn's patrol boat. Threats were weather; budgets were climate.

Three hundred and forty thousand. Free and clear. Suri's mother's clinic roofed and stocked. Bao's engine replaced with something that purred instead of prayed. Her name off Meridian's books. She could run charters for the rest of her life and never feel that cold breath on her neck again, the one that sounded like *final notice*.

And downstream, behind the dam they'd build on the aquifer's throat, the water would rise. Not the river's rise, which came and went with the seasons and which every family on the Reach had learned to read like a grandmother's moods—but a rise that never fell. Village by village. The fishing camps already empty. The birds already gone from the stretches where they always gathered, because birds know first, birds always know first. Forty years of graves going under. Old Wen's confluence, where he meant to be buried someday, becoming a depth sounding on somebody's navigation chart.

*They're not here to rescue anyone,* Voss had whispered, and Voss had been right, and here was the proof priced at two hundred thousand plus debt forgiveness, payable on delivery.

Paloma handed the document back.

Renn took it without expression. "You want time to consider."

"No," Paloma said. "I want you to understand what you're actually offering, so you can report accurately to whoever writes your checks. You're offering me money to help drown the Reach." She kept her voice low, conversational, pitched under the rain. "You're offering me the price of my freedom in exchange for helping make sure nobody else on this river ever has theirs. And you're doing it politely, on my deck, drinking my coffee, while my cook feeds your operation's prisoner rice in my galley." She turned to face him fully then, and let him see whatever it was in her face that made men on the docks step back without knowing why. "So here's my counteroffer, Mr. Renn. You take your paper, and your tender, and your four gentlemen, and you go back to Port Callow, and you tell them the Fairweather charter is void. Tell them the captain ran aground on principle. Tell them whatever keeps your paperwork tidy. But get off my boat."

For a long moment Renn simply looked at her, and the rain came down between them like a curtain neither of them could see through, and Paloma understood that she was seeing him decide something, and that the deciding was not going her way.

"You know what I admire about you, Captain?" he said at last, folding the document away into his coat, unhurried, a man closing a ledger. "Nothing. But I respect you, which is rarer and more useful." He stepped back from the rail, and the smile returned, holstered. "Check-ins resume tomorrow at oh-six-hundred. My superiors expect progress reports. I'd think hard, between now and then, about which version of tomorrow morning you'd prefer—the easy one, or the salvage one." He paused at the edge of the awning, silhouetted against the gray. "Oh—and Captain? The fever girl. Suri. When the monsoon seals the upper Reach, medical evacuation becomes impossible. If her condition deteriorates, that will weigh on your decision-making. I mention it only as a matter of logistics."

Then he was gone, boots ringing down the ladder to his tender, and the black patrol boat pulled away into the rain with its running lights doused, and Paloma stood alone at the rail with her hands flat on the wet wood and realized she was shaking—not from fear, or not only from fear, but from the specific tremor of a woman who has just heard the exact price of her soul and discovered, to her surprise and terror, that she cannot afford to sell.

* * *

Below, the galley lamp burned yellow. Old Wen had gone to his bunk an hour ago, but the rice pot still breathed its steam, and Voss sat at the table turning a spoon over and over without eating, and Suri lay in the corner berth with her fever-flushed face turned toward the sound of Paloma's boots on the stairs.

Bao looked up from the engine manual he wasn't reading. "Well?"

"He offered three hundred and forty thousand and a clean slate," Paloma said. "You, me, the boat, all of it. Deliver Voss and the logbook to Port Callow, forget the aquifer exists."

Suri stirred. "And if you'd said yes?"

"I'd be rich," Paloma said. "And every village from here to the delta would be a lake with names on it."

Voss set the spoon down. His hands were steadier than they'd been five days ago; the food was doing its work, and shame was doing the rest. "He told you what happens if you refuse."

"He told me the boat becomes salvage at dawn." Paloma lowered herself onto the bench, and the bench creaked the way the old boat creaked, familiar as a heartbeat. "He told me Suri's fever makes me vulnerable. He told me Voss comes with him either way." She looked around the little lamplit room—at Bao's broad worried back, at Suri's bright fevered eyes, at the sealed logbook sitting in its oilcloth on the shelf above the stove where Old Wen kept the tea, disguised among the tins. "He didn't tell me anything I didn't already know. Except one thing."

"What's that?" Bao asked.

Paloma reached up and took the logbook down from the shelf, and held it a moment, feeling its weight, feeling through the oilcloth the faint vibration that shouldn't have been there and was—the hum, the deep note, the river's voice carried in paper and ink.

"He showed me his budget," she said. "They've already paid for our silence. It's a line item. Which means they're not worried about us at all." She looked up, and the exhaustion in her face hardened into something else, something with edges. "Which means they haven't imagined what we might do next. Get the engine ready, Bao. Both engines. Wake Old Wen."

Bao frowned. "Cap'n—it's the middle of the night, and there's a patrol boat out there in the fog—"

"I know," Paloma said, and stood, and the logbook felt warm in her hands like a living thing. "That's why we're leaving now. While he thinks he's already bought us."

Above them, unseen through the rain and the dark, the river went on moving against its own current, patient as memory, waiting to see what its people would carry.
