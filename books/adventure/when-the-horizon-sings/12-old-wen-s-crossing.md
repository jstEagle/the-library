## 12. Old Wen's Crossing

The rain came in the afternoon like a debt collector—first a few fat drops that struck the deck with the weight of coins, then the sky simply opened and gave up pretending to be anything but what it was. Monsoon weather, three days early. Old Wen had called it at dawn, standing at the wheel with his face tipped into the wind, reading the air the way other men read scripture.

"Smell it," he'd said to me. "Green rot and iron. The river's counting down, Paloma. It always counts before it collects."

I hadn't wanted to hear it. I'd been too busy counting myself—the hours until Renn's check-in schedule tightened around our throats, the pages of the Kestrel's logbook hidden beneath the floor panel by my bunk, the distance between us and the relay tower on the ridge. Nine days had become seven. Seven was becoming a rumor.

Now I stood under the wheelhouse awning watching Renn's patrol boat ride at anchor fifty meters off our starboard beam, gray as a knife blade in the rain, and I understood that Old Wen had been right about more than the weather.

* * *

Renn came aboard at four o'clock with two of his men and an umbrella he didn't need, because the awning over his shoulders was enough theater for him. He was the kind of man who treated courtesy as camouflage. Everything about him was pressed, buttoned, reasonable. His voice never rose above the register of a man discussing freight rates.

"Captain Fairweather." He settled into the chair Bao usually occupied in the wheelhouse, uninvited, and arranged his hands on his knees like a patient man waiting for a bus. "Storm's coming early. I thought we should discuss logistics."

"Logistics," I said.

"The transfer of materials. Dr. Voss. Any documents recovered from the survey site." His eyes moved across the wheelhouse with the unhurried appetite of a customs inspector—over the chart table, the brass fittings Old Wen polished every Sunday, the little clay teapot Wen kept warming on the engine housing because he said a cold wheelhouse made for cold decisions. "You've been cooperative so far. I'd hate to see that change."

"We haven't found any documents," I said. "The Kestrel was stripped clean."

"Stripped clean." He smiled the way ice smiles. "Dr. Voss is a thorough man. But thorough men leave traces. Paper especially. Paper wants to be found—it's the whole reason paper exists." He stood, smoothing his jacket. "Tomorrow morning, then. My boat will come alongside at first light. We'll take Voss and whatever you've recovered downstream together. Safer, in this weather, to run in company."

There it was. Not a request—a schedule. A noose drawn one loop tighter, and he wanted me to watch him do it.

"I'll have the coffee on," I said.

He paused at the wheelhouse door, rain streaming behind him, and looked back at me with something almost like kindness, which from Renn was the most frightening thing yet. "You strike me as a woman who does the arithmetic, Captain. Do it tonight. All of it. Some numbers only work out once."

Then he was gone, down the ladder, across the gunwale gap, and his boat's engines woke with a sound like something swallowing.

* * *

Bao found me in the hold twenty minutes later, sitting on my bunk with the floor panel open and the logbook in my lap. He didn't ask how I was doing. He sat down across from me, knees nearly touching mine in the narrow space, and waited the way he always waited—for the machinery of me to finish turning over before he tried to start it.

"He knows," I said finally. "Or he suspects. Either way, tomorrow morning he comes aboard and he doesn't leave without it."

"So we don't be here tomorrow morning."

"In this?" I nodded upward at the drumming on the hull. "Wen says the channels will be soup. And if we run, they run faster. That boat of his has twin diesels and a hull that doesn't care what it hits."

Bao turned the logbook over in his hands, careful, the way he handled everything—engines, people, grief. "Then we copy it. Photograph every page. Give him the book and keep the truth."

"And when he reads it and realizes the truth is worth more than the paper?"

Bao was quiet a long moment. Rain filled the silence. Somewhere above us, Suri's fevered voice murmured through the deckboards—she'd been sleeping in snatches, dreaming aloud in a language that wasn't quite any language, describing currents and chambers and a hum she said lived underneath everything.

"Paloma." Bao set the book down between us. "When I was a boy in Chienzhou, my father kept a shop. One day the tax men came and said the shop owed triple what it owed. My father paid. Then they came again and said it again. He paid again. Third time, my mother took the ledger and burned it in the street and told them: now there is nothing to argue about." He looked at me. "I am not saying burn the logbook. I am saying some men cannot be paid. They can only be outrun, or outlived."

"Outrun, then. Tonight. In the storm."

"Wen won't like it."

"Wen doesn't have to like it. Wen just has to find us water."

But when we climbed up into the wheelhouse, Old Wen was already there, and he had heard, because Old Wen always heard, and he was looking at the rain with an expression I had seen only twice before in eleven years—once when we ran the Narrows ahead of a flash flood, and once when Suri's mother died.

"You want to cross the Serpent's Throat in this," he said. Not a question.

"If we stay, Renn takes everything tomorrow. Voss, the logbook, probably us."

"Mm." He touched the wheel—not gripping it, just resting his palm against the wood, the way a man touches the shoulder of an old friend. "The Throat in monsoon rain is a coin toss. You know that."

"I know that."

"And the girl." His eyes went aft, toward the cabin where Suri lay burning. "She can't stand a crossing like that. Her fever, the G-forces, the cold water shipping over the bow—"

"She'll die anyway if Renn gets her," Bao said quietly. "You saw what his men carry. You saw how they look at her."

Old Wen was silent. Then he did the thing he always did when the argument was lost but the principle wasn't: he grunted, poured tea from the clay pot into three chipped cups, and handed them out like communion.

"High slack at midnight," he said. "If the river gives us the window, we take it. If it doesn't—" He drank. "Then the river keeps its receipts, and we pay what it asks."

* * *

We should have left at midnight. We left at half past eleven instead, because at quarter past, Suri came out of her fever long enough to sit up, look at each of us with eyes gone clear and strange, and say: "The throat is open. Take the eastern seam, past the split rock. The water remembers the old channel even when the charts forgot it." Then she lay back down and was gone again, into wherever she went now, and none of us said what we were all thinking—that the river had gotten inside her, and that whatever it was doing to her, tonight it might be the only thing keeping us alive.

Old Wen took the wheel himself. He always did for crossings, but tonight he took it like a man taking a post, planting his feet wide on the deck plates, rolling his shoulders once, twice, settling into the wood and steel as if he and the Magpie Jay shared a spine. Which, after thirty years on the Reach, they did.

Fog came down with the dark, thick as wool. The running lights made two small smeared moons ahead of us and nothing else. Behind, Renn's boat burned its floodlights against the rain, a dirty glow on the southern horizon, anchored, patient, certain of its morning.

"Eastern seam," Wen murmured, mostly to the wheel. "Past the split rock. Hold on to something, all of you. This old lady is going to dance."

She danced. God help me, she danced. The Throat is a half-mile of channel where the river pinches itself between walls of limestone older than language, and in monsoon rain it stops being a channel and becomes an argument—water shoving against water, whirlpools spinning up out of nowhere, the current changing its mind every forty seconds. Wen read it with his hands and his hips and some organ I've never located in human anatomy, feathering the throttle Bao worked below, swinging the bow through seams of smoother water that existed for three seconds each and only where he expected them to be. Spray came over the bow in sheets. The hull groaned like a living thing. Once, the split rock loomed out of the fog dead ahead, black and fanged, and Wen said "there you are" the way you greet an old rival, and put us past it so close I could have touched it, could have scraped moss off it with my knuckles.

And then we were through. The channel opened, the violence fell away behind us, and the Magpie Jay ran quiet and wet through the rain-dark upper Reach with the ridge—and the relay tower somewhere on it—rising faintly ahead.

That was when the flare went up.

It came out of the fog astern, red and hissing, and lit the whole river the color of an open wound. Renn's boat. Underway. Coming fast, floodlights blazing, cutting the corner we'd cut because they'd had someone watching the Throat's mouth all along—or maybe they'd simply guessed, the way wolves guess, by reading fear in the water.

"They followed us through," Bao said, coming up the ladder soaked to the skin. "They're four minutes back. Maybe five."

"Can we lose them?"

"Not in the straight. Not in this visibility. They'll run us down in the open water." He wiped rain from his face. "Unless we go where their draft won't follow. The reed maze, past Heron Light."

"That's Wen's call."

We both turned to the wheel.

Old Wen hadn't moved. He stood exactly as he'd stood through the whole crossing, feet planted, palm on the wheel, but his head was cocked slightly, listening—to the engines, to the rain, to the pursuing diesel growl swelling out of the fog behind us. And I saw him do the arithmetic. I watched it move across his face like weather. Four minutes. The reed maze entrance, narrow, unlit, half-drowned already with the rising water. A boat our size threading it at speed in the dark would need every hand on deck and the wheel answered instantly, instantly, instantly, for twelve minutes without one mistake.

"Get Voss up here," Wen said. "Get the girl's blankets and bring her to the wheelhouse—warmest spot on the boat, and I want her where I can hear her if she wakes. Bao—all power to the searchlight, low mount, sweep the reeds for me. Paloma." He looked at me then, and in the red wash of the flare still fading behind us, his old face was perfectly calm. "Stand by the stern rail. Watch our wake. Tell me if it goes wrong."

"What could go wrong that I'd see before you feel it?"

"Debris," he said. "Floating logs ride high in floodwater. They come up behind you silent as sin." He smiled, small and crooked. "Go on, Captain. Your boat's awake. Let her work."

* * *

For nine minutes, it worked.

The reed maze swallowed us and spat fog back at Renn's floodlights; I heard their engines slow, confused, hunting, and felt something like hope, thin and dangerous, rise in my chest. Wen threaded the channels with Suri's fever-words in his ears and sixty years of the Reach in his hands, calling the turns half a second before he made them—"starboard seam, mind the snag, hard over"—and Bao's searchlight swept the reeds in patient arcs, and Voss crouched silent in the corner of the wheelhouse watching the old man steer with an expression I couldn't read and didn't try to.

Suri woke once, briefly, and said, "Left. After the third bend the channel splits—take the left, the right one drowned last spring"—and slept again, and Wen took the left, and the left was true.

On the tenth minute, I saw the log.

It came out of the dark astern riding our own wake, a flood-torn trunk the size of a rowboat, black and barnacled, invisible until the flare-light caught its wet crown. It was going to miss us—I could see that even in the second I had—it was going to slide past our port quarter by two meters, close but clean, nothing—

Except Wen saw it too, and Wen also saw that its trailing branches would sweep the stern where Suri's blankets had slipped and one corner of cloth trailed loose near the propeller wash, threatening to foul the screw, and there was no time to shout it, no time for anyone to move, no time for anything except what Old Wen did, which was what Old Wen had done his entire life, which was to put himself between the river and his people.

He let go of the wheel with one hand—only one, the other never leaving its post—and lunged the two steps to the stern hatch, and swept the trailing blanket clear with his arm, and the log's branch, whipping, caught him instead.

Not the branch. The rifle shot came from Renn's boat, from the fog, from a marksman who had found us in the flare-glow and fired at the wheelhouse lights—one round, professional, meant for the wheel or the man at it. It went through the wheelhouse window and through Old Wen's chest as he turned back toward the helm, and he finished the turn anyway. He reached the wheel. He put both hands on it. He corrected our heading by five degrees, holding us true in the channel, and then his knees folded, gently, the way a man kneels at prayer, and he slid down the wheel spokes to the deck plates with his palms still hooked over the rim, and the Magpie Jay held her line because he had already given her the course, because he had steered her so long she knew his mind.

The sound I made was not a word. I remember that much. I remember Bao catching the wheel above Wen's fallen hands, and Voss pressing his jacket against the wound, and the blood coming fast and dark and everywhere, and Wen's eyes finding mine—clear, unhurried, annoyed, almost—as if I'd caught him at something embarrassing.

"Eastern seam," he said. Blood at the corner of his mouth. "Told you. Coin toss." And then, quieter, with the ghost of the smile: "Mind the girl. Mind the boat. Mind—" His hand lifted from the wheel, trembling, and pointed forward, downriver, toward the tower, toward the truth, toward everything we hadn't finished. "Mind the receipts."

The river took the rest of the sentence. Rivers do.

* * *

We ran dark after that. Killed the searchlight, killed the running lights, and let the fog have us while Renn's boat blundered in the reeds behind, firing twice more at nothing, at rain, at ghosts. Bao held the wheel with tears cutting lines through the spray on his face and never missed a turn. Voss stayed below with Wen's body, wrapped in sailcloth, laid in the engine room because Wen had spent more of his life there than anywhere and it seemed right that he should travel warm.

At dawn the fog thinned to gauze and the ridge stood up out of the river ahead of us, black-green and streaming, the relay tower's skeleton visible at its crown like a mast of a ship sunk in the land. And behind us, far back, Renn's boat sat motionless in the open water, floodlights off, waiting, watching, certain that sooner or later the Magpie Jay would have to make contact or make a run, and that either way the day would deliver what Renn wanted.

I stood at the wheel beside Bao, my hands white on the rail, and did the arithmetic Renn had told me to do. And I understood, with a clarity that felt like the bullet's own cold passage, what the next number had to be.

"Renn hails us at eight," I said. "He always hails at eight. When he does, we answer calm. We tell him we're holding position, assessing damage from the crossing, awaiting instructions." My voice came out level, and I was grateful to it, because inside I was nothing but the sound of a body sliding down a wheel. "He asks about the logbook. We tell him we're still searching. He asks about Voss—we say Voss is secured. He asks about casualties."

Bao looked at me. "Paloma—"

"He asks about casualties," I said, "and we tell him the crossing was rough but everyone's accounted for. Everyone." I looked down through the deckplates, toward the engine room, toward the sailcloth, toward the man who had taught me to read water when I was nineteen and afraid of my own boat. "Renn doesn't get to know what it cost. Not him. Not yet. The price stays ours until we're ready to present the bill."

Below us, faint through the hull, Suri began to murmur in her fever again—but differently now. Steadier. Like someone reciting.

And the words, when Bao leaned down to listen and came back up pale as milk, were these: *"The tower is not empty. Someone is already lighting a signal fire on the ridge."*

Someone had reached the relay tower ahead of us. And they were announcing themselves to whoever was watching—which, on this river, in this rain, meant Renn.

I put my hand on the wheel, on the place where Old Wen's hands had rested, and turned the Magpie Jay's bow toward the ridge.

"Hold on," I said, to the boat, to the crew, to the dead man traveling warm below. "We're coming. And we're bringing every receipt."
