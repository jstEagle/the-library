## 13. Running Dark

The fog came off the water like a held breath finally released.

Suri felt it before she saw it—a coolness on her skin that had nothing to do with the hour, a dampness that tasted of silt and old stone. She stood at the bow of the Magpie Jay with her eyes closed and her palms flat against the rail, and the river spoke to her the way it had been speaking since the fever broke: not in words, never in words, but in pressures. In the way the current leaned left here and hesitated there. In the deep hum beneath her feet that no one else seemed to hear unless they stood very still and believed very hard.

Behind her, Paloma killed the engine.

The silence that followed was enormous. Suri had grown up on boats where the engine's rhythm was as constant as a heartbeat, and its absence made the whole world feel like it had stopped mid-sentence. She heard Bao down in the engine room, his voice low and unhappy, talking to machinery that couldn't answer. She heard Old Wen's absence most of all—the wheelhouse should have held his muttering, his tea thermos clinking against the brass rail, his endless commentary on the river's moods. The wheelhouse held only Paloma now, and Paloma said nothing at all.

"Talk to me," Paloma called softly from the wheelhouse window. "What do you feel?"

Suri opened her eyes. The fog was thick enough to drink. Ten meters ahead of the bow, the world simply ended, dissolved into grey wool. Somewhere behind them—close enough that she could imagine hearing it over the lap of water against the hull—Renn's patrol boat was running its searchlight along the banks, hunting them through the dark.

She thought about lying. About telling Paloma she felt nothing, that the fever had burned whatever strange thing the caves had put inside her clean out of her skull, and they should turn around and run for the delta with the engine roaring and hope Renn's boat was slower than theirs.

But Old Wen was dead because of this truth. He'd taken a bullet meant for her, meant for the logbook pages wrapped in oilcloth under her shirt, and he'd died at the wheel he loved with tea still warm in his thermos. Lying now would make his death a smaller thing than it was.

"I feel the channels," she said. "All of them. Even the ones that aren't on any chart."

* * *

They had argued about it for an hour after the scuffle, while Old Wen lay in the cold room wrapped in sailcloth and Bao sat with his head in his hands and Voss hovered by the door like a man who knew he wasn't welcome anywhere.

"We can't outrun him," Paloma had said finally, in the flat voice she used when the truth was too heavy for anything warmer. "Not with him watching the main channel. He knows we have to go downstream. There are exactly three ways through the Narrows, and he'll be sitting on all of them."

"Not exactly three," Suri had said.

Everyone had looked at her. Voss had looked at her longest, with an expression she couldn't read—hope, maybe, or fear wearing hope's clothes.

"When I was sick," she said slowly, feeling her way through it, "I dreamed about the river. Not dreaming like sleeping. It was more like... being shown things. There's a channel through the rock throat below Widow's Bend. The old charts call it a dead end, a wall of stone. But it isn't. The water goes through. It's been going through for longer than the charts have existed." She swallowed. "It's narrow. Narrower than anything we've ever run. And there's no light down there, and the current does something strange in the middle—I can't explain it, it folds back on itself—but if we go through without the engine, without noise, drifting on the flood tide..."

"You want to run my boat blind," Paloma said, "through a passage that doesn't exist, in fog, in the dark, past a man with guns."

"Yes."

Bao had laughed then, one short bitter bark. "Captain, I've been saying this whole trip smells wrong. But this—" he shook his head. "This is the wrongest smell yet."

And Voss, quiet in the doorway, had said: "The survey mapped that throat with sonar in the first week. We flagged it as impassable. But Dr. Okafor noted anomalous flow readings. She thought there might be a fissure system the sonar couldn't resolve." He looked at Suri with something close to reverence, and it made her skin crawl. "You're describing exactly what she suspected."

"I'm describing what I *know*," Suri said, and heard the certainty in her own voice and didn't recognize it. "I don't know how. But I know it the way I know my own hands."

Paloma had been silent a long time. Then she'd looked at the cold room door, behind which Old Wen waited for a burial they couldn't give him yet, and something in her face had closed like a fist.

"Drift it is," she said. "Bao, I want the engine cold and everything loose lashed down. Suri—you're my eyes. If you're wrong about even one meter of this, we die in the dark and Renn finds our bones next spring." She paused. "No pressure."

That was Paloma's way of being kind, Suri had learned. Jokes shaped like knives.

* * *

Now the fog owned everything, and the Magpie Jay moved on the flood tide alone, silent as a leaf.

Suri stood at the bow with a dimmed lantern shielded by her body, though she barely needed it. The river told her where it went. She could feel the banks—not see them, *feel* them—as pressures on either side, the way you feel walls in a dark room by the change in the air. Left bank falling away into a drowned willow stand. Right bank rising toward the old fish-smoking terraces, abandoned now, the villages empty since the fishing camps went quiet upstream of whatever lived in the caves.

The hum was louder tonight. She wondered if it was the fog, or the monsoon building somewhere north like a held drumroll, or if the thing in the water simply wanted her to hear it. She had stopped asking whether it was alive. You didn't ask whether the sea was alive. You just learned its moods.

"Banks narrowing," she called back, barely above a whisper. Sound carried strangely in fog; shouting would tell Renn's hydrophones exactly where they were, if he had hydrophones, which she was certain he did. "Fifty meters. Then it opens for a bit. There's a snag on the left—old teak, half-sunk. Give it ten meters clearance."

"How do you know there's a snag?" Bao's voice floated up from the deck beside her. He'd insisted on coming forward, armed with a boathook and profound skepticism. "You can't see ten meters."

"The water moves around it." She demonstrated with her hand, a small eddying gesture. "Everything in a river tells you what's in it, if you listen. The snag makes the current stutter. Like a person walking with a limp."

Bao stared at her. In the shielded lantern light his face was a mask of shadow and gleam. "That's either the most beautiful thing I've ever heard or proof you've lost your mind."

"Both," Suri said. "Probably both."

Ahead, the fog thinned for a moment and she caught it: the rock throat. Even in the dark she could see the shape of it—a place where the hills leaned together like conspirators, and the river poured between them through a gap that looked, from here, far too narrow for the Magpie Jay's beam. Black stone rose on both sides, wet and glistening, streaked pale with old high-water marks. The sound changed too. The current accelerated as it funneled in, and the gentle lap against the hull became a low urgent hiss, like the river whispering *hurry, hurry.*

Her mouth went dry. Knowing the channel and steering a thirty-ton wooden boat into it were different animals entirely.

"Paloma," she called softly. "I see it."

"So do I," came the reply, "and I hate it."

* * *

They drifted in on the flood tide with the current doing the work and Bao and Suri handling fenders and poles, ready to fend off stone that could crack the hull like an egg. Voss crouched amidships with the logbook pages in a waterproof bag strapped to his chest, and Paloma stood at the helm with both hands on the wheel she wasn't really using, steering by rudder trim alone, feathering the blade to keep them straight.

The gap swallowed them.

Stone closed in on both sides until Suri could have touched it—did touch it, once, her fingertips brushing rock slick as skin. The passage was maybe four meters wide where they entered. The charts said two. The charts, she understood now, had measured the wrong thing: they'd measured the rock, when they should have measured the water's intention.

Overhead, the walls leaned together and blotted out the sky. Fog swirled in the slot above them like smoke in a chimney. The lantern showed black water moving fast, faster than any current she'd ever drifted, and yet the boat rode it smoothly, almost gently, as if the river were carrying them in cupped hands.

"It's helping us," she breathed.

"Don't anthropomorphize the hydrology," Voss said automatically, and then stopped himself. She heard him exhale. "Although," he admitted, quieter, "Okafor said the same thing. About the aquifer. That it behaved less like geology and more like... temperament."

The passage narrowed. Three meters. Two and a half. The stone scraped close enough that Bao pressed himself flat against the cabin side, boathook braced, breathing in short controlled bursts. Suri felt the current fold ahead—the strange backward curl she'd described, where the water doubled on itself—and shouted a warning.

"Hard left! No—rudder left, but let the current take the bow right! Trust it!"

"Trust the water that's trying to kill us," Paloma muttered, but she did it. The Magpie Jay's bow swung right into the folding current, and instead of slamming them into the wall, the eddy caught the hull and slung them sideways through the tightest point with a meter to spare on each side. Stone whispered past the rail. Bao made a sound like a man praying in a language he'd forgotten.

Then—openness. The walls fell away. Sky reappeared, fog-grey and vast, and the current gentled, and they were through.

For a long moment nobody spoke. Then Bao sat down abruptly on a coil of rope and started laughing, high and slightly unhinged, the laugh of a man whose death had been scheduled and then cancelled.

"That," he managed, "was the worst thing I have ever done on purpose."

Suri realized her legs were shaking. She gripped the rail and laughed too, and found tears on her face she had no memory of crying.

Voss came forward slowly. He looked at her the way people look at a fire in winter—wary, drawn, unable to look away.

"What you did just now," he said. "In the caves, when you touched the water. What did it show you?"

"Why?" she asked. "So you can write it in a report? File me under 'anomalous'?"

"No." He said it simply, without defensiveness, and that simplicity undid some of her anger. "Because I spent eleven years treating that watershed as a resource. Tonnage, head pressure, diversion capacity. Numbers. And then I stood in that chamber and the numbers stopped meaning anything, and I have been trying ever since to find language for what replaced them." His voice dropped. "I scattered my own team. I burned my own career. And I still don't have the words. So when someone who clearly has a *connection*—"

"It doesn't show me things," Suri said. "It's not pictures. It's more like..." She searched. "You know how your mother's kitchen feels? Not looks—*feels*. Safe, or crowded, or sad, depending on the year. The river is like that. Whole stretches of it have feelings. The caves had a feeling like—" she hesitated, embarrassed, "—like being listened to. Like something very old turning its attention toward you and deciding you might be worth the trouble."

Voss nodded slowly, and there was grief in it. "Worth the trouble," he repeated. "Yes. That's the phrase I've been looking for since March."

* * *

They ran dark for six hours.

The fog held, mercifully, a solid white blanket between them and the patrol boat's searchlight. Suri steered them by feel through the maze of back channels below the throat—channels that appeared on no chart, that Old Wen had never mentioned in forty years on the Reach, that she suspected existed mostly because the river permitted them to exist. Twice she called turns so sharp that Paloma swore under her breath and obeyed anyway. Once they passed so close to a fishing village that Suri could smell woodsmoke and hear a dog barking, and she raised a hand in greeting to houses she couldn't see, hoping whoever watched their ghost-boat slide by in the fog would remember kindness and say nothing.

Toward dawn the fog began to thin, and with it their luck.

"Stern light," Bao called from the aft rail, voice tight. "Two points off our quarter. Running without lights, same as us."

Paloma materialized at the wheelhouse window. "Distance?"

"Close. Closing. He knows these waters better than we hoped and worse than we feared."

Suri reached out with the sense that still felt like a stranger living in her chest. The channel ahead split around a long island—reeds and dead trees, a place where the current divided and grew confused. Behind them, the other boat's wake wrote itself across her awareness like handwriting: fast, confident, cutting the corner where a careful pilot wouldn't.

"He'll take the east channel," she said. "He's in a hurry. The east channel looks faster."

"And?"

"And the west channel is slow and full of sandbars." She paused. "But there's a third way. Through the island itself. An old distributary, silted almost shut, but the recent rains reopened it. It's narrow and the reeds will hide us and it comes out below the island, ahead of him, if we're quiet."

Paloma was silent for three full seconds. Suri had learned to count them.

"Take us in," Paloma said.

The reeds closed over them like a green cathedral. They drifted through a corridor no wider than the boat, stems scraping the hull with a dry whisper, and on the far side of the vegetation screen they heard the patrol boat pass—engine rumble, voices, a searchlight beam strobing through the reeds in white slashes that fell inches from Suri's face and moved on. Someone aboard laughed. Someone else said something about wasting the night.

Then the sound faded downstream, hunting a ghost in the east channel, and the Magpie Jay slid out of the island's secret artery into open water under a sky just beginning to bruise pink at the edges.

* * *

They anchored at first light in a backwater Bao knew from smuggling days he refused to elaborate on—a cut in the mangroves deep enough to hide the boat from the air, shallow enough that Renn's deeper-draft patrol vessel couldn't follow even if he found the entrance.

Paloma brewed coffee that tasted like tar and salvation. Bao went below to check the engine they hadn't dared start all night, murmuring to it like an apology. Voss sat on the rail with his waterproof bag and didn't eat the bread Suri offered him until she pushed it into his hand.

"You could have run," she said, sitting beside him. "After Old Wen. After Renn's men boarded. You had the logbook. You could have gone into the jungle alone and taken your chances."

"I'm a geologist, not a guerrilla." He turned the bread over in his fingers. "Also—" he stopped. Started again. "When we went through the throat. When you shouted that warning about the current folding. You trusted me to lash those pages properly. You trusted me to keep my head." He looked at her. "Nobody on my own team trusted me by the end. I'd earned that. I know I did. But last night, in the dark, you all trusted me anyway, and I—" his jaw worked, "—I intend to be worth it. That's all. That's the whole speech."

Suri bumped her shoulder against his, briefly, the way you greet family. "Eat your bread, Doctor."

From the wheelhouse, Paloma's voice carried across the quiet water, flat and grim:

"Radio's picking up traffic. Port authority band." A pause, the crackle of tuning. "There's a bulletin. They're calling the Kestrel's crew fugitives. All seven names. Voss first on the list." Another pause, longer. "And there's a bounty notice, private issuer, no agency listed. For the recovery of 'survey materials.' Reward sufficient to buy every boat on this river twice over."

Bao emerged from the engine hatch wiping his hands. "Every captain from here to the delta is going to be looking for us now. Not just Renn. Everyone."

Paloma stepped out of the wheelhouse into the grey-gold morning light. She looked tired in a way that had passed beyond exhaustion into something mineral, permanent. Her eyes found Suri's.

"They've made everyone on the Reach an enemy," she said. "Which means we can't stop anywhere, can't resupply, can't ask help of anyone." She took a breath. "Except the transmitter tower on Cape Meridian ridge. Voss says it's reachable overland from the caves—if the caves give us a path. Two days' trek through country that's about to drown when the monsoon hits."

Suri felt the hum rise in her chest, faint and patient, like a question asked a second time.

"The river already showed me the route," she said quietly. "I just haven't told anyone yet."
