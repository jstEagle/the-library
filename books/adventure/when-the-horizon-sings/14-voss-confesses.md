## 14. Voss Confesses

Bao found the truth the way he found most things: with his hands.

The engine room of the Magpie Jay was his cathedral—oily, humming, honest. Every problem in it could be solved with a wrench and patience, and every lie announced itself as a rattle, a shudder, a wrongness in the rhythm that no one else heard but him. He'd been lying under the main housing for an hour, chasing a vibration that shouldn't exist, when Voss came down the ladder without asking permission.

The geologist moved differently now than he had when they'd pulled him half-starved out of the mangroves. Fed, rested, no longer clutching that rifle like a rosary, he still carried himself like a man walking on river ice—testing each step before trusting it. He lowered himself onto the crate beside the coolant lines, folded his long frame down into the space Bao hadn't offered, and said nothing for a long time.

Bao kept working. He knew this species of silence. It was the silence of a man rehearsing.

"You've been watching me," Voss said finally. His voice was rough from disuse, sandpaper over gravel. "Since the rock throat. Since I told Fairweather about the channels."

"Paloma," Bao said, not looking up. "Her name is Paloma."

"I know what her name is." A pause. "I signed her charter paperwork once. Two years ago. The Kestrel's first survey run. She doesn't remember me."

Bao did look up then. In the amber work-light, Voss's face was all planes and hollows, the beard grown in patchy where he'd been starving, the eyes set deep and burning low, like coals banked for a long night. "She remembers everyone," Bao said. "She just files them under what they wanted from her."

"What did she file you under?"

"Family." Bao turned back to the housing and loosened the inspection plate. "Which is worse, if you're planning to disappoint her."

* * *

The vibration was real. He could feel it now through his palm pressed flat against the casing—a tremor at the edge of hearing, rhythmic, patient. Not mechanical. Nothing on the boat made a sound like that. It had started three days ago, after the caves, after Suri went into the water and came out changed, and it hadn't stopped since. Bao had checked everything twice: shaft alignment, bearing play, the mounts, the exhaust. The Magpie Jay was sound. The sound wasn't coming from the Magpie Jay.

It was coming through it. Through the hull, through the water itself, up into the steel like the river was knocking.

He'd mentioned it to no one but Old Wen, who had touched the deck with his bare foot, closed his eyes, and said, "Low tide of the voice," which meant nothing and explained everything, the way Old Wen's sayings did. Now Old Wen was dead and buried under stones at the bend he'd loved, and Bao carried the saying around like a stone of his own.

"The hum," Voss said quietly.

Bao's head came up fast. "You hear it too?"

"I've been hearing it for eleven months." Voss leaned forward, elbows on knees, hands hanging between them like something surrendered. "That's why I'm here. That's why any of us are here. So yes, engineer. You're right to distrust me. And you're right to want to know. And I'm going to tell you both things, because Suri nearly died touching that water, and because your cook took a bullet that was aimed at a piece of paper I wrote." His jaw worked. "I owe this boat a debt I don't know how to pay except with the truth. So. The truth."

Bao wiped his hands slowly on a rag, deliberately, giving himself time. Above them, through the deck plating, he could hear Paloma moving at the wheel, Suri's low voice, the river's endless commentary against the hull. He sat down across from Voss, close enough to smell the woodsmoke and fever-sweat still clinging to the man's clothes.

"Start with the plan," Bao said. "The diversion. Renn's employers. Say it plain."

Voss nodded once, like a man accepting a sentence.

* * *

"It's called the Reach Diversion Project," Voss said. "Officially, a flood-control initiative. Unofficially—" he exhaled through his nose, a bitter ghost of a laugh, "—unofficially it's the largest water theft in the hemisphere's history, and I drew the maps for it."

Bao said nothing. On the Magpie Jay you learned that silence pulled more confession than questions.

"There's an aquifer under the upper Serpent's Reach," Voss went on. "A vast one. Karst limestone, dissolved over millions of years into chambers and channels—the flooded cave system we were surveying. It holds more fresh water than every reservoir in the coastal provinces combined. Clean, cold, pressurized by the head of the highlands. The Ministry's plan was elegant, I'll give it that. Dam the lower gorge, seal the natural outlets, and pipe the aquifer straight to the industrial corridor four hundred kilometers east. Hydro power, irrigation for export crops, municipal supply for the new refinery cities. They'd have called it progress."

"And the river?"

"The river," Voss said, "becomes a trickle. Seasonally, at best." He looked at his own hands. "I ran the models myself. Downstream flow drops eighty-one percent in dry season. Sixty-four even in monsoon. The fishing camps go first—they're already abandoned, you saw that. Then the villages. Then the delta fisheries, which feed two million people. The government's own internal assessment projected displacement figures they classified within a week of receiving them. I know because I filed the request to see them, and the denial came back stamped *not relevant to geological scope.*"

"They knew," Bao said. It wasn't a question. He thought of the empty camps drifting past, the fish racks rotting, the cooking pots left behind because their owners had been paid or frightened or simply erased from the conversation. "They knew what it would do, and they sent you anyway."

"They sent me because I was good," Voss said simply. "And because I believed them. That's the part you need to understand, engineer—I'm not confessing to being a monster. I'm confessing to being *useful*. There were twelve of us on the Kestrel. Hydrologists, seismologists, cartographers, two Ministry observers who mostly drank and played cards. We were told we were mapping flood risk. I signed off on the preliminary diversion feasibility report in the spring. My name is on page forty. I certified the aquifer as, quote, *geologically stable and hydrologically inert.*"

"Inert," Bao repeated.

"That's what the instruments said." Voss's voice dropped, and something in it changed—the register of a man approaching the place where his certainty had broken. "Pressure readings, flow rates, temperature gradients. All consistent with dead water. An underground tank. That's how we treated it. That's how the plan treated it. A tank to be drained." He rubbed his face with both hands. "Then in the autumn, we pushed into the deep chambers past the third sump. The ones that aren't on any map because the water moves against current there, and our instruments started returning numbers that made no sense. Flow reversals synchronized across chambers separated by kilometers of rock. Pressure waves that propagated faster than physics allows in open water. And the sound."

"The hum."

"The hum." Voss looked up, and his eyes in the work-light were wet, though his face didn't crumple; it only burned harder. "You've been feeling it in your hull for days. Imagine it in a limestone chamber the size of a cathedral, amplified, harmonic, *structured*. Not random. Not seismic. Structured, Bao. Patterns repeating across hours. Responding. We dropped a probe into the fourth chamber and the water rose to meet it—rose, against gravity, against every law we'd written—and held it there, suspended, for six minutes, while the harmonics shifted through eleven distinct frequencies. Then it set the probe down on a ledge. Gently. Like a hand returning something it had finished examining."

The engine room was very quiet. Even the river seemed to lean closer against the hull. Bao became aware that his heart was pounding and that the tremor in the deck plates had strengthened, just slightly, as if the subject of the conversation had drawn near to listen.

"Suri described it," he said hoarsely. "In her fever. A network. Living. Older than anything."

"She undersold it, if anything." Voss stood abruptly, unable to stay seated, and paced the narrow aisle between the coolant lines, his shoulders nearly brushing both walls. "It's not an animal. It's not a single organism. Best model we could build—and Chen built most of it, our systems biologist, before—" he stopped, steadied himself, "—before the end. The best model is that the entire watershed is *one thing*. The aquifer, the springs, the seasonal floods, the fish migrations, the sediment cycles, the monsoon itself—all of it connected through that living water network the way your organs are connected through blood. The Reach isn't a river running through a landscape. The Reach *is* the landscape. It's been regulating this basin since before there were nations to claim it. Before there were people. And we had signed a document proposing to cut its heart out and pipe it east."

"So you scattered your team." Bao said it flatly, but inside him something vast was rearranging itself, tectonic, slow. "Eleven months ago. That's why the Kestrel was sitting clean at anchor. That's why there were no bodies."

Voss stopped pacing. When he turned, his face had gone old. "I didn't scatter them," he said. "I *disbanded* them. There's a difference, and the difference cost lives, so let me be exact." He held up one finger. "First, I falsified the final report. Submitted the inert-water findings. Certified the project feasible. Because if I'd reported the truth, the Ministry would have sealed the site, accelerated the timeline, and brought in security contractors—which is exactly what happened anyway, so perhaps I was naive to think I had months instead of weeks." A second finger. "Second, I convened the team, showed them what we'd actually recorded, and gave them a choice. Sign the certification and take the Ministry's money, or walk away with me and burn our careers. Eleven chose to walk. One—the junior cartographer, Adeyemi—chose the money. She's the one who sold us to Renn. She's the one waiting at the tower."

"You knew that too."

"I suspected. I hoped otherwise. Hope is the last instrument to fail." He laughed, short and joyless. "Third. I dispersed the team across the basin with copies of the raw data—eleven sets, hidden in eleven places, each of us carrying a fragment so no one seizure could recover the whole record. My job was the hardest evidence: the original logs, the probe recordings, the harmonic transcriptions. I hid them in the caves themselves, in a dry chamber above the fourth sump, and I stayed nearby to guard them. For eleven months. Eating fish and fern roots. Watching the water sing through the rock at night and trying to decide whether I'd discovered a miracle or become the man who almost murdered one."

Bao sat with it. Around them the engine room ticked and settled, and beneath everything, steady as a second heartbeat, the hum persisted. He thought about Voss holding a rifle on them in the mangroves, screaming at them to turn back, refusing to explain—and understood it now not as madness but as a man so saturated in secrecy that openness had become physically difficult, a swimmer who'd spent a year underwater forgetting how to breathe air.

"You tried to sabotage our radio," Bao said. "Back at the Kestrel."

"Yes."

"Why? If you wanted the truth out—"

"Because Renn monitors every transmission on this stretch, and a distress call from the Magpie Jay would have brought him down on you a week early, before you knew anything, before you could choose." Voss crouched again, forearms on knees, meeting Bao's eyes directly. "I've watched governments bury discoveries my whole career. I wasn't going to watch them bury this one—or the crew that found it. But I couldn't tell you the truth either, because the truth makes you a target, and I had no right to make that choice for you." His mouth twisted. "Except that's exactly what I did, isn't it? By finding you. By leading you upstream. I made the choice for you the moment I let you find the Kestrel. I keep telling myself I had no alternative. I keep telling myself a lot of things."

* * *

Above them, boots crossed the deck—Paloma, heading aft, her step unmistakable, that rolling gait she'd never lost even with a bad knee. Bao listened until it faded toward the stern rail.

"Paloma thinks you're a coward," he said. "Or a martyr. She hasn't decided which insult fits."

"She's right to think both. I've earned both." Voss was quiet a moment. "What does she think of the logbook?"

Bao hesitated. This was the cargo hold of the conversation, the thing they'd all been circling: the Kestrel's sealed log, cracked open by his own careful hands three nights ago, its pages now wrapped in oilcloth and hidden inside the starboard ballast compartment—the one place on the boat no inspector ever looked, because no inspector cared how a riverboat balanced. Paloma had read it once, gone gray, and read it again. Suri, fever-bright, had recited passages from memory she'd never seen. And Renn wanted it with a hunger none of them mistook for bureaucratic diligence.

"She thinks it's a bomb," Bao said. "And she's deciding whether we're the ones who should throw it, or whether throwing it makes us the same as them."

"It's not a bomb." Voss shook his head slowly. "A bomb destroys. This is the opposite. It's proof. Twelve professionals' worth of instruments, measurements, recordings—independent corroboration that the aquifer is alive and the diversion will kill it and everything downstream. It's the only reason the broadcast will stick. Without it, Paloma's transmission is a captain's rant and my testimony is a disgraced scientist's fantasy. With it—" he met Bao's eyes, "—with it, it's a crime scene. Documented. Dated. Signed."

"By you."

"By me. Page forty and all of it." Voss smiled, and it was the saddest thing Bao had seen since Old Wen died. "I told you I owed this boat a debt. That's the payment. My name, my career, my credibility—nailed to the truth like a warrant. Let them come for me. I'm the one who certified the lie; I should be the one who un-certifies it, publicly, at whatever it costs."

Through the hull, faint but insistent, the hum swelled—two tones now, braided, rising. Bao felt it in his back teeth, in the soles of his feet, in the wrench lying loose on the deck plate, which buzzed against the steel like a struck tuning fork. He watched Voss notice it too, watched the geologist go still with an expression Bao had last seen on Suri's fevered face: recognition, and fear, and underneath both, unwilling and enormous, awe.

"It knows," Voss whispered. "God help me, after eleven months I can almost read it. That pattern—that's the one from the fourth chamber. The one it made when it was *asking* something."

"Asking what?"

"I don't know." Voss's voice cracked on it. "We never answered. That's the whole tragedy, engineer. Something ancient has been singing into this basin since before human memory, and the first time anyone finally heard it, we responded with a dam proposal."

Bao got up, crossed to the hull, and laid his bare palm flat against the cool steel. The vibration climbed his arm, patient, immense, unhurried—as if it had waited ten thousand years and could wait nine days more, or ninety, or forever, though the monsoon and men with guns meant they could not.

Above them, feet pounded across the deck—Suri's this time, fast, urgent—and her voice came down the ladder-well, breathless and strange:

"Bao! Come up! The water's doing something—you need to see the channel markers. *All* of them are lit."
