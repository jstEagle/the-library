## 15. The Reach Answers

Suri had dreamed the cave before they ever reached it.

That was the first thing she understood, standing on the wet limestone lip with the lantern light trembling around her and the black water below breathing in and out like something asleep. She had dreamed this exact curve of stone, this exact smell—mineral and cold and old, older than the word *old*, old in a way that made her think of her grandmother's hands and then of nothing human at all. In the fever she had walked these chambers. Now her boots found the grooves her dream-feet had worn, and the grooves were real, and she did not know whether to weep or laugh or run.

"You're certain this is the way?" Paloma stood behind her, rain-slicked, one hand resting on the coil of rope at her hip as though the rope might answer for her if her voice failed. Three days of fog-running and rock throats and Voss's confessions had carved new lines into the captain's face, but her eyes were the same: measuring, stubborn, waiting for the world to argue.

"No," Suri said honestly. "But the water is."

The water was.

She could feel it the way she had felt it in the fever—not as sound, exactly, not as sight, but as *attention*. The aquifer beneath the Reach was not a thing. It was a *many*, a braided intelligence of current and pressure and dissolved stone, and since the night her fever broke she had been able to feel it noticing her, the way you notice a warm hand on the back of your neck. It had shown her the hidden channel through the fog. It had shown her the dry shelf behind the falls where Renn's patrol boat's searchlight swept harmlessly overhead. Now, she was almost sure, it was showing her the way up to the ridge—to the relay tower, and the transmitter that could carry the logbook's truth to every port on the coast before the monsoon sealed the world shut behind them.

"Explain it to me one more time," Bao said. He had the logbook pages wrapped in oilcloth against his chest, and he kept one hand pressed over them the way a man keeps a hand over a wound. "Slowly. Because what you're describing is a river with opinions."

"It's not opinions," Suri said. "It's memory. It's—" She stopped, frustrated, and pressed her palm flat against the limestone. Cool. Damp. And beneath the damp, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall, the hum. "When you dam a river, what do you take from it?"

"The flow," Paloma said.

"You take its *continuity*." She traced a line in the wet stone, one channel splitting into three. "This water has been moving in the same patterns since before there were names for it. Every channel it has ever cut, every chamber it has flooded and abandoned and reflooded—it remembers all of it. It's all still in there, braided together. And the government wants to dam the upper aquifer and divert the flow into the lowland reservoirs, and that wouldn't just flood the villages downstream. It would—" she searched for the word and found it in the fever, in the dream, in the voice that was not a voice— "*amputate* it. Cut the memory out of the body."

Voss stood apart from them, at the edge of the lantern light, and said nothing. He had said very little since his confession on the boat. Confession, Suri had learned, was not the same as absolution, and the man wore his like a stone sewn into his coat.

"And it knows you," Paloma said. It wasn't a question. "The night of your fever. You touched the water and it—" She gestured vaguely, at Suri, at the dark, at the whole impossible arrangement of the last two weeks.

"It noticed me." Suri almost smiled. "That's the part none of you understand. It's not a god. It's not a monster. It's a *listener*. It's been listening to the river people for a thousand years—every song Old Wen ever hummed at the wheel, every prayer thrown into the current, every name spoken over a drowned boat. It listens. And once, just once, it answered me back."

* * *

They went in at slack tide, when the river's breathing slowed and the entrance chamber's water level dropped to expose a shelf of stone no wider than a coffin. Paloma went first with the lantern, then Suri, then Bao with the pages, and Voss last, because no one had asked him and he had not offered.

The dark inside was total in a way surface dark never was. The lantern made a small golden room around them and beyond it the stone went on forever, wet and gleaming, ribbed like the inside of a throat. Their footsteps echoed wrong—echoes arriving late, or early, as if the cave were considering each sound before returning it.

Suri walked with her hand trailing along the wall, and with every step the hum grew stronger. It was in her sternum now. In her teeth. She had stopped being afraid of it somewhere in the last three days and that frightened her more than fear had—how easily she had made room for it, how at home it felt.

"Stop," she said suddenly.

Paloma stopped. Ahead, the shelf ended at a black pool, still as poured glass, and beyond the pool the passage continued—or seemed to. In the lantern light Suri could see the problem: the ceiling dipped to meet the water. There was no dry way through. There was no way through at all that a person could swim with a pack and a lantern and live.

"There's nothing here," Bao said quietly. "Suri, there's nothing here."

"I know." She knelt at the pool's edge. The water was warm. That was the thing no one believed when she told them—the deep water of the Reach was *warm*, blood-warm, in chambers where no sun had ever reached. She pulled off her glove and put her bare hand in.

The hum rose to meet her like a dog hearing its name.

It moved up her arm, into her chest, and she let it. That was the trick, she had learned; that was the whole secret. You didn't speak to it. You *opened*, the way a door opens, and what came through was not words but *shapes*—the memory of water, ten thousand years of it, every flood and drought and wandering channel laid out like a city seen from above. And in that city, she looked for what she needed.

*A way up. A dry road to the ridge. Show me. Please. We're trying to save you. Help us save you.*

The pool shivered.

Not from her hand. From below, from everywhere, from the whole standing body of the aquifer shifting its weight like a sleeper turning over. And then the water began to move—not rushing, not surging, but *withdrawing*, sliding back from the shelf with a long sighing sound, uncovering stone that had not seen air in a hundred years, maybe a thousand. A channel opened through the pool: a low, dry gallery, its floor slick with wet silt but its ceiling high enough to walk, running up and away into the dark at a gentle, deliberate, almost courteous incline.

Nobody spoke for a long moment.

"Okay," Bao said finally, in a small voice. "Okay. Rivers with opinions."

"It's not for us," Suri said, and her own voice came out strange, thick. She understood it all at once, standing there with her hand still in the warm water. "Don't you see? It's not doing this because I asked. It's doing it because we're the first ones who ever *asked*. Renn's people came with sonar and drills. The survey team came with core samples. Everyone who ever came here came to *take a measurement*. We're the first ones who came to say we'd carry its story out." She looked up at Paloma, and the lantern light wavered between them. "It's choosing, Captain. It's been choosing this whole time. The birds leaving the water, the fish avoiding the deep pools, the river going quiet where Renn's boats pass—it's not avoidance. It's a *vote*. And it just voted."

Paloma crouched beside her at the pool's edge. For a moment the captain's hand hovered over the water, close enough to touch, and Suri saw something move across her face that she had never seen there—not fear, not greed, but a kind of naked, wondering grief, as if Paloma Fairweather were meeting the river she had loved her whole life for the very first time.

"I've run this water since I was nine years old," Paloma said softly. "I thought I knew her." She withdrew her hand without touching, and stood. "All right. We go up. Bao, the pages stay dry or we turn around. Voss—"

"I know the way the water remembers," Voss said from the dark. His voice was flat. "I helped draw the plans to cut it. I'll walk last."

* * *

The gallery climbed for hours.

It was not empty. That was the second thing Suri understood, and it undid her more thoroughly than the parting of the water had. The walls were marked—faint, ancient, deliberate. Handprints in red ochre, faded to ghosts. Notches cut in rows, counting something. And in one wide chamber where the ceiling opened into a natural dome, the stone was carved all over with spirals, hundreds of them, interlocking, and when Suri put her ear against the center of the largest one the hum was loudest there, and it was not random. It was structured. It rose and fell in patterns, in *phrases*, and the spirals were not decoration.

They were notation. Someone, someones, a thousand years ago or five, had written down the river's song in stone.

"The river people," Bao whispered, his lantern trembling. "The old ones. Before the fishing villages, before even Old Wen's stories. They knew."

"They didn't worship it," Suri said. Her throat was tight. "They *studied* it. They listened the way I listened. These spirals—" she traced one, and the hum shifted under her fingertip as if the stone itself were leaning into the touch— "they're not prayers. They're correspondence. Questions and answers. Look—" she moved to the next spiral, and the next— "each one is different. Each one is a different part of the song. They were writing it down because it *changes*. Because it's alive and it changes, and they wanted to keep up."

Voss stood in the middle of the chamber with his head bowed, and when Suri glanced at him she saw that he was crying, silently, without any change in his posture, the way a man cries who has forgotten how and is doing it badly.

"I modeled it as an aquifer," he said to no one. "Confined karst system, recharge rate, drawdown coefficients. I put numbers on it. I signed my name to a plan that would have—" He stopped. Started again. "My daughter used to sing in the bath. When she was small. And I never—" He pressed the back of his hand to his mouth and said nothing more, and no one asked him to.

Paloma touched Suri's shoulder. "Can you still feel it? The way through?"

Suri closed her eyes. Opened. The hum was everywhere now, in the stone, in the air, in the warm updraft rising through a crack in the dome where a thread of daylight fell like a plumb line—a chimney, a natural shaft, breathing. And through it, faint and unmistakable, she felt the shape of what lay above: the ridge, the old relay tower the survey had built its camp beside, the transmitter mast leaning against a sky going the color of wet slate.

The monsoon was close. She could feel that too, in the water's mood—a gathering tension, a held breath, the whole Reach bracing for the sky to open.

"There," she said, pointing at the crack of daylight. "It's showing us the way out. The tower's less than an hour above us."

"And Renn?" Bao asked.

The hum answered before she could, and what it gave her was cold: a shape in the water downstream, engines idling, men with guns, a net of attention drawn tight around the ridge. The river could show them the way. It could not fight for them. It had no hands, no guns, no boats. All it had ever had was memory and patience, and patience, Suri understood with a chill, was about to run out. When the monsoon broke, the aquifer would rise, and everything it had shown them—the dry galleries, the breathing shafts, the gentle courteous channels—would close like a fist.

"It knows he's there," she said. "It can't stop him. It can only show us the road and count on us to walk it fast."

"Then we walk fast," Paloma said.

They climbed the last stretch in near silence, up through the breathing crack, hand over hand on wet stone, until they emerged through a screen of fern and hanging root onto the ridge's shoulder—and stopped, all four of them at once, because the view below stole the breath they had left.

The Reach unrolled to the horizon, swollen and brown and gleaming under the bruised monsoon light, and across its whole vast face the water was *singing*. Not the hum of the caves—something vaster, a harmonic rising off the floodplains, audible now even in the open air, a deep chord that Suri realized had been in her dreams since the fever, had been in the river people's songs all along, had been in Old Wen's humming at the wheel on quiet nights. The old man had known it without knowing. They all had. The river had been singing its one long note forever, and only now, standing above it with the logbook's truth against Bao's chest, did any of them finally hear the words inside it.

*Remember me. Carry it out. Remember me.*

"Go," Paloma said, and her voice cracked on it. She pointed up the ridge to the relay tower, its mast black against the storm light, a half kilometer of open ground between them and it. "Suri, you've done more than anyone had a right to ask. Bao, the pages. Voss—" a pause, heavy with everything unspoken— "you get to watch it go out. Move."

They moved. And they were a hundred meters out, fifty, the tower rising ahead of them against the first fat drops of rain—when Suri, glancing back the way they had come, saw the figure rise from the rocks at the cave mouth where the survey camp had been.

A man. One of Voss's scattered team. Standing very still, watching them, a radio already raised to his mouth.

The first drops of the monsoon hit the stone like thrown gravel, and the river below them sang louder, and Suri began to run.
