## 16. Betrayal at the Tower

The ridge trail began where the river gave up pretending to be gentle. Paloma felt the change in her boots first—soft alluvial mud giving way to shale that clicked and slid, each step a small announcement she couldn't afford. Above them the jungle thickened into a green dark that swallowed sound, and below, far below, the Serpent's Reach ran high and brown with the monsoon's first offerings, carrying branches and whole trees south like a fleet of refugees.

They climbed in single file. Voss led, because he knew the survey cutters' path even after two months of rain trying to erase it; then Paloma; then Bao with the oilcloth-wrapped logbook pages strapped flat against his chest beneath his shirt, where he'd insisted on keeping them despite Paloma's argument that the boat was safer. Suri had stayed aboard the *Magpie Jay*, anchored in the hidden channel behind Split-Tooth Island, running the engine twice a day to keep the batteries fed and watching the water for Renn's patrol craft. It had been Paloma's decision, and she had told herself it was strategy—Suri's fever-knowledge of the channels made her the best pilot they had, and someone had to hold their escape.

She had also told herself it wasn't because Suri still moved carefully, as if the water inside her hadn't entirely settled.

*You're splitting your family across a ridge to serve a piece of paper,* Old Wen would have said, if Old Wen were alive to say it. She pushed the thought down where it lived now, wrapped in oilcloth of its own, next to the grief.

"Two hours," Voss said, not turning. His voice had gone thin from weeks of half-rations, but his legs were iron. "The tower sits on the old relay knoll. You'll hear it before you see it—the wind through the guy wires. Like a tuning fork."

He was right. They heard it at the top of the last switchback: a low, wavering note, almost musical, threading through the rain-forest hush. Paloma stopped, one hand on a strangler fig's buttress root, and listened. The sound reminded her of something she couldn't name—the caves, maybe, that hum Suri described in her fever, the living chord of the aquifer. For a moment the two sounds seemed to answer each other across the miles, man-made wire and ancient water singing the same interval.

Then the note changed. A new tone joined it—a diesel generator, coughing somewhere close.

Paloma's hand found Bao's arm. He'd heard it too. His face did what it always did when trouble arrived: nothing at all, a stillness so complete it looked carved.

"Generator," he breathed. "Somebody's running power."

"The tower has solar," Voss whispered. "It always has. Nobody needs a generator unless—" He stopped. Paloma watched the arithmetic finish behind his eyes, watch the color leave him.

Unless somebody planned to stay a while.

* * *

They came at the knoll from the north, belly-down through wet fern, and saw the truth before they understood it: the *Magpie Jay*'s dinghy was not supposed to be here, and neither was the man standing at the tower's base in a government-issue rain shell, talking easily into a handheld radio while two others unloaded crates from a hover-sled.

But the dinghy was there—no. Not theirs. A contractor skiff, gunmetal gray, Renn's markings. And the man talking was not a stranger.

Paloma knew the profile even before Voss made a sound beside her, a small wounded noise like a bird stepping on its own foot.

"Merrin," Voss breathed. "That's Ansel Merrin. Hydrologist. My second." His hands had closed into fists in the fern. "I sent him east with three others. I gave him the eastern cache coordinates. I told him to wait for my signal."

"And he came here instead," Paloma said quietly.

"He's telling them where we are." Voss's voice cracked on it. "Right now. He's telling them everything."

Bao shifted beside her, and Paloma felt rather than saw him counting: four men visible, probably six total, one generator, one skiff—and no way back down the trail without passing within thirty meters of the camp. The rain chose that moment to thicken, a gray curtain dropping over the knoll, and Paloma made the calculation that would define the rest of her life: they could retreat and lose the tower, or move now, in the rain, and take it.

"We go for the transmitter," she whispered. "Voss, you know the equipment. Bao—"

"I have the pages," Bao said. His hand pressed flat against his chest, over the oilcloth. "I'm not leaving them."

"I know," Paloma said. "That's why you're going first."

* * *

Later—much later, in the worst hours, she would replay those ninety seconds frame by frame and find the exact moment it went wrong. It was not the rain, which covered them well enough. It was not the generator, which masked their footsteps. It was the dog.

Nobody had mentioned a dog. A lean, yellow-eyed thing tied near the mess tarp lifted its head as Bao crossed the open ground between the treeline and the tower stairs, and it didn't bark—it did something worse. It stood. Silent, attentive, pointing like a compass needle, and one of the men loading crates followed its gaze and shouted a word that dissolved the rain into gunfire.

Paloma hit the mud. Bullets chewed the fern above her, and through the chaos she tracked Bao—Bao, who had taken the stairs two at a time, who had reached the transmitter shack door, who turned back for her. Of course he turned back for her. That was Bao. Twenty years of engines and loyalty, and he could no more leave her in the mud than he could leave a valve ungreased.

"Bao! Inside! Send the broadcast yourself!"

"There's no key set!" he shouted back, ducking as a round sparked off the railing. "Voss has to align the—"

Merrin stepped out of the smoke of the generator exhaust and shot him.

Not to kill—to drop. A pistol round through the meat of Bao's thigh, and Bao went down on the tower stairs with a sound Paloma had never heard him make, a short explosive grunt of pure surprise, as if betrayal were a physical weight landing on his spine. Two contractors swarmed him before Paloma could fire a shot. She saw them peel the oilcloth package from his chest like skinning something alive, saw Bao grab one man's wrist and nearly break it before the rifle stock came down across his temple.

"No!" Voss was screaming, rising out of the fern in plain view, and a contractor leveled at him, and Paloma fired wide to draw the aim, and then the world was muzzle flash and rain and Merrin's voice cutting through it all, clear and almost gentle:

"Captain Fairweather. We only want the papers. Walk away and everyone keeps breathing."

She looked at Bao, facedown on the stairs, blood diluting pink in the rain around his head. She looked at Voss, dragged upright by two men who handled him with the careful contempt reserved for madmen. She looked at the logbook pages in Merrin's hands—her crew's blood price, the drowned villages, the living water, everything Old Wen had died buying time for—

And she ran.

Not away. Down. Through the fern, off the trail, into the ravine on the tower's west side where no sane person would flee, because sane people didn't take a forty-degree slope of wet shale in the rain. She fell twice, tore her palm open on a root, tasted blood and loam, and kept going, because the alternative was standing still and standing still meant dying or worse. Behind her the shouting organized itself into pursuit, and ahead of her the ravine dropped toward the river, toward the hidden channel, toward Suri and the *Magpie Jay* and the only plan left, which was no plan at all.

* * *

She reached the water an hour past dusk, bleeding, shaking, half-drowned from a crossing she barely remembered making. The hidden channel behind Split-Tooth Island lay black and silent under the rain.

The channel was empty.

Paloma stood on the mud bank and called Suri's name until her voice shredded, soft at first, then screaming it, then just saying it, over and over, a woman reciting a prayer that had stopped working. No answering engine. No running light. No *Magpie Jay*.

Only, eventually, a shape in the reeds: their spare fuel drum, punctured, and wedged in the mud where the current had deposited it, one of Suri's red hair ties.

They had come for the boat while the rest of them climbed the ridge. Of course they had. Merrin had known about Split-Tooth—he'd helped chart these channels during the survey, Voss had said so once, casually, a detail that had seemed harmless then and now sat in Paloma's chest like a swallowed stone. They had taken her boat, her engineer, her navigator's memory of every safe passage on this river, and left her a punctured drum and a hair tie and the rain.

Paloma sat down in the mud. She let herself have exactly sixty seconds of it—the shaking, the useless animal grief, the white noise where a future used to be. She counted the seconds against her pulse because that was the only clock she trusted anymore.

At fifty-eight, she noticed the light.

Far downstream, around the bend of the main channel, a searchlight swept the water. Then another. Renn's patrol craft, running the Reach in force now, sweeping for survivors. Which meant Renn believed there might be survivors. Which meant somewhere between the tower and this mud bank, the net had a hole in it.

And which meant, she realized, her mind moving with the cold clarity of a flooded engine finally catching, that they had taken the *Magpie Jay* but not Suri—not yet—or Suri was aboard as a captive, feeding Renn's pilots channels that might be true or might be fever-gifted lies. Either way, Suri was alive. Suri was thinking. And Bao was breathing when they carried him off the stairs, because dead men don't get carried gently.

Three of hers, scattered. One logbook, torn from Bao's chest—but Bao had been Bao to the end, and when Paloma replayed the moment the stock came down, she remembered something she'd missed in the chaos: his hand, splayed against the mud of the stair, fingers spread. Five fingers. Four pages missing from the wrap when Merrin held it up.

One page still tucked in his boot. Or palmed. Or passed. Bao didn't do things by accident.

Paloma rose out of the mud, wrung the river from her sleeves, and turned upstream, away from the searchlights, toward the one place on the entire Serpent's Reach that Renn's boats could not follow and Merrin's maps could not chart: the flooded caves, where the water moved against the current, and where, if Suri's fever had taught them anything at all, the river itself had begun choosing sides.

The rain came down harder. Somewhere behind her, the horizon flickered with the monsoon's true arrival, sheet lightning walking the ridgeline like a sentry changing post.

"Hold on," she said—to Bao, to Suri, to the boat, to the dead man at the wheel he loved. "I'm coming back for all of it."

The river, high and hungry and humming faintly, very faintly, with that impossible chord, carried her words upstream.
