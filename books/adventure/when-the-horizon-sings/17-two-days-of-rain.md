## 17. Two Days of Rain

The rain did not fall so much as arrive, all at once, like a debt called in.

Suri felt it before she heard it—a pressure change in her ears, a hush along the water that even the engine couldn't break—and then the sky simply opened, and the Serpent's Reach became a different river entirely. The banks she had known since childhood dissolved into brown turbulence. Trees leaned and groaned. And somewhere upstream, in a channel only she could see behind her closed eyes, the water was rising toward the rock throat they'd threaded in the dark.

She stood at the stern rail under the awning, wrists raw where the zip ties had been, watching Renn's men lash the Magpie Jay to a drowned fig tree while the world came apart around them.

They had taken everything except her knowledge. That was their mistake.

* * *

Renn kept her in the wheelhouse because it was easier than keeping her anywhere else. There was nowhere for her to go—the nearest bank was forty meters of churning floodwater, and the men had taken Bao's tools, Old Wen's charts, the flare gun, everything with an edge or a spark. They left her the chart table, ironically, because Renn believed charts were the same thing as knowledge, and he wanted her where he could watch her use them.

"Show me again," he said, spreading the survey map across the table, weighting the corners with brass fittings he'd stripped from Old Wen's station. Rain hammered the wheelhouse glass so hard the world beyond was a gray smear. "The deep channel past Kettle Bend. You told my pilot it shoals to a meter."

"It does." She kept her voice flat, tired, the voice of someone who had stopped fighting. "In dry season."

"And now?"

"Now the whole bend is one channel. Two fathoms, maybe more. The flood erases the bars." She traced the line with one finger, letting him watch her do it, letting him believe she needed the map at all. Behind her eyes was a different map entirely—one drawn in fever, in light, in the patient pressure of something ancient moving through the water. On that map, Kettle Bend was marked not with depth soundings but with a sensation, like a hand pressed flat against her sternum: *safe, but loud.*

"You understand I have no reason to trust you," Renn said. He wasn't looking at the map. He was looking at her face, the way a man reads weather.

"No," she agreed. "You don't. But you have less reason to trust your own pilot, who has run this river maybe six times, in a season when the river has stopped being itself." She let a little bitterness into it, real bitterness, because it was true and truth made the best bait. "You took our navigator's body off this boat and threw our captain into the jungle. What you have left is me, and I am the only person alive who knows what this water is doing right now."

Something moved behind his eyes—not warmth, nothing so human as warmth, but the acknowledgment of a useful tool. She had seen that look before, on buyers at her mother's clinic, appraising equipment.

"The transmitter," he said. "How long to reach it by water?"

"If we run today, through the flood—" she pretended to calculate, drawing lines that meant nothing, "—two days. If we wait out the worst of it, four. Maybe five. The channels are moving. What was safe this morning may be a rock garden by tonight."

He studied her a long moment. Then he rolled up the map and said, "Get some rest," which was his way of saying *I'm watching you*, and left two men on the deck below her bunk, smoking under the awning, passing a bottle back and forth.

Suri lay in her hammock in the dark and listened to the rain and did not rest at all.

* * *

The radio was the thing she mourned most.

Bao had built it from salvage—half military, half fishing-boat, held together with solder and stubbornness—and its voice was a warm crackle she'd fallen asleep to a hundred nights. Renn's communications man had locked down every band, but locks were Bao's language too, and he had taught her enough: *if you ever need me, key the mic three times without speaking. Three clicks. I'll hear it in the noise floor if I'm alive to hear anything.*

On the first night, wedged in her hammock with the rain roaring, she waited until the deck watch changed, until the smokers' bottle was half empty and their laughter had gone loose, and she slid into the wheelhouse barefoot, silent, heart slamming against her ribs like a trapped bird.

The set was powered down. She brought it up with the volume at zero, found the band, and keyed the microphone. Once. Twice. Three times.

Nothing. Static, thick as the rain.

She tried again an hour later, and again near dawn, and on the fourth attempt—just as she was about to give up, just as despair was settling into her chest like cold water filling a hull—she heard it. Faint, buried under the static, almost imagined:

Three clicks back.

Then, barely, a whisper of a voice, so garbled she had to close her eyes and let the shape of it reach her rather than the words: *"...alive... moving... tell..."* And then gone, swallowed by the storm.

She sat in the dark wheelhouse with her forehead against the cool glass and cried without making any sound, because Paloma Fairweather was alive, and Voss was alive, and they were moving, and somewhere out in all that drowning green the truth was still walking toward the coast on two exhausted legs.

Then she wiped her face, checked that the transmit lamp hadn't glowed in anyone's sightline, and went back to her hammock to plan how to lie beautifully.

* * *

The lying began in earnest the next morning.

Renn wanted progress. His superiors downstream wanted progress. Every hour that passed without the logbook pages in his hands was an hour in which the story could grow legs, and Renn was a man who understood stories the way he understood rivers: as things to be dammed and diverted before they flooded the wrong fields.

So Suri gave him progress.

"There's a cutoff channel past the drowned village," she told him, pointing at a place on the map that she knew—from the other map, the true one humming behind her eyes—was a dead-end slough choked with strainer trees. "It saves six hours against running the main stem. My captain would never take it in flood, but your pilot is good, and the water's high enough."

She watched him weigh it. She watched him choose speed, because speed was what fear always chose.

The Magpie Jay nosed into the cutoff at midmorning, and for twenty minutes it was magnificent—the old boat surging through brown water, spray coming over the bow, Renn's men grinning at each other like boys—and then the channel bent, and narrowed, and ended in a wall of tangled snags with the current pushing them helplessly into it.

It took them four hours to work free. A strut of the rail was torn away. One of Renn's men went into the water briefly, and came out white-faced and shaking, having learned in thirty seconds what river people learn in childhood: that the Reach forgives nothing, and keeps receipts.

Renn came to the wheelhouse afterward, soaked to the waist, and stood very close to her, and for a moment she thought—calmly, strangely calmly—that this was how it ended, thrown to the flood for a bad channel.

"My mistake," she said, before he could speak. "The flood moved the snag line. It wasn't there when I ran this stretch in April." She met his eyes. "Kill me and you're navigating blind through monsoon water with a hostage crew and a deadline. Keep me and I'll get you there. Those are your options. I'd think carefully."

The silence stretched. Rain filled it.

"Next mistake," Renn said quietly, "and there won't be a conversation," and he left her alone with the map and her hammering heart and the knowledge that she had spent nearly all her credit in a single morning.

But it had worked, in the way that mattered. Four hours lost. A crew rattled. And Renn now half-distrusted her channels—which meant that when she fed him the next one, the false one, the one that would take them looping away from the coast while Paloma and Voss closed the distance—he would hesitate, and check, and lose another hour deciding.

Endurance, Old Wen used to say, isn't holding still. It's paying in small coins so you keep something in reserve. She paid. Hour by hour, coin by coin.

* * *

That night the fever's map spoke to her again.

It happened in the borderland between waking and sleep, the place where the caves had first touched her—that humming chamber where the water moved against the current, where something vast and slow and patient had flowed up through her palms and rearranged the architecture of her mind. She had never told anyone the whole of it. Not Paloma, not Bao. Some things you carry because saying them aloud makes them smaller.

But in the dark, with the rain drumming the roof like ten thousand fingers, the network reached for her again—or she reached for it; the direction hardly mattered anymore—and she felt the river entire. Not a line on a map. A body. Veins and arteries, the aquifer breathing beneath the karst, the flood not a disaster but a pulse, the whole watershed turning over in its sleep like something waking from a long dream.

And within that body, two small warm points moving through the jungle on the eastern ridge. She didn't know how she knew. She simply knew, the way you know where your own hand is in the dark.

*They're ahead of schedule,* she thought, and the thought seemed to travel, seemed to be received, and though no answer came in words, the water around the anchored Magpie Jay pressed gently against the hull, once, twice, like a heartbeat acknowledging a signal.

She slept then, really slept, for the first time since Old Wen died.

* * *

The second day, Renn received word from downstream.

She watched him read it in the wheelhouse doorway, watched his jaw tighten, watched him crumple the paper and stand looking out at the flood for a long time. Later she heard the fragments from the deck: relay tower compromised, contractor team overdue, coastal authorities asking questions. Somewhere out in the rain, Paloma had done whatever Paloma had gone into the jungle to do, and the story was growing legs exactly as Renn feared.

He came to her at dusk, wet, hollow-eyed, and put the survey map on the table between them.

"How far," he said, "is the transmitter from here. By the fastest route. Your honest estimate."

Suri looked at the map, and looked at him, and understood that the game had changed. He wasn't asking for navigation anymore. He was asking whether to abandon the boat and cut overland himself, and her answer might decide whether he stayed aboard—where she could keep steering him wrong—or vanished into the jungle with his best men, leaving her with a skeleton crew and a chance.

She thought of Paloma walking through that drowning green. Of Bao in whatever cage they'd put him in. Of Old Wen at the wheel he loved, forever.

"Honest estimate," she said slowly, "the overland route is suicide in this rain. The ridge trails wash out. I've seen it." She tapped the map. "By water, following the main stem, day and a half. But—" and here she gave him the truest thing she had, because the truest things make the sharpest hooks, "—your pilot can't hold the Magpie Jay in flood current at night. I can. If you want to make that transmitter before your superiors stop answering your calls, you need me at the wheel, and you need to leave tonight."

Renn stared at her. Rain sheeted off the awning between them like a curtain.

"You're offering to run my boat for me," he said.

"I'm offering to get this over with," she said. "For all of us."

It was, she reflected later, the biggest coin she had ever spent. Everything, wagered on one throw: that Renn's fear of his own masters was greater than his suspicion of her; that he would trade caution for speed one last time; that a boat in motion, with her at the wheel and the flood at its throat, offered chances that a boat at anchor never would.

Renn looked at the map a long moment. Then he said, "Wake the pilot. We leave in twenty minutes," and walked out into the rain.

Suri stood alone in the wheelhouse, hands steady on the wheel Old Wen had died holding, and felt the river rise around them, patient and immense, carrying its secrets toward the sea.

Ahead, through the rain-grayed dark, the channel forked—one branch wide and lit by Renn's searchlight, the way she had promised him; the other narrow, black, unmarked on any paper map, known only to her and to the water.

Twenty minutes, she thought. Twenty minutes to choose which darkness to steer into.

She wiped the glass with her sleeve, and waited, and listened to the horizon sing.
