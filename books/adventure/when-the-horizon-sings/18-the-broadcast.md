## 18. The Broadcast

The rain had found them two hours before dawn and did not intend to let go.

Paloma climbed the last switchback of the ridge with Voss a half-step behind her, both of them moving through a downpour that came down not in drops but in sheets, in whole moving walls of water that turned the jungle into something oceanic. The trail Old Wen's charts had promised was gone—washed to mud, swallowed by root tangles—but the tower rose ahead of them through the murk like the rib of some drowned god, its guy-wires singing in the wind with a sound she almost recognized.

Almost. Not quite the caves' hum. Nothing was quite that.

"Relay station," Voss shouted over the rain, pointing at the squat concrete bunker at the tower's base. "Coastal trunk line. If it still has power, it can reach every port authority from Greywater to Cape Meridian."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then we've climbed a mountain for nothing and I'll owe you an apology I don't have time to deliver."

The bunker door was swollen shut with humidity. They hit it together, shoulder to shoulder, three times before it gave with a groan of rusted hinges and exhaled a breath of air so old and dry it felt like opening a tomb. Inside: dust, a desk, a wall of dead equipment—and one cabinet-mounted transmitter the size of a coffin, its status lamp burning a stubborn amber green.

"It's on solar trickle," Voss said, already moving, pulling a canvas satchel from under the desk as if he'd left it there himself years ago. Maybe he had. Survey teams cached supplies like squirrels; it was the first honest thing about him. "Backup battery bank's probably flat, but the array tops it up. Give me ten minutes."

"You have five." Paloma set the oilcloth bundle on the desk and unwrapped it with hands that would not entirely stop shaking. Not cold. She'd stopped telling herself it was cold somewhere on the second day of rain. The logbook pages were warped with damp but legible, every page Bao had photographed twice, every chart Suri had memorized in her fever and re-drawn from memory in charcoal on sailcloth because paper couldn't be trusted anymore. The diversion plans. The aquifer surveys. The government seals. The list of downstream settlements—forty-one named communities, eleven thousand people, their villages drawn in careful surveyor's ink inside a blue shaded zone labeled *inundation profile: permanent*.

Eleven thousand people who thought the river belonged to them.

She spread the pages across the desk and looked at them until they stopped looking like paper and started looking like what they were: a sentence already written, waiting only for someone to read it aloud.

Voss worked at the transmitter's guts with a soldering iron and the focus of a man performing surgery on his own child. Rain hammered the bunker roof like fists. Somewhere below them, down the flooded slopes, the Serpent's Reach was rising—she could feel it in the way the ground seemed to breathe, in the subsonic tremor that came up through her boots. The river was coming for its valley. It always did. The only question this year was what else it would carry away.

"Frequency's live," Voss said quietly. "Antenna's intact. We're lucky."

"We're not lucky." She fed the first page into the document scanner he'd rigged—a salvaged survey unit, jury-built, held together with wire and prayer. "We're just early. There's a difference, and the difference is work."

He looked at her then—the first time in two days of forced march he'd looked at her as anything other than a means of transport—and something in his exhausted face shifted. "You could still take the money," he said. "Renn's offer. It wasn't small."

"No," she agreed. "It wasn't."

"You have debts. A boat that's barely floating. A crew that trusted you and got shot at."

"Yes." She aligned the first page under the scanner's light. The blue inundation zone glowed faintly, like something drowned. "And one of my crew is dead because of what's on these pages, and another is on Renn's boat right now keeping him busy with lies, and I am not going to let either of those things be for nothing. Start the clock, Doctor. We broadcast until it's done or we're interrupted. Whichever comes first."

* * *

It took forty minutes to transmit what the government had spent years burying.

They sent it wide and they sent it plain: every port authority on the coast, every shipping registry, every newsroom whose frequency Voss could pull from memory—which turned out to be most of them; a man who'd spent a career hiding data knew exactly where all the doors were. No encryption. No authentication codes. Plain text and scanned images, repeated three times each, because Paloma Fairweather knew how rivers worked and she suspected bureaucracies worked the same way: things got lost unless you made losing them expensive.

She read the cover statement herself, into a microphone older than she was, her voice sounding strange and enormous in the concrete room.

*"This is Paloma Fairweather, master of the riverboat Magpie Jay, transmitting from relay station nine on the upper Serpent's Reach. What follows is the complete field record of the government survey vessel Kestrel, transmitted without authorization. It documents a planned diversion and impoundment of the upper Reach watershed. It documents forty-one settlements scheduled for permanent inundation. And it documents—"*

She paused. Her throat closed around the next words, and she made herself say them anyway, slowly, the way you speak over open water.

*"—it documents that the aquifer system beneath the Reach is not inert. It is a living hydrological network of a kind unrecorded anywhere in the scientific literature. It responds. It communicates. Dr. Elias Voss, the survey's own geologist, will attest to everything in this record under his own name and seal. I am sending it to everyone, because I have learned that when you send the truth to one office, it drowns. When you send it to all of them at once, it has nowhere to drown."*

Voss huffed something that was almost a laugh. "That's going in the textbooks," he said. "*Nowhere to drown.*"

"Shut up and scan."

Page by page. Chart by chart. The Kestrel's sealed log, transcribed in Bao's neat hand where the original had been too fragile. Suri's charcoal maps of channels no survey had ever recorded, annotated in her fever-sharp script: *the water remembers where it has been.* Photographs of the cave chambers, of the current that ran uphill, of the place where Suri had put her hand into water that put something of itself back into her.

Outside, the rain thickened. Thunder rolled down the valley in long, grinding volleys, and beneath it—fainter, steadier, unmistakable once you'd heard it once—came the hum. Rising with the water level. The river filling its throat, clearing it, singing louder the fuller it got.

Paloma tried not to listen. She failed.

* * *

The transmission was seventy percent complete when the radio crackled with a voice that was not theirs.

"—station nine, station nine, do you copy, this is an authorized government channel, cease transmission immediately, acknowledge—"

Voss's head came up like a deer's. "That's a coastal relay bounce. Someone's answering on our frequency."

"Or someone's tracking it." Paloma kept feeding pages. "Keep scanning."

"Fairweather." The voice again, closer now, resolving through static into something she knew. Something that made the skin of her arms tighten. "I know that cadence. You always did read your cargo manifests like scripture."

Renn.

She keyed the mic. "Security Contractor Renn. You're transmitting on an open emergency band. That's a fineable offense in three jurisdictions."

"So is theft of government property, piracy of a licensed charter, and impersonating a rescue operation." Calm. Reasonable. The voice of a man reading terms and conditions aloud while his men kicked in your door. "Paloma. You're on a ridge in a monsoon with no boat and no exit. Whatever you think you're accomplishing, it ends today. The question is whether it ends with you walking down this mountain or being carried."

"The question," she said, "is whether eleven thousand people get to keep their homes. Everything else is weather."

Behind her, Voss loaded the next chart. The scanner whirred. Seventy-five percent.

"You've read the file," Renn said, and for the first time something moved underneath the calm—not anger, exactly. Tiredness. "Then you know it's not murder, it's arithmetic. Forty-one villages against power for six million people in the delta cities. Against flood control that saves hundreds of thousands. You want to talk about drowning? Every year the lower Reach floods anyway and kills more than those villages hold. This project *ends* that. Somebody pays. That's not villainy, Captain. That's engineering."

"That's somebody else doing the paying," Paloma said. "I've run cargo for the delta cities for nineteen years. I like the delta cities. But I've never once met a city that drowned itself to save a village, and I don't expect to start today."

Eighty percent. The scanner ate the aquifer resonance studies, the biological anomaly reports, the pages where the Kestrel's own scientists had written, in increasingly shaken handwriting, *the medium appears to be the message.*

"Last chance," Renn said. "Walk away. I'll even let you keep the boat."

"The boat's mine. It was always mine. You never had it—you just borrowed my courage and called it salvage." She looked at Voss. "How much longer?"

"Twelve minutes if nothing breaks."

"Nothing's breaking." She keyed the mic one more time. "Renn. Tell me one thing, since you're feeling conversational. Old Wen. Sixty-three years old. Cooked for survey crews before you were out of school. He died at a wheel on my boat because your men wanted a book. Was that arithmetic too?"

Silence on the channel. Long enough that she thought the storm had eaten it.

"He pulled a weapon," Renn said finally.

"He pulled a *ladle*. He was sixty-three and he stood between your men and a girl with a fever, and you want to call it a weapon engagement." She heard her own voice go low and even, the voice she used in rapids, the voice that meant the water had decided and there was nothing left to argue with. "Come up the mountain, Renn. Come see what the river does to men who do sums with other people's lives."

She cut the channel. In the sudden quiet, Voss said softly, "That was either very brave or very stupid."

"On this river," Paloma said, "those are the same word."

* * *

Ninety-two percent. Ninety-four. The rain outside had become a solid roaring thing, and through the bunker's high slit window the world was white water and black lightning. Paloma's shoulders ached from hunching over the scanner. Her eyes burned. She thought about Suri aboard the Magpie Jay, feeding Renn's navigator false channels in that sweet fever-calm voice of hers, buying minutes with lies the way Old Wen used to buy fish with stories. She thought about Bao in whatever cell or hold they'd put him in, and whether he was counting rivets or planning escapes, and she knew—with the certainty of twenty years of knowing her own crew—that he was doing both.

Ninety-six percent.

"They'll have landed a party at the base of the ridge," Voss said. He'd stopped pretending otherwise an hour ago. "If Renn answered us personally, he's close. Twenty minutes, maybe less, in this rain."

"Then we have twenty minutes to send four percent."

"Four percent is the settlement registry and the final seal page. It's the part that makes it undeniable."

"Then it's the part we send last," Paloma said, "so it's the part they arrive too late to stop."

The scanner whirred. Lightning walked down the ridge and the lights flickered and Voss swore and threw a bypass across the battery bank with his bare hands, and the machine lived, and the percentage climbed.

Ninety-eight.

And then, through the rain-roar and the thunder and the deepening hum of the river below, Paloma heard something else.

Engines. More than one. Not on the ridge trail—on the *river*, down in the flooded valley, the sound carrying strangely up through the storm. And threaded through it, thin and impossible and utterly familiar, a sound that stopped her heart mid-beat:

The Magpie Jay's horn. Two short, one long. Their old signal. *Crew to deck. Now.*

Suri.

"She's loose," Paloma breathed. "She's got the boat—"

"Or Renn's using the horn to make you look," Voss said. "He knows your signals. He's had days."

"I know the difference between my boat's horn played by my crew and played by a stranger. Two short, one long, half-second late on the long. That's Suri. She always was half a second late." She was already moving, grabbing the oilcloth, repacking the remaining pages with hands that finally, finally were steady. "How much longer on the last pages?"

"Three minutes. Paloma—if you go down there and it's a trap—"

"If I stay up here and it's real, she's alone on that boat with armed men and a river that's rising faster than anyone's math." She slung the bundle across her chest. "Finish the transmission. All of it. Send the seal page to every frequency you can find, twice. Then get off this ridge, Doctor, because I promise you the river's coming up it."

She paused at the bunker door, one hand on the rusted frame, and looked back at the transmitter's amber light—still burning, still faithful, still pouring the truth out into the storm to every port and newsroom on the coast.

"Hey, Voss," she said. "When this reaches Greywater—when it reaches everywhere—say it plainly. Say the river is alive. Don't let them turn it into a footnote."

"It won't be a footnote," he said. "Not after tonight."

She stepped out into the deluge, and the rain took her like the river taking a tributary, and below her in the white chaos of the flooded valley the Magpie Jay's horn sounded again—two short, one long, half a second late—and then a third blast, longer, wrong, urgent, a signal that wasn't in any codebook Paloma knew.

A distress signal. Suri's own.

Down through the lightning, down the drowning trail, Paloma Fairweather began to run.
