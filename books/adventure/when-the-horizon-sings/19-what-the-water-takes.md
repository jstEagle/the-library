## 19. What the Water Takes

The rain came down like a verdict.

Paloma stood at the base of the relay tower with the last logbook page pressed flat against her chest, inside her jacket, against her heartbeat, and watched Renn's men fan out through the flooded treeline with the unhurried confidence of people who had never once been told no. The monsoon had turned the world to gray water and gray sky, and between them the tower rose on its concrete footing like a drowned man's finger pointing at nothing.

Two days of jungle. Two days of Voss moving ahead of her through the deluge like a man who had memorized the shape of his own guilt, and Paloma following because there was nowhere else left to go. The transmitter was the last mouth the truth had. Everything else—the boat, Bao, the survey records in their waterproof case—was downstream somewhere in Renn's hands or the river's.

*Not everything,* she thought, and felt the paper shift against her ribs. *I kept one thing back.*

"Fairweather." Renn's voice carried through the rain without effort, the way certain voices do, built for orders rather than conversation. He stood at the tower's base where the maintenance ladder began its climb, and he did not look at her so much as through her, cataloguing. "You've made this very tiring."

"You could give up," she said. "It's an option. People do it."

Something moved behind his eyes—not anger, she realized. Calculation. He glanced up at the tower, at the antenna farm swaying wet and black against the clouds, and then back down at the water pooling around the foundation blocks, brown and fast-moving now, carrying branches and whole sections of riverbank past them toward the coast.

"No," he said. "I don't think I will."

He raised one hand, and two of his men came forward carrying things that were not climbing gear. Canisters. A spool of det cord that caught the gray light like a length of pale intestine.

Paloma's stomach dropped through the flooded ground. "You're going to blow it."

"I'm going to *tidy up.*" Renn said it mildly, almost kindly, which was somehow worse. "A storm-damaged relay station. Tragic. The whole Reach is drowning; who's going to audit one fallen tower?" He tilted his head at her. "You should have taken my offer, Captain. I want you to know that. You'd have been rich, and everyone you care about would be alive or free, depending on how we're counting."

"And the villages?"

"What villages?" And there it was—the whole philosophy in three words, delivered without a flicker. He turned to his men. "Set the charges at the footing. North face first."

They moved with professional calm, wading through water that was rising around the tower's legs with a speed that even Paloma, who had read this river her whole life, found unsettling. The flood had come early and come angry. Old Wen would have known what it meant. Old Wen would have read the water the way other men read scripture, and he would have told her: *the river is not rising, girl. The river is coming.*

She looked at the foundation blocks and saw it then, what Renn's men in their efficiency had not bothered to see. The water around the north footing wasn't pooling. It was *circling*. Moving in a slow, deliberate spiral, the way water moves around something it is considering.

Voss appeared beside her out of the rain, close enough that she could smell the jungle on him—rot and green and two days of fear. His voice was barely above the downpour.

"The aquifer vents under this ridge," he said. "That's why they built the tower here—dry rock, stable footing. But the rock is only dry because the water underneath is holding still." His eyes tracked the spiral current. "It isn't holding still anymore."

"You're saying the river's going to take the tower down on its own."

"I'm saying the river has been patient," Voss said, "and patience runs out like everything else."

On the tower's north face, Renn's men finished taping the cord and retreated, and Renn himself produced a detonator from his jacket with the small ceremony of a man opening a bottle of wine. He looked across the flooded clearing and found Paloma's eyes, and held them, and she understood that this was for her—that he wanted her watching when the last copy of the truth came down in pieces.

"Captain," he called. "Last chance to hand over that page."

She put her hand inside her jacket, slowly, and drew out the folded paper—and let the wind take it.

It was a stupid risk, a gambler's risk, the kind of thing Old Wen would have cuffed her ear for. The page lifted, flapped once like a wounded bird, and went sailing out over the brown water, tumbling, and every head in the clearing turned to follow it—including, for exactly two seconds, Renn's.

Paloma ran.

Not away. *Toward.* Toward the ladder, while every gun in Renn's team was pointed at a piece of paper. She hit the rungs climbing, boots slipping on wet steel, hauling herself up hand over hand while the rain tried to peel her off the tower. Below her, shouting. A shot cracked and took a chip out of the railing a hand's width from her face, and she climbed faster, because below was death and above was only weather.

The transmitter room was a metal box bolted to the tower's waist, forty feet up, its door swinging loose on one hinge. She threw herself inside and slammed it behind her and found—miraculously, absurdly—the equipment intact, dry, powered by the cable that ran down into the ridge and whatever generator shed the government had buried there. Someone had built this thing to survive the end of the world. Perhaps they'd known what would be asked of it.

Her hands shook so badly she could barely work the panel. Frequency. Open band. Every port authority, every newsroom, every coastal office that Voss had listed for her in the dark of the trek, recited twice until she had it cold. She keyed the mic.

"This is Paloma Fairweather, master of the riverboat Magpie Jay, transmitting from relay tower nine on the Serpent's Reach." Her voice came out steadier than her hands. "What follows is the complete field record of the government survey vessel Kestrel. Listen all of you. Listen to what your government found, and what it plans to do with your water."

She opened the case of documents she'd carried strapped to her back through two days of jungle—Voss's copies, the ones Renn didn't know about, the ones that had cost Old Wen his life to keep moving—and fed the first page to the scanner with hands that would not stop trembling.

Below, the detonator clicked.

The explosion came up through the tower's bones like a scream through a throat. The whole structure lurched, swung, rang like a struck bell, and Paloma was thrown against the bulkhead with the scanner still whirring, page after page feeding through, the transmission light burning steady green. They hadn't brought enough charge to drop it—they'd brought enough to *wound* it, and a wounded tower forty feet tall does not fall straight. It leans. It groans. It considers.

Through the porthole glass she watched the north footing crumble into the brown water, concrete blooming apart like rotten fruit, and she watched the spiral current widen to receive it.

* * *

Renn stood in water to his knees and understood, in the slow way understanding comes to men who have never needed it before, that the river had changed its mind about him.

The tower leaned but did not fall. That was almost insulting. But the footing was gone, the charges had spent themselves in churning mud and shattered rock, and the water that rushed into the wound they'd made was not the polite brown flood of an ordinary monsoon. It was cold. It came up around his thighs with a pressure that felt intentional, and where it passed, the silt of the riverbed lifted and swirled and did not settle.

"Sir!" One of his men, the young one, the one whose name Renn had never bothered to learn. "Sir, the ground's going—we need to get back to the boat!"

The boat. Yes. The patrol boat sat anchored in the channel fifty meters out, lights burning yellow through the rain, and between here and there the water was rising with a speed that no longer pretended to be natural. Renn looked at it and did the arithmetic he had always been good at: assets, liabilities, acceptable losses. The tower still stood. The woman was still in it, transmitting God knew what to God knew whom, but transmissions could be denied, discredited, drowned in counter-narrative. What could not be survived was the paper. The original record. The thing that made denial impossible.

He saw it then—a white flutter in the churn, snagged on a half-submerged root maybe ten meters out, the logbook page the captain had thrown to the wind. Foolish woman. Sentimental woman. The river had brought it right back to him like a gift.

"Cover me," he said, and waded out.

The water took him at the third step.

Not gradually. Not the way water usually takes a man, inch by inch, giving him time to bargain. One moment it was at his knees and the next it was at his chest, as if the river had simply reached up and pulled him down into itself, and Renn—who had spent his whole career making other people afraid—discovered what it was to be small. He grabbed for the root. The page was there, plastered against the wood, inches from his fingers, and he seized it, actually seized it, felt the wet paper crumple in his fist—

And felt the root move.

No. Not the root. Something beneath the root, beneath the riverbed, beneath the whole drowned ridge—a vast slow shifting, like a body turning over in sleep, and the water around him lit faintly from below with a pale blue-green glow that he recognized, in the last rational corner of his mind, from Voss's fevered descriptions. From the caves. From the thing they had found and planned to dam.

It was everywhere. It had always been everywhere. Under every channel, every tributary, every well and fishing hole from the highlands to the sea, one continuous living darkness, and he was standing in it, and it was awake.

The current closed over his head like a door.

He fought, because that was what he was. He fought the way he had fought his whole life, efficiently, ruthlessly, spending everything he had, and the river spent more. The page tore from his fist and went spinning away into the brown, and he lunged after it because he could not help himself, because the page had become the last thing in the world that mattered, and the river let him chase it just far enough.

The flood-swollen channel, running hard toward the coast, took him the way it took branches and boats and the foundations of towers: without malice, without mercy, as freight.

His men stood on the shrinking bank and did not follow. Men like that know the difference between an order and a suicide, whatever their contracts say.

* * *

The tower was dying around Paloma, and she kept reading frequencies.

Each burst went out clean—coastal authorities, port registries, the newsrooms in Harbortown and Saltmere and the capital beyond them, each packet of scanned pages confirmed received, confirmed received, confirmed received. Somewhere downstream, in offices with dry floors and hot tea, people she would never meet were beginning to read what Elias Voss had measured in the dark. The diversion plans. The dam schematics. The eleven settlements marked in the survey's own handwriting as *acceptable inundation zones.*

Eleven villages. Eleven names. She read them aloud into the microphone, one by one, because documents could be disputed but names were names.

The floor tilted fifteen degrees and stayed there. Water was coming up the ladder shaft now, black and glowing faintly, and she understood with a strange calm that the river was climbing the tower after her—not angrily, not hungrily, just thoroughly, the way it did everything. She had perhaps minutes. She keyed the mic one last time.

"To anyone receiving: the diversion must not proceed. The aquifer is alive. I've seen it. It knows we're here." She paused, and heard herself add, softly, "It forgives nothing. But it settles accounts."

Then the north face finally gave, and the tower began to go down.

Falling from a height is mostly waiting. She remembered that from a childhood fall off the Magpie Jay's mast—how long the air holds you, how much thinking fits inside two seconds. The transmitter room tore loose and dropped through rain and roar, and Paloma wrapped her arms around the document case and thought, with absurd clarity: *Old Wen died at a wheel he loved. Let me land near water I love.*

The impact never came.

Something caught her—caught the whole falling room—decelerating it with a violence that still broke two ribs and filled her mouth with blood, but broke the fall nonetheless. Through the shattered porthole she saw the impossible: the floodwater beneath the tower had risen up in a swelling mound, a hill of brown water standing where no hill should stand, cradling the wreckage the way a hand cradles a bird. Around the mound the river raged on in its ordinary channels, indifferent, but here, just here, it had chosen to be gentle.

Then the mound collapsed, and the wreckage, and Paloma, went into the flood together, and gentleness was over.

The cold hit her like a wall. The river had her now, tumbling her end over end through brown roaring dark, and the case was torn from her arms, and she thought distantly: *at least it's out, at least it's sent, the rest doesn't matter*—and then her head broke surface for one gasp and she saw the page. Her page. The decoy, the one she'd thrown to the wind, white and ragged, riding the current ten meters away, and some stubborn animal part of her brain that did not accept finished said *go*, and she went.

She was a strong swimmer. It didn't matter. The current was stronger than anything, and it was taking her the same route it had taken Renn, and she knew it, knew it the way you know a road in a nightmare, and she reached for the page anyway because reaching was all she had left. Her fingers brushed paper. Her lungs burned. The grey light above the water was getting further away.

A horn sounded—one long bellowing note, unmistakable, a riverboat's voice.

The Magpie Jay came out of the rain like a rumor, patched and listing and missing half her rail, and Paloma's heart broke open with joy before her mind caught up to ask how. Lines flew. Suri stood at the bow with a throw-ring coiled in both hands, hair plastered to her skull, eyes blazing with a fever-light that was no longer fever, and she threw with a precision no deckhand alive could match—the ring arced through the deluge and landed across Paloma's shoulders like a blessing.

"Hold!" Suri screamed. "Hold on, Captain!"

The river pulled. The line sang. For one endless moment Paloma hung between them—the water's grip and the rope's—and felt the current consider her, weigh her, decide. Then the pull eased. Not released. *Eased.* The way a creditor releases a debtor who has paid in full.

They hauled her aboard hand over hand, and she came over the rail coughing brown water and laughing, actually laughing, broken-ribbed and bleeding and laughing, because Bao was there—Bao, soaked and bruised and alive, having slipped his captors somewhere in the chaos of the flood—and Suri was there, and the old boat groaned beneath her feet like a living thing glad of its crew.

"How," Paloma managed. "How do you have my boat."

Suri's smile was wild and tired. "Renn's helmsman surrendered her when the river started glowing. Said no contract covered that." She looked out at the churning water, at the fading pale light beneath it. "Also I may have suggested some channels to him. Channels that don't exist."

Paloma followed her gaze downstream, to where the flood ran hard toward the coast, carrying with it branches, silt, a crumpled white page, and a man in a black jacket who had chased the page too far. She watched the place where the water had taken him until the rain erased it.

"He went after it," she said quietly. "The decoy. He drowned chasing a blank page."

"The river keeps its receipts," Suri said—an Old Wen phrase, worn smooth by use, and hearing it in her voice nearly undid Paloma entirely.

Voss emerged from the wheelhouse, gaunt and grey, and looked at the water for a long time. "It let you go," he said. Not a question.

"It settled with me," Paloma said. She put her hand over her broken ribs and over the empty place where the case had been, and found that neither hurt as much as expected. "Everything's transmitted. All of it. There's nothing left to steal, nothing left to drown. Whatever happens next happens on dry land, in courtrooms, in daylight." She turned to Bao. "Get us downstream. Slowly. We've paid enough for speed."

The Magpie Jay turned her battered bow toward the coast, and the rain began, at long last, to slacken.

Behind them, the relay tower lay half-sunk in the shallows, its antenna pointing at the clouds like a question already answered. Ahead, the horizon was a thin bright line where the storm ended and the sea began—and from somewhere beneath the hull, deep in the flooded channels where no engine sound could reach, Paloma heard it again: the low harmonic hum, rising through the keel, through the deck, through the soles of her feet.

Not a threat. A receipt, stamped and filed.

The river knew what it was owed. And somewhere downstream, in the offices of the powerful, the bill was already arriving.
