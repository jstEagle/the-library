## 20. Aftermath in High Water

Bao had spent thirty years listening to engines, and he had learned that every machine told you the truth about itself if you were patient enough to hear it. A bearing on its way out sang a thin note before it screamed. A fuel line about to fail ticked like a clock counting down. The Magpie Jay, moored at the customs pier in Port Meridian with a borrowed engine mount and a hull scarred from the rock throat, was talking to him now—a low, uneven shudder through the deck plates that said *I am tired, but I am not done.*

He lay under the housing with a wrench in one hand and listened, and for the first time in weeks the truth he heard was simple: they had made it.

The radio in the wheelhouse had been going for three days straight. Not their radio—the whole coast's. Every newsroom, every port authority, every river office from Meridian to the salt delta had received what Paloma had sent from the ridge transmitter, and the words had gone off like water finding a crack in a dam. Bao kept the volume low while he worked, but he could not keep himself from listening. He had helped crack the Kestrel's sealed logbook; he knew every line of it by heart now, could recite the diversion schematics the way other men recited prayers. Hearing strangers read them aloud on the air—engineers arguing flow rates, hydrologists saying *this aquifer is alive, this is impossible, this is impossible*—was like hearing his own heartbeat broadcast over the harbor.

"—confirming receipt of survey records attributed to the vessel Kestrel," a voice said, clipped and official. "The Ministry of Waterways has issued an emergency injunction halting all construction activity in the upper Serpent's Reach pending independent review. Repeat: all construction halted."

Bao closed his eyes under the engine housing and let the wrench rest against his chest.

Halted. Pending review. Words made of paper, and paper was exactly what Renn's employers had trusted all along—paper contracts, paper secrecy clauses, paper penalties designed to sink a riverboat captain without a ripple. Now the paper was turning against them. There was a satisfaction in that which Bao did not entirely trust. Machines taught you that nothing ran forever on momentum alone. Someone still had to hold the wheel.

* * *

They came for Voss on the fourth day.

Bao watched from the Magpie Jay's rail as two government launches eased up to the customs pier, white hulls, ministry crests freshly painted over where older markings had been sanded away. Men in gray uniforms stood in them without touching their weapons, which was itself a kind of message. Dr. Elias Voss stood on the dock waiting for them, dressed in clothes Suri had bought him from the market stall—clothes that fit him badly because none of them had known how much weight he'd lost in the jungle. He looked smaller than the man who had once held them at gunpoint beside a sabotaged radio. He looked, Bao thought, like a bearing that had finally been allowed to stop spinning.

Paloma walked him down the pier. Bao couldn't hear what she said, but he saw Voss stop, turn, and take her hand in both of his—the way a man takes the hand of someone whose forgiveness he has no right to expect—and then he saw Paloma say something short and shake her head, and Voss almost smiled, and then he was in the launch and gone upstream toward the capital and the hearing rooms where his testimony would be weighed like ore.

Suri appeared at Bao's elbow. She was thinner than when they'd started, the fever having burned something out of her that hadn't grown back, but her eyes were clear in a way that still unsettled him slightly, if he was honest. She looked at the river the way Old Wen used to look at weather charts—with recognition.

"He'll testify," she said.

"He'll try." Bao wiped his hands on a rag that had been clean a month ago. "Governments don't drown witnesses, Suri. They just let the paperwork do it slow."

"You think they'll bury it?"

"I think," Bao said, "that the logbook is in eleven thousand hands now, including three newspapers that hate the Ministry personally. Paper burns. Copies don't." He nodded at the radio shed down the quay, where a young clerk was nailing up a notice. "Come on. That'll be about us."

* * *

It was about them.

The notice was a summons, typed on ministry letterhead, addressed to *Captain P. Fairweather and crew, vessel Magpie Jay*, regarding charges of unauthorized charter operation, theft of government property, and obstruction of an authorized public works survey. A courier delivered it with the flat courtesy of a man delivering a bill, and Paloma read it standing in the wheelhouse doorway with the rain coming down behind her in gray sheets—the monsoon proper now, the real one, the sky wringing itself out over the coast day after day.

"Obstruction of an authorized survey," Paloma read aloud. Her voice was dry as driftwood. "We obstructed them from drowning forty villages. I want that framed."

"They'll want statements," Bao said. "Depositions. They'll pull us apart looking for a version of events they can use."

"Then we give them the true one."

"Suri's testimony is—" He stopped. He didn't know how to say it. How do you write down on a deposition form that the river spoke to your navigator in a fever, that the currents parted for her, that the water chose? The ministry men would smile the way educated men smile at ghosts.

"Is the only testimony that matters," Suri finished quietly. She had come up behind them both, silent as her namesake cat. "Let them call me delirious. Delirious people don't draw channel maps that save patrol boats from sinking. Ask the harbor pilot who checked my chart against his soundings." She almost smiled. "Ask him twice."

* * *

The hearing room in the Port Meridian customs house smelled of mildew and old varnish and too many bodies sheltering from the rain. Bao sat at a long table between Paloma and Suri, facing a panel of five officials behind a raised bench, and he thought, absurdly, of the engine room—of sitting before a bank of gauges, each needle trembling with information, none of them telling you the whole truth alone.

The magistrate in the center was a woman named Corvane, silver-haired, with the patient eyes of someone who had heard a thousand liars and perhaps four honest people. She read the charges aloud. Unauthorized charter. Theft of government property—the logbook, the survey records. Obstruction. Then she set the papers down and looked at Paloma over her spectacles.

"Captain Fairweather. You signed a contract with the Ministry's Office of Surveys. Salvage and rescue of the vessel Kestrel. Under terms of absolute discretion."

"I did."

"And you broke those terms."

"I did."

Corvane waited. The rain filled the silence against the high windows. Bao realized, watching Paloma, that she was doing what she always did at the wheel in bad water—not rushing, not fighting the current, just holding steady and letting the water tell her where the rocks were.

"The contract also required me to report my findings to a security contractor named Renn," Paloma said. "Mr. Renn's orders were to recover the survey records and ensure the discretion clause was enforced. In the course of enforcing it, Mr. Renn shot and killed an unarmed member of my crew. An old man. At the wheel of my boat." Her voice didn't rise. It got quieter, which was worse. "So when I reached the transmitter, madam magistrate, I had a choice. I could hand the truth to the man who killed Old Wen, or I could hand it to everyone. I chose everyone. If that's obstruction, then obstruct me."

The room went very still. Somewhere in the gallery a pen scratched furiously—one of the newspaper people, Bao guessed. There were more of them than there should have been. The broadcast had made the Magpie Jay famous in the way boats become famous: briefly, loudly, and at cost.

Corvane turned a page. "Your engineer will confirm these events."

It wasn't a question, but Bao answered it anyway. He stood, because Old Wen would have stood, and because his knees complained and he ignored them.

"I'll confirm everything," Bao said. "And I'll add what the captain's too proud to say herself. We took that charter because we were broke. We knew it smelled wrong from the first page—we said so on the deck of our own boat. We went anyway because we needed the money, and because we believed, right up until we found the Kestrel empty, that it was a rescue." He looked down the table at the panel, at each face in turn. "I've kept engines running for thirty years, and I know the difference between a machine that fails and a machine that's been failed by the people operating it. Your ministry built a machine for drowning villages and called it a survey. We didn't obstruct it. We shut it down before it killed anyone else. You can charge us for that. But you'll have to charge us honestly."

The pen in the gallery scratched on. Corvane regarded him for a long moment, and something moved behind her eyes—something that might have been, in a different life, in a different country, respect.

"Mr.—"

"Bao. Just Bao."

"Mr. Just Bao." And there—it was brief, quickly suppressed, but the corner of her mouth moved. "Noted."

* * *

The depositions took nine days, and the monsoon took the city apart around them while they gave it. Rain came down the streets in brown rivers; ferries stopped running; the lower quarter flooded ankle-deep and then knee-deep, and Bao spent his evenings back aboard the Magpie Jay pumping bilge and re-packing seals, keeping her alive through the wet season the way you keep any old thing alive—stubbornly, and with more grease than dignity.

The verdicts came in pieces, the way justice always does, arriving not as a wave but as a slow tide.

First: the diversion project formally suspended. Not cancelled—suspended, pending review, the review itself pending funding, the funding pending politics. Bao read the announcement on the wire service and felt the familiar engine-room skepticism. Suspended meant the machine was switched off, not scrapped. But switched off was something. Switched off bought time, and time was the whole game. Voss had scattered his team to buy weeks. The broadcast had bought months. Whether anyone bought years—that was a fight for drier seasons.

Second: the Ministry disavowed Renn. This came with a press release so carefully worded it barely admitted the man had existed—*a contracted security operative acting outside the scope of his engagement*, as though he had been a stray dog nobody owned. No mention of Old Wen. No mention of the bullet. When Bao pointed this out to Paloma, she was quiet for a long moment, looking at the rain.

"They'll never name him," she said. "That's how they apologize. Quietly, and to themselves."

"Then we name him," Bao said. "Every deposition. Every interview. Every time some reporter wants a story about the singing river, we make them print the name of the old man who died at the wheel." He tapped the wheelhouse bulkhead, where Suri had painted Old Wen's name in small gold letters beneath the compass, where a helmsman could see it. "Machines remember their dead, Captain. So do crews."

Third—and this was the piece that surprised everyone—the charges against them were reduced, amended, and partially withdrawn. The theft of government property was struck outright; a ministry auditor, it emerged, had determined that the Kestrel's logbook had been *improperly classified as restricted material contrary to public-record statutes*, which was bureaucratic language for *we cannot prove you stole what legally belonged to the public anyway*. The unauthorized charter remained on the books, a fine assessed, payable in installments. The obstruction charge was converted into something called a *civic disclosure commendation*, which Paloma read three times and then laughed at until she had to sit down.

"A commendation," she wheezed. "For getting Old Wen killed and losing my boat. Frame that next to the obstruction notice. Let history sort out which one is true."

But it was the fourth piece that mattered most, and it arrived not as paper but as a person.

Voss came back downstream on a ministry launch, pale from the hearings, thinner still, carrying a folder of testimony transcripts and a look Bao recognized from mirrors—the look of a man who has been taken apart bolt by bolt and reassembled slightly wrong. He came up the gangway uninvited, and nobody invited him off.

"They adjourned the inquiry," he said, standing dripping in the wheelhouse. "My testimony's entered into the record. All of it. The aquifer, the ecosystem, the diversion timeline. I told them the water is alive and one of the commissioners wrote it down without flinching." He laughed, a cracked sound. "Thirty years I thought discovery meant ownership. Find it, file it, fence it. I sat in that room today and watched five strangers argue over whether a river has legal standing, and I understood—I finally understood—that the question was never whose it is. The question is whether we're the kind of people who ask permission before we break something irreplaceable." He set the folder on the chart table. "The review board reconvenes after the monsoon. They'll want the crew present. Officially, as witnesses." He paused. "Unofficially, I think, as the only people the record can't dismiss."

Paloma looked at the folder, then at Voss, then out at the drowned gray harbor where the Magpie Jay rode her lines, scarred, patched, engine coughing on a borrowed mount.

"We'll be there," she said. "Somebody has to hold the wheel."

* * *

That night the rain slackened for the first time in a week, and Bao climbed topside to check the mooring lines and stayed, because the sky had broken open along the eastern horizon into a long seam of copper light, and the harbor lay flat and gleaming beneath it.

He heard it before he understood it.

A hum. Low, harmonic, rising from somewhere beyond the breakwater—from the open water, from the direction of the Reach, carried impossibly across miles of flood-swollen current. It wasn't machinery. He knew every sound machinery made; he had spent his life learning them. This was older. It moved through the hull plates and up through the soles of his feet and settled somewhere behind his sternum, and it sounded, he thought with the hair rising on his arms, like a chord being held. Like something vast and patient saying *I am still here.*

Suri came up the companionway and stood beside him, and she didn't ask what he was hearing, because she already knew. She tilted her head, listening, and her expression was the one she wore reading channels no chart recorded.

"It carries farther in flood season," she said softly. "More water. More voice."

"Do the others hear it?"

"Paloma hears it. She won't say so." Suri smiled faintly. "Old Wen would have loved it."

Bao looked east, at the copper seam, at the drowned horizon where the caves lay full of singing water, and he thought about engines again—about how the best ones, the ones worth keeping, are the ones that run rough and scarred and refuse, season after season, to quit. The review board would convene. The ministry would regroup. Somewhere in a filing cabinet the diversion plans waited, patient as rust. Nothing was finished. Nothing was safe.

But the river was still singing, and the crew was still aboard, and the truth—copied eleven thousand times over—was loose in the world where paper could burn but copies couldn't.

Below decks, faintly, the Magpie Jay's borrowed engine ticked as it cooled, counting down to nothing in particular. Bao laid his palm flat on the warm housing, the way you'd steady an old friend.

"Hold together," he told her. "We're not done yet."

Out past the breakwater, the horizon hummed its long agreement, and the high water carried the sound downstream—to the delta, to the sea, to whatever came next.
