## 21. The Price Itemized

The confluence where Old Wen wanted to be buried was a place Suri had never thought much about until now—a wide green elbow of water two days downstream of the caves, where the Serpent's Reach folded itself into the Silverbranch and the current ran lazy and braided, like an old man dozing in the sun. Old Wen had loved it. He used to stand at the rail on the downriver runs and point his pipe at the joining of the waters and say, *That is where the river tells itself its own story.* Suri had always assumed it was just something old men said.

Now she stood on the bank with a shovel in her hands and the story was hers to tell.

They had wrapped him in the canvas from the aft awning—the one he'd complained about for six years, the one that leaked in exactly three places, which he'd mapped and memorized and navigated around the way he navigated everything, by long acquaintance rather than argument. Paloma had sewn the seams shut herself, sitting up two nights with Bao's sail needle and a lamp, her hands steady in a way Suri recognized and distrusted, because she'd seen that steadiness before, on the night they'd hidden his death from Renn, and she knew it for what it was: a dam holding back a country of water.

The grave went down easy. The soil at the confluence was dark and soft, river-silted, generous. It seemed wrong, somehow, that the earth should make it easy. Suri had expected the ground to fight them. She had expected everything to fight them, the way everything had fought them since the caves—the current, the government, the rain, the men with guns. But the shovel bit and turned and the hole deepened and the river murmured beside them like it had all the time in the world, and maybe it did. Rivers were patient in a way people were not. Rivers kept their receipts, Paloma always said, but they never seemed in any hurry to collect.

Bao spoke first. He was not good with words on dry land—his eloquence lived in engines, in the grammar of torque and compression—but he had written something out on a scrap of paper in his careful engineer's hand and he read it in a voice that cracked only twice. It was about Old Wen's hands. How they were never still. How he could hold a cup of tea and a chart and a pipe and somehow also be gesturing at the horizon. How Suri had once watched him mend a torn chart with rice paste and strips of paper bag, and the mended chart had outlasted three new ones.

Suri spoke second. She had planned to say something about his cooking, about the way he'd made feast-days out of nothing, out of dried fish and wild ginger and whatever the banks offered up. But when she opened her mouth what came out was the thing she'd been carrying since the fever, since the caves, since the water had poured its long memory into her and not entirely poured it back out.

"He knew the river was listening," she said. "Before any of us. He never said it straight, because he was Old Wen, but he knew. He'd change course sometimes for no reason he'd explain, and later we'd find a snag or a sandbar exactly where he'd turned away from. He was reading it. He'd been reading it his whole life and he never once bragged." Her voice went thin. "I only learned to read it because it read me first. He earned it. That's not fair, but the river doesn't do fair. It does accounts. And his account was good."

Paloma spoke last, and briefly. She took the pipe—recovered from the wheelhouse, still smelling faintly of his tobacco—and buried it in his hands before they closed the canvas. Then she said, "You kept us fed and found and mostly honest, old man. The boat will miss you worse than we will, and we'll miss you past measuring." And that was all, and it was exactly right, and Suri understood that Paloma had spent those two nights of sewing deciding on those two sentences the way she decided on channels through fog.

They filled the grave. Bao cut a marker from a fallen ironwood limb and burned Old Wen's name into it with a soldering iron, the letters slightly crooked because Bao's hands were shaking and none of them pretended otherwise. *WEN. HE READ THE WATER.* Paloma poured a cup of rice wine into the current, and Suri poured a second, and the river took it without comment, the way it took everything, keeping its receipts.

They stood a long time after. Long enough that the light went from gold to copper. Long enough that Suri's knees ached from standing still, which was a strange new ache, because for weeks now her body had only known the aches of motion—running, hauling, hiding, holding on.

It was the birds that finally moved her. A flock of them came down the Silverbranch at dusk, the same white egrets that had gone missing from the upper Reach, that had known to leave before any of them did. They settled in the shallows across from the grave, dozens of them, still as paper lanterns, and Suri felt something in her chest unclench for the first time since the fever broke. The birds had come back. The river was telling itself its story again, and Old Wen was in it now, permanently, at the place he'd chosen.

*That is where the river tells itself its own story.* He'd known he'd end up here. Suri was almost certain of it. Old men who navigated by long acquaintance navigated their own deaths the same way.

* * *

The letter came to the clinic in Suri's mother's name, which was the first strange thing, because nobody wrote to her mother. Her mother ran her clinic the way Old Wen had run his galley—out of stubbornness, generosity, and a permanent state of financial emergency—and the only mail she got was bills.

Suri was there because there was nowhere else to be. The Magpie Jay was moored at the Tallow Street dock under a tarp that flapped like a wounded bird, and every time Suri looked at her she felt the grief stack up another layer, so she'd taken to spending her days at the clinic, folding bandages and mopping floors and pretending to be useful while her mother pretended not to notice that her daughter flinched at the sound of running water.

The letter was thick. Good paper. No return address, only a postmark from a coastal city three hundred miles away and a bank's seal in the corner.

Her mother read it standing in the doorway, and Suri watched her mother's face do something it almost never did: go still with disbelief. Then her mother sat down on the patient's bench, which she never did, because there was always someone who needed the bench, and read it again.

"Three hundred thousand," she said. "In trust. Endowed. The interest alone covers the lease, the supplies, two more staff." She looked up. "There's a condition. Or—no. Not a condition. A note."

"Read it."

Her mother held the paper out, and her hand wasn't steady, and Suri took it and read the note aloud because her mother's voice had gone somewhere and couldn't come back.

*For the clinic of Amara Seng, who mends what the river breaks. No acknowledgment is necessary or desired. The donor owes this debt to someone who is no longer in a position to collect it himself, and asks only that it be paid forward in his manner: quietly, and to everyone.*

There was no signature. There was a small drawing in the corner of the note, inked with a fine, precise hand—the kind of hand that had spent decades signing geological surveys and government filings. It was a bird. An egret, standing in shallows, drawn in maybe nine lines.

Suri read the note three times. The paper trembled. Around her the clinic went on—her mother's assistant grinding herbs, a child crying over a splinted arm, the ceiling fan ticking through its worn arc—and she stood in the middle of it with a fortune in her hands and felt the shape of a man she'd known for eleven days and hated for six of them.

Voss. It had to be Voss. The precision of it, the anonymity worn like armor, the guilt converted into endowment because guilt was the only currency he had left in quantity. He had signed off on a plan to drown a hundred settlements, and then he'd seen what the plan would drown, and then he'd scattered his own team and set a river against a government, and now he was paying out the rest of his life in installments to strangers. She thought of him in the fog-dark passage through the rock throat, hands on the line beside Bao, hauling when his arms had to be screaming, saying nothing, owing everything.

"He can't even let us thank him," her mother said, half laughing, half crying. "Who does that? Who sends three hundred thousand and asks to be *forgotten*?"

"Someone keeping books," Suri said. "Someone who knows what he owes and to whom, and knows he can never square it, so he just—keeps paying."

Her mother was already moving, already alive in the way she only got when there was work to plan—pulling out the lease papers, muttering about the back room, about a second cot, about the well pump that had been dying for two years. The clinic would live. Her mother's hands would keep mending what the river broke, and somewhere a hollow man would hear about it, or not, and it would not be enough and it would have to be enough, because that was how accounts like his worked.

Suri folded the note along its careful creases and put it in her shirt pocket, over her heart, where the fever's hum still lived sometimes, faint as a struck bell fading. She suspected she would carry it there for a long time. Some debts you don't spend. Some you just keep, to remember what things cost.

* * *

The engine was worse than Bao had said, and Bao was an optimist about machines and a pessimist about everything else, which meant when he said *worse* he meant catastrophic.

Suri stood in the engine room with him three days after the burial, holding the lamp while he lay half inside the Magpie Jay's heart with a wrench and a vocabulary that would have made Old Wen proud. The monsoon's early arrival had drowned the lower cylinders during the escape. Salt water in the block. A cracked head. The main bearing scored like a record nobody should ever play again.

"New engine," Bao said, sliding out, grease to his elbows, hair flattened with sweat. "Not repair. Not patch. New. The block's done. I could rebuild it and we'd get maybe a season, and then she'd quit us somewhere with no current and no shame."

"Cost?"

He told her. Suri sat down on the tool chest and did the math she'd been avoiding for a week. The charter money from the government was gone—frozen, seized, *evidence*, every coin of it. The reward Paloma had refused on principle from three different newspapers. Paloma's own savings, which had gone into the legal fees that were, so far, holding the charges at bay. What was left in the boat's tin box wouldn't cover a used engine, let alone a good one, and the Magpie Jay without a good engine was a very expensive piece of driftwood.

"We could charter her as a houseboat," Bao said, without conviction. "Tourists. They like the lower Reach. It's safe."

"The lower Reach is boring."

"The lower Reach is *alive*, Suri. I've developed opinions about that."

She almost laughed. It came out as something else, something between a breath and a sob, and Bao looked at her with the helpless tenderness of a man who could fix anything with a flame and a wrench except this. He sat down next to her on the tool chest, which groaned, and they sat together in the smell of oil and bilgewater, in the belly of the boat that had carried them through everything and now lay broken in the shallows of their luck.

"Old Wen would have a plan," she said.

"Old Wen would have a plan and a pot of tea and he'd have made us drink the tea before he told us the plan, and the tea would be terrible and the plan would be insane and it would work." Bao rubbed his face. "Paloma has a plan. She won't say it yet. She's waiting for something."

"For what?"

"For us to ask. She thinks we've paid enough. She's wrong, but she's polite about it, which for Paloma is practically a religious experience." He stood, offered his hand, pulled her up. "Come on. I'll show you what I *can* fix. It's a short list. It'll be good for your humility."

They climbed out into the evening. The dock lamps were coming on down Tallow Street, and the river ran past them, high with the monsoon's first real swell, brown and muscular and utterly indifferent. Suri put her hand on the rail. Under her palm, through the wood, she felt it—that hum, that harmonic, faint as memory. The Reach singing what it knew. She was the only one of them who could hear it from the surface, and some days she wished she couldn't, and most days she was glad, and she had stopped trying to sort out which kind of day this was.

Paloma was waiting on deck with the tin box open on her knee and three cups of terrible tea already poured.

"Sit down, both of you," she said. "I've been doing the accounts."

Suri sat. The tea was, as prophesied, terrible. The river sang. And Paloma Fairweather, who had lost her boat's engine, her savings, her clean record, and her cook, and had somehow kept her crew, looked at the two of them over the steam and said the last thing Suri expected:

"I know where there's an engine. And I know exactly how much it's going to cost us to take it—and it isn't money."
