## 1. What the River Keeps

The river had been lying about its depth all week, and Freya Amberdale was the only one on the water who knew it.

She read the lie the way other people read weather. A smooth brown surface where the current should have been braiding white meant a submerged gravel bar. A glassy tongue sliding along the north bank meant the flood had carved a new channel overnight and hadn't bothered to tell anyone. The Vessany border river—officially the Serle, though nobody who worked it called it anything but the Line—was running three meters over its banks and moving like something with an opinion, and every ferryman between Torv Hollow and the delta had tied off and gone home to drink.

Freya had not gone home. Home was a word she'd stopped using around the same time two governments stopped letting her use their maps.

Instead she poled a flat-bottomed skiff through drowned willow country with a paying passenger in the bow—a wool merchant named Daskin who wanted to cross from Vessany into the free territories without appearing on any manifest—and she did it at flood stage because that was when the patrols pulled back to the high ground and the river became, briefly, hers.

"Shouldn't we be dead by now?" Daskin asked. He was gripping the gunwale with both hands, which she appreciated. Passengers who respected the river lived longer.

"The river doesn't kill you," Freya said, walking the pole through soft bottom and finding the hard seam beneath it, the old roadbed that ran under the floodwater like a spine. "The river just shows you what you actually know. Most people find out it's nothing."

"You're cheerful."

"I'm expensive."

She swung the skiff wide around a fence post standing alone in what had been a pasture that morning, reading the eddy lines behind it, and put them into the slack water on the far side of the drowned orchard. This was the part of the work she still loved, if she was honest—the part where her hands knew things before her head did, where the country forgave competence even when it didn't forgive anything else. She'd been banned from guiding in Vessany for six years and from the Karstian marches for four before that, and neither ban had ever managed to reach her hands. Her hands still knew the water. That was the insult of it. You could erase a woman's name from the registries, but you couldn't erase the ten thousand small decisions her body made without asking her.

Daskin paid her at the willow stand on the free-territory side, counting out coin with wet fingers, and then he was gone up the bank and she was alone, which was how she preferred the world arranged.

She should have turned back immediately. Flood stage was no time to linger; the river changed its mind hourly and punished sentiment. But the light was doing something strange on the water below the orchard—a slow revolving gleam, bronze-colored, too regular to be a log turning—and Freya found herself poling down to look, telling herself it might be salvage worth money, which it sometimes was, and not admitting that she never could leave a thing half-surfaced and unexamined. Curiosity was the flaw that had built her whole life, and every government that had ever banned her had identified it correctly.

It wasn't a log.

It was a chest, maybe sixty centimeters long, oak banded in bronze, tumbling end over end in the slow water behind a snag of root. It rode low but it wasn't sinking—the construction was too good for that—and as Freya brought the skiff alongside and got her gaff hook into the banding, she saw the stamp.

A mountain range above a kestrel in flight, wings spread, talons forward.

Her breath went out of her all at once, the way air leaves a room when a door slams somewhere far away.

She knew that crest. Not from history books, though the books had it too, printed gray and apologetic in the chapters about nations that used to exist. She knew it from a letter. One letter, the last one, written in her father's cramped leftward-leaning hand on paper that had traveled so far to reach her that the ink had been smudged by rain in three countries. *They'll tell you I was never anywhere worth being,* he'd written. *But look for the bird over the mountains, little wren. Everything true is stamped with the bird.*

She'd been nineteen. She'd looked for the bird for eleven years, in archives and pawnshops and the margins of smugglers' charts, and she had found exactly nothing, and eventually she had done what sensible people do with grief that won't resolve: she'd buried it under work and kept the shovel close.

Now the bird was turning slowly in the floodwater six feet from her boots, attached to a chest that the Serle had decided, after forty years, to give back.

*Everything true is stamped with the bird.*

"Damn you," she said quietly, to her father, to the river, to whichever of them was responsible. Then she hauled.

The chest came aboard heavier than it should have, and the skiff wallowed, and Freya spent a bad thirty seconds bailing and leaning against the current before she got her balance and her breathing back. Up close the bronze was green with age except where the stamp sat proud of the corrosion, protected by its own relief, sharp as the day some Karstian smith had struck it. There were letters around the rim. She tilted it toward the gray light: *VESPER KESTREL.* A ship's name. An airship's name—she recognized the registry format from her father's other papers, the ones the Vessany constabulary had let her keep because they thought they were sentimental value only.

An airship that had been missing for forty years, sending its strongbox down a border river at flood stage. Which meant the wreck it came from was upstream. In the mountains. In the high glacial lakes, where the ice had been retreating for a decade like a tide going out and leaving everything it had swallowed standing naked in the light.

And she was not the only person in these borderlands who could do that arithmetic.

She heard the patrol before she saw it—the flat slap of an outboard running quiet, the particular rhythm of men who were searching rather than traveling. Flood season brought them out anyway; floods loosened things loose people shouldn't have, and the Vessany Border Service ran sweep patterns down the Line every rising water, scooping up contraband, corpses, and guides working illegal crossings. Freya had been scooped once, early in her career, and had spent nine months learning exactly how patient a bureaucracy could be.

She killed her own engine—if a paddle and a pole could be called an engine—and slid the skiff into the willow tunnel along the bank, pulling the chest down flat in the bilge and throwing her oilskin over it. Through the leaves she watched the patrol boat come down the channel she'd just left: gray hull, four men, a floodlight sweeping the water with the bored thoroughness of people being paid by the hour. One of them was using a grappling rake, dragging the shallows. Looking for something.

Or looking for whatever else the river gave up.

The boat slowed near the snag of root. The man with the rake leaned out.

Freya did the math without deciding to. Current, distance, the bend downstream, the willow tunnel's depth of cover, the fact that her passenger route was blown for the day regardless. Then she backed water, slow and silent, one blade at a time, easing the skiff deeper into the flooded timber while the rake splashed forty meters behind her. It took everything she had to move that slowly. Every nerve said run. Running was noise, and noise on floodwater carried like a shot.

The patrol boat hung at the snag for a long moment. Then the outboard coughed back to life and it moved on downstream, and Freya sat in the dark under the willows with her heart going like a drum and the chest under her oilskin radiating cold through the planks like a held secret.

She gave it twenty minutes. Then she came out of the timber, crossed the Line going the wrong direction—back into Vessany, because the free territories had patrols too and theirs were better paid—and ran the drowned back-channels north with the skill that had made her famous and the illegality that had made her infamous, both deployed at once, until the light went copper and the first ridge of the Vergha range stood black against it.

There was a river town called Sennet Hallow forty years ago. Now there was a fuel dock, a gear broker who owed her, and eleven houses that pretended not to see anything, clustered on a bluff above a cutoff oxbow where the patrols didn't bother sweeping because officially nothing was there.

She beached the skiff in the reeds below the bluff as full dark came down, and stood a moment in the cold with the chest in her arms, feeling its weight settle against her ribs.

Forty years. Her father had been gone thirty-one of them—disappeared into the marches on an expedition whose permits had been revoked retroactively, whose sponsors had disowned him, whose name had been scrubbed from the guild rolls with a thoroughness that had taken Freya's own future down with it, because the daughter of a disgraced guide inherits the disgrace like a birthmark. *Amberdale* was a word that got applications returned unopened in two countries. She'd built her whole crooked, competent life in the gaps that word left behind.

And now the river had coughed up his crest, his ship's name, his handwriting waiting somewhere inside this box like a voice held behind a door for three decades.

She could feel the old grief cracking open along its seams, and underneath it something worse and more alive: not sadness. Hunger. Eleven years of looking, collapsing into one night of having found, and the found thing heavy in her arms and the whole machinery of two governments presumably already turning toward it, because rivers deliver things to everyone equally and salvage law in Vessany said a chest bearing a foreign state seal belonged to the Crown, and the Crown paid informants well.

Somewhere upstream, in the thin cold air of the high lakes, the rest of the Vesper Kestrel was emerging from the ice. And Freya understood, with the same certainty she felt for current and load and weather, that whoever reached it first would own the truth of what had happened to Karstia—and to her father—and that she was currently the only person alive who knew the wreck existed, carrying the only proof of it, standing in the reeds with patrol lanterns already moving along the far bank like a slow fire coming down the Line.

She shifted the chest higher, found the trail above the bluff by memory, and started climbing.

Behind her, the river kept the rest.

* * *

Sennet Hallow's gear broker was a woman named Pell who traded in equipment that had fallen off the backs of legitimate expeditions, and she opened her door at midnight holding a lamp and wearing the expression of someone who had been expecting trouble and was disappointed only by the shape of it.

"No," Pell said.

"I haven't asked anything yet."

"You're wet, you're alone, you came up the bluff trail instead of the road, and you've got a look. The last time you had that look, I lost my best client and gained a year of nightmares." Pell stepped back anyway, because that was the arrangement between them: refusal first, always, and then the door. "What is it?"

Freya set the chest on the tarpaulin-covered table and folded back the oilskin.

Pell went very still. People who dealt in gray-market gear developed a professional stillness around objects that could get them killed, and Freya watched her arrive at the correct assessment in about two seconds flat. "That's a state seal."

"That's a dead state seal."

"Worse." Pell crouched, examined the stamp without touching it, and read the letters around the rim. When she straightened, her face had gone carefully blank, which from Pell was louder than shouting. "Karstia. My grandmother used to sing songs about Karstia. Songs about a country that isn't on maps anymore don't get sung unless somebody's grandmother came from there." She wiped her palms on her apron. "Where did this come from?"

"The river."

"The river doesn't have opinions about dead nations."

"It does during flood season." Freya told her—the short version, the version with no names in it except her father's, and she hesitated even over that, because saying *Aldric Amberdale* aloud in a room with a witness was a decision, and she made it deliberately, watching it land. Pell's eyebrows went up and stayed there.

"Aldric Amberdale," Pell repeated. "The Aldric Amberdale? The one they erased?"

"He was just my father. The erasure was other people's project." Freya worked the bronze bands. They were locked, of course—good locks, old locks, locks built by people who expected the contents to matter—but the hasp had been stressed by forty years of water and one collision with a snag, and Freya had been breaking into things since she was twelve. It gave with a crack that sounded enormous in the small room.

Inside, packed in tarred wool gone stiff as bark: a logbook fragment, water-stained, its cover stamped with the same kestrel-over-mountains. A coil of wire. And a slip of oilcloth wrapped around something flat and rectangular that clinked.

She unwrapped it. A key. Brass, long-shanked, stamped with a number: *7.*

Beneath the key, folded twice, a page torn from a larger document. She smoothed it under the lamp. Coordinates, in latitude and longitude precise enough to make her mouth go dry, and beside them, in her father's hand—the leftward lean, the hard downstroke she'd know blind—the words:

*If the water gives this back, the ice has given up the ship. Come before the fleet does. Bring nothing you can't carry. —A.A.*

Freya read it three times. Around her the room stayed quiet, and outside, far off across the dark valley, she could hear the patrol boats working the Line, their engines rising and falling like something breathing.

"He knew," she said. "He knew the glacier would retreat. Forty years ago, he knew."

"Then he also knew someone would come looking," Pell said. "People who hide things that carefully expect company." She glanced toward the shuttered window, toward the sound of engines. "Question is whether you're the company he wanted, or the kind that comes with contracts and salvage rights and a corporate charter."

Freya looked down at the coordinates. High glacial lakes. Six days' hard travel from here, five if the passes were open, longer if the passes were watched—and the passes were always watched now, since the incident, since her name became a warning label pinned to whole ranges of map.

The engines across the valley rose, fell, rose again. Closer. Working the oxbow now, maybe. Working the bluff.

"Pell. How fast can you make me disappear from here?"

"How fast can you stop wanting to?" Pell asked, but she was already pulling gear lists from under the table, because that was the arrangement between them too. "Because I can hide the chest. I can't hide you. And Freya—that stamp is going to bring more than patrols. Things like that bring fleets."

Outside, the first engine cut to idle directly below the bluff, and a lantern beam began to climb the trail she'd come up.

Freya closed her hand around the brass key until the teeth bit her palm, and reached for her pack.
