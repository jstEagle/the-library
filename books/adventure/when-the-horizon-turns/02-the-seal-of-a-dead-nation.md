## 2. The Seal of a Dead Nation

The town was called Lowmark, which was a joke, because the river had drowned it twice and would do it again before the decade turned. Freya reached it an hour past dark, soaked to the sternum, the chest strapped to her back like a second spine. She came in from the reed flats instead of the road, because roads had eyes and she'd spent three years learning to move like something the maps had forgotten.

The ferryman's sister kept rooms above her net-drying shed. No name on the door, no questions at the stair. Freya paid in the old way—coin slid under a jar of pickled eels—and let herself into a room that smelled of tar, wet wool, and someone else's tobacco. One window, shuttered. One bed with a straw tick that had opinions. Good enough. She'd slept in worse for better reasons.

She set the chest on the floor and didn't touch it for a long moment.

That was the discipline her father had taught her, back when he was still teaching her anything: *the moment after the find is the most dangerous moment, Freya. Your hands want to know before your head does. Make them wait.*

She made them wait. She lit the stub of candle she carried, checked the shutter's seams, wedged a chair under the door handle, and only then knelt and looked at the thing she had carried eleven kilometers through flood country with patrols sweeping the banks behind her.

Bronze. Salt-scabbed. The size of a sea chest built for a child, or a man with very few belongings. And stamped into the lid, deep enough to survive forty years underwater and a river's temper:

A kestrel, wings folded, falling. Encircled by a broken crown.

The seal of Karstia.

Her breath went out of her slowly, the way air leaves a dry suit when the seal fails. She'd seen the crest twice in her life. Once in a history primer, in the chapter titled *The Border Adjustments*, four paragraphs long, ending with the sentence *Karstia ceased to exist as a political entity in the year—* and then the year, which she'd forgotten, because who memorizes the death date of a country?

And once in ink, on paper, in her father's hand.

She went to her pack with hands that were not entirely steady and drew out the oilskin wallet she had carried since she was nineteen years old. Inside: a climbing permit, expired. A letter of reference from a man who had since testified against her. And the last letter her father had ever sent her, postmarked from a town that no longer appeared on any map of Vessany.

She'd read it so many times the creases had gone soft as cloth. She didn't need to open it to see the crest at the bottom—the kestrel falling, the broken crown—doodled small, almost shy, in the corner where he'd always signed off with a sketch instead of a name. *A habit of his,* her mother had said. *A joke between old friends.* Her mother had never explained what friends.

Freya laid the letter beside the chest and looked at the two crests until the candle guttered and she had to trim the wick with her thumbnail.

*Same stamp. Same die, maybe. The same hand that pressed this bronze pressed that ink.*

The river hadn't coughed up a curiosity. The river had coughed up a message, and it was addressed to her.

* * *

She opened the chest with a pry bar and more care than she'd ever shown a lock.

The hasp was fused with corrosion, but the chest itself had been built to last—Karstian work, if the primer was to be believed, and everything else in the room was starting to say it was. Under the lid, sealed under a layer of pitch that flaked away like old skin: oilcloth bundles. Inside the oilcloth, wrapped in a wool blanket that had gone stiff with forty years of river, a book.

Not a whole book. A fragment—maybe thirty leaves, water-ravaged at the edges, the spine snapped, the cover long gone. But the interior pages had been written in pencil, and pencil, her father had told her once, is the only honest ink; it doesn't run, it just waits. She could read most of it by candlelight if she tilted the pages and let the shadows do the work.

The handwriting was not her father's. It was tighter, more official, a ship's hand.

*Third day running the lake circuit. Ice coming down off the Vergha earlier than the charts say. Captain wants the cargo moved below the waterline—says it's too true to float.*

Freya read the sentence three times.

*Too true to float.*

She turned the page. More of the same—a ship's log, terse, professional, the voice of a man doing a job he did not entirely like. Dates that meant nothing to her and then, suddenly, meant everything: the year was the last year of Karstia. The month was the month the primer said the borders were "adjusted."

*Passengers quiet. Too quiet. They know what we're carrying and they don't like the company it keeps. Officer Amberdale says we should have burned it in the capital and been done.*

Freya stopped breathing.

She read the name again. *Officer Amberdale.* Not a common name. Not a name you stumbled over twice in one lifetime unless the stumbling was arranged.

Her father's first name had been Anselm. A. Amberdale. She told herself it was coincidence, that Amberdale was a valley name, a common as dirt name in the border ranges, that half the shepherds east of the Vergha were Amberdales of one stripe or another. She told herself this while her hands shook hard enough that she had to set the page down on her knee.

The log went on. The entries grew shorter as the pages grew fewer, as though the writer were running out of either time or the will to record. The last legible entry was four lines:

*Anchored in the high lake below the Vergha seracs. Captain's orders. We scuttle her at dawn, deep water, north basin. The Kestrel goes down with her truth and may God forgive us for the depth we're burying it at.*

*The Vesper Kestrel.*

Freya sat back on her heels in the tar-smelling room in a drowned town at the edge of a country that had erased her, and she understood, with the clean cold clarity that came to her sometimes on a mountain when the weather turned and there was nothing left to do but act, that her entire life had been standing in the shadow of a sentence she had never been allowed to read.

Her father hadn't been a passenger on that ship. He hadn't been a victim of it. He'd been *crew*.

* * *

She worked through the rest of the chest by the discipline of not thinking, which was a discipline she was very good at.

Under the logbook: a brass sextant, Karstian make, seized solid. A tin of pencils gone to soft lead mush. And at the bottom, flat as a pressed flower, a sheet of heavy paper folded in eighths, the folds rotted through at the creases.

She opened it flat on the floorboards.

It was a chart. Hand-drawn, the surveyor's hand confident and quick—the same hand, she realized, that had sketched the kestrel in the corner of her father's letters. The high lakes below the Vergha seracs, rendered in ink that had held its line for forty years. And in the north basin, where the logbook said the Kestrel went down, a single set of coordinates, boxed twice, with a notation beneath in her father's handwriting—she knew it the way she knew her own pulse, from the margins of a hundred childhood lessons:

*42 fathoms off the old shelf. She'll keep. She'll keep better than we will.*

Freya read that line until the candle burned low enough that the letters started to swim.

*She'll keep better than we will.*

Her father had known. Standing on the deck of a scuttling airship in a lake that didn't officially exist, he had known that the truth would outlive the men who buried it, and he had left coordinates anyway—left a door open, a thread trailing, the way a man leaves a lamp burning in a window for someone he isn't sure is coming home. Maybe he'd meant it for himself. Maybe he'd meant it for whoever came asking after him.

Maybe he'd meant it for her.

She folded the chart along its ruined creases, and her fingers found, in the corner of the last panel, something she'd missed: a small inked mark, half worn away. She tilted it to the candle.

The kestrel, falling. The broken crown. And beneath it, so small she had to hold the paper an inch from her eye: a single word in Karstian, a language she did not speak but whose alphabet she had once traced over and over as a child, copying her father's letters because she liked the shapes.

She sounded it out the way he'd taught her, syllable by stubborn syllable.

*Vrathen.* Debt. She only knew the word because he'd used it once, drunk on plum brandy on a porch in a valley that had since closed to her, saying that everything in the borderlands came down to *vrathen*—who owed whom, and how the interest compounded.

The past, she thought, had stopped being abstract somewhere back on the riverbank. Now it had presented an invoice.

* * *

She was folding the chart away when the paper slipped, and something else came loose from the bottom of the chest—a second sheet, thinner, cheaper, the kind of paper a printing house ran off by the thousand.

She almost didn't look at it. She was tired, and the candle was dying, and part of her wanted to sleep for a year before she thought another thought about Karstia or kestrels or the man whose name was in a dead ship's log.

But she was a professional. Professionals look at everything. She smoothed the sheet flat against her knee.

It was a bounty notice. Fresh—the paper hadn't even yellowed, the ink still sharp, printed within the last season, maybe the last month.

A woodcut portrait filled the top half. It was a good likeness, which was the worst part. The artist had caught the set of her jaw, the scar through her left eyebrow from the Serac Point job, the look she'd been told she had in the mountains—the look of a woman assessing the fastest way down. Below the portrait, in block letters:

*FREYA AMBERDALE. WANTED FOR QUESTIONING. GUIDING VIOLATIONS, BORDER INFRACTIONS, ASSOCIATION WITH FORBIDDEN ROUTES. REWARD: 400 VESSANY CROWNS. LAST SEEN: RIVER PROVINCE. APPROACH WITH CAUTION. SUBJECT IS AN EXPERIENCED MOUNTAINEER.*

Four hundred crowns. She did the arithmetic without wanting to. Four hundred crowns was a season's wages for a dockhand. It was a new boat for a fisherman. It was, for a family in a flood country like this one, with the river rising and the monsoon coming, the difference between a bad year and a survivable one.

She was worth a survivable year to almost anyone who laid eyes on her.

And beneath the official text, in smaller print, the line that made her go cold in a way the river hadn't managed:

*Subject believed to be in possession of materials of interest to the Crown Recovery Office. Any materials recovered with the subject to be surrendered intact. Reward doubled for intact materials.*

*Materials of interest.* The Crown Recovery Office. She turned the phrase over. She'd never heard of it, which meant it was new, which meant it had been created recently, which meant someone in Vessany had recently decided that forty-year-old Karstian materials were worth a bureaucracy and a doubled bounty.

Someone knew the Kestrel was surfacing.

She thought about the flood, the river coughing the chest loose at exactly the reach she happened to be guiding through. She thought about the patrol boats sweeping the banks within the hour, fast, coordinated, as though they'd been told to look for something specific. She thought about the ferryman's sister taking her coin without a flicker, and wondered what a room above a net shed cost when the buyer was asking the right questions.

The glacier was retreating. She knew that—every guide on the border knew it; the high lakes had been rising and shifting for a decade, eating new shoreline, letting go of old secrets. If the Vesper Kestrel had been buried at forty-two fathoms under the north basin, and the ice above it had spent forty years melting back like a blanket being pulled away—

Then the wreck was rising. Or the water over it was thinning. Or the shelf it rested on was about to slide into the light.

And someone—some office with a crown on its letterhead—had done the same arithmetic she just had, and gotten there first, and printed her face on a sheet of paper to make sure she didn't get there at all.

Freya sat in the dark with the bounty notice in her hands and felt something settle in her chest that wasn't fear. Fear was for things you couldn't name. This had a name now. It had coordinates. It had her father's handwriting on it and a ship's log calling him *Officer* and a line—*she'll keep better than we will*—that read, tonight, in a drowned town, like a message posted forty years ago and delivered just now, by floodwater, to the one person left alive with the name to claim it.

*They doubled the reward for the materials,* she thought. *Which means they think I have them. Which means they know what the chest was. Which means the only thing standing between the Crown Recovery Office and my father's last secret is a woman worth four hundred crowns dead or eight hundred intact.*

She blew out the candle.

In the dark, the river worked at the town's pilings, patient as interest accruing, and Freya Amberdale sat on a stranger's bed and made a list, because lists were how she kept her hands from shaking.

One: the log fragment and the chart could not stay in the chest. The chest went to the bottom of the reed flats before dawn, weighted, recoverable, deniable. The papers went into the oilskin wallet, against her skin, where a search would have to get personal to find them.

Two: she could not go north alone. The high passes above the Vergha were watched—had been watched since the incident, since the trial, since two governments had agreed with unusual speed that Freya Amberdale should never again stand on a glacier. She'd need a boat, a route, and gear, and there was exactly one man on this stretch of river who would sell her any of the three, because he was the only one whose hatred of governments outweighed his hatred of her.

Ivo Renk. Outfitter, smuggler, and the last man alive who'd climbed with her father.

Three: she had six tides, maybe, before the monsoon closed the high country and the Crown Recovery Office closed everything else.

She lay back on the straw tick and did not sleep. Above the shutter, through the gaps in the boards, she could see the first gray suggestion of dawn, and she thought about her father's handwriting, steady even at the end, even on a sinking ship: *she'll keep better than we will.*

"Well," Freya said to the dark, to the river, to the man who had left her a debt instead of an inheritance, "let's find out."

At first light she would go and find Ivo Renk, and she would ask him for the one thing in the world he had sworn never to give her: a way back up the mountain.

The river, meanwhile, kept everything else.
