## 3. A Guide Nobody Hires

The rain found Ivo Renk's outfitter shop the way it found everything in Drenmark—sideways, with intent, and without apology.

Freya stood under the sagging awning across the lane for a full minute before crossing. Not because she was afraid of Ivo. Ivo had never once turned her in, never once overcharged her, never once asked a question he didn't need the answer to. That was the whole of their friendship, and it had held for six years, which was longer than most marriages and considerably longer than her relationship with any border official in two countries.

She was waiting because the shop's window told her something was wrong.

Ivo's window was a ledger. He arranged it the same way every season—ropes coiled left, pitons and hardware center, the expensive stuff at eye level where the wealthy guides with clean papers would see it and buy without haggling. It was a language, and Freya had read it since she was nineteen years old, standing on a crate to reorganize it while her father tallied accounts in the back.

The window was wrong.

The hardware center was half empty. Not sold-out empty—*picked-over* empty, the way a wall looks when one buyer has taken the best of everything in a single transaction. And where the expedition-grade dry bags usually hung, there was nothing at all but hooks and the pale rectangles of paint where the sun hadn't reached.

She crossed the lane and pushed through the door. The bell above it gave its usual broken half-ring, the clapper long gone, and the smell hit her the way it always did: hemp, wax, gun oil, and the particular staleness of canvas that had been folded wet one too many times.

Ivo was behind the counter, doing nothing. That was wrong too. Ivo Renk at sixty-one did nothing the way a dam holds water—there was always pressure behind the stillness. Today he was just standing there with a cloth in his hand, wiping down a carabiner that was already clean.

"Freya Amberdale." He didn't look up. "I was wondering when the river would spit you out."

"You heard."

"Everyone heard. Chest came out of the flood, girl with a dead nation's seal on her back ran the customs line at Vell's Crossing like the hounds of the apocalypse were behind her." He set the carabiner down. "Half of that is true, I assume."

"Which half?"

"The running." He looked at her then, and she saw it in his face—not suspicion, not fear, but something worse. Something that had already done arithmetic. "You're wet, you're thin, and you're standing in my shop three days after the biggest salvage rumor since the Kestrel was a going concern. What do you need?"

"Gear. Full kit. Cold-water dive if you've got it, and I know you've got it because I sold you half of it."

"I don't."

The word landed flat. Freya blinked. "You don't *what*?"

"Don't have it. Don't have most of it." He jerked his chin toward the window. "You saw. Man came through nine days ago. Paid cash. Bought everything—dry suits, regulators, the good line, the compression bags, two of my sonar units, and every boat north of the ferry dock that floats."

"Every boat."

"Every boat. Chartered, not bought—his people are still here, still eating in my sister's tavern, still asking questions with a Vessany accent that learned its vowels in a corporate office." Ivo picked up the carabiner again, wiped it, put it down. "Halvern Deep Recovery. Ever hear of them?"

The name went through her like cold water through a torn sleeve.

Halvern. She'd heard it in fragments over the years—a salvage concern, well capitalized, well connected, the kind of operation that showed up after disasters and left with the valuable parts. She'd never had reason to care. Now the chest in her hiding place upriver felt suddenly very far away, and very loud.

"Why would a deep recovery fleet care about a river town on the Vessany border?"

"Because three weeks ago a glacier calved into the upper lakes and something forty years underwater decided to stick its nose into the air." Ivo's voice was carefully, professionally bored, the voice he used when the information was worth more than the conversation. "And because whatever's down there has a Karstian seal on it, and there are people in this world who pay very well for dead nations' secrets."

Freya kept her face still. Six years of being a name nobody hired had taught her that. But inside, the arithmetic was running now too, and it was running fast.

Six days ago she'd pulled a chest out of the flood. Nine days ago Halvern had stripped this shop. Which meant they hadn't come for the chest. They'd come for the wreck—and the chest was a loose end they'd only recently learned existed.

"They're looking for it," she said. "The chest."

"They're looking for whoever has it." Ivo set the cloth down and folded his arms. "There's a notice. Went up in the tavern two nights ago."

"Show me."

He hesitated—actually hesitated, which he never did—and then reached under the counter and produced a folded paper, sliding it across without letting go of one corner, like a man feeding a hand into a trap to see if it snaps.

She took it.

Her own face looked back at her. Not a photograph—a sketch, but a good one, the kind done by someone who'd worked from a description and had a talent for it. The jaw, the wind-burned cheekbones, the hair tied back for a climb. *FREYA AMBERDALE. WANTED FOR QUESTIONING REGARDING RECOVERED PROPERTY OF UNCLEAR PROVENANCE. REWARD NEGOTIABLE. INQUIRE HALVERN DEEP RECOVERY, FERRY DOCK OFFICE.*

"Questioning," she read aloud.

"That's the polite version. The tavern version is that Halvern's lead diver has been walking the docks asking after a woman traveling alone with river mud to her knees." Ivo's eyes came up. "A diver, Freya. A good one, by reputation. Name of Marun."

The name meant nothing to her. The *title* did. Halvern hadn't sent an accountant to find her. They'd sent someone who could read a riverbank the way she could, and that was a different order of problem.

"They think I'm still in the valley."

"They think you're smart, which is worse. Smart means you went to ground, and going to ground means supplies, and supplies means outfitters." He spread his hands. "I've had three visits. Three. Men with clean boots asking have I seen you, would I tell them if I had, and wouldn't it be a shame if my import licenses came under review."

"What did you tell them?"

"That you came through in the spring and stole a tent." The ghost of a smile. "They wrote it down. People believe what confirms what they already think of you."

Freya set the notice on the counter and looked at it for a long moment. Her face. Her father's jaw. The reward negotiable, which meant they didn't know what the chest was worth, which meant they didn't know what was *in* it, which meant she still had the only advantage that mattered: she knew things they didn't.

But they had boats. They had sonar. They had a fleet, and she had a chest hidden under a fallen spruce forty miles upriver and no way to reach it that didn't end at a dock.

"I need a boat," she said.

"You need a miracle with an outboard." Ivo came around the counter and stood at the window beside her, looking out at the rain. "Every hull that draws less than a meter is under Halvern's charter until monsoon season ends the season. They've booked the ferry, the barges, the fishing skiffs, the *tour boats*, Freya. There's a man at the ferry dock whose whole job is to write down the name of every person who asks about going north."

"And when does their charter end?"

Ivo was quiet for a moment. When he answered, his voice had dropped into the register he used for bad news delivered honestly.

"They gave themselves a clock. Six tides before the monsoon makes landfall—that's their window, their own survey says the lakes turn to soup after that, visibility zero, salvage impossible. So they sail on the sixth tide, they work the wreck while the weather holds, and they're gone before the first real storm." He turned to look at her. "Which means you have six tides to get upriver, get to that lake, and get to whatever's in it before a fleet with corporate lawyers and a navy surplus tender does."

The rain drummed the awning. Somewhere down the lane, the tavern door opened and closed, and voices spilled out and were swallowed again.

Six tides.

She'd crossed the Vergha Pass in three days with a broken rib. She'd free-climbed the Sere Wall in weather that killed two better-equipped climbers. But those had been races against stone and weather, and stone and weather didn't post notices with your face on them.

"Who's left?" she asked. "Who's not chartered, not licensed, not afraid?"

Ivo laughed—a single dry note, no humor in it. "You know who's left. You've always known who's left." He held up one finger. "Osk."

"Osk is a smuggler."

"Osk is a *flexible* mariner with an unlicensed hull, no charter, no papers, and a profound indifference to whose seal is on whose cargo. Which describes, if I'm being honest, most of your social circle." He leaned on the counter. "He's moored at the old tannery slip. He'll rob you, but he'll rob you fairly, and he'll get you there. That's more than anyone else on this river can say."

Freya looked at the notice again. Then she folded it, carefully, along its existing creases, and put it in her pocket.

"Ivo. The gear."

"Back room. What Halvern didn't buy." He was already moving, hauling aside a curtain, his voice going brisk and businesslike, and she understood that this was his way of saying what he couldn't say—that she was a fool, that she was dead, that she was the closest thing he had to a daughter and he'd been waiting nine days for her to walk through that door so he could help her do the stupidest thing of her life. "Cold-water suit, your size, because I remember your size even if the rest of the world's forgotten you. Regulators—two, because one always fails. Line, lights, the good knife." He started stacking things on the counter. "And a rebreather head I've been saving for a customer who never came back, which you'll take and not argue about."

"I can't pay for all this."

"You can pay for what you have. The rest is a loan against whatever you find up there." He didn't look at her. "Your father outfitted half this valley once. Amberdale Expeditions. People forget."

"People remember," Freya said quietly. "That's the problem."

Ivo stopped stacking. For a moment his hands were still on the counter, and the shop was very quiet, just the rain and the river beyond it and the two of them standing in the middle of everything her name had cost.

"He didn't do it," Ivo said. "Whatever they say he did. I knew him. He carried a boy out of a whiteout on the Vergha with two broken fingers and never told anyone. That's the man I knew."

"You didn't know everything about him."

"Nobody knows everything about anybody." He resumed stacking, faster. "But I know this: a man who sinks a ship to hide the truth is a man who thought the truth would hurt someone. And a man like that, Freya, is a man whose daughter should probably find out *why* before she decides what to do with what he left her."

Freya stared at him. "How do you know what he—"

"I don't." Ivo finally looked at her, and his eyes were the same gray as the rain. "But I know the shape of it. Forty years, a dead nation, a wreck in a lake that nobody was supposed to find, and now the glacier gives it up and every vulture in the borderlands is circling. I've been in this trade thirty years, girl. I can read a window." He pushed the stack of gear toward her. "Take it. Take the boat. Take the six tides. And when you find out what your father did—because you will find out—remember that knowing why is not the same as forgiving."

* * *

The tannery slip sat at the river's industrial edge, where the water ran gray with tannin and the smell of old hides had soaked into the pilings so deep that no rain could ever wash it out. Osk's boat was moored there, and Osk was sitting on its gunwale eating something out of a tin, and he watched her come down the alley with the flat, unhurried assessment of a man pricing cargo.

He was smaller than she remembered—she'd seen him last at her father's memorial, eleven years old and already running contraband—and he'd gone soft in the middle in the way of men who lived on boats, but his hands were quick and his eyes missed nothing.

"Freya Amberdale." He said her name the way you'd read a label. "Ivo called ahead. That was kind of him and expensive of you."

"Ivo called ahead?"

"Of course. He wanted me to know you were coming so I could decide whether to be here." He finished the tin and set it down. "I decided to be here. Draw your own conclusions."

"I need to go north. Upriver, past the ferry, past the checkpoint at Sorrel Bend, all the way to the high lakes."

"Everyone needs to go to the high lakes." Osk spat over the side, with feeling. "The high lakes are *popular* this season. A fleet of them, all white hulls and clean boots, up and down the river like a wedding procession. They offered to charter me too, you know. Good money. Very polite. Very legal." He smiled, and it wasn't a friendly smile. "I said no."

"Why?"

"Because polite money comes with paperwork, and paperwork comes with questions, and I've spent forty years making sure nobody has a reason to ask me any." He hopped down onto the deck. "You're offering me something else. I can smell it. What?"

"First claim. On anything non-Karstian aboard the wreck."

Osk went very still.

"You don't know what's aboard the wreck," he said slowly.

"I know it's a Karstian airship. I know it's been under the ice for forty years. I know that whatever Halvern wants from it, they want it badly enough to charter a river." She held his gaze. "I'm not selling you the Karstian cargo. I'm selling you everything else. Personal effects. Non-national salvage. Whatever the lawyers can't claim."

"The lawyers," Osk repeated. "You're expecting lawyers."

"I'm expecting the opposite of lawyers. I'm expecting the kind of find that makes lawyers necessary." She let that sit. "Six tides, Osk. That's their window. After that the monsoon closes the lakes and everyone goes home and whatever's down there stays down there for another forty years—or comes up in pieces in a Halvern warehouse with a patent number on it."

Osk looked at her for a long time. The rain had stopped; the river ran past them, brown and swollen and carrying, somewhere in its tons of moving water, the memory of everything it had taken from the mountains.

"Your father," he said at last, "once offered me a job. Real expedition, real money, the Vergha route. I was nineteen and I said no because I was a coward about ice." He shrugged. "Now I'm fifty and I'm not a coward about anything, which is what age is for. I'll take you. But hear the terms, and hear them once."

"I'm listening."

"The passes are watched. Not by Halvern—by Vessany. Since the Amberdale incident, the border service keeps eyes on the high routes, and their eyes are better than they used to be. If we're stopped, I'm a smuggler with a fast boat and a fine to pay. You're a blacklisted guide with a dead nation's seal in her pack." He held up a hand before she could speak. "I'm not saying no. I'm saying that if it goes wrong, I save the boat and you save yourself, and neither of us owes the other a funeral. That's the deal. That's the whole deal."

"That's the deal," Freya said.

Osk nodded once, and turned to his rigging, and the conversation was over, because with smugglers the deal is the relationship and everything else is weather.

Freya stood on the tannery slip and looked north, up the river, toward the country that had erased her father's name and the mountains that had erased her own. Six tides. Somewhere up there a glacier was retreating off a wreck that had no business existing, and somewhere behind her a diver named Marun was walking the docks with her description, and somewhere in a chest under a fallen spruce her father's handwriting was waiting to tell her what he'd done.

She had six tides, one smuggler, a borrowed rebreather, and a name nobody would hire.

It would have to be enough.

She stepped aboard.

* * *

They cast off at slack water, when the river's pull was weakest and the checkpoints were changing shift, and Osk took the boat upcurrent with the easy, contemptuous skill of a man who had been breaking rules on this water since before Freya was born. The town fell away behind them. The valley narrowed. The mountains came down to meet the river, and the river got faster, and by the time the light went gray-blue with evening they were past the last licensed dock and into water that belonged to nobody.

Osk killed the engine at a bend where the current pooled and let the boat drift while he ate. Freya stood in the bow and watched the banks slide past—spruce and scree and the first white shoulders of the high country, all of it familiar, all of it forbidden, all of it hers in the only way that mattered, which was that she knew it stone by stone.

"Your father's routes," Osk said behind her. Not a question.

"His routes."

"They closed all of them. You know that. After the incident, the border service mapped every line he ever pioneered and put a station on each one." He chewed, swallowed. "They closed them because of him, and they keep them closed because of you. You understand that, don't you? You're not banned from the mountains, Freya. You're the *reason* the mountains are banned."

The words went in deep, the way true things do.

She thought of the notice in her pocket, her father's jaw drawn in charcoal. She thought of the chest, and the crest, and the coordinates in a dead man's hand. She thought of Ivo saying *knowing why is not the same as forgiving*, and Osk saying *you're the reason*, and the two sentences locking together like carabiners, forming something she couldn't yet climb.

Ahead, upriver, the first checkpoint light came on at Sorrel Bend—a hard yellow eye sweeping the water.

Osk saw it too. He didn't startle. He just finished his food, wiped his hands on his trousers, and said, conversationally, "There's a side channel two bends up that the patrols stopped watching in the spring flood. Probably. If we're quiet, and if the river's kind, and if your luck is anything like your father's—"

"It isn't."

"Then we swim." He reached for the throttle. "Hold on to something that floats."

The engine woke, low and muffled, and the boat swung north into the dark, toward the checkpoint, toward the mountains, toward the six tides that were already running out.

Freya held the rail and watched the yellow light sweep closer, and made herself a promise with the cold coming off the water: she would reach the wreck before Halvern did. She would read every word her father had left. And whatever the truth was—whatever it cost, whoever it burned—she would carry it down out of the mountains herself, or she wouldn't come down at all.

The light swept the water. Osk cut the engine.

They drifted into the dark, silent, toward the channel and the long climb beyond it.
