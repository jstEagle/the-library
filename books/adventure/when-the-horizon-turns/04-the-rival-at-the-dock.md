## 4. The Rival at the Dock

The order came down at six in the morning, in the form of a man who knocked on Sefa Marun's cabin door without waiting for an answer.

"Brase wants you on the dock," he said. "Wants the chest found before we sail."

Sefa was already awake. She'd been awake since four, lying in her bunk aboard the *Halvern Vigil*, listening to the river town of Tarnholt wake up around her—cart wheels on wet cobbles, gulls arguing over fish guts, the low groan of a crane that hadn't been maintained since before she was born. She'd slept badly in every port since the charter began, and she'd stopped pretending otherwise.

"Find a chest," she said. "On a flooded border river. In a town with nine docks."

"That's why he wants you." The man—Pellik, logistics, a face like a filing cabinet—shrugged. "You're the one who notices things."

She noticed, for instance, that Pellik wouldn't look directly at her. That was new. Two weeks into the operation and the Halvern staff had settled into a comfortable contempt for their lead diver—the woman they'd hired out of the coastal yards because she could work deeper and colder than anyone on the payroll and didn't complain about either. Contempt was fine. Contempt you could work under.

This was something else. This was the look people gave you when they suspected you were a problem.

"I'll be up in ten minutes," she said, and closed the door before he could answer.

* * *

Tarnholt smelled like everything the river had taken and given back. The flood had peaked three days ago and begun its slow retreat, and now the town sat in the aftermath like a man going through his own pockets—dazed, damp, counting what was missing. Boats were hauled up on the mud in attitudes of surrender. A line of salvagers worked the shallows with grappling hooks, dragging for whatever the water had coughed loose: fence posts, a piano, half a chapel roof, a child's doll with one porcelain eye that stared at the sky as if it had seen enough.

And chests. That was the word moving through the dock crowd like smoke through a keyhole. Chests. The flood had opened something upstream—a grave, a cache, a wreck—and the river was paying out its contents in installments. Three had come ashore already. Two were junk, rotted through. The third—

The third had a seal on it, and the seal had started everything.

Sefa stood at the rail of the survey barge and watched Corvel Brase hold court on the main dock. He was easy to pick out even from distance: the only man in Tarnholt wearing clothes that fit him. Charcoal coat, no mud on it, which was its own kind of magic. He spoke quietly to the harbormaster and the harbormaster leaned in like a plant toward light. Brase never raised his voice. He didn't need to. Money did the raising for him.

Forty years ago, Karstia had existed. She knew this the way everyone of her generation knew it—from the margins of history books, from the way older relatives went quiet at certain names, from the maps where the space between Vessany and the northern ranges had been printed over with neutral gray and a note: *Territorial administration transferred, see current edition.* Forty years ago, Karstia had been erased—not conquered, not absorbed, just *edited*, the borders redrawn so smoothly that within a decade schoolchildren believed the mountains had always belonged to whoever held them now.

Her grandmother had been born there. Had sung songs in a language Sefa couldn't speak, had cooked food whose name she'd never learned, had died in a coastal city two hundred miles from home, in a house with nothing in it that admitted where she came from.

Sefa had signed onto the Halvern charter because the pay was triple yard rate and the dive specs were interesting—a deep-water recovery in high glacial lakes, pre-monsoon window, target unspecified. *Target unspecified* should have been her first warning. Corporations didn't spend fleet money on unspecified targets.

Then the chest had come ashore with a bronze stamp on it, and the stamp had been a double-headed kestrel inside a mountain ring, and Brase had gone very still when he saw it, stiller than she'd ever seen a man go, and the whole operation had pivoted overnight from salvage to hunt.

Because the seal was Karstian. And whatever the chest had carried, Brase wanted it back—or wanted whoever took it found—before anyone else understood what it meant.

"Marun." Brase's voice reached her across the deck without effort, the way sound carries off cold water. "Walk with me."

* * *

They walked the length of the main dock while the harbor woke around them. Brase moved like a man touring property he already owned.

"The chest that came ashore Tuesday," he said. "Bronze-stamped. You've heard."

"Everyone's heard."

"It didn't come ashore alone. There was a second crate, smaller, logged by the toll station upstream at Verren's Ferry. It went into the water there and never surfaced at the collection point here." He handed her a photograph, glossy, recent: a wooden crate, waterlogged, stamped with the same kestrel ring. "Someone pulled it out between the ferry and here. Sixteen miles of riverbank. I want to know who."

"You think a salvager got to it first."

"I think a salvager doesn't matter." He said it mildly, which made it worse. "A salvager takes the brass fittings and burns the rest. What concerns me is someone who takes the whole crate and disappears with it intact. That's not greed. That's intent."

"And if it's just some kid who found it and sold it for drinking money?"

"Then this conversation costs us nothing but your morning." He stopped at the end of the dock and looked out at the river, brown and swollen and patient. "You grew up along this border, Marun?"

"Coastal. South."

"But family from the interior."

It wasn't a question, and she didn't confirm it. Brase smiled at the water.

"You read old script? Pre-transfer Karstian cursive?" 

"Some."

"Good. If the crate surfaces, I want you reading it, not a hired archivist." He turned back toward the barge. "Start at the ferry landing. Work downstream. Talk to the hook-men, the mudlarks, the toll keeper. Someone saw something. People always see something; they just don't know what they're seeing until you show them what to look for."

He paused, then added, almost as an afterthought: "Report only to me. Not Pellik, not the security detail. Only me."

Which meant, Sefa thought as he walked away, that whatever was in the crate was worth more than the fleet. Worth more than trust.

* * *

The Verren's Ferry landing was a hour's walk downstream, and she walked it because walking gave her time to think and boats gave other people time to watch her.

The toll keeper was a woman named Dessa with forearms like mooring rope and the flat stare of someone who'd been asked questions by officials before. Sefa bought her honesty with two cups of the landing's terrible coffee and a twenty-minute conversation about river levels that was actually a conversation about nothing, and then, casually, about cargo.

"Flood brought down all kinds," Dessa said. "Mostly junk. Some good timber. One night—Tuesday, maybe Wednesday, hard to say, the fog was thick—a skiff came through heading north. Paid the crossing fee in cash, didn't want a receipt."

"Alone?"

"One person. Hooded. Kept to the middle of the boat." Dessa shrugged. "But here's the thing. They had gear. Real gear. Climbing kit, expedition grade, packed like they knew exactly what weather was coming. Nobody packs like that for a river run."

"What kind of climbing kit?"

"Rope coils. Crampons hanging off the pack. Ice tools." Dessa's eyes narrowed slightly, remembering. "And a chest lashed down under the tarp. Bronze corners catching my lamp. I remember thinking, that's a long way to carry something heavy."

North. Upstream. Toward the high lakes.

Sefa thanked her, left coins on the counter, and stood a while at the landing looking north up the gray ribbon of water, at the country beyond it rising into foothills, into ranges, into the white silence where the glaciers lived and where, according to the survey charts locked in Brase's cabin, a lake basin waited with something in it that had waited forty years.

Whoever had the crate was headed for the same place the fleet was. Alone, under-equipped by fleet standards, ahead of them by days.

Brase would call that a problem.

Sefa found herself calling it something else. Something she didn't have a word for yet.

* * *

She spent the next day working the docks the way Brase had ordered—talking to hook-men, checking the mudlark camps, showing the photograph of the crate to faces that showed her nothing back. It was honest work, mostly theater, and she performed it well enough that Pellik stopped giving her that filing-cabinet look.

On the third morning, at the fish market dock, she saw the climber.

She almost missed her. That was the thing Sefa would turn over later, again and again: how close it came to never happening. The market was loud, the light was bad, rain coming down in the fine persistent way that made everyone hunch. A woman stood at the chandler's stall at the dock's far end, negotiating for something—rope, from the way she handled the sample coil, running it through her hands with a familiarity that wasn't professional so much as native. The way you touch a thing you've trusted with your life.

Expedition jacket, patched at the shoulders. Boots resoled twice. A pack set down beside her with the straps already adjusted, already broken in, positioned for a grab-and-run. Everything about her said *mountains*, said *long solo routes*, said *the kind of person who reads weather the way sailors read water*.

And everything about her said *careful*. She stood with her back to a piling, sight lines covered, weight balanced. She paid in cash. She kept her hood up against the rain, and when the chandler's boy leaned in too close to the pack she shifted it, smooth as breathing, so it stayed against her hip.

Sefa drifted closer, browsing a stall of tangled tackle she had no interest in, and caught the fragment of negotiation carrying over the wet air:

"—need it rated for ice, not just wet rock. And I'll take the pitons if they're not rusted through."

The chandler said something about price. The woman said something back, low, and there was an accent in it—not thick, worn down like a coin passed through too many hands, but there. Northern vowels. Interior vowels.

Karstian vowels. Or close enough that Sefa's grandmother would have heard it across a room and gone still.

The woman turned to test a carabiner against the light, and for half a second her hood slipped back and Sefa saw her face: sharp-boned, sun-weathered, a scar through one eyebrow, eyes the flat gray-green of glacial water. Younger than Sefa expected. Harder, too, in the specific way of someone who'd been told *no* by institutions until she stopped asking.

There was a bounty notice pinned to the harbormaster's board, Sefa remembered suddenly. One of several. Most were for smugglers and debt-runners. But one had been posted by the Vessany Border Authority, and it featured a face like this one, and a name she'd clocked without thinking much of at the time because the notice was months old and half faded:

**FREYA AMBERDALE — GUIDING LICENSE REVOKED — DO NOT ENGAGE ALONE — REPORT TO AUTHORITY.**

Amberdale. The name had meant nothing to her then. Now, standing ten feet from the woman herself, watching her buy pitons with cash she counted out carefully, Sefa felt the name land somewhere below her ribs.

Because Amberdale wasn't just a blacklist entry. The older hook-men talked about the Amberdale incident the way fishermen talk about storms that killed their fathers—there had been an expedition, years back, up in the passes above the lakes, and it had ended badly, and the guide's name had been struck from two countries' rolls, and the passes themselves had been closed afterward, officially, permanently. Closed to everyone.

Except, apparently, to the woman buying pitons at the fish market dock.

Freya Amberdale finished her transaction, coiled the rope into her pack with quick economy, and turned north along the dock.

And stopped.

Not dramatically. Just a hitch in her stride, half a step, the kind of pause a wild animal makes when the forest goes quiet. She didn't turn her head. She didn't need to. Sefa watched her shoulders change—watched awareness move through her like current through wire—and understood that the woman had registered her the same way she'd registered the woman: the wrong kind of attention, the wrong quality of stillness, a hunter's posture in a buyer's clothing.

Two hunters, recognizing each other across ten feet of wet planking and forty years of buried history.

Amberdale resumed walking. Unhurried. Perfectly unhurried, which was itself a message: *I know you saw me, and I'm telling you I don't care.* She passed the end of the dock, turned up the lane toward the north road, and was gone into the rain and the crowd.

Sefa stood there with a coil of worthless tackle in her hands and her heart going harder than any dive had ever made it go.

* * *

That evening, Brase summoned her to the survey barge's chart room. Maps everywhere, the lake basin rendered in Halvern's proprietary contour shading, red pins marking beacon deployment points. He poured her a drink she didn't touch.

"Progress?" he asked.

"Docks are clean. Mudlarks found nothing. Toll records show northbound traffic, mostly timber barges." A pause she calibrated precisely. "One skiff, unlogged, night of the flood peak. Could be our courier. Could be nobody."

"Direction?"

"North."

Brase studied the map, and Sefa studied Brase, and the words she did not say stood in her throat like a swallowed stone: *I saw her. I know her name. I could give you everything.*

She thought about the bounty notice. She thought about how simple it would be—one sentence, one report, and Halvern's security detail would fold Freya Amberdale into custody before she cleared the valley, and the chest, and whatever it carried, would be corporate property by week's end. Clean. Professional. Exactly what she was paid for.

She thought about her grandmother's hands, kneading bread in a kitchen that contained no evidence of Karstia, singing a lullaby in a language her granddaughter couldn't speak, in a house where the past had been packed away so thoroughly it might never have existed at all.

She thought about a bronze kestrel inside a mountain ring, and who else alive might recognize it, and what it would mean to be the one who handed it over to a man like Corvel Brase instead of the ones it belonged to.

"Keep working the docks," Brase said finally. "And Marun—if you do surface anything solid, anything with a face attached to it, I want it within the hour. Not tomorrow. Within the hour." His eyes were pleasant and bottomless. "The fleet sails in five tides. After that, the monsoon owns the mountains, and whatever opportunity exists will exist without us. I don't intend to lose it to some lone climber with a grudge and a head start."

"No," Sefa said. "I don't suppose you do."

She left the chart room, crossed the wet deck, and stood at the rail looking north, where the last light was dying on the shoulders of the ranges and the rain was turning to sleet at altitude, somewhere up there, in country closed to everyone except the woman who'd been banned from it.

Five tides. A lone climber with a head start. A wreck in a lake that was only now giving up its dead.

Sefa realized she had already made a decision, somewhere in the fish-market crowd, in the half-second their eyes nearly met—that she had chosen, without letting herself notice choosing, to keep the name behind her teeth.

Now she just had to decide what to do with it.

She went below, pulled Halvern's survey charts from her locker, and began, very quietly, to copy.
