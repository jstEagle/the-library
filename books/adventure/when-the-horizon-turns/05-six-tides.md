## 5. Six Tides

Osk's boat was called the *Marrow*, which told you everything you needed to know about his sense of humor and nothing about his sense of mercy. She found him at the third slip past the fish market, where the river went brown and fast with the melt, scraping barnacles off a hull that had probably never seen salt water. He didn't look up when she stopped at the edge of his tarp.

"I don't hire blacklisted guides," he said.

"I'm not asking for a job."

"Then you're asking for something worse." He sat back on his heels and finally looked at her, and she watched him do the arithmetic every man in this river country did when they saw her face: the jaw like her father's, the name that had been whispered through three border towns before the patrols ever printed it on paper. "Amberdale."

"Just Freya."

"There's no just about it." He tossed his scraper into a bucket and stood, wiping his hands on a rag that was dirtier than his hands. "You know how many people have come to me since the incident asking after the high passes? None. You know why? Because anyone fool enough to go north is fool enough not to ask me first." He squinted at her. "You're going anyway."

"The Vesper Kestrel," she said, because lying to a man like Osk was a waste of both their mornings.

Something moved behind his eyes—not surprise. Recognition. Which meant he'd already heard the rumor, or already been asked, or already turned down money from someone who wanted the same wreck. "Halvern's fleet chartered every boat with a draft under two meters," he said. "Six tides before monsoon landfall, that's their window. You think I'm going to lose my hull and my license running one woman upriver against a corporate salvage armada?"

"You're a smuggler, Osk. You don't have a license."

"I have an arrangement with the men who would otherwise arrest me, and arrangements are worth more than licenses." He walked to the rail and spat into the current. "Why should I risk it?"

She'd rehearsed this on the walk down from the river town, rehearsed it against every counter he might raise, and now that the moment came she found she didn't want the rehearsed version. She wanted him to understand what she was actually offering, so she said it plain.

"First claim. Anything non-Karstian aboard that wreck—cargo, fittings, salvage metal, whatever Halvern would pay for—you get it. I take only what belongs to Karstia. Papers, seals, anything stamped with the crest."

"And if it's all cargo? If there's a hold full of trade goods down there?"

"Then you'll be rich and I'll be heartbroken, and we'll both have gotten what we came for."

He laughed—a short, ugly bark, the laugh of a man who'd heard better offers from worse liars. But he didn't say no, and in Osk's economy, not saying no was nearly a signature. He studied her a long moment, the way he might study a river before crossing it in flood.

"Your father," he said. "Aldric Amberdale. He ran the Vergha Pass before you were weaned. My uncle carried rope for him twice."

"I know."

"Do you know what happened up there?" And there it was—the question she'd been dreading since the chest came out of the water, the question everyone asked and no one answered honestly, least of all her. "The Amberdale incident. Everyone says the name like it's weather. Nobody tells me what it was."

* * *

She could have lied. She'd gotten good at it—forty months of blacklists taught you the shape of a clean lie, how to keep it small enough to carry. But Osk was about to stake his boat on her, and a man like that deserved the truth, or at least the version of it she could stand to say out loud.

"There were eleven of us," she began. "Seven years ago. A survey expedition into the Vergha, mapping a route through the icefall for a mining consortium. I was lead guide. My father's old routes—I'd reconstructed them from his letters, the ones he wrote before he disappeared. We were four days past the last marker when the weather turned."

She remembered it in pieces, the way trauma files things: the whiteout arriving like a wall falling; Marta Senn's voice counting climbers over the radio and getting to nine and stopping; the sound the cornice made when it let go, less a crack than a sigh, as if the mountain had been holding its breath for years and finally exhaled.

"The route I chose put us under a serac field. My father's notes said it was stable. His notes were twenty years old. Three people died. Two more lost fingers and feet. The consortium sued, Vessany revoked my permit, and Karstia—" she stopped, corrected herself, the old reflex, "—and the border commission blacklisted me in both countries within the year. They said I'd falsified the route assessment. That I'd pushed on for the fee."

"Were the assessments falsified?"

"No." The word came out harder than she intended. "But I can't prove it, because the original documents went missing from the consortium's office the week before the inquiry, and the man who could have vouched for my judgment was dead in the ice." She looked at the river rather than at him. "That's the incident. That's the name. Eleven went up, eight came down, and one of them has spent seven years being a cautionary tale in towns she can't legally enter."

Osk was quiet for a while. He picked up his scraper and put it down again without using it.

"My uncle said Aldric Amberdale once carried a broken client down the Vergha on his back," he said. "Four days. Through weather that killed a patrol party the same season. He said the mountains didn't make mistakes around your father—the maps did." He met her eyes. "So either you're your father's daughter or you're not. I haven't decided."

"Neither have I," she said, and meant it more than he knew.

* * *

They signed nothing, because men like Osk didn't sign things. He named a price that made her wince and then halved it when she showed him the bronze stamp on the chest, turning it in the light until he saw the crest and went still in a way that told her he understood exactly what he was looking at.

"That seal hasn't floated on this river in forty years," he said quietly.

"It floated yesterday."

He took the chest aboard himself, wouldn't let her touch it, stowed it under the wheelhouse tarp like something that might bite. Then he set terms: departure at slack water, two days from now, and a stop at Kellin's Reach where he had business she wasn't to ask about. In exchange, first claim on non-Karstian salvage, and her silence about whatever the business at Kellin's Reach turned out to be.

"One more thing," he said as she turned to leave. "The passes are watched. Since the incident, the border commission keeps spotters on the Vergha approaches in summer—half of them are mine, if I'm honest, paid to tell me when the coast is clear. Right now the coast is not clear. There's a patrol rotation through the lower valley every six hours, and Halvern's advance team has been buying topographic sheets from every map seller between here and the lake. They're not waiting for their fleet, Freya. They're scouting ahead of it."

"Then I'll move faster than their scouts."

"Everyone says that." He looked at her with something that wasn't quite pity and wasn't quite respect. "Your father said it too, the last time he came through here. Thirty-eight years back, maybe thirty-nine. Bought rope and lamp oil and wouldn't tell me's father what for. Went up the Vergha with a crew nobody saw again." He shrugged. "Whatever he was doing up there, the mountain kept it. Let's hope it's kept it well."

She walked back along the slips with the bargain settled and the clock already running in her chest. Six tides. She counted them the way she'd learned to count breath at altitude—in, hold, out—and by the time she reached the room above the chandlery where she'd been sleeping with the chest's logbook under her pillow, she'd stopped counting tides and started counting hours.

* * *

Sleep didn't come, so she read instead. The logbook fragment again, its water-swollen pages soft as cloth, her father's handwriting marching across the coordinates in that tight, slanted script she'd have known anywhere—had known anywhere, for years, in letters that arrived like contraband and stopped arriving altogether the winter she turned nineteen.

*Coordinates in his hand. The Vesper Kestrel, sunk in the high lakes.*

She'd built her whole life on those letters without knowing it. Every route she'd pioneered, every pass she'd crossed ahead of the sanctioned parties, every map line she'd redrawn—she'd been following him, chasing a man who'd vanished into the same mountains she'd been banned from, leaving nothing behind but handwriting and a silence she'd filled with ambition. And now the letters had led her here, to a dead nation's crest and a drowned ship and a question she'd been circling for forty months without naming:

*What was Aldric Amberdale doing on a Karstian airship?*

The bounty notice was folded inside the logbook where she'd tucked it, her own face looking back at her in cheap ink—FREYA AMBERDALE, WANTED FOR QUESTIONING, REWARD NEGOTIABLE, ISSUED BY ORDER OF THE BORDER COMMISSION. She'd laughed when she first saw it, a bitter little sound in the dark of the river town. Questioning. As if they wanted answers. They wanted her stopped, the way they'd wanted her father stopped, the way the border countries had always handled Ambedales who went where they weren't permitted.

Except her father hadn't been stopped. Her father had gone up the Vergha with a crew nobody saw again, and come down—or hadn't—with something worth sinking a ship over.

She lay back and let the river's sound fill the room, and for the first time since the chest surfaced, she let herself feel the other thing underneath the fear and the fury: shame. Not for the serac, not for the deaths—that grief she'd carried so long it had worn smooth, like river stone. The other shame. The one that lived in the fact that she'd spent seven years telling herself the blacklist was injustice, pure and simple, and had never once asked whether her father's routes—the ones she'd trusted with eleven lives—might themselves have been part of some older wrong. The mountain had judged her and she'd called it corruption. Now the river had coughed up a chest that suggested the judgment might have been smaller than the crime, or larger, and either way she'd been walking blind through country her family had helped make blind.

*Inheritance curdles,* she thought. *But it also ripens. Depends what you do with it.*

Two days. Then slack water, then the *Marrow*, then north into passes that remembered her name in the worst way.

* * *

On the second evening, coming down to the boat with her pack and her dive gear wrapped in oilcloth, she felt it before she saw it—the particular emptiness of a dock where a boat should be and isn't.

The *Marrow* was still tied up. But Osk stood at the gangplank talking to two men in gray jackets, and the way he held his shoulders—loose, agreeable, a man performing patience—told her everything. One of the gray jackets had a clipboard. The other had a Halvern Deep Recovery tab pinned to his collar, and beside them, at the foot of the slip, a launch with an official pennant idled against the pilings.

Customs impoundment. She'd seen it done to a dozen boats in a dozen towns, always politely, always with paperwork, always effective.

Osk caught her eye over the gray jackets' heads. He didn't shake his head. He didn't warn her off. What he did was glance, once, almost imperceptibly, upriver—past the fish market, past the last moored hull, toward the long gravel shallows where the cart traders beached their flat-bottomed punts at dusk.

Then he turned back to the clipboard and raised his voice, complaining loudly about compensation schedules, giving the gray jackets something to watch that wasn't the woman with the oilcloth bundle walking unhurried along the seawall, counting her remaining hours, looking for a punt that could float a liar and a guide as far as the mouth of the Vergha.

Five tides, she thought. Maybe four and a half.

The climb would have to start tonight.
