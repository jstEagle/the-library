## 6. The Pass That Remembers

The boat died at Kessel Reach, and it didn't even have the decency to die dramatically.

Freya watched from the willow scrub above the landing as two Vessany water police cutters boxed Osk's flat-bottomed barge against the pilings. No shots, no shouting. Just a man in a gray uniform stepping aboard with a clipboard while another looped a towline with the bored efficiency of someone who did this forty times a season. Osk stood on his own deck with his hands in his pockets, arguing about paperwork she couldn't hear, and Freya understood within thirty seconds that the argument was theater. They'd been looking for his boat specifically. Which meant they'd been looking for her.

She should have felt surprised. She felt instead a cold, almost professional clarity—the same thing that used to arrive on bad routes when the cornice above started ticking. *Decision time. Stop wasting breath.*

Osk had been paid half up front and would keep it; smugglers didn't refund. He'd also, she suspected now, sold her route to whoever was asking. Not maliciously. Just the way water found cracks. She'd known that when she hired him and had planned for it, which was why she'd gone ashore at Miller's Bend, four kilometers back, and told him she'd reboard at Kessel Reach.

She hadn't reboarded. And now the pass was hers alone.

* * *

The Vergha Pass wasn't on maps anymore. That had been the point of mapping it.

Twelve years ago, Freya Amberdale had spent nineteen days in these mountains with a barometer, a hand-drawn base map, and a stubbornness her father had called either inheritance or disease. The official routes north crossed at Vergha's western shoulder—a long, safe, stupid traverse that added six days and funneled every traveler past a checkpoint. Freya had found the eastern notch instead: a knife of a couloir, a shelf system stitched across the headwall like scar tissue, and a final chimney that went at grade three if the ice was right and killed you if it wasn't. She'd named none of it. Naming things got them claimed, and she'd wanted the pass to stay wild, stay hers, stay unrecorded.

Then the incident happened—*the* incident, the one with the client and the crevasse and the inquiry that read her logs upside down until they said what the tribunal needed them to say—and both countries had solved the problem of Freya Amberdale by erasing her. Licenses revoked. Borders closed. Her pioneering routes struck from the registers as though terrain could be unsurveyed by spite.

But you can't strike a route from the mountain itself. The mountain kept her handwriting.

She climbed toward it through the afternoon, following a drainage no map showed because she'd never shown anyone. The chest rode high on her pack where it wouldn't soak, wrapped in oilcloth, its bronze seal pressing a cold oval against her spine through the frame. Every switchback put another hundred meters between her and the river, and the river took the news of her with it downstream—to patrols, to Halvern's fleet, to whatever machinery Corvel Brase had already spinning. She didn't look back. Looking back was for people who believed the past might change its mind.

By late light she reached the first shelf and allowed herself the luxury of a long look south. The valley below had gone blue and smoke-colored, one thread of woodsmoke rising from a herder's hut. Somewhere down there were people who would sell her location for less than the cost of their winter feed. She turned north.

The notch stood against the sky like a held breath.

* * *

She climbed into the couloir at dawn because ice is honestest at dawn, before the sun works its lies into the surface. The walls rose sheer on either side, granite streaked black with forty years of meltwater, and the going was exactly as she remembered: steep enough to demand everything, broken enough to forgive nothing. Her tools bit old placements. Her boots found old edges. Twice she stopped, hanging on front points, staring at a hold she knew intimately and had never expected to touch again, and the strangeness of it nearly made her laugh out loud—a sound that would have echoed badly, so she swallowed it.

This was the part nobody understood about being banned from mountains. It wasn't the loss of income. It was this: knowing a wall better than your own hands and being forbidden to touch it. Twelve years of other ranges, other work, always competent, never home.

Two-thirds up, the memory arrived without permission, the way it always did on this kind of ground.

She was nine. Her father had brought her to a crag below the tree line—not teaching, exactly. A. Amberdale never taught; he *tested*, quietly, constantly, and only later would you realize you'd been examined. He'd handed her the rack and said, *Build me an anchor that would hold a falling truck.* She'd spent an hour on it, sweating, certain she'd failed, and when she finished he'd checked every piece, nodded once, and said, *Good. Now take it apart and build a better one, because the truck is still coming.*

She'd hated him a little, that day. She loved him completely. Both things stayed true after he vanished, and neither helped.

She topped out of the couloir at midmorning with burning forearms and the notch ahead—a gap in the ridge no wider than a road, wind screaming through it from the high lakes beyond. From here, on clear days, you could see the basin: a scatter of glacial water like spilled mercury, and somewhere among them, under forty years of ice, a ship with her family's crime folded in its hold.

Today the sky was not clear. Today the sky was the color of a bruise going bad.

* * *

The storm caught her on the descent shelf, which was the worst possible place for it, because storms don't care about topography and shelves offer nowhere to hide.

It came off the northern icefields like something released rather than arriving—one minute spindrift haze, the next a white roar that erased the ridge, the route, the difference between sky and stone. Freya moved on instinct and twelve-year-old memory, counting paces between cairns she'd built herself at twenty-three, feeling for the shelf's tilt with her poles. Visibility collapsed to arm's length. The temperature fell so fast her jacket crackled.

She'd been in whiteouts before. She knew the arithmetic: shelter or die, and choose fast, because the choosing gets harder as the cold gets into the deciding.

The cave was a rumor she'd carried for twelve years. On her original survey she'd noted a deep overhang on the shelf's underside, forty meters past the third cairn, big enough for two, dry floor, defensible. She'd logged it and never used it, because on that trip the weather had behaved. Now she counted paces—thirty-one, thirty-two—and swung her axe left on faith, and the faith rang true: hollow air, then rock, then the sudden blessed silence of standing behind the mountain while it screamed.

She got inside, lit the stove, and let her hands stop shaking before she let herself think.

* * *

The cave was deeper than she'd logged. Storm-light filtered in gray and sourceless, and once her eyes adjusted she saw why: the overhang narrowed into a true chamber at the back, floored with fine dry scree, and against the far wall sat shapes.

Her first thought was *bears*. Her second thought was *other climbers*, and her hand went to her knife before her brain caught up with the geometry of the shapes—too regular, too stacked, too patient. Nobody stacked gear like that except someone organizing it, and nobody organized gear in a cave and left it unless they were never coming back.

She played her lamp across it slowly.

Rope coils, stiff with age, fused into amber crescents. Aluminum stakes gone dull and speckled. A wooden crate with its lid sprung, and printed along the slats in faded but legible block letters:

**AMBERDALE EXPEDITIONS — EST. VEZZA KARSTIA**

Freya sat down on the scree without meaning to.

She knew the name. Of course she knew the name—it had been on the side of her childhood garage, stenciled by her father's own hand, the company he'd run before the borders closed and the commissions dried up and the letters started arriving from places he wouldn't name. But she had never heard of an Amberdale expedition to the Vergha. His logs, the ones she'd inherited, the ones the tribunal had confiscated half of—there was no Vergha in them. There was no *anything* in the last two years of them. The gaps had always looked like grief.

Now they looked like logistics.

She opened the crate with hands that were steady in the way of hands pretending. Inside: pitons wrapped in wax paper. A primus stove, disassembled and greased for storage. A canvas rucksack, mildewed but intact. And beneath everything, flat and square and wrapped in oilcloth just like hers—

A second chest. Smaller. Unstamped. Locked.

She knelt there a long time, the storm hammering outside like a fist on a door, and felt the mountain rearrange itself around her. This wasn't coincidence. Caves didn't stock themselves. Her father had come this way—this exact shelf, this exact overhang—with equipment stamped in his own name, carrying a locked box into the high lakes, years before the Vesper Kestrel went down. Before, maybe, the ship had even sailed. The route she'd pioneered at twenty-three, the route she'd been banned for, the route that had defined her whole adult life as trespass—he had walked it first, in secret, and never written a word of it where she could find it.

Or he had written it, and someone had taken the words.

She thought of the logbook fragment in the chest on her back. Coordinates in his handwriting. *Crew,* the fragment had implied, in the margins of cargo manifests she hadn't fully decoded yet. Her whole life she'd built herself out of his absence—an orphan shaped by a mystery—and she'd made the mystery romantic. The lost guide. The silenced hero. The man the borders ate.

The cave offered no romance. The cave offered a locked box and a storm and the growing, grinding suspicion that she had spent twelve years climbing in a man's footsteps without ever once seeing them.

*You're not pioneering anything,* she told herself, and the thought landed like a fall onto rock. *You're retracing. You've been retracing since the river coughed up that chest. Maybe since before that.*

Outside, the whiteout howled. Inside, Freya Amberdale sat in her father's camp, in her father's route, in her father's silence, and understood that the wilderness wasn't judging her at all.

It had already judged him. She was just reading the verdict.

She reached for the small chest's lock, and stopped—because her lamp caught something she'd missed: scratched into the stone beside the crate, low, half-hidden, in a hand she'd know anywhere because she'd seen it on every note he ever left her:

*A. A. — If you're reading this, I'm sorry. Go back.*

She looked at the words for a long moment. Then, because she was her father's daughter, and because the truck was still coming, she picked up the pry bar from the crate and went to work on the lock.
