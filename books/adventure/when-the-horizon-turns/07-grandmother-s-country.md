## 7. Grandmother's Country

The survey tent smelled of diesel and cold plastic, and Sefa Marun had been lying in it for three days.

Not lying the way most people lied—loud, defensive, sweating. Her lies were quiet, procedural, folded into the rhythm of competent work. She arrived early. She logged hours. She produced documents with the right margins and the right stamps, and every one of them was a small, precise act of theft.

On the table before her, weighted at the corners with brass dive weights, lay Halvern Deep Recovery's master chart of the lake basin. Bathymetric lines in blue ink, contour intervals in red, the Vesper Kestrel's projected position marked with a neat corporate triangle and the notation *TARGET — DO NOT DISTRIBUTE*. The original had cost Halvern eleven years of satellite passes, two bribed hydrologists, and one drowned survey drone. Sefa had been given custody of it because she was, by every measurable standard, their best diver and their most reliable employee.

She fed a fresh sheet of drafting film into the copy frame and lowered the lamp.

The light hummed. Blue lines became ghost lines on film, faint but legible, the kind of thing you could read against a window or hold up to a fire. She'd learned the technique from her grandmother—not the copying, but the principle of it. *Anything worth keeping, keep it twice,* Amalya used to say, pressing dried figs between layers of cloth so they'd survive the winter. *Once for the house, once for the heart.*

Sefa thought about her grandmother more than was professionally advisable these days.

She thought about her hands, which were knotted like tree roots and always smelled faintly of anise. She thought about the way she'd sung when she worked—half under her breath, a tune with no name that Sefa had assumed for thirty years was just something old women hummed. It wasn't until last spring, in a diaspora café in Corvane City, that she'd heard it again, whole, from a stranger's radio: a Karstian lullaby about a shepherd who loses his flock to the river and finds them, in the last verse, turned to stars.

*Turned to stars,* Sefa had thought, standing in the doorway with a coffee going cold in her hand. *It was never about the sheep.*

The copy lamp clicked off its cycle. She lifted the film, checked it against the master, found it good, and slid it into the false bottom of her instrument case, beneath the depth gauges where no customs officer in Vessany would ever think to look, because divers' tools were boring and borders were interested in weapons and money, not in the geometry of a lake.

Three sheets now. Three nights of copying. Tomorrow the fleet's advance boats would arrive at the dock below, and after that there would be no more private hours in this tent, no more charts left unattended, no more versions of herself that could plausibly coexist.

She should have felt like a traitor. That was the strange part. She kept waiting for the feeling and it didn't come. What came instead was the lullaby, surfacing at odd moments—the way grief surfaces, the way bodies do in lakes—unbidden and patient.

*Turned to stars. Turned to stars.*

Her grandmother had been born in Karstia, in a valley town whose name Sefa could not pronounce correctly no matter how many times she practiced, because the language had gone out of official use the year her mother was born and her mother had refused to pass it on. *We speak forward, not backward,* her mother said. Her grandmother had never argued. She'd simply sung, and cooked, and kept a tin box of letters written in a script that made her mother leave the room, and when she died she'd left the tin box to Sefa, who was the only grandchild who ever asked about it.

Inside the box: forty-one letters, a pressed sprig of some mountain flower Sefa couldn't identify, and a single photograph of a young woman in a Karstian survey corps uniform, standing beside a balloon gondola, laughing at someone outside the frame.

Karstia had surveyed its own mountains once. Had mapped its own lakes. Had put women in gondolas and let them laugh. And then forty years ago it had stopped existing—not conquered, not destroyed, just *erased*, borders redrawn, name struck from treaties and atlases, its people scattered into Vessany and Corvane and places farther, told quietly and then less quietly that the past was a liability.

Sefa had grown up in Vessany speaking forward, not backward. She'd joined Halvern because Halvern hired anyone with talent and asked nothing about origins, which she'd taken for generosity until she understood it was actually indifference wearing a nice coat. She'd risen fast. She was good at her work in the way that made people stop questioning you, and she had told herself for nine years that the past was a country she'd emigrated from, and that was fine, and the tin box could stay shut.

Then the chest had come up the river with a dead nation's seal on it, and Brase had called a briefing, and she'd watched a room full of well-paid professionals look at the crest of Karstia and see only *salvage rights*.

"Marun." Brase's voice, from the tent flap.

She closed the instrument case in one smooth motion and turned, pen already moving across a progress log as though she'd never stopped working. "Captain."

Corvel Brase entered the way he entered everything—like the room had been reserved for him in advance. He was not a large man, but he occupied space thoroughly. His eyes went to the table, to the master chart, to her logbook, in that order, and Sefa felt the particular cold of being audited by someone who trusted no one and was right to.

"Status."

"Bathymetric overlays are reconciled," she said. "I've flagged three viable dive corridors into the target zone. The northern approach is cleanest—less silt load from the melt stream—but the ice channel there is narrow. We'll want the small submersible for recon before we commit divers."

He nodded slowly, taking the information, and she watched him take it and knew that ninety percent of what she'd just said was true, which was the trick. Lies built entirely of falsehood collapsed under pressure. Lies built on real work held weight. She had spent three days doing genuine, excellent survey analysis so that her betrayal would have structural integrity.

"And the chest?" Brase said. "The one that came downriver. Whoever pulled it from the flood will try for the wreck—they always do. Freelancers can't resist a head start." He picked up a bounty notice from the clip board and held it toward her. A woman's face, sharp-boned, windburnt. FREYA AMBERDALE, the header read. OBSERVE AND REPORT. DO NOT DETAIN.

"You've seen her?"

"No," Sefa said.

It wasn't even a lie. She'd seen a woman matching the description at the dock two days ago, buying rope with cash, keeping her hood up, moving with the specific economy of someone who knew she was being looked for—and Sefa had stood ten feet away holding a requisition clipboard and said nothing, done nothing, let her walk past into the crowd like water closing over a stone.

She still hadn't decided if that was mercy or cowardice. Possibly both were the same thing at that altitude.

Brase studied her face. He had a habit of studying faces the way other men studied instruments, looking for needle-deviations. "If you see her, you report it immediately. Not because she matters personally. Because anything she knows, we want to know first." He set the notice back on the clip. "Sentiment is expensive, Marun. I don't pay for it."

"No, sir."

"The fleet sails on the morning tide after next. Have your dive team manifests ready tonight." At the tent flap he paused, half-turned. "Your grandmother was Karstian. I've read the personnel file."

The temperature in the tent dropped ten degrees, or Sefa's blood did.

"It's relevant only insofar as I find it useful," Brase continued pleasantly. "People with roots in that basin read terrain differently. You notice things my Corvane hires don't. That's why you're lead diver." He smiled, thin and correct. "It's also why I'll know, before you do, if those roots start pulling. Good night."

He left. The tent flap settled. Sefa stood very still over the master chart, listening to her own heartbeat, and understood with total clarity that she had just been warned, recruited, and threatened in the space of four sentences, and that Brase considered all three to be the same sentence.

She finished the progress log. She wrote *all systems nominal* in her tidy hand, which was true of everything except her.

* * *

That night she couldn't sleep, so she did what her grandmother had done on sleepless nights: she opened the tin box.

The lantern in her bunk cabin was low. Around her, the advance crew slept in the bunks above and below, breathing the close air, dreaming whatever salvage crews dreamed. Sefa sat wrapped in a blanket with the box on her knees and took out the letters one at a time, reading the ones she could read—her grandmother's later letters were in trade-common, written to a sister who'd fled to Corvane—and skipping gently over the ones in old Karstian script, which she couldn't read yet, though she'd started learning, secretly, from a primer bought under a bookseller's counter.

Letter nineteen. She'd read it a dozen times. It was dated the spring before the erasure, and it said:

*They are selling the maps, little bird. Our own surveys. Men I trained with are carrying our lake basins to foreign buyers and calling it science. Amalya says stay out of it, Amalya is wise, but I helped draw those contours and I will not watch them become someone else's property. If the country must die, let the maps at least stay ours.*

Sefa read it twice. Then she sat with it, cold spreading through her chest, because she finally understood what she'd been doing for three nights in the survey tent.

She wasn't betraying Halvern. She was finishing letter nineteen. She was the granddaughter of a woman who'd sworn the maps would stay theirs, holding drafting film up to a lamp in the dark, keeping them twice—once for the house, once for the heart.

The lullaby rose in her throat before she could stop it. She clamped her mouth shut, eyes darting to the bunk above her. No stirring. Just the creak of the hull and the lap of river water against the pilings.

*The shepherd lost his flock to the river.* Forty years ago, a nation had gone into the water. Everyone said it was gone. But her grandmother had sung otherwise, night after night, to a child who hadn't known she was being taught a survival instruction: *what drowns turns to stars; what sinks is not lost; wait for the water to change.*

The glacier was retreating. The lake was giving up its dead. The mast of the Vesper Kestrel was breaking the surface for the first time in four decades—that was why Halvern was here, why the clock was running, why six tides mattered. The water was changing. And Halvern intended to be standing on the shore when the stars came up, holding a purchase order.

Sefa repacked the tin box. Then she took out the instrument case, removed the three sheets of film, and weighed her options the way she weighed dive plans—currents, risks, bottom time.

Mailing them was the problem. The river town had one post office, and Brase's people had been arriving in it for a week. Any clerk might be bought; any package might be logged. She needed a route that looked like nothing—a parcel with no flag on it, addressed somewhere dull.

Then she remembered the chandler's shop by the fish market, the one that shipped spare parts all along the river corridor. Dive shops shipped dive gear constantly, in plain brown paper, to outfitters in every town. No one read the labels on regulator parts. She could address the film inside a parts order to a hardware supplier in Corvane City—two streets from the diaspora archive, run by a man named Teodor Vasch whose brother was the archive's night custodian. She'd drunk tea in that shop last spring. She'd heard the brothers argue, affectionately, about whether Karstia had ever really existed or whether people had dreamed it collectively out of homesickness.

She'd send the stars by parcel post.

* * *

At dawn she walked to the chandler's with a genuine parts list in one hand and the film sewn into the lining of a spare dive glove in the other. The morning was gray and warm-smelling, monsoon-adjacent, the river running high and brown and muscular. Gulls argued over the fish market. Somewhere upstream, thunder muttered like a bureaucrat clearing his throat.

Teodor Vasch was behind the counter, exactly as she remembered: small, quick-eyed, hands stained with grease. He took her parts list, quoted a price, and began wrapping regulators in newspaper without any change of expression while she spoke, softly, facing the shelf of O-rings.

"There's a glove in my bag. Left one. It needs to go in the shipment to your brother's supplier in Corvane. Same parcel."

His hands didn't pause. "Fragile contents?"

"Stars," she said. "Handle gently."

Something moved behind his eyes—recognition, or memory, or both. He'd grown up singing that lullaby too; everyone her age in the scattered towns had, without knowing what it was. He tied the parcel string, stamped the label, and said, in the tone of a man discussing weather, "My brother keeps the archive open late on Thursdays. In case anyone's research runs long."

"Tell him the research is coming home," Sefa said.

She walked out into the gray light with her hands empty and her heart hammering like a fist on a door. Behind her, the parcel went onto the mail boat with a hundred others, anonymous, brown-papered, bound upriver toward a basement full of songs and scripts and pressed flowers, where three sheets of film would arrive carrying the exact coordinates of a drowned ship that a dead country had once owned the right to map.

By Thursday, the archive would have what Halvern had. By Friday, elders in five diaspora towns would know where the Vesper Kestrel lay. Whatever happened next—whatever Brase salvaged, patented, sold—the truth would have witnesses who couldn't be bought, because they'd already paid everything there was to pay, forty years ago, in a currency called home.

* * *

The fleet sailed on the morning tide after next, eleven vessels in line astern, engines low, riding deep with equipment. Sefa stood at the rail of the lead boat and watched the river town shrink behind them, the chandler's roof, the fish market awnings, the post office with its faded sign.

Brase joined her at the rail, hands clasped behind him, watching the same view.

"You know what I admire about you, Marun?" he said. "You never waste motion. Every hour you work, something gets done." He gazed out at the brown water. "I expect the next six tides to be the most productive of your career."

"Yes, sir," Sefa said.

Ahead, the river bent north toward the high country, toward cloud and cold and the glacial lakes, and beneath the deck plates her instrument case rode in its locker with its false bottom empty now, its work done, its secret already traveling faster than the fleet.

She hummed, barely, under the engine noise—four notes of a song about a shepherd and a river—and fixed her eyes on the horizon, where the weather was stacking up dark and early, and thought: *four tides, maybe less. The water is changing faster than anyone planned.*

And somewhere ahead of them, already in the hills, a woman with a bounty on her face was climbing toward the same lake with a dead man's coordinates in her pocket—and Sefa had let her go, and would have to decide, very soon, what she'd do the next time their paths crossed.
