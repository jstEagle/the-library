## 8. The Lake Below the Ice

The last hour of the climb, Freya stopped counting steps and started counting breaths. It was an old trick, one of the first her father ever taught her: when the air thins, when the legs lie, when the mind starts bargaining—narrow the world down to the width of your own lungs. One breath. The next. The scree field above her was the color of old bone, and the wind came off the ice in long flat shoves that tried to fold her into the mountain.

She had been walking for nine days. Nine days since Osk's boat was impounded at the Kess ferry, since she'd watched two Vessany customs cutters circle his rust-bucket trawler while he stood on the deck with his hands up and his mouth running, buying her time with noise. Nine days since she'd gone up the Vergha Pass alone with a pack she'd cut down twice to make the weight. Her boots were held together with wire and prayer. Her ribs ached from a fall on day four that she'd decided not to think about anymore.

But the pass had let her through. The mountain had let her through. And now, cresting the last moraine ridge as the sun dropped behind the western wall of the basin, Freya Amberdale stopped counting breaths and forgot how to take them.

The lake was below her, a mile of water the color of a bruise held up to light—deep teal shading into black where the depth fell away. It sat in a bowl of granite and ice, ringed by three glaciers that came down from the high col like slow white tongues. And there, a third of the way out from the near shore, breaking the surface at a slight, stubborn angle, was a spar of dark wood.

She knew what it was before her mind finished the word. *Mast.*

The Vesper Kestrel had been underwater for forty years. Every account, every rumor, every drunk story in every river town had agreed on that: the airship went down in the high lakes in the last year of Karstia, and the ice took it, and the ice kept it. But ice keeps nothing forever. She could see it now—the near glacier had retreated, a raw crescent of exposed rock and gray till marking where its toe had been when the maps were drawn, when her father's letters were still arriving. Forty years of warming, a few hundred meters of retreat. And at some point in the last season, maybe the last month, the lake had risen or the ice had thinned or the currents had shifted, and the Kestrel's mast had come up for air like a drowned man's hand.

*That's why they're coming now,* she thought. *That's why Halvern chartered every boat on the river the same week.* Not documents. Not a hunch. The glacier itself had opened the grave, and the vultures had felt the ground move.

Freya stood on the moraine until the cold worked through her coat, and she let herself feel it—really feel it—for exactly as long as she'd allow. Awe first, huge and wordless, the kind that made her feel like a fleck of grit on a cathedral floor. And braided through it, tight as wire, dread. Because the prize was real. She could see it. Which meant everyone else could see it too, or would soon, and the first of six tides was already going out.

* * *

She made camp in the lee of a granite shelf two hundred meters above the shoreline, close enough to watch the water, high enough to see anyone coming. By the time the light failed she'd eaten cold noodles and half a brick of pressed grain, checked her gear twice, and built the small alcohol stove down inside a rock ring so the flame wouldn't show. The temperature was already falling through freezing. In four or five days the lake's margins would start skinning with ice. After that, diving became a different sport entirely—the kind you did once.

She got out the logbook fragment and read it again by stove-light, though she could have recited it. *Vesper Kestrel, Karstian Merchant Air Registry 114-K, lost with cargo, position withheld by order of the Crown Salvage Office.* And beneath the printed line, in the slanted, impatient hand she'd have known anywhere on earth: a set of coordinates, and one word. *Wait.*

Her father had written *wait* on coordinates for a wreck he'd officially had nothing to do with. She'd turned that word over for two weeks now and it still wouldn't sit flat. Wait for what? Wait for whom? A man who'd been missing for twenty years, writing instructions to no one, sealing them in a chest that a river was supposed to keep for another century?

The lake made a sound then—a deep, glassy groan that rolled across the basin like a whale calling under the floor of the world. Freya's whole body went still. It was the ice out on the water, shifting, settling, the glacier calving somewhere in the dark. She'd heard that sound before, as a child, on expeditions she'd been too young to be on. Her father used to say the high lakes talked in their sleep. She used to think it was a joke. Sitting alone above a mile of black water with a dead nation's seal in her pocket, it didn't feel like a joke anymore.

She slept in shifts, twenty minutes at a time, and in the gray of the next morning she went down to scout.

* * *

The shoreline was a jumble of granite slabs and stranded ice—bergy bits that had calved off the glacier toe and run aground, melting into the gravel with a constant quiet hiss. The water at the margin was so clear it looked shallow, and then three meters out it simply stopped being water and became depth, a vertical wall of teal going down and down into nothing. Freya crouched at the edge and let her eyes adjust.

She could see the wreck.

Not all of it—the light didn't reach that far—but enough. The Kestrel had come to rest on a shelf of the lakebed that sloped up toward the glacier side, and the retreating ice had changed the currents, and the currents had done what currents do: scoured the silt away from her high side. The hull's upper curve emerged from the gray like the back of a sleeping animal, and along it, still fastened after forty years, ran a line of brass fittings catching what little light fell that deep. The mast rose from it at its stubborn angle, and rigging—wire, not rope, wire lasted—fanned down from the crosstrees and hung in the water like the strands of some enormous drowned instrument.

An airship's gondola, a hull of doped canvas over alloy ribs, a keel of timber and steel. Forty years in water just above freezing, under pressure, under ice. And still she lay there, whole enough to recognize. Freya felt her throat tighten. Ships were supposed to be broken by this. The Kestrel looked less sunk than *kept*.

She forced herself to work. Awe was a luxury; inventory was survival.

The mast itself was the obvious landmark—she fixed its bearing against the western glacier's shoulder and paced it off against the shoreline features. The hull lay maybe two hundred meters out, on a heading she could swim on a single compass line. Depth: she couldn't know without sounding, but the scour pattern and the light told her the hull top was deep enough that free-diving was a joke and hard-hat work was a death wish. She'd need her rebreather rig, the compact one she'd bought from Ivo Renk along with everything else that had emptied her account. Bottom time at that depth, cold water, altitude-adjusted tables because they were at nearly three thousand meters and every table she'd memorized was written for sea level. Everything would be against her. That was fine. Everything was always against her.

She walked the shore for two hours and found three things that mattered.

First: a dive point. A granite slab on the eastern shore formed a low ramp into the water, sheltered from the main current flow that came off the glacier melt on the west side. From that slab, the wreck lay almost directly across the deepest bowl of the lake, but a submerged ridge—she could see the water's color change over it—ran out toward the hull like a causeway. A swimmer could follow the ridge, stay shallower longer, save her bottom time for the wreck itself. It was, she thought with a grim little laugh, exactly the kind of route she'd have pioneered and gotten banned for.

Second: signs. Not fresh ones—old ones, weathered soft. A rusted piton hammered into the granite above the ramp, the kind of forged iron nobody manufactured anymore. A flattened patch of ground back from the water where a tent had stood long enough to kill the moss in a tent-shaped ghost. And half-buried in the gravel near the slab, when she dug with her fingers until her nails ached: a brass carabiner, corroded green, stamped with a maker's mark from a foundry that had closed the year Karstia fell.

Someone had been here before. Decades before. Someone who'd climbed down from the pass with expedition-grade gear and stood on this exact slab and looked at this exact water. She didn't let herself finish the thought. She put the carabiner in her pocket anyway, because she was apparently the kind of person who collected evidence against her own family now.

Third, and worst: she found the tide line. The lake was fed by melt and drained by a siphon channel under the eastern ice, and its level breathed on a slow cycle the old guides called the six tides—a rise and fall of nearly a meter over six days, tied to the glacier's melt pulse and the mountain's strange interior weather. She found it in the clean band of wet rock above the current waterline, and she found it *high*. The water was already going down. Tide one, ebbing.

She stood at the ramp and did the arithmetic she didn't want to do. The lake's breathing wasn't just a number on a chart; it moved the currents. As the level dropped, the outflow through the eastern siphon would strengthen, and the flow across the wreck site would go from a drift to a river. Somewhere around the third or fourth tide, diving that deep bowl would go from difficult to suicidal. And somewhere around the sixth, the first hard freeze would put a lid on the whole question until spring—by which time Halvern Deep Recovery would own the lake, the wreck, and whatever truth was rotting in the hold.

So the window wasn't six tides. It was two. Maybe three.

* * *

She spent the afternoon preparing, because preparation was the only prayer she believed in. She laid out the rebreather and checked every seal, every scrubber canister, every O₂ sensor, twice. She rigged her descent line with a spare anchor line, weighting it with rocks in a mesh bag because she had no grapnel. She cut a slate into a dive slate and wrote the wreck's bearing on it in grease pencil. She ate everything she could force down, because cold water at altitude burned fuel like a furnace, and she checked the sky every twenty minutes.

The sky was the problem. It had been building all day—the western wall of the basin wearing a cap of cloud that kept thickening, flattening, going the gray-white color that mountain people learn early means *business*. A storm up here wasn't rain. A storm up here was wind that could pin you in your tent for two days, snow that could erase your route, cold that could turn the lake margin into a rink overnight. If it broke before she dove, the window might close to one tide.

She thought about Halvern's fleet. Six tides, Osk had said—their target date. Which meant they'd planned around the same breathing water, the same freeze, the same melt pulse. Which meant their divers were better equipped than she was, and there would be more of them, and they'd have survey boats and sonar and a director somewhere counting money. But they also had to bring all of that up the same passes she'd just come down, and the passes were watched since the Amberdale incident, and every day of delay was a day she gained.

She lay in her shelter that night and listened to the lake talk. The groans came more often now, longer, the ice out past the mast shifting with the dropping water. Somewhere under all of it lay a ship full of answers, and somewhere in those answers lay her father's name, and somewhere around her father's name lay whatever word *wait* had been waiting for.

She thought about the carabiner in her pocket. She took it out and turned it in the stove-light. The maker's mark was half gone, but she knew the foundry—everyone in Karstian families knew it; it had stamped half the climbing gear in the country. Her father's own rack, the one she'd learned on, had carried the same mark.

*Someone reached the wreck before,* she thought. *Someone stood on that slab with good gear and looked at the water, and then left.* Left, or couldn't. And the logbook fragment had said *wait*, and the chest had spent forty years in a river, and none of it lined up into a story she could tell yet.

The wind picked up near midnight, moaning over the col from the west, and the first flakes came down fine and dry as ash. Freya watched them through the gap in her shelter and made her decision the way she made all of them now: fast, alone, and slightly terrified.

Tide two would turn in the morning. She'd dive on the turn, ride the slack water, follow the ridge out to the hull, and be back on the slab before the storm could decide to be serious. One dive. The mast, the hull, whatever the scour had opened. She wouldn't go into the wreck—not yet, not alone, not without knowing the currents. She'd just go down and look at the thing her whole life had been pointing at, and come back, and then decide how to spend the tides she had left.

She was almost asleep when she saw the light.

It came from the eastern shore—far down the lake, past the ramp, around the shoulder of the granite—a brief white stab, like a signal lamp shuttered twice, then gone. She came up on one elbow, heart slamming, and watched that stretch of shoreline for a long time. Nothing. Wind. Snow. The lake groaning in its sleep.

But the eastern shore was the shore the salvage fleet would come from. And no one else, as far as she knew, had any reason to be standing at the edge of this lake in the dark, flashing lamps at the water.

Freya lay back down slowly and did not close her eyes. Tide two was coming, and she was no longer sure she'd be diving alone.
