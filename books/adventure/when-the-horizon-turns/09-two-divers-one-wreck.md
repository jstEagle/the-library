## 9. Two Divers, One Wreck

Sefa Marun came down to the lake alone, which was against protocol and exactly how she liked it.

The survey beacons rode in a padded case on her back—six of them, brass-cored, each one a small lie that would tell Halvern Deep Recovery where the Vesper Kestrel slept. Plant them at the grid points, log the depths, transmit nothing until the fleet arrived. Corvel Brase wanted the wreck mapped before anyone else's eyes found it. He wanted it mapped *quietly*.

The glacier had other opinions about quiet. It calved while she was still a kilometer out, a sound like a mountain clearing its throat, and the report rolled across the lake and came back off the far shore twice. Sefa stood on the gray shingle and let it wash over her. Forty years of ice retreating, and now the water was giving things back. Her grandmother used to say the dead don't leave; they just wait for the weather to change.

She'd copied the charts two nights ago in the chart room, her hands steady, her heart anything but. The envelope with the archive's address sat in a mail satchel in Osk's river town, trusting to a smuggler's word. If Brase ever learned what she'd done, no lullaby would save her. So she did what she always did with fear: she put it in a dry bag and got to work.

The lake was wrong for diving and perfect for it. Meltwater cold enough to kill in twenty minutes, visibility that went from gold to green to black inside ten meters, and a bottom that had spent four decades undisturbed. She kitted up on the stones, checked her regulator twice, and slid in.

Cold closed around her like a fist. She breathed through it, the way her grandmother had taught her to breathe through grief—one long exhale, no wasted motion—and descended along the line she'd memorized.

The mast appeared first. A spar of dark wood rising out of the green gloom, furred with silt, tilted maybe fifteen degrees off true. Then the hull beneath it, a long shadow with ribs showing where planking had gone, and the whole vast shape of the airship lying on its side like a fallen bird. Sefa hung in the water above it and felt something move in her chest that had no professional name.

Her grandmother had been nine years old when Karstia stopped existing. Nine years old, and singing a song about a kestrel that flies so high the maps can't hold it. Sefa had thought for thirty years that the song was just a song.

She anchored the first beacon into the shingle forty meters off the bow. The second went by the mast. On the third, near the hull's torn flank, she saw the line.

A dive line, fresh nylon, running down from the surface and disappearing under the wreck's collapsed superstructure. Someone else was here. Someone else had beaten Halvern's fleet, beaten her, beaten everyone—and they were *under the ship right now*.

Sefa's hand went to the knife on her belt. Protocol said: identify, deter, report. Brase's voice said: find whoever took the chest before the fleet sails. Her grandmother's voice said nothing at all, just hummed, low, in a language that wasn't supposed to exist anymore.

She followed the line down.

* * *

Freya Amberdale had been under the Vesper Kestrel for an hour when the snag took her.

She'd found the breach on the starboard side—a tear in the hull wide enough to swim through if she exhaled just right—and worked her way into the corridor beyond, following her father's coordinates toward whatever the chest's logbook fragment had promised. The interior was a cathedral of silt and silence. Cabin doors hung open like mouths. A child's shoe sat in a drift of sediment, impossibly small, and Freya had made herself look at it and keep moving, because the answers were deeper in, and because stopping meant thinking, and thinking underwater was how divers died.

The line was her lifeline and her leash. She'd run it from the surface anchor down through the breach, paying it out behind her, and now, turning back from a collapsed bulkhead that blocked the forward hold, she felt it go taut against her ankle.

Not taut. *Caught.* Wrapped twice around something—a stanchion, a rail, some piece of the Kestrel's skeleton—and when she pulled to free it, the pull shifted forty years of settled wreckage above her.

A panel groaned somewhere in the dark. Silt bloomed around her like ink in water, and then there was no green light, no black distance, only white blindness and the line biting her ankle and her own breath loud as a drum in her ears.

Don't fight it. Her father's voice, from a training ground twenty years gone. Fight the water and the water wins. Feel for the wrap. One loop at a time.

She reached down. The silt was total. She found her ankle, found the line, found the wrap—and her fingers, numb with cold, clumsy with adrenaline, couldn't work the knot she'd tied too well. Her gauge read a third of a tank. The panic came anyway, rising like floodwater, and she rode it the way you ride any flood: by counting. Four breaths. Eight. Twelve.

That was when she saw the light.

A lamp beam cutting through the murk, swinging closer, and behind it a diver in gear too good to be a local and too new to be a ghost. The figure stopped an arm's length away. Through the fog of silt, Freya saw a face behind the mask—dark eyes, a jaw set hard—and saw those eyes go from her face to the line to her ankle, reading the situation in one pass the way only another working diver could.

The stranger didn't draw the knife. She drew a line cutter.

She pointed at Freya's ankle, then held up one finger: *one loop at a time*. Freya nodded. Together, blind and breathless, they worked the nylon free—the stranger cutting where cutting was needed, Freya unwinding where unwinding was possible—and the whole time Freya's gauge needle crept down and the silt refused to clear and the wreck creaked overhead like a ship deciding whether to become a grave again.

The last loop fell away. The stranger grabbed Freya's wrist, tapped her gauge—*low*—and pointed up.

They ascended together on the stranger's line, sharing the beam of one lamp through water that slowly, grudgingly, turned from white to green to the pale gold of surface light. When their heads broke the water, the sky was the color of pewter and the glacier was rumbling again, and Freya spat her regulator out and said the first thing that came into her head:

"You're Halvern."

* * *

"And you're the thief," Sefa said.

They'd swum for the shingle, shed fins and masks, and now stood three meters apart on the stones with the lake at their backs, breathing hard, sizing each other up. Sefa kept her knife sheathed and her weight balanced. The woman across from her was leaner than her bounty photo, wind-scoured, with the rope-scarred hands of someone who'd spent her life on vertical terrain. But the eyes matched. Freya Amberdale, blacklistee, guide of the Vergha Pass, the woman whose face was pinned to every salvage office wall between here and the coast.

"Thief implies an owner," Freya said. "Who owned the chest the river coughed up?"

"The nation that owns that wreck." Sefa jerked her chin at the mast, breaking the surface fifty meters out like a grave marker. "Or did. My employers seem to think ownership transfers to whoever has the biggest boat."

"Your employers want the logbook."

"My employers want everything. That's rather the point of employers." Sefa wrung out her hair, watching the woman watch her. Two days Brase had been hunting this ghost, and here she was, close enough to touch, alone except for whatever camp she'd hidden in the rocks. One radio call. One coded burst on the fleet's channel, and Sefa's contract bonus landed, and Halvern's divers swarmed this shoreline by tomorrow's tide.

Her thumb brushed the radio clipped to her vest. Freya saw the gesture and didn't flinch. That was the thing that unsettled Sefa—not the boldness, but the *tiredness* underneath it. This was a woman who'd already lost everything worth threatening.

"You're not calling it in," Freya said.

"Don't tell me my job."

"I'm telling you what you're doing. Your thumb's nowhere near the switch."

Sefa looked down at her own hand as if it belonged to someone else. The truth was she'd known since the second beacon. She'd seen the crest on the chest lid in the intelligence brief—double-headed kestrel, wings spread over a broken crown—and she'd heard her grandmother's voice singing in a kitchen that smelled of caraway and woodsmoke, in a country that existed only in songs now. And she'd looked at this woman's face in the bounty file and read the same story there: a family name buried alive, a daughter still digging.

"Why did you come alone?" Freya asked. "Fleet's not due for days."

"How do you know when the fleet is due?"

"Because I've been watching the lake for two tides, and nobody who plans a salvage operation lets a single diver plant beacons first. You're the advance. Which means the people behind you trust you." Freya stepped closer, over the wet stones, close enough that Sefa caught the smell of glacial water and woodsmoke on her. "And you haven't called them. So either you're very bad at your job, or you're very good at pretending to be."

The wind came off the ice field and pushed between them. Somewhere upslope, a rock ptarmigan complained and went silent.

"My grandmother was Karstian," Sefa said.

It came out before she'd decided to say it. Freya went very still.

"Lullabies," Sefa went on, hating how much she was giving away and unable to stop. "Songs about a kestrel too high for the maps to hold. She never told me what happened to it. She died before I thought to ask, and by then asking was illegal in three countries." She met Freya's eyes. "So no. I'm not calling it in. Not yet."

"Not yet is doing a lot of work in that sentence."

"It's all the sentence can carry." Sefa crouched, pulled the survey beacon from her kit, and set it on the stones between them like a chess piece. Brass core, Halvern stencil, a transmitter that would paint this whole basin for Corvel Brase's fleet. "You know what these are?"

"Beacons."

"Claims. Every one I plant is a legal pin in the map saying Halvern found it first. By the time anyone else arrives, the paperwork's done and the past belongs to a corporation." She turned the beacon over in her fingers. "I planted three before I found your line."

"And the other three?"

"In my bag. Unplanted." She stood. "I'm not on your side, Amberdale. Don't mistake me. I have a contract, a captain who watches me, and a director who reads people like cargo manifests. But whatever's in that wreck—whatever your father—" She stopped. Watched Freya's face change. "You didn't know he was crew."

It wasn't a question. Sefa had read the intelligence brief; she knew the logbook fragment named an officer. But the way this woman's jaw tightened said the knowing was new, or new-shaped, and it hurt.

"He was a passenger," Freya said quietly. "In every letter he ever wrote me. He was flying home."

"Then we're both about to learn what the water keeps." Sefa shouldered her kit. "The fleet sails on the next tide after tomorrow. Brase arrives with it—Corvel Brase himself, which tells you what he thinks is down there. You have until then to find whatever you came to find, and I have until then to decide which lie I tell him about these beacons." She paused at the tree line, half-turned. "There's a deep hold section aft, below the collapsed bulkhead. My charts show a structural void there the surveys never explained. If I were looking for a captain's safe, that's where I'd cut."

"Why are you telling me that?"

"Because my grandmother sang me to sleep in a dead language, and I'd rather the truth come up out of that lake in *your* hands than in his." She started up the slope, then stopped once more. "Amberdale. Your line work down there was sloppy. Tie off your wraps next time—or don't go back down at all."

"I'm going back down."

"I know," Sefa said. "So am I."

She climbed into the rocks and was gone, and Freya stood alone on the shingle with the mast rocking in the swell and the first spit of rain arriving out of the west, and understood that the race had just changed shape. It was no longer her against the fleet. It was the truth against the clock, and the clock had six tides left on it, and one of them had just ticked in her favor for reasons she didn't yet dare to trust.

Above the lake, unseen, Sefa keyed her radio and reported to Captain Voss in a flat, professional voice: beacons planted, site secured, no sign of the intruder. The lie went out clean across the water, and she watched the storm clouds build over the pass and thought: *Grandmother, I hope you were listening.*

Down on the shore, Freya checked her remaining air, re-rigged her line, and looked at the dark water where the Kestrel waited.

Two dives left. Maybe three. And somewhere below the collapsed bulkhead, in a void the surveys never explained, her father's last secret was holding its breath.
