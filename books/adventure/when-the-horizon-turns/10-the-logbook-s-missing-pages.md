## 10. The Logbook's Missing Pages

The hull of the Vesper Kestrel was a cathedral that had drowned.

Freya hung in the green half-light, her lamp beam sweeping across ribs of steel furred with forty years of silt and pale growth. The airship lay on its side in the deep channel below the ice shelf, masts down, envelope long since rotted to lace, but the gondola frame had held—Karstian shipwrights built for mountains even when they were building for sky. She could feel it in the bones of the wreck: stubbornness, engineering as a form of pride.

Her dive line ran taut above her to the crack in the ice where Sefa waited, regulator ready, watching the gauge on Freya's tether like a woman holding a rope over a cliff. They'd agreed on the signals. Three tugs: come up. Two: trouble. One: keep going.

Freya gave herself one more minute at the breach point, where the portside plating had split along a seam like a zipper pulled by the glacier's slow hands. Cold pressed through her suit until it felt less like temperature and more like attention. The lake wanted to know what she'd come for.

*The safe,* she told herself. *Get the safe. Get out.*

The captain's cabin would be aft, under what had been the deck—now a wall of collapsed bunks and cable drums fused into one grey mass. She finned along the corridor, lamp carving a tunnel through the murk, past a galley where a pot still hung from its hook, past a doorway framed in brass that someone had polished every day right up until the day they couldn't anymore.

She found the cabin by its door, which was the only one still hanging true.

Inside, the room had folded in on itself. A desk bolted to the floor stood upright against what was now the ceiling, drawers open, their contents scattered in a slow-motion explosion four decades old. Papers everywhere—or what had been papers. Most had gone to pulp, a soft grey sediment that stirred at the first push of her fin and hung in the water like breath.

The safe was under the desk. Bolted down, as she'd guessed. Karstian captains didn't trust weather, borders, or anyone.

It took three swings of her pry bar to break the lock. Each impact rang through the hull and out into the lake, and each time she flinched, certain the sound carried all the way up to Halvern's survey beacons, to Brase's divers sealing the upper channel, to everyone who wanted this wreck more than she did. But the water swallowed the noise, and nothing came down the corridor but silt.

The door swung open on a darkness that wasn't empty.

A canvas-wrapped bundle, tarred and tied. A brass cylinder, sealed at both ends with wax that had gone the color of old bone. And beneath them, wedged into the corner as if someone had shoved it in at the last second, a logbook bound in oiled leather, its cover stamped with the crest she knew better than her own heartbeat—the mountain-and-kestrel of Karstia.

Freya's hands were steady. That surprised her. Somewhere beneath the cold and the neoprene and the forty years of waiting, some part of her had expected her hands to shake when she finally touched this. Instead they moved with the same competence she brought to a rope anchor or a crevasse rescue: check, secure, stow. She zipped the logbook into the hard case clipped at her chest, tucked the cylinder and bundle into her mesh bag, and checked her air.

Half. Time to go.

She gave the cabin one last sweep of the lamp. On the bulkhead, half-hidden behind a drift of silt, a photograph hung in a frame gone green—a crew lined up on a gondola rail, squinting into sun, names inked on the matting in a hand too blurred to read. She memorized the faces instead. One of them might be her father, young, in a uniform she'd never seen him wear.

One tug on the line. *Keep going.* She was already going.

* * *

Sefa met her at the ice hole with wide eyes and a question she didn't ask out loud, because questions were dangerous up here where the wind carried sound across the whole basin. She hauled Freya out, wrapped her in the shelter tarp, and got a thermos of broth into her hands before she said anything at all.

"Well?" Sefa said.

Freya worked the case open with numb fingers. The logbook sat inside, dry, dark, intact. The leather had cracked along the spine but held. She could smell it even through the cold—old paper, tar, something faintly metallic, like the wreck itself had soaked into it.

"Captain's safe," Freya said. "There's more. A sealed tube, a wrapped bundle I haven't opened. But the book—"

She opened it.

The pages were dense, ruled, written in a quick slanted hand that shifted twice in the first twenty entries—three different officers keeping the record, maybe, or one man whose handwriting deteriorated as whatever happened to the Vesper Kestrel began to happen. Dates ran back eleven months before the sinking. Routine stuff at first: cargo manifests, weather, engine hours, a passenger list annotated in margins with small dry judgments. *The Vessany trade attache snores. The Karstian consul drinks his weight in plum brandy and calls it diplomacy.*

Sefa read over her shoulder, close enough that Freya could feel her breath. Neither of them spoke. The wind moved the tarp. Somewhere out on the ice, a pressure crack fired like a rifle and rolled away south.

Then the entries changed.

Eleven months out, the tone tightened. Cargo began arriving at night. Sealed crates, no manifests, loaded under guard. The captain wrote: *Received additional freight, 14 units, unlisted. Orders from the Ministry say only this: too true to float.*

"Too true to float," Sefa repeated softly. "What does that mean?"

"I don't know yet." But Freya's stomach had already begun to understand. You don't sink cargo because it's valuable. You sink it because it's dangerous—to someone specific. Because if it ever surfaced, it would say something somebody couldn't afford to hear.

She turned pages faster now. The last month of the Vesper Kestrel's life compressed into short, strained entries. The route bent north, away from any scheduled lane, toward the high lakes. Crew morale noted twice, both times badly. And then, six days before the sinking date her father's letter had given her, an entry that stopped her breathing:

*Crew amendment. Officer A. Amberdale transferred aboard at Vergha landing, per Ministry directive. Manifest amended. He is not to be listed among passengers.*

Freya read it again. Read it a third time. The letters didn't rearrange themselves. *Officer A. Amberdale. Crew—not passenger.*

"That's—" Sefa started.

"My father." Freya's voice came out flat, stripped. "That's my father's name. In the crew log. Six days before this ship went down."

She had built her whole life on a different version. Her father the guide, the expedition leader, the man whose name was on the gear stamped AMBERDALE EXPEDITIONS, the man who vanished into the high country when she was nineteen and left her letters full of ciphers and coordinates and love. The blacklist, the banned routes, the incident nobody would explain—all of it she'd framed as consequence, fallout, the price other people made her pay for a mystery he'd left behind.

But this was different. This put him *inside*. Not chasing the wreck. Not mourning it. *Aboard.* Under Ministry directive, manifest amended, hidden among the crew. Six days before the ship sank in a lake no scheduled airship had any business visiting.

"He helped sink it," Freya said. The words tasted like lake water. "He was here. He was part of this."

"You don't know that."

"The entry says crew, Sefa. Crew don't get listed by accident." She flipped forward, hunting, needing the next page to contradict her. It didn't. Four days out from the end: *Amberdale reports the freight secured. He is the only one who asks the right questions, which is either a comfort or a warning.* Then, two days out: *All hands briefed on final mooring. Amberdale has requested to remain aboard through the operation. Captain grants it. God help us all, I think he means to see it done properly.*

*See it done properly.* Her father hadn't been a victim of the sinking. He hadn't stumbled onto it. He'd asked to stay. He'd volunteered for the last act of a ship being murdered in a glacial lake, and the captain had blessed it with a prayer.

Freya closed the book. Opened it again, because closing it felt like drowning and opening it felt like breathing, and she needed to breathe.

"The rest," she said. "Where are the rest?"

She went through it fast now, page by page, thumbing toward the final entries—and hit the gap. Between the second-to-last entry and the blank pages at the back, a ragged edge. Then another. Then a block of them, torn out in a hurry, the stubs uneven, ripped rather than cut. Someone had stood exactly where she was sitting, in exactly this kind of cold, and torn out the ending of the story with shaking or urgent hands.

She counted the stubs. Nine pages. Nine pages missing from the final week of the Vesper Kestrel's life—including, almost certainly, the entry that would have described the sinking itself, and everything the ship had been carrying that was *too true to float*.

"Someone got here first," Sefa said quietly.

"Not Brase. His fleet hasn't dived the hold—he doesn't have the location, we've watched his beacons, they're all wrong-side of the channel." Freya turned the logbook over, examining the spine, the tear edges. Old damage. The leather around the stubs had relaxed decades ago; the paper had yellowed evenly. "This wasn't recent. This was years ago. Maybe... maybe right after the sinking."

"Who?"

And there it was—the answer arriving before she wanted it, the way answers do when you've spent your whole life avoiding the question. Who else knew the coordinates? Who else had letters with ciphers and a crest? Who else had the skills to reach a wreck in a high glacial lake, alone, without a fleet?

"Amberdale Expeditions gear," Freya said. "In the cave at Vergha Pass. Stamped, decades old. He came back. Or he never really left." She stared at the torn stubs. "He tore out the pages. My father tore out the ending."

"Why would he tear out the ending of his own—"

"Because the ending named people." Freya heard herself saying it, the logic assembling itself with the terrible speed of a rockfall. "You don't tear out nine pages unless those nine pages are worth more than the book. Names, orders, evidence—whoever the *too true to float* cargo implicated, the proof of it was in these pages, and he took it out and hid it somewhere, and then he disappeared, and I spent fifteen years thinking he abandoned me when actually he was—"

She stopped. Her hands had started shaking after all. Late, but thorough.

Sefa put her hand over Freya's, on the closed logbook, and didn't say anything for a while. Out on the lake, the light was changing—the flat white glare of early afternoon sliding toward the blue-grey that meant the temperature was dropping and the ice channel wouldn't stay open much longer.

"Your father," Sefa said finally, carefully, "was crew on a ship that was sunk on purpose, carrying cargo someone wanted dead, six days after he boarded under a false listing. And then the pages about it vanished, and so did he, and your family name got buried with the ship." She exhaled. "Freya, that's not a man who abandoned you. That's a man who was running from the same people who blacklisted you."

"Or protecting them." Freya pulled her hand free, gently. "Or hiding what he did. We have nine missing pages and a head full of theories, and every theory makes him someone different. Hero. Coward. Criminal. Father." She looked out at the ice, at the mast of the Vesper Kestrel breaking the surface a quarter mile east like a grave marker nobody had visited. "I came here to clear his name. Now I don't know which name I'm clearing."

* * *

They cached the gear in the rocks above the shoreline as the light died, wrapping the logbook, the sealed cylinder, and the tarred bundle in the shelter tarp and weighting it under stone slabs against the wind. Freya saved the bundle for last. Her fingers hovered over the knot.

"Open it?" Sefa asked.

Freya untied it.

Inside: clothing, rotted soft. A tin cup. And folded into a waterproof sleeve, a single sheet of paper, dry as ash, written in the quick slanted hand she recognized from the logbook margins—but this wasn't the captain's writing. This was newer. This was her father's.

Not a letter. A note, brief, left in a safe he must have known only he would ever return to:

*If you're reading this, the lake gave up its dead. I'm sorry for what the finding will cost you. The truth is not in the ship. I moved it. Follow the equipment. —A.A.*

Freya read it three times. The wind picked at the tarp corners. Down the slope, the lake went dark by degrees, and the first stars came out over the ridge where, two valleys north, her father's old camp was either a ruin or an answer.

"He moved it," she said. "The pages. Before he vanished, he came back here and moved the truth out of the ship and hid it somewhere else." She looked up at Sefa, and despite everything—the shaking hands, the curdled inheritance, the father dissolving and reforming in her mind with every page—she felt the old thing rise in her chest, the thing that had gotten her banned from two countries and kept her alive in both of them.

Not grief. Direction.

"Follow the equipment," she repeated. "Above the lake. His camp." She checked the sky, the wind, the thin crescent of moon that would give them just enough light to travel by. "Three tides left before the monsoon, and Brase's divers start sealing the channel at dawn. We move tonight."

Sefa shouldered her pack. "And when we find the pages?"

Freya zipped her father's note into her jacket, over her heart, where the last letter had lived for fifteen years.

"Then I finally find out who my father was," she said. "And God help whoever decided that was theirs to sell."
