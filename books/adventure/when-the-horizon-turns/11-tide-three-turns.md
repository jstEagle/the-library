## 11. Tide Three Turns

The fleet came up the lake road two days early, and Sefa knew it before she saw it because the birds left.

She was on the eastern shore with a survey slate in her hand, pretending to take depth readings at a point where Halvern's charts said nothing lived. The water was the color of old bottle glass, and under it, forty meters down, the Vesper Kestrel waited with its secrets. She had been standing there for an hour doing arithmetic that had nothing to do with depth: how long until the main fleet arrived, how many tides Freya Amberdale had left to find what she was looking for, how many lies Sefa could reasonably tell Corvel Brase before he stopped believing them.

The answer to the last one, apparently, was none.

She heard the fleet before she saw it—diesel engines downshifting on the switchback road, the particular groan of heavy barges being winched off trailers. Then they came around the shoulder of the mountain: three salvage barges, two dive tenders, a command vessel with *HALVERN DEEP RECOVERY* painted along its flank in letters you could read from orbit. Cranes folded like sleeping insects. Floodlights already mounted. A floating city built for one purpose.

And at the rail of the command vessel, hands clasped behind his back like a man admiring scenery he owned, stood Corvel Brase.

Sefa's stomach did something slow and cold. Two days early. She'd had a schedule in her head—a clean one, built around the fleet's official arrival, built around the six-tide window before monsoon landfall. Two days early meant her margin wasn't a margin anymore. It meant every hour she'd spent copying charts and mailing them north to the diaspora archive in Vessany City was now an hour someone might ask her to account for.

She looked down at her slate. The numbers on it were real enough. She'd learned that much from Brase: always have real numbers. A lie wrapped around truth holds; a lie wrapped around nothing sinks.

She packed the slate away and started walking toward the landing point, because the worst thing she could do was be found not looking for them.

* * *

Brase received her in the command cabin, which smelled of coffee and new electronics, and offered her a chair the way a man offers a chair when he has already decided you won't be sitting in it long.

"Sefa Marun." He said her name carefully, both parts of it, the way people did when they'd done their homework. "Lead diver. Karstian grandmother. Top of your certification class twice. Resigned from the Vessany Coastal Survey over an ethics dispute nobody will describe to me in detail—which I've always taken as a compliment."

"Most people just check my references," Sefa said.

"I am most people's employer." He poured coffee into two tin cups without asking, pushed one across the chart table toward her, and didn't drink his own. That was the thing about Brase that unsettled people who worked for him: he never performed comfort. Every gesture was load-bearing. "You've been busy since the advance team dropped you. Show me."

She unrolled her charts on the table—the basin survey, the dive points she'd marked, the ice channel readings. Real work, all of it, done in the hours she wasn't doing the other work. She watched his eyes move over it and tried to read him the way you read water: pressure underneath, surface telling you only half the story.

"The mast is breaking the surface at the north inlet," she said. "Glacier retreat exposed it sometime in the last eighteen months. That's why the wreck is findable now and wasn't in forty years. The hull sits at thirty-eight meters, intact as far as sonar shows. Ice channel on the east side gives us a dive window of maybe four hours a day before refreeze."

"And the chest?"

"Gone before we got here. Salvaged by person or persons unknown. We recovered fragments of the packing crate downstream in Vessany territory."

"We know." He said it mildly. Mildly was worse than sharply. "We also know the person. Or persons." He turned a tablet around on the table so the screen faced her. On it was a photograph, grainy, taken from distance: a woman with cropped hair and river mud to the knee, hauling a bronze-stamped chest through reeds. Freya Amberdale, caught mid-stride by some border camera, looking exactly like what she was—someone running with something that belonged to history.

"You know her," Brase said.

"I've never met her."

"You know *of* her. The blacklist. The Amberdale incident. It's in the briefing file, and it's in your personnel notes—you requested the borderlands assignment specifically." He let that sit. Then, in the same mild voice: "You also mailed a package from Vessany City nine days ago. Post office on Canner Street. Weight, four hundred grams. Contents, per the postal manifest you were careless enough to sign: cartographic reproductions."

The cabin was very quiet. Somewhere below deck, a compressor cycled. Sefa kept her face still, kept her breathing even, the way she did at thirty meters when something went wrong with the plan and panic was the one thing guaranteed to kill you faster than the water.

"A survey archive," she said. "Standard practice. Duplicate records off-site in case of equipment loss."

"To the Karstian Memory House," Brase said. "A diaspora archive run out of a converted chapel by volunteers. Charming place. I visited it myself, years ago, when I was building this project." He finally drank his coffee. "Here's what interests me, Marun. You're good. You're careful. You committed one act of sentiment in an otherwise flawless operation, and you did it early, when it could still be corrected. Which tells me you haven't decided whose side you're on yet. People who've decided don't make mistakes that small. They make big ones, or none."

Sefa looked at him. He was maybe fifty-five, silver at the temples, a face built for boardrooms and hearings—handsome the way a well-drafted contract is handsome. Nothing cruel in it. That was the trap of Corvel Brase. Cruelty you could brace against. He was something rarer: a man who genuinely believed everything he did was legitimate, because legitimacy, to him, was whatever paperwork said it was.

"You're going to fire me," she said.

"I'm going to promote you." He smiled, and it was a professional smile, calibrated, warm at exactly the temperature warmth is supposed to be. "Sit down, Marun. Let me tell you what this company is actually doing here, since you've been loyal enough to deserve the truth and disloyal enough to need it."

* * *

He walked her through it with charts of his own—corporate charts, slick and color-coded, the same lake rendered as an asset.

"The Vesper Kestrel carried the Karstian treasury archive when it went down. Not gold—paper. Records. Forty years ago, when Karstia was dissolved, a great deal of documentation needed to disappear. Who signed what. Who sold what. Who was paid, and by whom, and through which banks in which countries that prefer not to be named." He tapped the hull outline on his chart. "That ship is a vault full of other people's crimes, sitting in cold fresh water, perfectly preserved, and until eighteen months ago it was under sixty feet of ice where no one would ever look."

"And Halvern wants to recover it," Sefa said. "For scholarship."

"For *custody*." He said the word like he was handing her something heavy. "There are eleven governments, three banks, and more families than I can count whose entire legal position rests on those documents staying lost. If they surface in a courtroom, half the border treaties of the last forty years become renegotiable overnight. If they surface in *our* archive—" he spread his hands, "—they surface under our copyright. Our access terms. Our licensing. History becomes a product, Marun, and products have customers, and customers can be managed. The alternative is chaos. What we're offering the world isn't censorship. It's *order*. Someone has to hold these things responsibly. We're simply the ones equipped to."

"And the descendants?" Sefa heard her own voice go flat. "The people whose grandparents' country that was? The ones who sing the songs and keep the names? Do they get a licensing agreement too?"

"They get stability," Brase said, without a flicker. "Which is what they've been living on for forty years anyway. Look—I've read your file. Your grandmother came out of Karstia in the last boats. You grew up on the lullabies. You think I'm the villain of this piece because I wear a corporate badge instead of a folk costume." He leaned forward. "But ask yourself who benefits if those documents come up wild. Not the villagers. Not the diaspora kids learning songs from grandmothers. The first government or syndicate that grabs them uses them as blackmail, and the second one burns them, and then your grandmother's country is erased *twice*—once by treaty, once by fire. I'm offering controlled release. Authentication, publication of the harmless material, sealed custody of the dangerous material. With your name on the custody protocol, Marun. Lead custodian. Your credentials, your community standing, your face on the process. The Karstian diaspora trusts exactly one person in this operation, and it's you."

The coffee had gone cold in her hands. She set it down carefully, because her fingers wanted to do something else with the cup.

"You know I tipped the archive," she said. "And instead of firing me, you want to make me the public face of locking the past in your basement."

"I want to make you the reason it doesn't end in blood." He straightened. "But yes. Those are your options, as I see them. Help me build a custody framework the diaspora can live with—or watch my divers raise that hull on my schedule, patent the contents under Halvern's name, and sell access back to the very governments that killed Karstia in the first place. They'll pay, Marun. They'll pay *enormously*, and they'll sleep fine afterward, and your grandmother's lullaby will still exist, sung to children who will never learn why their family name appears nowhere in any record from before the dissolution." He paused. "Or option three. There's always an option three. You go to the authorities, or the press, or the Memory House, and tell them Corvel Brase is a monster. And by the time anyone with jurisdiction reads your letter, the documents are in a bonded vault in a country with no extradition, and you're a disgraced diver with an ethics dispute on her record and a very specific memory of signing a postal manifest. I don't threaten people, Marun. I just describe consequences accurately. It's a habit from the industry."

Sefa stood very still. Outside the cabin windows, the barges were anchoring in formation, cranes unfolding, floodlights testing themselves against the grey afternoon—one bright stutter of light across the water, like the lake blinking.

She thought about Freya Amberdale somewhere on the mountain above them, alone, carrying a chest and a grudge and a dead father's handwriting. She thought about the Memory House chapel, the volunteer archivist with ink-stained fingers who had written back to her within a week—*we have been waiting forty years for someone to send us anything at all.* She thought about her grandmother's hands making bread, humming, never once saying the word Karstia aloud, as if saying it might summon the men who had taken it.

And she understood, with diver's clarity, what Brase was really offering. Not a choice between loyalty and betrayal. A choice between betrayals. He'd built the options so that every exit led through him.

"Three tides," she said. "The monsoon makes landfall in three, maybe four. Your dive schedule doesn't work unless the weather holds, and it won't. You need me in the water more than I need your custody protocol."

Something moved behind Brase's eyes—approval, maybe, or appetite. "So you'll consider it."

"I'll consider it," Sefa said. "I consider everything. It's a habit from the industry."

He laughed, genuinely, and for a moment he looked almost human. "Take the evening. The advance dive brief is at dawn. I'd like your recommendation on the primary penetration route by then." He held the cabin door open for her, courteous to the last, and added, softly, in the doorway, close enough that only she could hear:

"Oh—and Marun. The woman on the mountain. If you see her again, remember that whatever she's carrying, it's worth more to me intact than to either of us destroyed. And I reward discretion. Ask anyone I've ever bought."

* * *

She walked the shore in the blue hour, past the moored tenders, past divers checking gear by lamplight, and stopped where the scree gave way to the first true slope of the mountain. Above her, the glacier hung in the dark like a held breath, and beyond it, invisible, the north inlet where a mast broke the surface of black water.

Dawn brief. Primary penetration route. Her recommendation, her choice, on the record, in front of Brase and every diver in the fleet.

If she recommended the east channel, the dive would be fast and safe and the fleet would be inside the hull within two days—and whatever Freya hadn't yet found would be found by men with patents in their pockets. If she recommended the long western approach, through the unstable ice shelf, she'd buy time—days, maybe—but she'd be putting her own divers into water that could kill them, and Brase would know exactly what she'd done and why.

Either way, she was choosing. Either way, someone paid.

Far above, near the glacier line, a light flickered once among the rocks. Small. Deliberate. Three flashes, pause, two flashes—not a signal any Vessany patrol used, but one every child of the diaspora knew, because it was the signal Karstian climbers had used on the high passes for a hundred years, passed down in songs and climbing schools and grandmothers' stories.

*Someone is up there. Someone is watching.*

Sefa stood in the dark with the fleet's floodlights burning behind her and the old signal blinking above, and understood that the third tide had turned, and whatever she did at dawn, there would be no surfacing from it unchanged.
