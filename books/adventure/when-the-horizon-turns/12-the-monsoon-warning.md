## 12. The Monsoon Warning

The buoy sang at dawn.

Freya heard it from the tent she'd pitched in the lee of a granite shoulder above the lake—a thin, repeating tone carried across the water like a struck glass. She'd been listening for it for two days without admitting that was what she was doing. Now it came, and her body knew before her mind did: the pitch had changed. Higher. Faster. The rhythm of bad news.

She crawled out into cold light and looked down at the lake. The Vesper Kestrel's mast stood out of the water where it had stood for forty years, invisible until the glacier's retreat bared it—now leaning slightly east, furred with old ice-scour, absurd and lonely as a fence post in the middle of nowhere. Beyond it, near the far shore, one of Halvern's weather buoys bobbed on its tether, blinking amber.

Amber meant watch. Red meant run. The buoy was halfway between, cycling faster than it had yesterday.

Sefa was already up. She stood at the waterline with her hands jammed in her jacket pockets, watching the buoy the way you'd watch a stranger walking toward your house. She'd arrived three days ago with survey beacons and a Halvern work order she'd since stopped pretending to follow, and Freya still hadn't entirely decided whether trusting her was courage or stupidity. Probably both. Most of her decisions lately were both.

"It changed in the night," Sefa said without turning. "I timed it. Forty-one seconds between pulses yesterday. Thirty-three now."

"Meaning?"

"Meaning the pressure gradient's steepening." Sefa finally turned. She had her grandmother's face under the salvage-fleet tan—the same flat cheekbones, the same way of going very still when something mattered. "I've read these buoys my whole career. That tone isn't 'watch.' It's 'hurry.'"

Freya looked south, past the lake, past the pass they'd come through, toward weather none of them could see yet. The sky up here was clean and hard and blue, which somehow made it worse. Clean skies over the Vergha were the sky lying to you.

"Four tides," she said.

Sefa didn't argue. That was the thing about her Freya was learning to trust: she didn't waste breath defending numbers that were already dead. Six tides had been the fleet's planning window, the margin Osk had promised passage against, the comfortable arithmetic of people who thought the monsoon would keep its appointment like a bureaucrat. Weather didn't keep appointments. Weather came when the pressure told it to, and the pressure had just changed its mind.

"Four tides," Sefa agreed. "Maybe less if the jet stream kinks. After landfall this basin becomes a funnel. Every drainage from the icefield dumps into this lake at once, and the surface layer goes to slush, and visibility underwater goes to nothing. You couldn't dive it with a searchlight and a prayer."

"So we dive today."

"We dive today." Sefa said it like signing something.

* * *

They spent the morning preparing the way Freya had once prepared climbs—with a checklist recited aloud, item by item, because voices kept hands honest. Two sets of dive gear salvaged and cannibalized: Sefa's professional rig, warm-water rated and patched with parts Freya had stripped from Osk's impounded boat before the border patrol took it. Freya's own kit, older, assembled across three countries' worth of black-market outfitters, held together by stubbornness and good knots.

The deep hold was the problem. They'd mapped the wreck across four dives now—the crew deck, the flooded corridor, the captain's cabin where Freya had cracked the safe and found the logbook gutted, its most important pages torn out in a hurry by someone who'd had exactly the same idea she was having, decades earlier. The hold proper lay beneath, down through a collapsed cargo hatch at thirty-eight meters, in water so cold it pulled heat through neoprene like a debt collector. At altitude, at this depth, their bottom time was minutes. Their margin for error was thinner than the ice that had hidden this ship for a generation.

"The torn pages," Sefa said, while Freya checked her regulator for the third time. "You're sure they're not just lost? Paper rots. Forty years in cold water—"

"They weren't in the safe to rot." Freya sat back on her heels. "The safe was sealed. Silt line inside the door was undisturbed except where the logbook lay. Whoever tore those pages did it before the ship went down, or within hours after. And they took them somewhere dry, because paper that matters gets kept dry. My father taught me that." Her voice caught on *my father*, and she let it, because hiding it cost more energy than spending it.

"A. Amberdale," Sefa said quietly. "Crew, not passenger."

"Crew, not passenger." Freya had turned that phrase over so many times it had gone smooth as river stone. Her father hadn't been aboard the Vesper Kestrel by accident. He'd helped sink it. He'd torn the pages naming the men who sold Karstia—and he'd done it, the journal said, to protect his family, because those names belonged to men whose grandchildren now ran Vessany, and truth like that doesn't stay buried, it metastasizes. He'd chosen exile. He'd chosen the blacklist that had eaten her life whole, without ever telling her why.

She wasn't ready to forgive him. She might never be. But she needed those pages, because pages could be verified, and memory—she glanced at Sefa, who had spent last night reciting copied entries back to herself like scripture—memory could be disputed, dismissed, called grief-mad invention by anyone with a lawyer.

"Last chance to say this is insane," Freya said. "We dive alone, no support boat, no recompression chamber within two hundred kilometers, racing a storm system, into a wreck that's already killed—"

"Nobody," Sefa said. "It hasn't killed anybody. It's been waiting politely under the ice for forty years. It's the politest thing in this story." She shouldered her tank. "Besides. If we wait for Brase's divers to seal the hull, everything down there becomes Halvern property by Tuesday. I've seen their contracts. They patent shipwrecks, Freya. They'd have the names trademarked before the ink dried."

That was the other clock, the one ticking under the weather clock. Tide three had brought the Halvern fleet into the lower lake—two salvage barges, a dive tender, generator lights burning all night on the southern shore like a small ugly town. Corvel Brase had sent an offer across the ice that Freya still hadn't answered, wrapped around a threat dressed as courtesy. And this morning, scanning the far shore through Osk's battered binoculars, she'd counted dive tenders staging. Brase wasn't waiting for the monsoon either. He was waiting only long enough to look patient.

"Then we go now," Freya said, "while his people are still eating breakfast."

* * *

The water took them at 0740, and it was like being swallowed by a country that had never agreed to be mapped.

Cold first—cold that clamped down through the suit and found every place Freya had ever been injured. Then dark, going from grey to brown to a black that pressed on the eyes. Then the wreck, rising out of the silt floor in the beam of her lamp: the Vesper Kestrel's hull, heeled hard to port, its plating furred with pale mineral bloom, its name still legible along the bow in flaking gilt letters. *VESPER KESTREL.* A nation's pride, spelled out in gold, sunk by its own hired hand.

Freya moved down the hull toward the collapsed cargo hatch, following the guideline she and Sefa had laid on previous dives, checking her gauge more often than the cold made comfortable. Thirty meters. Thirty-four. The hatch gaped below them, a square of deeper darkness ringed by twisted steel. On their last dive the silt outside it had been smooth, marked only by their own fin wash from the day before.

Today there were other marks.

She saw them at thirty-six meters and stopped, hand closing on Sefa's arm. Sefa swung her lamp down. Both beams converged on the silt apron beside the hatch, and there—crisp, un-silted, wrong—were scuff patterns. Fin prints. A drag trail leading into the hatch mouth. And anchored into the hull plating, half-buried but unmistakable, a piton. Not a modern titanium salvage anchor. An iron piton, hand-forged, its head stamped with a mark Freya knew better than her own heartbeat.

A mountain axe head crossed by a compass needle.

Amberdale Expeditions.

The cold stopped being temperature and became time. Freya hung in the black water thirty-eight meters down, breathing recycled air through a regulator that suddenly sounded too loud, and stared at her family's stamp on metal that had no business being here, and felt the years fold up like a map being closed. Someone had been here. Someone with her father's gear, her father's methods, her father's ironwork—here, at this hatch, at some point in the last forty years, diving a lake that had been sealed under ice until the glacier let go.

But the glacier had only exposed the mast recently. She'd watched it break the surface herself, days ago, the first breach in a generation. Before that, the wreck had been entombed. Unreachable.

Unless someone had reached it anyway. Through the ice. With drills and nerve and a reason worth dying for.

Her father's journal, found in his camp above the lake, had ended mid-thought, the last entries about tearing the pages and sinking the truth. But journals end; men don't. What had he done afterward? Come back? Sent someone? Left a cache?

*The pages aren't missing,* Freya thought, and the realization went through her like the cold, total and clarifying. *The pages are hidden. By him. Somewhere only he could find again—or someone he trusted.*

Sefa tapped her wrist. Gauge check. Bottom time bleeding away. But Freya couldn't move yet. She put her glove against the piton, against the stamped mark, and the metal was iron-cold and utterly real, and forty years collapsed to the width of her hand.

Then Sefa grabbed her harness and pointed, hard, toward the hatch interior—and Freya's lamp caught what lay beyond the scuff marks, deeper in, wedged in the collapsed framing where the hold met the forward bulkhead.

A container. Small, rectangular, riveted shut, crusted with mineral deposit except along one edge where recent hands had chipped the crust away. And stenciled on its face, faded but legible:

**AMBERDALE EXPEDITIONS — PROPERTY OF — RETURN IF FOUND**

Below the stencil, in handwriting she would have known blind, in the dark, at the bottom of any sea:

*For F., when she stops running.*

Freya's vision blurred—not from the water, not from the pressure. From something older than both.

Sefa's hand closed on her arm again, gentle this time, and pointed upward: air, time, the storm building somewhere above them like a fist slowly closing. They had minutes. Maybe enough minutes.

Freya tore her eyes from the stencil, checked her gauge—thirty-one hundred psi, bottom time nearly spent—and made the diver's calculation that was also a daughter's calculation: the container was wedged fast, riveted, too heavy to free safely in the time they had. Tearing at it now meant a snagged line, a silt-out, a death by impatience in a hole in the world.

So she did the harder thing. She memorized it. The position relative to the bulkhead seam. The depth. The angle of the wedge. She burned it into memory the way her father had once burned names into pages he then tore out—because some things you carry out of the dark with nothing but your own mind, and trust that your mind is built well enough to hold them.

Then she took Sefa's signal line, and together they rose out of the hold, out of the wreck, out of the black, leaving the container sitting in the cold with her father's handwriting on it, patient as the ship itself.

* * *

They surfaced into wind that hadn't existed an hour ago.

The lake had changed texture—small sharp chop instead of glass, whitecaps starting at the far end where the valley narrowed toward the icefield. The sky to the south had gone the color of wet slate, and a wall of cloud stood on the horizon like a range that hadn't been there yesterday. The buoy's pulse, when the wind carried it, was down to twenty-nine seconds.

Sefa got them both onto the rocks, stripped her mask, and stared south with water streaming off her jaw. "Four tides was optimistic," she said. "Look at that wall. That's the leading edge. We have three. Maybe two and a half."

Freya barely heard her. She was kneeling on the rock, dripping, staring at her own gloved hands as if they'd touched something radioactive. Because they had. Time itself.

"He was here," she said. "Or he sent someone. Before the ice ever broke. They dove through it—drilled down, reached the hold, cached the pages in that container, sealed it, and left. Decades ago." She looked up at Sefa, and her voice came out low and rough. "Which means everyone who's been racing us—the fleet, Brase, the tides—they've all been racing toward a box that was already packed before any of them were born. The question was never whether the truth survived. It's been sitting down there the whole time, waiting for me."

"Waiting for *F.*" Sefa said quietly. "When she stops running."

Freya laughed, and it came out wrong, closer to something else. "He knew. All those years of silence, the blacklist, letting me grow up thinking he'd abandoned me to save himself—he knew I'd come. He left the coordinates in his last letter like breadcrumbs. He planned the whole route from the grave."

"Then he also planned for Brase," Sefa said. "And for the monsoon. And maybe—" she hesitated, and Freya watched her decide to say the next part, watched loyalty bend another degree further from Halvern and toward whatever the two of them were becoming, "—maybe for the fact that you wouldn't be able to open that container alone. Riveted seams like that, corroded forty years—you'll need cutting gear, stable conditions, time. None of which we'll have after landfall. Which means we get it out *before* the storm, or we explain to a dead man why his daughter failed a test he wrote for her."

Wind gusted off the lake, carrying spray and the buoy's quickening pulse. Across the water, on the southern shore, Halvern's generators flared brighter against the darkening sky—Brase's people working late now, feeling the same pressure, moving on the same shrinking window. Somewhere over there, Osk sat in a holding pen trading information for cigarettes, and every cigarette bought was a piece of Freya's route sold.

Freya stood. Her legs were steady. Whatever was shaking in her was deeper than her legs.

"Two and a half tides," she said. "Cutting gear from Osk's boat stores—I know what he carries, even impounded, the patrol warehouse on the north shore has a loose window and a drunk guard. We cut the container free tomorrow, on the next dive, and we're gone before Brase's tenders finish their breakfast." She looked south at the wall of cloud, and then down at the water, at the wreck she could no longer see but could feel beneath her feet like a heartbeat. "He wrote me a letter, Sefa. Forty years ago, at the bottom of a frozen lake. *For F., when she stops running.*"

"And have you?" Sefa asked. "Stopped running?"

Freya picked up her fins and started up the rocks toward the tent, toward the plans, toward the last narrow window of calm water this lake would give them.

"No," she said. "But tomorrow I stop swimming away."
