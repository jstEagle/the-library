## 13. Amberdale Expeditions

The wreck gave up its secrets in the order of their weight, and the lightest came first.

Freya spent the morning of tide four in the forward hold, working by touch where her lamp failed. The Vesper Kestrel lay on her port side at forty meters, and everything aboard had migrated downslope over four decades—brass fittings, crockery, a rain of rivets that had rusted free and now carpeted the lowest deck like dark hail. She moved through it slowly, methodically, the way she'd once moved through avalanche debris: reading what the mountain—or in this case the lake—had chosen to keep.

The equipment traces were what had pulled her back down after a night of almost no sleep. Yesterday, wedged behind a collapsed bulkhead, she'd found a dive tank stamped with a logo she knew better than her own heartbeat: a stylized peak above three letters, **AMB**. Amberdale Expeditions. Her father's company, dead before she was old enough to remember it alive.

One tank could be coincidence. Salvage divers rented gear from everywhere.

But then there had been the piton, driven into the hull plating near the torn safe—a modern expansion bolt, not Karstian wartime hardware—and beside it, coiled in the silt like a sleeping snake, a length of static rope with a distinctive blue tracer weave. Her mother had woven tracer into every rope the company ever bought. *So we can find each other in the dark,* she used to say. Freya had learned to climb on ropes with that exact weave, before the weaving stopped, before everything stopped.

Someone from Amberdale Expeditions had been inside this wreck. Recently enough that the bolt showed no more than a film of corrosion. And there was only one person it could have been.

She surfaced at noon with numb hands and a question that wouldn't stay submerged, and Sefa was waiting on the rocks with tea brewed over a spirit stove and an expression Freya was learning to read as *I'm not going to ask, but I noticed.*

"You found something," Sefa said.

"I found my father's dive gear."

Sefa handed her the tin cup. Steam curled off it and vanished sideways in the wind coming off the icefield. "In the wreck."

"In the wreck. Bolted in. Like he meant to come back." Freya drank too fast and burned her tongue and didn't care. "Sefa, the pages weren't torn out by whoever sank the ship. They were torn out *after*. By someone who got here first."

"Your father."

"Who else knows these routes? Who else could cross Vergha Pass alone?" She stared out at the lake, at the black mast standing above the waterline like a grave marker nobody visited. "He didn't just help sink the Kestrel. He came back. He's been here."

"He's been gone eleven years," Sefa said gently.

"Then he came back before he went." Freya set down the cup. "And he left something. People like him always leave something. He just never left it anywhere I could reach."

* * *

The camp was exactly where she would have put it, which was the worst part.

She found it on the second day, following logic instead of luck: a shelf of granite two hundred meters above the lake's north shore, sheltered from the prevailing wind, with sightlines down to the mast and a scramble route to the water that avoided the crevassed tongue of the glacier. Any guide worth her certification would choose it. Her father had taught her to read terrain like text, and the landscape up here was a sentence she finished without meaning to.

She saw the weathered orange of tent fabric first—sun-bleached to the color of old bone, but intact, the fly still guyed out with a competence that said the last person to strike camp had expected to return. Then the stone windbreak. Then the small cairn over a supply cache, and beside it, weighted under a flat rock, a sealed aluminum case stenciled with the peak-and-letters she'd known all her life.

Freya stood at the edge of the camp for a long time without moving.

There was a version of this moment where she cried. She'd budgeted for it, honestly, somewhere between the pass and the lake, expecting grief to ambush her in some high cold place the way it always did. But what actually happened was stranger: she felt *watched*. Not by ghosts—by standards. The camp was tidy. The cache was properly built. The windbreak was laid in courses, not dumped. Eleven years dead, and Aldous Amberdale's fieldcraft was still immaculate, and hers suddenly felt like a student's, and she stood there at thirty-four years old wanting to make her bed straighter because her father might inspect it.

*You're not angry at him,* she thought. *You're angry that you still want his approval.*

She crossed the camp and opened the case.

Inside, packed in waxed cloth against the damp: a second journal. Not the ship's logbook—that was wrapped separately beneath it, the famous missing pages absent, their absence itself a kind of presence. This was a personal book, bound in green canvas, the cover soft with handling. Her father's handwriting filled the first pages in ink that had faded to the color of weak tea, and the last entries were pencil, pressed hard, written fast.

She sat down on the windbreak stone with the journal in her lap and read until the light changed.

* * *

The early entries were expedition notes. Depths, temperatures, visibility windows, the slow retreat of the glacier measured season by season across nine years. He had been coming here since before the blacklist, since before her memory began—coming back summer after summer to a wreck everyone else believed was unrecoverable, waiting for the ice to give up the hull the way it was giving it up now.

Then, seven years in, the entries changed character.

*I got the hold open today,* one read. *Forty meters is past what I should be diving alone. I know that. I did it anyway. The captain's safe was where the manifest said it would be. I have the logbook. I am writing this by candlelight in a country that has erased me, holding the only complete account of how Karstia died, and I do not know what to do with it.*

Freya turned the page.

*I thought I would feel triumphant. I feel sick.*

*The pages I removed name six men. Not soldiers, not saboteurs—the men who sold the border charts to Vessany's intelligence service, who let the Kestrel be sunk with proof of the sale aboard so that no one in Karstia could ever contest it. The ship wasn't lost. It was silenced. And the men who arranged it took payment in land grants and ministries, and their names are on buildings now. Their grandchildren run the ministries.*

*I sat with those pages for three nights. Every option ends badly. Publish them, and the families destroy us—they have courts, they have press, they have the blacklist machinery that already ate our reputation once. Bury them, and the truth dies with me and Karstia stays a map error forever.*

*Then I thought about Freya.*

Freya's hands had gone very still on the page.

*She is four years old. She laughs like her mother. If I surface with these names, if I fight, they will not come for me—they will come for what I love, because that is how these people operate; it is how they operated on Karstia itself. So I made the choice I will regret for the rest of my life, which is at least a long enough term for the interest to accrue.*

*I tore the pages out. I left the rest of the logbook in the safe and re-sank it, because a half-truth in a dead ship harms no one, and I took the names ashore to decide. I have not decided. I have been deciding for six years.*

*The blacklist was the price of silence, and I paid it gladly. Better a disgraced guide than a murdered daughter. But I never told her. I let her grow up ashamed of our name, thinking we fell for incompetence or worse, because the truth would have painted a target on her back, and shame is survivable in a way that being hunted is not.*

*I chose her safety over her pride. God forgive me, I would choose it again.*

The last entry was pencil, undated except by the storm references around it—late season, the year before he disappeared.

*I am older now. The dives are harder. I think I have maybe two more seasons in me at this depth. I need to decide what happens to the names when I stop. There is a girl grown now who climbs better than I ever did, and they banned her for my sins, and she thinks I abandoned her to the mountains' mercy. Someday I will tell her everything. I have rehearsed it a thousand times. In every version she forgives me, which tells me the versions are lies.*

*If you are reading this, Freya—I could not protect you and tell you the truth at the same time. No one can. I picked wrong. Or I picked right. Ask me again when you've had a child of your own and see what you pick.*

*The pages are not in the case. You already know that, or you would not be reading calmly. I sent them where I judged safest, and I am sorry, because I know you, and safe was never what you wanted. You wanted the mountain. I closed it to keep you off the summit they'd shoot you from.*

*Forgive the choice, not the cowardice. They are different things, though I hid one inside the other for eleven years.*

Freya read the final lines three times. The wind moved through the camp, lifting the bone-colored tent fabric and settling it again, and somewhere below, the glacier calved with a sound like a door closing in another room.

She did not cry. What she did was worse: she understood.

Every humiliation reassembled itself with new wiring. The hearing where they'd stripped her license—the chairman had been Vessany's former interior minister's son-in-law; she knew that now, could place him among the six names the way you place a face at a funeral. The border closure after "the Amberdale incident," the incident itself a fiction stitched around her father's crossings. Two countries' blacklists, decades of exile, her whole adult life spent guiding illegal routes through terrain she'd pioneered—all of it not punishment but *containment*, a fence built around a family that knew too much, maintained by men whose grandfathers had sold a nation and gotten away with it.

He hadn't fallen. He'd knelt. Deliberately, for years, in the mud, so that she could stand on ground no one thought worth shooting at.

And he'd let her hate him for it. That was the part that broke something loose in her chest—not the sacrifice, but the *strategy* of it, the willingness to be despised by his own daughter as the cost of keeping her invisible. Shame as camouflage. Disgrace as armor. He had weaponized their ruin.

"I'm not grateful," she said aloud, to the camp, to the wind, to the man who wasn't in it. Her voice cracked on the second word anyway. "You don't get that. You kept me safe and you made me small and you never asked which I'd have preferred."

The camp said nothing. Camps never do.

But she stayed the night there, in her own tent pitched beside his ghost of one, and she slept better than she had in eleven years, which she resented deeply.

* * *

Dawn came up gray-gold off the icefield, and with it came the sound Freya had been dreading since tide one: engines.

Not Osk's smuggler's engine, long impounded. Not the distant thrum she'd catalogued yesterday and dismissed as weather. This was close, disciplined, multiple—Halvern Deep Recovery's fleet, arriving a full tide ahead of schedule, working its way up the final river reach below the lake.

She stood at the edge of the shelf with the journal zipped inside her jacket and counted masts through her binoculars. Three vessels, one heavy-lift barge, dive tenders already deploying. And on the lead ship's deck, a figure in a director's coat, motionless, looking up at the shoreline with the patience of a man who had never once needed to hurry because other people's desperation did his hurrying for him.

Corvel Brase. She'd never seen him, but she knew the posture from Halvern's promotional materials—she'd studied them in a river town while planning this, the way you study a predator's tracks.

They hadn't seen the camp yet. The shelf was screened by rock, and the light was wrong for glass. She had hours, maybe less.

Below, the lake waited with its black mast and its gutted safe and its forty-year lie. Above, the passes home were watched. Between them: one journal naming six families, no torn pages, and a bell still somewhere in the deep hold that could authenticate everything she now knew.

Her father had spent his whole last decade choosing silence, and it had bought nothing—not safety, not peace, not even a clean death in a warm bed. The names hadn't protected her. They'd just rotted quietly in whatever hiding place he'd sent them to, while the men who profited grew old comfortably.

Maybe silence had been right in 1989, when the machinery of two governments could crush a family without breaking stride.

It was not 1989 anymore. The glacier had retreated. The wreck was visible. Brase was here, and Brase didn't want the truth—he wanted leverage, and leverage was exactly what six names became when you held them over the grandchildren who now ran Vessany.

If the truth was going to exist in the world either way, Freya decided, watching the tenders cut white wakes across the gray water, then it was going to exist *hers*. Read aloud. Witnessed. Owned by the people it belonged to—the diaspora, the descendants, the ones who sang lullabies in a language the ministries had tried to bury.

She zipped the journal tight against her ribs and started down toward the lake, toward the wreck, toward the bell.

Behind her, the wind flattened the grass on her father's grave-cold camp, and ahead, the first Halvern diver splashed into the water that had kept all their secrets.

Two tides left. Maybe one.

Freya ran.
