## 14. What Brase Wants

Corvel Brase had never liked the high country. It was a place that made promises it couldn't keep—thin air, thin ice, thin margins—and he had built his career on the opposite principle: everything in writing, everything insured, everything priced. So when the floatplane dropped him onto the gray water of the staging lake and the wind came off the glacier like something with an opinion, he buttoned his coat and reminded himself that discomfort was simply overhead.

The Halvern fleet sat at anchor in three tiers: the survey barge *Meridian Rule*, two dive tenders, and the salvage crane barge that had cost more to charter than most towns spent in a year. Floodlights on the water. Generator hum. Forty personnel moving with the drilled calm of people who knew the schedule mattered more than the weather.

Sefa Marun met him at the gangway. She looked like she'd slept badly, which pleased him. Rest made divers sentimental.

"Director."

"Marun." He handed her a tablet. "Beacon grid status."

"Ninety percent placed. The eastern shelf is unstable—the ice channel's widening faster than the models predicted." A pause, measured to the millimeter. "We should consider accelerating the deep-hold survey."

"We will. After I deal with a complication." He watched her face as he said it, the way he watched every face, cataloguing the flicker before the mask came down. There it was. Good. He'd expected nothing less from her; that was why he'd hired her, and why he'd been watching her since the river town.

"What complication?" she asked.

"Your smuggler friend," Brase said. "Osk Vellin. My people picked him up last night at the lower rapids, trying to run cargo south ahead of the monsoon. Cargo being himself, mostly. He talks when he's cold. He's been cold for nine hours."

* * *

They kept Osk in the hold of the *Meridian Rule*, which was not a prison but functioned as one, because the nearest shore was forty kilometers of water and glacial scree and the nearest town was a rumor. Brase descended the ladder himself. He preferred to conduct these conversations personally. Delegation was for tasks, not leverage.

Osk sat zip-tied to a cargo ring, wrapped in a survival blanket that someone—probably out of professional courtesy among river rats—had actually given him. His beard was crusted with frost. He looked up at Brase with the flat appraisal of a man who had been interrogated before and found it survivable.

"You're the money," Osk said.

"I'm the director of recovery operations for Halvern Deep Recovery," Brase said. "Which is a longer way of saying yes."

"The woman who took my boat—Amberdale. You want her."

"I want what she has."

Osk smiled through cracked lips. "Then you've got a problem, because what she has doesn't fit in your safe."

Brase pulled a crate over, sat, and let the silence do its first shift of work. Around them the hull ticked and groaned, the lake pressing in like a patient creditor.

"Tell me about the wreck," Brase said finally. "Not the salvage manifest. Not the brass and the bell and whatever else you were promised. Tell me why a blacklisted guide burned her last bridge to get up here six tides before anyone else."

Osk shrugged, which cost him something, tied as he was. "She didn't tell me. That was the deal. First claim on anything non-Karstian, no questions. I don't ask questions. Asking questions is how you end up zip-tied to things."

"And yet here you are."

"Here I am." Osk's eyes went to the bulkhead, then back. Something moved behind them—not fear exactly. Calculation wearing fear's coat. "You know what I hauled upriver for her? Rope, tanks, a rebreather rig older than both of us, and a case of climbing hardware stamped with a name I recognized. Amberdale Expeditions. That crest used to be on every route map in the Vergha, before the incident. Before they burned her out of two countries." He tilted his head. "You're not salvage, are you. You're not here for the metal."

Brase let the smile come, thin and professional. "Mr. Vellin. What did she find at her father's camp?"

And there it was—the flinch. Small, but Brase had spent twenty years buying information from people who thought they weren't selling, and flinches were currency too.

"There's no camp," Osk said.

"There is. Your impounded boat was searched this morning. In the chart locker, under your spare charts, we found a page torn from a journal. Water-stained. Karstian script on one side, border-common on the other. Someone gave it to you for safekeeping, or you stole it, or you were meant to deliver it—I don't much care which." He produced a photograph, held it up. "The handwriting matches letters recovered from the Amberdale family archive. Which means the journal exists, which means Freya Amberdale has it, which means—" he folded the photo away "—she knows what's in it."

Osk stared at him. Then he laughed, a dry rasp with no humor in it anywhere. "You think I know what's in it? She read it alone. Came down off that ridge looking like she'd seen her own ghost. Wouldn't say a word. Just told me the deal was done, the wreck was hers, and I could take my cut and go." His jaw tightened. "I went. Look where going got me."

"So you can't tell me what the journal says."

"I can tell you what it does." Osk leaned forward against the ties, and now the calculation dropped its disguise entirely. "It makes her dangerous. To you. To whoever sent you. Because men like you don't chase a dead nation's airship across two borders on a survey budget. Somebody's paying you to be here, and whatever's in that book, it's worth more than the ship, and she's holding all of it." He settled back. "So. Do I get warm, or do I get thrown overboard?"

Brase stood. "You get warm. And a percentage, if your information holds." At the ladder he paused. "For the record, Mr. Vellin—nobody gets thrown overboard on my boats. We're a certified operation. We have standards."

He climbed into the gray morning light, and behind him Osk called out, cheerfully miserable: "That's what worries me!"

* * *

In the operations cabin, Brase spread the campaign across the table the way other men spread maps of treasure. Because that was the thing Osk had gotten wrong, and the thing everyone always got wrong: there was no treasure. Treasure was for children and divers. What Brase dealt in was rarer and infinitely more liquid.

He laid out the fragments his people had assembled. The logbook pages recovered from the captain's safe—copies, the originals still unaccounted for, presumably with Freya. The torn journal page. Forty years of Karstian diaspora records his researchers had quietly purchased from three archives and one desperate widow. And threaded through all of it, the names.

He wrote them on the glass wall of the cabin in his own hand, because some work deserved the dignity of penmanship. Nine names so far. Officials of the old Karstian coastal government who, in the final winter of the war, had sold the evacuation manifests, the harbor defenses, and the location of the civilian transport fleet to Vessany's intelligence service—for accounts in banks that still existed, under names that still voted. The *Vesper Kestrel* hadn't been sunk by enemy action. It had been sunk deliberately, by its own escort officer, carrying proof of the sale—proof that would have burned those nine names and their descendants out of respectable society forever.

A. Amberdale, crew. Escort officer. The man who put the truth on the bottom of a lake instead of in the hands of either government, because both governments were bought, and because he had a daughter.

Brase stepped back and looked at the nine names, and felt the particular warmth he always felt at this stage of an acquisition. Two of the nine had grandchildren sitting in the Vessany assembly right now. One chaired the border commission that controlled every salvage license on this lake—including, technically, his own. Another's family owned the shipping concern that had tried to buy Halvern out twice. These weren't historical figures. They were shareholders. Living, voting, exposed shareholders.

That was the product. Not gold, not artifacts, not the melancholy brass of a drowned nation. Leverage. The names could blackmail half the border governments—or, packaged correctly, they could be *sold back*. Quietly, privately, to the families who needed them buried again. Subscription pricing. Annual renewals. History as a service.

Scholarship was what you called it when you needed a permit. This was what it actually was.

The knock came at the door. Sefa Marun, tablet in hand, expression professionally blank.

"Director. The eastern beacon array is reporting drift. If the ice channel closes, our deep access window shrinks to two tides."

"Then we dive in two tides." He capped the marker. "Marun. A question, off the record."

"Sir."

"When you were in the river town—before the fleet sailed—you reported three possible contacts matching the target profile. You declined to confirm any of them." He turned from the glass. "One of them was a woman with river mud on her boots and a Karstian crest wrapped in oilcloth. Describe her again. Slowly."

Sefa's face did the thing he'd hired it to do: nothing. But nothing, sustained too long, became its own confession, and he watched her decide—watched the small internal vote, counted, adjourned.

"Tall," she said. "Dark hair, sunburned. Moved like a guide. Carried herself like someone who'd already decided the patrol wasn't going to catch her."

"That's a description of competence, Marun. I asked for a name."

"I didn't get a name."

"No," Brase agreed pleasantly. "You got a look. I've reviewed the dock footage myself. You spoke to her." He let that sit. "I'm not accusing you of anything. I'm telling you how this ends, so you can choose your position while positions are still cheap. In seventy-two hours, Halvern will own the only authenticated account of Karstia's fall. Every museum, every government, every diaspora family will license it from us or live with our version. The woman holding the journal is standing between this company and a fifty-year revenue stream. When we take it from her—and we will—I'd like you on the right side of the line. Whichever line you decide that is."

Sefa held his gaze. Somewhere below them, the lake shifted, ice groaning like a ship changing its mind.

"And if I'm already on the wrong side?" she said.

Brase smiled. "Then you'll want to move quickly. Wrong sides erode."

She left. He returned to the glass wall, to the nine names, and added a tenth with a question mark after it—the buyer he hadn't identified yet, the descendant whose interest in this lake had funded the whole expedition through three shell consultancies. His client. The one who wanted the names not sold but destroyed, along with everyone who'd read them.

Brase didn't particularly care which outcome won. Sale or suppression, the margin was the same. But he did care about the journal, and the journal was on a ridge above this lake in the hands of a woman who had nothing left to lose, which made her the most expensive object in the borderlands.

Outside, the first spit of the monsoon's advance guard tapped against the windows—early, days early, the weather buoys all screaming it at once. Brase checked the forecast, adjusted the schedule, and made a note to have Osk moved somewhere warmer and more persuasive.

Two tides to the deep hold. One woman on the ice between him and the only asset that mattered.

He began drafting the offer.
