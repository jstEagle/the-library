## 15. The Offer on the Ice

They came for her at first light, when the lake was still holding its breath.

Freya had been awake since the fourth tide began—lying in the collapsed tent above the shoreline, listening to the glacier calve somewhere in the dark, counting the groans and cracks the way she used to count her father's footsteps on the stairs. The journal was against her chest, wrapped in oilcloth, tied with the same knot he'd used on his gear forty years ago. She hadn't slept. Sleep would have meant dreaming, and dreaming would have meant the camp again, the small stove, the careful handwriting explaining why he'd drowned a nation's truth in a lake of ice.

She'd almost convinced herself the night had passed clean.

Then she saw the lights.

Three of them, moving down the glacier tongue in a staggered line—headlamps, not lanterns, which meant Halvern. Which meant Brase. She was rolling the journal into her pack before the second light cleared the icefall, and she was on her feet when the third one stopped, deliberately, fifty meters out on the lake ice, close enough to be seen and far enough to be a statement.

"Amberdale." Corvel Brase's voice carried across the flat ice the way voices do at altitude—clean, intimate, stripped of everything but itself. "You're a hard woman to corner politely."

Freya stood at the tent line with her crampons half-buckled and did the arithmetic she always did: wind, distance, ice thickness, exits. The shoreline rock was forty meters east. The ice channel they'd dived through two days ago had refrozen thin and gray. The glacier tongue to the north was a maze of seracs that Brase's people would hate and she would love.

"You could have just shouted from the moraine," she said. "I'd have heard you."

"I wanted you to see who's with me."

The middle light detached from the other two and came on alone. Freya's stomach did something complicated and slow, like a knot being tied underwater.

Sefa Marun stopped ten meters from Brase and stood with her hands at her sides, helmet lamp angled down at the ice, not at Freya. Her dive suit was Halvern issue. Her harness was Halvern issue. Everything about her said exactly what she was supposed to say: *I chose them.*

"You found Osk," Freya said.

"Your smuggler talks when it's warm and he's fed." Brase stepped forward, and now she could see him properly—gray coat, no rope, no crampons, walking the ice like a man who had never once in his life considered falling. "He told us about the camp. About the journal. He's very sorry, by the way. He said to tell you the passes were watched and he told you so."

"He did tell me so."

"Then no one's lied to anyone this morning except the usual people." Brase stopped at what he'd clearly calculated was conversational distance and folded his hands. Behind him the two remaining lamps held position like punctuation. "I'm going to make you an offer, and I'd like you to hear all of it before you do whatever it is mountain people do instead of listening."

Freya glanced at Sefa. "And her? Is she here to listen too?"

Sefa's lamp didn't move. "I'm here," she said, and her voice was flat and even and told Freya absolutely nothing, which was itself an answer of a kind.

*She's turned,* Freya told herself. *Brase got to her the way he gets to everyone—showed her the price of the other road and made it look like a cliff.* But there was a smaller voice underneath, the one that had shared a regulator with this woman forty meters down in black water, the one that remembered Sefa's hands steady on the snagged line, and it said: *You don't know that yet. Don't spend your anger before you know what it's buying.*

"Talk," Freya said.

* * *

Brase talked the way he walked—without any visible fear of the surface giving way.

"Two blacklists," he said. "Vessany, for the Vergha Pass routes. And Ostenmark, for the Kestrel Valley incident. Both of them built on the same finding—reckless guiding, unauthorized access, endangerment of licensed personnel. I've read the files. They're thin, Amberdale. They're thin because they were never really about your routes. They were about keeping an Amberdale away from this lake, and everyone involved has been dead long enough that nobody remembers why."

The wind came down the glacier and moved his coat and he didn't shift his weight.

"I can have both revoked. Not appealed—revoked. Vessany's survey office owes a favor to a board member who owes a favor to me. Ostenmark's is simpler; their licensing minister is a man who signs things when the signature is worth more than the paper." He let that sit. "Your father's name comes back too. A. Amberdale, listed crew, Vesper Kestrel—restored as what he actually was, which the record currently doesn't say. No traitor. No ghost. A man with a name on a ship's roster, explained, contextualized, closed. Your family name comes out of the ground, Freya. I can do that by the end of the month."

The journal was a small hard weight against her back, inside the pack, and she was suddenly aware of it the way you're aware of a bruise.

"And in exchange," she said.

"You give me the pages. The torn ones. The names." Brase's voice didn't rise or fall; that was the frightening part. Most men selling something perform the selling. He didn't. He stated. "You've read the journal. You know what your father tore out. Nine names. Officials who sold Karstia's border defenses to the coalition that erased it—men whose grandchildren hold ministries, charters, and salvage licenses in three countries today. Your father hid them to protect you. I understand that. I'm not asking you to forgive him. I'm asking you to finish the transaction he started."

"Finish it," Freya said. "You mean sell it."

"I mean *price* it." For the first time something moved in his face—not anger, not even enthusiasm. Recognition, maybe, of a counterpart. "Those names are worth more than any cargo on that wreck. Half the border governments would pay simply to make sure they never surface. The other half would pay to make sure they do—at a time of their choosing. I'm not a monster, Amberdale. I'm not going to publish them and burn those families' grandchildren. I'm going to sell the *choice*. To the people who have the most reason to buy it. That's not betrayal. That's the market finally pricing something honestly."

Freya looked past him at Sefa.

"And you," she said. "The archive. The charts you copied. The lullaby your grandmother—"

"Don't," Sefa said, and the word came out with an edge on it, the first crack in the flatness. She caught it, smoothed it. "Don't do that. Don't stand there and inventory what you think you know about me."

"Then tell me what I don't know."

Sefa was quiet for a moment. The lake ticked and settled beneath them, ice speaking its slow language.

"Brase found the archive raid before I found out it had failed," she said. "He showed me the manifest at four this morning. Everything I sent them—the charts, the survey notes, my grandmother's name in the donor register—confiscated, catalogued, and now it's *his*. He owns my grandmother's country's paper trail, Freya. He owns it the way he wants to own those names." She lifted her lamp at last, and the light caught her face, and there was something in it Freya couldn't read at all, which frightened her more than anger would have. "So I'm standing here. Doing the math you're doing. That's all I'll say on the ice."

* * *

Brase let the silence run long enough to work, and then he did the thing Freya had been waiting for and dreading: he made it personal.

"Your father chose badly," he said, "but he chose for love. I've never once held that against him. You should know that. Forty years he carried those pages, and in all that time he never sold them, never traded them, never even used them to buy his daughter's way off a blacklist he knew was unjust. He just sat on a mountain and let you grow up disgraced by association." Brase tilted his head. "Was that love? Or was that a man who couldn't bear to spend the only currency he had?"

"Careful," Freya said.

"I don't need to be careful. That's the point of the offer." He spread his hands. "You've spent your whole life paying for his choice. The bans. The towns that wouldn't license you. The clients who booked you and then quietly unbooked you. You're the best guide in three ranges and no one will legally follow your rope. I'm offering you the one thing every salvage fleet and every archive and every well-meaning diaspora committee *can't* give you, which is your own name back, clean, in writing, enforceable. All you have to do is hand me nine names that aren't yours to protect. They were never your father's to protect either. He just decided they were, and you've been paying the invoice ever since."

The lake boomed under them—a deep tectonic note, ice flexing over black water—and Freya felt it come up through her crampons and into her teeth.

She thought about the camp above the lake. The small stove. The careful hand. *I did this so you would never have to know them.* Nine names, and a daughter raised in the shadow of a disgrace that had been, all along, a wall her father built around her—with her inside it, and the truth outside, and the wall made of her own reputation.

"He was wrong," she said quietly.

Brase's eyebrows rose a fraction. "Go on."

"Not about protecting people. About the method. He thought a secret could be a shelter." She looked at him. "It's not. It's a debt. And you're not the one holding the paper—you're the one who found out the paper exists."

"Then we agree the paper should have an owner." Brase smiled, and it was the first genuinely warm thing she'd seen him do, and it was worse than everything cold before it. "One night. That's what you're going to ask for—everyone asks for a night—so I'll grant it before you do. Until the fifth tide turns. My camp is on the moraine; my divers will not approach the wreck, the channel, or your tent. You have my word, and I'd point out that my word is currently the only currency in this transaction that has ever been spent in public."

Freya said nothing.

"Freya." Sefa's voice, low. "One night is real. Take it."

And there—only there, in the two words *take it*, in the rhythm of them—Freya heard it. Sefa had said *take it* the way a guide says *step left*, the way you speak to someone standing on ice you know better than they do. Not pleading. *Placing.*

Freya kept her face still and her lamp low and said, "One night. Until the fifth tide turns. And then I'll give you an answer on this ice, in front of your witnesses."

"That's all I ask." Brase turned, then paused, half-smiling back over his shoulder. "For what it's worth, I hope you choose yourself. Your father never did."

The three lights went back up the glacier tongue, and Sefa's was last, and it did not look back, and Freya stood alone on the lake until even the echo of their crampons was gone.

* * *

She did not go back to the tent.

She walked east along the shoreline rock with the pack tight against her spine and the journal's weight riding between her shoulder blades, and she made herself think like a guide instead of a daughter, because the daughter had been outmaneuvered twice today and the guide hadn't been outmaneuvered yet.

Brase had Osk. That meant he had the route down. The fleet was on the lake—she'd seen the survey ship's lights two nights ago, anchored off the far shelf—and his divers were under orders to stand down, which meant they were under orders to *appear* to stand down. He'd given her a night because a night cost him nothing. He already believed he knew what was in the journal. He already believed the pages were recoverable, or that she'd already recovered them, and that her only real choice was which buyer she sold her silence to.

He had made one error, and it was the same error everyone made about her: he thought the mountain was the obstacle.

Freya climbed to the shelf above the camp, where the wind came off the icefield clean, and sat with her back to stone, and took out the journal, and read the last page again by the gray pre-dawn. Her father's hand, cramped and cold-pressed:

*If you are reading this, you found what I buried twice. I am sorry. Not for the hiding—for the years I let you carry the weight of it without the reason. A name is not a shelter. I knew that. I was a coward in the shape of a protector. Do better with what I left you than I did.*

Do better.

She looked down at the lake. Sefa's light was gone, but somewhere down there, under the gray refreeze of the dive channel, the Vesper Kestrel hung in the dark with her bell in the deep hold, and the fifth tide was already turning, and the monsoon buoys had said four tides, which meant three now, which meant every hour Brase gave her was an hour he was also spending.

And Sefa had said *take it* like *step left*.

Freya closed the journal and tied the oilcloth and looked north, to where the icefall glittered in the first real light, and began, very quietly, to plan the kind of climb that got people blacklisted.

The fifth tide would turn in nine hours. She intended to be underwater when it did.
