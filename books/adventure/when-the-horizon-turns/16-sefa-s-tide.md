## 16. Sefa's Tide

Sefa had rehearsed the confession for two days, and she still couldn't get the first sentence right.

She'd tried it standing over her dive kit in the dark of Osk's boathouse, where Freya was patching a drysuit seam by lamplight. She'd tried it walking the shoreline at low tide, counting stones. She'd even tried it into the mirror of a fogged porthole glass, which was a mistake, because the woman looking back at her looked like someone about to do something irreversible.

The problem was that every version began with *I lied to you*, and every version ended with *I'm sorry*, and between those two points lay the whole shape of what she'd done — weeks of it, methodical, patient — and no arrangement of words made that look like anything except what it was.

So on the morning of the fifth tide, when Freya came out of the water with the last of the survey beacons pried off the rocks and said, "Brase moves tonight. We're out of time," Sefa stopped rehearsing and simply started.

"I didn't turn," she said.

Freya went very still. Water ran off her suit and pooled around her boots. "Say that again."

"The night Brase cornered me on his deck — the offer, the choice he gave me." Sefa kept her voice level, the way she kept her breathing level at forty meters, because panic was just arithmetic done too fast. "I said yes so he'd believe I said yes. That's all it was."

Freya set the beacon down in the shingle. Slowly. The way you set down something that might go off.

"You stood next to him," she said. "On the ice. You watched him make me an offer while I was deciding whether to trust you with my life, and you said nothing."

"Yes."

"For how long? How long have you been —" Freya's hand made a small, furious gesture that couldn't decide what it was reaching for. "Since the dock? Since the lake?"

"Since before the lake." Sefa sat down on an overturned crate, because if this was going to happen, it would happen at eye level. "Halvern hired me eight months ago for my grandmother's name, not my diving. They wanted a Karstian face on the salvage manifest. It makes the paperwork look like recovery instead of theft. I took the job because I thought I could watch them from inside." She exhaled. "Then the chest came up the river, and your name came up with it, and I realized Halvern wasn't watching the wreck. They were watching whoever found it first. So I got there first too."

Freya lowered herself onto the opposite crate. Not sitting so much as landing.

"The charts," she said. "Chapter and verse, Sefa. What did you actually do?"

Sefa told her. All of it, in order, because if she skipped anything Freya would find the gap later and fill it with the worst possible version.

The survey charts, three nights after the fleet anchored: she'd copied the basin soundings onto tracing film in the chart room, working by red lamp while the duty officer slept twenty feet away, and mailed the copies to the Karstian diaspora archive in Vessany's southern valley — the one run by old Maret Voss and her volunteers, the one that kept songs and ledgers and land records in filing cabinets that smelled of camphor. Her grandmother had sent letters there for thirty years. If anyone alive knew how to keep Karstian paper safe, it was them.

The torn pages: when Freya recovered the captain's safe and the journal surfaced from her father's camp above the lake, Sefa had asked to see the pages naming the officials who sold Karstia out. She'd read them twice. Then, on a night Freya thought she was sleeping, she'd copied them — not photographed, not traced, but written out longhand in her own script, in a cipher her grandmother taught her when she was nine, a child's cipher built from a lullaby, the kind of thing no corporate cryptographer would think to look for because it wasn't designed to survive capture. It was designed to survive inside one person's head.

And then she'd carried the originals down off the mountain herself, wrapped in oilcloth inside a spare weight belt, and put them on a mail barge at the river town under a false manifest, addressed to the archive, insured for nothing, worth everything.

"They've been in Maret Voss's hands for six days," Sefa said. "By now they're split across three locations, none of them labeled, none of them where a raid would look. Brase can't patent what he can't find. He can't blackmail half the border governments with pages that don't exist outside a valley full of grandmothers who've been hiding things from governments their whole lives."

Silence. The lake worked at the ice edge behind them, that slow grinding breath glaciers made when the weather turned.

Freya rubbed both hands over her face. When she lowered them, her eyes were wet, but her voice was dry.

"Do you know what I did last night?" she said. "I lay awake running through every conversation we've had since the dock, trying to work out which ones were real. You shared a regulator with me at the bell. You held my line when the channel collapsed. And some part of me was doing arithmetic the whole time — *is this the part where she sells me* — and I hated myself for it, and now you're telling me the arithmetic was right, just pointed the wrong direction."

"I know."

"No, you don't." Freya stood. "You don't get to say *I know*. You let Brase stand me down on the ice and offer to buy my father's name back like a debt collection, and you stood there wearing his side of it, and you never once gave me a reason to hope. Do you understand what that costs a person? I spent forty years being the daughter of a traitor. One more night of it doesn't cost you anything. It cost me—"

She stopped. Breathed. Sefa watched her rebuild herself plank by plank, the way she did, the way mountain people did, and felt the full weight of what she'd chosen to protect by choosing silence.

"I'm sorry," Sefa said. "You're right. I should have told you before the ice. I told myself I was protecting the operation. That was true, and it was also a lie, because the operation was never the point. The point was that if I told you and you looked at me the way you're looking at me now, I'd have had to feel it sooner."

Freya turned away toward the water. For a long moment the only sound was the glacier and the wind coming down the valley carrying the first wet smell of the monsoon front, days early, wrong, urgent.

"The originals are safe," Freya said finally. It wasn't forgiveness. It was triage. "That's what matters. Tell me the rest. Where exactly. Who has them. What happens if Brase goes looking."

And that was when Sefa heard it — the engine.

Not the fleet's heavy diesels, which announced themselves a valley away. This was lighter. A skiff, running quiet, coming up the eastern shore where nobody had business being, and it was close enough that whoever was aboard had already seen the two of them talking on the shore like conspirators, which is exactly what they were.

"Sefa." Freya was already moving, scooping the beacon parts into her pack. "How many people knew about the archive?"

"Just me. Maret Voss. Two of her volunteers who handle the sensitive holdings." Sefa's stomach dropped through the floor of the conversation. "Why?"

"Because either that skiff is lost fishermen," Freya said, "or Brase didn't need you to tell him where the pages went. He needed you to *think* you'd gotten away with it."

* * *

They watched from the tree line as the skiff nosed against the shore below their camp. Two men got out. They weren't fishermen — fishermen didn't wear matching rain shells with the collars clipped high, didn't move like men following coordinates instead of fish. They went through the camp efficiently, turning over the crates, reading the labels on the gas cylinders, and one of them held up something small and pale that caught the light.

A page. A single sheet of paper, and even at this distance Sefa recognized the shape of her own handwriting.

No. Not hers. *Imitation* of hers. She'd left decoy pages in the camp deliberately — a partial copy, ciphered, planted where a searcher would find it — because a search that finds something stops searching. It was standard practice from her salvage certification, and she'd taught it to Halvern's own security team two years ago at a conference in Corvel Brase's own conference room.

Which meant these men knew exactly what they were holding. Which meant they knew it was a plant. Which meant—

"They already hit the archive," she said quietly.

Freya didn't answer. She was watching the second man, who had walked to the waterline and was doing something with his hands. A moment later a thin whistle crossed the lake — three notes, repeated — and from the far shore, faint as memory, three notes answered.

Signal protocol. Halvern field teams used it for line-tending under ice. Sefa had invented half those signals herself.

"How long ago?" Freya asked.

"Dawn raids happen before first light. If they moved on the valley at dawn—" Sefa did the arithmetic and hated the result. "It's been hours. It's done. Whatever happened there happened while we were planning our night dive."

They stayed in the trees until the skiff left, running quiet toward the fleet anchorage, and then they went down to the camp and Sefa picked up the decoy page with hands that wouldn't quite stay steady.

It was her handwriting. It was her cipher. And across the top, in ink that hadn't been there when she'd planted it, someone had written a single line:

**MARET VOSS SENDS HER REGARDS. SHE KEPT THE REAL ONES. ASK YOUR GRANDMOTHER'S LULLABY WHERE.**

Sefa read it three times. The letters swam. "This isn't Brase's hand."

"No," Freya said slowly. "Brase burns bridges. Whoever wrote this wants us to *keep going*." She looked up, and Sefa saw the same thought land in her that had landed in her own chest a beat earlier, cold and bright as lake water. "If the archive was raided, and Maret Voss is alive enough to leave messages in decoy pages, then the raid failed. Or it succeeded, and she wanted it to."

"There's a third option." Sefa folded the page into her suit pocket, against her skin, where paper could at least drown with her honestly. "The raid happened. They took *something*. And whatever they took, Maret wanted us to believe it wasn't the pages — whether or not that's true."

"So we can't know."

"We can't know." Sefa looked out at the lake, at the place where the Kestrel's mast broke the surface like a splinter of the past refusing to rot, at the fleet lights beginning to kindle across the water as the short day died. "Here's what we know for certain. Brase thinks he has leverage. Whether he does or not, he believes it, and a man who believes he's won gets careless about the details. And the only copies of those names that no raid can touch—" she tapped her own temple, then pointed at Freya's, "—are the ones we memorized. Every name. Every date. Every office. I can recite mine in my sleep. Can you?"

Freya was quiet for a moment. "My father made me memorize things my whole childhood. Trade routes. Star fixes. Escape routes out of three countries. I thought it was just how he showed love." Her jaw tightened. "Turns out he was training me for this. Yeah. I can recite them."

"Then here's the tide we're on," Sefa said. "Tonight we dive the hold for the bell, because the bell is physical proof and proof is the only thing that makes testimony worth believing. Tomorrow, whatever Brase does with whatever he took from that valley, we stand up in front of people and we speak the names out loud from memory, and memory doesn't have a safe to crack." She paused. "But Freya — if Brase has the real pages, and he figures out we can recite them anyway, we stop being witnesses he can buy. We become loose ends he has to cut. On open water. In monsoon season."

Freya shouldered her pack. Out on the lake, the first drops came down — fat, warm, indifferent, the vanguard of a storm that had promised four tides and was delivering in two.

"Then we'd better get the bell tonight," she said, "while the water still lets us."
