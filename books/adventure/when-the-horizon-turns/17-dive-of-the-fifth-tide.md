## 17. Dive of the Fifth Tide

**Freya**

The ice channel was dying by inches.

Freya lay flat at its mouth and watched it happen. All night the glacier had groaned like a ship working loose from its moorings, and now, in the gray hour before dawn, a crack ran the length of the channel—a black seam in blue-white, widening as she watched, exhaling cold air that smelled of deep water and old metal.

"That's our door," Sefa said beside her. "And it's closing."

"Then we go through it before it does."

Below them, half a kilometer down the shoreline, lanterns moved on the ice where Halvern's divers had established their perimeter. Brase hadn't wasted the night. Three dive stations, a recompression tent, a winch rig over the deepest point of the basin—and around the wreck itself, a grid of steel mesh panels being lowered to seal the hull openings. Freya had watched them through her father's battered monocular until her hands went numb.

"They're caging it," she said. "Like an animal."

"They're patenting it," Sefa corrected. "There's a difference only lawyers can see."

The bell was the argument they could make that no lawyer could answer. A ship's bell carried its name cast into bronze—*Vesper Kestrel*, and beneath it, if Karstian naval tradition held, the yard and year. It was physical proof. It couldn't be redacted, torn out, or bought back. In Freya's pack sat the journal with its names; in Sefa's head lived the pages she'd memorized in one desperate night. But testimony needed corroboration, and the bell was corroboration you could ring.

If they could reach it. If Brase's divers didn't reach them first. If the channel held for another twenty minutes.

"The tide turns in forty," Sefa said. She checked her gauge, then checked it again, as if repetition could improve the number. "Current through the channel will reverse. After that, no way in or out."

"Then we have forty minutes to do what the fleet needs six tides for."

Sefa looked at her. In the pre-dawn gray her face was all planes and shadow, her grandmother's cheekbones sharp under wind-chapped skin. "You say things like that," she said, "like the mountain cares about your schedule."

"The mountain doesn't care about anything." Freya shrugged into her harness. "That's why I trust it. It never lies to get what it wants."

She thought of Brase on the ice two nights ago, his voice warm as a heated cabin: *Your blacklists lifted. Your father's name restored.* Everything she'd wanted for eleven years, offered in exchange for the truth. And Sefa standing at his shoulder, silent, apparently turned—and Freya had spent every hour since wondering which version of that silence was real.

She still didn't know. That was the thing about rope partners: eventually you had to weight the rope.

"Last check," Sefa said, and ran through it without being asked—valves, gauges, knife, light, the spare regulator on its hose. Her hands were steady. Her voice wasn't quite. "When we're under, we don't talk about Brase. We don't talk about names. We're just two divers doing a job."

"And after?"

"After," Sefa said, "we'll see who's still breathing."

They went into the channel feet first, sliding down the slick blue throat of it, and the glacier closed its sound around them like a hand.

* * *

**Sefa**

The channel was worse than the charts suggested—which was fitting, since the charts were hers, copied from Halvern's surveys and doctored just enough to send the fleet's divers to the wrong entry points. She'd bought them hours with that lie. Maybe enough hours. The water inside the channel moved in slow spirals, lit by her lamp into shifting cathedrals of blue, and every few meters the passage narrowed until her tanks scraped stone and she had to turn sideways, exhaling, listening to her own bubbles race upward toward a sky she couldn't see.

Freya moved ahead of her like she'd been born in this kind of dark. Of course she had. This was the woman two countries had banned for going where maps said not to, and watching her swim was watching the ban make sense. Every movement economical. Every hesitation read and answered before it became a problem. Once, at a junction where the channel split, Freya paused for less than three seconds—lamp sweeping left, right, down—and chose the lower route with a certainty that felt less like decision than recognition.

*She's been dreaming about this place for years,* Sefa thought. *Maybe longer than I've been dreaming about Karstia.*

Her grandmother had sung to her in a language Sefa only half understood, songs about mountains that kept accounts and rivers that forgave nothing. She'd thought they were lullabies. Standing in the wreck's shadow two nights ago, sharing air with this stranger whose family name was carved into its history, she'd understood they were instructions.

The channel opened. They dropped out of the ice into open black water, and below them, caught in the crossing beams of two lamps, the *Vesper Kestrel* hung in the lake like a held breath.

Forty years underwater and the hull still had its shape—listing hard to port, superstructure collapsed into itself, but whole. Whole enough. Survey beacons blinked along its spine in Halvern's green, marking it like territory. And across the cargo deck, visible even from here, the first mesh panel had been lowered, its steel grid throwing lattice shadows across the wreck.

They'd sealed the main hatch already. But ships are full of doors, and navies build redundancy into everything—including escape. Freya pointed: aft, low, a maintenance access half-buried in silt, too small for salvage crews to bother with. Too small for anything but a diver who knew ships, or a daughter who'd grown up on her father's deck plans.

They descended together, side by side, and the cold came up through their suits like a rising question.

* * *

**Freya**

Inside, the ship was a throat.

Her lamp found corridor walls furred with pale silt, a handrail emerging from the gray like something surfacing to look at her. Forty years of stillness, and her bubbles broke it—each exhalation a small blasphemy against the quiet. She moved aft by feel and memory both, her father's deck plans unfolded behind her eyes: crew sections here, galley there, and amidships, forward of the engine housing, the ceremonial spaces where a navy kept its identity.

Where a navy kept its bell.

The corridor narrowed. Somewhere above, faint through forty meters of water and steel, she heard it—the winch. Brase's crews were lowering more mesh. Working fast now, because Corvel Brase did not spend five tides of preparation to lose his prize to two women and a hole in the ice.

*Faster,* she told herself. *Be faster.*

The bell housing was exactly where the plans said it would be: an alcove off the ceremonial passageway, the mounting bracket intact, and hanging in it—

Not the bell.

A chain. Fresh links, modern steel, looped through the bell's crown yoke and anchored to a deck ring some Halvern diver had installed within the last day. They'd secured it in place rather than remove it—no time to cut it free before sealing operations, so they'd locked it down instead, planning to extract it properly once the site was theirs.

Freya hung in the water and stared at it, and felt something cold move through her that had nothing to do with the lake.

Brase had known. Not suspected—known. He'd prioritized the bell on day one because he understood exactly what she understood: that the bell was the difference between a story and a fact.

Beside her, Sefa's lamp steadied on the chain. Then it moved to Freya's face, and even through a mask, even in green-tinted lamplight, Sefa's expression asked the question plainly: *Now what?*

Freya drew her knife.

Modern anchor-grade steel against forty-year-old bronze fittings and a deck ring that had slept in silt since before she was born. She set the blade against the shackle pin and leaned her whole body into it. The pin gave a millimeter. Silt bloomed around them like smoke. Above, the winch sound changed pitch—closer, or heavier load, she couldn't tell which and couldn't afford to care.

Second attempt. The pin groaned, gave another millimeter, stopped.

Then Sefa's hands closed over hers—not pulling her away, but adding force. Two bodies on one blade, four lungs' worth of leverage, and the pin shrieked free in a burst of rust and sediment that whited out both their lamps.

In the sudden blindness, Freya felt the bell come loose into her arms. Heavy. Real. Cold as a verdict. She wrapped the lift line around its yoke with fingers that felt like someone else's, clipped it, checked it twice, and sent it up the line toward the channel mouth and whatever waited there.

It was when she turned back that she saw her gauge.

* * *

**Sefa**

She saw it in Freya's posture before she saw the dial: the particular stillness of a diver reading bad news twice. Sefa swung her own lamp over. Freya's primary gauge read nine hundred psi. Should have been near two thousand. The cold, the exertion, the depth—or a slow leak somewhere in the last hour, invisible in the dark, patient as everything else in this lake.

Nine hundred psi. The channel transit alone would take six hundred if they pushed it. Less if the current had turned early. More if the current had turned early and disagreed with them.

Freya looked up and shook her head once. *Keep going.* Pointed deeper down the passageway—toward the captain's country, toward anything else worth having.

Sefa took her wrist and turned the gauge toward her own lamp so there could be no ambiguity, then made the signal that ended all arguments underwater: thumb up. *Surface. Now.*

For a moment Freya didn't move. Sefa watched her fight it—the whole expedition balanced on this descent, the journal, the names, her father, eleven years of exile, all of it screaming *one more compartment, one more minute*—and Sefa recognized the expression because she'd worn it herself, once, in different water, when wanting something badly enough had nearly cost her everything.

She put her hand flat on Freya's chest, over the lungs. Held it there. *Breathe. Choose.*

Freya breathed. Chose.

They turned for the channel, and the lake turned against them.

The current hit at the junction where the passage split—Freya's lower route, chosen with such certainty on the way in, now funneling the reversing tide into a river running the wrong way. Their lamps caught silt streaming past them, horizontal, fast. Freya went first, hauling herself along the channel wall hand over hand, and Sefa followed, and the numbers got worse with every meter. Six hundred psi. Five hundred. The exit crack they'd slid through at dawn showed as a gray smudge far above, impossibly far, and the current pressed them back down toward it like a hand on the crown of the head.

Three hundred psi, and they were barely halfway.

Freya's movements changed. Sefa saw it happening—the economy fraying at the edges, a kick slightly wrong, a grip missed and recovered. Carbon dioxide building. The body's quiet mutiny. At two hundred psi Freya's regulator started breathing hard, each inhalation a struggle, and at one-fifty she stopped moving entirely, clinging to the channel wall, and her lamp swung wildly and settled on Sefa with an expression Sefa would carry for the rest of her life.

Not panic. Calculation. Freya Amberdale, drowning in a glacier, was calculating how to die inconveniently slowly so as not to slow her partner down.

"No," Sefa said, into her regulator, uselessly, and unclipped her spare hose.

* * *

**Freya**

The second regulator came out of the dark and hit her chin, and instinct did what pride couldn't: her hand shot up and took it.

Shared air. Buddy breathing, the old way, one mouthpiece between two people ascending a frozen throat while a glacier decided whether to keep them. They'd never rehearsed it together. They'd known each other nine days. And it worked anyway—Sefa taking two breaths, passing it; Freya taking two, passing it back—with a rhythm that assembled itself out of nothing, out of trust extended on credit, out of the simple arithmetic that two people alternating breaths had twice the time of one person spending hers alone.

Between exchanges, in the seconds when the mouthpiece wasn't hers, Freya existed in a strange silver silence. Sound fell away. The current roared distantly, her heartbeat filled her skull, and the world contracted to a single point of warmth passed hand to hand in the dark.

*This,* she thought, absurdly, in the last hundred feet. *This is what he was protecting.*

Her father had torn the pages out and sunk a ship and chosen exile, all to keep his family clear of men like Brase—and Freya had spent eleven years hating him for the exile without understanding the shape of the choice. Now, breathing on borrowed air, she understood it completely. He had done what Sefa was doing. Given his air to someone else. Paid for it in years.

The gray smudge above grew. Became a crack. Became a doorway.

They surfaced into the channel's upper chamber—a pocket of trapped air under the ice, blue-lit, echoing with drips—and Freya tore her mask off and coughed and coughed and hung onto the ice lip while her lungs remembered their job. Sefa surfaced beside her, spat her regulator out, and for a long moment neither of them said anything. There was nothing to say that the breathing hadn't already said.

Above them, through the thinning ice, daylight. The tide had turned. Through the water below, faint but unmistakable, came a new sound: engines. Many engines. The fleet, moving on the lake.

Sefa laughed—a short, cracked, disbelieving sound. "We have it," she said. "We actually have it."

Freya looked up at the ceiling of ice between them and the sky, and listened to the engines, and did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding since the bell left her hands. The lift line had brought the bell up to the channel mouth—but the channel mouth was on the open lake side, past the pocket, past the narrowing. And between that spot and the shore lay a mile of thinning spring ice, directly in the path of Corvel Brase's recovery operation.

The bell was up. But it wasn't out.

And through the ice overhead, moving fast, came the crunch and grind of vehicles—Halvern's tracked transports, running the lake shelf toward the channel, toward the lift line, toward the bronze proof sitting unattended in the dark.

Sefa heard it too. Her face went still.

"They'll find the line," she said.

Freya pulled herself toward the narrow crawl that led out of the pocket, muscles screaming, lungs raw, and found her voice came out steady, which surprised her.

"Then we'd better be there when they do."
