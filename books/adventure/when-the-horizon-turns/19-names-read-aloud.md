## 19. Names Read Aloud

The valley hall had been a granary once, before Karstia fell and the grain stopped moving. Sefa could still see it in the bones of the place: the high slit windows built to keep out thieves rather than weather, the iron rings in the beams where sacks had hung, the floor worn into a shallow bowl by eighty years of boots carrying weight from one end of the room to the other. Now it held people instead of grain. They'd come down out of the diaspora villages all morning—by truck, by mule, on foot through the last dry hours before the monsoon made the roads into rivers—and they filled the hall the way water fills a hull: quietly, completely, without asking permission.

Sefa stood at the back with her grandmother's shawl around her shoulders and her hands shaking.

Not cold. The hall was warm, too warm, three hundred bodies and no ventilation worth the name. Her hands shook because in forty minutes she was going to stand up in front of all of them and recite, from memory, the names of eleven men who had sold a country.

She'd copied those names twice—once onto paper for the archive, once into herself, the way her grandmother had taught her to memorize lullabies when she was small enough to sleep against a shoulder. *Don't write it down first,* Grandmother used to say, holding Sefa's fingers against her own throat so the girl could feel the words hum. *Write it here. Paper burns. Throats remember.* It had seemed like an old woman's superstition then, one more piece of a dead nation's furniture. Now it was the only vault left. Brase's raiders had taken the archive at dawn—Sefa had seen the smoke from the ridge, thin and businesslike, the smoke of files burning rather than buildings—and every page they'd hidden was ash or confiscation. What remained was what lived in two heads. Hers and Freya's.

Freya sat near the front, on a bench beside an old man who kept touching her arm as if confirming she was solid. She looked wrecked. She looked like a woman who had surfaced from a lake that wanted to keep her, which was accurate. Her hair was still stiff with mineral crust from the fifth-tide dive, and there was a bandage at her wrist where the ice channel had taken its toll, and when the hall's noise crested she didn't flinch at it, just watched the crowd with the flat patient attention of someone reading terrain.

The bell sat on a trestle table beside her.

It was smaller than Sefa had expected. Salvage fiction liked bells big as bathtubs, bronze monsters you needed a crane for. The Vesper Kestrel's bell was barely wider than a dinner plate, green-black with forty years under the lake, and the Karstian crest—the same crest that had started everything, stamped on a chest coughed up by a flood river—was worn almost smooth on its face. Almost. Not quite. Enough letters survived that the elders who'd examined it that afternoon had gone silent, and then one of them, a woman named Dagny Holt with forearms like dock rope, had begun to cry without making any sound about it.

That was the thing Sefa kept failing to explain to herself: how much weight a small object could hold. Halvern Deep Recovery had spent millions charting this basin, and what the money had been hunting was leverage—names to sell back to governments, blackmail priced by the syllable. But the elders hadn't gone to the bell looking for leverage. They'd gone looking for confirmation. And the bell had given it, the way a grandmother's voice gives it: *yes, that's real, yes, that happened, yes, you're not crazy.*

A man climbed onto the granary platform where the weighing scales used to sit. He was young—younger than Sefa—with the careful diction of someone educated in exile schools, and he introduced himself as Tam Vessik of the archive committee, though everyone already knew why they were here.

"We are not going to perform history tonight," he said. "We're going to do something harder. We're going to check it."

Murmur ran through the hall like wind through wheat.

"Two days ago," Vessik said, "a woman I'd never met mailed us pages torn from a ship's logbook. Yesterday morning those pages burned. Some of you are angry about that. I'm angry about that." He let it sit. "But the woman who sent them is standing in this room, and she can say the contents with her eyes shut, and so can another woman in this room whose family served the other side of this story. So before we mourn anyone, before we curse anyone, we're going to do what our grandparents did when the maps were redrawn and nobody official would tell us the truth anymore. We're going to test memory against memory until what survives is true."

He turned toward the back of the hall. "Sefa Marun."

* * *

Walking to the platform took eleven steps and lasted approximately a year.

Sefa had briefed executives. She had presented survey findings to rooms full of people whose combined salaries exceeded the valley's annual economy. None of it had felt like this, because none of it had ever mattered whether she was believed. Here, belief was the whole cargo.

She faced the hall. Found Freya's eyes. Freya gave her a single nod, guide to diver: *the line holds.*

"The logbook named eleven men," Sefa said. Her voice came out steadier than her hands. "Officials of the Karstian border ministry and two officers of the northern fleet, who arranged in the winter before the fall to move the treasury reserves, the census records, and the evacuation manifests out of the country and sell their contents to Vessany and to Halvern's predecessor company. The Vesper Kestrel carried the proof of the arrangement. It was sunk to bury it."

"Names," said Dagny Holt, from the front row. Not hostile. Hungry.

"I'll give them slowly," Sefa said, "because your job is to stop me when I'm wrong."

She gave the first name. *Minister of Borders Oren Vask.*

The hall did something she hadn't anticipated. It didn't cheer or gasp. It went to work. An old man two rows back closed his eyes and moved his lips, counting something on an internal abacus. A woman in a headscarf pulled a battered notebook from her coat—actual paper, smuggled, hoarded—and began flipping pages with the speed of long practice. Somewhere in the balcony a teenager whispered into a phone, and Sefa realized with a jolt that the diaspora had done what the archive couldn't: scattered itself across a hundred private servers, a hundred family group chats, a hundred grandmothers' memories, and no corporate raid could burn all of it.

"Vask," repeated the old man with the internal abacus. "There's a song. My mother sang it. *Vask locked the gate and pocketed the key.*" He frowned. "But the song says Vask was fleet, not ministry."

"Fleet auxiliary," Sefa said. "The logbook lists him under both postings. That discrepancy is in the text."

The old man exhaled slowly. "Then the song is right and my memory of the song is wrong, which is worse." A ripple of grim laughter moved through the hall, and Sefa felt the room tilt from audience to crew.

Second name. Third. Each one landed and was taken apart—checked against songs, against ledger fragments, against a wedding invitation from 1979 that named a godfather, against the fact that one family in the eastern villages still refused, to this day, to speak aloud the surname Kellam, and had never known why until now. That one undid Sefa a little. A family had carried an unexplained wound for forty years, a superstition of silence passed down like a birthmark, and she was the person telling them where the scar came from.

By the seventh name, the verification had found its rhythm: Sefa spoke, the hall tested, the truth rang or it didn't. Twice she hesitated and the room waited without pressure, because they understood—she could feel them understand—that memory under pressure was exactly what they'd come to witness. Once she got a patronymic wrong and Dagny Holt corrected her gently, *"his mother was Ilse, not Ilva; Ilva was the sister who emigrated"*—and Sefa wrote the correction into herself the way her grandmother had taught her, feeling it settle behind her sternum.

The ninth name was the one she dreaded.

"Officer of the northern fleet," she said, "seconded to the border commission. Aldous Amberdale."

The hall changed temperature.

She heard it before she understood it—a shift in the quality of the silence, the sound of three hundred people arriving at the same thought at slightly different speeds. *Amberdale.* The name that had been a curse word in these valleys for a generation. The name attached to the incident that had gotten a guide blacklisted from two countries. The name of the woman sitting five rows from the front with a bandaged wrist and her father's journal in her bag.

Freya stood up.

Nobody had asked her to. Nobody had told her not to. She walked to the platform with the unhurried gait of someone crossing scree, and she stood next to Sefa—not behind, not opposite, *beside*—and faced the hall.

"You should hear this part from me," Freya said. "Since it's mine."

* * *

What followed, Sefa would spend a long time trying to describe afterward, and never quite manage.

Freya didn't defend her father. That was the first thing. Sefa had braced for defense—the fierce daughter's speech, the reframing, the *you don't know what he sacrificed*—and none of it came. Freya opened the journal, read the relevant passage aloud in a flat clear voice, and let the words do what words do.

*"I tore the pages because they named the men who sold us, and I tore them because naming them would have killed the people I love faster than the men ever could. I helped sink the ship. I chose the lie over the light. I have told myself for forty years that this was protection. Tonight I am no longer sure it wasn't just fear wearing protection's coat."*

Silence, after. The deep kind, load-bearing.

Then Dagny Holt rose from the front row, joints cracking, and looked up at Freya with eyes that had clearly done this kind of arithmetic before, over longer decades and worse losses.

"My brother was on the evacuation manifests," she said. "Karstia's last manifests. The ones your father's ship was carrying proof about. When the country fell, those records vanished, and four hundred families—including mine—spent forty years not knowing if our people died in the flooding or were resettled or were sold. We couldn't mourn properly. You can't mourn without a body and you can't hope without a record, and he took both away from us in one night." Her voice didn't rise. That was the terrible part of it; it stayed level as a lake surface. "So I want to say this carefully, girl, because I've had two days and you've had less."

"I'm listening," Freya said.

"He wasn't a villain. Villains don't write apologies." Dagny tapped the journal without touching it. "And he wasn't a hero, whatever the young ones are already deciding, because heroes don't drown the truth under a glacier and let four hundred families rot in the not-knowing. He was a man who loved specific people more than he loved everyone else, and that is the oldest sin there is, and half the time it's also the reason any of us survive." She looked around the hall, then back. "We will not make him a monument. We'll put him where he belongs—in the account, with the cost listed honestly. Mourning without absolution. Can you live with that?"

Freya's jaw worked once. Sefa watched her take it the way she'd watched her take the ice channel: assessing the load, deciding it would hold, stepping on anyway.

"Yes," Freya said. "That's more than I hoped for. That's the only honest thing anyone's offered me since this started."

Dagny nodded once, briskly, and then—to Sefa's lasting astonishment—reached up and gripped Freya's bandaged wrist, briefly, hard, the way you grip a rope hand-over-hand, and sat back down.

The hall breathed out. And then, because grief in this community had always traveled with work, Tam Vessik called for the tenth and eleventh names, and the verification resumed, and the machine of memory ground forward into the night.

* * *

They finished past midnight.

The eleventh name checked true against a fragment of census microfilm that a schoolteacher had kept sewn into a coat lining since the evacuation, and when it rang correct the hall didn't erupt—it settled, the way a structure settles when the last beam is set. Vessik read the final tally: eleven names, ten verified outright, one corrected and confirmed, zero disproven. Then he did the thing Sefa would think about for years. He didn't declare victory. He asked the elders to choose where the record would live.

"Not in one place," Dagny Holt said immediately, before he'd finished the question. "One place is a target. We learned that yesterday." Murmurs of agreement. "The names go into the songs—that's already done, some of those songs were carrying pieces of this for forty years without us knowing why. The tally goes to every village hall that wants it, handwritten, no copies centralized. And the original testimony—" she glanced at Sefa and Freya—"stays in throats. Yours two especially. You're the vaults now. Congratulations and condolences."

"That's not how archives work," said the teenager from the balcony, aggrieved.

"It's how *ours* works," Dagny said, "which is why it's still here and the official one is smoke."

Afterward the hall emptied slowly, people leaving in clusters, talking low, the way people talk after a funeral that went the way funerals should. Sefa ended up at the trestle table with the bell, alone except for Freya, who came and stood beside her and looked down at the worn crest the way you look at a route you've finally topped out on—not triumphant, just emptied.

"Your grandmother's shawl," Freya said. "You wore it tonight."

"She'd have wanted a report." Sefa touched the fringe. "She used to sing me a song about a bell that would ring when the country came home. I thought it was a fairy tale. A bell, Freya. Of all the things in that wreck, we risked our lives for a *bell*, and it turns out the fairy tale knew what it was doing."

"Bells don't prove anything legally," Freya said. "Brase will point that out if he ever surfaces anywhere again. Provenance, chain of custody, all his vocabulary."

"Bells don't need lawyers." Sefa turned the crest toward the lamplight. "They need ears. There were three hundred pairs in that hall tonight, and every one of them goes home to somebody."

Freya was quiet a moment. Outside, the first fat drops of the monsoon had begun hitting the granary's tin roof, irregular, testing, the sky clearing its throat before the real performance.

"There's a messenger coming up the valley road tomorrow," Freya said at last. "Osk sent word down before the storm closed the pass. Official correspondence—from Vessany. Both ministries." She said it lightly, but Sefa had spent enough hours sharing a regulator with this woman to hear the current underneath. "About blacklists. About what gets lifted and what doesn't."

"And you're not opening it tonight?"

Freya looked at the rain beginning to silver the slit windows, and shook her head once, slow, like a climber declining a summit push with weather coming in.

"Whatever it says," she said, "it says the same thing in the morning. And tonight I'd rather keep—" she gestured vaguely at the hall, the bell, the whole improbable evening— "this. Before the paperwork tells me what it cost."

Sefa understood. She draped the shawl tighter and picked up the lamp, and together they left the bell sleeping on its trestle in the emptying hall while outside the monsoon arrived in earnest, drumming the valley roof, filling the river, turning the world the color of beginnings.
