## 20. The Price of the Truth

The letter from the Vessany Border Commission arrived on a Tuesday, in an envelope so official it seemed to hum. Heavy cream stock, embossed crest, the kind of paper that had never been touched by rain.

Freya read it three times on the steps of the valley hall before she could make herself understand it.

*In light of recent disclosures and verified testimony regarding the Vesper Kestrel incident, the Border Commission of Vessany hereby lifts the blacklist imposed upon F. Amberdale in respect of the Vergha corridor and associated lowland routes, effective immediately.*

She read that sentence four times. Then she read the next one.

*The high glacial reserves, including the Kestrel basin and all approaches thereto, remain closed to unauthorized access under the amended Mountain Sanctity Act. This closure is permanent.*

Permanent. The word sat on the page like a stone dropped into still water.

Sefa found her there, sitting with the letter in her lap while the valley went about its business around them—elders carrying crates of records into the hall, children chasing each other between the drying racks, someone tuning a fiddle in a doorway the way people here had tuned fiddles for generations, including the forty years when no map admitted they existed.

"Well?" Sefa said.

"They gave me back half my life." Freya held up the letter. "The flat half."

Sefa sat down beside her on the step. She didn't say anything for a while. That was one of the things Freya had learned to trust about her—the willingness to let silence do its work instead of rushing to fill it with comfort.

"The Vergha's open," Sefa said finally. "That's not nothing. You pioneered that route. They banned you for it, and now it's got your name on the survey markers whether they like it or not."

"It's got *Amberdale Expeditions* stamped on gear rusting in caves," Freya said. "They didn't put my name on anything. They just stopped erasing it."

"Same thing, some days."

"No." Freya folded the letter along its crease, carefully, the way her father had folded his last letter to her. "It isn't. And I think I'm only now learning the difference."

* * *

The hearing—if you could call it that—was held in the valley hall two days later, because the diaspora council insisted that anything touching Karstian history be decided on Karstian ground, or as close to it as geography allowed. Vessany's lawyers came up from the capital in two grey cars, and Corvel Brase came in a third, alone, without his fleet, without his divers, without the smile he'd worn on the ice.

He looked smaller out of his element. Freya noticed it the way you notice a mountain missing its snowcap—same shape, wrong color, something essential stripped away.

The hall was full. Elders in the front rows, the ones who'd spent three nights verifying names against songs and ledgers and a wedding shawl embroidered with dates. Diaspora families who'd driven up from the river towns. A handful of journalists, because Freya had insisted on journalists, because cameras had been the whole point on the ridge and she wasn't going to let the truth go back underground now that it had learned to breathe.

Osk sat near the back with his arm in a sling and his pride intact, which was more than Brase's men had left him. He caught Freya's eye and tipped an imaginary cap.

The reading of findings took most of the morning. The elders' verification held—every name matched, every date corroborated by sources no corporation could buy, because they'd never been for sale. The officials who had sold Karstia's borders in exchange for positions and patents were named aloud, one by one, their descendants present in some cases, pale in others. One elderly woman stood when her grandfather's name was read and said, clearly, "We knew. We always knew. We just never had the words in the right order." Then she sat down again, and the hall let her have her silence.

Then it was Brase's turn.

He stood at the front like a man giving a quarterly report. "Halvern Deep Recovery acknowledges the historical significance of the materials recovered from the Vesper Kestrel wreck site," he said. "Our charter was, from inception, an effort at professional preservation—"

"Your charter," said Elder Vashen, who was ninety-one and had crossed the border the night Karstia fell, "was a salvage license bought from a government that didn't own what it was selling. Sit down, Mr. Brase. You've been sitting down your whole life; you're just standing up for once."

Someone laughed. It broke something open in the room—not cruelty, exactly, but the specific relief of a community that had spent forty years being polite to power and was done with it.

Brase sat down.

The legal accounting came after lunch. Freya listened to it the way you listen to a river gauge: numbers rising, nothing you can do but watch.

Halvern's charter: revoked, pending review, effectively dead. The salvage fleet impounded at Osk's dock—Osk charging them storage, which Freya thought was the single most beautiful act of commerce she'd ever witnessed. The survey charts Sefa had copied and mailed: seized as evidence, then released to the diaspora archive, where they belonged. Brase himself: no charges. Not one.

"That's it?" Freya said, when the advocate from the capital finished. "He tried to buy blackmail material off a drowned nation and sell it back to the governments that helped drown it. He cornered us on ice. He captured Osk. He raided an archive at dawn. And he walks?"

"He walks," the advocate agreed, unbothered. "Because everything he did, he did through channels. Charter documents. Salvage permits. Contracts of employment. Legally, Mr. Brase is a businessman whose venture failed. Morally—" she shrugged, a small precise shrug that carried more weight than the whole legal summary— "morally is not my department. The archive has him. History has him. He'll spend the rest of his career explaining why a dead nation's bell rang in his face."

Freya looked across the hall at Brase. He was watching the elders pack away the verified ledgers with an expression she couldn't quite name—not defeat, not rage. Something more like recalculation. A man already pricing his next venture, whatever it would be.

He caught her looking. For a moment, something human flickered through—maybe regret, maybe just recognition, one calculator acknowledging another.

"You could have sold those pages," he said quietly, when the hall emptied and they stood briefly in the same current of departing bodies. "Restored name, lifted blacklists, both of them. I offered you the whole package."

"I know."

"You got half. You understand you got half."

"I understand I got the half that's mine," Freya said. "The other half was never yours to sell. That was the whole problem with you, Mr. Brase. You kept trying to buy things that were already owned."

She walked out into the afternoon and did not look back. But she heard him behind her, saying something to one of his remaining staff in a voice stripped of charm, and she thought: *there he is. The real ledger.* Some men never stop counting. They just change currencies.

* * *

The second letter came a week later, forwarded through the diaspora archive with a note in Sefa's handwriting: *You should see this before you decide anything.*

It was from the other country. The northern one. The one whose blacklist had cost her more than Vessany's ever had, because the north held the ranges she'd loved first—the granite spires above the Merrow valleys, the icefields where she'd made her first solo crossing at nineteen, the routes she'd cut with her own hands and her father's old pitons before anyone knew her name meant anything bad.

*The Ministry of Interior, following joint inquiry with the Vessany Border Commission and testimony provided by the Karstian Heritage Archive, hereby reviews and revokes the entry ban imposed upon F. Amberdale. All designated wilderness areas of the northern ranges are reopened to licensed guiding activity under standard permit conditions.*

She read it standing in the doorway of the room she'd rented above the archive, the same room where she'd copied pages from memory while Sefa kept watch, and her hands shook.

Not with joy. That was the strange part. She'd imagined this moment for six years—in dockside bunks, in storm camps, in the long gray hours when the blacklist felt less like paperwork than like weather, something atmospheric that followed her everywhere. In every version, the letter arrived and something in her chest unlocked and she wept or shouted or booked passage north by nightfall.

Instead she stood very still and felt the weight of what the letter didn't say.

*Under standard permit conditions.* Her father's routes in the north—the ones he'd cut, the ones she'd extended—ran through country that bordered the old Karstian frontier. Country that had been quietly absorbed, renamed, resettled, the way all conquered country gets. The descendants of the men in the torn pages lived there too. Their grandchildren ran the mining concerns and the guide associations and the ministry offices that stamped permits.

The truth was out now. Read aloud, verified, re-homed. And the mountains were open.

But walking back into those ranges wouldn't be return. It would be arrival in a place that had moved on without her, wearing her family's name like a scar everyone recognized and nobody discussed. Every client she guided past a renamed peak would be a small negotiation with history. Every permit office would remember. Restoration, she understood now, wasn't a door swinging back open. It was a different door, in a different wall, opening onto a room that looked almost like the one she'd lost.

Sefa climbed the stairs and leaned in the doorway, reading over her shoulder the way she did, uninvited and welcome.

"So?" she said.

"So it's done. Both of them. I'm free."

"You don't sound free."

Freya set the letter down on the table, next to her father's journal—or what remained of it after she'd given the bulk to the archive. One page stayed with her, folded into her jacket pocket where it had lived since the ridge. His apology. Four lines in his cramped hand, written the year she turned twelve, addressed to a daughter he apparently hadn't trusted himself to face:

*I took the easy treason and called it love. If you ever learn what I did, hate the deed and keep the man, or don't—it's your right either way. I only ask that you climb higher than I dared and look farther than I could stand to.*

"I sound like someone doing arithmetic," Freya said. "Six years of exile. Two blacklists. A father who sank a ship to protect me from a truth that ended up protecting everyone but costing me the one sanctuary I actually wanted." She tapped the closed highlands notice, still lying on the table beside the letters. "Vessany keeps the Kestrel basin locked forever. Permanently. The glacier will keep retreating, the mast will keep coming up, and I will never stand on that shore again except as a visitor on someone else's permit."

"Chapter twenty-one of your life," Sefa said. "I hear we're taking some kids up to see the mast before winter. Elders arranged it. Legal, permitted, chaperoned." She grinned. "You're listed as technical advisor. Which means you get to stand on someone else's permit and pretend it doesn't sting."

"It stings."

"Good. Stinging means it's real." Sefa picked up the northern letter, scanned it, and set it down with new care. "Merrow valleys. Granite spires. Your first icefield." Her voice softened in a way Freya had heard only twice—once over a shared regulator at fifty feet, once on the ridge with the bell. "We could go north. You and me. New outfitting license, new name on the door. Those ranges never knew your disgrace. Only your skill. You said it yourself."

"I said a lot of things on a drowning shoreline."

"You meant most of them."

Freya looked at the letters spread on the table like a hand of cards—one lifted, one upheld, one revoked, one permanent. An accounting. Not the one she'd wanted, not the one she'd imagined in six years of cold bunks. But honest. That was more than her father had managed, more than Brase would ever manage, and maybe honesty was the only inheritance worth carrying forward.

"My father chose badly for loving reasons," she said. "I'm going to choose well for the same ones. There's a difference, and I intend to spend the rest of my life proving I can tell them apart."

She gathered the letters, squared their edges, and slid them into the journal's oilskin wrap—except the apology page, which went back into her pocket, where it belonged.

"North, then," she said. "But first—the lake. Before the ice takes the mast. There are some young Karstians who need to see what their grandparents survived, and I promised the elders a guide who knows the approach."

Sefa smiled. "Technical advisor."

"Don't push it."

Outside, the valley light was turning gold, and somewhere below the fiddle had found its tune, and far to the south, behind a lock that would never open for her, the glacier went on retreating from a wreck that had finally told the truth—and the horizon, as her father might have said, was turning toward something none of them could own.
