## 22. First Ascent, New Name

The letter came up the valley on the third day, riding in the mail satchel of a boy who climbed better than he walked.

Freya watched him come down the moraine with the easy, careless footwork of someone who had never been told a mountain could kill him, and she felt the old ache start in her chest before she even saw the envelope. She knew that kind of paper when she saw it. Official weight. Government crease. The last time an envelope like that had found her, it had ended two careers and one country's willingness to spell her name correctly.

The boy reached the rope-up point where the team was sorting gear, flushed and pleased with himself.

"Freya Amberdale?" He said it like a question, but his eyes said he'd already checked her against the wanted posters of his imagination and found nothing.

"Guide," she said. "That's the name on the license."

He handed over the envelope, took the coin Sefa flipped him without being asked—Sefa always knew when coin was needed—and went back up the trail the way he'd come, light as weather.

Freya turned the envelope over once. Vessany's border office seal. And beneath it, stamped smaller, the crest of the second country—the one whose name she still couldn't say without tasting iron. *Kestrel Province.* They'd kept their old name after Karstia dissolved, the way a widow keeps a ring.

"You going to open it?" Sefa asked. She was coiling rope with the economy of someone who never wasted a motion, and she didn't look up.

"Camp," Freya said.

"It's from a government."

"They're all from governments." Freya slid it into the inner pocket of her jacket, over her heart, where important things went to be carried and not yet faced. "It'll keep until camp."

Sefa finally looked at her then, and there was something in her face that wasn't quite a smile but lived in the same neighborhood. "You know what my grandmother used to say about letters from officials."

"She had a lot to say about officials."

"She said: *a letter is just a door you haven't decided to walk through yet.*" Sefa snapped the coil tight. "You've been carrying this one for three days already. I watched you not-open it at the dock."

"That was a different letter."

"It was the same letter with a different stamp." Sefa shouldered her pack. "Come on. We're losing light, and your clients want to see the col before dark."

*Your clients.* Freya let the phrase settle over her like sun-warmed stone. Not her smugglers. Not her desperate, illegal, off-the-books parties slipping across borders that had closed to her name. Clients. Paying, permitted, insured clients, walking into mountains under a license that read **AMBERDALE & MARUN — MOUNTAIN OUTFITTERS**, registered in a province that had never heard of the Amberdale incident, the Vergha crossing ban, or any of the other small bureaucratic deaths that had followed her for six years.

These ranges didn't know her disgrace. They only knew her skill. That was the whole business plan, and so far it was working.

She checked the sky out of habit—high cirrus, no build, six hours of good light—and started up.

* * *

The mountain was called Serren Vosk, which meant nothing in particular in a language nobody spoke anymore, and it stood eleven thousand feet above a valley of larch and slate-green water. It was not a famous peak. It was not a peak anyone had died on or named wars after. It was exactly the kind of mountain Freya had spent her whole life ignoring, because there was always something bigger, harder, more forbidden on the horizon.

That was before the horizon turned.

Now she led four clients up its western flank at a pace calibrated to the slowest of them—a retired ferry engineer named Doss who had more money than knees, and his niece Pell, nineteen and hungry in the way Freya remembered being hungry, all appetite and no scar tissue. Behind them came the Voss brothers, quiet men who ran a bakery in the provincial capital and had saved for this climb for two years. None of them were Karstian. None of them needed to be. But when Sefa had posted the expedition notice in the diaspora hall back in the lake country, all four had signed up within a week, and Freya understood why without asking. There were people now—whole generations of them—who wanted to stand on high ground that belonged to no one's grief.

"Guide." Pell caught up to her on the switchbacks, breathing hard, refusing to admit it. "Is it true you crossed the Vergha Pass alone? In a storm?"

"Where'd you hear that?"

"Sefa tells stories at the hall. Everyone comes to hear them." Pell grinned. "She says you navigated by memory of routes you invented yourself."

"I tell stories too," Freya said. "Mine are mostly about how she lost a boot to a crevasse and cried about it for a week."

Pell laughed, and the sound bounced off the rock and came back cleaner, and Freya thought: *this*. This was what the mountains had always been for, before they became borders, before they became evidence, before they became the place where her father had buried a nation's truth under forty years of ice. Mountains were just places where people carried each other. Everything else was paperwork.

They reached the high camp as the light went long and gold, and the Voss brothers got the tents up while Doss sat on a rock breathing thin air like a man renegotiating with God. Sefa had the stove going before Freya had the first anchor line set, because Sefa was the kind of partner who did half the work before you noticed the work existed.

It was only when the clients were fed and zipped into their tents, and the wind had dropped to that hush that means the mountain is listening, that Sefa nodded at Freya's jacket.

"Well?"

Freya took out the envelope. Three days of carrying it had softened the corners, worn the government crease into something almost gentle. She held it over the stove's small heat for a moment, an old guide's trick to loosen glue, and worked it open.

One page. One paragraph. A signature.

She read it twice.

Then she folded it along its original lines, very carefully, and put it back in her pocket.

Sefa waited. Sefa was very good at waiting; it was most of what diving was.

"The second blacklist," Freya said finally. "Kestrel Province. Revoked. Effective—" she checked the date, though she'd memorized it already, "—eleven weeks ago. They mailed it to my old address in Vessany. Someone there forwarded it. Took it eleven weeks and three countries to find me."

"And?"

"And it says I'm welcome to apply for a mountaineering permit in Kestrel Province." Freya looked at the fire. "It says the review board 'regrets the duration of the previous restriction.' Regrets. Eleven years of my life and they regret it the way you regret a shipping delay."

"What are you going to do?"

"I don't know." That was the truth, and it surprised her how easily it came out. Six months ago she would have lied, or joked, or gone silent. Now she just said it. "I spent so long wanting this. Every night on the river, every illegal crossing, every time some border clerk looked through me like I was weather—I wanted this exact piece of paper. My name, clean. Both names, clean. And now I have it, and it feels like—"

She stopped.

"Like reading your father's journal," Sefa said quietly.

Freya looked up. Of course Sefa would say that. Sefa had read it too, by firelight, in the weeks after the storm, when the two of them had copied the torn pages out from memory and checked them against the songs and the elders' records until the names rang true. Sefa knew what was in that journal better than almost anyone alive. She knew that Ander Amberdale had helped sink the *Vesper Kestrel*, had torn out the pages naming the men who sold Karstia, had chosen exile and disgrace and a daughter raised on silence—all to protect a family that the truth would have destroyed.

He'd chosen badly. He'd chosen for love. Both things were true, and the elders in the valley hall had said so out loud, and Freya had learned to hold both truths in one hand without dropping either.

"Yeah," Freya said. "Like that. You spend years thinking the thing you want will fix everything, and then you get it, and it turns out the thing was never the wound. The wound was the waiting."

Sefa poked the fire. Sparks went up and died among the real stars.

"My grandmother waited thirty years for Karstia to come back," Sefa said. "Every night, the lullaby, the maps she drew from memory. And when the truth finally surfaced—when we rang that bell on the ridge with the whole diaspora watching—she cried, and then she said the strangest thing." Sefa's voice did something careful. "She said, *I mourned a country for thirty years, and it turns out what I missed was a kitchen.*"

Freya huffed something that was almost a laugh. "What did she mean?"

"I think she meant that nations are abstractions, but kitchens are real. The smell of bread. People who know your name." Sefa glanced toward the client tents. "People who sign up to climb with you because of who you are, not what was taken from you."

The fire settled. Above them, Serren Vosk's summit ridge caught the last light and held it, white-gold, like a page waiting to be written on.

"Tomorrow," Freya said, "we put four people on a summit who have never summited anything. That's the whole job. That's enough."

"And the letter?"

Freya took it out one more time. Unfolded it. Read the regretful, bureaucratic, hard-won words again. Then, instead of folding it away, she held a corner to the stove flame until it caught, and dropped it burning into the fire ring, where it curled and blackened and gave up its ink to the dark.

Sefa didn't say anything for a long moment. "You waited three days to burn it?"

"I waited three days to decide I didn't need it." Freya watched the last corner glow and go gray. "Vessany lifted one ban and kept the other. Kestrel lifts theirs and says I'm welcome back—if I apply. If I ask permission. If I walk back through their door and let them decide who I am this time." She shook her head slowly. "I'm done letting countries decide who I am. I spent six years being *the woman on the blacklist*. Then one season being *the woman who exposed the truth*. Neither one was me. This—" she tapped the license tin, stenciled AMBERDALE & MARUN, propped against her pack, "—this is me. Chosen. Not inherited."

"Not inherited," Sefa repeated, and something passed across her face—her grandmother's kitchen, maybe, or the wreck below the ice, or the moment underwater when she'd pressed her regulator into a stranger's mouth because the stranger had become a sister somewhere between breaths.

"Besides," Freya added, "if I go back to Kestrel Province, who's going to keep you honest on the ice?"

"No one," Sefa said solemnly. "I'd be unbearable within a month."

* * *

Summit day came in cold and clear, the kind of morning that feels earned rather than given.

They left camp at four, roped in pairs, headlamps bobbing up the frozen scree like a slow constellation. Doss moved slower than yesterday but steadier, having made peace overnight with whatever bargain old men make with altitude. The Voss brothers climbed like they baked—patient, precise, no wasted flour. And Pell climbed like fire, always ten meters ahead until Freya's low whistle reeled her back.

At the col, the sun came up over ranges none of them could name, ridge after ridge marching east into haze, and Doss stopped and gripped the rope and said, hoarsely, "I ferried water for forty years. Never once saw it from up here." Nobody laughed. Everybody understood.

The final pitch was snow and honest work, and Freya led it the way her father had taught her—reading the slope, testing with the axe, trusting the mountain to tell the truth if you asked politely. Somewhere around the last hundred meters she realized she wasn't thinking about blacklists, or letters, or the man whose shadow she'd spent her whole life climbing out of. She was just climbing. It was the freest she had felt since she was a child on her father's shoulders, before either of them knew what he'd done.

They topped out at 9:14 in the morning.

Four clients, two guides, one small cairn, and a horizon that went on forever in every direction, unowned, unbanned, unbothered by the affairs of nations.

Sefa pulled a small cloth bundle from her pack. Inside was the ship's bell of the *Vesper Kestrel*—or rather, a bronze casting of it, made by the diaspora smiths from the original's mold, because the original itself now hung in the valley hall in the lake country, rung once a year on the anniversary of the Names Read Aloud. This replica traveled. It had been to three summits already. It was becoming a tradition, and traditions need only one person to decide they exist.

Sefa struck it once with her axe handle. The note went out thin and bright across the thin air, and Pell burst into tears without seeming to notice, and the Voss brothers bowed their heads, and Doss took off his hat.

"For everyone who couldn't climb," Sefa said. "And everyone who's still climbing."

Freya added, because it was her turn, because they alternated: "For the ones who chose badly, and the ones who choose better."

Then she did something she hadn't planned. She took out her father's single kept page—the apology, the one line he'd written for her alone, *I sank the truth to keep you afloat, and I only ever hoped you'd forgive the water*—and instead of reading it aloud, she simply held it up to the wind for a moment, letting the air move through her fingers and the paper both.

Then she put it away. Some inheritances you carry. Some you set down. The skill of knowing which was which—that, she decided, was the only thing her father had ever really left her, and it was enough.

They descended into the afternoon, and the mountain let them go.

* * *

Base camp, dusk. The clients slept the heavy sleep of the successful. Sefa was already in her bag, one arm flung out, asleep the instant she'd stopped moving, as always.

Freya sat up with the last of the tea, watching stars come out over the col, and that was when she saw the light coming up the valley—not a lantern, faster, steadier. A runner's lamp, moving fast, which meant news, which these days meant either money or trouble, and she'd learned to stop fearing both.

The runner was a girl, younger than Pell, breathless, carrying a message tube sealed with wax.

"From the valley hall," the girl gasped. "From Elder Vashen. She said—she said you'd want it tonight, not tomorrow. She said the survey team went back to the lake."

Freya broke the seal with her thumb.

The glacier had retreated further than any model predicted. The autumn melt had opened the eastern channel, and the *Vesper Kestrel*'s hull—forty years under ice, five months exposed—was now visible nearly to the waterline. Whole decks. Intact cabins. And in the captain's cabin, wedged behind the safe, the survey team had found something else: a second chest, smaller, sealed with the same bronze crest, addressed in handwriting Freya knew better than her own.

Addressed to her.

Elder Vashen had written one line at the bottom: *The lake keeps giving things back. Come see what else it kept.*

Freya read it twice, then looked up at the stars, then at Sefa's sleeping shape, and felt the old pull in her chest—not grief this time, not dread. Something closer to the feeling of standing at the base of an unclimbed route, reading the line, knowing it would cost everything and give back more.

"Sefa," she said, and her partner's eyes opened instantly, the way they always did, diver-reflexes, awake before the word finished. "How do you feel about going back to the lake?"

Sefa smiled in the dark. "I'll get the boats."
