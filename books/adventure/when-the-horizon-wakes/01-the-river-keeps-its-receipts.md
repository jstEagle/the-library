## 1. The River Keeps Its Receipts

The river had been lying to Edmund Dravenmoor all morning.

She knew it the way she knew most things about water—not from instruments, though she carried three of them, but from the particular quality of its silence. A river that runs honest makes a certain sound: a low continuous conversation between current and stone, constant as breathing. This one kept breaking off mid-sentence. Every few hundred meters the murmur would falter, resume, falter again, like a man rehearsing a story he hadn't quite memorized.

She knelt at the bow of her boat with a hand trailing in the brown water and let the cold climb her wrist to the elbow. Thirty-one degrees north of the survey line. Depth gauge reading four meters where the chart said seven. Silt in suspension so fine it turned the water the color of weak tea, which meant something upstream was shedding soil it shouldn't be shedding.

"Receipts," she said aloud, to no one, because ten years of solo work had made a habit of narrating to herself and she'd stopped being embarrassed by it somewhere around year two. "You're keeping receipts on somebody."

The delta did not answer. It never did. But it had a way of filing things away—bones, boats, whole villages—and producing them later, downstream, at its own convenience, usually when it could do the most damage. The old ferryman at Kessel's Landing used to say the river never lost anything, it only lent. She'd thought it a drunkard's proverb once. Seven years of surveying this watershed had revised her opinion.

Edmund eased the throttle forward and let the boat slide upriver into the green throat of the channel, past curtains of hanging vine that brushed the canopy like wet hair, past drowned trees standing hip-deep in their own reflection, their crowns still alive, still leafing, stubborn as anything. The air was thick enough to chew. It tasted of rot and green growth and, faintly, of smoke—not woodsmoke, not cooking fire. Something chemical underneath. She filed that away too.

Her contract this season was simple and unglamorous: hydrographic survey of the upper distributaries ahead of the monsoon, depth soundings, bank erosion markers, flow rates. Work nobody wanted because nobody lived up here and nothing here paid. Which suited her exactly. Ten years ago she'd learned that the fastest way out of a famous name was to go where the name meant nothing, and nothing meant less than a government chart of a river nobody used. Out here she wasn't *the daughter of*. Out here she was just the woman in the dented aluminum boat with the bad carburetor and the good eyes.

The sandbar appeared around the ninth bend.

She almost missed it. That was the thing people never understood about reading wilderness—it wasn't dramatic. It was a wrongness the size of a fingernail. The bar itself was ordinary: a pale crescent of deposited silt, maybe forty meters long, fringed with reeds, sitting low in the channel where the current slackened around the bend. Ordinary except for one detail, and the detail was this: the birds weren't using it.

Sandbars were real estate in this country. Whistling ducks claimed them by noon. Waders worked the margins in nervous little congregations. Turtles hauled out to bake until they looked like river stones. This bar sat empty in full daylight, and the reeds along its spine stood perfectly straight, unbent, unbroken, in a place where every other bar she'd passed bore the trampled geometry of wings and feet.

Edmund cut the engine and drifted.

* * *

She came ashore at the downstream tip, deliberately, the way she always came ashore anywhere—slow, loud, announcing herself to whatever might be listening. Old habit from country where you didn't want to startle anything with teeth. The mud took her boots to the ankle and held them with a sucking reluctance, and she waded the last few meters rather than fight it.

The gear was arranged in the center of the bar, in the open, in a line.

Not scattered. Not strewn the way water scatters things, not heaped the way panicked hands heap them. Arranged. Six items laid out parallel on a groundsheet that someone had staked down at the corners with cut saplings, the stakes driven flush, the corners squared. Even at twenty meters she understood she was looking at order, and order on an empty sandbar in the middle of nowhere is one of the most frightening sentences the natural world can write.

She crouched at the edge of the groundsheet without touching it and read.

Government issue. She knew the kit on sight—the olive-drab dry bags with the ministry's stencil half worn off, the collapsible water drums, the satellite phone case with its foam lining gaping open and empty, the medical pouch with a red cross stenciled on canvas gone gray with damp. Survey equipment, some of it: a theodolite case, a sediment sampling kit, a field notebook in a waterproof sleeve. The notebook was swollen fat with water, its pages fused into a single brick of pulp. Whatever it had said was gone.

Everything was water-stained. Not soaked—stained. The tide mark on each item sat at the same height, a clean horizontal line about eight centimeters up, as if the whole arrangement had been submerged briefly and evenly and then raised again. Or as if the river had come up over it and gone back down, which rivers do, but—

But the groundsheet was dry on top. The stakes were dry above the mudline. And the bar itself showed no scouring, no debris wrack, none of the litter a flood leaves behind the way a burglar leaves fingerprints. Water had touched these things gently, or been made to look like it had.

"No tracks," she murmured. She said it twice, because the first time didn't feel true.

She circled the site slowly, widening each ring, reading the mud the way other people read print. Mud keeps accounts better than paper does. It should have been a ledger of comings and goings: boot prints, drag marks, the scuffed chaos of a camp struck in haste or abandoned in fear. Instead the surface around the gear lay smooth as poured wax, marked only by the delicate stitched lines of bird feet near the reed fringe—bird feet that stopped, all of them, in a loose arc two meters short of the groundsheet, as if the ducks themselves had drawn a boundary and declined to cross it.

Eight centimeters of water had covered this bar recently enough to stain canvas. Eight people's worth of equipment had been left here. And not one footprint led to it or away from it.

Edmund stood very still in the heat and felt the unease arrive, the way it always did, low in the stomach first, then climbing.

People didn't leave here. That was the sentence her instincts kept writing and rewriting. People who walk away leave trails; even careful people leave compressed grass, snapped sedge, the ghost-sign of passage. People who are swept away leave gear torn and tangled, distributed by violence, snagged in roots. This gear had been *placed*. Squared corners. Flush stakes. An empty phone case set face-up, deliberate as a headstone.

Somebody had staged this bar. Somebody had wanted it found—or wanted it found looking exactly like this.

* * *

She spent two hours on that sandbar and photographed everything twice, once with flash and once without, and sketched the layout in her own notebook because paper doesn't fail the way batteries do. She measured the tide line. She sampled the silt under the groundsheet and found it undisturbed below two centimeters—no digging, no burial, nothing hidden beneath. She pried the medical pouch open with a stick, not her fingers, and inventoried it through the fabric: gauze, tape, ampoules, all present, all unused. Nobody had bled here. Nobody had bandaged anything.

The notebook she handled last. Waterproof sleeves failed eventually; everyone who worked rivers knew that. She worked the zip apart with a knife tip and teased free the top edge of the fused pages, and got exactly one fragment legible before the rest surrendered to pulp—a corner of a page, ink feathered but readable:

*Day 6 — V. insists the marker is natural formation. It is not. Aldous D. documented the same—*

And there the words drowned.

Edmund sat back on her heels with the fragment trembling slightly in her tweezers and the heat pressing down on her neck like a hand.

*Aldous D.*

There were perhaps four thousand people in the world named Aldous, and most of them were over seventy and lived in England. But there was exactly one Aldous whose name appeared in field notes written by government survey teams in this watershed, and his surname was stitched into her own skin whether she wore it or not.

Aldous Dravenmoor. Her father. Dead seven years this autumn, in a country he never told her he was returning to, on a dig he never told her existed, leaving behind a career like a cathedral and a daughter like a locked room.

She put the fragment in a sample bag with hands that were steadier than they had any right to be, and she told herself it was coincidence. Names echo. Surveyors read old papers; old papers carry old names. There was no reason on earth for a vanished government team to be chasing footnotes from a dead man's last expedition, and even less reason for those footnotes to lead to a sandbar with no footprints on it.

The river murmured its broken sentence at her. Faltered. Resumed.

She looked up at the tree line across the channel and realized, with a slow cold prickle between her shoulder blades, that the forest had gone quiet. Not peaceful-quiet. Attentive-quiet. The cicadas had stopped. Somewhere in the green dark a single bird called once, a question with no answer in it, and then nothing.

Edmund packed her kit in ninety seconds flat, pushed the boat off the bar stern-first, and did not stop moving until the ninth bend was behind her and the bar was a pale sliver in her wake. Only then did she allow herself the radio.

* * *

Base station was a tin-roofed office in Kessel's Landing staffed by a man named Oduya who answered on the fourth try, his voice arriving through static like a swimmer surfacing.

"Dravenmoor. You're supposed to be at marker fourteen by now."

"I'm at marker eleven and I'm going to stay there tonight." She kept her voice level, professional, the voice of a contractor reporting, not a woman reporting. "I found government equipment on a sandbar at the nine-bend reach. Ministry stencils. Survey kit. No personnel."

Static. Then: "Describe it."

She described it. The arrangement, the tide line, the missing tracks, the empty phone case. She described the notebook fragment and watched her own words carefully, giving him the surveyor's language, the neutral inventory, and only at the end—because she owed him honesty, if not everything—she said, "The notes reference Aldous Dravenmoor. My father. His last expedition."

The silence that followed had a different texture than the static. Longer. More considered.

"There was a team through that reach," Oduya said finally. "Ministry hydrographic unit, eight personnel, running pre-monsoon gauging stations. They checked in twelve days ago and haven't since. We've had it flagged." A pause. "You found no bodies."

"None. No blood. No struggle. No tracks at all, Oduya. On soft mud. In a week-old campsite. That's not possible unless someone cleaned it, and you can't clean mud that thoroughly without leaving cleaning marks, and there aren't any. Either that gear fell out of the sky or—" She stopped herself.

"Or what?"

"Or it was placed there," she said. "By people who arrived and left without touching the ground. Which is also impossible. So my professional recommendation is that your eight-person team didn't leave that sandbar. They were taken from it. By something that plans."

Another silence, longer, and when Oduya spoke again his voice had flattened into the bureaucratic register she'd learned to dread, the register that meant a decision had already been made above his pay grade. "Your instructions are to continue the survey. Mark the location, log the find, move on. A recovery unit will handle it after the weather window."

"After the monsoon," Edmund said. "You're sending a recovery unit after the monsoon."

"The schedule is the schedule."

"Oduya." She closed her eyes. The boat rocked beneath her, patient, complicit. "Those are eight people. And whatever took them knew how to make a scene look like nothing happened. That's not a crocodile. That's not the river. That's a person, or persons, and they're still operating in my survey area, and you're telling me to keep working alone in it."

"I'm telling you what I'm authorized to tell you." For just a moment the bureaucrat slipped and something human sounded through, tired and young. "Edmund. Log it. Move on. Please."

The line clicked dead.

* * *

She anchored that night in the lee of marker eleven, in a backwater where the current forgot about you, and ate cold rice and tinned fish and did not sleep.

Instead she sat in the stern with her father's oldest notebook—the one thing of his she'd kept, the one thing she'd taken from the estate sale before the auctioneers arrived, bought back with money she pretended she hadn't wept over—and read it by lamplight while moths threw themselves against the glass.

He'd written it thirty years ago, young and hungry, before the fame, before the foundations and the documentaries and the slow corruption that fame pours into a man like resin into wood. The handwriting was fierce, slanted, alive. And halfway through, in an entry dated from a river town she recognized because she'd surveyed its rotted pilings last spring, he'd sketched a stone marker: a column of carved glyphs, geometric, unlike any script she knew, rising from the mud of a flooded reach.

*Beneath the ninth bend,* he'd written. *The locals will not say its name and the river will not give it back. Something here was buried on purpose, and the burying was the achievement, not the thing itself.*

Beneath the ninth bend.

Edmund lowered the notebook and looked out over the black water, at the moon lying on it in pieces, at the drowned trees standing sentinel, and she understood—with the terrible clarity that comes at two in the morning to people who work alone—that she had not found the sandbar by accident. Rivers keep receipts, yes. But they deliver them, too. To specific addresses.

Twelve days ago, eight people had walked calmly into the water carrying their own packs, and the mud had agreed to forget them, and the government had agreed to wait until after the rains, and the only person in three hundred kilometers of wilderness who could read what had happened there had been steered to that bar by a current that broke off mid-sentence.

Something upriver had planned all of it. And tomorrow, against every instruction, against the closing weather, against ten years of hard-won anonymity—

Edmund turned the boat's nose toward the ninth bend, and started the engine.
