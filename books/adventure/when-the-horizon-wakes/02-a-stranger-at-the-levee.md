## 2. A Stranger at the Levee

The town was called Serrano's Landing, though the river had eaten most of what Serrano had ever landed there. What remained was a levee of black mud and rotted pilings, a street of plank walkways that floated rather than stood, and a scatter of buildings—supply house, fuel depot, a cantina with a tin roof that sang all night when the wind came off the water. Everything smelled of diesel, fish scales, and the green rot of the delta waiting downstream like an open mouth.

Edmund tied off at the fuel dock just before dusk, because arriving in daylight meant conversation and she'd had her fill of it upriver. The *Magpie* sat low in the water, riding heavy on her bad luck: a hairline crack in the portside hull seam weeping slow as a liar, a propeller chewed half to lace by something submerged near the second bend, and an engine that had begun making a sound Edmund could only describe as a cough before a confession. Three days' work if she did it herself. Two days and real money if she paid the yard man, Ovidio, who was honest about everything except his prices.

She counted what she had under the awning of the supply house while rain began—a fine, warm drizzle that wouldn't last, the sky only clearing its throat before the monsoon arrived for good. Not enough. Not nearly enough for repairs plus provisions plus the fee Ovidio would charge for storage through the wet season, which he called insurance and everyone else called robbery.

"You look like a woman doing arithmetic she doesn't like."

The voice came from behind her, low and pleasant, with the kind of accent that had been sanded smooth by expensive schooling until its origins were anyone's guess. Edmund didn't turn right away. She finished counting, folded the bills into her breast pocket, and then turned, slowly, the way she did everything when a stranger was involved.

He was leaning against a stack of fuel drums with the ease of a man who had never once had to calculate whether leaning would cost him anything. Tall, dark-haired going silver early at the temples, dressed in field clothes that were field clothes the way a stage set is a landscape—correct in every particular, too clean by half. His boots were polished. That was the first thing she noticed, and the first thing she filed away, because no one wore polished boots to Serrano's Landing. The mud here climbed you like ivy.

"Arithmetic's the only honest thing left," she said. "You want something?"

"A guide." He smiled. It was a good smile, calibrated, the smile of a man who knew exactly how much wattage to deploy and when. "Into the delta. Downstream, past the Green Throat, as far as the flooded forest."

"Wrong season."

"The season is precisely why I need someone who knows it." He pushed off the drums and extended a hand. "Casimir Vane."

She didn't take it. She looked at it instead—at the calluses that were there but wrong, arranged along the palm like they'd been described to him rather than earned—and let the silence do her talking. He withdrew the hand without embarrassment, which also told her something. Men who embarrassed easily didn't survive out here, and men who didn't embarrass easily usually had somewhere else they'd learned their composure.

"Government contract," he said, and produced a folio from inside his jacket, flipping it open with a flourish that was a little too ready, a card presented a beat too fast. "Office of Regional Survey and Recovery. We've lost a team upstream of the delta proper. Eight people, eleven days silent. I'm authorized to mount a recovery effort before the monsoon seals the channels."

Edmund looked at the card. Heavy stock, embossed seal, the ink still sharp enough to smell. She'd seen government paper before; her father had wallpapered his study with it, decades of permits and commissions and letters of commendation that had meant nothing the day the money stopped. But there was something off here, some small wrongness she couldn't name yet, the way you can't name a wrong note in a chord but feel it in your teeth.

"Recovery teams have their own guides," she said. "Contract men from the coast. Why come to a landing this small?"

"Because the coast men won't go this late, and I asked around." He tilted his head. "They said if I wanted someone who reads water the way priests read scripture, I want Edmund Dravenmoor."

There it was. Her name, spoken easily, warmly, like a key turning in a lock she'd thought she'd changed. She kept her face still. Ten years of practice kept her face still.

"They told you wrong," she said. "I survey rivers. I don't play rescue."

"Three days ago you reported abandoned equipment on a sandbar at Kilometer Forty-One. Government issue. No bodies." His voice stayed pleasant, but something underneath it had sharpened, the way a blade doesn't change shape when it turns edge-on. "That team is mine to find. You found their leavings before my office even knew they were missing. That makes you either very good or very lucky, and luck doesn't read tracks."

So he'd done his homework. Fine. So had she, now, and the ledger was already unbalanced. "Who told you I reported it?"

"The report goes through channels. Channels talk." He shrugged, spreading his hands—an apology made of gesture. "Look. I'll be direct, since directness seems to be your dialect. I need to be on the water within two days. After that, the monsoon closes the delta for four months, and whatever happened to those eight people stays buried until spring—if it surfaces at all. Every guide worth hiring has already refused me or priced themselves past reason. You're the last one, and I suspect the best one, and I am authorized to pay handsomely for both facts."

"How handsomely?"

He named a figure. It was obscene. It was, she calculated instantly, three times what the repairs would cost, plus provisions, plus a season's cushion, plus enough left over to tell Ovidio's prices to go to hell. It was the kind of number designed to end conversations, and it nearly ended hers.

"I want half up front," she said.

Something flickered across his face—not disappointment, not surprise. Satisfaction, quickly banked, like a lamp turned down. "Done."

"And conditions." She stepped closer, close enough to see that his eyes were gray-green, river-colored, and just as hard to read at depth. "One: we go by my rules on the water. My boat, my routes, my calls. You don't argue with the river and you don't argue with me. Two: when I say turn back, we turn back. Contract or no contract, dead men don't pay invoices. Three—" she held his gaze, "—you tell me what you're really looking for. Bodies, survivors, papers, treasure. I don't care which. But I read water better than people, and people worse than water, and I can't afford surprises I haven't mapped."

For a long moment he said nothing, and the rain ticked on the awning above them, and somewhere down the street the cantina roof began its evening song. Then Casimir Vane laughed—a short, genuine sound, the first uncalibrated thing she'd seen him do—and Edmund felt a small, unwelcome flicker of interest, the way you feel current under still water.

"Fair terms," he said. "Here's my honesty, such as it is. Officially: locate the team, recover remains or survivors, document site conditions. Unofficially—" he glanced around, theatrical, though the street was empty except for a dog chewing something ancient on the planks, "—there are questions about how they vanished that my superiors want answered quietly. Eight trained people don't disappear without a trace. Someone wants to know if it was accident, desertion, or something else. And they'd prefer the answer not appear in any report with a wider circulation."

"Something else," Edmund repeated. "Meaning what?"

"Meaning I don't know yet. Meaning that's why I need you." He met her eyes and, to his credit—or his craft—didn't flinch. "I'm not asking you to trust me, Dravenmoor. I'm asking you to take my money and watch me like a hawk the whole way down. If I lie to you, you'll know it before I do. Isn't that what trackers do?"

It was exactly what trackers did. It was also, she reflected later, the most disarming thing a liar could say.

* * *

They walked the levee together as the light died, because she wanted to see how he moved before she agreed to share a boat with him, and because Ovidio's yard closed at full dark and the *Magpie* needed tarping. Vane walked well—that was the second strike against him. City men stumbled on levee mud, arms windmilling, dignity drowning. Vane placed his feet like a man who'd been taught balance somewhere real, his weight low, his eyes reading the ground ahead without appearing to. Polished boots, sure feet. Another entry in the ledger of things that didn't add.

"Your boat leaks," he observed as they approached the dock.

"Everything leaks. Boats, governments, stories." She crouched at the gunwale and checked the seam by touch, fingernails finding the hairline crack the way a mother finds a fever. "Yours included, I'd guess."

"My stories are watertight."

"That's how you know they're lies. True stories leak. They're porous. Life gets in." She straightened, wiped her hands on her thighs. "Ovidio will start on the hull tomorrow at first light. We provision after. We leave day after, dawn tide, whether the engine's happy or not."

"Dawn tide. Agreed." He watched her drop the tarp over the bow, and after a moment took the far corner and helped, pulling the canvas taut with more competence than charm required. "Can I ask you something? Off the record?"

"You can ask. Record-keeping is your department."

"Why does a woman who reads water like scripture work surveys nobody's heard of? Your reputation says you could have a chair at any institute on the coast. Instead you're alone on a dying boat measuring silt for agencies that barely return your letters."

The question slid in sideways, polite as a knife between ribs. Edmund pulled the last rope tight and considered her answer the way she'd consider footing on rotten planks.

"Rivers don't know who my father was," she said finally.

Vane was quiet for three steps of the walk back toward the supply house. When he spoke again, his voice had lost some of its polish, and she couldn't decide whether the loss was sincerity or craftsmanship, and she hated that she couldn't decide, and she noted that hating it was itself information.

"Aldous Dravenmoor," he said softly. "Of course. I should have led with respect instead of arithmetic."

"Don't." The word came out harder than she intended, edged with ten years of rust. She stopped walking. The rain had thickened, drumming the tin roofs, running in bright ropes off the eaves. "Whatever you think you know about him, put it away. I'm not his daughter on this river. I'm a surveyor with a cracked hull and a client who pays half up front. That's the whole of it."

"All right." He raised his hands, palms out, and for a moment—the rain, the dimming light, the dog trotting past with its prize—he looked almost young, almost harmless. Almost. "Half up front, your rules, your river. And Dravenmoor—for what it's worth, I'm glad the channels talked. A man could do worse than be found by you."

"A man could do better than being found," she said. "Ask your eight missing people."

His smile didn't waver, but it thinned, and beneath it, for just a heartbeat, she saw the thing the smile was built to cover: a calculation running fast and cold, the same arithmetic she'd been doing all evening, each of them pricing the other as shield or threat and neither yet certain which.

* * *

She dreamed of the sandbar that night, the way she'd dreamed of it every night since Kilometer Forty-One. The gear laid out neat as an offering—canteens in a row, boots paired toe-to-heel, a radio with its antenna coiled like a sleeping snake. No tracks leaving. No tracks arriving. Just the arrangement, deliberate as handwriting, and the water rising around it with terrible patience, taking its receipts.

She woke before dawn in the room above the supply house, rain gone quiet, the air so wet it felt breathable like soup. Below, she heard voices—Ovidio's grumble, and beneath it another voice, smooth and pleasant, negotiating. She lay still and listened to Casimir Vane buy her repairs with money that was too easy and questions that were too wrong, and she told herself the feeling in her chest was just the season changing.

But her instinct, the one that had kept her alive in places maps forgot, said otherwise. It said: you have hitched yourself to something downstream of you, and rivers only run one way.

By the time she came down the stairs, Vane was waiting at the foot of them with two cups of coffee and a folio under his arm, and the folio was thicker than it had been yesterday, and one corner of a page showed the embossed corner of a photograph he tucked away a half-second too late.

"First light tomorrow," he said, offering the cup. "Shall we?"

Edmund took the coffee. She didn't drink it. She looked at him over the rim, at the polished boots and the river-colored eyes, and said, "We shall," and began, silently, to count.
