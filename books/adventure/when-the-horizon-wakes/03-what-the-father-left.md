## 3. What the Father Left

The boat was named *Magpie*, which Casimir Vane decided was either an act of honesty or a confession. She collected things, this vessel—mud, rust, the smell of diesel and river rot—and she leaked with the steady patience of a thing that had made peace with its own death.

He sat in the stern under a tarp strung against the drizzle and read his file by the light of a shielded lamp, because the real work of any commission began with reading, and he had learned long ago that the first draft of a person was always written by someone else.

**SUBJECT: DRAVENMOOR, EDMUND MARGARET.**
**AGE: 34.**
**OCCUPATION: Hydrographic surveyor, freelance. Contracts: remote, low-visibility, frequently declined by others in the field.**
**NEXT OF KIN: NONE LISTED.**

That last line had been true for seven years, and it was the line that had gotten him the job. His employers did not collect people; they collected absences. A woman who had erased herself so thoroughly that even her father's obituary writers had spelled her name wrong—that was not a person to them. That was a gap in a ledger, and gaps could be filled.

He turned the page. The paper was good stock, heavy, meant to be burned and never was. Somewhere beneath him the bilge pump coughed, thought about it, and started again.

**FATHER: ALDOUS DRAVENMOOR (DECEASED, 7 YRS). Archaeologist. Author of *The River Roads* (standard text), *Cities Under Water*, and forty-one years of field notes, most of them unpublished, all of them now property of the estate trust. Final expedition: unaccounted for. Final dig: location unknown. Final field journal: never recovered.**

Casimir had read Aldous Dravenmoor's books at university the way other men read scripture or pornography—with that particular hunger that comes from wanting something you cannot name. The old man wrote about drowned cities the way lovers write letters. *The river does not destroy what it takes,* one famous passage ran. *It keeps it. It keeps everything. It is the only honest creditor in the world.*

And then, seven years ago, the great man had gone up some unnamed tributary with eleven students and a grant from a foundation whose board members were now, coincidentally, Casimir's employers, and had come back as a box of ashes and a mystery. Eleven students came back. Aldous did not. Neither did the twelfth student, though no one had ever been able to prove there had been a twelfth.

His employers believed otherwise. His employers believed there had been a twelfth, and a thirteenth, and a dig, and a find, and that somewhere in the flooded green throat of the delta lay an artifact Aldous Dravenmoor had spent his last year calling, in the two letters that survived, *the Meridian*. One letter said: *I have found a crown for whoever holds it.* The second letter, dated nine days later, said only: *Burn this after reading. I mean to put it back.*

He had not put it back. Or he had, and someone had taken it out again. That was the question the money wanted answered, and money, Casimir had found, was patient in the way water was patient—it got where it was going eventually, and it wore down whatever stood in its path.

Below decks, Edmund Dravenmoor was repairing her engine with a wrench and a vocabulary he pretended not to hear through the hull.

He had watched her for three days before making contact at the levee, and those three days had been the most instructive part of the commission so far. He had expected the daughter of Aldous Dravenmoor to be one of two things: a society woman trading on the name, or a bitter academic grinding her teeth in some departmental basement. What he had found instead was a woman who lived alone on a leaking boat at the ass-end of a dying river town, who bought fuel with cash, who signed her survey contracts as **E. MARSH**, and who had looked at his government credentials with the flat professional assessment of someone checking a stranger's knots before trusting him with her life.

She hadn't asked his surname. He'd offered it twice. Both times she'd let it fall into the water between them like a coin she had no intention of picking up.

*E. Marsh.* He smiled at the file, at the neat typed line: **KNOWN ALIASES: "MARSH."** As if a woman who called herself after wet ground was hiding something exotic, rather than simply refusing to be found by the right people.

There was a photograph clipped inside the folder. It was eleven years old, taken at some university gala: a tall man with white hair going wild at the temples, mid-gesture, mouth open on some anecdote—the great Aldous in full sail. And half-turned away at the frame's edge, caught as if trying to leave the picture entirely, a young woman of twenty-three in a dress that fit her the way a uniform fits a conscript. Her chin was down. Her eyes were not on her father but on the door.

Even then, Casimir thought, she was already leaving.

He'd pulled the personnel records too, of course. Standard diligence. There wasn't much. She'd done her graduate work under her father's direct supervision for exactly one season—one season on the upper river, the summer she turned twenty-three—and then nothing. No degree completed. No academic appointments. No co-authored papers. A decade of contracts signed E. Marsh, hydrographic surveys in waters so remote that the contracting agencies sometimes seemed embarrassed to admit they needed them done.

A woman who had walked out of a legend and never once looked back. Except—

Except that she hadn't, quite. That was the detail that kept snagging him, the loose thread his thumb kept finding. Because when he'd mentioned, at the levee, that the missing government team's last known coordinates fell along the old Dravenmoor survey routes, she had gone still. Not interested-still. Not greedy-still. Still the way water goes still over something deep.

She knew those coordinates meant something. And she had agreed to take him into the delta anyway, for money she plainly needed and plainly hated needing, into the exact current that had swallowed her father.

People run from things, Casimir had learned. It was the direction they ran *toward* that told you the truth of them.

The engine below cut out. Silence rushed in, thick with rain on the tarp and the slap of water against the hull. Then footsteps, and she came up the companionway with grease to the elbow and a rag over her shoulder, and stopped when she saw the lamp and the papers.

For a moment neither of them moved. Rain ticked on the canvas above them. Downriver, thunder cleared its throat.

"Fuel lines," she said. "She'll run tomorrow if the pump holds." Her eyes went to the folder—not curious, he noted, but cataloguing. Weighing it the way she'd weighed his credentials. "You read a lot."

"It's a bad habit. I'm trying to cut down."

"What is it? Survey reports?"

"Commission paperwork." He closed the folder with deliberate casualness, and watched her watch him do it. "Boring stuff. Budgets, indemnity forms. The government's genius for turning adventure into accounting."

Something flickered across her face—not belief, not disbelief. A kind of tired recognition, as if she'd seen this exact performance before and had merely never bothered to learn how it ended. "Indemnity forms," she repeated.

"For the families. Of the missing team." He held her gaze and lied to her with the full warmth of his considerable charm, and felt, for the first time in years, the strange sensation of the charm landing on nothing—of warmth poured onto ground too wet to hold it. "Eight people are missing, Ms. Marsh. Someone has to sign for their lives."

She looked at him a long moment. Rain drummed. Somewhere out in the dark the river moved with that vast slow muscular patience of moving water, carrying everything toward the sea.

"You can call me Edmund," she said finally, and he understood suddenly that it was not a gift. It was a test, or a trap, or simply the truth deployed like a weapon—which, with this woman, might be all the same thing. "Since we're going to be spending weeks on a boat together. No point pretending we're strangers we're not."

"And am I allowed a name in exchange?"

"You gave me one already." She wiped her hands on the rag, unhurried. "Whether it's the right one is your business."

She went below without waiting for an answer, and Casimir sat alone under the tarp with his file and his lamp and the rain, and found himself doing something he almost never did with a subject.

He found himself wondering.

Not *where is she*—he knew where she was; she was sleeping four feet below him in a bunk narrower than a coffin. Not *what is she worth*—the figure was in the folder, itemized. But the other question, the unprofessional one, the one that crept in around the edges of commissions like damp around a window frame:

What does a woman carry, that she'd rather be nobody than be herself?

He opened the folder again. The photograph. The girl at the edge of the frame, eyes on the door. And beside it, the transcript of the one interview anyone had ever gotten out of her, three years ago, a local newspaper piece about flooding in some district no one cared about, in which a surveyor named E. Marsh was quoted once: *"Rivers don't lie. People do. That's why I work on rivers."*

Casimir laughed softly, alone in the rain, and it was not entirely a pleasant sound.

Below him, the bilge pump coughed and resumed its patient war against the water coming in. Above him, the monsoon's first outriders dragged their curtains of rain across the hills. And somewhere ahead of them both, up the green throat of the delta, eight people were missing, an old man's last secret waited in the drowned dark, and the water—honest creditor, keeper of receipts—held all of it, and would give none of it up for free.

He killed the lamp and sat in the dark, listening to the leak, and thought about the second letter. *I mean to put it back.*

In the morning, he decided, he would start lying to her better. Or possibly—for the first time in his career—he would try telling her the truth, and see what broke.

* * *

Dawn came gray and dripping, and with it the smell of the delta proper: mud and vegetation and something older underneath, something mineral and cold, like the breath off stone in a deep cellar. They were past the last town now. Behind them the levee country flattened into haze; ahead the forest stood right down into the water, black-green and breathing mist, and the river widened until it stopped being a river at all and became a country that happened to be wet.

Edmund brought the *Magpie* through it the way other people breathed—reading channels Casimir couldn't see, easing off the throttle where the water went glassy over drowned fences, opening it up where the current braided silver. She hadn't slept much. He could tell by the set of her shoulders, by the coffee she drank black from a dented tin cup without appearing to taste it.

"The team's route," she said, without preamble, eyes on the water. "Your missing eight. They went up the Serrano branch?"

"That's the last confirmed fix, yes."

"Serrano floods first. Always has. If they're alive, they've gone to high ground—the mission stations, the old survey camps." She glanced at him, quick and assessing. "Your government friends would know that if they'd hired anyone who'd actually worked this river."

"They may have prioritized speed over local knowledge."

"They prioritized cheap," she said, and returned to the channel.

He let the silence sit. This was the part of the craft no one taught you: the silence was where subjects told you everything. He watched her hands on the wheel—capable, scarred across two knuckles, sun-browned—and thought about the file's strangest entry, buried in an appendix he doubted his employers had even read.

**NOTE (UNVERIFIED): Subject reportedly visited the Aldous Dravenmoor memorial service, duration approx. 11 minutes. Did not sign the book. Did not speak to colleagues. Observed standing at rear of hall during eulogy, then departed.**

Eleven minutes. He turned that over as the forest slid past, dripping. Seven years estranged, and she had come—had stood in the back of a room full of people mourning a man she'd walked away from, for eleven minutes exactly, and left without signing anything. Not staying. Not staying away. Holding some position between the two that no one had thought to give a name.

"Question," he said.

She didn't look over. "Those cost extra."

"Why 'Marsh'? Of all the names you could have picked."

The throttle eased back a fraction. For a moment he thought she wouldn't answer, and he was composing his apology—charm, self-deprecation, the usual kit—when she spoke, eyes still forward.

"My mother's family were fen people. Marsh cutters, generations back, before the drainage." A shrug that was mostly shoulders. "They worked wet ground nobody else wanted and they were good at it, and nobody ever wrote a book about them." The throttle came back up. "Seemed fitting."

*Worked wet ground nobody else wanted.* Casimir filed it away and understood, with a small cold click, that she had just given him more truth in ten seconds than most subjects surrendered in a month—and that she knew it, and had done it deliberately, the way you feed a dog you don't yet trust: enough to keep it friendly, never enough to fill it.

Ahead, the Serrano branch opened off the main channel like a wound in the forest, and the mist over it hung lower and darker than anywhere else, and Edmund Dravenmoor—who refused her own name, who mourned in eleven-minute increments, who had agreed to sail straight into the water that took her father—took them into it without hesitation, and Casimir Vane sat down among his lies and realized, with genuine unease, that for the first time in his career he could not predict which of them was hunting the other.
