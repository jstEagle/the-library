## 4. Into the Green Throat

The delta did not announce itself. It arrived the way a rumor arrives—first a change in the light, then a change in the smell, and by the time you noticed the water had gone from brown to green-black and still, you were already inside it.

Edmund stood at the tiller of her boat, the *Magpie*, and watched the treeline come down to meet them. Not a bank anymore—there was no bank, not here—but forest standing hip-deep in its own reflection, kapok and fig and the pale ghost-trunks of drowned palms, their roots braided into the water like knuckles. Above them the sky had begun to stack itself with cloud, great gray shelves rising out of the southeast in slow, patient ranks. The first storm bands. The monsoon's outriders.

Behind her, Casimir Vane sat on the supply crates with his polished boots braced against the gunwale, and she noticed—he'd changed them. Or scuffed them. Sometime between the levee town and this morning he had taken a knife or a stone to that mirror shine, and now they looked like boots a man might actually own who worked rivers for a living. It was such a small lie. That was what unsettled her about it. A man who lied big lied once; a man who lied small was lying constantly, the way other people breathed.

"Rules," she said, without turning around.

"Rules?"

"You wanted a guide. This is the guide part." She cut the engine to half-throttle as the channel narrowed between two buttress-rooted figs whose crowns met overhead, closing the sky to a green seam. Her voice dropped without her deciding to—the flooded forest did that to people. "Rule one. The water is a road. Everything uses it. If you hear something moving that doesn't sound like fish, you don't look for it. You listen to where it *stops*."

"And if it doesn't stop?"

"Then it was never interested in us." She eased the *Magpie* around a submerged fence post, all that remained of some farm the river had eaten a generation ago. "Rule two. We don't camp on the ground. Ever. Platforms, boats, ruins above the flood line if we can find them. The ground belongs to things with more patience than us."

"You say that like you've met them."

"I've lost arguments to them." She let that sit. "Rule three. Rain starts, we stop moving. Not because of visibility. Because every sound you know becomes a different sound in rain, and the first thing a person does when they're frightened is misidentify something. Misidentification is how people die out here. Not teeth. Not water. Guessing."

Casimir was quiet for a moment. When she glanced back at him, he was watching the canopy slide past with an expression she hadn't seen on him before—not the levee-town charm, not the easy contract-flashing confidence. Something more attentive. Hungrier, almost, but a clean hunger, the hunger of a man looking at a country he genuinely wanted to understand.

"All right," he said. "Rule four?"

"There is no rule four."

"There's always a rule four."

"The rule four is that there is no rule four," she said, "because men who make lists of rules start believing the list will keep them alive. The list won't. Attention will. The list just gives attention somewhere to stand."

He laughed—a real one, short and surprised—and something in her chest registered the sound and immediately filed it under *dangerous*. She turned back to the channel before her face could report anything.

They ran until midday, threading the drowned forest by channels only she knew, marked in her head by trees rather than charts: the lightning-split kapok, the fig with the termite cathedral climbing its trunk like a gray organ, the stretch where the water went suddenly cold over a sunken channel of old riverbed. Twice she stopped the engine entirely and poled, listening. Once for the barking of a troop of monkeys that had gone silent too fast. Once for nothing at all—for the quality of silence itself, which on this river was a kind of speech.

Casimir watched her do it both times and said nothing, which she noted. Most clients would have asked. Asking was a way of pulling focus back to themselves. He simply waited, hands loose on his knees, eyes moving over the water in the same rhythm hers did. And when she poled through the cold channel and he felt the temperature drop against his trailing hand, he looked at her—not at the water, at *her*—with a question he didn't voice.

"Old riverbed underneath," she said anyway. "The delta shifts. What's map and what's memory depends on the decade."

"You've worked here long."

"Long enough."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the answer I sell." But she heard how flat it came out, and hated that she heard it, because it told him he'd found an edge. He smiled faintly, like a man pocketing a coin he'd found on the street, and looked back at the water.

* * *

The storm bands reached them in the middle of the afternoon—not the monsoon proper, not yet, but its heralds: hard gray squalls that came down the channels like freight trains made of water, lasting twenty minutes, drowning the world, and then hauling off eastward leaving everything steaming. Edmund ran the *Magpie* into the lee of a ruined chapel during the second one—a mission church abandoned so long that a strangler fig had grown straight up through the collapsed roof, its aerial roots hanging in the doorway like the strings of some enormous instrument.

They ate cold rice and dried fish under the dripping eaves while the rain hammered the leaves into a single continuous roar. Casimir rigged the tarp without being asked, and he rigged it *correctly*—high side into the wind, runoff channel dug with his boot heel, guy line tensioned with a trucker's hitch tied fast enough that she caught herself staring at his hands.

"Where did you learn that?" she asked.

"Various places." He settled back against a root. "I've been a lot of men, Dravenmoor."

"That's not reassuring."

"It wasn't meant to be. Reassurance is for people selling you something." He tore a piece of dried fish with his teeth. "You want to know where, specifically."

"I want to know why a man with government paper and city hands ties knots like a deckhand."

He considered her across the small wet space between them. Rain drummed. Somewhere in the flooded dark, a bird made a sound like a hinge.

"My father ran coastal freight," he said finally. "Small boats, honest rot, dishonest ledgers. I grew up splicing line before I could write cursive." A shrug, elegant even sitting in mud. "Then boarding school erased it. Then ten years of desks buried it. Apparently it keeps." He flexed his fingers, looking at them as though checking whether the claim were true. "Apparently it keeps better than the cursive."

It was a good story. It had texture, specific shame, a father with crooked books—that last detail offered up precisely because it cost him something, which was the oldest trick in the confidence man's book and one Edmund knew intimately, having grown up watching her father deploy honesty like bait. She believed every word of it. That was exactly why she didn't trust any of it.

But she found, sitting there in the roar of the rain, that she liked him anyway, in the way you like a knife that holds an edge. And she distrusted herself for it, and put both observations away in the place where she kept things she'd examine later, alone, when liking someone couldn't get anyone killed.

The squall passed. They went on.

* * *

She smelled the second camp before she saw it.

Smoke that has soaked into ash and then into rain and then into wood has a particular signature—cold, mineral, wrong. Living fires smell of whatever feeds them. Dead deliberate fires smell of intention. Edmund killed the engine a full kilometer downstream of the smell and poled the *Magpie* into the shallows beneath a wall of bamboo, and stood there in the green half-light with her head slightly tilted, reading.

"Casimir. Off the crates. Slowly."

He moved without argument, without questions, crouching low in the hull. Another point in whichever column she was keeping for him. She poled them forward another few hundred meters, until the channel bent and opened onto a shallow lagoon ringed by drowned acacia, and there it was.

The survey team's second camp—or what the fire had left of it.

Government-issue tents, melted to black lace over their frames. Crates split open, their contents sorted through and left: instruments, cookware, a radio casing pried open and gutted. A tarp hung from a branch like a flayed hide. Everything wore a skin of gray ash washed thin by the first squalls, so that the whole site seemed to be dissolving back into the forest ahead of schedule, the green already sending up exploratory shoots through the fire-hardened ground.

Edmund held the boat against a root for a long time before she let them land.

"Burned," Casimir said quietly. "Accident?"

"No."

"How can you tell from here?"

"Because of what's *not* burned." She brought the *Magpie* alongside a fallen trunk and stepped onto it, testing weight before committing. "A cooking fire gets away from a camp, it takes the tents nearest the fire first, unevenly, and it dies where the fuel thins. Look at the burn line." She walked the perimeter without touching anything, boots finding the firm ground by instinct. "It's a ring. Even. Started in multiple places, or accelerant was laid in a circle. And the fuel stores—" she nodded at a stack of split wood at the camp's edge, scorched on its outward face only, "—were pushed *away* from the flames before they caught. Someone protected their own firewood while they burned the camp."

Casimir had gone very still. "Deliberate."

"Deliberate, controlled, and unhurried." She crouched at the edge of the ash field. "Panic burns hot and fast and messy. This burned like a chore. Like cleaning up after yourself." She looked up at him. "Eight people don't burn their own camp like this unless they're leaving and don't want it found. And eight people don't leave and don't want it found unless—"

"Unless they're not running from something," Casimir finished. His voice had lost its levee-town music entirely. "They're going *to* something."

Edmund didn't answer. Because the alternative was worse, and she could see it forming behind his eyes the same way it was forming behind hers: that the team hadn't been driven out by danger at all. That whatever had happened here had been agreed upon. Organized. That eight trained government surveyors had packed their own packs, torched their own camp with methodical care, and walked into the flooded forest *together*—

She crossed to the far side of the site, where the ground firmed up under a stand of bamboo, and there she found what her instincts had been walking her toward since the smell first reached her.

Footprints. Dozens of them, pressed into the clay where the team had mustered before moving out—boot prints, government issue, mostly uniform in size, overlapping where they'd gathered. No scuffs of struggle. No drag marks. No sprint-marks, no stumble-marks, no prints deepened at the toe the way prints deepen when a body is carried. Just a loose congregation of standing figures, and then a column of walking ones, heading south-southeast into the deep delta.

Toward the storm. Toward the monsoon. Toward nothing any sane expedition would choose.

"They walked," she said aloud. She hadn't meant to say it aloud. "Carrying their own gear. In good order. At night or near it, by the lamp oil they took." She rose slowly, turning a full circle, scanning the treeline as though the missing might simply step out and explain themselves. "No bodies. No blood. No tracks leaving except theirs. Eight adults walked out of a burned camp into the worst season this delta has, in formation, and the forest swallowed them without leaving so much as a torn sleeve."

Casimir had come to stand beside her. Close. She could feel the warmth of him through the humid air, and the fact that she noticed annoyed her.

"Your rule four," he said quietly. "There is no rule four. What happens when the list runs out?"

"Then you're down to attention." She was still scanning the treeline. "And my attention is telling me that whatever took these people didn't chase them. It *invited* them."

Something moved in her peripheral vision—Casimir's head turning, sharp, toward the southern treeline, toward the same gap in the bamboo the boot column had used. And for one unguarded instant, before the charm came back down over his face like a visor, she saw something in his expression that did not belong to a man hearing this news for the first time.

Recognition.

Not surprise. Recognition—the flat, settling look of a man confirming a thing he had already been told.

It lasted less than a second. Then he exhaled, shook his head, and said, with admirably steady wonder, "Invited. By what? There's nothing out here worth walking into a monsoon for."

"Exactly," Edmund said. "So either your government sent eight people to die for nothing—" she turned and looked at him fully, letting him see that she had seen, letting him decide what to do with being seen, "—or they were going to meet something. And somebody knew that before we ever left the levee."

The clouds stacked higher in the southeast, gray going to slate going to bruise. The first true rain of the monsoon touched the canopy far upstream, a sound like distant applause.

Casimir held her gaze for three full seconds. Then he smiled—small, tired, and for once completely unreadable—and said, "We should find high ground, Dravenmoor. Rule two."

He turned to help load the boat, and Edmund stood a moment longer among the bootprints of eight vanished strangers, watching the storm come down the green throat of the delta like a door slowly closing behind them.
