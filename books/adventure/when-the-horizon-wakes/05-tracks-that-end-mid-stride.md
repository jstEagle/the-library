## 5. Tracks That End Mid-Stride

The rain had stopped an hour before dawn, and the burn site smelled like a hearth that had been left to die.

Edmund stood at the edge of the clearing with her boots sunk two inches into black mud, letting the smell tell her what her eyes were still sorting out. Char. Wet ash gone gray and paste-like. Underneath it all, the green rot of the delta itself, patient as ever, already climbing back over the ruin the way water climbs a hull. Somewhere in the canopy above, a bird she couldn't name made a sound like a hinge swinging.

Behind her, Casimir Vane was quiet for once.

She'd noticed that about him in the three days since they'd pushed into the flooded forest: he went quiet at exactly the wrong moments. Not when silence was polite—when it was informative. When she crouched to read sign, when she held up a hand, when the river changed its voice. He watched her instead of the woods, and he filed things away, and she pretended not to notice because pretending not to notice was also information she could use later.

"Tell me what you see," he said now, softly, like a man in a church he wasn't sure he was welcome in.

"Give me a minute."

She gave herself ten.

* * *

The camp had been a government survey camp—she'd known that from the first site, the sandbar with its abandoned gear—but knowing it and standing in its bones were different animals. Eight tents had stood here. She counted the rotted rectangles of groundcloth pressed into the mud, each one still holding the ghost-shape of a floor, and came up with eight without trying. A cook tarp had been strung between two trees; one guyline still hung, furred with moss. The fire pit held the calcined remains of a hundred meals' worth of tin.

And someone had burned it. Deliberately. Thoroughly.

That was the thing the government report would never say, because the government report had been written by men reading satellite passes through cloud cover. But Edmund could read a burn the way other people read handwriting, and this one had grammar. The fire hadn't spread—it had been *laid*. Accelerant, probably kerosene from their own stores, poured in a ring around the tent line and lit from downwind so the flame walked through the camp in one clean pass. The trees at the clearing's edge were scorched only on their inner faces. No panic. No stumbling flight. Nobody had knocked over a lantern and run.

Somebody had stood here, in the dark, and set this place on fire with care.

"Eight tents," Casimir said behind her. He'd counted too. Of course he had.

"Eight." She moved toward the waterline. The camp sat on a low bluff of compacted silt, maybe four feet above the current level of the channel, and the mud between the tents and the water was the kind that kept everything—the delta's great ledger, the river keeping its receipts. "Stay off the center. Walk where I walk or don't walk."

He fell in behind her, matching her steps exactly, which annoyed her more than if he'd blundered. Competent men were harder to dismiss than clumsy ones.

The mud told its story in the flat grey light, and the story was wrong.

She saw it first as a feeling, before she could name it—the same feeling she got when a map didn't match the terrain, a small cold dissonance under the breastbone. Then she knelt and put her fingers near a print and understood.

Footprints. Dozens of them. Booted, uniform, heading from the camp toward the water in loose parallel lines—not scattered, not converging, not trampled by struggle. Just eight sets of prints, walking.

Walking calmly. Carrying weight.

"Vane." Her voice came out flatter than she intended. "Come look at this. Carefully."

He crouched beside her, close enough that she caught woodsmoke and some expensive soap underneath it, absurd in this place. His eyes tracked the prints the way hers did, and she watched his face do something complicated and then go smooth again. He was good at that. Too good.

"Depth," she said, because saying it aloud would make it real. "Look at the depth."

Each print sank deep into the silt—deeper than a man's body weight alone would press, even a big man, even in soft mud. And at the toe of nearly every print, a second impression, shallower, smeared forward: the drag of a pack being set down and shouldered, or shifted, or settled. Straps would have cut grooves. She found them—two parallel furrows in the heel of one print, the signature of a loaded rucksack riding on shoulders that had carried it for years.

"They packed," she said. "They took their gear. All of them. And they walked into the water."

"In the middle of the night," Casimir said quietly. "After their camp burned."

"Or before. The burn—" She stopped. Reconsidered. Looked back at the careful ring of accelerant, the orderly scorch, the fire that had needed a steady hand and no fear. "No. After doesn't work. You don't burn your own camp and then go wading. You burn it to erase where you've been. Which means they burned it on the way out. Which means—"

"Which means they weren't running," Vane finished. "They were leaving."

"There's a difference." There was, and the difference sat in her stomach like a swallowed stone. Running left a certain signature—splayed toes, staggered lines, dropped things. This left another. Eight people had eaten dinner, banked their fire, gone to their tents. At some hour in the dark, they had risen, shouldered their packs, doused everything worth saving, poured fuel around the rest, lit it, and walked in step—more or less in step—down to the water, and kept walking.

No bodies downstream. She'd dragged every sandbar and snag for forty miles below the second camp and found nothing but the river's ordinary dead: branches, a deer skull, plastic in blue ropes. No bodies meant they hadn't drowned. No tracks leading anywhere except the water meant—

"The boat," Casimir said. "They had a boat."

"Their boat is at the first camp. I checked the manifest against the equipment log twice." She heard her own voice sharpening and let it. Anger was easier than the other thing. "They walked into a flooded forest at night carrying full packs, in monsoon season, with no boat, no lights—there's no wax stubs, no spent batteries in the fire, nothing—and no bodies ever came out. That's not an accident, Vane. Accidents leave messes. This is the tidiest thing I've ever seen a wilderness do, and the wilderness isn't tidy."

"So what did it?"

"I don't know yet." She stood, knees cracking, and wiped her hands on her thighs out of habit though the mud was past caring about. "But somebody planned it. Somebody with authority. You don't get eight trained people to march into black water at night unless they believe the person telling them to knows something they don't."

"And you trust the mud to know all that?"

"The mud doesn't lie." She looked at him. "People lie. Mud just keeps the receipt."

Something flickered across his face then—too fast to name, gone before it landed. He smiled instead, the easy professional smile she'd begun to catalog the way she cataloged snake species: beautiful, and to be given a wide berth.

* * *

She worked the perimeter while the light strengthened, because light was a tool and she intended to use all of it. The storm bands had passed in the night and the sky was rinsed and high, and for a few hours the delta would behave itself. She circled the clearing wide, reading the understory, and found what she expected: nothing. No trail out the back. No food caches. No latrine trench dug fresh in the last week. Whatever the team had done after arriving, they had not planned to stay long, and they had not planned to come back.

It was on her third circuit, at the water's edge where the bluff shelved down into the channel, that she saw the stone.

It shouldn't have been there. That was the first thing. The delta was alluvial—silt on silt on silt, laid down in brown layers like the pages of a ledger, and stones the size of a man's torso did not occur naturally within a hundred miles of this bend. She'd surveyed this whole reach two seasons ago. There had been no stone.

Now there was one, half-sunk in the mud at the exact point where the eight sets of footprints entered the water, angled like a doorpost, like a gate marker, like the kind of stone you raise when you want people to know where to stand.

Her heart began doing something slow and heavy, and she hated it, and she waded to it anyway.

The mud came up over her shins, cold as a hand. She dug with her palms, working the silt away in slabs, and the stone emerged inch by inch: dark basalt, dense, river-worn on its lower half but crisp-edged above the old waterline. Someone had shaped it. Someone had carved it. And someone had brought it here—hauled it, floated it, barged it, she didn't know how—and set it upright in the mud at the threshold of the water, and then eight people had walked past it into the dark.

The carving was a spiral. Not decorative—a working spiral, cut deep, its groove worn glossy at the bottom as if many thumbs had traced it. Around the spiral ran a band of characters she did not recognize and, worse, half-recognized: angular strokes with a rhythm to them, a cadence, a grammar. Not random. Writing. Or something that wanted to be writing.

She knew it.

Not from any registry—she had memorized the registries, the national archaeological indices, the colonial-era surveys, her father's published corpus, the whole dusty apparatus of official knowledge, and none of them contained this mark. She knew it from a green notebook with a cracked spine that had lived in her father's field kit, the one he'd carried the last year, the year everything went strange, the year he stopped publishing and started talking about *the Meridian* at dinner in a voice she'd never heard him use before—reverent, and hungry, and afraid.

There had been sketches in that notebook. She'd seen them once, at fifteen, flipping through it when he wasn't home, looking for nothing in particular. Spirals. Gate-stones. Columns of angular characters with a cadence to them. And in the margin of one page, in his tight draftsman's hand, a note she had never forgotten because it had frightened her without her understanding why:

*They marked the doors. We were never meant to find the doors.*

Edmund knelt in the mud with the cold gripping her legs and felt seven years collapse like wet cardboard. The delta narrowed to the size of a notebook page. Her father's voice—dead seven years, drowned in a river not so different from this one, his final dig unaccounted for, his body never recovered either, and she had never let herself think the thought all the way through until this exact moment—her father's voice said *they marked the doors*, and the stone in front of her was a door, and eight people had walked through it carrying their own packs.

"Dravenmoor."

Casimir's voice, from the bank. Careful. He'd seen her face change, and whatever he was, he was smart enough to know the moment had shifted.

She didn't answer. She put her hand flat on the spiral, on the worn groove where countless thumbs—or one thumb, many times—had polished the stone, and the rock was cold in a way the water wasn't, cold like something pulled from deep storage, and she told herself it was basalt, basalt holds cold, that's physics, that's nothing.

"Dravenmoor." Closer now. He'd risked the mud. "You know this mark."

"Do I?" She made her voice bored. It cost her.

"You went white." He crouched at the edge of the water, not touching the stone, watching her hand on it with an expression she couldn't file—was it concern? Calculation? Both wore the same face on him. "You looked at that carving like it owed you money."

"It matches nothing in any registry." She lifted her hand. The cold stayed on her palm a moment longer than it should have, or she imagined it did, and both possibilities were bad. "Which means it's unrecorded, which means whoever put it here didn't want it recorded, which means the vanished team found something the government's maps don't know exists." She stood, water sheeting off her legs, and turned to face him fully. "So before we go one step further, you're going to tell me what your contract actually says. Not the version you flashed at the levee. The real one."

For a heartbeat—one clean, measurable heartbeat—he considered lying to her. She watched him do it. She was getting good at watching him.

Then the light shifted, or the wind did, or something in the man's architecture gave way a quarter-inch, and he said, "Walk with me out of the water first. What I have to show you shouldn't get wet."

* * *

They sat under the cook tarp's one surviving guyline, on the last dry ground for twenty miles, and Casimir Vane took from inside his jacket a oilskin packet, and from the oilskin packet a photograph, and from the photograph a face she had spent ten years learning not to think about.

Aldous Dravenmoor. Younger than she remembered him being at the end—this was decades old, a university portrait, black and white, the famous smile, the famous jaw. On the back, in faded ink, a single line in a hand she recognized as well as her own:

*Aldous Dravenmoor, 1971. Property of the Meridian Group.*

"My employers," Casimir said, "have been looking for his last dig for seven years. They hired me six months ago." He paused, and she watched him choose, deliberately, how much truth to spend. "I'm not sure they told me everything either."

Above them the canopy dripped its slow count, and out at the waterline the carved stone stood in the mud like a finger raised to its lips, and somewhere far upriver, thunder cleared its throat.

Edmund looked at her father's face and felt the past reach through the mud and touch her ankle, gently, the way the drowned do.

"We're not leaving," she heard herself say. "We're going deeper."
