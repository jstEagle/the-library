## 6. The Monsoon Closes the Door

The sky had been threatening for two days, and on the third morning it stopped threatening and simply arrived.

Edmund woke to a sound like the world tearing open. She rolled out of her hammock, bare feet hitting water an inch deep across the deck of the *Magpie*, and stood in the grey half-light watching the rain come down in sheets so dense they erased the forest. Not rain—rain was something with edges, something you could stand beside. This was a wall. The river had gone from brown to white with violence, and where yesterday there had been a channel and a far bank, now there was only churning water and the suggestion of trees drowning in it.

"Up," she said to Casimir, who was already awake, already dressed, his polished boots ruined days ago and replaced with rubber ones she'd traded for at the last village. He'd learned fast. That was one of the things that kept her off balance about him.

"I heard it." He came out onto the deck, hair plastered flat, and looked at the wall of water with an expression she couldn't read. "Is that normal?"

"That's the monsoon. The real one. Everything before this was rehearsal." She was already moving, checking lines, checking the bilge pump that wheezed more than it worked. "We can't run upstream in this. The current would take us backward faster than the engine pushes forward, and if we capsize—" She didn't finish it. The delta in monsoon season did not give back what it took.

"So we go forward."

"We go sideways." She pointed through the rain to a shape barely visible on the rise of ground ahead—a dark block of masonry strangled in vines, half-swallowed by the green. "Mission station. Old one. Pre-war, maybe older. If the roof's even partially intact, we hole up until the worst passes."

Casimir squinted against the downpour. "You've been here before."

"No."

"You navigate like you have."

"Water tells you where things are," Edmund said, hauling the anchor line. "You just have to listen. Now stop talking and start hauling."

* * *

They reached the mission station by midmorning, running the boat up a flooded creek that had once been, judging by the drowned fence posts, a garden path. The building materialized out of the rain all at once: stone walls thick as a man is tall, arched windows empty of glass, a bell tower with no bell. The roof had mostly held—one long wing of slate tiles still doing its work against heaven's opinion—and beneath it they found a chapel, a refectory, and a corridor of small cells with doors hanging like broken wings.

It smelled of wet stone and old incense and bat guano, and to Edmund, soaked to the bone and shivering, it smelled like survival.

They got the boat tied to what remained of the garden gate, got their gear under the best roof, got a fire going in the chapel's stone hearth with wood Casimir split from a rotted pew while Edmund watched him with narrowed eyes. He swung the axe competently. Not showily, not clumsily. Competently, like a man who had done it before and saw no reason to mention it.

"You're full of surprises," she said.

"My father believed a gentleman should be able to do everything and admit nothing," Casimir said, setting another log on the stones. "I've kept half the tradition."

"And which half?"

He smiled at her over the growing flame. "Guess."

* * *

By afternoon the world outside had ceased to exist. The rain came down without pause or mercy, hammering the surviving roof tiles like applause from an enormous indifferent audience, and the flooded creek crept up the mission steps and into the lower corridor, so they moved everything upstairs to the refectory, where a row of tall arched windows gave onto nothing but grey roaring water.

Edmund rigged a line between two pillars and hung their clothes to dry. Casimir produced from his pack a tin of tea, actual tea, good tea, and a small brass kettle, and she decided not to ask how a government survey liaison traveled with loose-leaf assam, because the answer would be a lie and she wasn't in the mood to collect them.

They sat on the floor against the wall because there was no furniture, drinking tea out of tin cups, listening to the storm try to unmake the world.

"The survey team," Casimir said eventually. "Eight people. You found their gear on the sandbar."

"Some of it."

"What do you think happened to them?"

Edmund turned her cup slowly. "People don't vanish cleanly. Wilderness doesn't work that way. If the river takes them, it gives something back—shoe, jacket, body, eventually. If animals take them, there's blood, there's drag marks, there's mess. What I found on that sandbar was arranged. Packs stacked. Boots paired. Like a camp someone tidied before leaving."

"People don't tidy before being murdered."

"No," Edmund said. "But they might walk away on purpose. Or be led."

Casimir was quiet for a moment. "Led by whom?"

"That's the question I keep arriving at," she said, "and the answer keeps coming back wrong."

Outside, lightning turned the windows white, and thunder followed close enough to shake grit from the ceiling beams.

"Tell me why you're really here," Edmund said.

And there it was—the moment she'd been waiting for since the levee, when he'd flashed that contract too quickly, too smoothly, the way a card sharp shows you a queen so you won't look at his other hand. Casimir set down his cup with care, and she watched him choose, watched the machinery turn behind those charming eyes.

"There's a file," he said. "A private commission. The government team was looking for something in this delta—an artifact, a relic, whatever word you prefer. My employers wanted it found first, or found instead. I was hired to make sure the search succeeded." A pause, precise as a surgeon's cut. "That's most of the truth."

"And the rest?"

"The rest isn't mine to tell yet." He met her eyes, and here was the strange thing: he looked unhappy about it. Genuinely. "You'll hate me when you hear it. I'd rather you hear it later than never."

"That's a coward's arithmetic."

"It's a careful man's," he said. "I've been careless exactly once in my life and it cost me everything, so now I count."

Edmund studied him. The firelight moved on his face, and behind the polish she could see the fatigue, the genuine fear he carried about something—not the storm, not the river. Something else. It made her want to dislike him less, which annoyed her, because wanting to dislike him less was itself suspicious.

"Aldous Dravenmoor," she said.

His cup stopped halfway to his mouth.

"He died seven years ago," she went on, watching him carefully. "Famous archaeologist. Last dig never accounted for. Everyone who funded him spent years afterward sending people into rivers looking for what he found. People like your employers." She shrugged. "It's a small profession. Everyone knows everyone's ghosts."

"You knew him," Casimir said quietly.

"Everyone knew him." Another lie, smaller than his, folded into the space between thunderclaps. "My father talked about him enough. Said power changed him near the end. Said he stopped caring about the past and started caring about owning it."

Casimir set down his cup. For a long moment neither of them spoke, and the rain filled the silence like water filling a hull.

"My turn, then," he said. "Why does a woman who reads water like scripture, who navigates drowned country like she was born in it, spend ten years taking surveys nobody else wants? You're too good for this work, Edmund. I mean that as flattery and diagnosis both. Someone with your skills should have a name. Should have a reputation. Instead you're a ghost who fixes boats and vanishes upriver every season." He tilted his head. "What are you hiding from?"

"Debt," she said. "Bad knees. The usual."

"Try again."

"Try asking better questions."

He laughed then—a real laugh, surprised out of him—and something in the room shifted, some tension easing that Edmund hadn't known she'd been carrying. They were both liars, sitting three feet apart in a drowned church, and somehow that made it easier, not harder. Two people keeping secrets could sit closer than two people telling the truth.

"All right," Casimir said. "Better questions. What did Aldous Dravenmoor find, at the end? In the journals I've read—and I've read fragments, smuggled pages, secondhand transcriptions—he talks about something he calls the Meridian. 'A crown for whoever holds it.' Those are nearly his last written words." He looked at her. "Does that mean anything to you?"

The name landed in Edmund's chest like a stone dropped down a well. Her father's notebooks, the ones she'd burned—or told herself she'd burned—the sketches in the margins, the carved stone marker she'd found at the burn site matching drawings she'd hoped to forget. *A crown for whoever holds it.* She had read that phrase at nineteen years old and understood, even then, that her father had found something that found him back.

"No," she said. "Means nothing."

Casimir held her gaze for a long moment, and she knew—he knew—that she was lying. And she watched him decide, visibly, deliberately, not to press. Filing it away. Keeping his own ledger.

"Then we're both rich in mysteries," he said, and refilled her cup.

* * *

Night came early under the storm, and with it a different kind of weather inside the mission station. The fire had settled to coals. Their clothes had dried. There was nothing left to do but exist in the same small space, and existence, given enough hours, erodes performance.

Casimir talked about his mother first. Not planned—it came out sideways, triggered by the wind finding a gap in the tower and moaning through it like a voice. She had been a singer, he said, in a city he wouldn't name, and she had died badly and young, and his father had responded by turning grief into ambition and ambition into a boy trained to be useful to powerful men. "I was raised to be a key," he said, staring into the coals. "Keys don't get to have opinions about doors."

"And now?"

"Now I'm standing in a door wondering if I was cut for the lock." He glanced at her. "Your turn. Trade me something true."

Edmund pulled her knees up. Outside, the flood had risen to swallow the garden entirely; the mission had become an island, and they its only castaways.

"My father was a difficult man," she said. Which was true. "Brilliant. Famous. The kind of famous that eats families. I grew up in camp kitchens and museum basements while strangers worshipped him. When I was old enough to have my own opinions, he didn't want them. When I left, he wrote letters. I didn't answer." She paused. "He died before I could decide whether that was a mistake."

"That's not hiding," Casimir said softly. "That's just grief."

"Grief is what I'm hiding behind." She heard herself say it and was startled—she hadn't meant to give him even that much. But the storm had taken the world down to this room, this fire, this stranger, and something in her had grown tired of holding the whole weight alone. "There. One true thing. Don't spend it all at once."

"I'll invest it wisely," he said, and for once there was no charm in it at all, only a strange gentleness that frightened her more than any lie.

She slept in the chapel that night, on a pew dragged before the hearth, and lay awake a long time listening to the rain. Somewhere in the dark, Casimir was awake too—she could tell by his breathing, deliberate and controlled, a man lying still on purpose. Both of them performing sleep. Both of them thinking.

* * *

She dreamed of her father.

Not the legend—she'd stopped dreaming the legend years ago—but the man, standing waist-deep in black water, holding something above the surface that caught light that had no source. His face was wrong in the way faces in dreams are wrong, shifting between the father who taught her to read currents and the stranger he'd become in his final year, the one whose letters grew strange and urgent, the one who wrote *it wants to be used, Eddy, it wants a hand* in a hand that shook.

In the dream he said her name. Not Edmund. The name nobody living called her anymore.

She woke before dawn to silence—the first silence in thirty hours, shocking as a slap. The rain had thinned to a steady grey whisper. Through the chapel window, the flood stretched out flat and gleaming, and the drowned forest stood in it like a congregation.

Casimir was already at the refectory window, dressed, watching the water. He didn't turn when she came in.

"Flood's dropping," he said. "Another day, maybe two, and we can move."

"Move where?"

He was quiet a moment. Then: "Toward the coordinates in Dravenmoor's last letter. Toward whatever the survey team found." He turned, and his face in the grey light was stripped of all its charm, all its performance, down to something rawer and more honest. "Because I think they found it, Edmund. And I think whatever took them is still there."

Edmund looked past him, out at the waking water, and felt the carved stone marker's shape rising in her memory again, patient as a hook.

So did she.

* * *

They spent the day preparing, and the day after that waiting, and on the third morning the water had fallen enough to float the *Magpie*. They loaded in silence, an ease between them now that was not quite trust and no longer pure suspicion—something being built in the space between their lies, brick by withheld brick.

As they pushed off from the mission steps, Edmund took one last look at the drowned church. In the bell tower, where no bell had hung for decades, something glinted—briefly, in the early light—and was gone.

She told herself it was glass. Old glass, catching sun.

She told herself that all the way down the flooded creek, and almost believed it.
