## 7. Eight Names, One Survivor

The rain had settled into a rhythm by the second day, as if it had made an agreement with the mission station and intended to keep it. It came down the broken roof in ropes and sheets, filled the cistern until it sang, drummed on the tin awning over the old chapel porch in a language Edmund almost understood. She had grown up with monsoons the way other children grew up with lullabies. This one was ordinary. That was what frightened her about everything else.

The station had been abandoned for forty years or more—she'd judged it from the corrosion on the door hinges and the way the banyan roots had shouldered through the east wall of what had once been a dispensary—but its bones were good. Thick coral-lime walls, high ceilings gone black with mold, a rectory that still had half a roof. Casimir Vane had claimed the room with the intact window and called it strategy. Edmund took the records room instead: a narrow chamber off the chapel where the missionaries had kept their ledgers, their baptismal rolls, their correspondence with a diocese that no longer answered.

The paper was her reason for staying dry, though she hadn't said so aloud. Ruined stations kept records. Records meant names. And names were the only thing left of eight people who had walked out of the world without leaving tracks.

She worked by lamplight while the storm pressed at the shutters like something wanting in. The ledgers were swollen with damp, their spines cracked, their ink feathered where water had found them decades ago, but the missionaries had been thorough people—thorough in the way of those who believed God was in the accounting—and they had catalogued everything. Baptisms. Burials. Supplies received from upriver. Supplies sent downriver. A whole civilization of small numbers, ending abruptly one year when the government had redrawn the district and the river had taken the rest.

Casimir appeared in the doorway near dusk, shaking water from his hair, carrying two enamel mugs of cistern water boiled with the last of the tea. He had shed his polished pretense somewhere back in the delta; his boots were ruined now, his shirt gray with mildew, and he looked better for it, the way a painting looks truer when the varnish cracks.

"Any luck?" he asked.

"I don't know yet." She took the mug. Their fingers didn't touch, and she noticed that she noticed.

He lowered himself onto an overturned crate, long legs folded with a dancer's economy, and watched her work without pretending not to. "You've been at those books since dawn."

"They're the only witnesses we have." She turned a page with a bamboo spatula she'd cut for the purpose. "Paper remembers what people won't."

"And what do you hope it remembers?"

"That the survey team came through here." She nodded toward the chapel. "There's a register at the desk inside. Travelers signed it—missionaries made everyone sign, it was practically a sacrament. If the team passed this way before they vanished, there'll be a date, a name, maybe a note about their route."

"And if they didn't?"

"Then I'm reading forty years of dead men's grocery lists for nothing, and you can mock me freely."

"I would never." He sipped his tea. The lamplight moved on his face and she caught again that quality she couldn't name—the charm sitting on him like clothing rather than skin, something worn well but worn. "Though I confess I'm curious why it matters so much to you. Eight strangers. A government contract. You could have stayed on your sandbar reading your river."

Edmund turned another page before answering. Partly because it was true, and partly because she wanted to see if the lie would come easily. It did, which told her something.

"The gear on the sandbar bothered me," she said. "I told you that."

"You told me people didn't leave there. They were taken."

"Taken by something that planned." She looked up at him. "Plans leave paperwork, Vane. Everything leaves paperwork. Rivers, governments, churches. I want to know whose plan it was."

Something flickered behind his eyes—there and gone, quick as a fish turning in brown water. "And if the plan belongs to something worse than the wilderness?"

"Then I'll know its handwriting."

He laughed, low and genuine, and raised his mug to her. "To handwriting, then."

* * *

The travel register gave her the team on the third morning.

She found it in the chapel itself, in a ledger chained—actually chained, iron ring rusted into the desk—to the lectern where parishioners had once signed in. The pages were furred with damp and the last entries were thirty years old, faded to the color of weak tea, and Edmund had nearly given up on it when she saw the fresh ink.

Fresh relative to the rest. Four months old. Ballpoint, blue, pressed hard enough to emboss the next three pages.

Eight signatures. She read them twice, then a third time, copying each into her field notebook with the care of someone handling live ordnance. The team leader had signed first, in a hand that slanted hard to the right, urgent even in a formality: **Dr. Yusuf Okonkwo-Bell, Hydrological Survey Unit 4, Ministry of Water Resources.** Below him, seven more—engineers, a cartographer, a medic, two technicians, a logistics officer named Priya Raghavan who had added, beneath her signature, in small neat letters: *God help us, the maps are wrong.*

Edmund sat back on the pew and let that sentence sit in her chest for a long moment. *The maps are wrong.* Every surveyor's nightmare. Every surveyor's confession, eventually. She had written versions of it herself in reports she'd never submitted.

Below the eighth signature, the ledger offered one more line, and it was this line that stopped her breath.

Not a ninth signature. A ninth *entry*, in different ink—pencil, faint, written in a hurry or written by someone who didn't want to be remembered clearly. No name. Just a notation in the margin beside the date, in a hand she did not recognize:

*Ninth joined at confluence. Vouched by O-B.*

Vouched by Okonkwo-Bell. The ninth member of the expedition had never signed the register at all. He—or she—had simply been *added*, late, at the confluence, on the word of the team leader alone. And whoever recorded the addition had used pencil, because pencil can be erased, and had used no name, because a name is a handle and handles get gripped.

Edmund read the marginal note until the letters lost meaning. Then she went back through her notebook, through the copies she'd made of the manifest from the abandoned gear on the sandbar—that first day, that first cold prickle along her spine—and laid the two lists side by side under the lamp.

Eight names on the register. Eight names on the manifest.

But the manifest's equipment inventory listed nine rations allotments. Nine sleeping kits issued. She had noted the discrepancy weeks ago and dismissed it as bureaucratic sloppiness, because bureaucracies were sloppy, because it was easier to believe in sloppiness than in arithmetic that didn't add.

Nine kits. Eight signatures. One man who existed in the supply chain but not on any list of souls.

"Oh," she said quietly, to the empty chapel, to the rain. "Oh, you clever bastard."

Casimir's voice came from behind her, careful. "Found something?"

She didn't startle, which she counted as a victory. "Come look at this. Tell me what you see."

He crossed the aisle and bent over the ledger, and she watched him read—not skimming, actually reading, his lips slightly parted, his charm set aside like a tool no longer needed. When he reached the marginal note his brow creased.

"A ninth person," he said. "Added late. Vouched for personally by the team leader."

"Never signed. Never named. But issued full kit." She tapped her notebook. "Someone joined this expedition who officially doesn't exist. Someone Okonkwo-Bell trusted enough to vouch for without documentation—which means either the ministry sent him off-book, or Okonkwo-Bell brought him himself."

"Or the ninth person arranged to be brought."

"Yes." She felt the shape of it now, the terrible clean geometry. "That's the part that fits. Look at the sandbar again in your head. Eight sets of gear, folded, weighted, arranged. No struggle. People who walk into a river carrying their own packs aren't captured, Vane. They're *led*. Led by someone they trusted enough to follow into floodwater at night without a fight."

"You think the ninth man walked them into the water."

"I think the ninth man walked them somewhere, and the water is where the trail ends because the water is where he wanted it to end." She stood, and her knee cracked from hours on the hard pew, and she barely felt it. "Every instinct I have says the delta didn't take these people. The delta is being blamed for it. Something planned this, and the plan required a guide who was already inside."

Casimir was quiet for a long moment. Outside, the rain thickened, and thunder rolled down the river valley like furniture being dragged across heaven's floor.

"There's a question you're not asking," he said finally.

"Ask it, then."

"If the ninth man led eight trained government personnel calmly to their deaths—or their disappearance—what did he lead them *to*? People don't do that for nothing. Not eight times over. There's a purpose in this, Edmund, and purposes have owners."

She had been circling that question for two days the way her tongue circled a broken tooth. Now he'd said it plainly and she could not unhear it. "I know," she said. "And I have an idea what the purpose might be, and I hate the idea, and I'm going to tell you anyway because you've earned half of it."

His eyebrows rose. "Half?"

"Half is generous. Consider it a down payment." She closed the ledger gently, the way one closes a coffin. "My father spent the last year of his life looking for something in this delta. He wrote about it obsessively—a relic, an artifact, he called it the Meridian in his notes. He believed it was real. Everyone around him started believing it too, and it wrecked every one of them, including him. His final dig was never accounted for. Neither was he, properly. Seven years ago the river took his camp and the government wrote 'lost, presumed drowned' and everyone breathed a sigh of relief."

Casimir had gone very still. Stillness, she was learning, was what Casimir Vane looked like when the truth got close.

"And you think the ninth man is looking for the same thing."

"I think the ninth man knew my father's work. I think the survey team's route—" she opened her notebook to the map she'd drawn from the gear tags and the register dates, the route reconstructed from resupply points, "—doesn't follow the river at all past the third tributary. It follows *my father's route*. The route from his journals. Which means someone fed it to the ministry, or someone on that team already knew it." Her voice came out flatter than she intended. "Which means this has been circling my family's grave for years, waiting for enough water to drown the evidence."

The lamp guttered. Rain hammered the shutters. In the dark of the chapel, the ledger lay closed on its chain like a thing sleeping.

Casimir spoke softly. "Edmund. What was your father's name?"

She looked at him—at the ruined boots and the mildewed shirt and the eyes that were too attentive by exactly one degree—and understood, in the way one understands a trap only after stepping in it, that she had just handed him something he had been fishing for since the levee.

"Aldous Dravenmoor," she said, because the lie had gotten too heavy to carry, and because some part of her wanted to see what he'd do with it.

He didn't flinch. He didn't exclaim. He exhaled slowly, like a man setting down luggage he'd been hauling much too far, and said, "I know."

* * *

They stood facing each other across the pew while the storm tried the doors, and Edmund discovered that betrayal, done properly, feels less like a wound than like a recalculation—every prior kindness re-weighted, every easy laugh audited, the whole architecture of trust rebuilt in seconds on new assumptions.

"How long?" she asked.

"Since before I met you."

"Why?"

"Because I was paid to find you." He held her gaze without charm now, without polish, and she grudgingly admitted that the nakedness suited him. "Not the river. Not the team. You. Aldous Dravenmoor's daughter. The sole heir. My employers wanted to know where you were and what you knew, and I was the instrument they chose, and I told myself it was research, and then it wasn't, and then we were in the boat and I ran out of honest options."

"So everything—the contract, the questions, the—"

"The contract was real. The questions were real. The rest was real too, whatever it was." He passed a hand over his face. "I'm not asking forgiveness. I'm telling you the shape of the cage so you can decide whether I'm inside it or outside it."

Edmund turned away from him and looked at the ledger, at the pencil-thin ghost of a note that had unraveled everything. Ninth joined at confluence. Vouched by O-B. Someone had entered the expedition wearing trust like a stolen coat, and had led eight people into the green throat of the delta, and the trail ended in water, and the water was lying on his behalf.

And now the same current had carried her a stranger who lied beautifully, and she had liked him, and liking him had been the plan all along.

"The enemy isn't the wilderness," she said quietly. "It never was. The wilderness just gets blamed."

"No," Casimir agreed. "It doesn't."

She picked up her notebook, and her knife, and her father's route memorized in her head where no one could steal it, and she looked at the shuttered window where the monsoon leaned its whole weight against the mission station like a siege.

"Then here's what happens now," she said. "You're going to tell me everything—your employers' names, the commission, all of it—before that storm lets up. Because when it does, I'm going after the ninth man. And whether you're walking beside me or swimming home is entirely up to how honest the next hour is."

Outside, the thunder answered, and somewhere downriver, in the drowned dark where eight people had gone ahead, something waited that had already learned her name.
