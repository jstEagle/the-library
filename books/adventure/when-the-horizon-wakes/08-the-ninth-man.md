## 8. The Ninth Man

The rain had been falling for two days without once deciding to be gentle about it.

Casimir Vane sat in the ruined mission station with his back against a wall that had once been whitewashed and was now the color of old teeth, and he listened to the monsoon hammering the tin roof like a fist on a door that would never open. Across the room, Edmund Dravenmoor crouched over a crate of mission records she'd hauled down from the loft, her lantern turned low, her hair plastered dark against her neck. She hadn't spoken to him in six hours. Not since the marker stone. Not since she'd gone very still in the mud and said, in a voice he'd come to think of as her surveyor's voice—flat, precise, dangerous only in what it refused to carry—"I've seen this before."

He knew what she'd seen it in. He'd known since Rotterdam, when the file first crossed his desk and he'd read the name *Edmund Dravenmoor* twice before understanding that Edmund was a woman, and that the woman was the daughter, and that the daughter was the point—the whole point, the only point—of everything that followed.

His real commission lay folded in the oilskin wallet inside his coat, three pages of typescript and one photograph, and he had carried it upriver like a man carries a knife he means to use and then doesn't. He could feel its weight now through the wet wool. The rain gave him an excuse to wait. The rain gave him an excuse for anything; that was what he liked about weather—it absolved everyone of their silences.

But the silence had curdled. He could see it in the set of her shoulders as she turned another brittle page. She wasn't reading anymore. She was waiting too, and Edmund Dravenmoor did not wait the way other people waited, patiently or petulantly. She waited the way a river waited behind a levee: with total, geological patience, and with the absolute certainty that eventually it would go through you rather than around.

"Casimir." She didn't look up. "You've been watching me for ten minutes."

"Have I?"

"You count things when you're nervous. Your fingers, the roof leaks, the seconds between lightning and thunder. You counted eleven just now." She closed the ledger she wasn't reading. "You're not nervous about the storm."

No, he thought. He was not.

* * *

Here is what Casimir Vane had been hired to do, in the language of the document he could recite from memory:

*Locate E. Dravenmoor, sole surviving blood relation of Aldous Dravenmoor (d. seven years past, cause officially recorded as fever, unofficially unrecorded). Establish rapport. Do not disclose affiliation. Assess whether subject possesses knowledge of the final expedition's location or contents. If yes, report. If no, cultivate until yes.*

There had been more—a paragraph about discretion, a paragraph about expenses, a paragraph written in the particular bureaucratic dialect that meant *we will deny you if this goes wrong*—but those were the bones of it. Find the daughter. Befriend the daughter. Determine whether the daughter knew where her father had died looking for something his own journals called, in a phrase the client had underlined twice in pencil, *a crown for whoever holds it.*

The Meridian. That was the word they used in the office in Geneva, always quietly, the way people say the name of a dead person they're not sure is really dead. Casimir had spent four years working for men who collected antiquities the way other men collected grievances, and in four years he had never seen them afraid of an object. They were not afraid of the Meridian. They were hungry for it, which was worse, and hungrier than he'd ever seen them, and the hunger had started the moment Aldous Dravenmoor's last letters began circulating among collectors seven years ago—fragments, quotations, a single line repeated across three different letters in three different hands, as though the old man couldn't stop writing it even when he tried:

*A crown for whoever holds it.*

And then Aldous died, and the dig vanished—not the artifacts, the dig itself, coordinates and crew and all—and the hunger went underground and grew teeth. Two years ago they'd funded a government survey into the delta, dressed up as hydrology. Eight names on the manifest. The survey had stopped transmitting in the third week of the dry season, and the client had stopped pretending it was about hydrology at all.

Casimir had been given the file on Edmund Dravenmoor the same week. He'd read it in a hotel bar in Rotterdam with rain on the windows, and he remembered thinking, with the small cold clarity that sometimes came to him before a lie got big: *she has no idea what her father found, and no idea what it costs.*

He had been wrong about that, he suspected now. He had possibly been wrong about everything.

* * *

"Edmund." He said her name and watched her shoulders change, the way a landscape changes when weather arrives. "There are things I should have told you at the levee."

"There are things you should have told me every day since the levee." She finally looked at him, and the lantern light did something unkind to her face, hollowing the cheeks, throwing the eyes into shadow so that what remained was mostly gaze. "I'm going to ask you questions, Casimir. I want you to understand that I already know most of the answers are lies. I want you to tell me anyway."

"All right."

"Why does a government hydrologist carry a photograph of my father?"

The question landed exactly where she'd aimed it, because of course she'd seen it—she saw everything, that was the trouble with her, the gift and the affliction of her—he'd kept the wallet buttoned for six days and then last night, half-asleep against the mission wall, he'd checked it, one glance, one flick of the oilskin, and she'd been awake. Of course she'd been awake. She slept like a heron, one eye on the water.

He took out the wallet. He took out the photograph. He laid it on the floor between them rather than handing it to her, because some things you don't hand a person; you let them pick it up themselves, so the choosing is theirs.

It was a studio portrait, twenty years old. Aldous Dravenmoor in his forties, black beard going grey at the chin, one hand resting on a case of instruments, the other holding a hat he clearly resented having to hold for the camera. A famous face. A face from textbook margins and museum lobbies and the one obituary Edmund had presumably read forty times and answered, by her silence, never.

Edmund picked it up. Her hands were steady. He noticed that her hands were always steady except when she was tired, and she was tired now, and they were steady anyway, which meant she was spending something to keep them that way.

"Who sent you?" she asked.

"The people who funded the survey team."

"The eight people who walked into the water."

"I don't know that they walked into the water," Casimir said. "I know that my employers lost contact with them, and that they believe the team found something. Or found where something is." He made himself hold her eyes. "They believe your father found it first. Seven years ago. And they believe he hid it, and died hiding it, and that the only person alive who might know where is sitting in front of me in a flooded church."

"And what is *it*, Casimir? Say the word. You've been saying *it* for six days like a man avoiding a hole in the road."

"The Meridian."

Something moved behind her face—not recognition, exactly. Recognition's older sibling. The look of someone hearing a word they've heard before in a house where they weren't supposed to be listening.

"My father's journals," she said slowly. "I was fourteen. He caught me reading them and burned two of them in the yard. But there was a third—I'd already hidden it—and there was a phrase in it he'd written over and over, in the margins, in the middle of survey notes, everywhere, like a tic. Like a prayer he hated." Her voice didn't rise. It lowered, which was worse. "*A crown for whoever holds it.*"

"Yes."

"What is it? What does it *do*?"

"No one knows. That's the honest answer, and I'll give you the dishonest one too, because you'll want both: my employers believe it does whatever the holder needs it to do. Influence. Leverage. They use words like *coherence* and *authority*. They talk about it the way men talk about a woman they intend to marry for her money." He paused. "Your father's journals suggest they're right to be interested. His last letters get strange. Grandiose. He writes about the Meridian like it writes back."

Edmund set the photograph down with exaggerated care, the way you set down something when the alternative is throwing it.

"So the survey team came here for it."

"The survey team came here to find where your father found it. Hydrology was the cover. Eight specialists, and one of them—" he stopped.

"One of them what?"

Casimir had rehearsed this part. He'd rehearsed it on the boat, in the green throat of the forest, at the burn site while she knelt in the mud reading a story in ash that ended mid-stride. He'd rehearsed it because he'd known, since the marker stone, that the story had a shape and that she would find the shape before he was ready to give it to her, and he'd wanted to be the one who named it.

He discovered, saying it aloud, that rehearsal was useless. Some sentences you can't practice your way out of.

"One of them wasn't on the original manifest. A ninth name, added late, paid through a separate account. My employers didn't add him. We think your father's old circle did—or what's left of it. Someone who knew the delta, knew the journals, and wanted the Meridian for himself." He watched her. "Someone went rogue, Edmund. Before your government team ever launched. The team may not have been hunting the Meridian at all by the end. They may have been being hunted by it—or by the man who wants it."

The rain filled the silence for a long time. Somewhere above them a beam ticked as the water found a new way through the old wood.

"Aldous had students," Edmund said at last, very quietly. "Half a dozen he trusted. One he trusted more than me, near the end." Her thumb moved along the edge of the ledger, back and forth, a small unconscious motion like water worrying a bank. "Marek."

"You know him."

"I know a boy who ate dinner at our table and called my father *the old river* because he said you couldn't dam him, only redirect him." She looked up. "I was twelve. I thought it was affectionate."

"And now?"

"Now I think some people redirect rivers," Edmund said, "and some people become the flood."

* * *

He should have stopped there. He knew it even as he opened his mouth—that there was a version of this confession that could survive, trimmed and shaped, the way he'd survived every other conversation of his life by giving people ninety percent of the truth and letting their goodwill build the missing tenth. But the rain had been falling for two days, and the roof leaked, and she was looking at him with her father's eyes in a face that had nothing else of her father in it, and Casimir Vane, who had lied beautifully to ministers and smugglers and one archbishop, found that the last lie had gone out of him somewhere between the burn site and this leaking room.

"There's more," he said. "The part I should have told you at the levee."

"Say it."

"I wasn't hired to find the Meridian." He heard his own voice go quiet, go honest, go somewhere unfamiliar. "Finding the Meridian is the job after the job. I was hired to find *you*. To locate Aldous Dravenmoor's heir, establish trust, and determine what you know. You weren't my guide, Edmund. You were the assignment. Every mile of this river, I was reporting on you in my head. Cataloguing you. Deciding how best to use you."

The lantern flame stood perfectly still between them, as if it too were waiting.

"How long," she said.

"Since Rotterdam."

"Rotterdam." She said the word like she was tasting spoiled water. "That's where you picked up my trail."

"That's where I picked up *you*. The trail was easy. You leave footprints everywhere you refuse to leave a name. Ten years of surveys nobody wanted, contracts under initials, a woman who's erased herself so thoroughly that finding her was the hard part and knowing her was—" He stopped himself. Too late. She'd already heard it.

"Was what?" Softly. Terribly soft.

"Was the part I didn't plan for."

"Don't." She stood. The motion was unhurried, which somehow made it worse, like watching a lock turn. "Don't do that thing you do, Casimir, where you hand me a true feeling wrapped around a false frame and hope I take the bundle. You catalogued me. Fine. Good. Honest, finally. Now hear my catalogue of you: polished boots in a place with no roads. A contract flashed too fast. Small lies about small things—your brother in Lisbon who doesn't exist, the fever you never had, the reason you flinched at the marker stone before I ever touched it. You've been lying since the levee, and the lies were good, and I *knew*, Casimir. I knew by the second day. I told myself a tracker who reads water for a living ought to be able to read a man, and if I let myself be fooled anyway, that was my choice, my risk, my price for the company." Her voice cracked—not broke, cracked, like ice under a boot, holding but changed forever. "I chose to trust you knowing you were probably false. Do you understand what that cost? Because you never once chose anything. You were *sent*."

"Edmund—"

"The ninth man walked eight people calmly into the water," she said. "You walked me calmly up a river. I'm done being walked."

She took the lantern. She left the photograph where it lay, face-up, her father smiling his camera smile at a ceiling that was letting the century in drop by drop. At the door she paused—one breath, maybe two—and he understood with a clarity that felt like drowning that she was not pausing for him. She was pausing the way she paused at the edge of any water before entering it: reading the current, weighing the far bank, deciding if the crossing was worth what it would take out of her.

Then she pushed open the door and stepped into the monsoon, and the rain took her the way the delta takes everything—completely, without malice, without memory.

Casimir sat alone in the ruined mission with a photograph of a dead man and a commission he no longer wanted to complete, and listened to the storm close the world like a door.

She had taken the lantern. She had left him the dark. And somewhere out in all that falling water, alone, heading he didn't know where—to the coordinates she'd carried in her head for years, he realized, the ones she'd never needed him or anyone to find—Edmund Dravenmoor was walking toward her father's grave, and Casimir understood that whatever happened next, he had exactly one choice that wasn't a lie.

He reached for his boots.
