## 9. Alone Is a Skill

The rain had not stopped since she left the mission station; it had only changed its mind about how hard to fall.

Edmund moved through it the way her father had taught her to move through weather—head down, pace steady, anger banked like a coal carried in cupped hands. Behind her, the ruined station sagged into the flooded treeline, and behind that, somewhere in the grey curtain of the monsoon, Casimir Vane was either watching her go or already gone himself. She did not look back to find out. Looking back was a luxury the delta did not sell.

She had taken what she needed and nothing more: the canvas pack with its waxed seams, three days of dried fish and cassava cakes, the machete, the flare pistol with two shells, and the small brass compass that had belonged to Aldous Dravenmoor before it belonged to anyone's disappointment. The coordinates lived where they had always lived—in her head, inked into memory at fourteen years old, the night she'd memorized his last letter before burning it.

*Eleven degrees of nothing,* he used to say about remote survey work, *and one degree of everything.* He'd meant it as a joke about grid references. She'd never found it funny.

The flooded forest opened around her in green corridors, water thigh-deep and warm as breath, every trunk wearing a skirt of algae. She waded with the compass held flat on her palm like an offering, correcting for the current's lazy pull toward the sea. Somewhere overhead a hornbill laughed its harsh, broken laugh—the sound the river made when it thought something was funny, her father used to say, and she hated that even now, alone in the throat of the storm, his voice arrived uninvited and fit perfectly.

*Alone is a skill,* he told her once, crouched beside a fire in some other country, some other lifetime, whittling a tent peg with the concentration other men gave to prayer. *Most people only survive solitude. You should learn to live in it. It's the only country that never falls.*

She had been twelve. She had believed him completely. That was the cruelest part—she had learned the lesson too well, and by the time she understood he'd been teaching her how to leave him, the distance between them had hardened into geography.

* * *

The first night she camped in the fork of a great fig tree, wedged into the crook with her pack strapped above the waterline, and sleep came grudgingly, in pieces, like salvage.

In the pieces, her father was alive.

Not the father of the obituaries—the tall, sun-cured man with the laugh that started in his chest and the habit of naming rivers after people he loved. But the last-year father. The one who had come back from the Meridian expedition changed, as though he'd swallowed a lamp and couldn't stop glowing. Edmund remembered standing in the doorway of his study in the old house, seventeen years old, watching him read a letter by lamplight with an expression she had no word for then and only one for now: *entitlement*. Not joy. Joy spends itself. This was the look of a man who had been promised something and intended to collect.

*You're staring, Edie.*

*You're different.* She'd said it plainly, because she was nineteen going on forty and plainness was the only language they shared anymore. *Since you came back. You look at things like they're already yours.*

He'd set down the letter. And here was the thing she had turned over ten thousand times since, the stone she could not stop skipping across the years: he hadn't denied it. He'd smiled—a real smile, warm, terrible—and said, *Maybe I finally understand what things are for.*

Three weeks later he was dead. A helicopter gone down in the delta, they said, though no wreckage consistent with a helicopter was ever recovered, and the final dig site was never logged, and seven crew members signed nondisclosure agreements and scattered like startled birds. The foundation issued statements. The universities issued eulogies. The world got a legend, tidy and laminated.

Edmund got silence, and a name she stopped using, and coordinates she memorized from a letter she burned so that no one—not the foundation, not the vultures who circled his legacy, not herself on weak nights—could follow them anywhere but out of her own mouth.

She had carried them for eleven years like a splinter worked deep into the palm. Tonight, swinging gently in a fig tree while the rain stitched the dark together, she finally let herself ask the question underneath all the others:

If she reached the dig site—if it existed, if the water hadn't taken it—what would she do there? Grieve? Verify? Dig up the truth like a bone and hold it up to whatever light remained?

Or would she find the thing that changed him, still waiting, still patient, still *for* someone?

She slept badly after that, and dreamed of lamplight.

* * *

Dawn came the color of pewter, and with it the second day's labor: eight hours of wading and poling through drowned forest, navigating by the compass and by the subtle grammar of the current. The delta here was older than the maps suggested. Channels braided and unbraided overnight. Islands the size of churches appeared where none had been, built of silt and floating debris, anchored by fallen trees that had traveled hundreds of miles to die standing.

She read the water the way other people read faces. Where the surface went glassy over brown depth, she detoured. Where kingfishers perched low, she trusted the shallows. Where the current ran smooth but the air smelled wrong—sweetish, vegetal, like a cellar—she knew there was undercut below, a hollow bank waiting to swallow the unwary whole.

Her father taught her that too. That was the trap of him, she thought grimly, shoving through a curtain of hanging vines: there was no part of her competence he hadn't touched. Every skill she owned was also a souvenir of him. She could not be good at her life without carrying him in it.

Toward midday the forest thinned, and she saw the first sign.

A stone, half-sunk in the mud of a levee, carved with a spiral that ended in a closed eye. She knew it instantly—not from any registry, not from any textbook, but from the margins of his notebooks, where he doodled symbols he claimed were decorative. They had never been decorative. He had been mapping his way back the whole time, leaving breadcrumbs only his daughter would recognize, because only his daughter had spent her childhood reading over his shoulder.

Her hands shook, and she was furious at them for shaking.

* * *

The second night the storm bands tightened, and the rain came down like judgment, and she sheltered beneath an overturned longboat wedged in the roots of a kapok tree—a boat old enough that its planks had gone soft as bread, moss-furred, home to a family of bats who objected to her arrival in a flurry of leather wings and indignation.

She ate half her remaining food and made herself stop. Rationing was arithmetic; arithmetic was calm; calm was a skill, and she was very skilled.

But arithmetic could not keep the memories out, and the memories came with the dark.

The estrangement had not been one fight. It had been a slow erosion, a coastline giving way grain by grain. After her mother died, when Edmund was nine, her father had poured himself into work the way drowning men pour themselves into open water. Expeditions lengthened. Letters shortened. By the time she was fifteen she was spending more terms at boarding school than at home, and by eighteen she had perfected the art of being unreachable in ways that looked like diligence—remote postings, seasonal contracts, surveys in watersheds so obscure the maps apologized for their uncertainty.

People assumed she was following him. Colleagues at conferences, warm with wine, would say, *It's wonderful, you carrying on the family tradition*, and she would smile the smile she'd practiced in mirrors and say something gracious, and inside she would feel the old cold truth: she wasn't following him. She was patrolling the far border of his influence, keeping watch on the wilderness he'd loved more than her, making sure it stayed exactly where it was.

Then the letter came. Eleven years ago. Two months before the helicopter that wasn't a helicopter.

She had kept it unopened for six days. She could still feel those six days in her body, the way you feel a fever you broke decades ago. When she finally tore it open, his handwriting was agitated, slanting downhill like something running:

*Edie. I've found it. Or it has found me—I no longer pretend to know the difference. What I know is this: I was wrong about everything I ever taught you, except the rivers. Come with me. One last season, just us, the way it was. There is something under the delta that will make us both rich beyond—*

No. That wasn't how it began. She kept catching herself editing it, sanding off the ugly parts. It began:

*There is something under the delta that will make us both immortal.*

Immortal. Her father, who had taught her that the river keeps its receipts, who had named eroded islands after dead friends, who had buried a bronze mirror at a site once rather than let a collector buy it—he had written *immortal* in his own hand and underlined it twice.

She had put the letter in the stove. She had watched the corners curl and blacken, and she had felt nothing, and then she had felt everything, and then she had memorized the coordinates anyway—because she was her father's daughter, and her father's daughter burns the message but keeps the map.

Now, eleven years later, lying under a rotten boat while bats muttered overhead and the monsoon hammered the world flat, she admitted what she had refused to admit at the mission station, what she had refused to admit to Casimir Vane with his polished boots and his government paper:

She wasn't going to the dig site for the missing team. The missing team was the door, but the room beyond it had always been hers. She was going because the questions had compounded interest for eleven years. Because *immortal* needed answering. Because somewhere under that water was either the thing that killed him or the thing that made him, and she could not spend another season pretending she didn't need to know which.

Grief and anger braided together in her chest like the channels outside—impossible to tell where one strand ended and the other began, both pulling toward the same sea.

* * *

On the third morning, the forest ended.

Not gradually—the way the delta did everything, all at once. The trees simply gave up, thinning to stumps, then to skeletal snags rising from open water, and beyond them the channel widened into a vast flooded basin where the horizon was a rumor and the sky sat directly on the river with no land in between.

She checked the compass against the sun, then against the wind, then against the drift of debris moving steadily seaward. The coordinates put the site four hours ahead, at the heart of the basin, in water that would now be thirty feet deep over ground that had been dry when her father last stood on it.

She built a raft.

It took her most of the day—four lengths of dry snag-wood lashed with vine and the last of her cordage, a platform barely big enough for her and the pack, riding low and sullen in the water. She poled it out into the basin as the afternoon light went copper and the storm clouds stacked themselves along the western horizon like masonry.

The water here was different. She felt it before she understood it—too still, too deep-colored, holding its breath. Fish rose around the raft in slow, unhurried rings, as if surfacing to look at her. Once, something large passed beneath, displacing a swell that rocked the timbers without breaking the surface, and she did not see it and did not want to.

And then she saw the marker.

It rose out of the water ahead of her like a drowned man's finger: a basalt pillar, maybe nine feet of it still above the surface, carved top to bottom with spirals and closed eyes, furred with weed, ancient beyond any culture she could name. Her father's doodles had copied it faithfully. His notebooks had sketched it lovingly. Which meant he had stood here. Which meant the site was real, and close, and—

The raft bumped something beneath the surface. Soft. Yielding.

Edmund went still, machete sliding free of her belt, and looked down into the brown-gold water.

Below her, faint in the filtered light, stretched a lattice of timber and rope—scaffolding, excavation frames, the skeleton of a dig, preserved by the flood that had swallowed it. And caught in the frame, drifting gently with the current, pale as a face looking up:

A body. Long dead, waterlogged, clothed in field gear whose cut she recognized because she had grown up watching men wear it.

It was not her father. She knew that with a strange, cold certainty before her mind supplied the reason: the build was wrong, the height, the hair. It was one of his crew—one of the seven who had signed the agreements and vanished. Except this one hadn't vanished. This one had stayed, all these years, at the bottom of the site everyone swore didn't exist, holding position like a sentry.

And as the raft drifted closer, the current turning the body slowly in the water, Edmund saw what was clenched in its fist, raised toward the surface in a grip that death had made permanent:

A notebook. Small, waterproofed, wax-sealed. And on its cover, in handwriting she would have known blindfolded, in the dark, at the bottom of the sea—

her own name.
