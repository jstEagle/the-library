## 10. What Follows Quietly

The ruins announced themselves the way old grief does—gradually, then all at once.

Edmund had been poling through the drowned forest for two days since leaving Casimir standing in the rain with his confession still hanging between them like smoke. Two days of channeling west by dead reckoning and the compass she kept in a oilskin pouch against her sternum, following coordinates her father had burned into her memory a decade ago, back when he'd still been trying to make her his heir instead of his echo. The trees thickened. The water went from brown to black, tannin-stained and mirror-still, and out of that stillness rose walls: brick softened into rounded lumps by forty years of flood, window arches gaping like fish mouths, a bell tower leaning at an angle that defied physics and patience both.

A mission station once, or a trading post. Now just bones of masonry wearing a coat of green.

She brought the boat in through what had been a doorway wide enough for carts, the hull scraping something soft—silt, or a rotted door—and let the current settle her against a wall that still stood shoulder-high. The rain had thinned to a fine drifting mist that made no sound when it landed, which was somehow worse than the deluge. Sound carried strangely here. She'd noticed it an hour ago and told herself it was the architecture: hard surfaces, open water, echoes doing what echoes did.

But echoes repeated. They didn't *precede*.

That was the thing she couldn't stop turning over as she tied off the boat and climbed onto a stretch of collapsed roof that formed a dry-ish platform above the waterline. Twice today, in the channels, she had heard movement—a paddle knock, a branch releasing its load—and each time, when she'd stopped to listen, the sound had come again, a half-beat after hers stopped. As if someone were matching her rhythm. Learning it.

*You're tired,* she told herself. *You've been alone in the wet season with nothing but your own head for company. Heads do this.*

Her head had also told her, on the sandbar, that people hadn't left that government gear—they'd been taken. And her head had been right about that, God help her.

She made camp anyway, because the alternative was poling through black water in the dark, and exhaustion killed more river travelers than crocodiles ever had. Cold rations. No fire—the mist was too thin to hide the glow, and something in her had gone quietly, absolutely certain that she did not want to be seen. She sat with her back to a wall, machete across her knees, and watched the doorway where the boat came in, and let the dark assemble itself around her piece by piece.

It was during that watching that she understood, fully and without drama, the way you understand a thing you've known for days and only now permit yourself to say:

Someone had been behind her since the mission station. Since before it. Since the morning she'd struck out alone.

Not Casimir. She knew Casimir's movements the way she knew the river's—his paddling had a hitch on the left stroke, an impatience in the turns. This follower had no hitch. This follower moved the way she moved, or better: pausing when she paused, resting when she rested, never closing distance, never losing it either. Patient as sediment.

And there was only one person left in this delta who fit that description. The name surfaced without being called: *the ninth man.* The one who'd joined late. The one who'd walked eight people calmly into the water—or followed them out, or led them, she still didn't know which. The one whose existence Casimir had confessed to like a man coughing up glass.

Edmund sat in the dark with her machete and felt fear move through her body in slow waves, and then, somewhere past midnight, felt the fear change its chemical composition into something else. Something hotter. Anger always had been her fear's twin; they'd shared a womb her whole life.

*You've been running since the sandbar,* she thought. *Since the levee, if you're honest. Since your father died and you pointed your whole life away from his shadow. And it hasn't worked. It has never once worked.*

She stopped running at approximately three in the morning, sitting on a ruined roof in the drowned belly of the delta, and decided that whatever came next would come to her facing forward.

* * *

Dawn came gray and sourceless, the mist so uniform that the ruins seemed suspended in milk. Edmund ate, broke camp with exaggerated slowness, and did not get in the boat.

Instead she went up.

The bell tower leaned but held, its internal stair a spiral of cracked stone and iron rebar furred with rust. She climbed it quietly—years of survey work had taught her feet to find the silent part of any surface—and settled into the shadow beneath the belfry arch with a view of the flooded courtyard below, the boat, the doorway, and the long channel leading away east. Then she waited, and made waiting into a weapon.

The river taught you this if you let it: most creatures announce themselves twice. Once in intention, once in arrival. The space between was where you lived or died.

She waited through the mist burning off in slow rags. Through the first kingfisher's blue strike. Through the moment mid-morning when the light finally found the water and turned it from black to the color of strong tea. Her legs cramped. She let them. Somewhere below, a caiman slid off a submerged windowsill with a sound like a book closing.

He came at noon.

Not through the eastern channel, which she'd watched all morning, but from the west—from *behind* her original heading, which meant he'd circled in the night while she'd sat congratulating herself on her vigilance. A pirogue, narrow and low, poled by a figure in a rain slicker gone gray with mildew, moving with the unhurried economy of a man who had done this ten thousand times. He brought his boat alongside hers—not touching it, just reading it, the way she read abandoned gear—and then he looked up at the tower.

Looked directly at the arch where she crouched.

"Dravenmoor," he said. His voice was mild. Educated. It carried across the water without effort, pitched the way a teacher pitches a lecture to the back row. "I know you're there. You've been watching me since the second channel yesterday. You have your father's patience." A pause, and then, almost gently: "You don't have his faith."

Every hair on Edmund's arms rose. Not at the words—at the *name*. He'd said it aloud, casually, the way you'd greet a colleague. He knew who she was. Which meant the ninth man hadn't stumbled onto her trail. He'd been *following* it. Following *her*, specifically, toward the coordinates she'd told no living soul.

She stayed still. Stillness was information too; it told him she wouldn't be baited into revealing her position further, and it bought her time to study him as he studied the courtyard. Mid-fifties, maybe. Lean in the way of men who'd spent decades feeding themselves irregularly. When he pushed back his hood to wipe his face, she saw gray-shot hair plastered flat, a face that might once have been handsome in a severe, ascetic way, and eyes that were the problem. Eyes should be windows. His were doors closed from the inside, painted over, with a light burning behind them anyway.

Marek. It had to be. Aldous's protégé, the trusted student, the name Casimir had given her like a diagnosis.

"I'm going to leave my boat," Marek said, conversationally, as if they were discussing weather. "I'm going to walk up the north stair—it's sounder than yours. I'm going to come to the belfry, because we need to talk, and because you'll want to see what I'm carrying before you decide anything." He stepped out of the pirogue into knee-deep water and began wading toward the ruin's interior, dragging his boat behind him. "And Edmund—" the familiarity of it, the *warmth* of it, made her stomach turn— "please don't do anything we'll both regret. I've spent seven years looking forward to meeting Aldous's daughter."

*Seven years.* Since her father died. Since the dig went unaccounted for. He'd been out here the whole time, in the green throat of the delta, and everything that had happened—the vanished team, the burned camp, eight people walking calmly into the water—had flowed downstream from whatever lived behind those painted-over eyes.

Edmund drew her machete without a sound and began, very quietly, to move.

* * *

The tower's upper gallery ran in a loop around the belfry, connected to the north stair by a short passage floored with rotted planks over a twelve-foot drop into flooded rubble. She reached the junction ahead of him—he climbed slowly, deliberately, a man who believed time was his—and crouched in the passage's shadows with her back against cold brick, listening to his footsteps come up the stair. Unhurried. Even. A metronome.

The plan was simple and bad: let him reach the top, take him by surprise at the junction, put the machete to his throat, ask her questions. She'd never taken a prisoner. She'd never held a blade on a human being at all. Her hands knew survey instruments and outboard engines and the precise angle at which a crocodile skull hinges open, not this.

His head cleared the stairwell.

He was closer than she'd calculated—her sense of the stair's geometry had been off, the lean of the tower cheating the distances—and for one suspended instant they looked at each other across four feet of rotten floor, and his expression did something terrible. It didn't harden or sharpen. It *softened*. He smiled at her the way you smile at family.

"There she is," he said.

Then he lunged, and the world became noise.

Later, Edmund could never reconstruct the fight in order. Fragments: the machete's edge catching his forearm as he grabbed her wrist, the hot spray of it across her cheek. The floor plank giving way under his boot with a crack like a rifle shot, his weight swinging toward the gap, his free hand clamping her collar and hauling her toward the drop with him—his logic legible even in violence: *if I fall, she falls.* The wall slamming into her spine. His breath against her ear, saying, insanely, gently, "You'll understand soon. He left it for us. For whoever was *strong enough*—" Her knee finding the one solid plank. Her thumb finding his wrist, the tendon, the nerve channel every river worker knows from hauling lines. Pressing until his grip tore loose.

He went down—not through the gap but against the gallery rail, which groaned, held, and then surrendered one rotten length with a shriek of splitting wood. He caught himself on the stonework below, and Edmund saw blood sheeting down his forearm where she'd cut him, saw him look at the blood with an expression she'd remember longer than the pain in her own ribs: not shock. *Confirmation.* As if his wound proved something he'd been trying to believe.

"You're like him," he said from below, cradling the arm, backing toward the north stair, and his voice was still calm, still warm, and it was the calm that finally made her fury complete. "He fought it too, at the end. It doesn't matter. Nothing that's coming cares whether you consent."

"Eight people," Edmund said. Her voice came out low and shaking and harder than she'd ever heard it. "Eight people walked into the water with their packs on their backs. Was that consent too?"

"They went ahead." He said it with the serene certainty of scripture. "They're preparing the way. You'll see."

Then he was gone—down the stair, into the water, into the pirogue, moving west with a wounded man's haste, and Edmund stood in the belfry shaking, machete dripping, alive by perhaps four seconds' margin, and discovered that she wasn't afraid anymore. The terror had burned clean off, the way floodwater scours silt, and underneath was something simple and structural. Fury, yes. But also decision.

He dropped something climbing into the boat. A wallet of oilskin, old and swollen, spilling papers into the channel—one photograph, heavier stock than the rest, catching on a submerged sill where she could reach it.

She waded in and took it.

The photograph was thirty years old, its edges silvered, its colors shifted toward ghost-blue. A younger Aldous Dravenmoor stood in front of a trench wall bristling with survey stakes, mud to his thighs, grinning like a man who'd found God. And in his raised hands, held up to the camera with the pride of a father presenting a firstborn—

Edmund's breath stopped.

—a circlet. A ring of worked metal and set stones, small enough to hold in two palms, wrong somehow even in a faded photograph, its surface seeming to drink the flash rather than return it. On the back, in her father's handwriting—the handwriting she'd know anywhere, the handwriting from a decade of birthday cards she'd returned unopened:

*Found it. God forgive me. — A.D., 12 March*

Below that, smaller, pressed hard enough to tear the paper:

*It is not a crown. It is a mouth.*

Edmund stood in the flooded courtyard with the photograph dripping in her hands, and the last of her hesitation went out of her like heat from a stone. Her father had found it and been destroyed by it. Marek had found her father's trail and been remade by it. Eight people had walked into the water singing, apparently, and somewhere west, in the direction of coordinates she carried in her head like a bullet carried in a chamber, the thing itself waited in its drowned vault—waiting for her, or for Marek, or simply for the monsoon to finish opening the door.

She looked at the horizon, gray and endless, and did not flinch from it.

"No more running," she said aloud, to the ruins, to the river, to the dead.

Then she went to get her boat, and turned west, toward the dig.
