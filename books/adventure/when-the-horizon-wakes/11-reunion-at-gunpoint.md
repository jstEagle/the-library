## 11. Reunion at Gunpoint

### Casimir

The rain had stopped being weather and become a condition of existence.

Casimir Vane crouched at the edge of the flooded ruins—what the old charts called the Mission of Saint Ambrose, though nothing saintly remained of it except the bell tower, leaning like a drunk against the sky—and watched the water for signs of her. Three days since she'd walked out into the storm. Three days of tracking a woman who did not want to be tracked, which was rather like trying to follow smoke through a keyhole.

He had learned her tells by now. The way she broke fern stems at shoulder height so she could find them again on her return route. The way she avoided high ground—too exposed—but also avoided the deepest channels, where a boat could run her down. She moved like someone who had spent ten years being nobody in particular in places nobody wanted to be, and Casimir, who had spent those same years being very particular somebodies in very desirable places, was humiliated to admit how long it had taken him to read that language fluently.

His employers had given him forty-eight hours to bring her in. *By any means*, the message had said, and the phrase had sat in his chest like a swallowed stone. He'd composed three replies. In the first he apologized. In the second he negotiated. In the third—the one he sent—he told them precisely what they could do with their commission, in language polished enough that they would understand it was not rage but decision.

The reply came back in eleven minutes: his retainer frozen, his extraction contract voided, and a warning that the firm's relationship with him would be "reassessed." Somewhere in a glass office in a city that smelled of money instead of rot, a man he'd never met crossed Casimir Vane off a list. It should have felt like dying. Instead, sitting in a drowned church with leeches in his boots and no income and no plan, it felt like the first honest breath he'd taken in years.

Which left him with one asset: Edmund Dravenmoor's trail, and the fact that Marek—whoever Marek truly was—was also on it.

Casimir had found the photograph first. It lay in the mud near the collapsed refectory, half-sunk, its edges blooming with mold: Aldous Dravenmoor, younger than the famous portraits, holding something that caught the light wrong—not reflecting it but seeming to drink it. A crown, or a circlet, or a ring of metal that hurt to look at directly even in a photograph. On the back, in a hand Casimir now recognized from the journals: *M. — you are not ready. No one is. —A.D.*

Marek had been here. Marek had dropped it running—or fleeing—or hunting. And Edmund had gone on alone toward coordinates only she carried, straight into the arms of whatever her father's most trusted student had become.

So Casimir did the arithmetic he'd been avoiding. If he found her first, he could warn her. If Marek found her first—

He didn't finish the thought. He simply started moving, and kept moving, through water that rose past his knees and then his waist, through corridors of drowned forest where the trees stood like drowned congregations, following broken ferns and the small deliberate signatures of a woman who had taught him, without meaning to, how to be honest with the wilderness if with no one else.

He heard the water go wrong before he saw why.

There is a sound a river makes when it takes something, and there is a sound it makes when it is about to, and the second sound is worse—a kind of gathering, a held breath in the current. Casimir froze on the lip of a submerged levee, and thirty meters ahead, where the flood funneled between two ruined walls into a churning throat of white water, he saw her.

Edmund Dravenmoor was in the water.

Not swimming—swimming was controlled, swimming was choice. She was being *handled*, turned over and over like laundry in a machine, one arm hooked desperately around a piece of timber that was itself being dragged toward the gap between the walls. Her pack was gone. Her rifle was gone. Everything she owned in the world was gone or going, and the river was patient, and the river always won.

Later, Casimir would try to reconstruct what he'd been thinking in that moment and fail. There was no calculation. That was the strange part—the man who had built a career on calculation, who weighed every charm and every lie on jeweler's scales, went into that water with nothing decided at all.

He dove from the levee.

The flood took him the way it took everything, completely and without malice. Cold like a fist. Silt in his eyes, his mouth, the fine machinery of his ears. He surfaced once, saw the timber—saw her, white-knuckled, hair streaming like weed—and got a boot on a submerged beam and pushed, and the current rewarded the effort by nearly tearing his leg off. Then he was beside her, and her eyes found his, and even half-drowned she managed an expression that said, with perfect clarity: *you have got to be joking.*

"Take my hand," he shouted, and the river ate the words.

She didn't take his hand. Of course she didn't. She took his *forearm*—the grip of someone who knew hands slip—and hauled herself onto the timber, dragging him half up with her, and together they rode the log down the throat of the rapids while the two ruined walls scraped past close enough to touch, stone screaming against wood, the whole world narrowed to noise and cold and the animal fact of another person's weight against yours.

Then the channel widened. The water gentled as suddenly as it had raged, spilling them out into a flooded grove where the current wandered lazy among the trunks, and the timber ground to a stop against a root mass, and there was silence except for rain on leaves and their own brutal breathing.

Edmund rolled off the log into shallow water and lay there on her back, chest heaving, staring at the gray ceiling of sky.

"You," she said finally, "are the single most persistent problem I have ever encountered."

"I've been called worse."

"By whom?"

"My employers. Former employers." He coughed out a lungful of delta. "As of four days ago."

### Edmund

She should have walked away. She knew this with total certainty, lying in the shallows with her muscles shaking and her gear at the bottom of a channel, watching Casimir Vane wring out his ridiculous ruined jacket with the air of a man whose tailor had betrayed him personally.

She should have walked away because he had lied to her from the first hour at the levee. Because his contract had never been about the missing team. Because she was, as far as she could tell, the merchandise—sole blood heir of Aldous Dravenmoor, located and retrieved, a parcel with a pulse.

And yet.

He had come off that levee without hesitating. She had seen his face in the half-second before the water took them both, and there had been nothing on it—no calculus, no performance. Just a man diving. Ten years of reading people in bad places had taught her that the unguarded half-second was the only honest document anyone ever produced, and Casimir's half-second had been clean.

That didn't mean she forgave him. Forgiveness was a currency she'd stopped minting years ago. But trust, battered trust, the ugly load-bearing kind—that might be rebuilt on different foundations than forgiveness. Foundations like: *he dove anyway.*

They made camp on the second floor of a half-collapsed granary, above the waterline, with a roof that leaked in only four places. Edmund inventoried her losses—pack, rifle, food, the waterproof roll containing her father's coordinates, all of it gone, all of it *in her head*, which was the only place that mattered—and Casimir, without being asked, laid out what remained of his own supplies on a tarp between them. Two tins. Half a bag of rice. A flare pistol. A dry shirt, which he wordlessly passed across to her, because hers was torn to ribbons and she was shivering in a way she couldn't control.

She took the shirt. She didn't thank him. They both understood the transaction perfectly.

"All right," she said, when her hands had steadied enough to hold a tin cup of boiled rainwater. "Talk. All of it. Not the version you rehearsed on the boat."

Casimir looked at the fire for a long moment. Rain ticked on the roof. Somewhere below, the flood moved through the granary's drowned floor with a sound like slow applause.

"My employers," he said, "are a consortium. Old money, new money, money that doesn't like its name written anywhere. They fund expeditions, buy artifacts, occasionally buy governments when the price of artifacts goes up." He turned the cup in his hands. "Seven years ago, Aldous Dravenmoor died with his final dig unaccounted for. No findings filed. No artifacts recovered. The consortium has spent seven years and a great deal of money trying to work out why. Two years ago they hired me. My brief was narrow: locate Aldous Dravenmoor's sole surviving relative. Establish contact. Assess whether she knew anything about the final dig."

"And if she did?"

"Report back. Gently. Build trust." His mouth twisted. "I'm told I'm good at building trust. It's apparently my principal marketable skill, which says something damning about the world."

"And when you found me, you reported that I knew nothing."

"You told me yourself you'd spent a decade erasing him. I believed you." He looked up. "I want that on the record, Edmund—I believed you, and I reported exactly that, and they didn't believe *me*. Because they'd found the journals by then. Aldous's last journals. And the journals mention a relic he called the Meridian. 'A crown for whoever holds it.' And they stopped caring what you knew and started caring what you *were*."

"The heir."

"The heir. Bloodline matters to people like that. Superstition dressed as due diligence." He set the cup down. "Three days ago they ordered me to bring you in. By any means. I declined. They terminated my contract, froze my accounts, and informed me that I am now, professionally speaking, dead." He smiled, and for once there was no charm in it at all—it was a tired, real expression, and it looked strange and young on his face. "I'd like to report that I agonized. I didn't. I read their message standing in the rain outside a mission station, and I thought: they will send someone else. Someone who follows orders. And she's out here alone with Marek somewhere in the dark. And I found I cared about that more than I cared about the career. Which was, frankly, an alarming discovery. I don't recommend it."

Edmund listened to all of it without moving. The rain filled the pauses. She was aware, distantly, of how easy it would be to believe him—his voice was built for belief the way some men's fists were built for violence—and how that ease was exactly why she wouldn't surrender to it wholesale. But she tested each statement against what she'd seen: the flash of the contract at the levee, too eager; the smooth lies about small things; the moment in the mission station when he'd almost told her something and swerved. It all fit. It fit the way a key fits a lock it was cut for—which proved either honesty or craftsmanship.

"Why dive?" she asked. It was the question underneath all the others. "You could have followed me. Warned me from the bank. You went into that water knowing you might die for a woman who told you to go to hell."

Casimir considered the question with what looked like genuine effort.

"Because I've spent my whole life being liked," he said, "and I realized somewhere in the last week that I've never once been *trusted*, and that the difference between the two is the entire difference between a life and a performance. And you—" he gestured at her, at the ruined shirt, the steady eyes, "—you're the least performative person I've ever met. You're the realest thing in this whole drowned country. And I suppose some vain, stupid part of me wants to be real in the vicinity of you, even if you never forgive me. Especially if you never forgive me."

Silence. The rain thickened, thinned. Edmund drank her water and thought about her father, who had also been charming, who had also been believed too easily, who had died chasing a crown because power had offered itself as identity and he had accepted the terms.

"I don't forgive you," she said at last.

"I know."

"But Marek has the journals' context, probably has the dig's location, definitely has eight missing people or their corpses behind him. And I have no gear, no rifle, and coordinates in my head. So here's what we are, Casimir: partners of necessity. Not friends. Not yet, maybe not ever. We tell each other the truth from this hour forward—all of it, the ugly parts especially—and we assume nothing else. Can you live inside those terms?"

Something shifted in his face—relief, and under the relief, a kind of gravity, as if she'd handed him a weight he'd been asking to carry.

"Yes," he said. "For the first time in my life, yes."

"Then start now. What aren't you telling me?"

He didn't flinch. She gave him credit for that.

"Marek," he said quietly. "I've read the journals, Edmund. All of them, the ones the consortium recovered. Your father wasn't just afraid of the Meridian at the end. He was afraid of *Marek*—of what Marek was becoming. The last entries describe a student who'd begun to speak about the artifact the way converts speak about God. And your father wrote one sentence I haven't been able to shake." Casimir's voice dropped, and the rain seemed to lean in to hear it. "*If I fail, bury me at the deep channel, and bury it with me—for it does not sleep, it waits.*"

Edmund stared at him. The words landed in her chest like stones dropped down a well, striking something deep and cold that she recognized: the coordinates in her head, the ones she'd memorized at fifteen from a letter she never answered. The deep channel. Her father's marked grave.

"That's where I'm going," she said. "That's where I've been going since the burn site."

Casimir nodded slowly, and outside the storm gathered itself for another night, and far downstream—though neither of them knew it yet—Marek was already preaching to the willing.
