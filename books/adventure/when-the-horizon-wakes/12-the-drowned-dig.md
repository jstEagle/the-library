## 12. The Drowned Dig

They came at dawn, when the river wore its grayest skin and the mist stood waist-high over the water like something waiting to be introduced.

Edmund had not slept. She had sat in the stern of the leaking boat all night with her father's coordinates burning in her head, watching the channel narrow and the trees lean closer, and when Casimir finally stirred from his nest of damp canvas and asked if she wanted him to take the pole, she said no, and he had the sense not to ask twice.

The delta had changed overnight. The monsoon's first full fury had passed, leaving behind a landscape rearranged—new sandbars where channels had been, channels where forest had been, whole islands of torn-up reeds drifting downstream like funeral barges. The water was the color of steeped tea, opaque, patient. Somewhere beneath it lay the thing she had spent ten years not thinking about.

"Close?" Casimir asked. He was standing in the bow, one hand on the gunwale, and despite everything—the lies, the rain, the week of shared silence that was somehow more honest than their conversations—he had learned to read her face well enough to know when to be quiet and when not to be.

"Half a kilometer. Maybe less." She adjusted the pole. "My father's notes put the dig on a limestone shelf above the old channel. The survey maps from the nineties show a ridge there. If the flooding hasn't taken the whole shelf—"

"It will be there," Casimir said. "It's been waiting seven years. It can wait another hour."

She shot him a look. He smiled his small, careful smile, the one that no longer tried to charm her and had become, without her noticing, something closer to companionship.

The trees opened.

* * *

She had imagined it a hundred times over the years, usually at three in the morning, usually against her will. She had imagined a pit, a spoil heap, the orderly geometry of an excavation abandoned mid-breath. What she found instead was a drowned cathedral.

The ridge was there—barely—a spine of pale limestone rising perhaps two meters above the floodline, furred with new moss, stitched together by roots that had moved in like squatters. And built into the ridge, half-swallowed, was the dig.

Aldous Dravenmoor had not worked small. The excavation was a terraced cut into the hillside, three levels deep, each terrace shored with timber and stone that had gone gray and soft with seven years of wet seasons. A crane tower still stood at the top, listing like a drunk against a guy-wire that had rotted to a thread. Tarps hung in ribbons from a support frame, snapping gently in the breeze, the sound carrying across the water—*whup, whup, whup*—like something trying to remember how to fly.

And everywhere, the water. It had taken the lowest terrace entirely. The middle terrace was a shallow lagoon, and through its tea-colored skin Edmund could see the shapes of things below: the corner of a worktable, a row of sample trays fanned out on the silt like a dropped hand of cards, a wheelbarrow lying on its side with one wheel still slowly turning in some current she couldn't feel.

"God," Casimir said quietly. He had gone still in the way he did when he was genuinely moved and didn't want anyone to know it. "He built all this?"

"He built all of this." Edmund brought the boat alongside the middle terrace and made fast to a stone anchor point that her father's crew had cut into the bedrock—cut, she noted, with a precision that made her chest ache. Even his anchor points were beautiful. "He had forty people here at the height of it. Funding from three governments and a man in Zurich whose name he never said aloud. He told me once that this was the site he'd been walking toward his whole life. Every other dig was practice."

"And you never came."

"I never came." She stepped onto the terrace, and the stone took her weight with a sound like a held breath. "He asked. The letter came the winter before he died. He said—" She stopped. The words were still in her, sealed under ten years of scar tissue, and she found she could not yet open them. "He asked. I said no."

Casimir said nothing. He handed her the pack instead, and that was its own kind of answer.

* * *

The upper terrace was dry enough to walk, and it was there that the dig's last days began to speak.

Edmund moved through it the way she moved through any site—slowly, reading. It was a skill her father had taught her before she was old enough to resent him for it: *the ground keeps its receipts, Edmund. People lie, weather lies, the dead lie best of all, but the ground only ever tells you what happened.* She had spent a decade applying that lesson to rivers and sandbars, to the anonymous wilderness, because applying it to her father had become unbearable. Now she had no choice.

The camp had not been abandoned in panic. That was the first thing. Cooking gear was stacked and lashed under a lean-to. Tools were racked. Someone had oiled the crane's winch mechanism—she touched the gears and her fingers came away with the ghost of grease, degraded but present. This was a crew that expected to come back, or that had left in good order, or both.

But then she reached the survey tent, and the order broke.

The tent had been the site office—she knew it instantly, the folding table, the lamp, the map chest. And someone had torn through it. Not looters; looters take. This had been a search. Papers lay everywhere in drifts, water-ruined, ink blooming into gray flowers. The map chest stood open and empty. The lamp had been knocked over and had scorched a black fan across the table's surface before it died.

And on the table, weighted down with a stone, one page had been left deliberately. Protected.

Edmund lifted the stone and read.

It was a letter, or the draft of one, in her father's handwriting—the strong, slanted script she had spent her childhood watching across dinner tables in six countries. The water had taken the top third. What remained read like scripture read through a keyhole:

*—cannot be studied. I was wrong to think it could. It studies us. Every man who has handled it in the last month has changed, and they do not see it in themselves, and when I show them what they've become they call me the one who is lost. I am sealing the lower vault. Those who agree, meet me at the—*

The rest was gone.

Edmund stood very still, the paper trembling in a wind she didn't feel.

Casimir came to stand beside her, read over her shoulder, and for once had nothing charming to say. When he finally spoke, his voice was low. "The crew split."

"The crew split." She set the page down with hands that were not quite steady. "Look at the burn scar on the table. Look at the papers—they went through everything looking for the vault records, the location, the access sequence. One faction wanted what was down there. The other—" she gestured at the letter, at the stone that had been placed on it like a headstone, "—the other wanted it gone. And my father was the one who wanted it gone. Too late."

"Too late how?"

She turned and looked down at the drowned middle terrace, at the water lying over the worktables like a sheet over a face. "Because by the time he wrote that letter, he'd already spent months handling it. Studying it. Showing it to them. You don't talk a crew out of a thing after you've taught them to want it, Casimir. He must have known that. He must have stood right here and watched his own people look at him like he was the obstacle." Her throat tightened. "He spent thirty years digging things up and telling the world that knowledge was a duty. And then he found one thing he couldn't un-dig, and he discovered his life's philosophy had a floor, and the floor was rotten."

* * *

They worked the upper terrace through the morning, and the dig told them its story the way a dying man tells his—out of order, in fragments, with terrible clarity.

In the mess shelter, Edmund found the evidence of the fracture written in something more honest than paper: the seating. Camps have geographies, and this one had a border down the middle of the long table. On one side, the dishes and cups were arranged with a particular neatness—her father's people, the ones who kept order because order was how they held fear at arm's length. On the other side, the cups had been left wherever they'd landed, and someone had carved a single mark into the tabletop, over and over, dozens of times, until the wood was a pulp of small identical cuts.

A circle with a line through it.

She had seen that mark before. Not in registries, not in any government archive—Casimir had checked them all, she knew, because she'd made him. She had seen it in her memory, in the margins of her father's notebooks from his final year, sketched obsessively in a hand that grew less steady with every page. The mark that had been on the stone marker at the burned camp. The mark of the thing below.

"They were already wearing it," she said. She was crouched over the table, her fingertips resting beside the carved cuts without touching them. "The ones who stayed. They marked their own camp. Their own table. You don't do that for an employer. You do that for a—"

"Faith," Casimir finished.

She looked at him. He had gone pale, and she realized he was thinking of the vanished government team, of the eight who had walked calmly into the water with their packs on their backs. Of Marek. Neither of them said the name. It sat between them like a third person.

* * *

The lower terrace could only be reached at the water's edge, and only partway. Edmund waded in to her thighs, Casimir a step behind her with a line tied around her waist—her rule, not his, and he had obeyed it without argument, which was its own quiet evidence of how far they had come.

The flooded gallery had been the access corridor to the vault. She could see its mouth at the far end: a doorway of fitted stone, elegant, ancient, far older than the dig around it—her father had found this place, not made it. The water lapped at the top of the lintel. Below the surface, the corridor descended into a darkness that even the tea-colored flood could not soften.

On the corridor walls, above the waterline, her father's crew had left their record.

Not official notes. Not stratigraphy. Words, cut and painted and scratched in a dozen hands: *SEAL IT. IT WAKES. WE WERE WARNED.* And on the opposite wall, lower, in a different hand, angrier: *IT IS NOT YOURS TO BURY. HE IS NOT YOURS TO FOLLOW.* And beneath that, the circle-and-line, again and again, the paint still faintly, impossibly red.

Edmund stood chest-deep in her father's last battlefield and read the war written on its walls.

"Two crews," she said. Her voice echoed down the drowned corridor and came back changed. "One dig. He built this with forty people and it broke into two churches." She turned in the water, slowly. "And he lost. Look at it. His faction wrote their warnings and then they left, or they died, or they—" She stopped. She was looking at a section of wall near the vault door where the stone had been freshly broken, within the last few weeks, the fracture faces still pale. Someone had recently widened the doorway. Someone had been here.

Casimir saw it at the same moment. His hand found her shoulder. "Recent," he said. "Weeks. Maybe days."

"Marek."

"Or his people. Preparing the way." Casimir's jaw worked. "Edmund, if they've already opened the outer corridor—"

"Then the vault is close to open, and my father's last act is about to be undone." She waded back toward the terrace, water streaming off her, and stood dripping on the stone her father had cut, and felt something in her finally come to rest.

All her life she had carried two fathers: the legend, the great man on the conference stage, and the monster, the one who had chosen a hole in the ground over his daughter. She had hated one and mourned the other and never once, in ten years, thought to look for the third man—the tired one, the wrong one, the one who had been so certain of his own wisdom that he'd opened the door himself, and then stood in the doorway begging his own students to stop walking through it, and watched them go, and known it was his fault.

Not a legend. Not a monster. A man who failed, in the specific and ordinary way that brilliant men fail: too late, and too proud to say so while anyone was left to hear it.

"I understand you now," she said quietly, to the drowned dig, to the man who wasn't in it. "God help me, I understand you."

Casimir, standing behind her, said nothing at all. Which was when she heard it—faint, rising from the dark mouth of the corridor, from the vault beyond the flood:

A hum. Low, sustained, wrong. Not water. Not wind.

The sound of something that had been asleep for seven years, and had just been reminded it could wake.
