## 13. Inheritance of Rot

Casimir had read a great many documents in his life—dossiers, ledgers, love letters intercepted for money—and he liked to believe he could weigh a man's soul by his handwriting. Aldous Dravenmoor's journals destroyed that vanity in the first hour.

The notebooks were wrapped in oilcloth inside Edmund's dry box, and she had handed them over without ceremony that morning, after the dig's outer chambers gave up their secrets to the rising water. "You wanted the truth," she'd said. "Read it yourself. I've read enough for one lifetime." Then she had gone out onto the flooded gallery with her rifle and her silence, standing watch against a threat neither of them could yet name, and Casimir had been left alone in the half-drowned survey room with seven years of a dead man's hand.

The water had taken its toll. Whole months were lost to rot, pages fused into brick, ink bloomed into grey flowers. But enough survived. Enough survived to be damning.

* * *

The early journals were almost painful to read. Here was Aldous Dravenmoor at forty-five, writing in a script so precise it looked engraved: field notes on sediment layers, sketches of potsherds, small wry observations about his cook's war with the local cats. A man in love with the world's patience. Casimir found himself lingering over entries that had nothing to do with anything—a page devoted entirely to the way morning fog moved across a river valley, described with the tenderness other men reserved for mistresses.

*Fog is just the river dreaming aloud,* Aldous had written. *I intend to be awake for it.*

Casimir turned the page and felt something tighten behind his ribs. He had been hired to find this man's heir, not to mourn him, and yet here he was, three hours into a dead stranger's soul, grieving.

Then the handwriting began to change.

It was gradual—the way rot is gradual, invisible day to day, undeniable across a season. The engraved precision loosened. The margins filled. The entries grew longer and stranger, and the dates between them compressed, as if the man writing could no longer bear to put the pen down.

The Meridian entered the record in the spring of the final year.

*Found it,* the entry began, and then nothing else for two pages except a sketch: a circlet, or a crown, drawn in obsessive cross-hatching until the paper shone silver with graphite. *Or it found us. I no longer pretend there is a difference. It was buried facing down, which no burial custom on this continent explains, unless whoever left it wanted the earth itself to hold it shut.*

Casimir sat back. The ruined mission station dripped around him. Somewhere beyond the broken wall, Edmund moved along her watch circuit, boots whispering on wet stone, and he found himself listening for her footsteps the way a drowning man listens for a rope.

He read on.

*Vasquez held it for perhaps ninety seconds—just long enough to photograph it—and by evening he was arguing that we should abandon the permits, falsify the export papers, take it north ourselves. Vasquez. Twenty-two years with the institute. He wept when I refused him, and then he stopped weeping mid-sob, like a lamp going out, and said, "You'll understand soon," and smiled at me. I have known that man since before my daughter was born. He has never smiled at me since.*

Edmund's mother's name was in some of these pages—briefly, tenderly, before the entries turned inward. And Edmund herself appeared once, in an entry dated eight months before the end:

*E. writes. She wants nothing from me but honesty, which is the one thing I can no longer give her, because I no longer know what the honest version of my own motives is. The Meridian does not lie to you. That is its genius. It simply makes every lie you tell yourself sound like scripture.*

Casimir set the journal down on the survey table, next to a brass theodolite furred green with age, and pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes.

He thought about his employers.

He thought about the briefing room in the city, the polished table, the man called Corvane who had slid the contract across it with two fingers, the way Corvane had said *retrieval*, not *study*. He had assumed at the time it was the usual euphemism—the language institutions used to keep their hands clean. Institutions always wanted things studied. Catalogued. Locked in climate-controlled rooms where rich men could visit them and feel the particular thrill of owning what others feared.

But now, with the dead man's warnings spread out before him in his own deteriorating hand, Casimir did the arithmetic he had been refusing to do since the delta swallowed the government team.

Corvane's organization had funded three expeditions into this region in five years. Two had failed quietly; the third had vanished loudly. They had sent Casimir—not an archaeologist, not a scholar, but a finder of people—to locate not the artifact, whose location they could only guess at, but the blood heir of the man who had buried it. Why? Bloodlines meant nothing to science. Bloodlines mattered to inheritance. To claim. To *right*.

And there was the detail he had filed away and tried to forget: the second contract rider, the one he'd signed without reading closely because the money was obscene. Clause nine. *In the event the asset cannot be secured intact, the bearer shall be compensated for personal custody and transport.* Personal custody. They had written him a blank check to become what Aldous Dravenmoor had died resisting.

They didn't want to study the Meridian.

They wanted to wear it.

* * *

He kept reading because stopping felt more dangerous than continuing. The rain had settled into its monsoon rhythm against the mission roof—a long metallic drumming, patient as siege artillery—and the light through the shattered clerestory windows went from grey to the color of old pewter.

The middle journals documented the fracture of the expedition. Aldous's crew of eleven had split within six weeks of the find, exactly as the entry about Vasquez predicted. One faction wanted the thing sold; one wanted it studied under guard; and a third—smaller, quieter, growing—wanted it *used*. Their arguments were recorded in Aldous's hand with mounting horror, because he had begun transcribing them verbatim, as evidence, and the evidence was turning on him:

*I asked Halloway—he is twenty-six, decent, plays chess badly and honestly—why he had changed his position. He said, "I haven't changed. I've just stopped pretending the world doesn't need fixing." Then he listed, calmly, the number of people who die daily of preventable causes, and said the arithmetic was simple, and that my sentimentality was a luxury purchased by the suffering of strangers. He was not angry. That is what I cannot stop writing down. He was not angry. He was kind about it. He pitied me.*

Casimir's mouth had gone dry somewhere around the fourth such entry. He poured water from the dented canteen and drank without tasting it, and outside, thunder rolled up the valley like furniture being dragged across heaven.

Because he recognized the rhetoric. Not the specifics—the shape. He had heard that same serene arithmetic in boardrooms, in ministries, in the mouths of men who signed off on disappearances and called it stabilization. He had always assumed such men were made by ambition, by institutional gravity, by the slow accretion of compromises. It had never occurred to him that a *thing* could teach it. That corruption might have a curriculum.

*A crown for whoever holds it,* the journals had called the Meridian, in Aldous's earliest note. Casimir had read that phrase yesterday and found it merely grandiose. Now it read like a diagnosis. A crown did not make a king. It revealed one—or manufactured one, faster than any coronation, out of whatever raw material of grievance and certainty a man already carried.

Everyone has grievances. Everyone is certain of something. That was the horror of it, Casimir realized, staring at the rain-blurred wall. The Meridian didn't need monsters. It needed only people. People with a cause, a wound, a plan. Give it ninety seconds in a man's hands and his best qualities would do all the work—his compassion would become condescension, his conviction would become crusade, his intelligence would become the sharpest tool in the armory of his own self-justification.

*It never argues with you,* Aldous wrote, near the end. *That is how you know it isn't reasoning. Reasoning can lose.*

* * *

The last intact notebook was the thinnest, bound in cracked black leather, and Aldous had written in it during the final month, when the dig was failing and the crew was gone and the rains were coming. His handwriting had collapsed into a scrawl that slanted downhill across the page, as though the words themselves were being pulled toward some lower ground.

The ninth man filled these pages.

Marek. The name arrived without fanfare, in an inventory of supplies, and then again in a supply ledger margin: *M. took the west cache without asking. Not like him.* And then, at last, whole entries, and Casimir understood that Aldous had spent his final weeks watching his most trusted student with the helpless attention of a physician watching a beloved patient decline.

*Marek was the finest mind I ever taught. I say "was" and I hate myself for it. Yesterday he stood at the edge of the inner shaft for four hours without moving. When I finally spoke to him he said, "It's been talking to me, Professor." I laughed—I am ashamed of that laugh—and he didn't. He said, "Not voices. Justifications. It shows me why everything I've ever wanted to do was right all along. Do you know what a gift that is? Do you know how much of my life I've wasted on doubt?"*

*He has stopped eating with the others. He has started holding services—I don't know what else to call them—at dusk, by the shaft. Three of the crew attend now. They come back calm. Calmer than any of us have been in months. That calm is spreading faster than fear ever could.*

*I buried my best student alive today, in the only way left to me. I told him the inner vault is sealed and the mechanism destroyed. He thanked me. He said, "Then we'll wait. Water finds every door eventually." And he walked out into the rain, and two more followed him, and I sat down here to write the truth where someone might someday read it:*

*The Meridian does not corrupt the weak. There are no weak men left in this camp. It corrupts the sincere. It takes the purest engine in a man—his belief that he is right—and removes the governor. Everything after that is just velocity.*

*I have one option remaining and it is the grave. If I fail, let the water finish what I start. Let no one dig here. Let no one wear what I could not bear to hold.*

*If my daughter ever reads this—*

The entry ended there. The next page was water-ruined, and the one after, and then the notebook simply stopped, mid-life, the way a clock stops.

Casimir closed it very gently, as if the dead man might feel the slam.

* * *

He sat for a long time in the drumming dark while the implications arranged themselves with terrible patience, like soldiers forming ranks.

Clause nine. Personal custody. His employers had read these journals—or ones like them, recovered from the earlier expeditions—and they had read them not as warnings but as instructions. *A crown for whoever holds it.* Corvane's people believed the warning was a spec sheet. Every atrocity Aldous documented, every serene justification, every convert—Casimir could see it now through their eyes, laid out like a demonstration of capability. The vanished government team hadn't been a tragedy to them. It had been a field trial. Eight names, and somewhere out in the drowned forest, at least one survivor walking calmly, converted, waiting.

And Marek. Marek, whom Aldous had loved beyond all the others, who had received the full voltage of the thing and organized it into gospel. If Marek lived—if the ninth man had survived whatever happened at the burn site, had shepherded his flock ahead into the green—then he wasn't hiding from Casimir's employers.

He was their proof of concept. Possibly their partner. Possibly, in the arithmetic of men who wanted to wear crowns, their *product*.

Casimir looked down at his own hands, elegant, uncalloused, hands that had forged letters and lifted wallets and never once, in thirty-eight years, held anything he couldn't put down. He thought about clause nine and realized he had already decided, sometime in the last hour without noticing the moment of decision, that he would tear it out of the contract and feed it to the river. The question that remained was colder: whether his employers had sent anyone else besides him. Whether *he* had been the polite option, and the impolite one was already in country.

The rain thickened. Through the broken window he watched the flooded gallery below the mission, black water moving between drowned columns, and the horizon beyond it had no edge at all—just rain becoming river becoming sky, the whole world one seamless grey throat.

Then he saw the light.

Far out, past the tree line, where the drowned dig lay beneath its skin of water: a single point of orange, steady, deliberate. Not lightning. Lightning didn't hold still. A lantern, or a torch, planted at the edge of the ruin like a candle at a shrine.

And faintly, across the water, threaded thin by rain and distance, came something worse than a shout. Singing. Many voices, unhurried, in unison, singing a tune Casimir didn't know—and underneath his fear he registered the quality of it, the terrible calm of it, and understood that Aldous Dravenmoor had described exactly this in his final pages:

*They come back calm. Calmer than any of us have been in months.*

Casimir was moving before he finished the thought, journals under his arm, boots splashing through the flooded corridor, calling Edmund's name into the rain.
