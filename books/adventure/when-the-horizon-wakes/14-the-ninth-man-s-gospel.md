## 14. The Ninth Man's Gospel

The dig announced itself before we saw it.

We came up the last channel in the grey hour before dawn, and the water changed—went glassy and still in a way that no flooded forest has any right to be, as if something beneath it had smoothed the surface with a hand. The trees stood ankle-deep and perfectly reflected, so that we seemed to move through a country doubled on itself, above and below identical, and I could not have said which world was the true one. Casimir stopped bailing. Even the leak in the boat's belly seemed to hold its breath.

"There," he said softly.

The excavation rose out of the flood like the ribcage of something enormous that had lain down to die and been swallowed standing up. My father had built his last camp on a natural rise—a terp of compacted earth older than any map—and the monsoon had turned it into an island. Scaffolding of blackened timber still clung to the slope where the trench cut down through three strata of mud. Tarps hung in rags from poles, snapping in no wind I could feel. And everywhere, everywhere, the green: vines already threading through the shoring beams, ferns crowding the ladder rungs, the forest moving in to reclaim what Aldous Dravenmoor had torn open seven years ago and never lived to close.

I had carried these coordinates in my head for nine years. I had told myself they were just numbers, dead weight, a souvenir of a man I'd stopped loving long before he died. Standing there, watching the light come up over the drowned trench, I understood that I had been lying to myself the way my father lied to himself at the end: fluently, and with great conviction.

"We should circle the island first," Casimir said. "Read it before we land."

It was good fieldcraft, and it was too late. Because as we slid along the eastern shore, past a collapsed drying rack furred white with mold, someone on the rise above us struck a match.

The small flame bloomed in the half-dark, cupped in two hands, and by its light I saw a face looking down at us—not startled, not wary, but *patient*, the way a man is patient with a train he knew would arrive on schedule. He didn't call out. He didn't raise any weapon. He simply watched us pass, smoking, and when I twisted around in the boat to keep him in view, he lifted the cigarette in a small salute.

"Edmund," Casimir breathed. "Oars. Now."

But the current had opinions, and so did the man on the bank. By the time we fought our way back to the landing—the same rotted jetty my father's crew must have used, its pilings soft as cheese—he was waiting for us there, standing at the end of the planks with his hands loose at his sides. Behind him, out of the treeline, came four others. Silent. Unhurried. Their clothes were government issue gone native: survey-team khaki patched with bark fiber, boots wrapped in oiled cloth. The vanished team. Or what remained of them.

I counted. Four. Plus the man at the jetty's end. Five of eight accounted for, alive and walking, which was three more than any sane accounting of that sandbar had allowed.

"Dravenmoor," the man said.

Not *Ms. Dravenmoor*. Not *the daughter*. Just the name, spoken the way you'd speak the name of a river or a storm—as though I were a feature of the landscape he'd been expecting to find here all along.

"Marek," I said.

Something moved behind his eyes—pleasure, I think, that I knew him. In the photograph that had fallen from his coat in the flooded ruins, he had been younger, standing at my father's shoulder in front of a trench wall, both of them squinting into sun. Aldous's hand rested on the young man's arm in the proprietary way he touched things he believed were his. Marek himself had aged strangely since then—not old, exactly, but *smoothed*, as if the ordinary friction of living had been sanded off him. His face was the face of a man who had stopped asking questions and liked the silence that followed.

"You've read my file," he said, smiling faintly. "Of course you have. Your father kept files on everyone. It's how he loved people." He stepped aside from the jetty's end—an invitation, not a concession. "Come up out of the water. You're bleeding your boat dry, and the rain will start again within the hour. There's tea. Real tea; Tomas found a case of it in the mission stores before the flood took the mission. You'll want to be warm for what comes next."

Casimir's hand had found mine in the bottom of the boat—not romantic, tactical, pressing the little knife he always carried into my palm—and I closed my fingers around it and did the arithmetic I hated: five against two, high ground against low, and nowhere left to run that wasn't drowning. The monsoon had closed every door behind us except this one.

We went up.

* * *

The camp inside the tree line was clean. That was the first thing that frightened me properly—not the armed men, not the isolation, but the *order* of it. Firewood stacked by length. Water boiling in dented pots. Sleeping rolls aired over branches, each one squared. Eight bedrolls, though only five bodies moved among them. In the center of the clearing, laid out on a tarp with ceremonial neatness, sat the vanished team's equipment: instruments, rifles, radios—all of it arranged with the edges aligned, the way you'd arrange objects in a shrine.

"They went ahead," Marek said, following my gaze. He said it gently, the way you'd correct a child's honest mistake. "You've been reading their trail backward. Everyone does. The sandbar, the burn site, the tracks ending mid-stride—you thought you were following a disappearance. You were following a *departure*."

"Eight people don't walk into a flooded delta carrying their own packs because they want to," I said.

"Eight people walked into that water calmly because for the first time in their lives they were certain." He crouched by the fire and poured tea into tin cups, unhurried, hostly. "Do you know what fear is, Dravenmoor? Fear is uncertainty wearing a body. Every one of those eight came up that river afraid—of the flood, of the dark, of going home to lives that hadn't worked out. And then they stood where you're about to stand, and the certainty came into them, and the fear went out, and they walked into the water smiling." He handed me a cup. His fingers were steady as a surgeon's. "I watched it happen eight times. I never get tired of watching it."

Casimir took his own cup without drinking. "And the ninth name," he said. "The one that was never on the manifest. That was you."

"That was me." Marek settled onto a crate across the fire from us, knees apart, hands on his thighs, a professor taking his chair. "Aldous recruited me off the manifest deliberately. He wanted one person outside the government's ledger. One person whose absence couldn't be traced." A flicker crossed his face—grief, maybe, or the memory of grief, worn smooth as river stone. "He trusted me more than he trusted anyone. More than he trusted you, Edmund. I'm sorry. I know how that lands. But you were the daughter, and daughters inherit; protégés *choose*. He used to say that. He said love is assigned and devotion is earned, and only one of them survives contact with the work."

"My father," I said, and my voice came out flatter than I intended, "died believing he had failed."

"He died believing he had run out of time." Marek leaned forward, firelight climbing his smooth face. "There's a difference. You'll understand it soon."

* * *

Here is what I will say for Marek Solen: he did not threaten us. Not once, not in all the hours we sat in that clearing while the rain came and went in gray curtains beyond the trees. He fed us. He answered questions. When Casimir tested the perimeter and found two of the converts standing watch with unsurfaced patience, Marek only laughed softly and said, "They won't stop you leaving. Where would you go?"—and he was right, and we all knew it, and that was the trap entire. No bars. Just the flood, and the season, and five hundred miles of drowned country between us and anywhere human.

So instead he talked. That was his weapon, and God help me, it was well made.

He told us about the Meridian the way other men talk about scripture they've memorized so thoroughly they've stopped noticing it's strange. He had been there, at the end, when my father's crew fractured. Twelve people at the final dig, he said, and within six weeks of opening the inner stratum, twelve people had become two camps. The ones who wanted it sold. The ones who wanted it studied. And my father, alone in a third place, wanting it buried.

"The journals lie," Marek said. "Yours will tell you he was fighting corruption. Read them again when you're calmer. He was fighting *temptation*, and losing slowly, and he knew it, and that's why he sent the crews away one by one—that's why the dig records are such a mess, why men vanish from the roster mid-season. He was amputating. Trying to save the body by cutting off everything the thing had touched." He shook his head with something like tenderness. "Including me, at the last. He put me on a boat and told me never to come back. So I waited seven years for him to die, and then I came back, and I found what he'd hidden, and I understood what he never let himself understand."

"And what's that?" Casimir asked. His voice was light, conversational, the voice he used for lying—but I had learned to hear the steel under it, and there was steel now.

Marek looked at him with mild pity. "That it isn't corrupting anyone. That's the story frightened men tell. Corruption implies the thing changes you, bends you against your nature. It doesn't. It *clarifies* you. It takes what you already are—the ambition, the grievance, the hunger you've spent your whole life dressing up as virtue—and it stops letting you pretend. Every man my father—" he caught himself, smiled at his own slip, did not correct it— "*every man Dravenmoor* sent away left angry, and every one of them left *honest*. For the first time. The Meridian doesn't whisper lies, Dravenmoor. It whispers your own voice, with the editing removed."

I thought of the vault, then—or rather I thought of what I imagined the vault would be, the dark below the trench, the thing my father had died refusing. And I thought of the way, on the boat, in the worst hours, I had caught myself composing arguments for keeping it. *I could fund a hundred surveys. I could make them remember her name.* Small justifications, reasonable ones, mine.

I drank my tea so I would have something to do with my hands.

"The team," I said. "The eight. What happened to them?"

"I told you. They went ahead." Marek set down his cup. "Down into the dig, to the threshold chamber, to prepare it. The Meridian is not inert, whatever your father's notes claim. It wakes. And waking is a process, and processes need attendance. They volunteered. All eight, freely, within a day of arriving." He spread his hands. "You reconstructed their last night on that sandbar. Tell me—did anything you read there look like coercion? Did you find a broken branch, a dragged heel, a single mark of struggle?"

I had found nothing. That had been the horror of it, the thing that had kept me awake in the mission station: eight sets of prints walking evenly into black water, packs on shoulders, stride unbroken. I had told myself it was staging. I had needed it to be staging.

"It looked like what it was," Marek said, reading my face. "Consent. Complete consent. That's what certainty looks like from the outside, Dravenmoor. It looks like walking away from everything you were afraid of, and never once looking back."

* * *

Later—much later, in the dead hour when the rain paused and even the jungle held its tongue—I lay on a bedroll between sleeping converts and stared up at the tarp roof and tried to locate, precisely, the moment Marek had stopped being a man and become a mouthpiece. I couldn't find it. That was the second horror, worse than the first. There was no seam. No scar where the corruption had entered. He spoke of my father with real love. He remembered the names of the eight and said them softly, like a litany. He had buried one of them—Kessler, the geologist—when Kessler's heart gave out in the third month, and he described the burial with tears standing in his eyes, unashamed, and I believed the tears.

Conviction, I thought, staring into the dark. We spend our lives taught to fear violence, and violence is nothing, violence is a blunt instrument that breaks against the first locked door. What you should fear is a man who has found an answer. Because he will not break against anything. He will simply stand there, gentle and certain, holding the door handle, explaining to you with perfect kindness that the door was never locked at all—you only believed it was, because you were still afraid.

Casimir's hand found my wrist in the dark. He didn't speak. Neither did I. Around us slept people who had walked into a flood smiling, and somewhere below us, down at the bottom of a trench my father had dug and damned, something waited that had eaten eight minds whole and left the bodies walking.

* * *

Morning came gray and dripping. Marek was waiting at the head of the trench stairs with the rain steaming off his shoulders, and he had brought a lantern, and he was smiling.

"You've decided," he said. It was not a question. "I can see it. You have her way of deciding—your father's, I mean. The jaw sets." He turned and started down the worn steps into the earth, speaking over his shoulder, casual as a man inviting guests into a cellar. "Come, then. Both of you. There's a door at the bottom that only opens for his blood, and I have carried these seven years knowing I could never open it alone."

Behind us, quietly, the converts closed ranks along the trench rim.

"And Dravenmoor," Marek added, pausing on the stairs, lantern-light swimming up across his smooth, patient face. "Bring nothing you intend to use. She knows when she's being approached by a liar. She's had practice."

Below us, at the bottom of the drowned stair, something hummed.
