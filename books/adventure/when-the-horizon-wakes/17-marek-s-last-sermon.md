## 17. Marek's Last Sermon

*Edmund*

The storm did not end. It only changed its mind about how it wanted to kill them.

For two days it had come down in sheets, in walls, in the long gray hammering that turned the delta from a place into a condition. Now, as they ran the lower channel with the Meridian lashed under the thwart and the boat taking water faster than Casimir could bail, the rain shifted to wind—hard lateral gusts that came off the flooded forest like thrown fists, each one shoving the hull sideways toward the snags.

Edmund steered by feel more than sight. The channel markers were gone, drowned or torn loose; the banks had dissolved into brown water threaded with the pale ghosts of submerged trees. She read the current the way her father had taught her to read strata—one layer of violence over another, and somewhere beneath both, the old riverbed holding its shape, patient as bone.

"Left," she shouted, though the word was mostly swallowed. "Hard left at the split."

Casimir didn't ask why. That was the thing about him she'd stopped fighting: he asked questions all day, endless, charming, infuriating questions—but when it mattered, when the water stood up around them, he simply did what she said and trusted her to be right. He shipped the bail bucket, threw his weight against the gunwale, and the boat swung wide of the snag field just as a dead kapok the size of a church steeple slid past on their starboard side, its roots combing the flood like fingers.

Behind them, the second boat came on.

She heard it before she saw it—an engine note too steady for this water, too confident. Marek had found an outboard somewhere; Marek had found fuel, men, purpose. Eight people had walked into the river carrying their own packs, and now at least four of them were walking back out of it, rowing behind a man who spoke about drowning the way missionaries spoke about salvation.

"There." Casimir pointed with the bucket.

Through a tear in the rain: a low gray shape riding the swell a quarter mile back, running the same channel, closing. Figures in it, still as posts. One of them raised an arm—not waving, Edmund realized. Pointing. At their boat. At the thing beneath their floorboards.

"They're not shooting," Casimir said. He sounded almost offended.

"He doesn't want holes in us. Or in it."

The gust hit then, a full-body blow that laid the rain flat and shoved the stern around. Edmund fought the tiller, arms burning, boots braced against a floor that sloshed above her ankles. The bilge pump had died outside the rapids. The caulking was going. She could feel the boat becoming less a vessel and more a suggestion, held together by habit and prayer.

"Casimir. If we take on much more—"

"I know."

"If we go down, we lose it to the river anyway. It would be simpler."

He looked at her across the chaos, hair plastered to his skull, and there was nothing charming in his face at all—just the raw arithmetic of a man doing sums he hated. "Simpler isn't the word I'd use."

Ahead, the channel bent hard around a drowned levee and dropped. She could hear the drop before she could see it—a long rolling thunder where the flood funneled through the old sluice cut, water stacking up in standing waves, white-capped even under the rain. In dry season it would have been a riffle. Now it was a throat.

"We can't portage," she said. "No bank. No time."

"So we run it."

"So we run it."

She glanced back once. The gray boat was closer now, close enough to see faces—hollowed, rain-scoured, calm. That was what made her stomach turn. Not weapons. Not rage. Calm. They looked like people attending a service, and the service was them.

One of them was singing. She couldn't hear it over the storm, but she could see the mouth moving, the head tipped back, and some animal part of her brain understood that whatever hymn it was, it wasn't for their benefit.

Marek stood at the bow.

Even at distance, even through rain, she knew him—the stillness of him, hands folded, coat streaming, upright in a boat that bucked like a live thing. A man standing in a storm the way other men stood in doorways. Her father had drawn that posture once, in a notebook sketch she'd spent seven years trying to forget: *my student, watching me work.* Beneath it Aldous had written a single line she'd never understood until this month: *He listens to things that are not speaking.*

"Hold on," Edmund said, and put the bow into the throat.

* * *

*Casmir*

The world became noise.

Casimir had been shot at twice in his career, robbed once, and lied to so often it had become a kind of weather to him. None of it prepared him for the sluice. The boat went weightless, then sideways, then down—a drop that punched the breath out of him and filled the hull to the thwarts in one green slap. The Meridian's lashings groaned. His shoulder met the gunwale hard enough to whiten his vision. And through all of it Edmund was steering, actually steering, drenched and roaring instructions he couldn't hear, reading water that had no right to be readable.

They came out the bottom alive.

He understood this only gradually, the way you understand you've survived a fall after your body has already landed. The channel widened. The standing waves fell apart into chop. The boat wallowed, half-swamped, and Edmund hauled the tiller over and ran them into the lee of the drowned levee, where the current slackened enough for bailing to mean something.

He bailed. His arms moved on their own while his mind replayed the last thirty seconds in fragments: the drop, the white, Edmund's voice cutting through everything like a blade through cloth.

Then the engine sound again.

It came around the bend above the sluice, and Casimir watched, bailing, as Marek's pilot made the decision Edmund hadn't been given time to make. The gray boat hit the throat at speed, bow-on, engine screaming—and the river simply took its receipt.

It happened fast and completely. A standing wave caught the transom and lifted it; the bow dug; the whole craft pivoted as if it had been seized by the collar. For one impossible moment it stood nearly vertical, figures clinging, Marek still upright somehow at the prow like a figurehead refusing to fall—and then the river folded it under and the engine died mid-scream and the flood closed over the whole argument.

Bodies surfaced. Casimir counted them automatically, the way he counted everything: four. Five. Five heads bobbing in the boil below the sluice, swept downstream in the general direction of nowhere good. The current here was gentler but not kind; it carried them past the levee, past their hiding spot, close enough that Casimir saw one of them look directly at him as he went by. The man didn't reach out. Didn't call for help. He smiled, water streaming off his face, and let himself be carried away.

"That's wrong," Casimir said. His voice came out strange. "That is profoundly—people don't do that."

Edmund said nothing. She was staring at the water below the falls, counting too, and he watched her count arrive at the same number his had.

Five.

Six had gone over.

"Marek," she said quietly.

The rain hammered. The levee dripped. Somewhere upstream, wreckage knocked against drowned trees with a soft wooden knocking, like a door being tried.

"He's a strong swimmer," Casimir offered, because someone had to say something and charm was useless and silence was worse. "Old expedition man. He grew up on rivers, if half his file—"

"You've read his file."

"I've read everyone's file. It's a disease." He wrung out the bail bucket, set it down, made himself stop moving. "Edmund. Listen to me. We have the relic. We have a boat that's dying. We have maybe six hours of daylight if the storm gives us any daylight at all, and a two-day run to the delta mouth. Whatever Marek is doing right now—swimming, sinking, preaching to fish—we cannot stand here and wait to find out."

She turned to look at him, and in her face he saw something that frightened him more than the falls had: not fear. Recognition. She was looking downstream the way you look at a house you grew up in.

"He'll surface," she said. "Men like him always surface. My father wrote that about him once—he called Marek 'the man who cannot drown because he has already given his body away.' I thought it was poetry." She wiped rain from her eyes with the back of her wrist. "It was a warning label."

* * *

*Edmund*

They ran the rest of the day and failed to outrun anything.

The boat leaked like a confession. By midafternoon Edmund had stripped her shirt to wrap the worst seam, and by late afternoon the wrapping was failing, and by the time the light went rotten-gray and began to fail altogether, they were running on a channel she half-remembered from a survey three years ago, toward a fisherman's stilt camp that might or might not still exist, with the Meridian humming under the floorboards like a struck bell that refused to finish ringing.

She could feel it. That was the part she hadn't told Casimir yet, and might never tell him: since the vault, since she'd carried it, she could feel the thing the way you feel a hand resting on the back of your neck. Not gripping. Resting. Patient. All day, as they ran, it had been offering things—little mercies, little arithmetic. *If you kept the boat, you could survey every lost channel in the delta. If you kept it, no one would ever vanish again without you knowing where. If you kept it, your father's death would finally mean something.*

She had begun, somewhere in the last hour, to answer it. Just to keep her sanity. Just to say no out loud inside her own skull, over and over, like a woman walking past a doorway she used to live in.

The stilt camp existed. Barely—two huts on leaning poles, a drying rack, a canoe missing. Whoever lived there had evacuated ahead of the monsoon, leaving a cold fire pit and a string of dried fish that Casimir declared, with the gravity of a sommelier, "edible, barely, and I've never loved anything more."

They dragged the boat onto the rack supports, lashed it, bailed it, and sat under the leaking thatch eating smoked fish while the dark came down and the river talked to itself in the reeds.

Edmund took inventory because inventory was safer than thinking.

The Meridian: secured, wrapped, present, *awake*. The boat: one day afloat, perhaps two if the river forgave them, which rivers did not. Food: the fish, some cassava flour gone damp, rainwater in plenty. Casimir: bruised at the shoulder, exhausted, watching her across the fire pit with an expression she was learning to translate—it meant he was about to say something honest, and honesty cost him visible effort.

"The employers," he said. "Before the storm cut the radio. There was a message waiting. Standard recall protocol—they're sending a recovery team to the delta mouth. Two boats, armed. They expect me to deliver you and the artifact to them there."

"And you told them?"

"I told them I'd be there." He fed the fire a stick. "I also told them the artifact was damaged in the vault collapse. Which is true, in the sense that it's currently sitting in a leaking boat wrapped in my spare shirt, which I'd call damage of a kind."

"Why lie to them? You've served them for years. Your whole polished, commissioned, boot-shined life."

He was quiet long enough that the rain filled the pause. When he answered, he didn't perform it. That was how she knew it was real.

"Because I watched five people float past me today smiling," he said. "And I watched you refuse to smile back. And I've spent ten years being paid to find things for people who should never be allowed to hold them, and I told myself it was research, it was scholarship, it was neutral. It isn't neutral. Nothing is neutral. Marek taught me that today, floating past, grinning like a christened man." He looked up. "So. That's my sermon. Shorter than his, I hope."

Edmund turned the fish bone in her fingers.

"My father spent his last year answering that thing," she said. "Every journal entry gets shorter and stranger, and near the end they're not entries anymore, they're letters—to me, to Marek, to himself—all of them saying the same thing in different words. *Don't let it make sense. The moment it makes sense, you're gone.*" She tossed the bone into the fire. "He figured it out too late to save himself, but early enough to bury it. Marek never figured it out at all. He thinks the sense-making is enlightenment."

"And you?"

She looked at the wrapped shape beneath the tarp in the boat, and felt the hand on the back of her neck shift its weight, gentle as a lover adjusting a coat.

"And I hear it," she said. "Every hour. It offers me my father back. It offers me the delta mapped, the missing found, every wrong thing made right and me made right along with it. And the terrifying part isn't that it lies." She met Casimir's eyes across the small fire. "The terrifying part is that most of what it offers is true. It just leaves out what the truth costs everyone else."

Casimir nodded slowly. "Then we don't give it time to negotiate."

"No." She was already looking downstream, where the black water slid past the stilts, carrying rain and wreckage and, somewhere in it, a man who could not drown. "We go tomorrow at first light. Straight down the deep channel—my father marked it. He called it *the grave, if I fail.*"

"You're going to sink it."

"I'm going to sink it where no dredge will ever reach, in water that doesn't give things back." She lay back against the hut post and let the rain drum the thatch above her. "But first we have to survive the night, and the morning, and whatever Marek is building between here and the grave."

Far downstream, almost beneath the sound of the river, she thought she heard it—or imagined she did—a human voice, carried thin over the water, rising and falling in the cadence of a man preaching to a congregation the flood had scattered.

She pulled her blanket tighter and did not sleep, and did not answer, and kept watch until the sky went the color of ash and the horizon, at last, began to wake.
