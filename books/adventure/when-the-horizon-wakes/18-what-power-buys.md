## 18. What Power Buys

The storm's tail dragged itself across the delta like a wounded animal, all grumble and no teeth now, the worst of it spent against the drowned dig and everything they'd carried out of it. Rain still came in long silver curtains, but between them the sky showed pale seams, and once—just once—a blade of sun cut through and lit the flooded forest gold for three full seconds before the clouds sewed it shut again.

They had made camp on a shelf of high ground where an old levee had held, more or less: a crescent of mud and trampled reeds barely wider than the boat when it lay on its side as a windbreak. Casimir had rigged a tarp from the mast and half a roll of salvaged line, and under it they'd built a fire from driftwood that smoked sullenly but burned. The Meridian sat wrapped in oilcloth at the back of the shelter, wedged beneath the hull's ribs where it couldn't be seen.

Edmund could feel it anyway.

That was the thing she couldn't stop noticing—the way awareness of it worked through her like water through cloth. She wasn't looking at it. She was facing the fire, her boots steaming, a tin cup of river-boiled tea going cold in her hands. And yet she knew exactly where it was, the way you know where your own heartbeat is, the way you know a tooth that aches. Distance meant nothing. The oilcloth meant nothing. It sat there in the dark behind her and she felt it sitting there, patient as sediment.

*You're tired,* she told herself. *Tired people imagine things.*

But she had watched Marek stand in that vault with his hands open and his voice gentle, and she had understood him—not agreed with him, not forgiven him, but *understood* him, the way you understand a current by feeling it take your legs. That understanding hadn't left with the floodwater. It had stayed in her like a splinter working deeper.

Casimir came back from the waterline shaking rain off his collar. He'd been checking the boat's seams again, though they both knew the answer. She was taking water faster than they could bail; another day of running rapids would finish her. They were marooned here until the storm finished dying and the delta settled enough to risk the channels—and until one of them figured out what to do about the object under the hull.

He crouched at the fire, fed it a piece of driftwood, and looked at her across the flames. His face had lost the polish she'd first resented at the levee. Seven days of monsoon had stripped it down to something plainer and more honest: hollowed cheeks, a healing cut along his jaw, eyes that didn't perform charm anymore because there wasn't energy left to spare for performance.

"You've been quiet," he said.

"I'm always quiet."

"You're quiet like weather before it turns." He turned his cup slowly. "Talk to me, Edmund."

She almost said *nothing to say*. The old reflex. A decade of practice at being unreadable, at giving surveyors' answers to human questions. But she'd walked into a vault with this man and come out alive beside him, and some debt of honesty had accrued between them that she didn't know how to default on anymore.

So she said: "I caught myself planning."

"Planning what?"

"What I'd fix." She stared into the fire. "With it. If I kept it."

The rain filled the silence for a while, ticking on the tarp, hissing where it found gaps near the fire.

"Tell me," Casimir said quietly.

* * *

The trouble was that the plans made sense. That was the trap of it, and she needed to say it aloud to see how senseless it actually was, the way you have to read a forged map against real terrain to catch the lie.

"It starts small," she said. "It started small. Last night, trying to sleep, I thought: the survey office in Kesswick. The one that rejected my funding request twice because my father's name was on the application and they didn't want the politics. With money—with influence—I could fund every remote river survey in the country. Every stretch of water nobody wants mapped. I could put teams on rivers that kill people because nobody's bothered to chart their floods."

"That's not nothing," Casimir said. "That's a good thing."

"That's what I told myself." Her laugh came out wrong, too short. "And then—this is the part I need you to hear, because I need to know how it sounds outside my own skull—then I thought, well, if I'm funding surveys anyway, why let committees decide which ones? I know which rivers matter. I've read them my whole life. I could decide. And then I thought about the men who buried my father's last report, the ones who called his final years a disgrace, and I thought: I could end their careers. Quietly. Deservedly. And then—"

She stopped. The fire popped. Somewhere out in the drowned forest, a bird gave one wet, questioning note and got no answer.

"And then?" Casimir prompted, gently.

"And then I realized I'd been lying there smiling." Her hands had gone tight around the cold cup; she made herself loosen them. "Lying in the dark, soaked through, half-starved, with a man dead or converted and a cult hunting us downriver—and I was *smiling*, planning the reorganization of a government survey office. Like it mattered. Like it was the most natural thing in the world." She finally looked up at him. "I never smiled about work. Not even good work. That's not me. That's not mine."

"No," Casimir said. "It isn't."

"How do you know?"

"Because I've spent two weeks watching you give away your last dry socks to a drowning stranger and then deny doing it." He set his cup down. "Because I read your father's journals for a living, Edmund, or I did, and the man who wrote the early ones—the field notes, the real ones—he wrote exactly the way you talk. Plain. Careful. Suspicious of anything that comes easy. The Meridian doesn't make you want new things. It makes your own wants louder, and then it makes them righteous. There's a difference, and you just heard it."

She wanted to believe him. Belief didn't come; only exhaustion did, heavy as the wet air.

"There's more," she said. "The worse part."

"Go on."

"After the careers. After the surveys. I thought—" and here her voice dropped, because saying it made it real in a way thinking it hadn't— "I thought about the letter."

Casimir went very still. He knew about the letter. He'd confessed to reading her file, and her file, assembled by professionals, had contained the fact of Aldous Dravenmoor's final letter to his daughter, unanswered, returned unopened—or so the file claimed. She had never confirmed it. Until now.

"He wrote to me seven months before he died," Edmund said. "I never opened it. I told everyone—including myself—that I'd lost it. I didn't lose it. I burned it unopened on a beach in Tarrow, and I watched the tide take the ashes, and I told myself it was the cleanest thing I'd ever done." The firelight moved on her face. "Last night, under the hull, I thought: if I had it back—if I had *him* back, somehow, some version of him—I'd ask him why. Why the artifact changed him. Why power ate him alive. And then I thought: I wouldn't have to ask. I could *know*. I could hold the same thing he held and understand from the inside, and then I'd be the one Dravenmoor who finally mastered it instead of being mastered."

The words hung in the steam and smoke.

"And that," she said, "is when I got up and came and sat by the fire. Because that thought felt like coming home."

* * *

Casimir didn't answer right away. He fed the fire again, though it didn't need feeding, and she recognized the gesture for what it was—a man buying time with his hands so his face could catch up.

"When I took this commission," he said at last, "my employers gave me a briefing book. Forty pages on Aldous Dravenmoor. Excavations, patrons, scandals, the estrangement, the letter—they knew about the letter, by the way; someone in his circle talked before he died. And at the back, a psychological profile. Very expensive firm. Very confident prose." He turned his cup again. "One line has followed me since I read it. It said: *Subject does not seek power; subject seeks justification. Provide sufficient cause and he will supply his own permission.*"

"That was my father."

"That was your father," Casimir agreed. "And here's what forty pages and a very expensive firm missed, because firms don't watch people row leaky boats through flooded forests at four in the morning. You're not built like him. You're built like the crew faction that wanted the thing buried. The journals name them, near the end—Halloway, Osei, the cartographer whose name I can't pronounce. People who saw what it was offering and chose the mud over the crown. Your father envied them. You can read the envy between the lines. He wrote about Halloway the way starving men write about bread."

Edmund looked at him. "You're telling me I'm strong."

"I'm telling you that you caught yourself smiling," Casimir said. "That's the whole test, and you passed it, and it nearly cost you everything, and it will cost you again tomorrow night and the night after, because that's what it does—it doesn't stop knocking, it just changes what it's selling. Tonight it sold you justice. Tomorrow it'll sell you peace. Next week it'll sell you forgiveness for your father, which I suspect is the one you actually want, and which will be the hardest offer to refuse." He met her eyes across the fire. "I'm not reassuring you. I'm warning you. There's a difference, and I'd rather you hated me for the warning than thank me later for the comfort."

The rain thinned to a whisper. Under the hull, unwrapped, unseen, the Meridian waited with the patience of geology.

"The hardest fight," Edmund said slowly, "isn't Marek."

"Marek already lost," Casimir said. "He just doesn't know it yet. He thinks the thing chose him. Nothing chooses anyone, Edmund. It's a mirror with a voice. It tells you that you were right all along, and weak men build churches around the echo." He paused. "Which brings us to the part I've been avoiding, because you asked for terrible honesty and I intend to deliver it."

"Say it."

"My employers will send others." He said it flatly, without decoration, and she understood that the absence of charm was its own kind of respect. "Not because they're evil in some operatic way. Because they're reasonable men with committees and timelines, and the Meridian represents leverage they can't quantify and therefore can't ignore. When I don't report in—when the advance team downstream reports what I did—they won't send fanatics. Fanatics are unreliable. They'll send professionals. Surveyors, like you. People who read water and tracks and won't stop until the delta gives up its receipts." He smiled without humor. "I trained several of them."

"How many?"

"Enough. And the organization has subsidiaries I only saw the edges of. Foundations. Trusts. A maritime salvage concern that 'recovers' things governments prefer not to admit existed." He looked into the fire. "Sinking it in the deepest channel buys time. Years, maybe a decade, if the coordinates stay lost. But someone will come looking eventually, and the next searchers won't have your scruples or my conscience."

"Then we don't leave coordinates," Edmund said. "We leave nothing. No record, no report, no map. We let the delta keep it the way it keeps everything—silently, and forever, and without our help."

"And if they find it anyway?"

"Then whoever finds it meets the same mirror we met," Edmund said, "and I'll grieve for them the way I should have grieved for my father sooner. But I won't carry it for them. I refuse to spend my life standing guard over a thing that whispers. Guarding it is just holding it with extra steps."

Casimir studied her for a long moment, and something in his expression shifted—not admiration exactly, something rawer, the look of a man watching a decision get made that he'd been afraid no one would make.

"You mean that," he said. "You actually mean it."

"I mean it tonight," she said. "Ask me tomorrow. Ask me every morning until it's done. That's the price of owning a mind that thing has touched—you check your own work daily, like checking a boat's seams." She nodded toward the hull. "Which reminds me. How bad is she?"

"Bad." He said it simply. "Two seams weeping past caulking, the transom joint opening. One more hard day on open water and she goes down with us aboard."

"Then we sink the Meridian somewhere shallow enough to reach on foot," Edmund said, "and walk out."

"We could try patching her."

"We could," Edmund said, "and I love that boat, and I've loved her badly, the way I've loved everything—by using her until she breaks. But I'm done asking vessels to hold more than they can." She glanced back at the dark shape beneath the hull ribs, at the oilcloth that might as well have been gauze. "Tomorrow, when the water drops, we find the deep channel my father marked. *The grave, if I fail.* He wrote it in the margin of the last survey chart, coordinates and all, and I've carried those numbers in my head for seven years like a stone in my boot."

"You memorized his failure site?"

"I memorized everything he ever wrote," Edmund said. "That was the problem. Loving him from a distance means knowing him by heart." She drained the cold tea and grimaced at it. "Get some sleep, Vane. Tomorrow we bury an inheritance."

Casimir rose, hesitated, and then—instead of some polished farewell—simply squeezed her shoulder once, briefly, like a man sealing a pact he no longer needed words for, and rolled into his bedroll under the tarp.

Edmund stayed up.

She told herself it was for the watch. It was partly for the watch. But partly it was the other thing, the honest thing, the thing she'd promised herself she'd do daily like checking seams: she sat facing the wrapped shape in the dark and interrogated her own heart, methodically, item by item. Justice? Still wanted—but hers to pursue without a crown. Forgiveness for Aldous? Wanted desperately—and suddenly visible as exactly the bait it was, because forgiveness bought from a whispering stone wouldn't be forgiveness at all, just surrender wearing its clothes. Peace?

Peace, she found, was already here, in the rain and the smoke and the sound of a man she trusted breathing evenly ten feet away. It had been available the whole time, free, unbought. The Meridian had never offered peace. It had offered the *feeling* of victory, endlessly deferred, and she had nearly signed for it in installments.

Near dawn, the rain stopped altogether. The clouds tore open in the east, and the horizon woke in bands of copper and rose, and Edmund stood at the edge of the levee watching the light come down the flooded forest like a slow tide.

Behind her, under the hull, the Meridian waited to be carried to its grave.

Ahead, far downriver, faint on the morning air, came a sound that did not belong to the storm's aftermath or to any bird she knew.

An engine. More than one. Coming upstream.
