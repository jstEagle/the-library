## 20. Settlements

The river widened as we came down out of the delta's throat, spreading into a brown braided sheet that carried the monsoon's whole argument with it—driftwood, drowned palms, a child's shoe turning slowly in an eddy like something considering. Casimir Vane stood at the tiller of Edmund Dravenmoor's leaking boat and watched the water carry away everything he had spent ten years building, and found, to his genuine surprise, that he did not mind.

His hands were still shaking. They had been shaking since the vault, since the moment the Meridian came up out of its cradle wrapped in silt and light and he had felt—not its pull, but hers. The way she'd looked at it. The way he'd almost looked at it through her eyes. He'd spent his career learning to read people the way she read water, and what he'd seen in her face in that flooded chamber had frightened him more than Marek's sermon ever could: recognition. A woman meeting her inheritance.

And then she had refused it. Out loud. In front of the thing itself.

He glanced at her now, forward in the bow, one hand on the gunwale, watching the banks slide past with the flat attention of someone who no longer trusted anything the river showed her. Her hair was stiff with dried spray. There was a cut along her forearm she'd dressed herself, badly, and she hadn't mentioned it once. Ten days in the green throat of the delta and she moved like the boat was part of her body.

*You were hired to find her,* he reminded himself. *Not to follow her.*

But that distinction had died somewhere back at the mission station, drowned quietly between half-truths, and what remained was something he didn't have vocabulary for yet. He was a man who made his living in vocabulary.

The advance team would be at Serrano's Landing. He knew it the way he knew his own file numbers—the logistics memos he'd signed off on eight months ago, before any of this had a face. Four men, a seaplane on standby, a communications rig disguised as a geological survey. Their orders, should Vane fail to report: proceed to the last known coordinates, secure the artifact, terminate loose ends. *Loose ends* was the actual phrase. He had written the phrase himself, or approved it, which amounted to the same thing in the ledger of a man's soul.

"You're doing the thing," Edmund said, without turning around.

"What thing?"

"Where you smile at the back of my head."

He laughed despite himself—a short, cracked sound. "I'm rehearsing."

She turned then, leaning against the bow rail, arms crossed. "Rehearsing what?"

"The conversation I'm about to have with four people who want you in a crate."

Something shifted behind her eyes—not fear; he'd learned by now that fear came off her like weather off stone—but calculation. "Tell me the plan first this time," she said. "All of it. Before we're inside it."

"All of it." He eased the throttle down, letting the current do the work, and told her. The landing. The seaplane. The evidence—Marek's journals from the dig, photographs of the converts' camp, the recording he'd made of Marek's gospel in the vault while Edmund had been prying open the inner door, her father's final coordinates scrawled in Aldous's own hand. And the Meridian's grave: the channel marker, the depth soundings, the exact spot where they had weighted it and let it go. Proof of destruction. That was the currency. That was the only thing his employers would accept in place of delivery.

"And when they don't accept it?" she asked.

"They'll accept it." He heard how thin it sounded even as he said it. "Because the alternative is admitting their asset went rogue and their relic is at the bottom of a river, and organizations like mine—I mean, *theirs*—" he corrected himself, and saw her mouth twitch, "—survive on plausible deniability. They can't afford a scandal. They can only afford silence."

"And you? What do they afford you?"

He let the question sit. The engine ticked. Somewhere upstream, thunder muttered like an old man changing his mind about something.

"They afford me a pension," he said finally. "A name in a discreet file. Continued employment at the pleasure of people I've never met. It's been enough for eleven years." He met her eyes. "It isn't anymore. I need you to understand that before we dock, because once I hand them that folder, there's no version of this where I go back. They don't forgive defection. They archive it."

Edmund studied him for a long moment, the way she studied a stretch of water before committing the boat to it—reading the currents underneath, the snags, the things moving below the surface.

"Good," she said.

"*Good?*"

"I spent a decade watching people burn bridges to get to my father's money. It's the first time I've seen someone burn one to get away from it." She turned back to the bow. "Dock on the north side. There's a fisherman's track behind the sheds. If this goes wrong, I'm not leaving by the front."

* * *

Serrano's Landing smelled of diesel and wet rope and the sweet rot of fruit nobody had gotten to market in time. The monsoon's tail end still hung over everything, a gray ceiling leaking intermittent violence, and under it the town moved with the hunched urgency of a place that lived one flood season from erasure.

Casimir left Edmund with the boat at the fisherman's track and walked in alone, because that was the arrangement and because some doors you had to walk through by yourself or not at all.

They were exactly where he'd known they'd be. The survey office above the chandlery, windows fogged, a generator humming somewhere below. He climbed the stairs and knocked twice—his own old signal, three knocks meant trouble—and the door opened on a man named Ostrer, broad and gray-templed, with the patient eyes of someone who had processed many disappearances and expected to process one more.

"Vane." No warmth. No surprise either, which told Casimir the radio traffic had already reached them. "You're nine days overdue."

"The river was nine days difficult." He stepped inside. Three others: Kessler at the window with binoculars trained on the waterfront, a young technician whose name Casimir had never bothered to learn, and a fourth man sitting too still in the corner, hands folded, watching Casimir with the particular attention of someone whose actual job was not geology. "I have the report."

"Where's the package?"

Casimir set the folder on the desk between them. "There is no package."

The room changed temperature. Ostrer didn't move, but the stillness in the corner sharpened, and Kessler lowered the binoculars by degrees, like a machine powering down into readiness.

"Explain."

So Casimir explained. He laid it out the way he'd rehearsed on the boat, except that rehearsal had prepared him for the words and not for what it cost to say them. The vanished team. Marek—Dr. Marek Solheim, Aldous Dravenmoor's protégé, listed dead seven years, very much alive, very much converted. The eight researchers who had walked into the water carrying their own packs because a man with calm eyes and a relic's blessing had told them they were going ahead to prepare the rising. The dig site. The journals. The recording—he played forty seconds of it on the technician's own equipment, Marek's voice filling the fogged office, gentle and certain: *it chose me, it has always been choosing, and when it wakes, all of this will have been worth it.*

Ostrer listened without expression. When the recording ended, he said, "And the artifact?"

"Sunk. Weighted, deep channel, coordinates in the folder. Beyond recovery. I watched it go down myself."

Silence. The generator hummed. Rain started against the windows, soft at first, then with intent.

"You understand," Ostrer said at last, "that our instructions regarding unrecovered assets are explicit."

"I wrote those instructions," Casimir said. "That's rather the point. I know exactly what they're worth, and I know exactly what you'll do with them—which is nothing, publicly, ever, because the alternative is explaining to your board why you funded an expedition that fed eight scientists to a river cult. Burn the folder if you like. But Marek is still alive, his converts are still armed, and the Meridian is down there in the dark whether you acknowledge it or not. You can spend the next decade pretending none of this happened, or you can spend it making sure no one else goes looking. Those are your options. Delivery was never one of them."

The man in the corner unfolded his hands. "Mr. Vane. Your contract terminated the moment you failed to report. Do you understand what that makes you?"

"A free man," Casimir said. "Terrifying, I know."

"It makes you a liability."

"It makes me a man with copies." He tapped the folder. "Originals are already moving. You taught me redundancy in my second year. I was an excellent student—it's why you hired me."

Kessler had drifted, casually, until he stood between Casimir and the door. Casimir noted it without looking, the way he noted everything now, the tracker's habits infecting him after weeks in her wake. Behind Kessler's shoulder, through the fogged glass, he could see the waterfront, the sheds, the gray line of the river. He thought about Edmund on the fisherman's track with her hand probably resting on the knife she pretended she didn't carry. He thought about eleven years of being the smooth one, the charming one, the man who talked his way through rooms, and how every one of those rooms had been furnished by other people.

"Here's what happens now," he said, and his voice came out steadier than he had any right to expect. "You release the seaplane's standby order. You file Marek as an active threat with whatever internal channels actually care about living problems instead of dead relics. And you leave Edmund Dravenmoor out of every document from this day forward. She doesn't exist to you. Her father's estate is closed. The Meridian is a geological anomaly you declined to pursue. If I ever see her name in one of your files again—if I ever hear it spoken in one of your offices—I will make sure the board hears Marek's recording. All of it. With my commentary."

"You'd destroy yourself."

"I've recently discovered I'm very good at sinking things." He smiled, and for once it wasn't charm; it was the other thing, the real thing underneath, and he watched Ostrer recognize it and look away first.

The rain hammered. Somewhere below, the generator coughed and caught.

Ostrer picked up the folder. Weighed it in his hand like a man pricing a coffin. "You realize there will be consequences."

"There always are." Casimir walked toward the door, and Kessler, after a glance at the corner man, stepped aside—because Kessler was a professional, and professionals don't start wars over folders full of paper in rented rooms. "Send the bill to whoever inherits my pension."

At the threshold he stopped. Turned back. One last thing, and he wanted to watch their faces for it.

"For the record—since accuracy matters to people like us. Aldous Dravenmoor tried to bury that thing thirty years ago and died failing. His daughter finished the job in one afternoon. Whatever your files say about the Dravenmoor bloodline, correct them. History's going to remember her better than it remembers any of you, and frankly, better than it remembers me. Make sure your archives reflect that."

He went out, down the stairs, into the rain.

* * *

Edmund was waiting where the fisherman's track met the water, hood up, motionless, so much a part of the gray landscape that he'd have missed her if he hadn't known to look. She fell into step beside him without a word, matching his pace, reading him the way she read everything—with patience and without mercy.

"Well?" she said, when the sheds were behind them and the rain had covered the sound of their footsteps.

"They took the folder. They'll bury it, file it, and pretend the delta ate everyone." He pushed the wet hair out of his eyes. "And I'm archived. Officially unaffiliated. Which means no pension, no protection, no employers, and a modest but persistent likelihood of people asking me questions in rooms without windows."

"That's the cost."

"That's the cost." He looked at her. Rain ran down her face and she didn't blink it away. "Eleven years, Edmund. I built a whole person out of commissions and cover stories, and I just handed the whole architecture to a man who weighs folders like funeral arrangements. I keep waiting to feel worse about it."

"And?"

"And I feel lighter than I have since I was twenty-three years old. Which is either growth or hypothermia."

The laugh she gave him was small and real, startled out of her, gone almost before it arrived—but it arrived. They walked on along the track, the river sliding beside them, huge and brown and indifferent, carrying its driftwood and its secrets toward the sea.

Ahead, moored at the landing's far pier, a government patrol boat sat low in the water, official and wrong, and two figures stood at its rail watching the shoreline with the unhurried attention of men who were not fishermen.

Edmund saw them at the same moment he did. Her hand found his sleeve—not pulling him to cover, just touching, confirming.

"Searchers," she murmured. "Official ones. They'll be looking for the vanished team's remains." Her jaw tightened. "Which means they'll find them. And close the case. And write it all down neatly."

"And Marek?"

"Marek," she said quietly, "was never in that water."

They stood together in the rain, the burned bridge smoking behind them, the dark horizon ahead, and Casimir understood that whatever settlement they'd just made with his old life, the river had not yet finished settling its accounts.
