## 21. Marek, Unfound

The summons came by river post, which was itself an omen. Nothing good had ever arrived in the delta by air; the government preferred its bad news delivered slowly, water-stained, so that by the time you read it you had already made your peace with whatever it contained.

Edmund opened the envelope on the deck of the *Vespera*, moored at the survey station where the season's work had finally wound down. The paper inside was dry, official, and smelled of toner and distant offices. She read it twice, then set it down on the chart table beside Casimir's coffee mug and let him read it too.

*RE: Case File 4471-K — Disappearance of Government Survey Team, Kessarin Delta.*

*Remains located. Investigation concluded.*

"Concluded," Casimir said, tasting the word like a wine he didn't trust. "That's generous of them."

"They found what they were looking for." Edmund watched a heron stalk the shallows below the pilings, patient as arithmetic. "Or what they were willing to look for."

The letter went on in the bloodless dialect of bureaucracy: skeletal remains recovered from a collapsed section of the drowned dig site, consistent with eight individuals. Personal effects matched the manifest of the vanished team. Cause of death indeterminate — structural collapse, flooding, exposure. The delta, the letter implied, had simply closed its hand around them, as deltas do. No further inquiry warranted. Condolences to next of kin. Case closed.

Eight names. Eight sets of remains, or near enough to satisfy a committee three thousand miles from the water.

And no ninth man.

---

They flew north together — not because either of them wanted to attend the official debriefing, but because they had learned, over the past months, that some things were better faced side by side. The capital was all marble dust and air conditioning, a city that pretended the delta did not exist except as a line item. The hearing room smelled of carpet shampoo and fear-sweat disguised as cologne.

Edmund sat through four hours of it with her hands folded and her face arranged into professional blankness. Men in good suits presented maps she could have drawn from memory. Photographs of the recovery site flickered across a screen: the black water, the silted corridors of Aldous's last excavation, divers' lights swimming like nervous fish through chambers she had walked out of alive while others had not walked out at all.

A deputy director with a voice like wet gravel summarized the findings. Structural failure. Natural disaster. Tragic loss of life. He used the phrase *unfortunate circumstances* twice, and each time Casimir's jaw tightened by a degree Edmund could measure.

Then came the question Edmund had been waiting for, asked not by the panel but by a thin man in the third row whose badge said something about internal affairs.

"And the individual known as Marek Voss? Formerly of Dravenmoor's expedition? His remains were not among those recovered."

The deputy director consulted his notes with the deliberation of a man buying time. "No remains matching that description were recovered. However, given the extent of structural collapse at the site, and the passage of time—"

"You're saying he might have been buried deeper."

"I'm saying the site is unstable and further excavation is not recommended."

"Not *recommended*," the thin man repeated. He made a note. Edmund watched him make it, and understood that somewhere in this building, in a drawer or a file or a locked cabinet, there was already a folder with Marek Voss's name on it, and it was not marked *closed*.

She said nothing. She had learned when to say nothing the way her father had never learned it, and it was one of the few lessons she was grateful for.

Afterward, in the corridor outside, the thin man fell into step beside her. Up close he looked tired in the way of people who read files for a living and believed half of them.

"Dravenmoor." He pronounced it carefully, watching her face for flinches. There were none to find; she had spent a decade sanding that name smooth. "You were the one who located the site."

"I was the one who survived it."

"Yes." He glanced at Casimir, who had drifted a tactful six feet away and was studying a fire-safety diagram with great interest. "Your report mentions a ninth member of the survey team. Unlisted. Your account describes him as—" he checked a card, though Edmund suspected he knew it by heart "—*'calm, certain, and unafraid, which frightened me more than any weapon would have.'*"

"That's an accurate quotation."

"The government's position is that Marek Voss died in the collapse."

"The government's position," Edmund said, "is written in that letter you sent me. I've read it. It's very tidy."

The thin man almost smiled. "And yours?"

Edmund looked out the corridor window, past the marble and the traffic, toward the south where the land gave way to brown water and green throat and everything the capital preferred not to think about.

"My position is that I put a two-hundred-pound anchor on that man's boat wreck and it came up empty. My position is that one of his converts was seen alive in a port town four hundred miles north three weeks after the flood, buying supplies with cash and asking questions about overland routes. My position is that Marek Voss spent thirty years learning how to survive in places like that delta, and a building falling down is not going to be what kills him." She turned back to the thin man. "But I suspect your file already says most of that."

The tiredness around his eyes deepened into something almost like respect. "There is a file," he admitted. "It's thin. A photograph from the port town, blurry. A witness statement from a fisherman who claims he saw a man preaching on the shore at dawn, alone, to no one." He paused. "Would you be willing to consult? Formally. On the recovery effort."

Edmund considered it honestly, which took longer than she expected. She thought about the photograph — her father holding the Meridian, radiant with a joy she had never once seen him direct at his daughter. She thought about the vault flooding, the whisper in the dark that had sounded so much like her own voice. She thought about the long months since, waking some nights with her heart hammering, certain she could hear humming under the floorboards.

"No," she said.

"No?"

"I'll tell you what I know, once, now, and you can write it down. But I'm not going to spend my life hunting a ghost through the mountains. That road doesn't end anywhere good. I've seen where it ends." She held his gaze. "Find him if you can. But don't ask me to make him my horizon."

The thin man wrote something in his notebook. When he looked up, he nodded slowly, as if she had confirmed something he'd suspected about her all along.

"For what it's worth," he said, "the fisherman's statement includes a detail we couldn't verify. He said the man on the shore wasn't preaching to no one. He said it looked like he was preaching *to the water*."

Edmund felt the cold arrive in her spine on schedule, punctual as the monsoon. She let it pass through and kept her voice level.

"Then he hasn't changed," she said. "Thank you for telling me."

---

* * *

They stayed in the capital three more days, long enough for Casimir to burn the last of his bridges properly, with witnesses. The meeting happened in a hotel bar with brass fixtures and a pianist who played like he was being paid by the wrong note. Two of Casimir's former employers sat across the table from him: a woman with silver hair and a man whose handshake Edmund remembered from the hearing corridor, dry and brief as a lizard's tongue.

She wasn't invited to the table. She sat at the bar with her back to them and listened anyway, because walls in places like that were decorative.

"—the asset is presumed destroyed," the silver-haired woman was saying, in the tone of someone reading a weather report. "Which is regrettable, but the site data may still have value. If you were to return to the region—"

"There is no asset," Casimir said. "There never was an asset. There was a thing that ate everyone who picked it up, and now it's under forty meters of water in a channel that floods every spring, and I have watched what wanting it does to people from the inside. I'm done. Consider this my resignation, my testimony, and my goodbye, in that order."

"You're throwing away a considerable career, Mr. Vane."

"I'm throwing away a considerable leash. There's a difference."

Edmund ordered another coffee she didn't want and smiled at the bartender, and behind her she heard the lizard-tongued man say, quietly, "Your father would be disappointed," and heard Casimir answer, "My father sold insurance. He'd be thrilled I finally got a steady job," and heard the chairs scrape as the employers left without paying for their drinks.

Casimir settled onto the stool beside her a moment later. In the bar mirror she watched him watch her, and after ten years of reading water she had gotten reasonably good at reading him too: the charm worn down to something plainer underneath, and better.

"Well?" she said.

"They'll send others eventually. Smarter ones, or stupider ones. Probably stupider first." He turned his glass a quarter-turn on the bar. "I told them where the channel is. Let them come dive in monsoon current if they want. The river will handle the interviews."

"The river keeps its receipts," Edmund said.

"It does." He glanced at her reflection. "Does it bother you? Telling them anything at all?"

She thought about it. "No. Hiding the location only works if I'm the only one who knows, and I'd have to spend my life guarding the secret. That's just another way of carrying it." She drank the coffee, which was terrible. "Let it be ordinary knowledge. Ordinary things get ignored. That's the best protection the river can give it."

Casimir laughed — short, surprised, real. "You know, for a woman who spent a decade erasing herself, you've gotten very good at knowing exactly how much of yourself to leave visible."

"It's called surveying," Edmund said. "I map things. Including myself, apparently."

---

* * *

The last piece of news reached her a week later, at the northern rail terminus where the green country finally surrendered to wheat fields and the air smelled of dust instead of rot. They had come north partly to see the place themselves — the town where the convert had been spotted — and partly because Edmund had discovered, to her mild alarm, that she no longer knew how to travel without a destination.

The town was called Harrow's Bend, which was optimistic, since the river there bent twice. They found the fisherman easily enough; in towns that size, anyone who talked to strangers was a landmark. He was old and sun-cured and happy to talk, especially once Casimir bought him a beer at eleven in the morning.

"Saw him plain as Sunday," the fisherman said. "Dawn, down on the mudflats. Standing in the shallows with his arms out like this—" he demonstrated, elbows high, palms open, a posture that raised the hair on Edmund's arms because she had seen it before, carved in stone, in a marker registry that matched nothing official "—talking to the water. Not loud. Just talking. Like a man catching up with an old friend."

"What did he look like?" Edmund asked.

"Tall. Gray in the beard. Eyes like—" the fisherman squinted, searching "—like a man looking at something behind you. You know? Never quite at you." He shrugged. "I called out to him. Asked if he needed help. And he turns, slow, and smiles at me — polite as a deacon — and he says, 'She's safe where she sleeps. Tell her not to grieve. Grief is just love with nowhere to go, and she'll find somewhere to put it.'"

The beer glass sweated between the fisherman's hands. Casimir had gone very still beside her.

"He knew you'd come," Casimir said quietly, when the fisherman had wandered off to thank the beer. "He left that message knowing it would travel. Knowing someone would ask."

"He's been leaving messages his whole life," Edmund said. "His gospel needs an audience. Even an audience of one."

"So what do we do?" Casimir's voice was careful, the way it always was now when the question touched the shape of her future. "The rail line runs north from here. Overland routes into the mountains. If we wanted—"

"We don't," Edmund said.

She walked down to the mudflats while the light went long and gold, and stood where the fisherman said the man had stood. The river here was tame, wide and shallow, combed with ripples. Nothing whispered in it. Nothing hummed. It was just water doing what water does, going where gravity sends it, keeping its receipts in silt and silence.

Somewhere north of here, in country she had never surveyed, a gray-bearded man was preaching to rivers that could not answer him, carrying a faith whose god lay drowned beneath forty meters of brown current. He would gather followers, maybe. He would wait, certainly. He was patient the way rot is patient, and someday — a year, a decade, a lifetime — word would reach her that he had surfaced again, and she would have to decide all over again whether to hunt him.

But not today. Today the light was kind and the water was honest and Casimir was waiting up on the bank with two tickets and a question he was too polite to ask twice.

Some horizons, she thought, stay dark no matter how long you stare at them. That wasn't a failure of vigilance. It was just the shape of the world — and the trick, the whole trick, the thing her father had never learned and Marek had learned too well and she was learning now, slowly, like a language — was to keep watch without letting the darkness move in.

She turned her back on the north and walked up the bank toward the light.

"Casimir," she called. "What time's the train?"

He held up the tickets. "Dawn tomorrow. There's a survey contract waiting in the eastern ranges — big one, seasonal flooding, nobody else wants it." His grin was the old grin but built on new foundations. "I may have mentioned I know a tracker."

"Did you mention she comes expensive?"

"I mentioned she comes with opinions. Same thing."

Edmund laughed, and the sound carried out over the flat water and came back unchanged, and that night she slept without dreaming of humming, and in the morning the horizon woke the color of hammered tin, and she watched it lighten with her hands wrapped around hot coffee and her feet planted on the deck of a moving world —

— and did not hear, three hundred miles north, where the mountains began, a gray-bearded man standing at the headwaters of a cold clear river, smiling at the water, and saying, to no one, to everyone, to the dark:

*"Soon."*
