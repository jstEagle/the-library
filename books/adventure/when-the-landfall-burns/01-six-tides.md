## 1. Six Tides

The tide came in at Corlow Landing the way it always did — gray-green and patient, sliding up the pilings like it owned them, which in every way that mattered it did. Sable Amberdale stood on the deck of the *Kestrel's Debt* with a manifest curling in her damp hand and watched the water rise over the third rung of the boarding ladder, and thought, as she did every morning of her life on this river, that the Vessin gave nothing away for free.

"Salt pork's going bad if we don't get it under," Tam called from the hold, his voice bouncing up through the hatch along with the smell of tar and bilgewater. "And the rice bags are sweating. You want rice wine by the time we hit Halfway Light?"

"I want you to stop complaining about cargo you signed for," Sable said, but there was no edge in it. There never was, not with Tam. Forty years he'd been her first mate, back to when they were both younger than the boat, and complaining was simply how he breathed.

She folded the manifest into her coat pocket and went down the ladder herself, boots ringing on the rungs, because the salt pork *was* going bad and because standing still made her itch. It always had. Her mother had named her restlessness a sickness once, sitting on the porch at Corlow with her hands folded, watching the river go by without ever going anywhere on it. *You've got miles in your blood, girl,* she'd said. *Miles and more miles, and no landing will ever be enough.* Sable had been eleven. She'd taken it as a compliment.

The hold was dim and smelled of hemp rope, lamp oil, and the particular sweetness of rice beginning to turn in river humidity. Tam had the first pallet of pork barrels half-shifted, his big hands moving with the economy of a man who'd done this work so long his body had stopped consulting him about it. Gray in his beard now, gray coming into his knuckles, but still the strongest back between Corlow and the sea.

"We're heavy," he said as she took the other end of a barrel. "You feel it?"

"I feel it."

"Rice, pork, nails, lamp oil, and forty bolts of that cloth the weavers' guild swears is cotton but I'd bet my teeth is hemp." He set the barrel down with a thud that traveled through the deck plates. "Heavy enough that we'll sit low. Low enough that Widow's Tooth will be chewing at our keel if the flood's running mean."

"The flood's always running mean."

"That's my point."

They worked in the rhythm they'd worn smooth over decades — lift, swing, set, step back, lift again — while above them the landing woke up. Corlow was nothing much: a plank wharf, a customs shed with a roof that leaked in three places, a chandlery, a rum shop whose sign had faded to the ghost of a parrot, and behind it all the green wall of the delta country pressing close, palms and mangrove and the constant complaint of birds. But it was the last real landing before the upper reaches, and everything the river towns needed came through here on its way to nowhere, and so the mornings were loud with cart wheels and gull-cries and men shouting numbers at each other across the gaps between boats.

Sable liked the noise. She'd never admit it, but she liked it the way a man likes the sound of his own name spoken in a crowd — proof, however small, that he exists somewhere other than in his own head.

They had the pork stowed and were breaking open the rice when the boy came down the wharf at a dead run.

She heard him before she saw him — the slap of bare feet on planks, too fast and too uneven for a man carrying anything, and then he burst through the hatch-light at the top of the ladder, a customs-shed runner of maybe fourteen, breathing hard, eyes wide with the particular joy boys take in being the first to carry bad news.

"Captain Amberdale?" He gulped air. "Captain — they said — the inspector's asking for you. At the shed. Right now, he said."

"What inspector?"

"Halloway. Government survey office." The boy swallowed. "It's the survey team, Captain. Upstream. They've gone missing."

Tam's hands stopped on the rice bag. In the dimness of the hold, Sable watched him go very still, the way old rivermen do when a word lands wrong — not flinching, just listening past the word to what the river might have meant by it.

"Missing how?" she asked.

"Nobody knows. Six days now. The launch they sent up couldn't get past the bend — pilot wouldn't run the flood, turned back at Widow's Tooth." The boy's voice dropped, thrilled and hushed. "They say nobody will take the contract. They say it's suicide water this season."

"They say a lot of things," Tam muttered.

"Inspector Halloway wants you specifically, Captain. He said — he said you were the only one fool enough." The boy caught himself, went pink. "Begging pardon. That's what he said. Word for word."

"Did he." Sable wiped her hands on her trousers and looked at Tam, and Tam looked back, and between them passed the whole conversation they would have anyway, compressed into a single glance: *don't*, and *we need the money*, and *don't*, and *how much*, and *don't*.

"Tell the inspector," she said to the boy, "I'll finish my cargo first. Then he can buy my time by the hour like anyone else."

* * *

Inspector Halloway occupied the customs shed like a man occupying territory he intended to keep. He was younger than Sable expected — forty, maybe less, with the kind of neatness that read as either discipline or vanity, hard to tell which until it mattered. His uniform was government gray, pressed sharp, and his boots were wrong for the river: polished leather, thin-soled, city boots. He'd come up from the capital on the mail steamer, someone said, and hadn't taken his eyes off his papers since.

He didn't offer her a seat. That told her something too.

"Captain Amberdale." He consulted a folder without opening it, as though he already knew everything in it and wanted her to know he knew. "You hold the only current pilot's certificate for the upper Vessin above Halfway Light. Is that correct?"

"It's correct."

"You ran the flood last spring. And the spring before."

"When there was cargo worth running."

"There is cargo worth running now." He laid a paper on the table between them, face down, and rested two fingers on it. "Six tides' pay. Triple your standard rate. Plus a salvage bonus if the survey equipment is recovered intact."

Sable kept her face still. Six tides. She did the arithmetic without wanting to — six tides paid off the *Kestrel's Debt*'s hull insurance, the thing that had been eating her earnings for two years since the grounding at Saltmarsh. Six tides and she could stop taking cargo she didn't trust, stop saying yes to consignments packed by men who lied about weight. Six tides and maybe, maybe, a season where the boat worked for her instead of the reverse.

But she'd learned long ago that triple pay on a river job meant one of two things: the work was deadly, or the employer was lying. Usually both.

"Triple pay," she said. "For finding six grown men with instruments and radios who've managed to disappear in country I could cross blindfolded. What exactly happened to them, Inspector? Boats don't vanish. Radios don't go quiet unless somebody wants them quiet."

Halloway's fingers tapped the paper once. "That is precisely the sort of question the survey office hopes you won't need answered."

"And precisely the sort I'll need answered before I sign anything."

For a moment something moved behind his eyes — not anger; calculation, fast and cold, the look of a man re-pricing goods on a shelf. "A team of seven went upriver eleven days ago to complete the hydrographic survey of the middle reaches. On the fifth day their scheduled radio check failed to occur. It has not occurred since. Their camp was located by air three days ago — tents standing, supplies in order, no signs of violence or distress." He paused. "No signs of the team."

"No footprints leaving."

Something flickered. "You've read the report?"

"I've read rivers longer than I've read reports." She leaned forward, put her palms flat on the scarred table. "Here's what I know, Inspector, and you can write it down. When seven men vanish from a standing camp with their kettle warm and their boats tied, it isn't the river that took them. Rivers leave tracks. Floodwater leaves wreckage. If there are no tracks, then the men walked out on purpose, or were carried out, and either way somebody upstream knows why — and whoever sent me up to find them probably knows why too, and doesn't much care whether I come back."

The shed was quiet except for the tick of the tide gauge outside and, far off, the gulls working the fish scrap barges. Halloway regarded her for a long moment, and then, very slightly, the cold precision of him rearranged itself into something almost like approval, which was worse.

"Triple pay," he said again. "Plus hazard bonus. The contract stands until the dry season closes the channel — call it six tides of navigable water, perhaps fewer. After that, no vessel draws deep enough to reach the site, and whatever happened to those men stays buried until next flood." He slid the paper an inch toward her. It stayed face down. "I'm told you have debts, Captain. Hull insurance. Mooring fees at Corlow. A rather aggressive lender in Halfway Town."

Her jaw tightened before she could stop it. So he *had* read everything. Every ledger line, every marker she'd signed, every debt she'd sworn no one in a gray uniform would ever count against her.

"The survey office keeps thorough records," Halloway said mildly.

"I'll bet it does."

"Then we understand each other." He turned the paper over at last and set a pen beside it. It was a standard salvage-and-recovery contract, the kind she'd signed a dozen times — except the recovery clause listed not just instruments and records but *all sealed containers recovered from the survey area, to be surrendered unopened to the survey office upon return*. Unopened. Sealed containers. What sealed containers did a hydrographic survey carry?

She looked up. Halloway watched her with the patience of a man holding a good hand.

"One question," she said. "Before I sign. Why me? You've got a dozen licensed pilots on the lower river. Cheaper ones."

"Because they refused."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only honest one I have." For just a moment the bureaucratic polish cracked and she saw underneath it something tired and hard and human, and then it sealed over again like water closing over a dropped stone. "Sign or don't, Captain Amberdale. The tide won't wait, and neither will I."

Outside, the gulls wheeled and screamed over the landing, and the river slid past the pilings, brown and broad and patient, going somewhere it had already been ten thousand times. Sable picked up the pen.

* * *

Tam was waiting at the gangway, arms crossed, wearing the expression he'd worn the day she'd bought the *Kestrel's Debt* against his advice and the day she'd run the Saltmarsh bar against his advice and every day in between when her judgment and his caution had gone to war.

"Well?" he said.

"Six tides' pay. Triple rate. Hazard bonus."

"Suicide water."

"Probably."

"Sealed containers surrendered unopened." He spat over the rail, a habit the customs officers hated. "Salvage contracts don't say *unopened*, Sable. Rescue contracts don't say *unopened*. That's a treasure clause. Somebody upstream thinks there's something in that water worth more than six men's lives, and that gray-shirted peacock wants it brought home in one piece and nobody the wiser."

"I know."

"Do you." He unfolded his arms slowly. "And we're going anyway."

She didn't answer right away. She leaned on the rail and looked down the long brown reach of the Vessin, south toward the sea she rarely visited anymore, and then north, upstream, toward the green haze of the middle country where the channel narrowed and the bars shifted with every flood and Widow's Tooth waited with its granite grin. Somewhere up there, six tides of water left before the dry season shut the door. Somewhere up there, seven men had walked out of a warm camp and left no tracks, and a government man with city boots wanted a pilot desperate enough not to ask twice.

Desperate enough. That was the word, wasn't it. She thought of the insurance ledger in her cabin drawer, the red ink creeping up the columns like floodwater up a bank. She thought of the mooring master at Corlow, who smiled too widely when she paid late. She thought of her mother on the porch, *miles in your blood*, and how the miles had never once bought her anything but more miles.

And underneath all of it, quieter than the debt and older than the fear, the itch. The itch of a river she'd never finished reading. Of channels above Halfway Light she'd run only in high water, years ago, young and lucky. Of the upper reaches in flood — the one water on earth that still scared her, which meant it was the one water still worth running.

"Load the extra line," she said. "Double-bight the anchors. And tell the cook we eat well tonight, because tomorrow we go up the throat of it."

Tam studied her for a long moment, and then sighed the sigh of a man who had lost this argument for forty years and would lose it for forty more, and pushed off the rail.

"Six tides," he said. "Water'll be falling the whole way. Falling water moves bars, Sable. Every chart we own will be lying to us by day three."

"Charts always lie. That's why they pay for a pilot."

"Aye." He started down the wharf toward the chandlery, then stopped, and without turning around: "One thing, Captain. If it smells like salvage instead of rescue — if that gray man's hiding something — we turn around. Promise me that much."

Sable looked upstream. The light was going gold on the water, and the flood current ran strong and steady under it, carrying sticks and leaves and one broken oar, turning slowly, from some boat she'd never see, headed for the sea.

"I promise," she said, "to keep my eyes open."

Tam grunted — the grunt of a man filing a promise away for later collection — and went to buy rope.

Above the landing, the tide gauge ticked upward, patient as everything else on the Vessin. Six tides, the inspector had said. Sable stood alone at the rail of the *Kestrel's Debt* and felt the old itch rise in her blood like water in a lock, and knew, the way she knew the pull of a bar under her keel, that whatever waited upriver, she was already gone.
