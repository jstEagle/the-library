## 2. The Contract

The rain came in off the Vessin that morning like a debt collector — no warning, no courtesy, just a gray wall of water marching up the landing road while Sable Amberdale stood on her deck counting crates she could not yet afford to load.

*Magpie* sat low at her moorings, riding the swell of the flood tide with the patient patience of an old dog waiting to be fed. Around her, Corlow Landing stank of wet rope and tar and the sweet rot of fruit nobody would buy now that the survey boats had stopped coming down from the upper reaches. Three days since word came that the government team had gone silent above Widow's Tooth. Three days of officials standing on the customs house steps with their hats in their hands, asking after pilots, and three days of every pilot in Corlow discovering urgent business elsewhere.

Sable had not discovered any such business. She had simply watched, and waited, and counted.

"Forty-one," Tam said behind her, materializing out of the drizzle with his collar up and his hands jammed in his pockets like a man who'd rather they were around someone's throat. "Forty-one crates we can take if we take nothing else. That's eleven short of what the Vessin Line contract pays."

"The Vessin Line contract pays because the Vessin Line doesn't go where we're going." She touched the rail, feeling the deck breathe under her palm. A boat was always breathing. You learned to read it the way you read a lover's chest — the quick shallow lift of fear, the long slow fall of sleep. *Magpie* was awake this morning. So was she.

"You've heard, then." Tam's voice went flat. "You've heard what they're offering."

"I've heard what they're *asking.*"

"And?"

She turned to look at him. Twenty years Tam Brask had stood at her shoulder, through the smuggler's channels below Harrow Mouth and the ice year when the river froze solid enough to walk across and they'd nearly starved anyway, because walking a frozen river and piloting one are different trades entirely. He was older than her by a decade, grayer, broader, with a face like a dock cleat — something you tied things to and expected to hold. Right now he was holding himself very still, which was how she knew he already knew the answer.

"And I'm listening," she said.

* * *

Inspector Halloway received her in the customs house at noon, in a room that smelled of cold tea and wet wool and the particular institutional staleness of a building whose windows hadn't opened since the settlement was founded. Rain ran down the glass in ropes. Somewhere below, the river made its low continuous argument against the pilings, and Halloway spoke over it as though it weren't there, as though the river were a formality he had already declined.

He was not what she expected. She had pictured some red-faced administrator sweating through his collar; instead she got a man dry as ledger paper, forty perhaps, with pale eyes that did not move when the rest of his face moved. His hands were folded on the table between them like instruments laid out for surgery. On the table also: a single sheet of paper, face down, and a pen.

"Captain Amberdale." He said her name the way a man reads a figure off a chart — correctly, without interest. "Sit."

"I'll stand. I stand better than I sit."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth. Not quite amusement. Acknowledgment, maybe, the way a man acknowledges weather. "As you like. You're aware of the situation upstream."

"A survey team went quiet above Widow's Tooth four days ago. Six souls. The river's running nine feet over seasonal mark and climbing with the meltwater, and every pilot on this stretch has suddenly remembered their mothers told them never to run the flood." She let that sit. "I'm aware."

"Then you know why the pay is what it is." He turned the paper over and slid it across. "Triple standard rate. Half on signature, half on recovery of the team, living or dead. Plus salvage rights on any government property you recover incidental to the rescue."

Sable didn't touch the paper. She looked at it instead, the way you look at a sandbar before committing your keel to the channel beside it. Triple rate. Half up front. Her mind, traitorous and quick, was already doing arithmetic — the hull work *Magpie* needed, the debt sitting on her like ballast, the eleven crates' worth of shortfall Tam had counted that morning. The numbers arranged themselves into a shape she liked, and she distrusted them for exactly that reason.

"Rescue contracts don't pay triple," she said. "Recovery contracts do. Somebody wants those people found badly enough to make it worth dying for, or somebody wants something else found and the people are the excuse. Which is it, Inspector?"

For the first time, Halloway's hands unfolded. He laced his fingers together again, differently, a small precise rearrangement, and she understood she had been measured and found acceptable — or at least found useful.

"The government does not pay for excuses," he said. "It pays for results. The survey team was mapping the upper reaches ahead of the new lock works. Their instruments are expensive. Their findings are classified. And the window is closing — the dry season comes early this year, by all indications, and when it comes, the upper reaches become unnavigable for six months. If they are alive up there, they will need extraction before the water falls. If they are not alive—" the pale eyes lifted to hers, "—their records still need to come down."

"Records," Sable said. "Not bodies."

"Bodies keep. Records rot."

There it was, then, laid out plain as a channel marker: this was not a rescue. This was a retrieval wearing a rescue's coat. She should have stood up and walked out. Every instinct Tam had ever voiced and half the ones he kept to himself were screaming it. Instead she heard herself say, "You'll want me to sign a route plan. Pilots who run blind water get asked afterward why they took the line they took."

Halloway inclined his head a fraction. "Naturally. Show me your intended ascent."

And so she bent over the table, over the great linen chart of the Vessin that someone had weighted down at the corners with brass weights gone green with age, and began to trace the route with her finger. Corlow Landing to Harrow Reach. Harrow Reach to the Narrows. The Narrows to Widow's Tooth, where the river dropped through a gap in the rock wide enough for one boat and angry enough to kill two. Above Widow's Tooth, the country went blank on the chart — literally blank, the linen fading into unprinted paper, because the survey team had been the first in forty years to map it and their map was somewhere upstream with them.

Her finger stopped at a bend in the lower blank reach, where the river swung hard around a bluff and the chart showed only a question mark and a faint pencil notation in someone's dead hand: *shifting bars.*

"Cutter Bend," Halloway said.

She looked up. He was watching her finger, not her face.

"You know it?"

"I know of it." His voice was as even as ever, but he had leaned forward — a small thing, two inches maybe, but on a man built like a closed door, two inches was a shout. "Forty years ago there was a revenue cutter lost on that bend. The *Vessel's Grace*. Carrying payroll funds for the original settlements, as I recall. It went down with everything aboard. The river shifted after the flood of '61 and no one has located the wreck since." He straightened. "Irrelevant to your task, of course. But the sandbars near there — describe them to me. If the team went missing near the bend, you'd want to know what's under you."

Sable kept her face still. It was a skill she'd learned young, in card rooms and customs offices, and it served her now: *nothing to see here, Inspector, move along.* But inside, something had gone very quiet and very sharp, the way the air goes before lightning.

Because here was the thing about Cutter Bend. Here was the thing she had never said aloud in twenty years of running this river, the thing her father had told her once, drunk, on a night when the moon was full and the water high, and had denied the next morning with the practiced ease of a man un-telling a secret: her father had seen the *Vessel's Grace* go down. Had been a boy crewing a timber raft, had watched the cutter heel over in the flood with her money chests chained in her hold, and had spent the rest of his life diving that bend in every season the water allowed, finding nothing, telling no one, and dying poor with the location locked behind his teeth.

He'd told her the shape of the bottom once. Only once. *A bar like a sickle, girl, and the deep hole behind it, and the wreck lies in the throat of the hole with her masts broken flat.* She had been fourteen. She had thought it a story.

Now a government inspector was asking her, with the studied carelessness of a man asking about the weather, to describe the sandbars near Cutter Bend.

"They shift," she said. "Every flood moves them. Any description I gave you today would be a lie by Thursday. That's why they pay pilots, Inspector. We don't memorize rivers. We read them."

Halloway held her gaze for a long moment. The rain ticked against the glass. Downstairs, a clerk's pen scratched like a mouse in a wall.

"Of course," he said finally. "Then I'll rely on your reading." He turned the contract toward her and set the pen beside it with a small deliberate click. "Half payment transfers on signature. We depart tomorrow at first light. Do we have an agreement, Captain Amberdale?"

She picked up the pen. She told herself it was the money — the hull work, the debt, the eleven crates, the freedom that a sum like that could buy a woman who owned nothing in the world but a boat and a reputation. She told herself it was ambition, the itch of miles not yet traveled, the blank space on the chart that had been calling her since she was fourteen years old and a drunk man drew a river bottom in spilled rum on a tavern table.

All true. None of it the whole truth. The whole truth was that she wanted to see what was in the throat of the hole, and a man with pale eyes had just told her, without meaning to, that he wanted it too.

She signed.

* * *

Tam was waiting outside under the customs house eave, smoking, which he only did when he was frightened or furious, and sometimes both.

"Well?" he said.

"We leave at first light. Triple rate, half up front." She held up the paper-wrapped packet the paymaster's clerk had handed her, heavy as a brick, warm from someone else's vault.

Tam looked at it the way he'd look at a snake in a cargo hold. "Triple rate for a rescue. In flood season. With a crew of four." He shook his head slowly. "Sable. Rescues pay grief money. Salvage pays greed money. That man isn't paying us to find six lost surveyors. He's paying us to find something the surveyors found first, and I'd wager my pension the surveyors aren't lost at all."

"You think they ran?"

"I think tents don't walk away and kettles don't boil themselves, and when the official story and the money agree too neatly, somebody's writing both." He dropped the cigarette and ground it under his heel with more force than it needed. "That inspector asked you questions. What kind?"

"Sandbars," Sable said. "Near Cutter Bend."

Tam went still. The rain dripped off the eave between them, counting seconds. She watched him arrive at it — watched him remember, as she had remembered, the stories, the drowned payroll, the old man with rum on his breath drawing a sickle-shaped bar in spilled liquor. Tam had been there that night too. Tam had carried her home on his shoulders.

"He knows," Tam said quietly. "Somebody told him, or somebody told somebody, and now the government wants its forty-year-old stolen money back off the bottom, and it wants it quiet, and it wants it before the water drops." He looked at her, and his face was the cleat-face, the holding-face, but underneath it was fear, and underneath the fear was the other thing, the thing that had kept them partners for twenty years. "Say the word and I'll take the packet back inside. We load the forty-one crates and run freight like honest fools, and in six months we're still poor and still alive."

Sable looked out past him, past the eave, to where the Vessin ran brown and swollen between the pilings, carrying the meltwater of mountains she'd never seen toward a sea she rarely reached anymore. Nine feet over seasonal mark. Somewhere up there, in the blank space on the chart, six people were either dead or lying, and a wreck lay in the throat of a hole with her masts broken flat, and the dry season was coming early to close the door on all of it.

The itch of miles. The blank on the map. The money. She named each want honestly, the way you name rocks in a channel, because a pilot who lies about her own wants drowns same as anyone.

"No," she said. "We're going."

Tam exhaled through his nose. "Knew you'd say that."

"Did you?"

"Twenty years, captain." He pulled his collar up and stepped out into the rain. "I've been reading this river a long time too. First light, then. I'll wake the crew." He paused, half-turned, droplets silvering off his shoulders. "One thing. Whatever's up there — whatever he's really paying for — when we find it, we find it *ourselves*, understand? Not for him. He gets what the contract says and not one thing more. Because men like that write contracts with two bottoms, and the second one's always painted to look like deep water."

He crunched away down the gravel toward the boatyard, and Sable stood a moment longer under the eave, the payment packet heavy in her hand, watching the brown water slide past the pilings with all its secrets held shut beneath it.

Tomorrow at first light, they'd run the flood. And whatever waited in the throat of the hole at Cutter Bend, she meant to see it before Inspector Halloway's pale eyes did.
