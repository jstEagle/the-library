## 3. Running the Flood

They cast off from Corlow Landing on the last of the ebb, when the mudflats still breathed their low-tide stink of rot and iron, because Sable Amberdale had learned long ago that a flood tide wasted is a day stolen from you by the river itself. The *Kestrel's Debt* — sixty feet of blackened teak and stubbornness, named for the debt she'd been built to pay off and never quite had — slid off the pilings with her engine turning over slow and sullen, like an old man asked to climb stairs.

"Slack water in forty minutes at the Narrows," Tam said beside her, marking the chart with a stub of grease pencil. He didn't look up. He never looked up when he was worried; he looked down harder, as if the answer might be hiding in the contour lines. "If we're not through the first bend before it turns, we're fighting four knots of current instead of riding two."

"Then we'll be through the first bend," Sable said.

The morning came up gray-gold behind the eastern hills, mist standing in the flooded timber like the ghosts of trees that had drowned decades ago and never been given burial. The Vessin in flood was not a river so much as a rumor of one — its banks dissolved, its channel smeared across a mile of drowned country, snags and fence posts and the roofs of forgotten outbuildings breaking the surface here and there like the backs of sleeping animals. Somewhere under all that brown violence lay the true channel, the deep thread of it, and finding that thread was the whole art, the whole trade, the whole reason Inspector Halloway had needed to come to her.

*Why no one else would take the job.* She'd heard him say it to his superiors over the radio, or words to that effect, before they'd signed papers. It wasn't flattery. It was arithmetic. Three salvage boats had refused the contract outright. A fourth had quoted a price so obscene it amounted to refusal with extra steps.

Sable took the wheel herself and felt the boat answer her hands the way a good horse answers a rider it trusts — not instantly, not slavishly, but with a kind of conversation running through the timbers, hull to helm, water to wood.

"Deck crew to stations," she called aft. "Fenders in. Lines coiled. If anyone's carrying a knife, now's the time to have it loose in your pocket."

Below, she heard the sounds of compliance: boots on deck plates, the rattle of fender baskets, young Pell — no, not Pell, that was the survey lot — young *Doss*, their deckhand, laughing nervously at something Tam's nephew Bracken said back to him. Good. Nervous laughter was better than silence. Silence on a boat going into flood water was a bad omen, the way a dog gone quiet is worse than a dog growling.

The slack came early, as slack does above the Narrows, and then the river turned beneath them and began to run the other way, and Sable opened the throttle and let the *Kestrel's Debt* eat the current.

*

The first hour was almost gentle, if you knew how to read it. The trick of running flood water was not to fight it and not to surrender to it but to *argue* with it — to take the eddies where they gave you free miles, to cross the main chute at the exact angle where the current helped your turn instead of shoving your bow sideways into a snag. Sable had learned it from her mother, who'd learned it from a man whose name nobody remembered, and every pilot on the lower Vessin had learned it from somebody or drowned trying.

She stood with her weight low, knees soft, one hand on the wheel and one on the throttle lever, reading the water the way other people read print. There: a smooth tongue of current sliding between two boils — deep water, fast, clean. There: a line of bubbles curving downstream from a half-submerged willow — snag just under the surface, give it ten yards of room. There, off the port bow, a patch of water so flat it looked like glass lying on top of the flood — dead calm in the middle of a torrent meant only one thing, and the thing it meant was a log the size of a church pew sitting six inches below, waiting to open a hull like a letter.

"Log, port side, shallow," she said, not loudly, and Doss repeated it aft, and Bracken eased the starboard fender out without being told twice.

Tam climbed to the wheelhouse and stood beside her, arms folded, watching her hands rather than the river. That was his habit too. He said he could tell more about how a run would go from watching the captain's knuckles than from watching the water.

"You slept three hours," he said.

"I slept enough."

"You slept three hours and signed a contract with a man who asks questions about sandbars." He kept his voice level, pitched under the engine note, for her ears alone. "I've been turning it over all night, Sable. Salvage men ask about sandbars. Rescue men ask about people."

"And what did Halloway ask about?"

"Sandbars." Tam's jaw worked. "Cutter Bend specifically. Which side silts up first in a dry year. Whether the old channel marker chain is still shackled to the rock or whether it dragged loose in the spring ice."

Sable said nothing for a while. The wheel spoke to her palms — a light shudder through the spokes as the stern found an eddy line and crossed it, the boat yawing two degrees before the current caught her again. She corrected without thinking about correcting, the way a person breathes without thinking about breathing.

"He also asked," she said finally, "whether I'd ever salvaged anything off Cutter Bend myself."

"And have you?"

"No. But I've looked." She glanced at him. "Forty years ago a revenue cutter went down up there. The *Vessel's Grace*. Carrying payroll and settlement funds out of the hill country when the money went missing and the cutter conveniently sank with it. Official story was pilot error in flood season. Unofficial story, in every dockside bar from here to the coast, is that the money never left the wreck, because the wreck sits in water too cold, too fast, and too full of snags for anyone to work her — except maybe two weeks a year, in a dry season, at the bottom of a low tide."

Tam was quiet a moment. "And this is a dry season."

"This is the driest season in eleven years." She let that sit between them like a third passenger. "So either Halloway is the most thorough rescue inspector I've ever met, or he's hunting something that isn't people."

"You could've walked away."

"I could've." The mist was burning off ahead of them, and the river widened, and somewhere beyond the next bend the water began to make a sound she knew better than her own heartbeat — a long, rising, grinding roar, like millstones fed with gravel. "But I didn't. And you know why."

"Debt," Tam said sourly.

"Restlessness," Sable said, which was truer, though she'd have denied it under questioning. "Triple pay, Tam. Triple. That's the mortgage paid, that's the hull re-plated, that's two years of not taking cargo I don't want for people I like less." She rolled the wheel hard over as a boil lifted the port quarter and swung the bow toward the snag line. "And besides — nobody else would take the job. Somebody has to find those surveyors, if they're alive. And if they're not alive, somebody has to find out why."

"That's the part I'm afraid of," Tam said. "The *why*."

Ahead, around the shoulder of a drowned bluff, the Widow's Tooth came into view.

* * *

Every river has a place it keeps for killing pilots, and the upper Vessin kept hers at Widow's Tooth — a fin of black granite rising out of the channel like a headstone, splitting the current into two chutes that argued with each other for a hundred yards below before reconciling themselves into a single boiling seam. The left chute was wider but shallower, its floor littered with boulders that showed only as brown shadows and white scars. The right was deeper but narrow, hardly more than a boat's length and a half, hemmed between the Tooth itself and a submerged ledge the old charts called the Coffin Lid, because of what happened to boats that touched it.

In ordinary water, the right chute was a technical run, the kind of thing a competent pilot did without spilling her tea. In flood water, with the river running four knots and standing waves building off the granite, it was a different animal entirely. The current compressed against the Tooth and rebounded off the Coffin Lid and set up a standing wave train that could lift a sixty-foot boat by the bow and drop her like a toy, and twice in Sable's lifetime it had done exactly that, and both times the boats had come apart on the rocks and both times the widows of the pilots had stood on this bank, which was how the rock got its name.

There was no left-chute option today. The dry season had pulled the left chute down to eighteen inches of water over gravel. It was the right chute or it was nothing, and behind them, thirty miles downstream, a government inspector was trusting her with six missing lives, and ahead of her, somewhere past the Tooth, were those same lives or the proof of their ending.

"Bracken," she called. "Life vests on everyone. Doss, get forward and stand by the anchor — if I call 'let go,' you drop it clear and pray the flukes bite, because if I'm calling it, we're already wrong."

"Aye, Captain." His voice cracked on the second word. He was nineteen. She remembered being nineteen and terrified on this same deck, and she remembered that terror being useful, and she let him keep it.

Tam clipped his own vest and took the engine telegraph, which was ritual more than necessity — she ran the throttles herself in water like this — but rituals held crews together the way stitching holds cloth.

Sable brought the *Kestrel's Debt* around the last of the bluff and lined her up.

The right chute looked, from this distance, like a throat. White water stood in it in broken rows, five feet high at the crest, and the roar filled the wheelhouse and made the window glass buzz in its frames. Through the gap she could see the far side — the pool below, calm and green and deceptively kind-looking, the kind of water that forgave you everything once you'd survived getting to it.

"Half ahead," she said, and Tam rang it, and the engine note rose.

She steered for the seam — the visible line where the two currents met and fought, the place where the water stood tallest and meanest, because counterintuitively the seam was the deepest water, the only water deep enough, and the only line through the gap that didn't put you on the granite or the ledge. You aimed at the worst-looking thing on the river and trusted it to be the best.

The bow entered the wave train and the world became vertical.

The first standing wave took them and threw the stem skyward and for one long, ringing second Sable was steering a boat that was more cliff than vessel, the propeller thrashing air, the whole hull groaning up its length, and then the crest passed under them and the bow dropped into the trough and green water came over the foredeck in a solid sheet and tore the fender basket off its cleats and carried it away like a leaf. The wheel bucked in her hands — a full hand's width of play, the rudder biting and losing and biting again — and she rode it, giving the river its inch and taking back her degree, her degree, always her degree, small corrections, small, *small*, because big corrections in standing waves were how boats ended up sideways and sideways was how boats ended up dead.

Second wave. Bigger. It hit them quartering, off the port bow, and shoved the stern toward the Coffin Lid, and Sable felt the geometry of it in her spine before she saw it — the drift rate, the closing distance, the black fin of the Tooth sliding closer on the right as the current pivoted them like a door on its hinge. Too close to the ledge. Too close. She spun the wheel hard to starboard and fed the throttle a half-turn and the engine roared and the stern checked, hung, and began to swing back —

— and then the third wave took them broadside.

Later, Tam would describe it as the boat standing on her side like a horse rearing, and that was nearly true. The hull went over until the rail kissed the flood, until the wheelhouse windows went dark with river, until Sable was hanging on the wheel with both hands and her boots braced against the deck slope and the whole sixty-foot weight of the *Kestrel's Debt* balanced on a knife-edge between recovery and capsize, and in that balance, in that held breath of a moment, she heard everything with terrible clarity: the engine screaming unloaded as a propeller broke surface, Doss shouting forward, the crack of a mooring cleat tearing out of the deck and flying aft like a bullet, and underneath it all, steady as a clock, the sound of Tam counting.

He counted in eights. Old habit, from the fishing boats. Eight seconds was what he'd once told her a capsize took, stem to keel-up, once she started. He was on four.

Sable did the only thing left. She slammed the wheel hard over to port — into the capsize, into the fall, against every instinct screaming the other way — and kicked the throttle to full, and the rudder, half-buried, half-biting, grabbed a fistful of water and hauled the stern around just as gravity made its decision. The deck came back. The rail lifted. Green water sheeted off in cataracts off both sides, and the *Kestrel's Debt* slammed down onto her keel with a concussion that knocked Doss off his feet forward and rattled every plate in the hull, and then they were through — through the last standing wave, through the gap, out into the wide green pool below the Tooth, wallowing, shipping water, alive.

Five seconds. Tam had reached five.

For a while nobody said anything. The engine settled back to a working rumble. Forward, Doss sat down hard on the foredeck hatch and put his face in his hands, and Bracken put a hand on the boy's shoulder and left it there. Water ran out of the scuppers in ropes. The torn cleat lay in the scuppers, threads of teak still attached, like a tooth pulled.

Sable realized her hands were shaking and made them stop, mostly.

"Well," Tam said at last. His voice came out sanded flat. "That's why."

"That's why what?"

"Why nobody else would take the job." He wiped spray off the chart table with his sleeve, a small domestic gesture absurd after what they'd just survived, and Sable loved him for it. "That wasn't running the flood. That was the river deciding, and us arguing the verdict."

"The river decided in our favor."

"The river doesn't decide in anyone's favor. It decides, and we survive the decision." He looked at her, and there was something in his face she hadn't seen in months — since the bad winter, since the bankruptcy scare, since the night he'd told her the *Kestrel's Debt* might be worth more as scrap than as a ship. Trust. Fresh-minted trust, the kind that can't be manufactured, only earned in water exactly like this. "Crew's yours, Captain. All the way to wherever this ends. Even if wherever this ends is something to do with sandbars and brass and whatever Halloway's really after."

"You believe me now."

"I believed you before. Now I *believe* you." He tapped the chart. "Widow's Reach runs easy for six miles. Then the country narrows and the charts go blank — literally blank, Sable, the survey office never mapped above the falls. After that it's your memory or nothing."

"It's my memory," Sable said. "It's always been my memory."

She took them on upriver, into the drowned timber and the narrowing hills, and the crew moved differently around her after the Tooth — not carefully, the way people move around someone fragile, but closely, the way sailors move around a mast in a storm, drawn to the thing that holds. Doss came to the wheelhouse unasked and brought her coffee strong enough to strip varnish. Bracken re-rigged the torn cleat with a spare from the locker and spliced the frayed mooring line without being told. Small things. The liturgy of a crew deciding, collectively and wordlessly, that they were in this to the end of it.

By late afternoon the hills closed in on both sides, steep and dark with cedar, and the river narrowed to a hundred yards, then eighty, and the current ran strong and clean between walls of stone, and the light went long and amber across the water, and it should have been beautiful, and it was, and Sable couldn't feel the beauty of it. Because she knew this stretch. She knew every bend of it, every eddy, every sunken log her father had shown her at fifteen with a cigarette shaking in his fingers, saying *remember this one, girl, remember where the river hides its teeth.*

And she knew what lay around the fifth bend above the falls.

The camp.

Halloway stood at the rail amidships as the light failed, his precise figure motionless against the railing, watching the banks slide past with an expression she couldn't read at this distance and didn't much want to. Watching the banks. Not the water. A rescue man watched the water, looking for signs — debris, oil slicks, a life jacket hung in the branches. Halloway watched the *banks*, the way a man watches for a landmark he's only seen on paper.

Sable brought the boat around the fourth bend as the dusk came down blue and cold, and there, on the gravel shelf where the fisher families used to beach their punts, stood the survey camp — tents still pitched, guy-lines taut, a cook fire burned down to a pale eye of ash, and a lantern hanging from the ridgepole of the mess tent, swaying gently in the evening wind.

No smoke. No movement. No voices.

But the lantern was lit.

Tam saw it at the same moment she did, and his hand found her arm. "Sable," he said quietly. "Who lights a lantern and walks away?"
