## 4. Gear and No Tracks

The river gave them the camp the way a hand opens to show what it has been holding.

Sable saw it first from the wheelhouse — a pale smudge through the screen of drowned willows on the north bank, canvas white against the green-black wall of the flood forest. She brought the *Kestrel* in slow, backing her stern into the current so the bow swung toward shore without ever quite touching it, and stood a long moment at the glass before she gave the order to make fast.

"Nobody steps off until I say," she told Tam. "And nobody touches anything."

Halloway was already at the rail, case in hand, his boots planted with the particular stillness of a man who has rehearsed this arrival. He had not slept, or if he had, he'd done it sitting up in his cabin with the lamp burning; there was a crease pressed into his cheek like a fold in paper. But his eyes were clear, and they went to the camp with an eagerness he made no effort to disguise, and that eagerness sat wrong in Sable's stomach like a swallowed hook.

Three days upstream from Corlow Landing. Two days past the last charted settlement. The Vessin here ran wide and brown and muscular, shouldering its way between bluffs furred with cedar, and the flood had turned the low ground into a country of mirrors — water lying flat among the trees, reflecting sky where there was no sky, depth where there was no bottom. A bad place to lose a boat. A worse place to lose six people.

They came ashore at a shelf of packed mud below the camp, Sable first with a line, then Tam, then Halloway, who stepped onto the bank with his good trousers already ruined and did not appear to notice. The climb was short. The willows opened. And there it was.

Six tents, pitched in two neat rows on the highest ground — which meant whoever chose the site had read the water marks on the trees and known exactly how high the flood would reach. Guy lines taut. Stakes driven square. A cook fly strung between two cedars, and beneath it a folding table bearing a lantern, a tin of matches gone soft with damp, a deck of cards mid-game, one hand dealt and abandoned.

The kettle hung over a fire ring of stacked stone, and when Sable crouched and held her palm above it, the iron still carried warmth. Not heat. Not the memory of morning's fire. Warmth — the specific, fading warmth of something taken off coals within the hour.

She looked up at Tam. Tam looked back, and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that the kettle wasn't already saying.

"That's not possible," Sable said quietly. "This camp's been abandoned four days by the government's own reckoning. Four days of rain on the third day. Nothing holds heat through that."

"Unless the reckoning is wrong." Halloway had come up beside her without sound, which was a skill she was beginning to resent. He crouched, studied the fire ring, lifted the kettle lid with a pencil rather than his fingers. Inside: water, half-evaporated, a skin of mineral scale at the line where it had boiled down. He did not taste it. He looked at it the way an accountant looks at a column that doesn't sum.

"When were your surveyors last heard from?" Sable asked.

"Nineteen days ago. Radio contact, scheduled, missed."

"Then either your clock or mine is broken, Inspector, because somebody made tea here this morning."

He rose, brushed nothing from his knees, and began to walk the camp. And Sable watched him do it, because watching Halloway work was like watching a man search a room for a body while claiming to look for a lost dog.

He started at the tents — but not by opening flaps and calling out, as any man hoping for survivors would. He circled each tent in turn, reading the ground around it, reading the guy lines, pausing longest at the fourth tent in the second row, the one whose flap hung open on darkness. Then the instruments. A theodolite stood on its tripod at the edge of the clearing, leveled, capped, protected from the rain by a stretched oilcloth tied down with proper knots — knots Sable recognized as river knots, boat knots, the kind you tie when you expect weather and want your gear to be there after it. A survey team does not cap its instrument and cover it if they are running for their lives. A survey team does not leave a theodolite worth more than most men's houses standing in the open unless they mean to come back before dark.

There was no sign they had come back. There was also, Sable realized as she made her own slower circuit, no sign they had left.

That was the thing that began to crawl up her spine and settle between her shoulder blades. She had grown up on banks like this one. She knew what ground said. Ground always said something — it said *here*, it said *gone*, it said *how many* and *how heavy* and *which way*. Six people cannot walk out of a camp without writing on the earth. Six people carrying packs write deep. Even six people wading write in the soft margins of the flood, in the silt laid down along every margin of dry land.

But the ground around the camp said nothing. The mud at the treeline was unbroken, a smooth grey page. The path down to the canoe landing — and there was a landing, planks and pilings, a proper little dock for their survey boats — showed prints going *up* from the water, old ones, weathered soft, but none going away. It was as if six men had walked into the tents and been erased.

"Inspector," she called. "Your survivors didn't leave this camp."

Halloway straightened from the open tent. "Meaning what?"

"Meaning there's no trail. No tracks heading out. None. You don't get six people leaving a place without six sets of prints, and you especially don't get it in mud like this. So either they're still here—" she let that hang, and watched him not flinch, which was itself an answer of a kind, "—or they left in a way that doesn't touch ground."

"Boats," Halloway said. "They could have taken to the water."

"They could have. Except their boats are still tied at the landing." She nodded downstream. Two survey skiffs sat on their painters, shipping rainwater, one with its oars shipped and the other with a coil of netting in the bilge. "And except that a man wading to a boat leaves prints at the waterline, and the waterline down there is clean as a chapel floor."

For the first time, something moved behind Halloway's eyes. Not fear. Calculation — fast, cold, and quickly shuttered, like a ledger closed at footsteps in the hall.

"You have a theory, Captain?"

"I have a wrongness," Sable said. "I've run salvage ten years. Wrongness has a shape. This one's shaped like a stage."

Tam appeared at her elbow with the quiet gravity he wore instead of argument. He'd been through the cook fly. "Cards were dealt for a hand," he said. "Four players. Nobody picked up their cards. Kettle's warm. Lantern's full. There's bread under cloth, and it's not moldy yet — three days old at most, in this damp." He looked at Sable. "Nobody walks away from fresh bread."

"No," Sable agreed. "Somebody walks away from a lie, though. Or arranges one."

Halloway had moved to the fourth tent again. He stood before it now with his head slightly bowed, and Sable came up beside him and looked past his shoulder into the gloom. Bedrolls, six of them across the six tents, all of them slept-in — dented, rumpled, honest-looking. But inside this tent, on the folding table, lay a leather-bound logbook, and beside it a radio handset, cord neatly coiled.

"The radio operator's station," Halloway said.

"It's the radio operator's station," Sable said, "and the radio's dead. Battery's pulled. Look." She touched the empty housing. "You don't pull the battery to abandon a camp. You pull the battery so nobody can call out. Or so nobody can call *in*."

Halloway took the logbook without asking. He opened it with the pencil again, turning pages, and Sable watched his eyes move — not reading, she thought, *checking*. Skimming for something specific, the way a man runs his finger down a manifest looking for one name. Whatever he found or didn't find, his face never changed, and when he closed the book and tucked it into his case, he did it with the tenderness of a man pocketing evidence rather than the urgency of a man hunting clues to the living.

"Captain Amberdale." He turned to her, and the morning light came through the canopy and broke on his face in small coins. "I'll want your crew searching the perimeter. Thoroughly. Especially the ground between the camp and Cutter Bend, downstream."

"Cutter Bend," Sable repeated. "That's the second time you've named that bend to me. First night aboard, over the contract. You asked about sandbars there — whether the channel shifts, how deep the water lies off the point. Now you want my crew walking a line straight to it." She folded her arms. "A man looking for six missing surveyors doesn't care about one particular bend in the river, Inspector. A man looking for something *in* the river does."

The silence lasted three heartbeats. Somewhere above them a heron unfolded off a dead cedar and went south on slow wings, and the flood breathed against the banks, patient as arithmetic.

"Six people are missing," Halloway said at last. "The department wants them found. How I conduct the finding is my business. Your business ends at the rail of your own boat."

"My business," Sable said, "is whatever's killing the value of my contract. Because here's my arithmetic, Inspector, and I'll thank you to check it. Triple pay means someone expects triple risk. Missing surveyors, warm kettles, dead radios, and a government man who knows the name of one bend better than the names of six men. That's not a rescue. That's a recovery. And recoveries mean there's something down there worth recovering."

She saw it land. Not guilt — Halloway was built without that ballast — but a recalculation, a shifting of weights, the look of a man deciding how much of the truth she could be permitted to carry and still be useful.

"You'll be paid regardless of what we find," he said. "Or don't find."

"Oh, I know it. That's the part that keeps me up nights." She turned away from him deliberately, dismissal dressed as duty, and raised her voice for the crew. "Right! Perimeter sweep, pairs, stay in sight of each other. Tam, take the downstream line toward the bend. Juno—" she caught herself, corrected; the cartographer was not hers, not yet, not aboard, "—nobody goes past sight of the masts. We're looking for tracks, gear, blood, boats. Anything the river wrote down."

The crew moved. Boots sucked and released in the mud. And Sable stayed where she was, alone in the abandoned camp with a warm kettle and a dead radio and a deck of cards waiting for four players who had never existed, or had never left, or had never been.

She picked up the dealt hand. Four fans of five cards, face down where the players had left them. She turned the nearest one over. Seven of spades, bent soft from handling, from a game that had been played long enough to wear the pasteboard. These were not new cards. This was not a staged table set in an afternoon for the benefit of a rescue party.

Which meant the camp was real. Which meant the men had lived here, worked here, drunk tea here — and then, somewhere between one hand of cards and the next, had been made to disappear so cleanly that even the mud forgot them.

Sable set the card back down in its fan, aligning the edges, and felt the itch of the miles go quiet in her for the first time in years. In its place settled something older and colder: the certainty of a woman standing in a story that had already been written, holding a page someone else wanted burned.

Downstream, faint through the trees, came the sound of Tam's voice, sharp with a note she had never heard him use on a search line. Not alarm. Recognition.

"Sable! Captain — you'll want to see this. Down at the water. There's brass in the mud."

* * *

The brass plate lay half-buried at the foot of the landing, where the flood had peeled back a lip of bank and exposed forty years of river sediment in cross-section, like the rings of a tree cut sideways. Tam knelt over it with his sleeves rolled, not touching it, just looking, and the look on his face was the look of a man reading his own death notice in a language he almost spoke.

It was a nameplate. Ship's brass, verdigrised green-black, letters once gilded now ghosted with gold. Sable hunkered beside him and read it aloud, because some things need air put behind them:

"*Vessel's Grace.*"

The name meant nothing to Halloway, who had arrived at a decorous distance and was pretending to examine the dock pilings. But it meant something to Tam, whose family had fished the lower Vessin for three generations, and it meant something to the river itself, which had kept the name all this time in the only vault it trusted.

"A revenue cutter," Tam said softly. "Forty years back. Went down in the flood of '81 with a hold full of money that never belonged to the government — settlement funds, collected from the families upriver, being run down to the capital. Never delivered. Never recovered. Officially, the *Grace* sank with everything aboard, and the case was closed, and the families were told the river ate their money." He wiped his mouth with the back of his hand. "Unofficially, every salvage man on this coast has dreamed about her. And every one of them who went looking came back with nothing, because nobody could find her, and the ones who got close stopped talking about it."

"And now a government survey team vanishes twenty yards from her grave," Sable said, "and an inspector arrives knowing the sandbars at Cutter Bend by heart, and the kettle's still warm." She stood. The mud let her go reluctantly. "This isn't a disappearance, Tam. It's a claim staked. Somebody found the *Grace*, and somebody else wanted the finding kept quiet, and six men became the price of the quiet."

Behind them, unhurried, Halloway closed his case. "Captain," he said, "we're losing daylight. I suggest we return to the boat."

Sable looked at the brass plate a moment longer — at the gilding worn to ghosts, at the green rot creeping in from the edges, at the small stamped serial number near the mounting hole that some clerk in the capital had entered in a ledger forty years ago and believed forgotten forever.

Nothing was forgotten. The river remembered everything. It had simply been waiting for someone willing to read.

"We're done here," she said, and turned for the boat, and did not tell Halloway that Tam's fingers, quick and invisible beneath the plate's shadow, had already found the second thing the flood had uncovered — a corner of oak, black as iron, and set into it, unmistakable even through forty years of silt, the seam of a hatch.
