## 5. What the River Keeps

Tam Voss had been diving rivers since he was eleven years old, and in all those years he had learned one truth above the others: the river gives nothing back that it does not first make you pay for.

He paid for this morning with cold. The water at Cutter Bend came down off the high country still carrying winter in it, and even through the greased canvas suit it found him, seeping into the seams at his wrists and neck until his skin went numb and then past numb into a kind of burning. He stood on the stern platform of the *Kestrel's Wake* while Sable cinched the weight belt around his hips, and he watched his own breath come out white over black water, and he thought about how easy it would be to say no. To say the vis is nil, captain, the current's wrong, let Halloway send down one of his own men if he wants the bottom looked at so badly.

He did not say it. That was the trouble with Tam Voss — he had never once in his life said the sensible thing when the other thing was more interesting.

"Bottom's gravel shelf, then silt," Sable said, her hands quick and certain on the straps. She didn't look at his face; she watched his gear, because that was how she loved people — by their equipment, by whether their air line was coiled right, by whether they'd live. "Shelf drops off maybe four fathoms at the outside bend. You feel the pull of the eddy under the cut bank, you don't fight it, you ride it back up. You hear me?"

"I hear you."

"Halloway watches from the bow." Her voice dropped, just enough. "I'll be watching the line. If I haul twice, you come up. No questions, no finishing what you're doing. You come up."

"Twice," Tam said. "I know the code, Sable. I helped invent it."

She looked at him then, and there was something in her face he couldn't name — not worry exactly, because Sable Amberdale didn't permit herself worry where the crew could see it, but something adjacent to it, something that lived next door and borrowed sugar. "Forty years ago a boat went down here," she said. "That's all this is. A survey team wanted soundings, Halloway wants confirmation of some obstruction to navigation. Gravel, old pilings, whatever. There's nothing down there but mud and old wood."

"That's what you told me last time," Tam said. "Before the last time."

"The last time there was a drowned ferry and a widow who needed her husband's strongbox." She tightened the last strap harder than she needed to. "This time there's nothing."

The lie was gentle and both of them knew it was a lie, and neither of them said so, because that was also how they loved each other — by agreeing to hold the same false map and call it true.

* * *

Going down was like going into the eye of a green storm.

The river at Cutter Bend ran tea-dark with tannin off the flooded timberlands, and three feet beneath the surface the daylight gave up entirely. Tam went down the shot line hand over hand, feeling the current peel away from his body as he sank below its reach, into the slow deep water where the river kept its secrets the way an old woman keeps letters — pressed flat, undisturbed, going soft.

His boots found the gravel shelf. He stood for a moment, letting his ears equalize, letting the dark settle against him like a coat. The water wasn't empty. Nothing in a river is ever empty. He could feel the presence of the cut bank looming above him to starboard, a wall of packed earth and roots; he could feel the faint cold breath of springs seeping up through the gravel; and somewhere ahead, out in the channel where the current ran hardest, there was a shape. Not a shape he could see. A shape he could feel, the way you feel a staircase in a dark house — by the way the space itself was organized around something solid.

He followed the shot line to its anchor, then left it, paying out the tether Sable held above, walking the shelf toward the bend.

The wreck came up on him gradually, which was the river's courtesy. First a rail, furred with forty years of silt and weed, rising out of the bottom like the spine of some buried animal. Then ribs — oak frames gone black as bog-wood but still stubbornly themselves, still holding the ghost of a hull together. Then the whole long length of her, lying on her beam ends in the scour hole the current had dug beneath the cut bank, half-buried, half-exposed, her deck now a wall facing downriver into the dark.

Tam hung in the water above her and felt the hair rise on his arms inside the suit.

He had grown up on stories of the *Vessel's Grace*. Every child on the lower river had. The revenue cutter that turned pirate, or was robbed — the story changed depending on who told it and what they'd drunk — carrying the settlement funds out of Corlow in the spring of the great flood year, and gone, all hands, somewhere in the upper reaches. Forty years of dredgers and diviners and men with maps bought from dying sailors had hunted her, and the river had kept her, and here she was, and Tam Voss was standing on her grave.

*Obstruction to navigation,* Halloway had said. *Gravel, old pilings.*

Halloway had asked about sandbars near Cutter Bend before they'd ever left Corlow Landing. Asked with his little notebook open, asking Sable to describe the bar at low water, the depth over the scour hole, whether the channel shifted after flood season. Asking like a man who already had answers and wanted to check them against a pilot's memory.

Tam pushed the thought down where he kept such thoughts, in the same place he kept the memory of his father's debts and the price of a new boiler, and got to work.

The survey instruments were gone — that was the first thing he noticed, or rather the absence of them. Halloway had sent him down with a grapple hook and a measuring tape and instructions to confirm the extent of the obstruction, and Tam had brought, without telling anyone, a small pry bar and a canvas bag, because Tam Voss had never once descended into a river without intending to bring something back up.

He worked along the deck-wall of the wreck. The planking was soft in places, firm in others; the silt lay over everything like gray snow. Fish spooked from the shadow of the hull in sudden silver flashes. His lamp made a small trembling circle in all that black, and inside the circle the ship emerged piece by piece: a capstan, crusted shut. A hawse pipe choked with gravel. A nameboard torn loose and lying in the scour sand, and Tam knelt over it and brushed the silt away with the side of his hand, gently, the way you brush hair from a sleeping face.

Letters. Gilt on blackened oak, most of the gilt flaked away, but the letters were cut deep enough to survive.

VESSEL'S GRACE.

He stayed kneeling there longer than he should have. The cold worked deeper into him. Somewhere far above, filtered through forty feet of tea-dark water, the sun was a rumor. And Tam Voss, who had signed onto the *Kestrel's Wake* to escape a debt and had stayed because the river was the only church he'd ever believed in, knelt on the deck of the most famous lost ship on the Vessin and understood, with total clarity, why Halloway had asked about sandbars.

Then the current shifted — the tide turning, even up here, even this far inland, the whole river breathing in and out on the moon's schedule — and the movement stirred the scour sand at the base of the hull, and something caught the light of his lamp.

Not wood. Not stone. Metal.

It was half-buried where the hull met the sand, wedged against a frame butt, and it took him ten minutes of careful digging — working blind by feel mostly, the silt clouding up around him until his lamp showed nothing but brown murk — to work it free. It was a plate. Brass, by the weight of it, though the river had taken every shine out of it and left it the color of an old bruise. About the size of his two palms together. Stamped, deeply, in letters meant to be read by inspectors and clerks and men with ledgers:

REVENUE CUTTER *VESSEL'S GRACE* — REGISTRY NO. 114 — CORLOW STATION.

And beneath that, smaller, the stamp of the Treasury office of the settlement authority, and a date forty years dead.

A registry plate. Bolted to the hull near the stern, by regulation, so that any vessel could be identified by anyone who found her — or by any government man who came looking for a government boat full of government money.

Tam put it in his canvas bag, and the bag inside his suit, against his chest, and began the long ascent along the tether, and all the way up he could feel the plate pressing cold against his sternum like a second heart.

* * *

Sable hauled him over the stern rail herself, which she only did when she was worried, and peeled his hood back with the rough tenderness of a woman who had pulled drowning men out of the Vessin before and intended to keep doing it.

"Anything?" Halloway stood at the bow rail with his notebook, and his voice was mild, and his eyes were not. Inspector Halloway had a face like a well-made door — handsome, closed, and giving away nothing of the room behind it.

"Obstruction confirmed," Tam said, teeth chattering. He let them chatter; chattering teeth excused a lot. "Old wreck, beam-on in the scour hole. Hull's mostly buried. Deck's standing on the outboard side. She's big — cutter-class, sixty, seventy foot. You'd want divers with lift bags and a week of good water to clear her, and the water won't give you a week. Season's dropping fast."

"What kind of vessel?" Halloway's pen hovered.

Tam shrugged, a small motion inside the suit. "Old one. Wood's gone soft as bread. Couldn't read anything on her."

It was the easiest lie he had ever told, and it frightened him how easy it was. He had never lied to an official before. He had never lied to anyone wearing a better coat than his, on principle — the principle being that men in good coats had all the advantages already and a poor man's word was the only property he owned outright. He felt the principle crack like ice underfoot, and he stepped across the crack anyway.

Halloway wrote something. "Depth over the highest point of the wreck?"

"Three fathom, maybe a bit less on the drop of the tide."

"And the survey team's instruments — you saw no sign of them? Lines, buoys, sounding equipment?"

"No sign of anything but the wreck." That much was true, and Tam gave the truth the way you give a beggar a coin — precisely, and watching where it landed.

Halloway closed the notebook. "Good. We'll run soundings from the surface tomorrow and be done with it." He turned away, and then turned back, and for a moment the door of his face opened a crack, and behind it Tam saw something patient and cold and very old. "You're a careful diver, Mr. Voss?"

"Careful enough."

"Be more careful." The door closed. Halloway went forward to where the mate of the survey launch was rigging the sounding line, and Sable came and stood beside Tam at the stern rail, and for a while neither of them said anything, and the river said plenty.

"You're holding your chest funny," she said at last, quietly, not looking at him.

"Cold."

"Mm." She looked out at the brown water sliding past the hull, at the bend ahead where the cut bank leaned over the channel like a listening man. "Forty years ago," she said, "a boat went down here."

"So you've said."

"There's nothing down there but mud and old wood." She clapped him once on the shoulder, hard enough to mean it, and went aft to log the dive, and Tam Voss stood at the rail with a brass plate hidden against his heart and understood that Sable Amberdale had just told him, in the only language they shared, that she knew he was lying, and that she was lying too, and that whatever was happening at Cutter Bend, the two of them were in it together and Halloway was not.

* * *

Night came down on the anchorage like a lid.

They moored in the lee of the point, the *Kestrel's Wake* riding easy on the slack water, and Halloway took the survey launch's cabin lantern into his own quarters and did not emerge. Tam sat in the galley with a mug of chicory going cold in his hands and waited until the watch changed and the deck was Sable's alone, and then he went to the fo'c'sle, and shut the door, and lit the smallest lamp they carried, and took the plate out of his sea bag.

Up close, under lamplight instead of river-light, it was worse. Better. Worse and better. The registry stamp was crisp as the day the engraver cut it. The Treasury mark sat beneath it like a seal on a letter. And bolt-holes at the corners — four of them, the brass around them bright where the bolts had torn free — told him the plate hadn't fallen off. It had been ripped off, or pried off, or blown off, and none of those things happened to a ship resting quietly in the mud.

Somebody had been down here before him. Recently. The silt-scour around the frame butt was fresh; he'd noticed it down there and hadn't let himself think what it meant. Somebody had come looking for the *Vessel's Grace*, and had found her, and had started taking her apart — and had stopped, or been stopped, or had gone away to fetch tools and men and a bigger plan.

And the survey team that vanished — tents pitched, kettle warm, instruments abandoned, no tracks leaving — had come upriver with sounding gear and government papers and an inspector's contract, and had anchored, by pure coincidence, at the one bend on two hundred miles of river where a legendary wreck lay in four fathoms of tea-dark water.

Tam turned the plate over in his hands. On the back, scratched into the brass with something sharp — a knife point, a nail — someone had cut a small mark. He angled it to the lamp. Not letters. Numbers. A figure, crude but deliberate:

**7**

Seven of something. Seven chests? Seven men? Seven shares?

Or seven people who had come upriver on a survey, of whom six were missing and one was hiding somewhere with a knife-scratch of knowledge and a fear you could smell through a bulkhead.

The lamp guttered. Outside, the river moved in the dark, enormous and unhurried, keeping what it kept. Tam wrapped the plate in oilcloth and pushed it down into the bottom of his sea bag, under the spare socks and the coil of tarred twine, and sat there a long moment with his hands hanging between his knees.

He thought about Halloway's triple pay. He thought about the debt on the boiler, the interest compounding like floodwater, the letters from the Corlow bank that Sable burned unopened because reading them wouldn't change the numbers. He thought about what a man could do with a share of forty years' stolen settlement funds — buy the *Wake* outright, buy the debt free, buy Sable free of the contract-chasing and the cargo-gouging and the slow indignity of being brilliant on a river that paid her in pennies.

He thought about the diver's rule, older than any of them: what the river gives you, you bring up honest, or you leave it down.

And he thought about the scratch. **7.** Cut in brass by somebody who'd known they might not get another chance to write anything down.

Above him, on deck, Sable's boots crossed the planking, steady as a clock. Tam put his palm flat on the sea bag, over the hidden plate, the way you'd rest a hand on a wound or a promise.

In the morning, Halloway would run his soundings and ask his questions, and Tam Voss would have to decide which lies to keep and which to spend — and somewhere downstream, he'd have sworn even then, though he couldn't have said why, the wind had shifted and was carrying the smell of coal smoke from boats that weren't theirs.
