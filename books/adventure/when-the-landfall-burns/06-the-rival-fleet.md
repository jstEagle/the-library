## 6. The Rival Fleet

The smoke came first.

Sable saw it while she stood at the rail of the *Kestrel's Wake*, watching the brown water of the Vessin slide past Cutter Bend in long, muscular coils. It was a thin gray smudge against the western haze, low and broad, the kind of smoke that came from a coal-fired boiler run hard — not a fisher's cookfire, not a burning field. She had spent twenty years reading smoke the way other people read weather, and this smoke said one thing with perfect clarity: engines, and more than one of them.

"Tam," she called down to the deck, where her first mate was coiling line with the methodical patience of a man who trusted rope more than people. "Smoke downstream. Tell me I'm wrong about what that is."

Tam straightened, shaded his eyes, and was quiet for a long moment. The river filled the silence — the deep throaty chuckle of water over the bar at the bend, the tick of the current against the hull, a heron lifting off the far bank on wings like folded canvas.

"I'm not going to tell you that," he said finally. "Because you'd only get angrier at me for lying."

Sable took out her glass. The brass was hot from sitting in the sun on the chart table, and it burned her palm pleasantly as she raised it. The image swam, steadied, sharpened.

Three boats.

They came up the reach in echelon, bow waves white and deliberate, each one riding low and heavy in the water. Not survey boats, not government launches with their crisp paint and their nervous young officers. These were working hulls, wide-beamed and scarred, their decks cluttered with the unmistakable architecture of salvage: A-frames fore and aft, winch drums fat with cable, compressor hoses coiled like sleeping snakes, and diving gear — she could see the copper helmets glinting even at this distance, racked in rows along the rails like a strange orchard of metal fruit.

She knew those boats. She had known them since before she had a license, since before she had a boat, since she was fourteen years old and scrubbing bilge on a mud scow out of Corlow for wages that wouldn't buy boots.

*Brannigan.*

The lead vessel flew no colors, but she didn't need to. There was a gull worked into her wheelhouse door in hammered tin, wings spread, and under it a name Sable could read even through the heat shimmer: *Maren's Pride*. Behind her came the *Salt Oath* and the *Tern*, and behind all three, trailing a wake of diesel stink and certainty, came the thing that made Sable's jaw tighten: the knowledge that Odile Brannigan did not bring divers and winches forty miles into the flooded upper Vessin on a rumor.

"You said Halloway kept asking about sandbars," Tam said. He had come up beside her without her hearing, which he could still do after all these years, moving on a deck the way other men moved through a doorway. "You said he asked about Cutter Bend twice."

"He asked three times." Sable lowered the glass. "Once about the sandbars, once about the depth over the old channel, once about whether the flood had shifted the wreck line." She hadn't told Tam that last part. She should have. The omission sat between them now like a third person at the rail.

"And you didn't think that was worth mentioning."

"I thought it was worth mentioning when I'd decided what it meant." She handed him the glass. "Now we both get to decide together. Look at them. That's not a rescue outfit. That's not even a survey outfit. That's a fleet rigged for heavy lift, and they're coming upriver at flood tide like they've got an invitation."

Tam studied the boats through the glass for a long time. When he lowered it, his face had gone carefully blank, which was how Sable knew he was worried; Tam wore his worry the way other men wore good clothes, buttoned up and out of sight.

"That's Odile Brannigan's fleet," he said. "All three. That's everything she's got."

"Everything she's got," Sable agreed. "Which means whatever's down there in this reach, she thinks it's worth mortgaging the rest of her season for. Maybe the rest of her life." She turned and looked upstream, where the river narrowed between bluffs the color of old rust, where the flood water ran fast and green-brown and full of the dry season's impatience. Somewhere up there, a survey team was missing. Somewhere up there, Inspector Halloway sat in her cabin pretending to read reports while he waited for her to carry him toward something he would not name. And somewhere up there, apparently, was enough sunken money to pull three salvage boats off their contracts and send them running the same dying channel she was running.

"The race just became real," she said quietly.

"It was always real," Tam said. "You just didn't want to see it."

* * *

They made mooring at the leeward side of Gravel Hook, a spit of shingle and dead willow that broke the current just enough to let a boat lie quiet. Sable put the *Wake* there deliberately — not because she needed the rest, though the engine had been running hot since Widow's Tooth and deserved the mercy, but because the Hook commanded a clear view down the reach, and if the Brannigan fleet was going to pass her, she intended to watch them do it from a position of comfort rather than surprise.

Halloway emerged from the cabin as the anchor went down. He had changed shirts again — the man traveled with more linen than a bishop — and he looked out at the shingle and the willows and the brown water with the expression of a man reading a document written in a language he refused to learn.

"Why are we stopping?"

"Engine needs looking at," Sable said. "And the channel ahead narrows. You don't run the Narrows on a falling tide unless you have to, and I don't have to until morning."

"I understood the schedule was aggressive."

"The schedule is alive," Sable said. "That's better than aggressive. Aggressive gets you drowned." She nodded downstream. "Besides. Company's coming."

Halloway followed her gesture. His eyes found the smoke, then the boats beneath it, and Sable watched something move across his face — quick, controlled, gone almost before it arrived, but not quite gone before she caught it. Recognition. Not surprise. He knew who they were. He might even, she thought, have known they were coming.

"Salvage men?" he asked.

"Salvage woman," Sable said. "Odile Brannigan. Her family's been pulling things out of this river since before your government drew its first map of it. If there's anything worth finding between here and the headwaters, she'll find it or die trying, and she'll invoice whoever's left standing."

"Should we be concerned?"

"That depends," Sable said, "on what we're doing here." She held his gaze, level and pleasant, and gave him nothing else. Halloway returned the look with the flat courtesy of a man who had practiced being unreadable in front of mirrors, and went back inside, and Sable stood at the rail and thought: *he sent for them.* Or someone did. Either way, the arithmetic had changed. Whatever game was being played on this river, she was no longer sure she was a player. She might be a piece.

The thought sat in her chest like swallowed ballast.

* * *

The fleet came up on them an hour before dusk, when the light went long and gold and the river turned to hammered bronze. Sable heard them before she saw them clearly — the deep uneven throb of three engines not quite in step, a sound she had fallen asleep to as a child in the Brannigan crew bunkhouse at Corlow, back when her mother ran galley for Odile's father and the whole world smelled of coal smoke and tar.

*Maren's Pride* slowed as she came abeam, close enough that Sable could see the rust streaks under her ports, the new welds along her guardrail, the two dories lashed amidships. Divers lined her rail. Six of them, maybe eight, in canvas jackets, watching the *Kestrel's Wake* with the frank professional curiosity of men appraising another man's gear.

Then the wheelhouse door opened, and Odile Brannigan stepped out onto her own deck, and the river seemed briefly to arrange itself around her, the way it does around certain people, as if acknowledging seniority.

She was fifty now, maybe fifty-two, broad as a barge and just as hard to move, with iron-gray hair cut short against her skull and hands that looked like they had been carved from teak by someone in a hurry. She wore a captain's coat too warm for the season because she had worn it every day for thirty years and would wear it into the ground. She put one hand on her rail and looked across the hundred feet of sliding water at Sable Amberdale, and she smiled, and the smile was the worst part, because it was genuinely glad.

"Sable Amberdale," Odile called, in a voice built for carrying over engines. "As I live and breathe and pay harbor fees. They told me at Corlow someone fool enough to take the survey contract, and I said, well, there's only one fool on this river with the draft for it, so either it's her or it's nobody."

"Hello, Odile." Sable kept her voice light. "You're a long way from your home water."

"So are you." Odile gestured upstream with her chin. "Flooded reach, falling season, government contract. Your mother would have boxed your ears."

"My mother boxed my ears for less interesting reasons."

There was laughter from the *Pride*'s rail — real laughter, unforced, and Sable felt it land in her chest with an ache she hadn't expected. Half those faces she knew. Big Ferro with the burned forearm. Young Pell's cousin, the quiet one who sang off-key. People who had pulled her out of the water when she was nineteen and green and stupid. People whose funerals she would someday attend, or whose funerals would someday attend her, because that was the shape of a life on the river and everyone aboard both boats knew it.

"Come aboard for a drink," Odile said. "Or I'll come to you. Either way, we should talk, you and I, before the dark comes down and we start pretending we're strangers."

"We're not strangers," Sable said.

"No," Odile agreed, and for a moment the gladness in her face went complicated, shadowed, the way a clear pool goes complicated when a cloud crosses it. "We never were. That's rather the problem, isn't it."

* * *

They took rum in the *Kestrel's Wake*'s cabin, because Sable would not host the meeting on the *Pride* and Odile, to her credit, didn't push it. Tam poured three glasses and then found urgent business on deck, which was his way of saying he wanted no part of what the two captains were about to say to each other, and also his way of staying within earshot of the skylight, and Sable let him do both.

Odile drank half her glass in one motion and set it down and looked around the cabin — at the charts weighted with brass, at the repaired seam in the bulkhead, at the small shrine of chipped porcelain above the bunk where Sable's mother's river-charm hung on a nail.

"You keep her boat lucky," Odile said.

"She keeps herself lucky. I just keep her floating."

"Mm." Odile turned the glass by its rim. Outside, the light was failing; the *Pride*'s riding lamps had come on, yellow smears in the dusk, and the river talked to itself against the hull in the low continuous voice it used after dark. "I'll say the plain thing, Sable, because we're too old and too tired for the dance. What's down this reach? What did Halloway hire you to find?"

"A missing survey team."

"What's really down this reach?"

Sable turned her glass. "You tell me. Three boats, full dive complement, heavy-lift rig, forty miles past the last honest chart. You don't rig like that for bodies, Odile. Bodies don't need winches."

Odile smiled thinly. "No. Bodies don't need winches." She leaned back, and the lamplight caught the seams of her face, and for a moment she looked every year of her age and every mile of her river. "A man came to Corlow six weeks ago. Government man, but not one of yours — different office, quieter coat. Asked me what I knew about the upper Vessin. Asked me about the old wreck records. Forty-year-old records, Sable. Revenue service. A cutter lost in the flood of the spring year with — " she paused, and chose her words with visible care — "with a significant cargo, and no proper recovery ever mounted, because the flood buried her and the government wrote her off and everyone agreed to forget."

Sable kept her face still. Inside, pieces were moving, clicking, settling into new positions with small cold sounds. *Cutter Bend. The sandbars near Cutter Bend. The depth over the old channel.* Halloway's questions had not been about finding survivors at all. They had been about finding a wreck.

"And you told him?" she said.

"I told him the truth, which is that the upper reach in flood season eats salvage fleets whole, and I'd want triple rates and a signed indemnity before I took a winch up there." Odile's mouth twisted. "He paid the triple rates. He signed the indemnity. So here I am." She looked up, and her eyes were sharp and direct and, underneath the sharpness, genuinely sorry. "Here we both are. Running the same dying channel toward the same hole in the river, you on a government contract and me on a government contract, and only one of us I suspect knows what the cargo actually is."

"Do you know what it is?"

"I know what forty-year-old revenue records say a lost cutter was carrying when she went down." Odile finished her rum. "And I know what that much money, sitting in a hold in the dark for forty years, does to people. It makes liars of inspectors. It makes corpses of divers. It made a liar of me once, and it very nearly made a corpse of you, if you recall."

Sable recalled. She recalled it with a clarity that had not dulled in eleven years: the smuggling run she'd let Odile take the blame for, the customs court at Corlow, Odile standing in front of the magistrate with her chin up and taking the fine and the black mark on her record that had cost her two seasons of contracts, while Sable stood at the back of the room and said nothing. She had told herself a hundred stories about why — that the fine would ruin her and not Odile, that Odile's family could absorb it, that the truth would serve no one. All of the stories were lies, and both of them knew it, and neither of them had ever said so aloud, and the debt had sat between them ever since like a stone in a shoe.

"I remember," Sable said quietly.

"I know you do." Odile rose, joints cracking, and settled her coat. At the cabin door she paused, hand on the frame, and did not turn around. "One more thing, girl, and then I'll leave you to your charts. My client gave me a description of a rival pilot he wanted me to watch for. Your boat, your build, your route. Which means somebody told him about you before you ever signed your contract — somebody who knew exactly where you'd be running and when." She glanced back over her shoulder, and the lamplight made her eyes unreadable. "So whatever Halloway's paying you, ask yourself what he's paying you *for*. Because men like that don't hire pilots, Sable. They hire cover."

She stepped out into the dark, and her boots went heavy down the deck, and a moment later the *Pride*'s launch carried her back across the black water to her own lights.

Sable sat alone in the cabin for a long time. On the chart table, the upper reach lay open under its brass weights, the river drawn in faded ink, and at the bend marked *Cutter Bend* in her own handwriting there was a small penciled cross she had made the day Halloway asked his third question, meaning nothing by it, meaning only *here*.

She picked up a pen. Under the cross, in letters small and hard, she wrote: *What's in the hold?*

Outside, the river answered with its dark voice, and gave nothing away.
