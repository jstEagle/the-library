## 7. Halloway's Ledger

The rain came at dusk, a soft grey curtain that turned the river to hammered tin and drove the crew below decks. Sable stood in the wheelhouse long after she needed to, watching the water work itself into small furious knots around the pilings of the mooring stakes. They had tied up at a fisher's landing called Marn's Reach — three rotting platforms, one lamp burning yellow behind fogged glass, and nothing else for twenty miles in any direction. A good place to disappear. She was beginning to understand that the whole upper Vessin was a good place to disappear.

Halloway had eaten his supper alone in his cabin, as he did every night, and had turned in with the lamp trimmed low. Sable had watched the light gutter out through the gap under his door the way she'd watch a sandbar shift — patiently, without blinking.

Tam found her at the rail. He didn't say anything at first. He just leaned beside her, forearms on the wet wood, and let the rain speak for both of them.

"You're going to do it," he said finally.

"I'm going to look."

"That's what you said about the Brannigan girl's strongbox, and we lost a season over it."

"We learned something worth a season." She turned her collar up. "He asked me about Cutter Bend before we ever signed, Tam. Not Widow's Tooth, not the Devil's Elbow — the two places that kill pilots. He asked about the bend where a revenue cutter went down forty years ago. You don't ask that question if you're looking for six lost surveyors. You ask it if you're looking for something the river swallowed."

"Or if you read the same histories I did. There's a marker buoy charted there. Old wreck, hazard to navigation. Any inspector would—"

"At midnight? By name?" She shook her head. "He said *Cutter Bend* like a man says a woman's name he's been thinking about too long."

Tam exhaled through his nose, a long plume of breath that the rain ate instantly. "If you go in there and find nothing, you've insulted a government man on his own boat-charter, and we're three days upriver from anyone who cares about us."

"And if I find something?"

"Then I'd rather know it before the water gets any lower," he admitted. "Go. Take the lantern with the shuttered glass. And Sable—" he caught her sleeve, "—leave everything exactly how it lies. Men like him count their papers the way misers count coins. If one is out of place, he'll feel it in his sleep."

She squeezed his wrist once and went over the side of the wheelhouse into the rain.

* * *

Halloway's cabin sat amidships, a converted cargo bay the width of a coffin and twice as long, paneled in salvaged mahogany that some previous owner had stained the color of dried blood. Sable had been in it exactly once, to deliver his tea, and she had spent those four seconds cataloguing the room the way she catalogued a channel: where the deep water ran, where the snags waited.

The lock on his desk was brass, old-fashioned, a lever tumbler of the kind used on customs houses and church poor-boxes. It would have stopped a thief. It did not stop a salvage captain, because salvage captains carried tension wrenches and pick guns the way other women carried hairpins, and because Sable Amberdale had spent eleven years learning that every lock on the river was a door somebody wanted kept shut, which meant it was a door worth opening.

It gave with a click no louder than a raindrop.

Inside, the desk was a study in government order. Requisition forms, squared and clipped. A leather folder of the survey team's personnel files — six photographs, six names, six sets of fingerprints on cards that smelled faintly of ink. She held each photograph up to the shuttered light of her lantern. Aldous Vane, team leader, a lean face with the patient confidence of a man who had never once been told no by anything softer than weather. The radio operator, a round-faced boy called Merrick Stiles who looked like he still wrote home to his mother. Four others. She memorized them all, faces and names together, the way she memorized channel markers. Somewhere upstream these people were either dead or hiding, and either way someone aboard this boat knew more than he was saying.

Beneath the files, a folio sealed with red wax.

She hesitated then — truly hesitated, thumb pressed against the wax, feeling its small brittle resistance. Tam's voice in her ear: *leave everything how it lies.* But wax seals were made to be broken by the right person and read by the wrong one; that was half their purpose in this world. She cracked it along the fold and unfolded the pages inside.

The handwriting was not Halloway's. It was finer, older, the hand of someone who had learned penmanship when ink cost money. The letterhead bore the seal of the Colonial Revenue Office, and the date was nine days before the survey team had vanished.

*To Inspector Halloway, per your requisition of the pilot vessel Amberdale's charter:*

*Your primary objective remains the recovery of assets from the wreck identified as the revenue cutter Vessel's Grace, lost at or near the reach known locally as Cutter Bend. Estimated value of specie and registered notes: forty-one thousand crowns, being settlement funds embezzled from the Corlow Land Trust in the year '31 by persons unknown, presumed lost with the vessel.*

*Recovery is to be conducted quietly. No salvage claims are to be filed. No local assistance beyond the contracted pilot is authorized.*

*Regarding the survey party under Aldous Vane: should they be recovered alive, they are to be debriefed and released with standard severance. Should they not be recovered, the department will require confirmation of death sufficient to close the file. Drowning is the presumptive cause given seasonal conditions. A report listing all six as drowned will be acceptable and final.*

*Drowned,* Sable read again. *Acceptable and final.* As though the word were a stamp waiting for an ink pad. As though six lives could be closed out of a ledger the way a bad debt was written off.

But it was the last paragraph that turned her blood to river-cold.

*Be advised: intelligence received via the Corlow office suggests the survey's disappearance may not be accidental. The department does not discount the possibility that the party dispersed deliberately, under direction of its own leadership, to conduct private salvage of the Grace's cargo. Should this prove true, the individuals concerned are to be treated not as casualties but as thieves of government property, and the matter handled accordingly — though discretion in this regard outweighs all other considerations, including prosecution. The department's interest is the funds. Let the river keep whatever else it has taken.*

She sat back on her heels in the dark cabin and let the rain fill the silence.

So Halloway had known. Before they ever signed the contract, before he'd asked his soft-voiced questions about sandbars, he had suspected — no, he had nearly *known* — that Aldous Vane had walked his team into the wilderness and erased them, and he had sailed upriver anyway with a pilot and a crew as cover, hunting forty-one thousand crowns while preparing to write *drowned* across six names with a steady hand.

And the surveyors themselves — were they dead? Had Vane killed them, or paid them, or scattered them into the back channels with promises? The letter didn't care. That was the obscenity of it. The letter didn't care whether they breathed. The department's interest was the funds. Everything else was weather.

Sable folded the pages along their original creases, pressed the wax fragments back against the paper — a lie told with her thumbs, and an imperfect one — and slid the folio back beneath the personnel files. Then she paused, because lying beneath the folio, half-hidden by its corner, was a slim black notebook she hadn't seen on her first pass.

A ledger. Columns in Halloway's own cramped hand: dates, soundings, tide tables copied from her own charts — and running down the right margin, in entries that grew more frequent as the pages turned, a second column of figures that had nothing to do with depth. Numbers of crowns. Projections. *One-third share upon quiet recovery,* one entry read. *Negotiable upward if Vane eliminated as claimant.*

*Negotiable upward if Vane eliminated as claimant.*

She read it three times. The rain drummed. Somewhere aft, the boat's old timbers ticked and settled, the way boats talk in their sleep.

Halloway wasn't the department's hound. He was a partner. Or he intended to be — playing both ends, ready to arrest Vane or bury him depending on which man surfaced holding the stronger position. *Discretion outweighs all other considerations, including prosecution.* He had taken that line of his orders and built a career-sized hole through it.

Sable closed the ledger, returned it precisely to its angle beneath the folio, relocked the desk, and stood a moment in the dark with her hand flat on the mahogany, steadying herself as much as confirming the lock.

Then she heard the floorboard creak in the passage outside.

* * *

She killed the lantern and stepped into the shadow behind the cabin's hanging oilskin coat — Halloway's, heavy as a sail, smelling of camphor and government wool — as the door swung inward.

Not Halloway. Tam, moving low, shuttered lamp in hand, his face tight with a worry he'd never have shown on deck. He crossed to the desk, saw the faint scratch her pick had left in the brass plate, and put his palm over it as though he could heal the metal by touch.

"Sable," he whispered.

"Here." She slid out from the coats. "How long have you been standing there?"

"Since you cracked the seal. Someone had to watch the passage." He set the lamp down unlit. "Well?"

She told him. All of it — the letter, the drowned-and-final, the ledger, the share, the words *eliminated as claimant*. She spoke fast and low, and she watched his face go through the same progression hers must have worn: disbelief, then the sick settling-in of a man who has sounded a channel and found it deeper than his charts allow.

"The radio operator," Tam said at last. "Merrick Stiles. The boy in the photograph. If Vane staged the vanishing, the first thing you erase is the man who can call Corlow." He rubbed his jaw. "Six names, and Halloway's already chosen the word for all of them. And Vane — if he's alive, he's upstream sitting on forty-one thousand crowns, and the only people who know he's a thief are a government man who wants to be his partner and us."

"Not anymore," Sable said. "Now Juno knows too."

Tam looked at her sharply. "Juno?"

"The cartographer. Fifth day out, Halloway mentioned her — said she was the one who plotted their camps, so she'd know the country best. Which means if anyone slipped away from Vane's little disappearing act, it was her." Sable had been turning it over since the personnel file: a woman whose whole trade was knowing where things were, on a river where six people had ceased to be anywhere at all. "Fisher stilt-houses dot the banks above here. If she's breathing, she's holed up in one of them, and she's been listening to our engines come upriver for two days wondering if we're Vane's men coming back to finish it."

"That's a guess dressed as a plan."

"It's the only kind I have." She took up her lantern. "Tomorrow we make slow time. Soundings every furlong, engine trouble, whatever it takes. I want eyes on every stilt-house between here and Cutter Bend, and I want to be the one looking."

"And Halloway?"

Sable glanced back at the locked desk, at the wax she'd imperfectly mended, at the word *drowned* waiting in its fine old handwriting for six signatures it would never need to earn honestly.

"Halloway thinks he's the smartest man on this river," she said. "Let him keep thinking it a little longer. A pilot learns pretty quick that the most dangerous thing in any channel isn't the rock you can see." She opened the door onto the rain. "It's the one that lets you run aground nice and gentle, and waits to see what you do next."

Out on the black water, far upstream past the last of Marn's Reach lamplight, thunder muttered — or something that wasn't thunder, a flat rolling boom that came again, and again, spaced like deliberate work.

Tam caught her arm. "That's blasting," he said. "Somebody's blowing rock up there."

Sable stood in the rain and listened, and thought of dynamite, and of a hold full of crowns, and of a man named Vane who had already erased six people to reach it.

"No," she said quietly. "Somebody's blowing open a door."
