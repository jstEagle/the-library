## 8. The Sixth Man

The stilt-house leaned over the water like an old man over a well, and Juno Pell had been living inside its creak for nine days.

She had learned the house's language in that time — the groan of the pilings when the tide turned, the tick of the tin roof cooling after sundown, the particular knock the current made against the cross-braces at slack water, soft as knuckles on a door. She had learned it because there was nothing else to learn. Old Maren, the fisherwoman who kept the place, spoke perhaps forty words a day and spent most of them on fish. The radio was a box of rusted parts that hadn't carried a signal since before Juno was born. And the river below the floorboards went about its business, brown and patient, carrying everything away.

Juno sat at the window with her maps spread across her knees and did what cartographers do when they cannot draw: she measured her fear.

Nine days ago it had been the size of the whole sky. Eight days ago, the size of the river. Now, if she was honest, it had shrunk to something closer to the size of her own two hands — which frightened her more than any of it, because hands could hold things, and hers were beginning to hold something that felt dangerously like a plan.

Below her, Maren gutted catfish on the platform and threw the offal to the water, where the white shapes rose to meet it without hurry, as if they'd been paid to attend.

"You're drawing again," Maren said, without looking up.

"Thinking."

"Thinking looks like drawing, on you."

Juno looked down at the paper. Sure enough, her pencil had been moving. Not the survey charts — those were gone, left behind in the camp along with everything else Aldous Vane had wanted found. This was from memory: the upper reach above Cutter Bend, the channel braiding through the drowned forest, the sandbars drawn as she'd sounded them herself, weeks ago, before everything. A pilot's chart. A chart for someone running water no one else would run.

She didn't know yet who that someone would be. She only knew that whoever came upriver looking for six missing surveyors would need it, and that she was the only person alive who could give it to them.

* * *

It had rained the night the team vanished, and Juno had lain awake in her tent listening to it hammer the canvas, cataloguing sounds the way she catalogued elevations. Rain on canvas. Rain on leaves. The generator's uneven cough. Boots in mud.

Boots in mud, at two in the morning, moving *away* from camp.

Six sets of them, or near enough — she'd counted by rhythm rather than number, half-asleep, and told herself in the morning she'd dreamed it. That was the trick of it, she understood now. Vane had built the whole thing out of moments like that, moments a reasonable person would talk themselves out of. The boats gone from the bank. The tents still pitched, kettles still warm, instruments laid out neat as a shop display — because you don't pack instruments if you're leaving in a hurry, and you don't leave them if you're never coming back, so Vane had made sure they'd be found exactly as they were, a stage set for whatever searchers the government sent. No footprints leading out, because the rain had been allowed four hours to erase them. Six people swallowed whole by a river that swallows nothing quietly.

And one thing missing that shouldn't have been missing, and one thing present that should not have been there.

The missing thing was Desh. Desh Okonkwo, their radio operator, twenty-six years old, who hummed when he worked and kept a tin of sweets in his kit and had told Juno on the third night upriver that the stars here were wrong, too low, like they were leaning in to listen. His set was gone from the comms tent. His bedroll was gone. But his boots were still under the flap, lined up side by side, laces tucked in the way he always tucked them, and Desh did not walk anywhere barefoot through mud, not for anything short of fire.

The present thing was Vane's strongbox, which Juno had seen him carry into the treeline the night before the disappearance, and which had come back lighter.

She had pieced it together the way she pieced together coastlines — from fragments, soundings, the shape of the gaps. Vane wasn't lost. Vane had *left*, taking five of the team with him by some story she couldn't yet imagine, and he had left the camp dressed as a tragedy because a tragedy brought government boats, and government boats brought sonar, and sonar over Cutter Bend would find exactly what Vane already knew was lying there in forty feet of brown water: the *Vessel's Grace*.

Everyone on the survey had heard the stories around Corlow's fires. Forty years back, the revenue cutter had gone down in the flood season with a season's settlement funds aboard — taxes and land payments from three hundred families who had crossed the world to build something on this river, money meant for roads and mills and the school at Corlow Landing, all of it lost in one night of high water and bad luck, or so the official record said. Some said the crew scuttled her themselves to cover an embezzlement. Some said pirates. The river kept its version to itself.

But Vane had been asking questions since the first week. Quiet ones, at night, over the map table, his finger resting on Cutter Bend the way a man rests his hand on a locked door. Why had the old charts marked a hazard there that matched no shoal? Why did the 1880s survey note "iron obstruction, submerged" and then never follow it up? He'd asked Juno to pull the archive sheets twice, and both times he'd read them alone in his tent, and both times he'd come out with that expression she had mistaken, then, for scholarly satisfaction.

She knew better now. It had been the look of a man counting.

* * *

On the tenth day, the boy came.

Maren's grandson — she called him Pip but Juno suspected it was short for nothing at all — poled his skiff up through the drowned willows at mid-morning and climbed the ladder with a stringer of perch and a look on his face that made Juno's stomach drop before he said a word.

"Smoke," he said. "Downstream past the Narrows. Three boats, big ones, salvage rigging. Brannigan colors." He paused, enjoying the importance of his news the way boys do. "And a day ahead of them, a little boat running hard. Government pennant. Inspector's launch."

Juno stood so fast her knee cracked against the map chest, and for a moment the whole room tilted — the floorboards, the river, the forty years of sunken money, all of it wheeling around her like birds.

"How far?"

"Inspector's maybe a tide behind the salvage fleet. Salvage fleet's maybe two tides from the Bend." Pip frowned. "There's another thing. Man came through the fish camps yesterday asking after strangers. Paid silver for word of a woman traveling alone. Wore a plain coat but had soldier's boots. Maren told him nothing. Told me to tell you the north cut floods at evening, if I needed to say anything, which I don't, because there's nothing to say."

Maren, gutting fish below, said nothing at all, which was her way of saying a great deal.

Vane's men. Which meant Vane knew someone had slipped the net — or feared it — and was sweeping the banks. Which meant the clock Juno had been living inside had just started ticking audibly.

"Thank you," she said to Pip, and then, because he was fourteen and deserved honesty more than comfort: "You should go back down now."

He went. Juno stood at the window a long time, watching the river carry the afternoon seaward, and let the fear finish shrinking.

Because here was the arithmetic she'd been avoiding for ten days, laid out flat at last like a chart in good light:

If she stayed hidden, she stayed alive, probably. Maren would keep her; the fish camps kept their own counsel; winter would come eventually and swallow the whole question. And Vane would raise the *Grace* with Brannigan's winches or without them, launder the money through whatever channels he'd built in twenty years of government service, and three hundred families would go on believing their grandparents' fortune had drowned honestly. And Desh would stay dead, unavenged, unmourned by anyone but her, because the official record would say he drowned with the rest of them, poor souls, terrible business, the river keeps what it keeps.

Or she could go down to that river and put herself between Vane and the truth, armed with nothing but a pencil, a memory, and a chart only she could draw.

Her hands, she noticed, had stopped shaking somewhere around day seven. She looked at them now — ink-stained, rope-scarred from the survey work, steady on the windowsill — and understood that the decision had already been made somewhere below thought, the way a river decides its course long before the map shows it.

* * *

That evening Maren cooked the perch with wild pepper and the two of them ate on the platform while the light died copper-colored over the water. Juno laid her memory-chart on the boards between them, weighted at the corners with shells.

"I need to reach the salvage fleet," she said. "Or the inspector's launch. Whichever comes first."

Maren chewed. Swallowed. Looked at the chart longer than she'd looked at anything in Juno's presence, tracing the braided channel above Cutter Bend with one fish-scale-bright fingernail.

"My father ran cargo up there," she said at last. "Before the flood took the trade. Nobody runs it now. Water's too mean and getting meaner every dry season." Her eyes came up. "You're wanting to hand this to whoever's fool enough to try."

"I'm wanting to hand it to whoever deserves to win."

"And which is that?"

"The one who isn't Vane," Juno said. "I'll know them when I see them. Pilots are honest people. It's the job. You can't lie to a river and live, so the good ones stop trying it anywhere else."

Something moved through the old woman's face that might have been a smile in a younger country. She rose, joints cracking like the house itself, and went inside, and came back with a bundle wrapped in oilcloth: a skiff paddle, a jar of pitch, and a signal lamp, old military make, its lens clouded but whole.

"Lamp still works if the wick's fresh," Maren said. "North cut floods at evening. You can be downstream by tomorrow's dark if the water doesn't argue." She set the bundle down and fixed Juno with a look that had ended arguments for sixty years. "I'm not telling you it's foolish. You know it's foolish. I'm telling you my father used to say the river gives back to people who carry true things. So carry it true."

* * *

Juno worked through the dark hours by lamplight, finishing the chart.

She drew the channel as she'd sounded it: the safe line through the Narrows, the false deep at Widow's Tooth that had nearly taken their own boat's keel, the hidden cut through the drowned forest that appeared on no government sheet — she'd found it herself, hunting a benchmark, and had shown it to no one, not even Vane. *Especially* not Vane. At the bottom corner, where a cartographer signs her work, she wrote instead: *For the pilot. The sixth surveyor lives. Ask for Juno Pell.*

Then, beneath the chart, on a separate sheet, she wrote the other thing — the thing she had written once before and burned, and now wrote again because burning it had felt like letting Desh drown twice.

*Desh Okonkwo discovered Vane's correspondence with a private salvage broker. On the last night, Vane sent Desh alone to check the upstream gauge. Desh did not check the gauge. I heard the single splash. I did not see it. I have been ashamed of that for ten days and I am done being ashamed. Aldous Vane killed him for a strongbox of other people's money, and he will kill again — the diver who talks, the rival who guesses, the witness who surfaces. I am the witness. Come armed. Come careful. Come anyway.*

She signed it. Folded it into the oilcloth with the chart.

At first light the north cut began to fill, brown water climbing the willow trunks, and Maren held the skiff steady against the platform while Juno climbed down with her bundle and her fear, which had not disappeared — she checked, and it was there, riding in her chest like ballast — but which had changed shape entirely. It was no longer the fear of what Vane might do to her.

It was the fear of arriving too late.

The skiff slid out into the flooded cut. Behind her the stilt-house creaked its old creak, and ahead the river ran wide and brown and full of smoke-smell from downstream, and somewhere in that haze were three salvage boats heavy with winches, and a government launch, and a pilot brave enough or desperate enough to run the flood — and one man with a strongbox and a list of people who needed stopping.

Juno bent to the paddle. The cut carried her seaward, toward all of it, faster than she could have rowed alone, as if the river had heard Maren's father after all, and had decided she was carrying something true.
