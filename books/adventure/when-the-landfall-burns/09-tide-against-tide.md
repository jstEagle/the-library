## 9. Tide Against Tide

The smoke came up the reach like a rumor made visible — a gray smudge against the green wall of the far bank, thin at first, then thickening as the morning wore on. Sable saw it from the wheelhouse of the *Kestrel's Wake* while Tam was below coaxing the boiler to a fuller head, and she knew what it meant before she knew anything else about it. Woodsmoke on the water meant engines. Engines on this stretch of the Vessin, in this season, with the survey contract folded in her cabin and Halloway's sealed orders burning a hole beneath her bunk like a coal — engines meant Brannigan.

She counted the smoke. One plume. Then two, splitting off behind the first like a bird startled into two birds. Then three.

"Three boats," she said to the empty wheelhouse, and the words tasted like iron filings.

By the time Tam clattered up the ladder with grease to his elbows and a question already forming on his face, Sable had the glass up and steady against the window frame. The lead boat resolved out of the heat-shimmer: black hull, white superstructure gone yellow with age, a derrick amidships that could lift an engine block or a drowned man with equal indifference. The *Mercy of Crows*. She'd have known that silhouette anywhere on any river in the world. Behind her came the *Tithe* and the *Long Ledger*, both riding low, heavy with winch cable, dive gear, and men.

"Odile," Tam said, reading her shoulders rather than the glass. He always could. "How far back?"

"Half a day. Maybe less if they're running light." Sable lowered the glass slowly. "They're not running light."

The channel ahead bent east around a drowned willow stand and narrowed where the flood had pushed silt into a long gray tongue across the middle of the river. Beyond it the water braided — three threads of current separated by bars of wet sand that would be dry ground by week's end, each thread shallower than the last. The charts called it the Weave. The charts were four years old and optimistic. Every pilot who worked the upper Vessin knew the Weave as a place where you chose your water and lived or died by the choosing, because there was no room to change your mind once the banks closed in.

Sable set the glass down and felt something move in her chest that had no business being there. Not fear. Fear had been her companion since Corlow Landing, a cold pebble she'd swallowed and carried. This was different. This was older. This was the thing that had put her on the river in the first place, before debt, before Halloway, before any of it — the animal delight of a contest that mattered.

"She'll take the north thread," Tam said, squinting ahead. "Deepest water."

"She'll take the north thread because everyone takes the north thread." Sable spun the wheel a half-turn, testing the rudder's answer through the cables. "And the north thread funnels into the silt tongue at the bend. She knows that. So she'll slow, sound it, pick her line — and lose us forty minutes doing it careful."

"And we?"

Sable looked at him. Tam was fifty-one years old and had a face like a knot in hardwood, and he had been telling her to turn back since Corlow Landing, and he would keep telling her until the river itself told them both. But he wasn't telling her now. He was waiting, arms crossed over his greasy forearms, which was its own kind of answer.

"The south thread dies in the reeds," she said. "Except it doesn't. There's a cut."

"A cut." He said it flatly, the way a man repeats a word he intends to argue with later. "Through the reeds."

"Through the old oxbow. Before the flood took the bank out in 'sixty-one, the river ran wide through there. It still does, under the reed mat — six feet of water where the charts say mud. My father showed me when I was nine. I've run it twice since. Alone, in a skiff."

"In a skiff." Tam looked at the *Kestrel's Wake* — eighty feet of patched steel and stubbornness, drawing five feet even empty, drawing closer to seven with the barge gear lashed down. "Captain. You're asking me to believe in six feet of water under a reed mat that hasn't been sounded in fifteen years, based on a memory from when you were nine years old."

"I'm not asking you to believe." Sable put her hands on the wheel. "I'm telling you to stand by the leadsman."

He held her eyes for a long moment. Somewhere below, the boiler settled into its working rhythm, a heartbeat under the deck plates. Then Tam snorted — the shortest laugh she'd ever heard from him, more breath than sound — and went to the ladder.

"I'll get the pole," he said. "But if we plant ourselves in the reeds, I'm telling every pilot from here to the delta that you did it for spite."

"Spite keeps a hull honest," Sable said. "Ring ahead. Full steam."

* * *

The Weave opened before them like a hand with three fingers spread. From the wheelhouse Sable could read the water the way other people read print: the north thread ran smooth and brown and confident, and somewhere along its length the silt tongue waited, patient as a creditor. The middle thread wandered and split and rejoined itself, pretty and treacherous. The south thread slid close under the eastern bank and vanished into a standing forest of reeds taller than the boat's rail, gold-green and dense as a wall, moving in long slow waves when the wind came down the valley.

Behind them, distant but gaining, the *Mercy of Crows* blew her whistle — three short blasts, the salvage code for *I see you*. Odile's voice, if a whistle could be a voice. Sable answered with one long blast, which meant nothing in particular except *I hear you*, and heard Tam mutter below decks that the two of them might as well be writing letters.

Then the reeds were there, and there was no more time for letters.

"Leadsman ready!" Tam's voice from the bow, the pole in his hands, his weight forward on his toes the way he stood only when he trusted her and hated himself for it.

"Stand by." Sable throttled down. The *Wake* lost way, her bow swinging toward the reed wall, and for one suspended moment they seemed to be driving straight into the vegetation to die among the stems like a beetle in a jar. The reeds parted around the cut's mouth — barely. A slot of darker water, maybe thirty feet across, hidden so completely that from midstream it simply did not exist. She had found it at nine years old by falling into it, chasing a heron, in a boat that leaked. Her father had hauled her out, looked at the deep water under the reeds, and gone quiet in the way that meant he was filing something away forever.

"Easy ahead," she said, and threaded the needle.

The reed wall swallowed them. Sound changed first — the wind died, the river's noise muffled to a hush, and suddenly the world was green light and the smell of crushed stems and stagnant warm water and the creak of thousands of stalks brushing the hull on both sides. The channel was narrower than she remembered. Of course it was. Fifteen years of flood and silt. The reeds scraped along the plates with a sound like whispering, like the river murmuring its opinion of her.

"By the mark, five," Tam called, and then, a breath later, "and a half."

Five and a half feet under a keel drawing seven. No. Under a keel drawing — she recalculated in her head, fast, the numbers she kept stitched somewhere behind her eyes — six foot two, loaded as they were. Six inches of grace. The bottom here would be soft old ooze, not sand; soft bottom forgave, briefly, where sand tore.

"Slow ahead. Steer small." Her voice came out level and she was proud of it, because her pulse was drumming in her ears like rain on a tin roof.

The cut bent left. She bent with it, feeling for the current with the wheel the way you feel for a sleeping child's breathing in the dark. The current was there — faint, but real, a pull toward the oxbow's old course, the ghost of the river that used to be. Follow the ghost. That was the whole art of it. Charts told you where the water had been; ghosts told you where it still wanted to go.

"Four and a half," Tam called, and his voice had tightened. "Four and a quarter."

"Full ahead."

"Captain—"

"Trust me, Tam. The shallow spot is the bar at the bend. Past it, the oxbow deepens. If I slow down now we wallow and touch anyway. Way to do it is through."

A pause. Then the telegraph clanged under his hand, and the *Wake* gathered herself, and the reeds whipped past faster, bending away from the bow wave, and Sable flew a ship through a place where no ship was supposed to be able to fly.

"Four," Tam called. "Three and three-quarters—" and then the bow lifted, just slightly, just enough that Sable felt it in her knees before she heard it, the hull rising over the shoulder of the bar like a horse taking a fence, and the stern sagged, and for two full seconds the propeller churned in water too thin to bite, screaming its cavitation song, the whole boat shuddering—

—and then the bar fell away astern and the keel found six feet again, then seven, and the oxbow opened out around them, a broad dark corridor through the reeds, smooth as poured ink.

Tam didn't say anything for a while. When he finally spoke, his voice came up the voice-pipe slightly strangled.

"Three and three-quarters," he repeated. "Under our keel. Captain, I want it noted in the log that I aged eleven years in that bend."

"Noted. You look well for sixty-two."

The laughter that went through the crew was quiet but real — Deckin at the stern line, young Pell's boy hauling on the fenders, all of them grinning the particular grin of people who have done a thing together that cannot be explained to anyone who wasn't there. Sable let herself smile too, one hand on the wheel, and realized her palms were slick. She wiped them on her trousers and steered on.

Ahead, the oxbow curved northeast, cutting inside the great loop of the main channel. On the chart it was dead land. In truth it was a shortcut of nearly four miles, and every one of those miles was time, and time was the whole currency of this river this season.

Somewhere beyond the reed wall, muffled to almost nothing, she heard a whistle blow. Three short blasts. Then a pause. Then — and this made her stop smiling and start thinking — a single long blast, pitched low, held until it faded.

*I hear you,* it said. And underneath it, in the grammar of two women who had spent twenty years reading each other across crowded harbors: *I know what you've done.*

* * *

They broke out of the oxbow above Cutter Bend in the early afternoon, into sunlight so hard it lay on the water like sheet metal. Sable brought the *Wake* to the head of the reach and looked back downstream, and there, rounding the great loop they had cut inside of, came the Brannigan fleet — all three boats in echelon, smoke standing straight up from their stacks in the windless air, close enough now to see figures at the rails.

Close enough to see Odile Brannigan at the *Mercy of Crows'* rail, a field glass raised in Sable's direction.

Sable raised her own glass. Across the intervening mile of shining water, the two lenses found each other like gun barrels. Even at that distance she could read the set of Odile's shoulders — unhurried, squared, amused. Odile was not a tall woman and she was not a loud one, and she had once, in a typhoon off the delta, talked a sinking barge crew into calm with nothing but her ordinary speaking voice, and Sable had never stopped being a little afraid of that.

The *Mercy of Crows* did not slow at the silt tongue. That was the thing Sable noted, watching, the thing that turned her admiration into something sharper. Odile didn't slow, didn't sound, didn't hesitate. She drove her fleet straight at the bar that had stopped half the pilots on the river, and at the last moment swung hard — not north, not south, but through a gap between the tongue's tail and the middle thread that Sable had never noticed, a gap that must have opened with this year's flood, a gap that could not have existed last season.

The *Crows* crossed the bar at speed, her bow wave going white, her hull lifting exactly the way the *Wake*'s had lifted over the oxbow bar — the same physics, the same nerve, the same read of water that couldn't be taught, only inherited or earned. The *Tithe* followed her line perfectly. The *Long Ledger*, heavier, grounded briefly with a crunch they could probably hear in Corlow, backed off, swung wide, and came through on the second try.

Three boats through in twenty minutes, where a careful pilot would have spent an hour.

"Well," Tam said beside her. He'd come up without her noticing. "She found her own cut."

"She found a better one." Sable lowered the glass. "This year's flood gave it to her. Or she went looking for it. Either way—" She exhaled. "Either way, she's read this river as carefully as I have. Maybe more carefully, lately. I haven't been up here in three seasons. She runs cargo to the fish camps every month."

"You sound pleased about it."

"I am pleased about it." And that was the strange truth, sitting in her chest warm as a stone left in the sun. She should have felt dread — three boats against one, divers and winches against a patched hull and a borrowed inspector's contract, and whatever Halloway's sealed orders meant hanging over all of it. But what she felt instead was the oldest feeling she owned, older than debt, older than grief: the fierce clean joy of racing someone worth racing. All her life the miles had been an escape, running from one horizon to the next, and she had told herself that was freedom. It wasn't. This was. Two pilots, one river, one prize, and the water falling day by day beneath their keels like sand through an hourglass.

"Signal her," she said.

Tam gave her a look. "What signal?"

"The one she'll understand." Sable took the whistle cord herself and pulled it: two short blasts, a pause, two more. *Race you.* There was no such code in any book. There didn't need to be.

Across the water, the *Mercy of Crows* answered after a moment's silence: one long blast, rising at the end into something that was almost, unmistakably, a note of laughter.

The fleet began to gain.

* * *

They ran the afternoon reach side by side, a mile apart, neither giving ground. The channel narrowed past the burned landing at Harrow's Point, where the banks drew in and the current quickened and the water began to talk — a low continuous rushing undertone beneath the engine noise, the sound of a river that was getting shallower and knew it. Sable stood at the wheel and did not look at the gauge board, because she had looked an hour ago and the numbers had been bad, and looking again would not make them better.

Twice the fleets passed shoals within sight of each other, and twice the pilots traded whistle blasts across the water — warnings, courtesies, challenges, impossible to say which. Once, rounding a bluff where the current ran hard against the point, the *Crows* took a line so close to the rocks that Sable flinched at the wheel of her own boat a mile upstream, and Tam laughed out loud, and Sable found herself laughing too, helplessly, the joy of it breaking over her like spray.

Toward evening the sky went the color of hammered copper, and the river went quiet, and the smoke of the Brannigan fleet hung in the still air like a signature written across the valley. They would moor soon. Everyone would moor soon — no one ran the narrows above Cutter Bend in the dark, not even her. Not yet.

But as the light failed, Sable stood alone in the wheelhouse and studied the reach ahead through the glass, and the joy in her chest cooled and condensed into something harder. Because she knew what lay above them. She knew what the falling water was uncovering, inch by inch, tide by tide. And she knew that Odile Brannigan raced for salvage shares and salvage shares only, and that somewhere behind Sable in her cabin lay orders that had nothing to do with rescue, and that the man who wrote them was counting on her to deliver him to this exact stretch of river.

Two cuts through the reeds today. Two captains who knew the water. And above them both, at Cutter Bend, forty years of river keeping its secret — and starting, perhaps tonight, to give it up.

The first star came out over the eastern bluffs. Downstream, the *Mercy of Crows* blew her anchor signal, three bells, and her lights bloomed one by one along the darkening water.

Sable rang for reduced speed and reached for the whistle cord to answer — and stopped, her hand in the air, because across the reach, faint but clear in the dead-calm evening, came a sound that did not belong to any engine, whistle, or bell.

A single sharp crack. Then another. Flat echoes rolling down the valley.

Like dynamite.
