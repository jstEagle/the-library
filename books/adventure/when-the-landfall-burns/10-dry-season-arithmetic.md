## 10. Dry Season Arithmetic

Tam Voss had spent nineteen years reading water the way other men read ledgers, and by the third morning above Widow's Tooth he did not need a sounding pole to tell him the numbers were going bad.

He saw it first in the mud. The riverbanks along the middle reach wore their high-water mark like a dirty collar — a band of pale silt two hand-spans wide, fresh and glistening where the flood had licked them only days ago. Now that collar hung dry above the current, and each morning it sat a little higher, a little drier, like a hem being let out by some patient invisible tailor. The river was undressing for the dry season, and it was doing it fast.

He said nothing at first. He took his soundings the way he always did, standing at the bow with the pole, calling depths to Sable at the wheel in the flat, unhurried voice that was his one gift to her command: *seven feet over the bar, favoring port; six and a half past the snag; six even through the narrows.* But he kept a pencil stub behind his ear and a folded chart in his oilskin pocket, and every evening after supper he marked the day's readings against the survey sheets Halloway had provided, and every evening the gap between what the charts promised and what the river delivered grew a little wider.

On the fourth night he could no longer pretend it was error.

* * *

The *Kindly Tern* lay moored against the cutbank below a stand of drowned willows, her running lights dimmed to coals, her deck still ticking as the day's heat bled out of her planking. Upstream, maybe nine miles as the crow flew and twenty as the channel crawled, Cutter Bend waited with its secret. Downstream, further than Tam liked to think about, the Brannigan fleet's smoke had been visible all afternoon — three smudges on the southern horizon, patient as vultures.

Sable sat on the cargo hatch with her boots off, which she only did when something was wrong, because barefoot meant she was thinking too hard to care about splinters. Juno Pell perched on the rail beside her, knees drawn up, the cartographer's habit of stillness sitting oddly on a woman who'd spent two weeks pretending to be dead. Halloway had shut himself in his cabin again, door latched, lamp burning late. Tam had stopped knocking.

He spread the chart on the hatch cover between them and weighted its corners with four brass nuts from the engine spares drawer, because paper blew and truth didn't wait.

"Read it yourself," he said.

Sable read. He watched her eyes move down the column of figures — his soundings, dated, neat, the only tidy thing about him — and then across to the survey sheet's predicted depths for the same stations, and he watched the moment the arithmetic landed, because her jaw set the way it did before a hard run.

"That can't be right," Juno said quietly. "The seasonal tables say we have six tides of navigable water left in this reach. Minimum."

"The seasonal tables were drawn eleven years ago," Tam said, "in a year when the headwaters snowpack melted slow. I've been asking the river instead of the tables." He tapped the column. "We're losing better than a foot a day in the upper channels. Not a foot a week. A day. The sandbars at Cutter Bend were charted at five feet under. Yesterday they gave me three and two. By tomorrow night the *Tern* draws too much to cross them loaded, and by the tide after that she won't cross them empty."

Sable looked up. The dying light came off the water and moved on her face, gold going to copper going to dark. "Four tides," she said.

"Maybe four. Maybe three if the wind holds out of the east."

Nobody spoke. Somewhere back in the willows a bird made a sound like a hinge complaining, and the river went on talking to itself under the hull, low and constant, a conversation it never finished.

Juno broke first. "Then we go now. Tonight. Run up while there's still water under us."

"And come back when?" Tam asked, not unkindly. He liked Juno Pell. She had the particular courage of someone who had already lost everything worth losing and found she was still standing. But courage wasn't arithmetic, and arithmetic didn't care. "Say we make the bend tomorrow on the morning tide. Say we find your wreck, raise whatever's in her, load it. That's a full day's work if everything goes sweet, and nothing on this river has gone sweet since Corlow Landing. Then we turn around and run downstream through channels that are falling out from under us faster than we can sound them. If we ground on the way down — and we will ground somewhere, everyone grounds — there won't be enough water left to float us off. We don't strand going up. We strand coming home."

"So we leave the heavy gear," Juno said. "Light boat, light draft—"

"Light boat carrying what? Gold weighs what gold weighs. You want to leave the money on the bottom to save the boat?"

"I want to save the people the money belongs to!"

Her voice cracked on it, sharp and young, and immediately she looked away downriver, jaw tight, ashamed of the crack. Sable put a hand on her ankle — just briefly, just a touch — and took it away.

"She's right that it has to be soon," Sable said. "She's right that waiting kills us. But Tam's right about the shape of it." She pulled the chart closer and studied the reach above Cutter Bend, where the channel narrowed into the maze of shifting bars the old pilots called the Stitchwork because sewing a boat through it without tearing anything was considered a kind of miracle. "Here's the honest ledger. Four tides. We need one to run up, one minimum to work the site, one to run down. That leaves us one tide of grace for the whole enterprise — one tide for weather, for breakdown, for Brannigan, for Vane, for the river changing its mind. One."

"One is more than most men get," Tam said.

"It's less than I'd like."

From the cabin doorway, a voice like a filing cabinet opening: "There is an alternative to grace."

Halloway stood with his lamp behind him so his face was all shadow and his spectacles caught the light, twin coins of orange. Tam had decided early on that he disliked the man for his coldness, and had since revised the dislike upward for his precision, which was worse. Coldness you could forgive. Precision meant he'd already done this arithmetic himself, days ago, and had simply been waiting for the rest of them to catch up.

"Go on," Sable said, in a voice that invited him to regret it.

"The government launch carries shallow," Halloway said. "Two feet, fully provisioned. My orders anticipated seasonal constriction. If the *Tern* cannot safely complete the recovery, the funds can be transferred to my vessel at the bend and brought down independently. Your contract obligation concludes at the wreck site. You would be free to run light and fast."

"Free," Sable repeated.

"Unencumbered."

"You mean we haul your gold to the waterline and watch you chug off downriver with it while we pick our way home through a dying channel with nothing to show but our own keel marks."

"I mean," Halloway said, with the patience of a man explaining subtraction to a child, "that the mission does not fail because your boat draws six feet. The mission is the funds, Captain Amberdale. It has always been the funds."

Tam watched Sable's hands. They had gone very still on the chart, fingers spread, and he knew that stillness. It was the stillness of a woman holding something down — a hatch cover in a storm, a temper, a decision. Behind Halloway, in the dark of the companionway, Tam caught movement: the deckhand Rell, paused mid-step with a coil of line in his arms, listening with the frank appetite of crew everywhere for news that concerned pay or survival.

"And the surveyors?" Sable asked. "Still part of the mission? Or do they wash out with the season?"

"The survey team's status remains under investigation." Halloway's voice did not change at all, and that was its own answer, and Tam felt it land in his chest like a stone dropped down a well — that long fall, that long silence, no bottom yet.

Halloway withdrew. The lamp glow shrank up the companionway stairs and the dark came back in behind him like water filling a hole.

* * *

Later, when Juno had gone forward to sleep in the forepeak and the bank fires were banked to embers, Tam found Sable at the stern rail with the chart folded in her fist, looking down at the black water sliding past the rudder.

"Say the true thing," she said, without turning. She always knew his footsteps; nineteen years will do that. "You've been chewing it since supper."

Tam leaned on the rail beside her. Below, their wake curled away and unraveled, already gone. "The true thing is we should turn back."

She didn't flinch. He'd known she wouldn't. "Keep going."

"If we push on, best case, we come home rich and famous and the *Tern* needs new planking. Middle case, we come home broke and the *Tern* needs new planking. Worst case—" He stopped. Made himself finish it, because she deserved the whole weight of it, the way she never flinched from giving anyone else theirs. "Worst case, the river takes her somewhere in the Stitchwork with all of us aboard, and there's no salvage fleet coming for a boat nobody knows is lost, because the only people who know where we are are a dead man's cartographer and a government man who'd list us as drowned before our tea went cold."

"Yes."

"Sable. Nineteen years I've followed you into water no sane pilot would look at, and I've never once asked you to turn around. I'm asking now. Not for me. For her." He nodded forward, toward the forepeak where Juno slept. "That girl's got nothing left but this. If the river takes the *Tern*, it takes the last thing she's got to believe in. And for Rell, who signed on for wages, not for war. And for the boat herself, which has carried us farther than any boat has a right to."

Sable was quiet a long time. The embers of the bank fire breathed red. Out on the water, a log the size of a coffin went by, riding low, turning slowly, going wherever the river sent it.

"Do you know what my mother used to say?" she asked finally. "Back on the Corlow flats, when the season failed and there was nothing to eat but what the marsh gave. She'd stand in the doorway and say, *the river gives back what you're willing to lose to it.* I hated it. Thought it was just poor folk's fatalism dressed up pretty." She turned the folded chart over in her hands. "But she was right, Tam. It's not a bargain you win. It's a bargain you enter. And we entered it at Corlow Landing, the day I signed with Halloway. Everything since is just the interest coming due."

"Interest we can't pay if we're on the bottom."

"No." She looked at him then, and in the ember-light her eyes were steady and tired and absolutely certain, and Tam felt the argument die in him the way he'd known it would, the way it always did, not defeated but simply outlived. "But here's what I know about turning back. A man who turns back on this river doesn't get his old life returned to him intact. He gets to spend the rest of it knowing exactly where he quit, and the knowing eats more than the drowning would. I've seen it. You've seen it. Old Pellor, who ran from the Narrows in '09 — lived thirty more years and never slept a whole night, told anyone who'd listen how the water looked that day. Turning back isn't safe, Tam. It's just a slower way of losing."

"And pushing on?"

"Is a faster way of winning." A ghost of the grin, there and gone. "Or losing. That's the arithmetic. Nobody's worked out the sums yet. That's why they call it a gamble and not a wage."

Tam looked downriver, where the last smudge of Brannigan's smoke had faded into the general dark, and thought about Odile Brannigan, who owed him a bottle and a grudge in equal measure, and whose divers would not be doing arithmetic about grace tides. They'd be running on the falling water right now, gambling their hulls against the season, because Brannigan boats had always been built to be expendable and Brannigan crews had always known it.

"They'll push through," he said. "Whatever the water's doing. They'll come up the Stitchwork on the night tides and they'll lose a boat doing it, and they'll count it fair."

"Which means whatever we find at Cutter Bend, we won't find it alone."

"No."

Sable unfolded the chart one more time and held it flat against the rail, studying the Stitchwork's tangled lines in the near-dark, reading them from memory as much as sight. When she spoke again her voice had changed — lower, settled, the voice she used at the wheel when the water went bad and everyone aboard needed to hear that the person steering had already made peace with it.

"Then here's how we spend our four tides. Tomorrow's morning tide, we run for the bend, light and hot, before Brannigan clears the narrows. We work the site through the afternoon — no delays, no arguments, Halloway included. Evening tide, we start down. And if the river closes on us in the Stitchwork—" she tapped the map, once, hard, "—we beach her high and walk out. Boat's insured. Crew isn't."

"Generous of you, valuing me above the planking."

"You're worth at least two planks."

He laughed despite himself, short and rough, and the laugh did something to the fear — didn't kill it, nothing killed it, but it made room around it, the way you make room around grief. He gathered the brass nuts off the chart corners and rolled the paper, and for a moment neither of them moved, both looking upstream into the dark where the water was quietly leaving, inch by inch, hour by hour, taking their margin with it.

"One more thing," Tam said. "If we're spending tides like coin — someone should tell the crew the real price. All of it. Not Halloway's version."

Sable was silent. In the forepeak, faintly, Juno turned in her sleep, and the boat creaked, and the river ran on.

"There's a vote coming," Sable said at last. "Soon. Before the bend. Everyone hears the true ledger — the money, the orders, all of it — and everyone chooses with open eyes. I promise you that, Tam. But not tonight. Tonight we sleep, because tomorrow we race a falling river and a fleet that doesn't blink, and I need you rested and mean."

"I'm always mean."

"You're a lamb." She clapped his shoulder and headed aft, and Tam stayed at the rail a while longer, alone with the dark water and the arithmetic.

A foot a day. He kept hearing it. A foot a day, and somewhere ahead of them in the black the sandbars were rising like slow animals waking, and somewhere behind them three boats with iron winches were climbing, and somewhere beyond both, at Cutter Bend, a forty-year-old wreck sat in water that would not hold her secrets much longer — not because men were clever, but because the season was honest, and honesty, on the Vessin, always arrived on schedule.

He went below. Above him, through the deck planks, he heard Sable pause at the wheel, though there was no need to steer at anchor, and stand there a long moment with her hands on the spokes, holding a course against nothing, in the dark, waiting for morning.

It came gray and early, and with it, far downstream, a sound none of them wanted: the thin, carrying crack of a dynamite charge, rolling up the river like a question.
