## 11. Old Debts

The bend had a name on no chart — Sable called it the Hammock, a deep cut of slack water where two giant figs leaned over the current like old men sharing a secret. She brought the *Kestrel* in on the last of the flood tide, backed her down against the current with two turns of the wheel and a prayer she'd never admit to, and set both anchors fore and aft because the river here breathed at night, swelling and shrinking with some pulse older than any survey.

Brannigan's three boats came in an hour behind her, running lights dimmed, moving with the same unhurried confidence of a family walking to their own table. They moored thirty yards downstream, close enough to talk across, far enough that nobody forgot whose water was whose.

Sable stood at the rail and watched the lanterns come alive on Odile's lead boat, the *Marrowbone*, watched figures move behind the glass of the pilothouse, and felt the particular exhaustion of a day spent racing someone you couldn't afford to hate.

"You going over there?" Tam asked. He'd splinted nothing yet, but he cradled his left hand like it was already broken, and his eyes were red-rimmed from the wind.

"She'll come to us if I don't go to her."

"That's what worries me."

"Go wrap that hand. If she poisons me, you're captain."

Tam didn't laugh. He looked at the lanterns on the *Marrowbone* for a long moment, then said, quietly: "Eleven years ago you'd have gone over there with a knife in your boot."

"Eleven years ago I owned a knife." Sable turned away from the rail. "Get the good rum down. Not the stuff we drink."

* * *

Odile Brannigan sat in her pilothouse with her boots off and her chart spread across the wheel, which was how Sable knew she was working and not resting — Odile only took her boots off when her hands needed the space. The knock came at the door frame, and Odile said "It's open" without looking up, because of course she'd heard the launch leave the *Kestrel*, had probably counted the oar strokes.

Sable climbed in carrying a bottle of Corlow dark, the kind aged in charred barrels until it tasted like burnt sugar and regret. She set it on the chart, directly over Cutter Bend, and Odile finally raised her eyes.

They were gray eyes, river-colored, set in a face weathered to the texture of driftwood. Fifty-one years old and every one of them earned. Her hair was iron shot through with white, cropped short the way a woman wears it when she's spent decades under a hat.

"Corlow dark," Odile said. "You're trying to buy something."

"I'm trying to say hello."

"Hello costs nothing. That bottle costs eleven years." Odile pushed back from the wheel and gestured at the bench beneath the starboard window. "Sit, then. You always did know how to open a conversation with an apology you hadn't written yet."

Sable sat. Through the glass she could see the fires of Odile's crew on the bank, three small flames, and beyond them the black wall of the forest, and above it all the first stars coming out through the smoke-haze of the dry season. Somewhere upstream, four tides' worth of channel was dying by inches.

"Your diver," Sable said. "The one who went down at Widow's Tooth this afternoon. He came up too fast."

"He came up when my son-in-law told him to." Odile poured two fingers of the rum without asking, drank half of it, and set the glass down with the precision of a woman placing a chess piece. "You've been watching my boats."

"You've been watching mine."

"It's what we do." Odile's mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "It's all we've ever done, you and I. Watch each other across whatever water happens to be between us."

"The gap's thirty yards tonight."

"And still too wide to cross sober." Odile refilled her own glass. "So let's not be sober."

* * *

The thing about Odile Brannigan, Sable thought, watching her pour, was that she remembered everything. Not vindictively — that would have been easier. She remembered the way a river remembers: completely, patiently, and with total indifference to whether the remembering flattered anyone.

"You want to start," Odile said, "or shall I?"

"You were there first."

"I was there first." She turned her glass slowly on the chart, tracing a circle around the bend. "Nineteen years ago. The *Petrel*. Your first command, or near enough — that rotten little sternwheeler you bought off a dead man's estate with money you didn't have. You were running timber contracts out of Corlow, and you were twenty-four years old and certain the river loved you."

"It did love me."

"It didn't love anybody. It tolerated you right up until the night it didn't." Odile drank. "October flood. You tried to shoot the Narrows after dark because the contract paid double for early delivery, and the Narrows at night in flood water is a coin flip even for a pilot who isn't twenty-four and stupid."

"I wasn't stupid. I was poor."

"There's a difference?" Odile shrugged. "You broached on the rock shelf. Port side opened up like a letter. And you got your crew onto the roof, which was the one smart thing you did that year, and then you sat there on a sinking boat in flood water in October, and every soul on that river knew that nobody ran the Narrows at night. Nobody came."

"But you came."

"I was running contraband tobacco downriver and I saw your fire." Odile said it flatly, without decoration. "I had a hold full of goods that would have hanged me if customs found them, and a hull that drew too much water for the shelf, and every reason in the world to keep going south. And I put my nose into the Narrows at night anyway, and I tore a plank off my own keel doing it, and I pulled six people off your roof while your *Petrel* went to pieces underneath them."

Sable looked at her hands. "Six people. Two of them were children. The Voss girl and her brother."

"Yes."

"I never thanked you properly."

"No," Odile agreed. "You didn't."

"Because thanking you meant admitting I owed you, and I was twenty-four and proud, and—" Sable stopped. Started again. "And then three years later I made sure you took the fall for the Marrow run, and I told myself we were square."

The silence that followed had weight. On the bank, one of Odile's crew laughed at something, the sound carrying thin across the water.

"Tell it," Odile said. "Since we're being honest tonight. Tell it the way it happened, not the way you've been telling yourself."

* * *

Odile had told the story to herself so many times that it had worn smooth, like a stone in a current, but she made Sable tell it anyway, because the rough edges were the point.

The Marrow run. Year of the great seizure. Odile had forty chests of untaxed leaf in the *Marrowbone*'s false bottom, running the customs reach below Corlow, and a revenue sloop had come up the channel behind her with its lanterns dark. There had been two boats on the water that night — hers and Sable's, Sable running legitimate cargo, Sable who could have shown a light, could have signaled, could have done any of the hundred small things pilots did for each other when the law was the smaller danger.

Instead Sable had doused her own running lights, changed course, and let the revenue men find the only smuggler on the river.

"They boarded me at the Hammock," Odile said now, into the quiet. "Right here. This exact bend of water. They found the leaf, they impounded my cargo, they fined me everything I had and half of what I didn't, and I spent eighteen months paying it off with my boats working other people's freight. And you—" she pointed at Sable with two fingers, not quite accusation, not quite anything else, "—you signed the deposition. You told the magistrate you'd seen a strange boat running dark and couldn't say whose it was. You knew. You'd known my running lights since you were sixteen."

"I knew."

"Why?"

Sable took her time answering, and Odile let her, because the answer mattered more than the wait.

"Because the fine broke you," Sable said finally, "and I watched what it broke. You sold the second boat. You lost your crew. And I remember thinking — sitting in the customs house, signing that paper — that if Brannigan goes under, the upper river is mine. Every contract, every run, every mile. One rival fewer." She shook her head slowly. "That's the truth of it. Not fear. Not self-preservation. Ambition. I was twenty-seven and hungry and I fed you to the law to clear the table."

"And did the table clear?"

"For two years. Then you came back with the *Marrowbone* rebuilt and better crews than before, and you took the table back piece by piece, and I learned that rivers don't stay empty. Water finds its level. So do rivals."

Odile studied her for a long moment. The lantern swung gently with the river's breathing, throwing their shadows up the walls of the pilothouse, two shapes leaning together and apart, together and apart.

"Do you know why I really came for you that night in the Narrows?" Odile asked.

"Because you're not the kind of person who sails past a fire."

"I'm exactly the kind of person who sails past a fire. I've sailed past plenty." She turned her glass again. "I came because your father pulled my brother out of the shallows at Corlow Landing the summer we were children. Drowned nearly doing it. Never told a soul, never wanted thanks. My brother told me, though. Told me there was a family on the landing that paid their debts to the water, not to men." She lifted the glass slightly, toward Sable, toward the memory. "So when I saw your fire, I wasn't saving Sable Amberdale. I was settling a debt to a dead man who never asked for it. You were just the address."

Sable was silent for a long time. When she spoke, her voice was lower. "He never told me that."

"He wouldn't. Men like him pay quietly or not at all." Odile drained her glass. "Which is why the Marrow run stung the way it did. Not the money. The direction. I'd paid forward, and you'd paid backward, and for eleven years I've wondered which of us the river was keeping score on."

"And tonight?"

"Tonight I think the river doesn't keep score at all. I think we do, because we're small, and scorekeeping is what small creatures do in big places." She reached for the bottle. "But I'll tell you what I've decided, sitting in this pilothouse watching your lights all week."

"Tell me."

"I've decided the score's settled. Not forgiven — settled. The way a ledger settles. Both sides written, both sums paid, page closed." She poured for them both. "Because tomorrow we go back to racing, and I can't race you clean if I'm still carrying nineteen-year-old arithmetic."

* * *

They talked until the moon rose, red and swollen through the dry-season haze, and the talk moved the way river talk moves — sideways, circling, coming back to the same bends from different directions. Odile told her about the year the *Marrowbone* froze in at Grayhaven and her crew ate through a winter on catfish and stubbornness. Sable told her about the *Petrel*'s last hour, the sound the hull made coming apart, a sound she'd never described to another living person and found herself describing now, in the lantern light, to the one person who might understand it.

"You could have died in the Narrows," Sable said. "For me. For a debt to my father."

"So could you have, any night since. Running ahead of my fleet, taking cuts I don't know, pushing water nobody else would push." Odile smiled, and this time it reached her eyes. "We're both still paying, Amberdale. We just stopped calling it that."

Somewhere downstream, toward Cutter Bend, a sound carried up the black water — faint, rhythmic, mechanical. A pump, maybe. Or a winch. Or something being built in the dark.

Both women heard it at the same moment. Both went still.

"Vane's people," Sable said. "Working nights now."

"Working nights means working scared," Odile said. "Or working against a clock." She stood, joints cracking, and looked out the window toward the sound. "My divers say the water's dropped nine inches since noon. Nine inches, in half a day. That's not a season ending, that's a season sprinting."

Sable rose too. The rum sat warm and heavy in her, but underneath it something colder had woken. "Four tides," she said. "Maybe less."

"Then hear my offer, and hear it once." Odile faced her fully, and the lantern caught the gray of her eyes. "Tomorrow we run the same channel, and I'll race you for the wreck like I always intended to. But if it comes to blood — if Vane's people or Halloway's make it come to blood — my boats stand with yours. Not for you. For the river. Nobody strips a wreck with dynamite in living water and walks away clean. Not on my river."

"Your river?"

"Our river." Odile almost smiled. "Don't make it strange."

Sable put out her hand. Odile took it, and the grip was hard and brief and final as a knot tied off.

Walking back to the launch, crossing the thirty yards of black water that had been too wide for eleven years, Sable looked back once. The *Marrowbone*'s lantern burned steady in the pilothouse window, and behind it Odile's silhouette bent over her charts, already plotting tomorrow.

On the *Kestrel*, Tam met her at the rail, hand wrapped, question in his face.

"Well?" he said.

"We're still racing," Sable said. "But we're not alone anymore."

She was halfway to her cabin when the sound came again from upstream — closer now, clearer. Not a pump. Not a winch.

A single, flat crack, like a branch breaking underwater, rolling down the dark river toward them. Then silence, and then, very faint, voices shouting in a language of urgency that needed no translation.

Tam appeared beside her at the rail. "That came from the bend," he said. "From the wreck."

Sable was already reaching for the engine telegraph.
