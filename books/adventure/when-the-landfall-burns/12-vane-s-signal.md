## 12. Vane's Signal

The stilt-house smelled of fish oil and old smoke, and Juno Pell had learned to love it the way one learns to love a bandage — not for what it was, but for what it kept out.

Three days she had been here now, in the home of a fisherwoman named Maren Voss who asked no questions and salted her nets with the patience of someone who had outlived two husbands and every rumor the river had ever carried. Three days of mending charts by lamplight, redrawing the upper Vessin from memory because her instruments sat in an abandoned camp forty miles downstream, pitched in perfect order around a kettle that had gone cold the moment Aldous Vane decided his survey team would cease to exist.

She worked because work was the only thing that kept the image from surfacing: the radio operator, Pell's friend Doss, going into the water at Cutter Bend with the current taking him the way current takes everything, and Vane standing on the bank with his sleeves rolled, neat as a man who had just finished filing paperwork. *Accidents happen on rivers,* he'd said. *Rivers are where accidents live.*

Juno had run that night. Not bravely — she'd been sick behind the instrument tent first, hands shaking so badly she couldn't strike camp — but she had run, and Maren Voss had found her at dawn half-drowned in the reeds below the stilt-house, and had hauled her up the ladder without a word, as if fishing half-dead women out of the Vessin were simply another chore between the nets and breakfast.

Now, on the third evening, the river brought her a message.

* * *

Maren's grandson spotted it first — a boy of maybe ten with his father's squint and his grandmother's silence. He came clattering up the ladder steps and said only, "There's a bottle on the stake," and went back down again, because in this house messages arrived like weather and were discussed just as little.

Juno knew the stake. It was one of Maren's net markers, a blackened pole driven into the mud fifty yards off the stilts, visible from the single window that faced upstream. She made herself finish the contour line she was inking before she looked — a small act of discipline, holding her own hand steady — and then she looked, and her stomach dropped through the floorboards into the brown water.

The bottle was green glass, sealed with wax, tied to the stake with tarred twine. And knotted into the twine was a strip of red survey ribbon.

Only six people alive knew that their team used red ribbon on the left-hand stakes and white on the right. Five of them were supposed to be missing. The sixth was supposed to be dead.

"He knows I'm here," she said aloud, to the empty room, and heard how thin her voice sounded against the river's long murmur.

Maren appeared in the doorway with a lamp, unhurried, her gray hair bound in a scarf the color of old rope. "Then he's known for some days," she said. "Men like that don't spend money on bottles until they're sure of the address." She set the lamp down. "You want me to fetch it?"

"No." Juno pulled on the borrowed oilskin, too big for her, sleeves she'd have to roll four times. "If it's watched, I want him to see my face when I take it. Let him know I'm not hiding anymore."

It was a brave sentence and it cost her everything to say it, and halfway down the ladder her courage ran out entirely and she stood on the lowest rung with the river lapping at her boots, unable to make her legs go further. The water was the color of steeped tea. Somewhere under it was Doss. Somewhere downstream was Vane, with divers and dynamite and a fortune in stolen settlement funds rotting inside a revenue cutter that had lain in the dark for forty years. Somewhere upstream — or was it downstream now? — was Sable Amberdale's boat, carrying an inspector whose orders Juno had never read but could recite from dread: *recover quietly, list them drowned.*

"Girl." Maren's voice, from above. "The river doesn't care whether you're afraid. It'll hand you the bottle either way. Only question is whether you read it shaking or steady."

Juno laughed — a cracked, surprised sound — and waded out.

* * *

The message was written in pencil on survey paper, the cheap gridded kind, and it was signed with the little cartographic flourish Vane used on his maps, the one that made his name look like a compass rose. That flourish told her more than the words did. He wasn't ashamed. He was *proud*. He thought of this as correspondence between professionals.

*Miss Pell,*

*I trust the Vosswoman's hospitality is adequate. I have known Maren thirty years; she is discreet, which is why you chose well.*

*You have seen what must be done and drawn your own conclusions. I won't insult you with denials. What I will offer is this: the Grace carries eleven thousand crowns in settlement scrip and specie, and there is no honest ledger anywhere in the colonies that can account for it. When it comes up, it will come up to whoever holds the wreck. I am prepared to allocate you eight percent — eight hundred and eighty crowns — as consulting cartographer, payable on recovery, plus passage anywhere the maps end.*

*In exchange: silence, and distance. You will note that Captain Amberdale currently carries Inspector Halloway aboard under contract. You should also know that Halloway's instructions are not rescue instructions. He is to recover the funds quietly and file the survey party as drowned. If you warn Amberdale of my presence, you accomplish nothing except alerting Halloway that a witness survives — and Halloway cannot permit witnesses any more than I can. You would be signing the captain's crew into the same ledger Doss is in.*

*Think carefully. The dry season is closing the channels. In six days none of this will matter because none of us will be able to reach it. Choose before the water chooses for you.*

*A.V.*

Juno read it three times, kneeling on the wet planks with the lamp held high, while moths blundered against the glass and the river breathed beneath her.

He was lying, of course. Parts of it, anyway. Eight percent was a lie — men who killed a radio operator over a share did not then multiply the shares — and the promise of passage was a lie, and probably the arithmetic of six days was a lie too, softened to give her time to marinate in temptation rather than panic. But one part rang true as a struck bell, and that was the part about Halloway.

Because Juno had seen the inspector. Two nights ago, from the window, she had watched a government launch slide past the stilt-house running dark, no lights, no hail, moving upstream toward Cutter Bend with the particular urgency of a man late for an appointment he didn't want observed. Government launches did not run dark. Inspectors did not skulk past fisher stilt-houses unless they had reasons that would not survive daylight.

And Sable Amberdale — Juno had heard the name from Maren's grandson, who heard everything on the river — Sable Amberdale was piloting for him. Which meant either the captain didn't know what her passenger was, or she knew and had sold herself anyway, and Juno genuinely could not decide which possibility frightened her more.

She went back up the ladder and sat with the letter under the lamp while Maren mended nets across the room, the shuttle clicking like a slow clock.

"Burn it?" Maren asked eventually, without looking up.

"I don't know yet."

"You do know. You just don't like the answer." The shuttle clicked. "Paper like that, you burn it, you're choosing silence. Paper like that, you keep it, you're choosing ammunition. There's no third drawer."

"There's a third option," Juno said. "I could take the eight percent."

Maren's shuttle stopped. The old woman looked at her across the lamplight with eyes that had counted a great many bodies come down the Vessin, and said nothing at all, and that nothing was worse than any sermon.

Juno lasted perhaps a minute under it. "No," she said. "No. I know. But you have to understand what he's offering isn't money. It's *permission*. Permission to pretend Doss slipped on wet rock. Permission to let the families wait forever for a name. Eight hundred crowns to stop being the kind of person who counts." Her hands had started shaking again, and she pressed them flat against the gridded paper, against the neat compass-rose signature. "I counted. That's my whole trade. I count things and I put them where they belong. And right now the only place Doss belongs is in a report with Vane's name next to the word *murder*, and the only place that money belongs is back with the families it was taken from, and Vane is betting I'm too frightened to draw that map."

"Are you?"

"Yes," Juno said. "But frightened people can still hold a pencil."

* * *

The problem — and she turned it over all night, lying on the narrow bunk listening to the river slap the stilts — was delivery.

Warn whom? Warn how?

Halloway was useless. Worse than useless: the letter had convinced her that warning Halloway was exactly the wrong move. If his orders truly were to bury the affair, then telling him a witness survived was handing him a name to add to a list. And if his orders weren't — if Vane was lying about that too, poisoning her against the inspector preemptively — then she still couldn't risk it, because she had no way to check, and Vane had built his whole career on making other people check nothing.

Sable Amberdale, then. The salvage captain. But how did a woman hiding in a fisher's stilt-house reach a boat running the flooded reach without Vane learning of it? Vane clearly had eyes on the stilt-house — the bottle proved that much. Any signal Juno sent, any boat she hailed, any fire she lit, he would see it, and he would act, and his way of acting was written in the river already.

Unless the message traveled in a shape he wasn't watching for.

Juno sat up in the dark. Cartographers, she thought, were not the only people who made maps. River pilots made them too — made them in their heads, in memory, in the language of bars and cuts and channels that shifted with every tide. And a pilot named Amberdale was coming upriver through water no one else would dare, which meant she was navigating by something better than charts, which meant —

Which meant Juno might have something to trade that wasn't silence at all.

Because Juno had spent four days redrawing the upper Vessin from memory, and in those four days she had remembered something she'd surveyed two seasons ago and filed away as a curiosity: a cut. A side channel, unmarked on every official chart, that branched off above Widow's Tooth and rejoined the main reach below Cutter Bend — a shortcut through country everyone believed was solid marsh. She had mapped it for the survey because mapping forgotten things was her job. She had never reported its depth soundings beyond the office copy, because the office copy had gone into the same locked drawer as everything else Vane controlled.

A pilot who knew that cut could pass a fleet. Could pass a blockade. Could arrive somewhere her enemies believed she couldn't be.

"It's not evidence," Juno whispered to the dark ceiling. "It's better than evidence. Evidence tells people the truth. A map lets them *get there*."

Below her, the river went on saying whatever it always said, patient and indifferent, keeping its drowned and its secrets in the same brown ledger. Somewhere down that darkness, Doss was beyond anyone's help, and that fact sat in Juno's chest like a swallowed stone and would sit there for the rest of her life, and no percentage would ever dissolve it. But the living — the pilot, the crew, the families in the settlements who had waited forty years for eleven thousand stolen crowns — the living could still be reached, if she chose the route carefully and drew it clean.

At first light she took fresh paper and began to draw: the stilt-house, the net stakes, the channel markers, the cut above Widow's Tooth with its soundings penciled from a season when the water ran higher. She drew fast and she drew true, because this chart might be the last honest thing she ever made, and she meant it to survive contact with the river.

Then she rolled it tight, sealed it in Maren's second green bottle, and woke the grandson.

"The Brannigan fleet's picket boat," she said. "It passes the mouth of this slough twice a day — I've watched it from the window. Odile Brannigan is running the reach after the same wreck, and she has no reason to love Vane and no orders from Halloway. I need this to reach her deck, and I need it to look like flotsam, not post." She pressed the bottle into the boy's hands and saw her own fear reflected small in his squinting eyes. "Can you drift it into the channel without anyone seeing where it came from?"

The boy weighed the bottle, weighing her too, the way children weigh adults — without mercy and usually correctly. "Grandmother says you're the one who draws the river," he said.

"I'm the one who's trying."

"Then I'll get it wet," he said, and went down the ladder two steps at a time.

Juno watched from the window as he paddled the little skiff out among the stakes, saw him lean and let the bottle slip into the current at the edge of the ebb, saw the green glass turn once and vanish downstream toward the narrows where the Brannigan boats lay coiled like something patient waiting on the water.

Then she sat back down at the lamp, took a clean sheet of survey paper, and began a second document — not a map this time but a statement, dated, signed, witnessed by Maren Voss's mark: *I, Juno Pell, cartographer of the Upper Vessin Survey, attest that Aldous Vane did on the night of—*

If the bottle reached Odile Brannigan, the map would bring help. If it didn't — if Vane's watchers fished it out, if the river simply ate it — then the statement was her second cast, and she would find a third, and a fourth, because she had finally understood the thing the letter had been designed to make her forget:

Vane could buy silence, but he couldn't buy every pair of hands on this river, and he had made the mistake of putting his offer in writing.

Downstream, faint on the morning haze, she heard it: the deep-throated cough of a salvage winch being tested, and beneath it, thinner, the sound of dynamite crates being stacked on a deck.

They were going to blow the hold open today.

Whatever Juno had set loose on the water, it would have to swim faster than the blast.
