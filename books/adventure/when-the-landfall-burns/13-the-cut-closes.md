## 13. The Cut Closes

The marker was wrong.

Sable felt it before she saw it — a wrongness in the water's voice, the way the current spoke against the hull. For three days she had run the upper Vessin by ear as much as by eye, reading the river the way a mother reads a sleeping child, and now the child was murmuring something untrue.

"Tam," she said quietly. "Look at the cut marker."

He was at the bow rail with the lead line coiled in one hand, his other arm still stiff from hauling chain two days back. He squinted ahead to where the channel narrowed between a drowned willow stand and a gravel spit that shifted with every flood. The marker stood there — a peeled sapling driven into the bar, its crown painted the government red that Halloway's survey crew had used on every mark they'd set upstream.

"It's leaning," Tam said.

Everything leaned, out here. That was the trouble with rivers; nothing held true for long. But Sable raised her glass and studied it anyway, and what she saw made her jaw tighten. The sapling leaned *into* the channel. A marker planted properly leaned away from deep water, pushed by the current working at its base. This one had been pulled up and reset — recently, carelessly, by men who knew markers but not this river. The mud around its foot was still dark, still wet, not yet crusted by the sun.

"Somebody moved it," Tam said.

"Vane." She said the name flatly, tasting it like silt. "Or his people. Juno said he'd have eyes on us."

The morning was hot and glass-still, the kind of stillness the dry season laid over the river like a lid. No wind to ruffle the water, no cloud to break the glare. Ahead, the cut ran narrow and brown-green between walls of cane, and the water sliding through it looked — to a stranger's eye — smooth as poured oil. To Sable's eye it looked like a lie.

She could go around. There was an older channel, half a mile down, that wound wide and lazy through the oxbow — but the dry season had been drinking from it for weeks, and she'd measured less than four feet over its sill yesterday. Her boat, the *Kestrel*, drew five when loaded. Going around meant grounding, guaranteed, and grounding meant losing a tide they did not have. The arithmetic of the falling river had grown brutal: every hour the water dropped, more of the Vessin turned from highway to graveyard. They had counted six tides when they left Corlow. Then four. Now, if Tam's figures held, they had three — three rises and falls of the tidal push that kept the upper reaches navigable, and after that the channels would close like a fist.

Through the cut, if the marker were honest, there was nine feet of water over hard sand. She'd run it twice before, years ago, both ways.

But the marker wasn't honest.

"We could sound it," Tam offered. "Take the dinghy through first, drop leads every ten feet—"

"That costs us half a tide, and Brannigan's smoke is behind us." Odile's fleet had been visible at dawn as three gray smudges downstream, running the same dying river with divers and winches and no reason to be gentle about it. "And if we stop to sound, we stop in the open reach where anyone with a rifle can watch us do it."

"So we trust the marker?"

"I don't trust anything anymore." Sable lowered the glass. "I trust the river. And the river's telling me something's off." She looked at the water again, really looked, letting her eye trace the surface texture the way she'd taught Tam to do a hundred times. Smooth water over a bar doesn't look like smooth water over a hole. It has a different sheen, a different reluctance, a faint dimpling where the current trips over sand it's dragging forward. And there — just downstream of the false marker, where the channel should have been deepest — the surface lay too flat. Too settled. Like a blanket drawn over furniture.

"There's a bar moved into the cut," she said slowly. "New sand. The flood two weeks back must have shoved it in, and whoever reset that marker knew the old chart, not the new river. Or—" and here her voice went cold, "—or they knew exactly what they were doing."

"You think Vane's people put the marker in the wrong place on purpose."

"I think a man who murders his own radio operator wouldn't lose sleep over a sapling."

* * *

They took the cut anyway. Later, Sable would turn that decision over a thousand times, looking for the moment she could have chosen differently, and every time she'd arrive at the same place: there was no different choice. Not with three tides left, not with Brannigan burning coal behind them, not with a drowned diver and a stolen fortune waiting at Cutter Bend. The river had narrowed her options the way it narrowed channels, and all that remained was judgment — hers — and speed.

She took the *Kestrel* in slow, engine at half, Tam at the bow with the lead line singing in long arcs, calling depths in the old cadence.

"By the mark, seven... by the mark, six... and a half, six..."

Six feet under a five-foot draft left a foot of grace. Not enough. Not nearly enough, but the banks of cane were closing in behind them now, too tight to turn, and the only way out was through.

"And a quarter, six... by the mark, five—"

The lead came up streaked with fresh sand, grains still loose, still moving. Sable's stomach dropped. Moving sand meant the bar was alive, building, migrating with the last of the flood current straight down the throat of the cut.

"Full astern," she snapped. "Tam, get back from the bow—"

Too late. The *Kestrel* hit the bar the way a hand hits a table.

It wasn't a crash. It was worse than a crash — a long, grinding, shuddering seizure, the whole hull gripped and dragged, sand screaming along her keel, the deck canting hard to port as the stern swung free and the bow drove up onto the shoal. Sable was thrown against the wheel; the spokes spun out of her hands. Somewhere aft a crate broke loose and went over the side. The engine bellowed, racing, the screw churning brown water and spray, and then — silence, relative silence, the terrible quiet of a boat that is no longer answering her helm.

They were aground. Hard aground, broadside to the current, in a channel that was draining even as they sat there.

"Report!" Sable hauled herself upright. Her shoulder burned where the wheel had caught it.

"Everyone's up," Tam called from the foredeck, and then his voice changed. "Sable. We're taking water."

* * *

The breach was amidships on the port side, where the hull had come down across a buried snag — a hardwood limb driven into the bar by some old flood, lying in wait under the sand like a spear. The impact had opened a gash two feet long, and the river was coming through it in a steady, muscular rush, cold and brown, pooling in the bilge faster than the pumps could laugh at it.

They fought it the way you fight anything on a boat: immediately, without discussion. Tam got the collision mat over the gash from outside, hanging in the water on lines, pressing it home against the planking while the current tried to peel it away. Two of the crew — Pell and young Corso — worked the pumps in shifts, the handles rising and falling, rising and falling, water spitting from the discharge in pulses that matched their arms. Sable ran the engine at idle to keep power to the winch, rigging a chain bridle from the masthead to a dead tree on the gravel spit, because a boat broached on a falling tide does one of two things: she comes off on the next rise, or the river lays her over and drowns her where she sits.

The tide turned. The water began to fall.

By mid-afternoon the *Kestrel* sat in eight inches less water than she'd needed to float, canted fifteen degrees, mat straining, pumps thumping, and the sun laying bare the full treachery of the cut around them. The bar they'd struck was no bar at all — it was a sandbank forty yards long, newly built, sitting exactly where the government chart showed open channel. Exactly where the false marker pointed.

Sable stood on the exposed gravel spit in the long gold light and understood, finally and completely, what had been done to them. Someone had known the *Kestrel* would take this cut. Someone had known her draft, her route, her habits — or had simply guessed well — and had planted a marker in the one place that would put a boat on the new bank at speed. It hadn't been carelessness. It had been a trap laid with a sapling and a bucket of paint, and it had cost them everything the trap was designed to cost.

Two tides. The grounding and the flooding and the fight to save her had eaten two tides whole. When Tam worked the arithmetic that evening, chalk in hand on the cabin bulkhead, his numbers came out brutal: one tide remained. One single rise, tomorrow night, and then the cut would go dry and the *Kestrel* would sit on the mud until the rains came — weeks, maybe months — while Vane stripped the wreck and Brannigan sailed home rich and the drowned man at Cutter Bend stayed drowned.

"One tide," Tam said, and capped the chalk.

* * *

Tam was hurt. He didn't say so, which was how Sable knew it was bad.

She found him after dark, sitting on the afterdeck with his shirt off and the lantern turned low, probing at his ribs with fingers that moved carefully, the way you test ice. When he saw her he reached for his shirt, and she caught his wrist.

"Let me see."

"It's nothing. I twisted when we—"

"Tam." She said it once, quietly, and he stopped arguing, because twenty years on the same deck teaches a language beyond words.

Three ribs, cracked or broken, low on the right side, where he'd been thrown against the capstan rail when the *Kestrel* seized. The skin over them was already going the purple-black of storm clouds. He breathed in shallow, careful sips, guarding himself, and when Sable taped the ribs tight with the linen wrap from the medicine chest, he didn't make a sound — which frightened her more than any cry would have.

"How bad?" he asked, when she tied off the wrap.

"You tell me. You're the one who owns them."

"They'll hold." He flexed experimentally, winced, and settled. "Pumping might be a problem. Lead line's out — I can't swing it with these."

Sable sat back on her heels and looked at him. Tam had been her first mate since before the *Kestrel* was the *Kestrel*, since the old owner lost her at cards and Sable won her back with three seasons of salvage work. He had been the constant thing in a life that never held still, the fixed point she steered by when the miles blurred. And now he sat taped and bruised in lantern light, breathing like a man carrying a load of wet canvas, and she felt something crack in her chest that had nothing to do with hulls.

"I should have sounded the cut," she said.

"Yes," Tam said simply. "You should have."

Not cruel. Just true. That was the contract between them, older than any paper Halloway had ever drafted: no comfort bought with lies.

"I trusted my read over the lead," she went on. "I saw the flat water, I knew the marker was wrong, and I went through anyway because stopping felt like losing. To Brannigan. To Vane. To the calendar." She shook her head. "That's how rivers kill pilots. Not with storms. With hurry."

Tam was quiet a moment. Out on the black water, something big rolled — a fish, or a log riding the ebb — and the ripple lapped against the hull below them like a knock.

"You want to know what I keep thinking?" he said at last. "I keep thinking about the diver Vane sealed in the hold. Juno watched it happen. A man went down into that wreck to work, and Aldous Vane closed a hatch on him because the man talked too much." His shallow breath caught. "We lost two tides, Sable. He lost everything. So I can't work up much grief for our situation. We're alive, we're armed, and we've got one tide left. Some men would call that plenty."

Sable looked at him — taped, bruised, unbowed — and felt the despair in her shift its weight, the way ballast shifts when a boat comes off a bar. Still heavy. But movable.

* * *

She walked the spit alone after midnight, when the ebb had fallen far enough to show the wound in her vessel. The *Kestrel* lay heeled over in the starlight, mat bulging with the pressure of the trapped water behind it, and the gash beneath it was a dark seam in her flank like a split lip. Around her, the cut had transformed. Where the channel had run that morning, there was now a plain of wet sand gleaming under the stars, ribbed and rippled like the skin of some enormous sleeping animal, and the false marker stood absurdly upright in the middle of it, red-crowned, pointing at nothing.

Sable picked her way out to it and laid her hand on the sapling. It rocked easily in the mud. Fresh-cut wood smell rose from the peeled bark — someone had trimmed it within the week, somewhere nearby, with a knife or a hatchet. Which meant Vane's men had been standing on this very spit, doing this very work, while she and her crew slept or ate or argued not half a mile downstream.

She looked down the dry channel toward Cutter Bend, invisible in the dark, and let herself feel the full weight of it. Anger first — hot and specific, aimed at a man she'd never met who had murdered and lied and now, with a stick and a bucket of paint, had taken two tides from her. Then something colder underneath, something that had been gathering in her since the ledger, since Juno's testimony, since the hatch closed on a living man: purpose, hardened to the grain.

The river had turned hostile. Fine. She had never loved it for being kind.

She heard footsteps on the gravel and turned, hand going to the knife at her belt, and found Tam standing at the edge of the spit with a blanket over his shoulders and the lantern raised, his face yellow in its glow.

"Couldn't sleep either?"

"Ribs," he said. "They argue when I lie down." He came out to her, moving carefully, and together they regarded the stranded boat, the dead channel, the lying marker. "Corso found tracks, by the way. On the downstream end of the spit. Boot prints, three men, heading back toward the cane. And this." He held out his palm. In it lay a scrap of paper, sodden but legible, water-stained ink in a precise bureaucratic hand: coordinates, a date, and a single word — *confirm*.

"Halloway's people?"

"Or Vane's, copying Halloway's style. Either way, somebody's watching us close enough to litter." Tam folded the scrap away. "One tide, Sable. How do we spend it?"

She was quiet for a long time. The ebb whispered at the edge of the drying sand, patient as a debt collector. Somewhere far downstream, so faint it might have been imagination, she thought she heard the thump of a pump — Brannigan's fleet, working through the night, buying back the hours Vane had stolen from them.

"We careen her tonight," Sable said finally. "While she's dry. Patch the gash proper — no mat, real planking, real caulking. The mudflat below the spit will serve; we beach her at slack and roll her with the winch." She turned to Tam, and the lantern light caught something in her face that had not been there that morning — not hope exactly, but its stubborn cousin. "Then when the tide comes back tomorrow night, we go through that cut on the flood, past that marker, over that bar, with the water lifting us the whole way. And we don't stop at Cutter Bend. We don't anchor. We don't negotiate. We come down on Vane's camp like the river itself."

"With one patched seam and a mate who breathes like a bellows."

"With exactly that." She put her hand on his shoulder, gently, on the uninjured side. "Vane thinks he killed us today. That's the best weapon we have left — a dead boat, sailing."

Tam smiled despite the ribs, a thin, fierce thing. "And if the patch doesn't hold?"

Sable looked down the dark channel, toward the bend where a man had sealed another man inside a drowning ship, and her voice came out level and cold as deep water.

"Then we swim the rest of the way."
