## 14. Careening

The river gave us nothing that morning but a mudflat and an argument with the tide.

We came off the bar at first light, the *Kestrel's Mercy* dragging her belly through shallows that should have been three fathoms deep and were barely one. Sable had read the water right — she always did — but reading water is not the same as arguing with it, and the channel marker Vane's men had moved had put us hard aground on a shelf of grey silt that the falling tide was already abandoning. The hull had opened along a seam below the waterline, a split maybe two hands long, weeping river in slow, stubborn pulses. Not fatal. Not yet. But the dry season was eating the river faster than any chart predicted, and every hour we sat there was an hour the water stole from us.

"Careen her," Sable said, and there was no discussion in it.

I'd careened boats before. Every salvage hand has, sooner or later — you run a vessel up onto soft ground at high water, let the tide fall away beneath her, and work on her exposed hull in the window before the river comes back to drown your labor. It's honest work and ugly arithmetic. You get exactly as many hours as the tide gives you, and the tide does not negotiate.

The problem was that the *Mercy* drew too much water for the flat we had. We'd have to lighten her, warp her sideways, and pray the mud held her when she rolled.

* * *

We worked through the morning while the water fell around us like a held breath being slowly released.

First the cargo came out — crates of chain, spare planking, the diving gear Halloway had insisted on loading back at Corlow, all of it ferried ashore on our skiff and stacked on the mud among the mangrove roots, wrapped in oilcloth against the drizzle that never quite committed to being rain. Then the ballast, stone by stone, hauled up in slings until my shoulders burned and my palms were raw rope-work. Juno Pell helped without being asked, hauling with a grim efficiency that surprised me; I'd taken her for a map-and-ink woman, but she'd grown up on stilt-houses, and it showed in her hands.

By midday the tide had dropped enough that the *Mercy* listed hard to starboard, and Sable called for the warping.

This was the dangerous part. A boat rolling onto her side in soft mud can bury herself, or worse, roll too far and break her masts or open seams above the waterline that no patching will save. Sable stood at the rail with the fender lines in her fists, calling angles to me on the shore where I'd made fast to a deadfall trunk older than the settlement itself.

"Ease her — ease — Tam, watch the stern, she'll swing—"

She swung. Of course she swung. The current, what was left of it, took her stern like a hand on a door, and for one long breath the whole boat lay at the mercy of gravity and silt, creaking like a house in wind. Then the mud took her, gently, almost tenderly, and she settled at perhaps forty degrees, starboard side bared to the grey sky, water streaming off her planking in bright ropes.

Sable looked at her the way a mother looks at a child who has fallen down — relief and worry in the same expression.

"All right," she said quietly. "Let's see what the river did to you."

What the river had done was worse than we'd hoped. The seam split was there, yes, but the grounding had also sprung two planks near the garboard, and a third had worked its fastenings loose entirely, hanging by a single nail like a tooth in a bad gum. Below the waterline, the hull paint was scraped away in a long pale scar where the bar had dragged at her.

"She'll need copper," I said, running my thumb along the split. "Or lead sheeting. We've got neither."

"We've got canvas, pitch, and stubbornness," Sable said. "It'll hold to Cutter Bend."

"It has to," Juno said from the mud, and none of us argued, because she was right, and because behind her words sat everything we'd learned since dawn yesterday — the diver Vane had sealed in the hold, the ledger in her oilskin wrap, the families forty years owed. Some cargoes are heavier than ballast.

We fell to it. Sable drove the loose plank home and re-fastened it with screws cannibalized from a bulkhead cleat. I cleaned the sprung seams with a heated iron until they were dry enough to take oakum, then malletted the oakum home and flooded it with pitch. Juno stitched canvas over the worst of the split seam, layer on layer, painting each layer with pitch before the next went on, building a bandage the way her fisher kin built nets — patient, overlapping, meant to strain rather than stop.

The light went long and amber. The mud around us began, very quietly, to glisten. Somewhere downstream a bird called three notes and got no answer.

"The tide's turning," Sable said at last, straightening, her hands black to the wrist. "Two hours, maybe less. We float on the rise or we don't float at all."

That was when Odile Brannigan came around the bend.

* * *

I heard her before I saw her — the deep, unhurried throb of a heavy displacement engine, the kind of sound a working boat makes when she isn't hurrying for anyone. Then the lead boat of the Brannigan fleet slid into view around the mangroves, and behind her the second, and behind that the third, all three riding low with winches and dive ladders and men.

Every muscle in me went tight. Three boats against our one wounded hull. If Brannigan wanted Cutter Bend now, there was nothing between her and it but a patched seam and a prayer.

Sable stood on the tilted deck with pitch on her arms and didn't move. Neither did Odile, at the rail of her lead boat, and for a long moment the two captains just looked at each other across fifty yards of returning water, the way you look at weather.

Then Odile Brannigan did something I will remember until they plant me.

She put her helm over and ran her lead boat straight at our mudflat.

"Tam," Sable said sharply, "get the lines ready."

"I don't think—" Juno started.

"Lines," Sable said, and I got them.

But the Brannigan boat didn't come to board us. She came to ground herself — deliberately, expertly, easing up onto the flat thirty feet downstream of the *Mercy* with barely a shudder, letting the last of the falling tide settle her into the same kind of rest ours was in. Her crew poured over the side into shin-deep water, and Odile stood at her own rail and called across, in that gravel voice of hers:

"Amberdale! How bad?"

"Sprung seams and a split," Sable called back. "Patched. She'll float."

"Patched canvas floats about as long as a promise," Odile said. "You'll want pumps on the rise. And you'll want hands, because your crew looks like they've been digging their own graves." She paused, and something moved behind her eyes that might have been amusement or might have been memory. "I'm lending you both."

For a moment nobody spoke. Downstream, her second and third boats held off in the deeper channel, their crews watching us with expressions ranging from confusion to open suspicion. One of them — a big man with a winchman's shoulders — said something to his neighbor, and the neighbor laughed, and Odile turned her head just enough that the laugh died.

"You're giving up the race," Sable said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I'm not giving up anything," Odile said. "I'm banking. There's a difference, and you know it better than anyone." She stepped down onto her deck's edge, preparing to jump the gap of water between us. "A race run against a sinking boat isn't a race, Amberdale. It's a eulogy with a trophy. Patch your hull. Then we'll see who reads this river better."

She jumped, landed in the shallows, and waded toward us through water the color of weak tea, and her crew — after one collective heartbeat of disbelief — started passing pump hoses and tool bags down the line.

* * *

We floated the *Mercy* on the evening tide with four pumps going and eleven people who had no business trusting each other working in the same hull.

That's the part I keep turning over, even now. Salvage crews are a superstitious lot, and rival fleets more so — there are bends on this river named for the fights that happened at them. When Odile's big winchman, a fellow called Dree, dropped into our bilge beside me and put his shoulder to the brace without a word, I actually checked behind me for the trick. There wasn't one. There was just a tired man and a leaking boat and water rising, which turns out to be the oldest fellowship there is.

Odile worked like she commanded: precisely, loudly, and mostly by making everyone else feel they'd thought of her ideas themselves. She looked at Sable's canvas patch, pressed her thumb against it, and said, "Who stitched this?" and when Juno raised a hand, Odile studied her for a moment and said, "Stilt-house stock," and Juno said, "Yes," and that was the whole conversation, and somehow it mattered.

The pumps knocked and gulped. The pitch smell mixed with the mud smell and the diesel smell until the air inside the hull was thick enough to chew. Water came in over the patch in a thin, insistent trickle, and the pumps sent it back out, and for a few hours that stalemate was the entire world — the rhythm of it, the knock-gulp-knock, the lanterns swinging, six people bailing and bracing and passing tools in a space lit like a cave.

At some point Odile found her way next to me at the forward pump, and for a while we just worked. Then, without looking at me, she said:

"You were aboard the night she took the *Larkspur* off the Teeth. Four years back."

I kept my eyes on the gauge. "I was."

"She never told you why I was there."

"No."

"Running contraband," Odile said. "Same as half the river. Your captain caught me at it, and instead of turning me in to the revenue men at Corlow, she let me go — and let them believe it was her cargo, not mine. Took the smuggling charge herself. Cost her a season's license and most of her reputation in the upper reach." She pumped twice, three times, the handle rising and falling under her knotted hands. "I've owed her that for four years. Tonight I'm paying some of it back. Don't make a speech about it."

"I wasn't going to," I said.

"Good. Because if you do, I'll put you over the side."

But she smiled when she said it, or something adjacent to a smile, and I understood that whatever ledger existed between those two women, tonight's entry had been written in ink that wouldn't wash out.

Later, when the pumps had won their argument and the *Mercy* rode level and quiet at anchor in the deepening pool below the flat, the two crews shared food on the mudflat banks — salt pork and hard biscuit and Odile's hoarded coffee, which was better than it had any right to be. The drizzle had stopped. The sky over the eastern ridge had gone the color of a bruise healing, purple and gold at once.

Sable and Odile sat apart from both crews, on a fallen trunk, talking low. I didn't try to hear them. Some conversations belong to the people having them.

But I saw Odile take out a chart — her own, hand-corrected, the kind of document a captain guards like blood — and smooth it on her knee, and I saw Sable lean over it, and I saw Odile tap one spot twice, deliberately, and look up.

Whatever she showed her, Sable went very still.

Juno appeared at my elbow, following my gaze. "What is it?" she murmured.

"I don't know," I said. "But Odile Brannigan just gave Sable something off her own chart, and captains don't do that for free."

Downstream, dark and getting darker, the river ran on toward Cutter Bend, carrying its secrets at a walking pace. Two tides left, maybe three, before the channels died. And somewhere ahead of us, Aldous Vane was still breathing, still armed, and still standing on top of a drowned man's grave.

Odile rolled up her chart, stood, and said something short to Sable that I did catch, because it carried on the still air like a struck bell:

"Then we run together. But Amberdale — when we get there, the strongbox talks to me first."
