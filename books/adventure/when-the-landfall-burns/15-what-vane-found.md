## 15. What Vane Found

Juno Pell had spent eleven years learning to read a river from above it — the way a current braided around a snag, the way silt bloomed brown where a channel shifted in the night — but she had never learned to read a man the way she could read water, and standing now at the edge of Vane's camp, crouched behind a curtain of hanging moss and dead cane, she understood that this failure might cost her everything.

She had come ahead of Sable's boat at first light, paddling the survey's old skiff up a slough so narrow her elbows brushed both banks, following the coordinates she herself had drawn into the chart two years ago when the government still believed this stretch of the Vessin was worth mapping honestly. The wreck site lay in a drowned oxbow below Cutter Bend, where the river had abandoned its own course and left a pool forty feet deep, cold even in high summer, dark as tea steeped too long. She knew it on paper. She had never seen it with divers' lines strung across it like the rigging of a sunken ship.

There were three boats moored in the oxbow. Not Brannigan's heavy salvage hulls — these were leaner, meaner, painted the gray-green of the river itself so that from any distance they would read as nothing at all. A canvas camp stood on the near bank under the leaning fig trees: cook fire banked low, smoke thin and disciplined, tents staked with the neat geometry of men who drilled for a living rather than worked for one. And at the center of it all, a tripod of peeled timber over the deepest water, a winch drum turning slow and steady, feeding air line down into the dark.

Divers down.

Juno pressed herself lower among the cane roots and made herself small, made herself part of the landscape the way she'd taught herself to do in the survey years — motionless, breathing shallow, eyes doing all the moving. The morning was thick with river smell: mud and rot and the green bite of crushed vegetation, and beneath it, faintly, the diesel-and-rust reek of the winch engine. A kingfisher rattled past overhead and did not startle, which meant no one had moved recently along the bank. Small mercies.

Aldous Vane stood at the water's edge with his back half-turned to her, and even from sixty yards she knew the set of those shoulders. She had followed them for three years. He wore the same field jacket he'd worn the day the survey set out from Corlow Landing, the same one he'd worn the night Merrick's radio went silent, and there was a clipboard in his hands — of course there was, there had always been a clipboard, Aldous Vane believed the world was a form waiting to be filled out correctly — and he was ticking items off it while a diver's helmet broke the surface below the tripod with a sound like a kettle coming to boil.

The diver came up slowly, hand over hand on the descent line, and the tenders hauled him onto the gunwale platform and began working the bolts of his breastplate. Juno watched the ritual with the detached attention of long habit — she had logged a hundred dives for the survey, recording bottom times and air pressures in her own neat hand — and then the diver's face came free of the copper and she saw it was Doss Harrow, and Doss was talking before his mouth was even clear of the collar, talking fast and wet and angry, and though the wind carried most of it away downriver, enough reached her through the still air of the oxbow to turn her blood to river-cold.

"—not going back down, Aldous. I'm telling you straight. There's bones down there."

Juno went very still. Stillness was easy; her whole body wanted to be stone.

Vane said something low. She couldn't catch it. But she saw him close the clipboard, saw him set it down on the gunwale with exaggerated care, the way a man sets down something fragile when what he actually wants to do is throw it, and she saw Doss Harrow push his tenders' hands off him and stand up on the platform in his wool undersuit, dripping, gaunt, four days of hard diving carved into his face.

"I said what I said." Doss's voice carried this time, cracked and carrying. "You can dock my pay, you can put me off the crew, but I'm not sealing myself in a grave to make your numbers. Bones, Aldous. Two of them, wedged in the coal scuttle aft. Somebody went down with this boat alive and clawed at that hatch until their fingers were nothing. And the strongbox room—" he stopped, swallowed, started again. "The strongbox room's been opened. From inside. Chiseled. Somebody got to that money before us, or somebody tried, and either way there's a story down there and I'm not going to be the last man who knows it and goes quiet."

The oxbow held its breath. Even the winch seemed to idle softer. Out on the water a heron lifted, unhurried, white against the drowned timber, and folded away into the far bank like a page being turned.

Vane picked up the clipboard again.

"Doss," he said, and his voice was mild, reasonable, the voice he'd used in a hundred planning sessions, the voice that had talked six professionals into faking their own disappearance for a share of forty-year-old stolen money, "you're tired. Everyone's tired. The season's dying and we are nine days from having this done." He stepped closer to the platform. "What you found is exactly what we expected to find. Men drown. Boats sink. Forty years is a long time for a locked room full of desperate cargo. You log it, I'll file it, and when this is over you'll have more money than your mother's family saw in three generations."

"That's just it." Doss laughed, and there was nothing funny in it. "I don't want it quiet, Aldous. That's the difference between me and you, and I should've seen it back at the camp when Merrick didn't come back from the radio tent. I want it *loud*. I want the families on the lower settlements to know where their gran'mothers' money went. I want it in the Corlow papers with names." He pulled his gloves off, finger by finger, deliberate as a man laying down cards. "So here's how it is. I dive tomorrow, I bring up one box, and then we go public. All of it. The cutter, the theft, the survey, Merrick. Everything."

Juno's heart was a fist in her chest. She thought: *run*. She thought: *he doesn't know I'm here, you can be back at the slough before—* and then she thought of Sable Amberdale somewhere downstream, running the falling river toward this exact bend because Juno had told her to come, and she did not move. Moving was death. She had learned that much about men like this without ever wanting to learn it.

Vane looked at Doss Harrow for a long moment. Then he smiled, and it was the worst thing Juno had ever seen on a human face, because it was kind.

"You know what," Vane said gently, "I think you're right. I think it should be loud. I think you've earned the last dive — top pay, first pick of the boxes. Get him suited back up."

The tenders hesitated. One of them — Rennick, the big quiet one who cooked — glanced from Vane to Doss and back, and something passed across his face that might have been doubt. Vane turned the smile on him and it went out like a lamp.

"Suit him up," Vane said again, pleasantly. "We're burning daylight and tide."

* * *

They suited him up.

Juno watched it happen with the terrible clarity of a dream she could not wake from — the breastplate bolted, the air hose coupled and tested, the faceplate screwed down over Doss Harrow's eyes until he was brass and glass and riveted leather, a man sealed into his own argument. He gestured once, thumbs-up, the old survey signal, and she saw in the tilt of his head that he believed it. That was the thing she would carry afterward, the thing that would sit in her chest like ballast for the rest of her life: Doss Harrow believed it. He thought he had won. He thought the last dive meant the beginning of the truth instead of the end of the witness.

They ran him down the line into the dark, and the bubbles came up silver and steady, and the winch paid out hose, and Juno counted the minutes because counting was the only thing keeping her sane. Four minutes. Six. The pool lay flat and tea-dark, giving nothing back.

At eight minutes, Vane walked to the air pump.

He did not shout. He did not gesture. He simply took the pump handle out of the tender's hands — Rennick's hands — and set it aside, and then he reached down and closed the little brass cock on the manifold, the one that fed the diver's line, with the unhurried precision of a man shutting a window against rain. And then he stood there, watching the water, his hands folded on top of his clipboard, while the bubbles below grew farther apart, and farther apart, and stopped.

"Sir." Rennick's voice was barely a sound. "Sir, the line. His line."

"He's fouled it on the wreck," Vane said, not looking up. "Happens. Give him another minute to work himself clear."

The minute lasted a year. Juno's nails were in the meat of her own palms; she could feel the skin break and could not care. Down in the dark, forty feet under the cold tea of the oxbow, inside a hold that had already eaten two men alive, Doss Harrow was pulling at a hose that would never breathe again, and the man on the bank was watching the water with the patience of a fisherman, and the other two tenders were looking anywhere but at each other.

Then Vane reopened the cock, waited a decent interval, and called the dive.

They brought Doss up fast, too fast, the way you bring up a man you expect to be dead, and laid him on the platform, and cracked the faceplate, and there was nothing in his eyes but river. Water ran out of the helmet and pooled in the deck boards and dripped through, back into the pool, back into the place it had come from, and the oxbow accepted it without comment. Rivers always do.

"Fouled his line," Vane said to the tenders, quietly, and wrote something on the clipboard. "Tragic. Log the time. We'll note it in the report — hero of the recovery effort, died at his post. His share goes to his sister in Marsh Hollow, minus expedition costs." He looked up then, and swept his gaze along the treeline, along the cane, along the hanging moss, and Juno felt that gaze pass over her hiding place like a blade laid flat against her throat.

It moved on. Barely.

"Rennick," Vane said. "Get the spare suit aired. We're short a diver, not a deadline. And send Pell's old skiff-check down the slough — if anyone's been poking up that cut, I want to know before dark."

* * *

Juno did not remember backing out of the cane. She remembered fragments: the fig roots slick under her palms, the smell of her own fear-sweat cutting through the river stink, the skiff's painter cold in her fist. She remembered lying flat in the bilge of the little boat for what might have been ten minutes or an hour, drifting with the slough's lazy current, staring up at the canopy while the light came down through it in coins, listening to her own heartbeat slow by degrees.

Doss was dead. Doss, who'd taught her to splice line her first week on the survey, who'd kept a tin of peppermints in his kit for the cartographer because she got sick in chop, who'd been the only one of them who'd argued — *argued*, out loud, at the end — for the truth. And she had lain in the weeds and watched it happen and done nothing, because doing anything would have been dying too, and some animal part of her had chosen, cleanly and without consultation, to live.

She rolled over, found the paddle, and began to move. Not fast. Fast made noise. She moved the way water moves, patient and total, easing the skiff down the slough with the current doing most of the work, and the horror in her chest hardened as she went, cooling like slag into something with an edge.

Because here was the arithmetic she could not escape, the surveyor's arithmetic, the only kind she trusted: Vane had killed a man in daylight with witnesses and written it up as an accident before the body was cold. Which meant the body count was no longer a risk to him. It was a method. Merrick at the radio tent. Doss at the line. And every person who knew what lay in that hold — Halloway with his secret orders, Odile Brannigan with her winches, Sable Amberdale with her borrowed boat and her borrowed courage — was now walking toward a man who had discovered he could spend lives like small change and balance the ledger after.

And Vane knew someone might be watching the slough. She'd heard him say it. By dark he'd have eyes on the cut, and the question wasn't whether she could get back to Sable — the question was whether she could get back before Vane's pickets closed the slough, before the dry season dropped the channels, before the last tide died in the upper reach and the river shut like a book.

She rounded the third bend of the slough as the afternoon light went long and gold, and there, anchored in the mouth of the cut where it met the main channel, riding at ease with her laundry drying on the rail, was the salvage boat. Tam was on deck mending net, and he saw her and straightened, and his face changed as she came alongside — changed the way faces change when they read a report of a wreck.

Juno caught the gunwale. Her hands were shaking now, finally shaking, and her voice came out scraped raw and steady anyway, because she had counted the minutes and the minutes were all that mattered.

"Get Sable," she said. "Get her now. Vane's at the wreck, he's got divers down, and he just murdered one of our own." She swallowed. "And he's sent men to watch this slough. We have until dark."

Tam was already moving.
