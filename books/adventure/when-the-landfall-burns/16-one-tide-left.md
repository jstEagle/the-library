## 16. One Tide Left

The water told the truth before anyone else did.

Sable stood at the rail of the *Kestrel's Wake* and watched the river drop. It did not fall all at once — it never did — but in small confessions: a sandbar that had been two feet under at dawn now showed its pale spine like a dead thing surfacing; the current along the cut bank had thinned from a roar to a mutter; the smell of exposed mud rose up, sour and mineral, the smell of a river being taken apart bone by bone. Somewhere downstream, whole channels were closing like throats.

One tide left. Maybe less. The dry season was not coming anymore — it was here, eating the reach foot by foot, and when it finished there would be no running this upper water until the rains came back, months from now, if they came back at all.

Behind her, on the deck, her crew waited. Tam with his arm bound and splinted against his chest, his face still gray from the grounding but his eyes sharp as ever. Pell, the young deckhand, chewing the inside of his cheek. Old Marette at the winch, hands folded, patient as driftwood. And aft, across the gap of brown water, the lanterns of Odile Brannigan's *Iron Mercy* burned steady, her other two boats rafted alongside, their decks crowded with divers and winchmen who had come upriver for one reason only.

Sable had called them all together. She had something to say, and she was done saying it in pieces.

She turned and faced them.

"You've followed me through flood water and a breached hull," she said. "You've bailed with your hats and patched a hole with canvas and prayer. You've earned the truth, so I'm going to give you all of it, and then you're going to decide what we do with it. Not me. All of us."

Tam shifted against the rail. "Sable—"

"No." She held up her good hand. "I've been carrying this since Halloway's cabin, and I let myself believe I could decide alone. That's how men like Vane get made. They start deciding alone."

* * *

She laid it out the way she'd laid out charts a hundred times, flat and clear, because the truth deserved a steady hand.

"The survey team didn't drown," she said. "They were never lost. Aldous Vane staged the whole vanishing — tents pitched, kettle warm, instruments left neat as a museum — to buy himself time to find the *Vessel's Grace*. Forty years ago she went down at Cutter Bend carrying settlement funds that were stolen before they ever reached the families they belonged to. Farmers. Fishers. Widows. Whole villages paid into that fund and got nothing back but silence."

Pell spat over the rail. "And Vane wants it for himself."

"Vane wants it for himself. And Inspector Halloway—" she let the name sit a moment, tasting its weight, "—wasn't sent to rescue anyone. I found his orders. Recover the funds quietly. List the surveyors as drowned. He came upriver to bury them on paper the same way the river buried the cutter. Whatever Vane is, Halloway is the government's hand, and the government would rather have the money than the truth."

Marette's face didn't move, but her knuckles whitened on the winch handle.

"And there's more." Sable's voice dropped. "Juno Pell — the cartographer, the one hiding in the stilt-houses — saw Vane order a diver sealed into the hold yesterday. A man from Brannigan's own fleet, or hired from the same mud. He threatened to talk, and Vane closed the hatch on him while he was still down." She looked across the water at the *Iron Mercy*, where Odile stood at her own rail, listening, arms crossed, her scarred jaw set like poured concrete. "That diver may still be alive. Or he may be dead in forty feet of black water with a stolen fortune stacked around him. Either way, nobody's coming for him unless we do."

Silence, except the river. The gurgle of the dropping water. A night bird somewhere in the drowned timber upstream, calling once and going quiet, as if it too were waiting for an answer.

Tam spoke first, because Tam always spoke first. His voice was rough from the near-drowning they'd all shared at the bar. "Say the rest of it, Captain. Say what you haven't said yet."

Sable exhaled. "The rest is me. When I signed Halloway's contract, I knew it smelled wrong and I signed anyway, because triple pay meant the *Wake* free and clear, and maybe me free and clear too. I told myself I'd sort out the right and wrong of it after. That's the honest ugly truth. I ran this river for gold before I ever heard about a murdered diver." She looked around at their faces, lit orange by the deck lanterns, moths ticking against the glass. "So when you vote tonight, vote knowing your captain isn't clean. Vote knowing what she almost was."

* * *

Odile came across in her tender without being asked, which was itself an answer of a kind. She stepped onto the *Wake*'s deck the way she stepped onto everything — like she owned the landing rights. Up close, in the lantern light, Sable could see the years on her: the same years, mostly, the same river written into both their faces in different scripts.

"Your man sealed in my hold," Odile said without preamble. "Dorsan Ferro. Twenty-two years old. Nephew of my second engineer. I've been telling myself since Juno's message came that he slipped his line, got turned around in the dark. Telling myself stories." Her voice cracked, just once, like ice underfoot. "You're telling me Vane shut him in."

"I'm telling you what Juno saw."

Odile nodded slowly. She looked out at the black water, at the far bank where the drowned forest stood hip-deep and silent, and when she turned back her eyes were dry and terrible. "Then we're past salvage. You understand that. This stopped being about brass and paper the moment that boy's air ran out."

"It stopped being about brass for me three days ago," Sable said. "I just hadn't told my crew yet."

"So tell them the arithmetic." Odile planted herself by the mast, uninvited and unbothered. "Because they deserve the numbers before they vote. One tide. One. After this one turns, the channel above Devil's Elbow goes to shin-deep and mud, and every boat up there stays up there until the wet season — six months, maybe eight. We go tonight or we don't go at all. And if we go and it goes wrong, there's no salvage fleet coming behind us, no launch, no rope. The river keeps what it takes."

"We know the arithmetic," Tam said quietly. "We've been reading the bars all week."

"Then vote." Odile spread her hands. "But vote awake."

Sable raised the lantern higher. "All hands. All voices. Tam?"

Tam looked at his splinted arm, then at her. "I told you at Corlow Landing not to take this job. I was right about the contract and wrong about everything else." He straightened as much as the splint allowed. "I'm in. Not for the money — I stopped believing in that money the moment I pulled that brass plate off the wreck and felt how heavy a lie can be. I'm in for Dorsan Ferro, whoever he is. Nobody gets sealed in a hold on my river."

"Pell?"

The young deckhand swallowed. "My cousin's family farms on the Vessin flats. They paid into that fund. My aunt still has the receipt, framed on the wall, like it means something." His jaw tightened. "In."

"Marette?"

The old woman had been silent through all of it, and she stayed silent a moment longer, looking at the water with the expression of someone reading a very old letter. Then: "Forty years ago I worked a packet boat out of Corlow. I knew the master of the *Grace*. Decent man who took the blame for cargo he never stole, because the men who did steal it were the ones writing the reports." She looked up, and her eyes were flint. "I have watched this river protect thieves my whole life, Captain. Time it protected something else. In."

Every eye on the *Wake* turned aft, to the woman standing at the rail with her arms crossed and her fleet at her back.

Odile Brannigan was quiet for a long time. Long enough that the moths found the lantern again, tick-tick-tick, and the current muttered against the hull, and somewhere upstream a bar of sand slid a few inches in the dark with a sound like a sigh.

"You know what I came upriver for," she said finally. "Winches, divers, contracts. I came to lift a fortune out of a hole in the world and retire on it. That was the plan. That was always the plan." She uncrossed her arms. "And then some cartographer comes paddling down to my boats half-dead with fear, and tells me a boy I've known since he was stealing biscuits off my galley table is shut in the dark with the water rising, and suddenly the plan is worth exactly nothing." She turned to her own crew, visible across the gap, waiting on her word. "Brannigans don't leave their people in holes," she called across the water. "Run up the colors. We're going."

A cheer went up from the *Iron Mercy* — short, hard, not celebratory. The sound of people committing to something they might not come back from.

Sable felt something settle in her chest, something that had been loose and rattling since Corlow Landing, since the itch of miles, since the first lie she'd told herself about triple pay. It wasn't relief. It was heavier than relief. It was the weight of a thing chosen on purpose, with open eyes, in full knowledge of the price.

Conviction, she thought. So that's what it feels like. Like ballast.

* * *

They worked through the last hour of the ebb in a silence broken only by necessary words. Cargo lashed double. Pumps checked twice. The patched section of hull — canvas, pitch, and a plate borrowed from Odile's stores — sounded with a hammer every foot, Marette listening for the hollow note that would mean trouble. Tam, one-armed, directed the lashings with his chin and his voice, refusing the bunk Marette kept trying to send him to.

Sable stood at the wheel and studied the channel by lantern and memory both. Above them lay six miles of the worst water in the reach: the narrows, the drowned timber, and then the Devil's Elbow itself, where the river bent back on itself around a bluff and the current ran sideways and the bars moved with every season like furniture in a drunkard's house. Charts were useless there. Soundings were nearly useless. What worked was what lived in her skull — twenty years of runs, of near-misses, of the river teaching her its temper one lesson at a time.

"One tide," Tam said beside her, following her gaze upstream into the dark.

"One tide."

"And if the tide dies mid-run? If we ground again, worse than the bar?"

Sable was quiet a moment. "Then we ground," she said. "And we walk out, and we come back when the rains do, and we tell the families the truth even if we couldn't bring them anything else. But we don't turn around. Turning around is just drowning slower."

Tam huffed — almost a laugh. "When did you get to be the one saying things like that?"

"About three days ago," Sable said. "Somewhere between Halloway's ledger and a boy named Dorsan Ferro."

Across the water, lanterns kindled one by one on the *Iron Mercy* and her consorts. Engines caught — Odile's boats ran older, louder engines, and their coughing echo rolled up the reach like distant thunder. Odile herself appeared at her rail, and even across the dark water Sable could see the grin, sharp and joyless and ready.

"Sable Amberdale!" she called. "Last one to Cutter Bend buys the rum!"

"If we live!" Sable called back.

"That's the spirit!"

The moon was down. The stars were out, hard and cold, and the river beneath them was blacker than the sky. Sable put her hand on the wheel and felt the *Kestrel's Wake* answer before she gave the order — felt the hull lean toward the current like a dog toward an open gate.

"Stand by your stations," she said, and her voice carried fore and aft, calm and clear, the voice of a woman who had finally stopped running from anything. "We take the flood while it lasts. Full ahead."

The engines answered. The banks began to slide. And ahead of them, unseen, the Devil's Elbow waited in the dark with its teeth in the water.
