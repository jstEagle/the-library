## 17. Night Run

The moon did not rise that night, and Sable Amberdale had never been gladder of anything in her life.

She stood at the wheel of the *Kestrel's Debt* with the last light gone out of the western hills and the river gone the color of wet slate, and she felt the old quiet come down over her like a coat she had not worn since girlhood. No moon meant no glitter on the water to lie about depth. No moon meant no shadows cast by the drowned trees to confuse a tired eye. No moon meant the river was only what it sounded like — and sound was the one language Sable had never stopped speaking.

"Black as a bootlegger's heart," Tam said beside her, coiling the last of the stern line with hands that still moved stiffly where Vane's sabotage had wrenched them two tides ago. The splint on his forearm caught what little starlight there was, a pale sliver in the dark. "You sure about this, Captain?"

"No," Sable said honestly. "But I'm certain."

Behind them, three sets of running lights burned low — hooded lanterns screened to show only aft, per Odile Brannigan's orders — marking the positions of the *Iron Odile*, the *Marrow*, and the little pump-boat *Tessaly* with its extra divers crammed aboard like seed grain. Running shotgun, Odile had called it, and had spat over her rail to seal it, which among river people was a stronger oath than any signature.

They were going up to Cutter Bend in the dark because the dark was all they had left. One tide. When the water fell out of the upper reach tomorrow afternoon, the channels would close like a fist, and everything above Widow's Tooth would be mud and memory until next spring — if the dry season even gave them spring. Vane was at the wreck already. His divers were already down. And somewhere under forty years of silt lay the proof of what he'd done: the ledger, the strongboxes, maybe the body of the diver he'd sealed in the hold for threatening to talk.

Sable put the thought away. Fear was cargo too, and you didn't run a night reach loaded wrong.

"Soundings," she said quietly.

"Aye." Tam lifted the lead without being told twice. In daylight they'd have used the echo-box; tonight the machine's hiss would carry too far across black water, and besides, Sable trusted the line more than any instrument when the water was lying. The lead swung out, kissed the surface with hardly a splash — she'd taught him that throw years ago, back when he was a deckhand who couldn't find Corlow Landing with both hands — and came up reading by feel.

"Four fathoms, soft bottom."

"Good. Keep her coming."

The *Kestrel's Debt* climbed the river at half throttle, engine muffled by the blanket-wrapped exhaust stack Tam had rigged at dusk. Even so, the sound carried. Sound always carried on the upper Vessin at night; the cliffs along the eastern shore threw voices back across the channel like gossips throwing insults. Sable listened past her own engine, sorting the noise into layers the way another woman might sort laundry: the diesel's mutter underneath, the wind in the flooded willows above, and between them — the river itself, talking.

It talked differently at every bend. Over a gravel bar it hissed, a long sibilant whisper like a secret passed along a pew. Over clay it went silent, smooth and treacherous, saying nothing at all, which was how it said *beware*. Against rock it knocked, a low wooden knocking that came up through the hull before it ever reached the air. And where the current split around a sunken snag it made a sound Sable had no name for, a kind of worried gargle, the river clearing its throat before it swallowed something.

She steered by those sounds now, and by memory, and by the smell of the banks.

That last one would have sounded like madness to Halloway, sitting prim in the launch he'd insisted on bringing, but it was the oldest pilot's trick on the Vessin and nobody had ever written it down. Mud smelled one way, gravel another. A bank held by willow roots breathed green and cool even in the dark. A bank that was going — sliding, slumping, ready to give a boat its grave — smelled sour, mineral, like a coin left too long in a pocket. Twice in the first hour Sable smelled that sourness and took the *Debt* wide of the eastern shore, and twice Tam's lead found nothing where the chart said four feet of firm ground should have been.

"The charts are dead," he murmured after the second time.

"The charts were born dead," Sable said. "This river redraws itself every flood. What I've got up here—" she tapped her temple— "is just the newest edition."

Ahead, a hooded lantern winked twice from the *Iron Odile*: Odile asking, in the old salvage code, whether they were holding course or wanted the fleet to close up. Sable answered with a single flash — *holding* — and felt something loosen in her chest that she refused to examine too closely. Ten years ago the idea of Odile Brannigan's boats guarding her wake through the night reach would have been a joke told in a tavern to make hard men laugh. Tonight it was the only reason she dared run this fast.

Because she was running fast. Faster than sense allowed, faster than she'd ever taken the upper Vessin in the dark, and she knew exactly why: the tide was falling behind them even as they climbed, the river dropping out from under their keel inch by inch, and every minute bought upstream was a minute the channels stayed open ahead. Speed was life. But speed in the dark on an unmarked reach was also death wearing a faster costume, and the whole art of it — the whole terrible, beautiful art — was knowing which one you were wearing.

* * *

They came to the Narrows above Fisher's Stilt an hour into the run, and Sable slowed without deciding to slow, her hand moving the throttle on its own the way a man's hand moves to catch a dropped glass.

Here the river pinched between two ridges of black granite, and the flood-swollen current funneled through with a sound like sustained applause — except nothing here was applauding. In daylight the Narrows were a white roar you could feel in your teeth. At night they were a wall of cold spray-smell and a pressure against the eardrums, and somewhere inside that roar, Sable knew, waited the entrance marker.

Or had waited. Vane's men had cut the marker at the lower channel two days ago; there was no telling what else they'd touched.

"Lead," she said.

Tam threw. "Three fathoms."

"Again."

"Three and a quarter. Bottom's rising."

"Again."

"Three and a half — Captain, we're drifting toward the east wall."

"I know." She could hear the wall. That was the thing Tam had never quite learned, though he was the best mate on the river below the gorge: that the current spoke of the walls before the walls arrived, the way thunder speaks of lightning. The deep channel ran closer to the east ridge than any chart showed, hugging the rock within a boat's length, because the west side was all submerged shelf, hard sand barely a fathom down, smooth as a tabletop and just as unforgiving. To go west was to grind your hull off in thirty seconds. To go east was to trust your ears and your nerve and pass so close to the granite that you could have reached out and scraped your initials into it.

Every other pilot on the Vessin went west. Every other pilot on the Vessin had also, sooner or later, gone aground.

"East," Sable said, and eased the wheel over, and took her ship into the dark beside the rock.

The sound changed around her instantly — the applause splitting, part of it falling astern, part of it rising on the port bow as the main current slammed the cliff face and rebounded. Spray came aboard, cold as coins flung against her neck. She counted seconds in her head, matching the rhythm to distances she'd measured a hundred times in daylight: five seconds to the first eddy, seven to the seam where the rebound current met the main flow and made a boil you could set a cup on, eleven to the gap in the rock where a fallen slab created a backwater calm enough to moor a dinghy.

At nine seconds the knocking started under the keel — not touching, never touching, but the vibration of water compressed between hull and stone, a warning tremor like a tooth announcing decay. Tam's knuckles went white on the lead line. Somewhere behind, she heard the *Marrow*'s engine drop to a crawl, Odile's boats hanging back rather than follow blind into a passage only Sable knew.

Twelve seconds. Thirteen. The knock faded. The spray thinned. The roar opened out behind them into the broader bellow of the lower pool, and Sable let out a breath she'd been holding since the ninth second, and realized her shirt was soaked through, and could not remember when that had happened.

"Three fathoms again," Tam reported, voice not quite steady. "Soft bottom. We're through."

"We're through," she agreed, and keyed the lantern once — *come on* — and heard, faint and far behind, Odile's answering double-flash, and beneath it the *Iron Odile*'s engine climbing as the other captain brought her fleet up to follow the wake of a woman she'd once sworn she'd see broke and begging.

The river kept its own ledgers too. Maybe tonight some pages got torn out.

* * *

Past the Narrows the valley widened and the water gentled, and for a while the run became almost peaceful, which Sable distrusted more than danger. Peace on the Vessin was the pause between verses.

She used it. She made Tam take the wheel — carefully, his splinted arm braced against the binnacle — and went forward alone into the bow dark, gripping the rail, letting the wind and spray scour her face while she did the thing she did before every hard passage and had never once described to another living soul.

She introduced herself to the river.

Not aloud. Not in words, exactly. It was older than words, a habit laid down in childhood when her father ran cargo and she rode the bow as ballast-and-luck, and it went something like this: *I'm coming through. I know I take from you — freight, fish, passage, and once, a man I loved, though you never sent him back and I've stopped asking. Tonight I'm asking for one more passage, and I'll pay attention the whole way, which is the only coin you respect. I'll listen. I won't pretend I own you. Let me through.*

Whether the river heard, she had never known. But she knew this: the nights she asked were the nights she lived, and the one night she hadn't bothered — young, arrogant, running light and fast on a bet — the Vessin had nearly taken the *Debt* at Widow's Tooth and had taken two fingers off her father instead, and she had been paying attention ever since, the way you pay attention after a door has closed that you thought would always be open.

She went back to the wheel. Tam surrendered it gratefully.

"How far to the Elbow?" he asked.

"Two miles. Maybe less, the way we're eating water." She glanced at the tide-stick lashed to the pilaster — a plain cedar dowel marked with notches, read against the waterline, the poor man's tide gauge. It had dropped a full notch since the Narrows. "We're losing the fight slower than I feared and faster than I like."

"And the Elbow?"

Sable was quiet a moment. Ahead, the cliffs drew together again, farther off, a darkness darker than the dark, and she could just make out — or imagined she could — the particular hollow moan the wind made there, funneling through the notch the old-timers said the devil himself had sawed when the world was young and wet.

"The Elbow is where the river turns ninety degrees in a hundred yards," she said. "Outside of the turn, the water piles up against the bluff and stands — literally stands, a standing wave big as a barn, and behind it a hole that will suck a loaded barge under and keep it. Inside of the turn is a rock garden we can't see tonight. The safe line is a boat's width wide, right on the seam, and the seam moves with the level. Charts put it—" she almost laughed— "well. The charts put it wherever the charts put it. They've been wrong at the Elbow since they were drawn."

"So how do we find it?"

"Listen."

* * *

The Devil's Elbow announced itself the way it always did: a change in the wind first, the breeze dying as the cliffs closed, then the sound of the water thickening, deepening, gathering bass notes it hadn't had a mile back. Sable killed the running lights entirely — no point advertising themselves to whatever eyes Vane might have posted — and brought the *Debt* down to bare steerage way, and the whole night seemed to lean in around them.

"Everyone hang on," she said softly, and heard the order pass down the deck in whispers, and heard Odile's fleet close up behind her without being signaled, because Odile Brannigan knew what the Elbow was too, and knew whose night this was.

Sable closed her eyes.

It was a madman's trick and she did it anyway, because with her eyes open the darkness offered nothing but lies — shapes that weren't rocks, rocks that weren't visible at all — whereas her ears, freed of the competition, opened like flowers. The engine. The hull. The current's layered voices. She turned the wheel by fractions, listening the way a surgeon listens through a stethoscope, and the river told her everything.

There: the pile-up against the outside bluff, a continuous heavy drumming like rain on a tin roof, off the starboard bow. There: the rattle and clatter of the rock garden inside, small sharp knocks, a mouth full of stones, to port. And between them — thin, high, almost sweet, a note like a wet finger circling a glass rim — the seam. The place where two currents slid past each other without mixing, singing their friction into the night.

She steered for the song.

The *Kestrel's Debt* entered the Elbow at the exact center of the safe line, and for perhaps forty seconds Sable ceased entirely to be a woman steering a boat and became something else — a nerve ending of the river itself, a single continuous system of water and wood and will, the wheel an extension of her spine, the hull an extension of the current, the current an extension of the valley that had carved it. She felt the standing wave before it arrived, a swelling in the sound ahead like an orchestra inhaling. She felt the hole behind it, a silence shaped like hunger. She threaded the needle between them with corrections so small they were less than movements, intentions merely, and the boat went where she intended, and the barn-high wave stood off her starboard shoulder roaring like a crowd denied, and the rock garden clicked and chattered to port like teeth, and she passed between them untouched.

Then the cliffs released, the valley opened, the current spread out and calmed, and Sable opened her eyes and found tears freezing cold on her cheeks and did not know when they'd started, and did not care.

"Captain?" Tam's voice, very small.

"Cutter Bend," she said, and her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "Ninety minutes, if the water holds."

Behind them the lanterns flashed: Odile, twice, then — new, unprecedented — three times. *Well done,* in the old code. *Well done.* From Odile Brannigan it was worth more than triple pay, and Sable flashed back the humble signal, *just following the river*, and knew Odile would laugh her smoke-cured laugh on the *Iron Odile*'s deck, because they both knew the truth: the river had followed her, for once, and let her through.

* * *

They raised Cutter Bend as the sky in the east went from black to the color of a bruise healing.

And the first thing Sable saw — before the wreck site, before the tents, before anything — was light. Not the hooded, disciplined lights of a salvage fleet working careful. A raw, naked glare thrown up from the water's edge, blazing off the mist, and silhouetted against it, figures moving on the deck of a barge anchored over the sunken *Vessel's Grace*, and among the rigging, unmistakable even at this distance, the tripod and lowering-line of a charge being set.

"They're not diving," Tam breathed. "They're wiring the hold. Captain — they're going to blow her."

Sable's hand found the throttle. Downstream, far behind, Odile's lanterns flashed frantically — *wait, wait* — wanting to mass, to plan, to come in together.

But the tripod was rigged, and dawn was coming, and somewhere in that blazing camp a man named Aldous Vane was about to erase the only proof that he had murdered for money — proof that might include, right now, at this moment, a living man sealed in a steel room with the water rising.

Sable pushed the throttle forward until the engine screamed.

"Hang on," she said. "We're not waiting for the plan. The plan just ran out of time."
