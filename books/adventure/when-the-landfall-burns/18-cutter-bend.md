## 18. Cutter Bend

The river told us before we saw them.

It came up through the hull as a tremor in the timbers, faint and wrong, like a heartbeat felt through a door. Tam felt it too — I saw him lift his boot off the deck plates and set it down again, frowning, the way he did when the boat spoke a language he only half remembered. Then the sound arrived proper, rolling down the reach a full minute behind its messenger: a flat, concussive *whump* that the fog banks caught and folded and passed along, so that it seemed to come from everywhere at once.

"Dynamite," said Odile, at my shoulder. She had run her lead boat alongside mine for the last three miles, close enough that we could have passed a cup of coffee between the rails. Her voice was very quiet. "Small charges. Sounding shots. He's mapping the hold before he blows it open."

"How long?"

She consulted nothing but her own arithmetic, which was better than any chart. "Sounding shots first. Then the big ones. An hour, maybe less. He'll want it done before the water drops off his working deck."

An hour. The last hour of the last tide that would ever float a loaded boat up to Cutter Bend. After tonight the channel would go to mud and memory, and whatever happened at the wreck would belong to the wreck.

I put my wheel hard over and rang for full ahead.

* * *

Cutter Bend earned its name twice over. Once for the revenue cutter *Vessel's Grace*, forty years drowned in the deep scour-hole below the point where the river made its hardest turn. And once for the way the current itself cut the bank there — a raw, slumping face of red clay and torn willow roots that the river chewed at every flood and never quite swallowed. The water off that bank ran cold and green and deep, and it moved with a slow, coiled patience, like something that had learned to wait.

We came around the willow point with engines throttled to a whisper and the fog doing half our work for us, and there it all was.

Vane's camp strung along the shingle bar like a wound that wouldn't close: tents, a generator coughing blue smoke, floodlamps on poles burning pale against the gray morning. Two barges anchored over the scour-hole, cranes folded like sleeping insects, dive lines running down into water the color of old bottle glass. And men — a dozen of them, maybe more, moving with the particular economy of men who have been paid too much to ask questions. At the edge of the bar, stacked in wicker cradles like eggs in a basket, sat the dynamite.

"There," Tam said, and pointed with his chin.

Aldous Vane stood at the rail of the nearer barge in a dry, immaculate survey coat, consulting a slate with a diver's tender. Even at two hundred yards I knew the set of his shoulders — the shoulders of a man arranging the world into columns that all added up in his favor. Juno had described him to us in the stilt-house, her voice shaking: *he signed the orders with his left hand so the radio log would look wrong, he planned it all before we ever left Corlow, and when Reyes asked questions —* She hadn't finished that sentence. She hadn't needed to.

Beside me, Odile had gone very still.

"You knew," I said. It wasn't a question. "You've known about the *Grace* since the Brannigan charts were drawn. Forty years your family's kept this bend in a back drawer."

"My grandfather found her in the flood of '09 and couldn't raise her," Odile said. Her eyes never left the barges. "He tried for six seasons. Lost a brother to the scour-hole. After that, the family rule was: the *Grace* keeps her dead, and nobody wakes her for pocket money." She turned to me, and in the flat gray light her face looked carved. "But this man woke her. And he's killing people over it. That's a different arithmetic, Sable."

Downstream, faint but closing, came the low diesel note of a government launch.

Halloway. Right on schedule, the way a cold thing arrives on schedule.

* * *

We came in loud, in the end. There was no sense pretending otherwise — three boats, running lights burning, horn sounding one long note that rolled off the clay bank and came back changed. I put my bow straight at the gap between the barges and held it there until the tenders scattered and a man with a megaphone started shouting things about navigation hazards.

I killed my engine inside the shadow of Vane's crane and let the current carry us the last boat-length, quiet as a coffin.

"Captain Amberdale." Vane had come to his rail. Up close he was smaller than his voice, a neat, dry man with the sunburn of someone who resented weather on principle. His eyes did a slow inventory of my deck — the patched hull, Tam's bandaged arm, the Brannigan crews crowding their own rails on either flank — and dismissed all of it. "You've had a long run for nothing. The survey site is a government operation. I'll need you to withdraw downstream."

"The survey team," I said. "Six people. You want to tell me where they are?"

"A matter for the inspector general's office." He said it smoothly, but his hand had moved — just slightly — to rest on the rail beside a signal lanyard. "As I said. Withdraw."

"Reyes is dead." I let it out flat, the way you drop an anchor. "Your radio operator. Juno Pell watched you do it, or watched you order it, which is the same thing in the eyes of whatever court survives you. And there's a diver of yours down in that hold right now whose partner came up white as milk and wouldn't say why."

Something moved behind Vane's face — not guilt; I'd have known guilt. Calculation. The rapid, silent recalculation of a man discovering his ledger has a page missing.

"You've been reading other people's papers, Captain," he said. "That's a crime, in some jurisdictions."

"So is murder. So is dynamiting a grave with a man inside it." I raised my voice then, deliberately, so it carried across the water to the shingle bar, to the divers, to the tenders, to every hired man on that bank who had come upriver for wages and nothing more. "The *Vessel's Grace* went down carrying the settlement funds — the money owed to the families at Corlow, Harrow Fen, and the Twelve Mile. Forty years the river's held it. This man means to take it and let the families go on starving, and he's already killed to keep it quiet. Every one of you is standing on his evidence."

The bar went quiet. Even the generator seemed to cough more softly.

And into that quiet, unhurried, inevitable as silt, came the launch.

* * *

It came around the willow point flying the inspector's pennant, white hull, blue trim, a machine-gun mount at the bow with a canvas cover over it that fooled no one. Halloway stood at the rail in a dry coat that had never once been rained on, and beside him, to my stomach's cold surprise, sat two of his own survey men — alive, clean-shaven, hands folded, wearing the expressions of men who had been told precisely what to say.

They came alongside Vane's barge. Halloway stepped across without his boots even scuffing.

"Inspector," Vane said, and there was relief in it, naked and unguarded. "Amberdale's been making accusations. I want her detained, her papers seized—"

"Mr. Vane." Halloway's voice was mild. Mild the way ice is mild. He looked at the dynamite cradles on the bar, at the dive lines, at the barges, with the unhurried attention of a man pricing a room. "You've made rather a lot of noise for a quiet operation."

Vane's face did something complicated. "Sir, the recovery—"

"Is proceeding," Halloway said. "Which is why you're going to stand down your charges, secure your site, and await transport. There's been a change in the operational posture."

I watched Aldous Vane understand, in the space of about four seconds, what kind of man he had made his bargain with. It was almost pitiable. He had thought he was the spider. He had never noticed he was the fly the spider had been fattening.

"You can't—" Vane began. "The funds, the salvage rights, my contract—"

"Your contract," Halloway said, "was with a survey that officially drowned six weeks ago." He turned then, at last, and looked at me across the water, and his eyes were the same cold precise instruments they had been in his office at Corlow Landing. "Captain Amberdale. You're a long way from your contract terms."

"So are you, Inspector."

"Yes," he said, and almost smiled. "Isn't it convenient."

* * *

What happened next happened in the way these things do — all at once and in pieces.

Vane moved for the signal lanyard. I don't know if he meant to fire the charges out of spite, or panic, or some last mad arithmetic in which a blown hold was still a hold only he understood. It didn't matter. Tam was faster — Tam, one-armed and furious, had been easing forward along my rail since Halloway's launch appeared, and he simply reached across the gap between the boats and took the man's wrist and bent it back until the whistle lanyard dropped into the river and Vane made a sound like a kicked dog.

"Sit down," Tam told him, in the voice he used on green deckhands in weather, and Aldous Vane, survey leader, murderer, sat down on the deck of his own barge like a schoolboy.

Halloway watched all of it and did nothing. That was the thing I would carry out of that morning, the thing that sat in my chest like a swallowed stone: he watched a man he'd been sent to arrest commit assault in front of him, and he adjusted his cuffs.

"Nobody's arrested," I said to him. "You're not arresting anyone. Are you."

Halloway looked at me with something that in a better man might have been respect. "Captain, when this site is secured, the funds recovered, and the record corrected, there will be a full accounting. The accounting will say what the accounting needs to say. Six surveyors drowned. A regrettable accident. The funds returned to the treasury and disbursed through proper channels, over proper years, at proper cost." He glanced at the Brannigan boats, at my patched hull, at the two cowed survey men. "You can be part of that record or absent from it. Those are the options. I'd think a woman in your debt situation would count them carefully."

And there it was. The whole machine, laid open on the deck between us: not a cover-up being built but a cover-up already finished, waiting only for the river to cooperate. Vane had been the tool. The drowned diver, the dead radio man, the families at Corlow who had waited forty years — none of it was ever going to reach a courtroom. It was going to reach a filing cabinet.

Behind Halloway, down in the green water between the barges, a dive line trembled.

* * *

I saw it because I'd been watching the water instead of the men. A line paying out, then going taut, then jerking — not the steady rhythm of a working diver but the frantic short tugs of a signal nobody topside was answering. The tender at that line was one of Vane's hired men, and he was looking at the lanyard, and at Vane sitting on the deck holding his wrist, and at Halloway's cold face, and he was doing the arithmetic of a small man deciding which grave he'd rather be near.

"Inspector," I said. "Your operation has a diver down. And his topside man just lost his nerve."

Halloway didn't turn. "The diver's rotation ends in—"

The line jerked again. Three tugs, two tugs — the old signal, the one every river diver knows: *trapped, come now.*

Odile was already moving. I heard her voice crack across the water like a whip — "Brannigan crews, dive station, *now*" — and heard her winch dogs drop, and saw her divers hit the rail with masks in their hands. Tam was hauling on my own tender's line. Vane sat on his deck with his ruined wrist in his lap, watching the water that was about to give up everything he'd killed for, and Halloway stood very still at the rail of his clean white launch, and I understood, with a clarity that felt almost like calm, that whatever happened in the next hour, no one in authority on this river was going to lift a finger to open that hold.

The river would have to do it. Or we would.

"Captain," Tam said at my shoulder, low, his hand already on the dive locker. "The charges. Somebody's got to get down there before Vane's tender panics and fires them out of pure fear."

Below us, through forty feet of cold green water, in the drowned belly of the *Vessel's Grace*, a man was knocking on a door that had been shut for forty years.

I was already pulling on the suit.
