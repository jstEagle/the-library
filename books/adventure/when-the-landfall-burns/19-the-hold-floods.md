## 19. The Hold Floods

The river at Cutter Bend ran the color of old pewter, and it was not running so much as leaning — a slow, muscular press of water that had waited forty years for this morning and meant to have its say.

Sable Amberdale felt it through the deck plates before she heard anything: a tremor in her boots, faint and wrong, like the pulse of something buried that had decided to wake. She stood at the wheel of the *Kestrel's Wake* with one hand flat on the brass rail and watched Vane's camp come apart in orderly panic.

They had been readying the charge since before dawn. She'd counted them from two hundred yards off, glass steady in her fist: six men on the mud spit above the wreck site, a seventh working the winch barge, and below the surface — she could only guess, but she guessed well — divers down on the *Vessel's Grace*, feeding line into the dark, packing the charge against the hold's forward bulkhead where the hull had finally given up its argument with the silt.

Aldous Vane stood at the edge of the spit with his back to his men, and even at this distance Sable could read him. He had the posture of a man listening to a clock. Everything about him said *soon*. His coat was buttoned wrong. He kept touching his left cuff.

"He's going to fire it early," Tam said beside her. He'd come up without her hearing, which he never did; his arm was bound to his chest where the bar had taken him, and his face was gray around the mouth. "Look at the winch barge. They're hauling the divers' line in fast. Too fast."

"They're not hauling them in," Sable said. The understanding arrived cold and complete, the way bad news always did for her — all at once, no stages of grief, just the fact standing there. "They're clearing the water so nobody's near the blast. But they're not waiting for the divers to surface first."

Tam looked at her. "The divers are still down?"

"Two of them. Maybe three." She lowered the glass. Downstream, half-hidden behind the drowned willows of the far bank, Brannigan's three boats lay dark and quiet, engines cold, exactly where Sable had put them an hour before dawn. Odile knew. Odile was watching the same arithmetic. "Vane doesn't care if they're down. If they're in the hold when it goes, they're witnesses who can't talk."

The wind came downriver then, carrying woodsmoke and wet iron and the green rot-smell of the flooded forest, and beneath it something else — a thin, high sound, almost lost under the current. A whistle. Three short blasts, repeated.

Juno Pell, crouched at the bow rail with her maps wrapped in oilcloth against the spray, went rigid. "That's the survey team's signal," she said. "That's *our* signal. That's the diver's emergency call." Her voice cracked on the last word. "Somebody down there is still alive and knows what's coming."

Sable was already moving.

"Tam — get us in. Slow, quiet, no wake. Juno, get every lamp we own lit and strung on the stern, low over the water. If anyone comes up, I want light on them before their lungs give out." She was pulling her diving rig from under the wheelhouse bench as she talked, checking the air bottle by feel, by weight, by thirty years of habit. "And get a knife on a lanyard. A big one."

"Sable." Tam's voice had gone very careful. "You dive into that hold after the charge goes, you're diving into a collapsing wreck in flood current. That's not a rescue, that's a burial with extra steps."

She looked at him. There wasn't time for the speech she wanted to make, the one about how she'd spent twenty years taking things off the bottom and how the bottom had never once asked her whether the thing was worth money or worth saving. So she said only: "Then it's a good thing I'm the pilot, and this is my reach."

He didn't argue. That was the thing about Tam; he'd learned which battles were hers to lose.

The *Kestrel's Wake* slid downstream into the bend, silent as spilled ink.

* * *

Below the waterline, the world came apart in slow motion.

Renn Oduya had been a commercial diver for eleven years, and in eleven years he had learned one law above all others: the water does not hate you, but it does not know you exist. He clung to that law now, hanging in the black throat of the *Vessel's Grace*'s cargo hold with his partner Doss somewhere behind him and forty years of stolen settlement funds rotting in chests around his knees.

The charge was set. He'd set it himself, an hour ago, because Aldous Vane had told him to and because Renn Oduya had never yet in his life refused a direct order from a man holding his pay book. Then he'd gone back inside to check the placement, and Doss had come down after him, and then the surface line had started hauling away — not paying out, *hauling away*, the lifeline jerking upward through Renn's gloved hands like something fleeing.

Doss found him in the dark. Their helmet lamps crossed, two yellow blades in absolute blackness, and Renn saw his partner's face behind the port and understood everything at once.

"They're firing early," Doss said. The voice-vibrations carried through the water, mangled, unmistakable. "Line's cut, Renn. Line's *cut*. Somebody cut it."

"Then we swim." Renn turned toward the breach in the forward bulkhead — the gap the river had made decades ago, wide enough for a man, choked with silt and the bones of the ship's ribs. "Out through the break. Now. Don't look at the money."

But the hold had other ideas. Forty years of river-silt had packed itself into the *Grace*'s belly like grain in a silo, and the moment the two divers kicked for the breach, the silt rose. It came up around them in a brown bloom, blinding, thick as breath, and Renn felt the current find the hole behind them and begin to pull — a long, patient, irresistible drag, the whole Vessin reaching its fingers into the wreck to take it apart at last.

His lamp caught something as they fought upstream against the pull. Chests. Dozens of them, iron-banded, settled into the silt like teeth in a jaw, and one of them had burst open, and paper money — soft, swollen, gray-green, ruined beyond any counting — drifted past his faceplate in sheets, like the leaves of a drowned forest.

It looked, Renn thought absurdly, like the river had already taken its share.

Then the world went white.

There was no sound underwater, not really — there was pressure, a fist of it, closing on his chest and ears and the fluid of his eyes, and the wreck screamed around him in a language of groaning iron. The charge had been placed against the forward bulkhead, and the forward bulkhead ceased to be a wall and became a door, and through the door came the river — not seeping, not pouring, but *arriving*, a black column of it, folding the deck plates like paper.

Renn was thrown against a stanchion. Something gave in his shoulder. His lamp died. And in the new darkness he heard Doss screaming his name through the water, heard the scream turn to bubbles, heard the current take his partner sideways into the deep end of the hold where the silt had already swallowed the ladder.

He got one hand on the stanchion. He held on.

Above him, impossibly far above him, filtered down through forty feet of churning brown water, a single point of light appeared. Small. Yellow. Steady.

It began to descend.

* * *

Sable hit the thermocline at fifteen feet and the warm river became a cold one, and she let the cold sharpen her the way it always did.

Her lamp beam was a coin of yellow in a world of stirred chocolate. Visibility: nothing. Less than nothing — the blast had filled the entire bend with suspended silt, and she swam through it the way a bird flies through fog, by feel, by counting, by memory. She'd memorized the *Grace*'s lines from Juno's survey drawings two nights ago, lying in her bunk with a lamp burning while Tam pretended to sleep. Forward hold. Breach at the frame line. Ladder amidships. She descended along the anchor chain because chains don't lie, hand over hand, feeling the metal hum under her gloves with the aftershocks of the explosion.

The wreck was dying around her. She could feel it in the way the hull flexed — great slow groans rolling through the water like whale-song, the ship's skeleton shifting as the river finally, after four decades, pulled it apart. Plates were moving. Somewhere below her a bulkhead folded with a sound she felt in her teeth rather than heard.

And the current — the current was the thing no one who hadn't dived the Vessin in flood could understand. It came *through* the wreck, funneling into the breach Vane's charge had torn, and it pulled. Not hard, not violently. Patiently. Like a hand on her ankle belonging to someone who had all the time in the world.

She found the first diver by accident — his trailing safety line, severed, drifting across her lamp like a kelp strand. She followed it down and around a collapsed rib of the hull and there he was, wedged in the wreckage of the forward hold, helmet cracked, air hose fouled, conscious — she saw his eyes track her light — and pinned under a deck plate the size of a coffin lid.

No time for greeting. She got her knife out, cut the fouled hose free, jammed her own backup regulator into his port, and then put her shoulder under the plate and lifted with her legs, with her back, with every hour of winch-work and cargo-hauling the river had ever taught her. The plate moved an inch. Two. The diver wriggled. Three — and he was out, and she shoved him upward toward the coin of light that was Juno's stern lamps, and watched him go, and did not watch him for long, because the current was already spending its patience on her.

The second diver was deeper.

She found him by sound — a tapping, rhythmic, deliberate, coming up through the silt-choked dark from the aft end of the hold. Tap-tap-tap. Pause. Tap-tap-tap. Someone alive down there, knocking on iron, telling the universe he still existed. Sable followed the sound into the deep end of the hold, where the flood had opened the wreck like a gutted fish, where the burst strongboxes lay with their ruined money drifting in gray-green sheets, where her lamp caught — briefly, absurdly — a brass nameplate bolted to a bulkhead, polished by four decades of silt until it shone:

**VESSEL'S GRACE — REVENUE CUTTER — CORLOW STATION**

Forty years, she thought. Forty years this boat has been down here holding other people's sins.

The tapping led her under a fallen deck girder, into a pocket of trapped air that should not have existed — a bubble of the old atmosphere, compressed, foul, but *air*, and in it, clinging to a stanchion with one arm and holding a wrench in the other hand, was the second diver. Young. Maybe twenty. Face bloodied, eyes enormous behind his cracked port.

"I can't get my weight belt off," he said, and his voice came out thin and strange in the trapped air. "The buckle's jammed. The current — every time I try, it—"

"Stop fighting it," Sable said. "Fighting it is how it wins."

She wedged herself against the stanchion beside him, hooked her legs through the girder, and got both hands on his belt. The buckle was jammed with silt and bent plate. Above them the wreck groaned — a long, descending note, the sound of a structure deciding something. Silt rained down through the water around them, soft as snowfall, and each flake of it was a ton of river deciding where to settle.

"Hold your breath," she said. "On three, we drop together, and you kick for the ladder lights. Don't stop. Don't look back. Don't look at the money."

"What money?" the boy said, and almost laughed, and the laugh broke into a sob.

"That's the spirit."

The buckle would not give. So she stopped trying to unbuckle it and cut it — her knife through the webbing, three strokes, the boy's weight suddenly hers — and then the wreck made its decision. The girder above them shifted. The pocket of trapped air collapsed with a cough, and the hold came down around them in a slow avalanche of iron and silt, and Sable did the only thing there was to do: she wrapped both arms around the boy, kicked off from the stanchion, and let the current take them both.

For nine seconds — she would count them later, lying awake, and it would always be nine — she was not a pilot or a captain or a salvager. She was cargo. The river carried them through the breach in the forward bulkhead, tumbling, the boy's weight tearing at her shoulders, her lamp gone somewhere, her air gauge a small dead star she couldn't read. She felt the wreck's ribs pass over her like the fingers of a closing hand. She felt the open water take them. And then she kicked, and the boy kicked, and the two of them rose together through forty feet of brown blindness toward the yellow coins of Juno's lamps, which had multiplied — she saw, breaking the surface, that Brannigan's boats had moved in too, lanterns strung bow to stern, a constellation laid down on the water.

Hands took the boy. Hands took her. The sky, when she finally got her face into it, was the color of pewter going to silver, and the first thing she heard clearly was Odile Brannigan's voice, flat and furious and relieved all at once:

"—third one's still unaccounted. Vane's man says the third diver surfaced before the blast. Vane's man is *lying*, Sable. I've got his line reel here and it never paid out."

Sable hung on the ladder of her own stern, lungs full of fire, hair plastered to her skull, and looked downstream to where Aldous Vane stood on the mud spit. He was watching the rescue boats. His men were loading the strongboxes they'd rigged onto a barge — the ones the blast had shaken loose, the easy pickings — and Vane himself had not moved. He was watching her the way a man watches weather he has decided not to believe in.

And then he turned, and shouted an order, and the barge began to move — downstream, toward the narrows, heavy-laden, riding low, carrying whatever portion of forty years of theft the river had consented to give up.

Tam's hand closed on Sable's arm. "Air bottle's nearly empty and you're bleeding," he said. "Captain. You're bleeding."

"Later." She spat river water and hauled herself onto the deck, and the cold hit her all at once, and she shook so hard her teeth chattered, and none of it mattered, because she was looking at the barge, and doing arithmetic. One tide left. One barge, heavy and slow, running for the narrows where a man with a strongbox and no witnesses intended to disappear forever.

"Get me a blanket," Sable said, "and get me dry, and get this boat turned around."

Juno was kneeling beside the young diver, wrapping him, and she looked up with an expression Sable had never seen on her — not fear anymore. Something harder, forged overnight out of fear and refusal. "The third diver," Juno said quietly. "Odile's right. Nobody surfaced. Which means Vane sealed a man in that hold and fired the charge anyway, and now he's running with the money, and Halloway's launch is still an hour downstream."

Sable pulled the blanket around her shoulders and looked at the bend — at the dying wreck exhaling its last bubbles, at the barge shrinking downstream, at the water that had tried to keep her and failed.

"Then we're done waiting for the law," she said. "All hands. We run him down before the narrows close."

Behind them, unnoticed by everyone but the river, the *Vessel's Grace* gave one final groan and slipped another foot into the deep water, keeping — as she had kept for forty years — the last of what she'd been given to hold.
