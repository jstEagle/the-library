## 20. Deep Water Justice

The river had gone quiet in the way it did before it decided something.

Sable stood on the foredeck of the *Kestrel's Wake* and watched the brown water close over the place where Aldous Vane had gone down. There was no splash worth remembering — just a man in a fine survey coat, arms wrapped around a strongbox that weighed more than his conscience ever had, sinking with the slow certainty of an anchor finding its home. The current took the last of his bubbles downstream, and then even those were gone.

Nobody spoke. Odile Brannigan stood at the rail of her lead boat ten yards off, her scarred hands flat on the gunwale, watching the water as if she might see him pass beneath them, pale and clutching his prize, all the way to the sea. Tam leaned against the capstan with his arm bound tight against his ribs, his face gray from pain and blood loss both, but his eyes were dry. Juno Pell sat on the deck planking with her back against the mast, the ledger clutched to her chest like a child she'd pulled from a fire.

"He went under fighting," Tam said at last. "Wouldn't let go of it."

"They never do," Sable said. "That's how you know what they are."

She thought about saying something else — some words for the dead, or for the diver Vane had sealed in the hold two nights ago, whose name they still didn't know, whose body was somewhere below them in the flooded rooms of the *Vessel's Grace*. But the river didn't need eulogies. It kept its own accounts, and it had just settled one.

* * *

Halloway came alongside within the hour.

His launch cut through the wreckage-littered water with the crisp efficiency of everything he did — no wasted motion, no wasted feeling. He stepped aboard the *Wake* without asking leave, which told Sable everything she needed to know about how the morning was going to go. His coat was dry. His boots were clean. He had not been in the water, and he would not be going into it, and somehow that cleanliness was the most obscene thing on the river that day.

"Captain Amberdale." He surveyed the deck: the exhausted crew, the Brannigan boats rafted near, the dive lines still trailing into the current off Cutter Bend. "Report."

"You have eyes," Sable said. "You can see where we are."

"I see a wreck site, three salvage vessels, and one drowned fugitive." He produced a notebook — of course he had a notebook; the man probably slept with it under his pillow — and clicked open a pen. "I also see that the hold of the revenue cutter has been breached and partially pumped. Which means there is recoverable material down there. Government material." He looked up, and his voice went smooth and official, the voice of a man reading from a document he'd written long before arriving. "Under the Emergency Recovery Act of the Corlow District, all assets recovered from the *Vessel's Grace*, including specie, scrip, registered bonds, and associated strongboxes, are hereby designated property of the Colonial Revenue Office. I am instructed to secure them for transport to Corlow."

"Instructed by whom?"

"That is not your concern."

"See, that's funny," Sable said, "because I've read your actual orders. The ones in your cabin. The ones that say the survey team is to be listed as drowned and the funds recovered quietly, no inquiry, no witnesses. So when you say *instructed*, Inspector, I find myself wondering who's doing the instructing, and whether they know you've already decided how this ends."

Something flickered behind Halloway's eyes — not guilt; she doubted the man owned any — but calculation, quick and cold, recalibrating. He closed the notebook.

"Captain. You have performed a service. You will be paid. Triple rate, as contracted, plus hazard compensation. In exchange, you will sign a statement confirming that the events of the past nine days occurred substantially as follows—" and he began, in a flat bureaucratic drone, to dictate a version of history in which Vane was a lone criminal, the divers were lost in an accident, the sealed hold was a structural failure, and the money went back to the government vaults where it had always belonged.

"—and the families?" Juno said. She'd risen from the deck without anyone noticing, and now she stood at the rail, small and steady, the ledger still pressed against her chest. "The families of Harrow's Bend and Millbrook and the lower settlements. The ones the money was taken from forty years ago. The ones who paid the levy twice because the first payment vanished into this river along with the *Grace*. What do they get in your statement?"

Halloway regarded her the way a man regards weather. "The matter predates the current administration. Precedent cases of this age are resolved through archival review, not restitution—"

"Meaning nothing," Juno said. "Meaning a file in a drawer."

"Meaning process," Halloway corrected. "Process is how civilization works, Miss Pell. You of all people should appreciate that. You're a cartographer. You draw the map. You don't get to redraw the territory."

"No," Sable said. "But sometimes the territory redraws itself."

She turned and looked at the water over Cutter Bend. The current ran deep and green-black there, over the drowned hull of the *Vessel's Grace*, over the strongrooms where forty years of stolen settlement money still sat in its rotted crates — most of it, anyway. They'd raised what the divers could reach before the explosion: four strongboxes, two intact, their contents sealed in oilcloth and wax, worth enough to buy every boat at Corlow Landing twice over. Worth enough to buy Sable's freedom from the river, if freedom was something you could buy, which she had spent thirty years believing and no longer did.

Worth enough, too, for Halloway to carry back to whatever office had sent him, where it would vanish into the same drawer as the precedent cases and the drowned surveyors and the truth.

"Odile," she called across the water.

Brannigan looked up from her own deck. Ten days ago Sable would have said there wasn't a force on the river that could put those two captains on the same side of anything. Then Vane had sealed a man in a hold, and Halloway had started writing his tidy statement, and the arithmetic had changed.

"You heard all that?"

"Every word." Odile's voice carried easily across the gap. "Man talks like a tax notice."

"What's your fleet's position on government property?"

Odile smiled, and it was not a warm smile. It was the smile she'd worn at Widow's Tooth, twenty years ago, hauling Sable's first command off a rock with a winch and a curse and never letting her forget it since. "My fleet's position," she said, "is that we're salvage crews, not thieves. And salvage law says what the river gives up belongs to whoever the river gave it up *for*. That money was stolen from settlements. We know the names. Some of us grew up with the grandchildren." She spat over the rail, ceremonially, in Halloway's direction. "So as far as my fleet's concerned, the only government property on this river today is your launch, Inspector, and you're welcome to take it back to Corlow."

Halloway's jaw tightened. "You understand I have authority here."

"You have paper," Sable said. "Out here, the river has authority. And I'm its pilot."

* * *

They did it at slack tide, because even justice should be done properly.

Sable took the *Wake* out past Cutter Bend herself, alone at the wheel while Tam watched from the rail with his good arm and Odile's boats held station astern like mourners. Two miles down, where the channel dropped away into the old scour pool below the bend — sixty feet of cold black water that no dredge had ever touched and no chart bothered to mark — she brought the boat around and let the current swing her broadside.

The four strongboxes came up from the hold one last time. Sable had spent the night thinking about them, lying awake in her bunk listening to the river breathe against the hull, doing the arithmetic she'd been avoiding since the moment she'd understood what was down there. The fortune that could buy her freedom. The debt that hung over the *Wake* like a second anchor. All she had to do was say yes to Halloway, sign his statement, pocket the triple pay, and let the money ride to Corlow in a government wagon, and she'd never owe another soul.

And the families would wait another forty years. And the diver in the hold would be a line in an accident report. And Vane would be a regrettable incident, and Halloway would be promoted, and the river would keep its mouth shut the way it always did, because rivers don't testify.

Unless someone made them.

"We could split it," Tam had said that morning, quietly, the way men say things they already know the answer to. "Four ways between the crews. Nobody would ever—"

"Everybody would always know," Sable had said. "That's the trouble with us, Tam. We're river people. We remember everything."

Now she stood at the rail and watched the boxes go over the side, one after another, roped together and weighted with scrap iron from the *Grace*'s own hull, so that they fell fast and straight and did not drift. The water swallowed them without ceremony. Sixty feet down, into the scour pool, into the dark where no winch would reach and no act of parliament could follow, the stolen money of Harrow's Bend and Millbrook sank beyond everyone's reach forever.

It felt, Sable thought, like closing a wound. Not healing it — closing it, so it could finally start to scar.

"Some will call that destruction of evidence," Halloway said behind her. He had insisted on coming aboard for this, perhaps because he couldn't believe she'd actually do it, or perhaps because he wanted witnesses of his own. His voice was carefully level. "Destruction of government assets. That's a chargeable offense, Captain. Piracy, in some jurisdictions."

"It's not evidence," Sable said. "Evidence is things that prove something happened. This is the thing itself. And the thing itself was never yours." She turned to face him. "You want to charge me, Inspector? Write it up. *Salvage captain scuttles stolen funds rather than surrender them to the office that ordered a cover-up.* Put that in your report. See how far up the chain it gets before somebody starts asking why a cover-up needed ordering."

For a long moment Halloway said nothing. The current pushed gently against the hull, patient as a creditor.

Then Sable nodded to Juno, and Juno came forward, and held out the ledger.

It was a plain thing — oilskin-bound, water-stained, half its pages warped from a decade in a fisher's stilt-house — but inside it was the whole rotten architecture of the *Vessel's Grace*: the original manifests, the levy rolls from six settlements, the signatures of the revenue officers who'd loaded the strongboxes and scuttled their own ship, and — folded into the back cover, in Vane's own hand, recovered from his camp desk at the wreck site — the survey leader's private correspondence arranging the fake disappearance, the bribe schedule, and a single line, dated eleven days ago, that named the radio operator and the word *problem* in the same sentence.

"Juno Pell kept it safe for ten years," Sable said. "Through Vane hunting her, through your orders telling her she was drowned, through all of it. She could have sold it to Brannigan. She could have traded it to Vane for her life. Instead she carried it upriver in a fish crate." She took the ledger from Juno's hands and held it out to Halloway. "So here's your process, Inspector. Here's your archival review. Every name, every figure, every signature — including yours, in the margin of page forty, where you countersigned the quiet-recovery directive. An inquiry isn't optional anymore. Either you open one yourself, in Corlow, with your name attached and your career staked on it — or this goes to the *Corlow Gazette*, to the settlement councils, and to every magistrate between here and the capital, and the inquiry happens with you in the dock instead of at the table."

Halloway looked at the ledger for a long time. Around them the river ran on, indifferent, carrying silt and sunlight and the memory of everything ever sunk in it.

"You understand," he said finally, taking it, "that if I do this, I don't survive it either way. Inquiry or dock. My career ends at whichever door I choose."

"Yes," Sable said. "That's what justice costs. We checked the price before we bought."

He almost smiled. It was the closest thing to a human expression she'd seen on him in nine days. "Vane said you were the only pilot fool enough to run the flood. He undersold you." He tucked the ledger inside his coat, against his chest, where a decent man would have carried a letter from home. "The inquiry convenes in Corlow in three weeks. I'll want statements. Signed. From all of you."

"You'll get them," Sable said. "True ones."

"And the money at the bottom of the scour pool?"

Sable looked down at the green-black water sliding past the hull. Somewhere under it lay the price of her freedom, resting easy at last, keeping faith with the families it had been stolen from until the law caught up enough to give it back to them in figures and receipts — poorer than it had been, maybe, but honest.

"The river's holding it," she said. "River's the only party in this whole affair nobody's ever caught lying."

* * *

They ran downstream that evening, ahead of the dying light, because the dry season had taken the last of the channel's patience and the *Wake* had a hull that wanted Corlow's careening ways and a crew that wanted beds that didn't roll.

At the Devil's Elbow, where the moonless run had nearly killed them all two nights ago, the water ran low and glassy over the rocks, and Sable took her through on memory and sound alone one final time — the hiss of the shoal off the north point, the deep-water chuckle under the leaning cedar, the particular silence of the hole where the current doubled back on itself. Her hands moved on the wheel without consulting her. The river spoke; she answered. It was, she thought, the oldest conversation she knew, and she was no longer sure she wanted it to end.

Behind her, Odile's boats ran shotgun down the middle of the channel, lanterns lit, heading north to Corlow with contracts to negotiate and a fair share to claim. Ahead, the first stars were coming out over the low hills, and the smell of woodsmoke drifted off the lower settlements — Harrow's Bend, Millbrook, all the places whose names were written in a ledger now traveling toward the light.

Tam came up beside her, moving carefully around his bound ribs, and handed her a mug of the terrible chicory he insisted on calling coffee.

"Halloway'll try to bury it anyway," he said. "Men like him always do."

"Maybe." Sable drank. The chicory was awful. The evening was perfect. "But now he has to bury it in daylight, in front of the whole district, with Juno checking his figures. That's not nothing, Tam. That's the most justice this river's seen in forty years."

"And the money?"

"Gone where money can't hurt anybody." She glanced at him. "Regretting it?"

Tam considered the question honestly, the way he considered everything. "Ask me again when the debt collectors catch up at Corlow," he said. "But no. I don't think I am. Feels lighter, somehow. Boat and all."

Ahead, the landing lights of Corlow pricked the dusk — a scatter of yellow flames along the black water, lamps being lit in windows, in taverns, in the harbor office where a wire was already clicking out the first garbled rumors of what had happened upriver. Three weeks until the inquiry. Three weeks of statements and reckonings and the whole district learning, name by name, what had been taken from them and what had come back.

Sable eased the wheel over and brought the *Kestrel's Wake* home through the last of the flood, and found she was already wondering what the spring rise would look like.
