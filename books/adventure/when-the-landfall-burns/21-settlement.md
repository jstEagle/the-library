## 21. Settlement

The inquiry came to Corlow Landing in the last week of the dry season, when the river had shrunk to its bones and the mudflats lay cracked and pale as old crockery. They set up in the customs house, a building Juno Pell had walked past a hundred times without ever entering — a squat stone structure with a slate roof gone green at the eaves and windows fogged with salt-spray from the estuary. Now there were clerks inside, and ledgers, and a stenographer with ink-stained fingers who wrote down everything anyone said as though the words themselves might be worth money.

Perhaps they were. Everything else about the Vessin had turned out to be.

Juno sat on a bench outside in the shade of an awning, waiting her turn, and watched the families come up from the ferry dock. They arrived in ones and twos at first, then in clusters, drawn by letters that had gone out over the inspectorate's seal — letters that named them, impossibly, as heirs of the *Vessel's Grace*. Forty years was a long time. Some of the original claimants were dead, and their children came instead, gray themselves now, holding the letters at arm's length as if they might bite. One woman brought her mother's funeral photograph in a tin frame. A fisherman came still wearing his oilskins, as though he'd been called off his boat and hadn't thought to change.

They did not look like people expecting justice. They looked like people braced for disappointment, the way you brace against a cold current when you have no choice but to cross.

Juno knew the feeling intimately. She had spent six weeks being interviewed, first by Halloway's own clerks — before Halloway fell — then by a tribunal sent down from the capital: three magistrates, a surveyor-general, and a thin, patient woman named Advocate Serrano who asked questions like she was lowering a plumb line into dark water. Where were you on the night the radio went silent. What did Aldous Vane say when he gave the order to abandon camp. How many times did he consult the tide tables, and whose tables were they.

She had told it all. The staged disappearance, the gear left behind like stage dressing, the dead-drop notes in Vane's precise cartographer's hand — *the same hand that drew my charts*, she'd said, and seen the magistrates exchange glances. She told them about the diver sealed in the hold, though she'd had to stop twice to get through it, and Advocate Serrano had waited without hurry, pen motionless, until Juno could speak again.

And she had given them the ledger. Vane's ledger, recovered from the strongbox he'd drowned clutching, dried page by page over a charcoal brazier in Tam's galley while the whole crew took turns watching it like a sick child. It listed every payment, every diversion, every name Vane had bought or blackmailed across four decades of quiet theft — including, in a column near the end, entries initialed *R.H.*

Halloway.

* * *

She saw him only once during the inquiry weeks, and it was by accident. She had gone down to the landing at dusk to sketch the moorings — an old habit, hands needing work even when her head was empty — and found him standing at the rail of the government launch, alone, in civilian clothes she'd never seen him wear. No inspector's braid. No case file under his arm. He looked smaller out of uniform, the way a building looks smaller once you've been inside it.

He saw her and did not look away, which she granted him as a kind of courage.

"They're sending me to Merrow Point," he said. "Coastal lights inspection. Lighthouses." A sound that might have been a laugh, if laughter could rust. "Forty years of counting other men's thefts, and they'll have me counting lamp-oil."

"Is that a punishment?" Juno asked. "Or a retirement?"

"It's both," he said. "It's usually both." He turned back to the water, where the evening light lay along the channel like a seam of brass. "You understand I couldn't do what she did. Amberdale. Scuttle the hold. That money belonged to the Crown the moment it was entered in a ledger."

"And yet it never reached the Crown," Juno said. "It reached Vane. And Vane reached the bottom of the river. Funny how property works."

Halloway was quiet a long moment. Down the landing, a lantern was lit, and its light wobbled across the wet boards toward them and stopped short.

"She handed me the evidence," he said at last, "and made me watch her sink the rest. Do you know what she said? She said, *'Now it's not salvage. Now it's testimony.'*" He shook his head slowly. "I reported it exactly as it happened. Every word. It will follow me to Merrow Point and beyond."

"Good," said Juno.

He almost smiled. Almost. Then he stepped back onto the launch, and the rail caught the lantern light, and by morning the berth was empty and someone else's pennant flew from the halyard.

* * *

The reading of the restitution rolls took place on a Tuesday, in the customs house hall, with the doors thrown open because the room was too small and the season too hot. Advocate Serrano read the names aloud herself, unhurried, each one given its full weight, and behind her a clerk chalked figures onto a board: sums computed from the ledger, indexed across forty years, converted into the currency of the present day. The numbers were large enough that when the first one was read, a man in the back row stood up involuntarily, as if pulled by strings, and then sat down again, embarrassed, and wept into his hat.

Juno stood at the edge of the crowd and watched the faces more than the figures. There is a particular expression that crosses a person when a debt older than their memory is suddenly declared payable — not joy, exactly, and not relief either. Something closer to vertigo. The fisherwoman with the tin photograph held it against her chest through the whole reading, and when her name came — *Marta Ellery, heir of Tomas Ellery, stevedore, lost wages and seized holdings, year thirty-one* — she didn't move for a long moment, and then she laughed, one sharp note, the way people laugh at funerals when grief and arithmetic collide.

Afterward, in the doorway, Marta Ellery stopped beside Juno and looked out at the river, low and gleaming and full of secrets finally spent.

"My mother used to say the Grace went down with all our futures aboard," she said. "We thought she meant it as a curse."

"Maybe she meant it as a chart," Juno said. "Something to steer by."

Marta looked at her sideways. "You're the map woman. The one who hid in the stilt-house."

"I'm the one who kept the ledger dry," Juno said. "The maps came later."

"Well." Marta turned the tin frame over in her hands, once, twice. "Whatever you're owed, I hope they pay it. And whatever you're not owed—" she nodded at the river, at the whole shining wreck-strewn length of it— "I hope you take that too."

* * *

Odile Brannigan sailed north on the Thursday, three days after the final hearing, and Juno went down to see her off, mostly because Sable was going and Juno had learned, somewhere in the flooded channels of that terrible season, that she preferred to stand near Sable Amberdale when the world required witnessing.

The *Magpie's Answer* rode high and clean, patched hull gleaming with fresh pitch, and Odile stood at her rail with the fair-share contract folded in her breast pocket like a letter from a lover. The tribunal had ratified it — salvage rights on the upper Vessin, shared equitably among independent captains, Brannigan fleet included, on condition of open books and joint survey. Odile had signed it in front of the magistrates with a flourish that made the youngest of them flinch.

"You know what this means," Odile said to Sable, leaning over the rail as the lines came off. "Every season, same river, same channels. You'll never be rid of me."

"I know," Sable said. "That's the point of a contract."

Odile grinned, and for a moment the old hunger in her face was transfigured into something almost gentle. She spat ceremonially into the water — a captain's blessing, or a challenge, hard to say — and threw a small canvas bag down to the dock. Sable caught it. Inside, Juno saw later, was a brass fitting off the *Grace*'s rail, polished bright, the kind of thing a rival keeps as a trophy and returns as a gift.

The fleet went north on the morning tide, three boats in line astern, whistles sounding, and the sound carried all the way up the landing and into the town like a promise being kept at volume.

* * *

Sable offered Juno a bunk the same week. "Permanent," she said, in the galley of the *Kestrel's Wake*, over tea brewed strong enough to strip varnish. "Tam wants a proper chart locker. I want a chart maker who doesn't lie to me. You want—" she paused, choosing the word with a pilot's care— "somewhere to put things down."

Juno looked at the chart table, scarred and salt-ringed, and felt something in her chest unclench that she hadn't known was clenched. Six weeks of hiding, of dead-drops, of watching a man she'd trusted order a diver sealed into iron darkness — six weeks of holding truth like a live coal — and here was a woman offering her a table to draw on.

"I want to map it honestly," Juno said. "All of it. The bars that shift, the cuts that close, the depths nobody's sounded since the Grace went down. Not the Admiralty version. The true one."

"That'll take years," Tam said from the doorway, not looking up from the pump gasket he was rebuilding.

"Good," said Juno. "I'm tired of things that take weeks."

* * *

Her first honest chart was of Cutter Bend.

She drew it in the evenings, after the day's sounding runs, working by lamplight in the new chart locker Tam had built — cedar-lined, brass-cornered, smelling of shavings and river damp. She laid down the channel as it truly ran that season, marked the shifting bar where the *Kestrel's Wake* had grounded and bled, noted the cut that had closed for good and the deep hole below the bend where the *Vessel's Grace* still lay, broken and emptied and finally allowed to rest. At the site of the wreck she drew no treasure mark, no hazard symbol. Just a small notation in her own careful hand:

*Drowned here: one diver, name unknown; one thief, name known. River holds both. Let it.*

When she finished, she carried the sheet up on deck and showed Sable, who studied it for a long time in the last light, thumb resting on the little notation without reading it aloud.

"Accurate?" Sable asked.

"As anything on this river can be," Juno said. "Which is to say — true today. Ask me again next season."

Sable smiled, and Juno understood, in that moment, why crews followed this woman into floodwater and firelight and worse. It wasn't the miles. It had never been the miles. It was that Sable Amberdale looked at a thing made true and knew exactly what it was worth.

"First of many," Sable said, and rolled the chart, and gave it back to her like a commission.

* * *

On the last night of the inquiry, Juno walked out along the landing past dark. The town behind her was quieter than she'd ever known it — the way a place goes quiet after a fever breaks, everyone sleeping the sleep of the convalescent. Ahead, the river lay broad and silvered under a rising moon, running easy in its narrowed bed, carrying nothing now but water and the season's last leaves.

At the far end of the dock, a figure stood looking north. Juno nearly turned back — she had spent enough of her life around men watching rivers at night to last several lifetimes — but then the figure moved into the moonlight and she saw it was Marta Ellery, the tin photograph gone, replaced by a paper: the receipt of restitution, folded once, held in both hands like a compass bearing.

"They're reopening the stevedore hall," Marta said, without turning. "My grandfather's hall. With the money. First time in forty years there'll be work on this landing that isn't salvage or smuggling."

"That's a good use of a curse," Juno said.

Marta laughed — the real laugh this time, unguarded — and then grew quiet, looking upriver, where the channel bent away into darkness toward Cutter Bend and everything that had happened there.

"They say the river gives back what it takes," she said. "Forty years I thought that was just talk. Something fishermen say." She tapped the receipt against her palm. "But it's not talk, is it. It's arithmetic. It just takes its time."

"No," Juno agreed. "It's not talk."

Below them, the water slid past, dark and patient, keeping its last few secrets — the ones it would keep always, the ones no ledger would ever name. Juno stood beside Marta Ellery in the moonlight and felt the season settle over her like silt over a wreck: gently, completely, and for good.

Tomorrow she would begin the soundings for the new chart of the middle reach. Tomorrow the work would start that would take years, that would outlast the inquiry and the scandal and even, perhaps, the telling of this story. But tonight there was only the river, and the receipt in a stranger's hands, and the strange, unfamiliar weight of a debt paid — and somewhere downstream, at dawn, a boat would be coming up the channel with its captain at the wheel, running home on the morning tide.
