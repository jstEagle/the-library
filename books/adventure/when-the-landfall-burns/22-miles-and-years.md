## 22. Miles and Years

The river at dawn was a sheet of hammered pewter, and the *Kestrel's Wake* ran down it like a blade drawn slow.

Sable stood at the wheel with her hands light on the spokes, feeling the boat answer her before she asked. That was the thing about a mended hull — you learned its new voice the way you learned a scarred hand. The patch below the waterline was Tam's work, three weeks of careening and caulking and cursing on the mudflat above Cutter Bend, and now she rode with a faint sweetness in her turn to starboard, a little extra song in the planking when the current pressed her flanks. Not worse. Only different. A boat that had been broken and put back was never the same boat again, and neither was the woman who'd held her together with rope and stubbornness while the dry season tried to eat them both.

Mist stood in the reeds on either bank, knee-high and luminous, burning off in gold rags as the sun came up behind them. A heron lifted from the shallows with a sound like a sail filling. Somewhere upstream — miles behind them now, days behind them — the Vessin narrowed into its dying channels, and the wreck of the *Vessel's Grace* lay scoured and empty in deep water where Sable had sent her, hold flooded past any diver's reach, past any winch, past any government man with a ledger and a lie.

She had dreamed about that deep water twice since. Both times she woke calm.

"Tea." Tam appeared at her elbow with two chipped tin mugs, steam curling off them into the cool air. He looked older than he had six weeks ago — there was a new stiffness in his left arm where the bar strike had bruised the bone deep, and gray coming in at his temples that hadn't been there before — but his eyes were the same: steady, skeptical, loyal past all reason. He handed her a mug and drank from his own, watching the banks slide by. "You're smiling."

"I'm allowed."

"You're never allowed. It unsettles the crew." He nodded aft, where young Pell — not Juno, but the Corlow boy they'd taken on to replace the lost time, all elbows and questions — was coiling lines with the exaggerated seriousness of someone who'd heard too many stories about what happened to careless crews on this river. "He'll think we've struck fortune."

"We did," Sable said. "Just not the kind you can spend."

Tam snorted into his tea. But he didn't argue, and that was new too. Six weeks ago he would have argued. Six weeks ago, standing on the landing at Corlow while the flood tore past and the officials waved triple pay like bait on a hook, he had argued every step of the way upriver — *the contract smells of salvage, Sable, not rescue; Halloway's eyes are wrong; no good comes out of Cutter Bend.* He had been right about nearly all of it, and she had gone anyway, and somehow they had both come home whole, or near enough, and neither of them had said yet what that meant.

The river made the decision easy for a while. They ran down through the morning country, past the fisher stilt-houses where Juno Pell had hidden through the worst of it, past Widow's Tooth lying quiet and brown-sided in low water, a different animal entirely from the white-throated monster that had nearly put them under on the way up. The falling river had changed everything — bars exposed like the backs of sleeping animals, channels braided and shallow, the whole reach wearing its bones outside its skin. But Sable knew these bones. She had always known them. What was different now was that knowing them felt less like a cage and less like an escape route and more like — she searched for the word as the *Wake* slid through the Narrows, kissing the green side of the channel by memory alone —

More like a language she was finally fluent in.

* * *

They took the midday tide easy and moored at Corlow Landing in the long gold light of afternoon.

The landing had healed faster than the people. New pilings where Brannigan's fleet had battered the dock on their way up; fresh tar on the planks; the customs shed repainted a blue so bright it looked embarrassed. And everywhere, small signs of the money doing its work — a new roof on the chandlery, a second derrick at the coal yard, the widow Maren's kids in actual shoes, running the boards and shrieking. The inquiry had taken its slow bureaucratic months, but Halloway's forced hand had done what justice on the Vessin never managed on its own: the settlement funds' value had come home, parcel by parcel, to the families whose names had been robbed forty years ago in the hold of a revenue cutter. Some of those names were carved on stones in the Corlow churchyard now. Sable had walked past them one evening and tipped her hat, and felt something in her chest unclench that she hadn't known was clenched.

Odile Brannigan had sailed north a week ago, three boats riding high and empty of salvage but heavy with a fair-share contract from the Corlow consortium — honest work, honest split, her signature on paper that would hold. They'd parted at the dock with a grip of forearms and no speeches, which between the two of them was a eulogy and a love letter combined. *"You ever get tired of being right, Amberdale,"* Odile had said, *"come north. I hear the Graywater pays double for pilots who can read a river blindfolded."*

*"I'm never tired of being right,"* Sable had told her. *"It's my only virtue."*

And Odile had laughed, loud and real, and the sound had followed Sable around for days like warmth off a stove.

Juno was waiting on the dock. Of course she was. The cartographer had a tent of survey instruments and a rolled chart under her arm and the particular restless energy of a person who has rehearsed a speech. Her hair was shorter than it had been upriver — cut with a knife, by the look of it, in some moment of decision Sable could guess at. The last of Vane's shadow had burned off her the way mist burns off the reeds.

"Sable." She stepped aboard without asking, which was also new. "The Survey Office posted the position yesterday."

"What position?"

"Pilot-in-residence. Corlow station." Juno unrolled the chart on the deck boards and weighted the corners with winch handles, and there it was: the upper Vessin, drawn in Juno's own careful hand, every bar and bend and hidden cut rendered honestly for the first time in forty years — including the cut through the Narrows that only Sable had ever run, now named, in small neat letters, *Amberdale Passage*. "They want someone who knows the river in flood and in drought. Someone the other pilots will actually listen to. Someone who—" Juno stopped, and started over, plainer. "They want you. Permanent posting. House on the bluff. Your name on the charts."

Sable looked at the chart for a long time.

All her life she had measured herself in miles. Miles put behind her, miles of river between her and whatever she was fleeing — the debts, the smuggling charge she'd let Odile wear, the wrecks, the winters, the versions of herself she hadn't wanted to be. Every contract signed had been a door kicked open. Every departure, a small victory. She had thought of Corlow Landing as a place you loaded cargo and left, the way you'd think of a breath: necessary, brief, not the point.

But standing here, with the chart under her boots and the evening coming down soft on the water and Tam's silence beside her saying everything his mouth wouldn't — she counted differently now. She counted the seasons she'd come back to this river. The spring floods she'd ridden out at anchor in the lower bends because she couldn't bear to be anywhere else when the ice went out. The dry seasons spent learning the bones. The people. Maren's kids in their new shoes. Odile's laugh. Juno's stubborn, honest ink.

The miles had never bought her anything. The returning had.

"I'll need the house roofed properly," she said. "The bluff catches weather."

Juno blinked. Then grinned, quick and bright, and pulled the contract from inside her coat, already signed by three office men, already waiting.

Tam read it over her shoulder and said nothing at all, which from Tam was a wedding toast.

* * *

They ran downstream again at dawn, because Sable wanted to sign the papers at the Survey Office with river water still drying on her boots, and because — if she was honest, and the river had taught her honesty the hard way — she wanted one more run of the morning reach before it belonged to charts instead of memory.

The *Kestrel's Wake* went down through the mist with the current helping and the sun coming up ahead of them now, gilding the water all the way to the horizon. At the Devil's Elbow the channel coiled tight around a bluff of black rock, and Sable took it without touching the wheel, reading the boil lines and the drift of the foam, hearing the deep note of the channel against the shallow scrape of the bar, and the boat threaded the needle like a word spoken perfectly in a language only the two of them spoke.

At the mouth of the Narrows she slowed, and looked up at the small letters on Juno's chart, pinned now beside the wheel: *Amberdale Passage.*

"Funny," Tam said, beside her, tea in hand, the boy coiling lines astern, the river running wide and bright toward Corlow and everything after. "All these years I figured you'd die somewhere far from here, running from something."

"And now?"

"Now I figure you'll die right here," he said comfortably, "an old woman, in a house on the bluff, having run *toward* something your whole life, and everyone on this river will know exactly where to bury you."

Sable laughed, and put her hands on the wheel, and turned the bow for home.
