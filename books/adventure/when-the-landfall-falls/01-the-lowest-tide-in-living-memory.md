## 1. The Lowest Tide in Living Memory

The tide was wrong before Quentin Kavanagh noticed it was wrong.

That was the thing about a decade of river surveys—you learned water the way other people learned faces. You could pick a lie out of a current at forty meters, feel a false reading in your gut before the instruments confirmed it. So when the gauge on the Karst Coast station read 2.3 meters below chart datum at 06:40 on a Tuesday in late October, Quentin's first instinct wasn't wonder. It was suspicion. They checked the sensor cable, checked for silt fouling, checked the backup pressure transducer that hadn't been calibrated since the last contractor came through and probably never would be.

All three agreed. The sea had gone somewhere.

Quentin stood on the survey platform with a thermos of coffee going cold in one hand and watched the ocean do something oceans were not supposed to do. The tide tables said high water had passed an hour ago. The tide tables were, as far as Quentin could tell, being ignored. The water kept withdrawing—slowly, without drama, the way a guest leaves a party early, taking its coat and not saying goodbye. Mudflats that should have been three fathoms under lay steaming in the early light. A sandbar no chart acknowledged ran out from the headland like a brown tongue, maybe two hundred meters, maybe more.

And beyond it, black rock.

Quentin had worked this coast for eleven months. Long enough to know it was supposed to be featureless—a drowned limestone shelf, gently shelving, nothing to see. The Karst Coast got its name from the hills behind, not the water in front. The hydrographic charts showed blank blue with a courteous scattering of depth soundings, the cartographic equivalent of a shrug. Nobody surveyed here because there was nothing to survey. That was precisely why Quentin had taken the contract. Eleven months of measuring a place where nothing interesting could possibly happen, filing reports nobody read, answering to a regional office that couldn't have found the coast on a map if you spotted them the coastline.

Invisibility, Quentin had learned, was a discipline. You didn't achieve it once; you maintained it, daily, like dental hygiene. You used the name Q. Kavanagh on all correspondence and let the initial do its work. You never mentioned your mother. You never mentioned her books, or the documentary, or the endowed chair, or the thirty-year argument she'd been having with the entire field of archaeology about a civilization everyone else called mythical. You took contracts in places with bad cell reception and you became, professionally speaking, a function: *the surveyor*, *the consultant*, *that person who sends the spreadsheets*.

It had worked for ten years. It was working right now, this morning, until the sea decided otherwise.

Quentin set down the coffee, pulled on boots, and went down to look.

* * *

The mud was the first surprise. It wasn't coastal mud—it was riverine, fine-grained and grey-blue, banded with layers of charcoal and crushed shell. Quentin had spent enough years knee-deep in river cores to read sediment like handwriting, and this handwriting said *inland*. This was flood deposit. This was the kind of muck you found in an oxbow lake, four hundred kilometers upriver, not on an open coast. Either the sea had deposited it here against every principle of coastal dynamics, or—

Or the coast hadn't always been the coast. Or the river had once come down here differently. Or somebody had moved earth that wasn't theirs to move, a very long time ago.

Quentin crouched and pressed two fingers into the mud, then thought better of it and used a trowel instead. Old habit—the one habit from the old life that had survived the amputation. Clean cut. Bands, clearly defined: shell, charcoal, shell again, then a seam of something darker and finer that glittered faintly when the light caught it. Not mica. Quentin scraped a sample into a zip bag, labeled it in block capitals—*KC-1027-A, intertidal, anomalous exposure*—and told themselves the labeling was procedure, not excitement.

Then they walked out toward the black rock.

The sandbar was firm going, packed wet sand ribbed with the retreating water, and it made a long shallow arc that Quentin gradually understood was not natural. Natural bars meandered. This one curved with intent, concentric, like the outer ring of something. Like the rim of a bowl. By the time the thought finished forming, Quentin was close enough to see that the black rock wasn't rock at all, not exactly. It was stone that had been worked.

Steps. Wide, shallow steps, cut into dark basalt or something like it, descending in a spiral away from the exposed rim and down into the wet dark where the sea still pooled. Each step bore a groove worn down its centerline, the kind of wear you got from feet, from centuries of feet, from a traffic of feet that had stopped a long time ago and left their ghost in the stone.

Quentin stood at the top step for a long moment, coffee forgotten back on the platform, hands in pockets, doing the arithmetic they'd sworn off a decade ago. Spiral descent. Basalt on a limestone coast—that alone was wrong, geologically wrong, imported wrong. Wear patterns suggesting sustained use. Flood-deposited riverine sediment overlying a marine structure, which meant the structure predated whatever event had rearranged the river's mouth, which meant it was old, which meant—

Which meant it was exactly the kind of thing that had a name attached to it. A famous name. A name on book spines and conference programs and the dedication page of a monograph Quentin had never read but had seen in enough airport bookshops to recognize by color alone.

*Not my problem,* Quentin told the steps. The steps did not answer. Steps never did. That was the trouble with stones: they just sat there being evidence, patiently, until somebody picked them up.

Quentin took photographs. Not many—eleven frames, wide shots mostly, the kind of documentation any competent surveyor would file as an anomaly report and forget. No close-ups of the grooves. No detail of the carving that became visible at the third step down, when the light angle shifted: a line of small, regular marks, chiseled, deliberate, running along the riser like a sentence along the edge of a page. Quentin photographed those too, then immediately wished they hadn't, then did not delete them.

The tide had stopped going out. Far out past the bar, the water had gone glassy and still, holding its breath. Quentin knew enough coastal lore to know what stillness meant at that scale. Somewhere out past the shelf, the sea was gathering itself to come home.

They climbed back up the steps and did not look at the sentence in the riser again, which took a specific and considerable effort.

* * *

The fisherman was waiting on the mudflat when Quentin came back across the bar—an old man mending a net that did not need mending, seated on an overturned dinghy with the unhurried air of someone who had been sitting there long enough to watch everything Quentin had done.

"Morning," Quentin said, in the flat local register they'd practiced until it stopped sounding performed.

"Morning." The man didn't look up from his net. "You walked out to the steps."

It wasn't a question. Quentin considered lying and discarded it; the mud held footprints in both directions, plain as print. "Routine survey," they said. "Tide's reading strange. I went to look."

"Mm." The man drew a knot tight with his teeth, spat, and finally looked up. He had a face like driftwood, grey and grain-split, and eyes that had done a lot of looking at water. "Name's Ede. I fish this water. My father fished it, and his mother before him, and she used to say the sea keeps some rooms locked for a reason."

"The tide's the lowest anyone can remember," Quentin said carefully. "I'd call that a natural event."

"I'd call it a door opening." Ede nodded back toward the black rock, now already ankle-deep in returning water. "My grandmother saw it once, when she was a girl. Lowest tide in living memory, they called it then too. She said the steps came up out of the water and the old people wouldn't go near them. Said the Riverfolk built doors at both ends—one for the sea to use, one for the river—and when both open at once, you don't stand in the doorway."

Quentin kept their face professional. Inside, something turned over slowly, like a stone in a current. *Riverfolk.* A word from the old life. A word from dust jackets and footnote wars and a certain person's lifelong, ruinous, magnificent obsession. A word that most serious scholars had retired to the status of legend, the way you retire a jersey number.

"Folk stories," Quentin said.

"Sure." Ede began coiling his net, unbothered. "And folk stories are just history that learned to survive floods. You're the surveyor, yes? The one from the regional office."

"That's me."

"Eh." He slung the coil over his shoulder and stood, joints cracking like green firewood. He looked at Quentin for a moment longer than courtesy required, with an expression that was hard to parse—not hostility, not warmth. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man identifying a type. "You measure things," he said. "Good. Measure the tide. Measure the mud. Write your numbers." He started up the shingle toward his truck, then paused. "But leave the doorway alone. Some holes are meant to stay shut, and the ones that aren't will still be there after you've gone. That's the difference between you and them."

He drove off in a cloud of salt spray and diesel. Quentin stood on the mudflat with the sample bag in their pocket and the returning tide lapping at their boots, and told themselves, with the full force of ten years' practice, that none of it was their problem.

* * *

Back at the station, Quentin filed the anomaly report. It took twenty minutes. *Unusual tidal excursion, probable seiche or distant storm surge resonance, exposed sublittoral feature of possible anthropogenic origin, recommend follow-up by qualified marine archaeologist.* The last phrase was the important one—the professional hand-off, the polite door closing, the words that transferred responsibility upward and outward to someone whose name was not Kavanagh. Qualified marine archaeologist. Let the regional office chase that. Let it land on some university desk, some committee, some queue. By the time anyone qualified arrived, the tide would have taken the steps back, and the report would sit in a drawer, and Quentin would be invisible again.

They sent it. Then they made lunch, ate it standing up, and did not think about the sentence carved in the riser.

At 14:00 they checked the tide gauge out of pure habit. The water was back within half a meter of predicted levels, behaving itself, apologizing almost. The bar was submerged. The steps were gone. The whole episode was already arranging itself in Quentin's memory into something explainable—a meteorological curiosity, a story for the regional office Christmas email, *you'll never guess what the tide did*.

At 15:30, the regional office called. Quentin let it ring twice, composing the bored voice, and answered.

"Kavanagh."

"Q. Kavanagh?" A woman's voice, brisk, unfamiliar, with the clipped vowels of someone calling from a long way inland. "Hi, this is Priya Nair, regional hydrographic coordination. Did you file an anomaly report this morning? KC-1027?"

"I did. Seiche event, most likely. I've recommended—"

"Yeah, we got it. Listen." Papers rustled. "Two things. One, we're not the only ones who got a reading. Three other stations on the coast logged the same excursion, same window, plus a research buoy off the shelf recorded a drawdown of nearly three meters. That's not a seiche. That's not anything in the literature. Oceanographic are calling it unprecedented for this basin. Possibly ever."

Quentin leaned against the console. Outside the station window, the sea lay flat and innocent, hiding its steps.

"Two," Nair continued, "we've had inquiries. Already. Within six hours of your report hitting the system. Which is fast. Unusually fast." Another rustle. "One is academic—some historian, wants to know if the exposure matches a site description in an unpublished manuscript. The other—" a pause, the sound of someone deciding how much to say, "—the other is a company. Meridian Salvage Group. They're asking specifically about your report. Specifically about you. They want contact details for the surveyor on site."

The room got very quiet. The refrigerator hummed. Out on the water, a gull screamed once and gave up.

"What did you tell them?" Quentin asked. Their voice came out level. Ten years of practice went into keeping it level.

"Told them personnel details are internal. But Kavanagh—" Nair lowered her voice, conspiratorial, the tone of someone passing along office gossip rather than a warning, "—Meridian doesn't ask for contact details. Companies like that buy what they want. If I were you, I'd expect visitors."

She rang off with instructions to keep logging the gauge hourly, and Quentin stood in the little station with its humming fridge and its single cot and its eleven months of careful, cultivated nothing, and looked at the phone.

*Some holes are meant to stay shut.* Ede's voice, gravel and certainty. And the corollary, arriving now, unwelcome: some holes, once opened, pull things in. Tides. Historians. Salvage syndicates. Everything the water had been holding at bay for a century, all of it now flowing toward a gap in the coast the size of one anomalous report.

Quentin thought about deleting the eleven photographs. Thought about it seriously, thumb hovering over the screen, the way you hover over a letter you know you shouldn't send. The images sat there in the camera roll: the bar curving with intent, the steps descending into the dark, and at the third riser, small and sharp and patient, the line of carved marks that were either decoration or language.

Language. Because Quentin had zoomed in once, against all judgment, and the marks weren't random. They repeated. Three symbols recurring in sequence, over and over, like a refrain. And the middle symbol—the one that appeared at every repetition, the hinge of the pattern—was a simple, confident spiral.

Quentin had seen that spiral before. Once, as a child, on a thin gold chain around a neck bent over excavation notes at a kitchen table. Seen it and asked about it and been told, in a voice that was trying to be casual and failing, *it's just a pretty shape, sweetheart.*

They had believed her. For a while.

Outside, the sun was dropping toward the headland, and the tide was coming in over the steps at the rate of centimeters per minute, erasing the afternoon's evidence with the patience of something that had erased it before and expected to erase it again. Quentin put the camera in the waterproof case, put the case in the lockbox, put the key on the ring with the boat keys and the station keys, and stood for a moment in the middle of the room, listening to the sea breathe against the seawall.

Eleven months of invisibility. One morning of black rock.

By nightfall, Quentin had decided—firmly, deliberately, with the whole weight of a decade's discipline behind the decision—to do absolutely nothing further. To log the gauge, file the data, and let the qualified marine archaeologists inherit their problem.

It was a good decision. It was, in fact, the correct decision, and Quentin held onto it for the next thirty-six hours, right up until the morning a boat Quentin didn't recognize anchored offshore the station, close enough to see, far enough to deny, and stayed there. Watching.
