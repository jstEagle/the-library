## 3. People Who Ask Questions

The ferry into Sallow's Reach ran late, and Quentin spent the crossing doing what they did best: being no one. Hood up, clipboard out, the posture of a person counting something boring. A decade of practice. You didn't hide by disappearing; you hid by being so unremarkable that eyes slid off you like rain off oilskin.

The tide had come back in, of course. Tides always did. But the black rock stayed in Quentin's head, and the fisherman's voice with it—*some holes are meant to stay shut*—and the boat anchored offshore that hadn't been there when they'd entered the cave and was gone when they'd left. Quentin had spent two sleepless nights deciding that meant nothing. Boats anchor. Fishermen lie. The world is full of coincidences that mean nothing.

They were lying to themselves and knew it, which was why they'd come to Sallow's Reach at all instead of driving straight inland to the next contract. The port town had a records office, a chandler, a bar called the Drowned Rat where survey crews drank and nobody remembered faces. If someone had seen them at the cave, Quentin wanted to know who before it became a problem.

The town smelled of diesel and brine and, under it, the sweet rot of kelp drying on the breakwater. Monsoon season was six weeks out and the harbor already wore its pre-storm hush, boats double-moored, cranes idle. Quentin bought coffee from a cart on the quay and stood pretending to study a chart of the inner channel while actually watching the marina office.

That was where they saw the first one: a man in a clean gray jacket, too clean for the Reach, standing at the fuel dock with his hands clasped behind him like a man admiring scenery. He wasn't admiring anything. He was looking at the water in the direction of the Karst Coast, and he had the stillness of someone being paid to be patient.

Quentin turned away, walked up the hill toward the records office, and told themselves the man was waiting on a delivery.

* * *

The records office occupied the ground floor of the old customs house, all green filing cabinets and a ceiling fan turning with the conviction of something that had given up. The clerk, a woman named Bree with reading glasses on a chain, knew Quentin by their contract number only, which was exactly how Quentin liked to be known.

"Coastal survey, Karst sector," Quentin said. "I need historical tide tables. Lowest recorded levels, last hundred years."

Bree pulled the ledgers without comment. That was the beauty of numbers; nobody asked why you wanted them.

Quentin was halfway through 1974 when the bell over the door rang, and a woman came in like weather. Tall, mid-forties maybe, close-cropped gray hair, a canvas satchel worn crosswise over a linen shirt that had been ironed once, long ago, in another country. She went straight to the desk, set down a leather journal wrapped in a cloth as if it were an infant, and said, "Shipping manifests. 1961. The river trade, outbound cargo, anything with the Kesh-Taru stamp."

"We don't do research by shouting," Bree said mildly.

"I'm not shouting. I'm being efficient." The woman's accent was somewhere coastal and far away, vowels rounded soft but clipped at the ends. "Vess Odumbe. I've been writing to this office for eleven months. I received three replies, all of which told me my request was 'under review.'"

"It was under review."

"It was under a cabinet." Vess Odumbe leaned on the counter, and her eyes—dark, quick, tired in a way that wasn't about sleep—swept the room and snagged, briefly, on Quentin. On the chart. On the tide ledgers open in front of them.

Quentin looked back down at 1974. Read the same line four times.

"You're doing tides," Odumbe said. Not a question. She'd crossed the room without seeming to move. "Low-water records. Which stretch?"

"The coast," Quentin said. "General."

"No one surveys the coast generally." She said it without hostility, almost gently, the way you'd point out to a child that their shoes were on the wrong feet. "You're doing the Karst sector. There was a low water event in the spring of '61 that my grandfather wrote about. He believed—" She stopped herself, exhaled. "May I sit?"

"No," Quentin said.

She sat anyway, on the stool across the table, and unwrapped the journal with hands that were careful and slightly trembling. "My grandfather was Tomas Odumbe. River surveyor, like you. In 1961 he took a team upstream past the gorges looking for the source of the river trade—the origin point of certain artifacts that keep surfacing on the black market with no provenance anyone can trace. Four men went up. One came back, half-dead, with no memory anyone trusted. My grandfather never returned." She opened the journal to a page dense with a small, precise hand. "He wrote that the river keeps its accounts at both ends. Outflow and source. He found the outflow."

The fan creaked overhead. Somewhere outside, a gull screamed its one note.

"Found the outflow," Quentin repeated, flat.

"A sea cave on the Karst Coast. Exposed only at extreme low water." Her eyes came up and held Quentin's, and there was no cruelty in them, only the terrible patience of a person who has spent years assembling a picture and finally has enough pieces to see the shape. "The lowest tide in living memory happened nine days ago. And you're sitting here with the tide tables open."

Quentin closed the ledger. "I survey channels. Erosion. Sediment loads. It's dull work and I'm dull about it."

"Tomas wrote the same thing in his letters home. 'It's dull work, don't worry about me.'" She smiled, and it hurt to look at. "Dull people don't wrap their charts like they're protecting them."

Quentin looked down and realized they had, without noticing, folded one arm over the open chart case. They made themselves lean back. Made their shoulders loose. "Look," they said, "whatever your grandfather was chasing—"

"He was chasing the archive." Her voice dropped, and the whole room seemed to lean in with it. Bree had gone still at her cabinet. "Every child on this coast grows up on the story. The trading people who vanished, who kept their records carved in stone because paper rots and empires burn. The drowned library. My grandfather thought it wasn't drowned at all—that it was *deposited*, deliberately, at the river's mouth and the river's source both, the way you'd file copies of a document in two places." She tapped the journal. "He found the mouth. He died looking for the source. And now a stranger with sediment on her boots is pulling low-water records for the exact stretch of coast where the story says the mouth is."

"I think you should let me work," Quentin said quietly.

"I think you're the first person in eleven months who might know something real," Vess Odumbe said, "and I will find out what it is, because finding things out is the only thing I have left." She began rewrapping the journal, unhurried. "When you're ready to not be alone with it, I drink at the Drowned Rat. Every evening. I'll be the one not pretending to read."

She left the bell ringing over the door behind her.

Bree, from her cabinets, without looking up: "She's been in every day this week. Asking after any survey crews working the Karst."

"Wonderful," Quentin said.

"She also asked if anyone had been asking about the cave." Bree adjusted her glasses. "Someone has. Two men. Yesterday. Clean jackets. They left a card."

* * *

The card was on Bree's counter, weighted under a stone paperweight shaped like a lighthouse. Heavy cream stock, embossed. **MERIDIAN SALVAGE GROUP — CULTURAL RECOVERY DIVISION**, and beneath that a name: *R. Ansel, Liaison*, and a phone number and nothing else. No address. Companies with addresses wanted to be found. Companies without them wanted you to call.

Quentin held it between two fingers like something dead.

"They asked which vessels had run the Karst sector in the past fortnight," Bree said. "I told them vessel registrations are confidential."

"Did they believe you?"

"They tipped me twenty dollars, so either they believed me or they didn't care." Bree shrugged. "They were polite. Politer than the tax people."

Quentin walked out of the customs house with the card in their pocket and the afternoon light going copper on the harbor, and the gray-jacketed man from the fuel dock was leaning against the rail at the bottom of the steps, and he smiled like they were old friends.

"Quentin Kavanagh," he said.

Everything in Quentin went very quiet and very cold, the way it does in the half second before a ladder rung gives way. Because that was the name. Not the contract name, not the initials on the invoices—Q. Marsh, Q. Merrow, whatever the year required—but the name, the one they'd spent ten years burying under paperwork and distance, the one that belonged to a famous archaeologist's child and nowhere else.

"You have the wrong person," Quentin said.

"I really don't." The man pushed off the rail. Up close he was fortyish, pleasant, forgettable in the deliberate way of people whose job is to be forgotten—brown hair, brown eyes, a face like a form filled out correctly. R. Ansel, presumably. "We've been trying to locate you for some time. Your mother speaks of you."

"My mother," Quentin said, "is not a topic."

"No. Of course. Professional boundaries—I respect that." He produced a folder from inside his jacket, thin, squared corners. "Here's the situation, stated plainly, because I find plainness saves everyone time. Meridian holds salvage rights pending on a significant portion of this coastline. We understand an undocumented site may have been exposed during the recent tidal anomaly. We understand you were present at or near that exposure. We are prepared to retain you as a consultant. Site assessment, access logistics, preservation protocol. The fee would be"—he named a number, and it was obscene, and he watched Quentin's face while he said it—"per month, plus expenses, duration open-ended."

"I don't know what you're talking about," Quentin said.

"Almost certainly true," Ansel agreed pleasantly. "You don't know what we're talking about. But we know what you talked about." He tilted his head toward the harbor, toward the coast beyond it, invisible past the breakwater. "A fisherman saw a light in the cave mouth nine days ago. A small boat, rented from a marina that keeps poor records. And a surveyor's transit tripod photographed on the ridge above the beach by a drone we weren't flying." He smiled again, apologetic. "You've been careful, Mr.—Ms.—Kavanagh. Sorry, we weren't sure. You've been careful for a decade, and it's genuinely impressive. But careful isn't the same as invisible. Not anymore. Not since the tide went out."

A fishing boat chugged past the breakwater. Someone laughed somewhere, ordinary and far away. Quentin stood on the customs house steps with their heart going slow and enormous, like footsteps in an empty building.

"What happens if I say no?" they asked.

"Nothing dramatic." Ansel spread his hands. "We simply continue our own assessment. We have equipment you wouldn't believe and lawyers you would. The offer stands because you're good—you're apparently very good—and because local knowledge saves months. But we will proceed regardless. That's not a threat. It's a schedule." He tucked the folder under his arm rather than offering it, which was its own kind of message: *we're not done.* "Think it over. Ms. Odumbe drinks at the Drowned Rat, by the way. So do our people. This town is smaller than it looks." He descended two steps, then paused. "One more thing. Dr. Marion Kavanagh filed a research permit application with the regional authority last week. Coastal sector, Karst. Citing, and I quote the abstract, 'a thirty-year hypothesis regarding the deposition of the Riverfolk archive.' She's coming, Quentin. I assumed you'd want to know. Family should keep each other informed."

He walked off down the quay, hands clasped behind him, admiring the scenery.

* * *

Quentin didn't go to the Drowned Rat. They went to the room above the chandler's they'd been renting by the week, locked the door, sat on the bed with the mattress springs complaining, and took inventory.

The fisherman's warning. The boat offshore. Vess Odumbe and her grandfather's ghost. Meridian, who knew the name, the boat, the light in the cave. And now Marion—coming, permit filed, thirty-year hypothesis, *she's coming*—as if the universe had decided that the decade of silence had been quite long enough.

Ten years. Ten years of contract names and initialled invoices, of rivers nobody mapped and towns nobody visited, of becoming so thoroughly no one that even Quentin sometimes lost the thread of who was underneath. All of it built on one load-bearing wall: *nobody knows who I am.*

And it had failed in a single week, in a single town, on every front at once.

They got up, found the bottle of rum they'd been nursing since the last contract, poured two fingers, and didn't drink it. Outside, the harbor lights came on one by one, and somewhere down the hill the Drowned Rat was filling up with evening, and Vess Odumbe was in it not pretending to read, and one of Ansel's clean jackets was probably in it too, and none of these people were going away.

The rum sat untouched. Quentin looked at their own reflection in the dark window—a stranger, hooded, forgettable—and understood, with the clarity that arrives only after everything else has failed, that hiding was no longer on the menu. The question had changed. It wasn't *how do I stay invisible?*

It was *who gets to me first—and what do they get when they arrive?*

Down the hill, faintly, through the glass, they could hear the Drowned Rat's door bang open on a gust of laughter and noise, and hold, and bang shut.

Quentin picked up their jacket.
