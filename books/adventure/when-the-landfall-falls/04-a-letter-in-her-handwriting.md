## 4. A Letter in Her Handwriting

The letter came on the morning boat, wedged between a crate of engine oil and a bag of mail that smelled of diesel and someone else's life.

Quentin saw the handwriting before they saw the name. That was the cruelty of it. Thirty years, and the hand hadn't changed—the tall ascenders like masts, the *K* that always looked like it was bracing against wind. Quentin stood at the survey station's dock with the envelope in both hands and did not open it for eleven minutes. They timed it without meaning to. Old habit: when you can't decide, measure something.

The tide was coming back in below the dock, climbing the black rock it had abandoned three days ago, erasing the cave's threshold inch by inch as if the sea were trying to take back its mistake. Quentin wished it would hurry.

They carried the letter up to the station instead of opening it there. The station was a converted pilothouse on stilts above the mudline, one room, one cot, one table scarred by thirty years of other people's surveys. Quentin had chosen it precisely because nothing about it invited company. No photographs on the walls. No books with their name inside the cover. The only personal object in the room was a tide almanac with annotations in two hands—Quentin's neat columns, and beneath them, faintly, in pencil, a child's copying of the same numbers, done so long ago the graphite had gone silver.

They should have burned that too, years ago.

Quentin sat down at the table and opened the envelope with a survey knife, cleanly, the way you'd core a sediment sample.

*Dr. M. Kavanagh,* the return address said. Not *Professor.* Not the honorifics the journals used. Just the name, plain, which meant she had written it herself, which meant this was not a departmental courtesy copy. This was aimed.

*Dear Q.*

Not Quentin. Never Quentin. She had started calling them Q when they were six because, she'd said once, laughing over a site map, *you're the unknown variable, sweetheart.* Quentin had spent a decade making sure she never got to solve for it.

*I'll be brief, because I know brevity is the only courtesy you still accept from me.*

Quentin's jaw tightened. Good, they thought. You've learned one thing.

*The Karst Coast anomaly is all over the hydrographic bulletins—a tidal drawdown of nearly four meters, unexplained, localized. I don't believe in coincidence. I never have. You know that about me; it's the one thing you can't accuse me of forgetting.*

*Thirty years ago I argued that the Riverfolk didn't vanish. They consolidated. Everything we've found scattered along the trade route—potsherds, ledger fragments, the resin-seal technology—points upstream to a single administrative source, and everything points downstream to an outflow. A mouth. The ledgers had to be read somewhere the water could carry their words out to the world.*

*A sea cave on the Karst Coast, exposed by a tide no model predicted, in the exact littoral band my source theory requires?*

*Q, I know you're working that coast. Don't ask me how I know. You inherited my talent for being where the data is, even if you've spent ten years pretending you didn't inherit anything from me.*

*If you've seen it—if you've stood inside it—then you already know I'm right. And if you haven't, then you will, because I'm coming. I've chartered through Sallow's Reach. I'll arrive within the week.*

*You don't have to see me. I understand that you may not. But I'm asking—once, plainly, the way I should have asked for thirty years—to be told what you find. Not for the paper. For me.*

*Your mother,*
*M.*

There was no P.S. Marion Kavanagh didn't believe in postscripts. Anything worth saying, she'd say to your face or not at all, and she'd say it whether you wanted it or not.

Quentin read it twice. Then a third time, hunting for the trap in it, because there was always a trap, always a hook under the bait of her plainness. *For me.* As if thirty years of absence could be balanced by one sentence of asking. As if the ledger of their childhood could be settled with a single entry, entered late, in her own hand.

The anger came up slow, the way deep water comes up a core barrel—cold, pressurized, older than the sample.

She knew they were on the coast. Of course she knew. Quentin had built an entire invisible life out of remote postings and unlisted contracts, and she had simply read the hydrographic bulletins, because of course she had, because Marion Kavanagh read everything, tracked everything, kept her daughter filed somewhere in that vast archive of a mind between the resin-seal technology and the monsoon cycles. Not a person. An entry. A variable she hadn't solved yet.

And now the cave had handed her the equation.

Quentin stood up so fast the chair went over backward. They crossed the room in three strides, took the camp stove from its shelf, and set it on the table with more force than the action required.

Burning mail was childish. They knew it was childish. They did it anyway, because some gestures aren't decisions—they're pressure valves, and the pressure had been building since the fisherman on the dock said *some holes are meant to stay shut*, since the anchored boat offshore, since Vess Odumbe's careful questions in the port town, since Corliss Hale's smile that knew Quentin's name. The whole world was closing in like a tide coming back over exposed rock, and this letter—this one piece of paper—was the only thing left that Quentin could actually destroy.

So they destroyed it.

The envelope first. Then the pages, one at a time, corner to the flame, watching Marion's masted letters curl and blacken and lift off the table in flakes of ash that drifted up toward the ceiling beams and hung there, refusing to fall, refusing to leave.

*Within the week.*

Quentin watched the last word go and felt—not relief. Nothing like relief. Relief has a shape, a loosening. What they felt was the specific hollowness of having slammed a door and heard, through the wood, the sound of someone still talking.

They tried to work.

That was the discipline, the whole architecture of the decade: when the past rises, log data. Quentin spread the morning's tide readings across the table beside the stove's cooling ash and forced themselves into the numbers, and the numbers cooperated for almost forty minutes, and then Quentin's eye snagged on the ash.

It had fallen while they worked. It lay on the table in a fine grey drift, and the draft from the door had arranged it—or Quentin's mind had arranged it, which amounted to the same thing—into legibility.

*Q.*

Just that. The single letter, unmistakable, drawn in Marion's own burned hand, sitting in the wreckage of itself.

Quentin stared at it for a long time.

This was the thing nobody warned you about burning letters: fire destroys the paper, not the text. Quentin could still see every line. Could still hear the sentences in her voice—that dry, certain, lecture-hall voice that had narrated Quentin's entire childhood, that had explained river systems at dinner tables and burial customs on car rides and had never once, not once, asked a question about Quentin's own interior that it didn't immediately answer itself.

*Don't ask me how I know.*

Because she'd been reading the bulletins. Because she'd been reading Quentin, all these years, from a distance, the way you read a distant site from satellite imagery—patiently, annually, noting the changes, waiting for a feature to emerge that justified excavation.

That was the thought that finally made Quentin's chest hurt. Not the intrusion of it. The patience. Somewhere in a university office, Marion Kavanagh had been watching her estranged child's professional footprint the way she watched eroding coastlines—with scholarly concern and no intervention—and Quentin had mistaken that silence for freedom. Had built a whole self on top of it. *She doesn't follow my work. She doesn't think about me. I am successfully nobody.*

Nobody. Except to the one reader who'd never stopped reading.

Quentin swept the ash into their palm. It was still faintly warm. They carried it to the window and hesitated there, meaning to scatter it out over the mud, and then didn't—couldn't—because scattering it felt like another performance, another gesture for an audience of one, and Quentin was so tired of performing for her, even alone, even in ashes.

Instead they tipped it into the tide almanac, into the gutter of the spine, and closed the covers on it. Burial. The oldest archival technology there was. Let her handwriting sit in the record, sealed, undisturbed, like everything else the Riverfolk had ever trusted to stone and resin and deep water.

Let it wait thirty years. See how she liked it.

The afternoon went wrong slowly.

First the radio: the port authority at Sallow's Reach broadcasting a notice to mariners about the tidal anomaly, language stiff with official bafflement—*unexplained harmonic disturbance, vessels advised caution in the Karst littoral*—and beneath the officialese, unmistakably, the sound of a story getting bigger. Word travels upriver faster than boats do. By now half the coast knew about the cave, and everyone who knew had a different reason to want it.

Then the survey skiff's fuel gauge, which had been lying politely for a week, stopped lying altogether.

Then, at dusk, the boat.

It came around the headland running dark, no lights, and Quentin watched it from the window with the field glasses and felt the day's cold settle fully into their bones. It wasn't the Meridian vessel—he knew that hull's silhouette now, had logged it twice from the cliffs. This was smaller, leaner, a charter boat riding high and empty the way boats ride when they've come to collect a passenger rather than cargo.

It anchored offshore, exactly where the other boat had anchored three days ago, in the exact patch of deep water that sat opposite the submerged cave mouth like a held breath.

Waiting for the tide, Quentin thought. Everyone was waiting for the tide. The sea had shown its hand for three days and was folding again, and every player at the table—the syndicate, the historian, the woman who wrote letters in masted capitals—was watching the water to see when it would show it once more.

Quentin lowered the glasses. In the almanac on the table, under the closed covers, Marion's ashes sat in the gutter of the spine, and Quentin found they could feel them there, the way you can feel a splinter, a small buried wrongness that the body keeps reporting.

*Within the week.*

The charter boat rode at anchor, patient as arithmetic.

Quentin got out a fresh sheet of survey paper and began, methodically, to plan the next low tide: instruments, timing, the descent to the black rock, the cave. If Marion was coming—and she was coming, that much was certain, that much had been certain since the first word of her handwriting—then Quentin had a choice. Hide, and let her arrive to an empty coast and a locked station and a daughter-shaped absence she'd interpret, correctly, as an answer. Or stay, and stand on the black rock when her ferry came in, and let thirty years break over both of them like the tide returning.

Neither option was survivable. That was the thing about family. Every path through it cost something, and the only real decision was which loss you could carry.

Quentin looked at the almanac. Thought about the ash sleeping in its spine.

*For me,* she'd written. *Not for the paper.*

And underneath the anger, down where the cold water moved, Quentin felt the other thing stir—the thing they'd been drowning for a decade, which was not anger at all but its exhausted twin: grief. Grief for the mother who had existed briefly, really, in the margins of field notebooks, drawing little spirals in the corners of site maps while five-year-old Quentin copied them, before the career ate her alive and the hospital room and the dig won their terrible argument. Grief for a woman who had just written, in her own hand, the most honest sentence of Quentin's lifetime—*I'm asking, once, plainly*—and who would arrive within the week to find out whether honesty, offered thirty years late, counted for anything at all.

The tide turned outside. Quentin could hear it, a soft vast shifting in the dark, the sea beginning its long pull away from the coast again, uncovering, exposing, refusing to keep its secrets shut.

Three days to the next drawdown. Less than a week to the ferry.

Quentin picked up the pencil and wrote at the top of the fresh page, in their own hand—in the hand Marion had taught, the masts and the braced K, the inheritance that lived in the muscles whether Quentin willed it or not:

*Day 1. She's coming.*

Then, after a moment, smaller, underneath:

*God help me—I want to show her the cave.*

They looked at that sentence for a long time. Then they left it there. Some entries you don't erase. Some entries are the whole record.

Out past the headland, the charter boat lit a single lamp, and Quentin understood it the way you understand a knock at a door: not as a threat, not yet.

As a question.
