## 6. Mother, Arriving

The ferry from Corvane came in at seven-forty on a Tuesday morning, and Quentin knew before the gangway dropped who would be on it.

They'd felt it for two days the way you feel weather in a bad knee—a pressure behind the eyes, an itch of inevitability. First the letter, burned and unreadable except in memory. Then Vess Odumbe appearing at their table with her grandfather's journal like a woman laying down cards she'd been holding for sixty years. Then the Meridian men, polite as customs officers, already knowing Quentin's name, their survey history, their mother's surname. The whole coast was tightening around them like a net drawn by patient hands, and nets, Quentin had learned, always had one knot left to tie.

Marion Kavanagh stepped off the gangway in field boots and a coat too good for Sallow's Reach, carrying one canvas duffel and nothing else, because Marion Kavanagh had never once in her life checked luggage or waited for anyone to carry anything for her. She was seventy-one now. Her hair had gone from iron to white at the temples and stayed iron everywhere else, cut short in the same practical line she'd worn since Quentin was nine. She stood at the top of the ramp and scanned the dock crowd the way she scanned a trench wall—methodically, left to right, reading strata—and when her gaze reached Quentin it stopped without surprise.

Of course she'd known where to look. Quentin hadn't exactly hidden; they'd just stopped being findable, which was different, and apparently not different enough.

"Quentin." No hug. Marion didn't do hugs in public, or in private either if it could be avoided. "You've lost weight."

"You've gotten old."

"I've gotten *old*," Marion agreed, as if conceding a point in a seminar. "It happens to everyone who doesn't die young in a trench collapse, which is the only alternative I ever respected." She looked past Quentin at the harbor—the fishing boats, the rust-streaked breakwater, the black headland where the tide had fallen away from the world two weeks ago and shown everyone a door that should have stayed shut. "The Karst Coast. You picked somewhere with real geology. I'll give you that."

"I didn't pick it for you."

"No," Marion said. "You never did anything for me again after 2014. I'm aware of the record." She hoisted the duffel onto her shoulder—seventy-one years old, one clean motion, no grunt—and started walking toward the town without checking whether Quentin followed.

Quentin followed. That was the humiliating part. Ten years of invisibility, of river surveys in places so remote the supply planes forgot them, of answering to no name but the one on the contract, and all of it dissolved in eleven seconds because their mother walked off a boat and started moving and their feet simply remembered whose they were.

* * *

Sallow's Reach had exactly one establishment that could be called a hotel, and Marion took the room at its end without negotiating, then installed herself at the corner table of the attached café with a pot of tea and her reading glasses and a printout she'd clearly made before boarding. Quentin sat across from her because there was nowhere else to sit that wasn't running away, and running away in front of Vess Odumbe—who was watching from the bar with the frank interest of a woman who had waited three generations for this exact scene—was not an option.

"So," Marion said. "Tell me about the cave."

"There is no cave."

"Quentin."

"There's a rock overhang exposed by an anomalous tide. I filed a routine survey note. That's the entire official record."

Marion set down her tea with the deliberate care of someone who has broken cups before while angry. "I taught you stratigraphy when you were six. I taught you to read a section drawing before you could read chapter books. You think I can't hear a lie with a survey number on it?"

"It's not a lie. It's a classification."

"A classification." Marion smiled, and it was the worst smile in the world, the one she used on peer reviewers, the one that meant *you are about to be excavated*. "Fine. Let's classify. The tide on the fourteenth of last month reached minus two-point-three meters against a predicted low of zero-point-four. That's not anomalous, that's unprecedented—there's no meteorological driver on record for it, no seiche, no offshore event logged by any station within four hundred kilometers. Which means either your instruments were wrong—" she looked at Quentin over the glasses, "—and you have never once in your life filed a report based on instruments you hadn't personally calibrated twice, or something happened out there that nobody can explain yet. And my daughter, who has spent ten years making herself boring enough to disappear, filed a note about it anyway. Why?"

Because the ledgers had been carved in a hand that changed across three centuries and Quentin had stood in front of them with their heart going like a drill motor. Because the sealed jars had been stacked in rows with gaps where jars had been removed, deliberately, carefully, by people who expected to come back and never did. Because the stone door at the chamber's end bore a spiral sigil worn shallow by touch, thousands upon thousands of touches, and Quentin had known it the way you know a face in a crowd—

"Because it's a significant site," Quentin said, "and I'm a professional."

"You're a professional," Marion repeated softly. She unfolded the printout. It was a map—hand-drawn, old, the paper foxed brown along the folds—and Quentin recognized the coastline before recognizing anything else, because they'd grown up above a desk where this same coastline was pinned under glass. "Then let's be professional together. Thirty-one years ago I published a monograph arguing that the trading culture we call the Riverfolk—your vanished people, the ones every textbook calls 'enigmatic' because nobody bothered to learn their word for themselves—maintained a distributed archive. Not a temple library. Not a palace cache. A *distributed* archive, seeded at multiple points along the river system, duplicated so that no flood, no war, no dynasty could erase them. Every reviewer in the field called it elegant speculation unsupported by evidence. My funding dried up. My students drifted. My—" a pause, half a heartbeat, the kind of pause Marion allowed herself maybe once a decade, "—my family drifted too, though that was mostly my own fault and we both know it. And then a tide falls farther than any tide in living memory, on precisely the stretch of coast where my distribution model predicts an outflow node, and my child is standing on it." She turned the map around. A red circle sat on the Karst Coast, drawn decades ago, ink faded to rust. "So yes. Tell me about the cave."

* * *

What followed was two hours of the only kind of conversation they had ever been good at: archaeology as proxy warfare.

Marion laid out her model—outflow nodes at the delta, midriver, and source, redundancy as theology, the Riverfolk treating memory the way other cultures treated grain, stored against famine. Quentin countered with what they'd seen: the ledger walls weren't narrative, they were transactional, shipping manifests and water-rights adjudications, which meant the cave was administrative, which meant it was downstream of wherever the decisions got made. Marion's eyes went bright at *water rights*—bright the way they used to go at Christmas, at Quentin's first field school, at every discovery that mattered more than people—and she leaned forward and said, "Adjudications. Adjudicated *where*? There has to be a seat. Archives don't adjudicate, courts adjudicate, and courts sit at centers, and the center of that river system is—"

"The headwaters," Quentin said, and hated themselves for saying it, because Vess's grandfather's journal said the same thing and they hadn't meant to hand Marion that piece yet.

But Marion was already there, or nearly—her finger came down on the upper gorge country, the closed reserves, the land locked behind permits and legend both—and she said, quietly, with thirty years of certainty finally touching ground: "There."

And then, because she was Marion Kavanagh and mercy was not among her documented behaviors, she reached into her coat pocket and set something on the table between them.

A photograph. Old, creased white at the corners. Quentin in it was maybe seven, gap-toothed, sunburned, standing on a dig scaffold in a shirt two sizes too big. Around the child's neck, on a cord, hung a pendant: a flat disc of dark stone, incised with a spiral.

"I found this in my files last week," Marion said. "Looking for something else. Isn't that always how it goes." She tapped the photo. "You wore it for three years. Refused to take it off. Slept in it. Your father bought it from a market stall in Tessel Port the summer you turned five—the seller swore it was Riverfolk work, and he was probably lying, market sellers always lie, but the spiral is correct. The proportions are correct. I checked it against the sigil corpus forty times over the years and it never fit any typology I could construct, and I could never decide whether that meant it was fake or whether it meant everything I thought I knew was wrong." She looked up. "Where is it, Quentin?"

"I don't know."

"That's a lie."

"That's a *classification*."

Marion exhaled through her nose. Outside, a gull screamed at another gull about something neither would concede. In the bar, Vess Odumbe pretended very hard to read.

"When the door opened in my dream," Marion said—she still called it a dream, the recurring one she'd described in lectures, the chamber and the door and the spiral, the vision that half the field considered her great insight and the other half considered her great embarrassment—"the spiral was on the door. When I drew it from memory afterward, I drew it the way it appears in that pendant. Not the way it appears anywhere in the published corpus. Do you understand what I'm telling you? I couldn't have invented the correct form. Seven-year-old you wore the correct form around your neck for three years while I measured it and photographed it and argued with myself about it, and then it vanished from our lives the same year you did, more or less, and I have spent ten years telling myself that was coincidence." Her voice didn't shake. Marion's voice had never shaken in recorded history. But her thumb moved on the edge of the photograph, back and forth, a small erosion, a slow tide. "So I will ask you one time, plainly, mother to child, scholar to witness. Did you find the door? And does it match?"

The café noise receded. Quentin looked at the photograph—at the small sunburned child wearing a symbol they couldn't have understood, given by a father who was gone, kept by a mother who catalogued love the way she catalogued sherds, labeled and boxed and never once held—and felt the old anger rise, and beneath the anger, worse, the old grief, and beneath that, worst of all, the answer.

"Yes," Quentin said. "It matches."

Marion closed her eyes. For three full seconds she was absolutely still, and Quentin, who had spent a decade learning to read silence, saw in those three seconds the whole weight of thirty-one years—every rejected grant, every sneering review, every conference dinner where colleagues changed the subject when she approached—set down at last, like a load shifted from one shoulder to the ground.

"Thank you," Marion said. It came out rough. She cleared her throat and put the photograph away and became, instantly, again, Dr. Marion Kavanagh, and Quentin understood that the roughness was the most expensive thing she'd ever given them. "Then we have eleven days of dry season left, possibly fewer, and a site that cannot stand another spring tide, and a syndicate circling, and a historian in that bar who has been eavesdropping badly enough to be audible from here." She began packing her papers with brisk, precise motions. "We need Vess Odumbe's grandfather's journal. We need the ledgers transcribed. And we need to talk about the headwaters, because if the archive is distributed the way I believe it is, then that cave is not the treasure, Quentin. It's the receipt."

She stood, duffel on shoulder, and paused.

"For what it's worth," she said, not looking at Quentin, addressing the middle distance, the way she addressed everything that cost her, "you were right to file the note. Whatever else. You were right."

She walked out into the light, and Quentin sat alone with two cold teas and the shape of a spiral burning behind their eyes—the one on the door, the one in the photograph, the one on the pendant that was currently wrapped in a sock at the bottom of their survey kit, carried for ten years through every remote posting, never worn, never discarded, never once mentioned to a living soul.

From the bar, Vess Odumbe finally gave up pretending, crossed the room, and set her grandfather's journal on the table between them.

"I think," she said, "it's time you told us both what you're really carrying."
