## 7. Vess's Grandfather's Map

Vess Odumbe had learned to read her grandfather the way other people read weather.

The journal lived in a canvas satchel that had crossed three oceans and two marriages with her, and she did not open it in public anymore, because the last time she'd done that—in a ferry terminal in Sallow's Reach, of all places—a man had leaned over her shoulder and asked, too casually, whether the paper was for sale. She had closed it so fast she pinched her thumb. That was the year she learned that obsession was visible from a distance, the way heat shimmer is visible off a road, and that there were people who made their living following shimmer.

So now she read him at night, in her rented room above the chandlery, with the lamp turned low and the shutters cracked just enough to hear the harbor working. Sallow's Reach never slept entirely. There was always a winch somewhere, a hull knocking against a piling, the long sigh of water under the town. Her grandfather would have liked it here. He had liked any place where the river did the talking.

His name had been Tomas Odumbe, and he had been, by trade, a hydrographic surveyor—depths and channels, soundings and shoals—and by temperament something worse: a man who believed every river kept a diary and that it was rude not to read it. In 1961 he had come up the coast on a government contract to chart the Karst approaches, and somewhere between his last official sounding and his disappearance, the contract had stopped mattering. The journal told the story of that drift in his own cramped hand, forty-one entries, and then a forty-second entry that was only half a page and ended mid-word.

Vess had spent eleven years and most of an inheritance trying to finish that word.

* * *

The trouble with Sallow's Reach, she had decided within a week, was that everyone in it was pretending not to look at everyone else.

She noticed it first at the market—the way conversations dipped when strangers passed, the way the fishmonger marked certain boats with a nod. A town built on watching. And into this town, ten days ago, someone had walked out of the sea itself carrying a secret, and the whole place had tilted toward them like grass toward sun.

She had seen Quentin Kavanagh four times before she spoke to them. Once at the survey office, once on the seawall, twice at the chandlery counter below her room, buying rope both times, which struck her as either preparation or penance. They had the particular posture of a person who has trained their whole body to be unremarkable—shoulders drawn in, eyes doing their work peripherally—and Vess recognized it because she'd studied photographs of people who'd gone underground, literally and otherwise, and they all stood like that. You don't get to be invisible by accident. It's a discipline. It takes years.

She also recognized the name. Kavanagh. Everyone who had ever gone looking for the Riverfolk archive knew the name Marion Kavanagh; the woman had published the definitive refutation of the drowned-archive legend twenty years ago, a paper so rigorous it had killed funding for the search for a generation. Vess had read it until the spine broke. She had hated it until the hate wore through to something more useful: if Marion Kavanagh had bothered to refute the archive that thoroughly, then some part of her believed in it. Nobody builds a cathedral over nothing.

But the person buying rope was not Marion Kavanagh. The person buying rope flinched at the surname the way you flinch at a door slamming.

So Vess waited. She was good at waiting. She came down on the fourth evening, when the tide tables pinned outside the chandlery showed one of the strange extreme lows that had the whole coast muttering, and she found Quentin standing at the rail where the harbor road met the mudflats, looking out at rock no living person had seen before, black and glistening, going out and out toward a darkness at the cliff base that might have been shadow or might have been a mouth.

"You're the one who found it," Vess said.

Quentin didn't turn. "I'm the one who does surveys."

"Right." She came to stand beside them, leaving a careful arm's length of rail between them. "And the survey found a cave that appears in exactly zero charts, matches a structure described in a trading ledger fragment from 1840 that I have personally translated, and sits directly on the bearing my grandfather recorded the week before he rowed upriver and vanished." She watched the water instead of them, because watching people watch things gave away less. "I'm not asking what you saw. I'm telling you what I know, so you can decide how alone you want to be."

That got the flinch again. Smaller. But there.

"I don't talk about the cave," Quentin said.

"Good," said Vess. "Neither do I. Let's keep not talking about it together."

* * *

They walked, because it was easier to talk while walking and impossible to be followed discreetly on the mudflat paths, where every footprint printed itself in gray clay like a confession. Vess led them up the goat track behind the salt works to the ruined windmill on the bluff, a spot she'd chosen partly for the view and partly because there was only one way up, and she wanted to see anyone coming.

She set the satchel on the millstone and took out the journal, and her hands did the thing they always did, the small tremor, and she let Quentin see it because hiding it would have cost more than showing it.

"Tomas Odumbe," she said. "My grandfather. Hydrographic service, seconded to coastal charting, spring of 1961. Officially he surveyed the approaches for six weeks and filed a clean report. Unofficially—" she opened the cover, and the smell came up out of it, mildew and iron-gall ink and decades of her own handling, "—he started asking the river questions."

The early pages were beautiful in the way working documents are beautiful: soundings in neat columns, channel sketches, marginal notes in two languages. Then, entry nineteen, the columns began to break apart, crowded out by prose.

Quentin leaned in despite themselves. Vess watched them do it—the involuntary lean, the scholar's tell—and felt the first loosening in her chest she'd felt since arriving in this town.

"Here." She turned to entry twenty-three. "Read this one."

Quentin read aloud, quietly, the way people read handwriting they're learning. "*The fishermen at the third village will not go past the narrows after dark. Not superstition, I think—they are specific. They say the river has a memory and the gorge keeps it. One old man drew me a spiral in the sand and would not say what it meant, only that his grandmother's people were the ones who wrote things down, and that writing things down is how you survive drowning.*"

"Spiral," Quentin said. Flat. Carefully flat.

"The same one," Vess said, "that's carved over the stone door in your cave. Don't bother deciding whether to admit it—I've been inside the tideline approach twice at extreme low water, years ago, before the door was reachable. I saw the lintel. I couldn't get further, and I told myself that was prudence." She turned another page. "It wasn't. It was fear. My grandfather's fear, inherited. We'll come back to that."

Entry thirty-one was the map.

Not a proper map—a working traverse, drawn across two facing pages, the river rendered as a single bold line running up through hatch-marked walls that narrowed and narrowed until the line itself seemed squeezed. Elevations penciled along the margins. Distances in chains. And along the route, six symbols Vess had spent years decoding: a falls, a bend shaped like an elbow, a drowned town marked with a small square and the word *ferry*, a side canyon, a shelf of white rock, and at the top of the page, where the river line simply stopped, a circle with a spiral inside it, and beneath it, in her grandfather's hand:

*THE SOURCE IS NOT THE BEGINNING.*

"He wrote that phrase four times in the journal," Vess said. "Always the same. Never explained."

Quentin was silent a long moment. Out past the bluff, the light was going bronze on the water, and somewhere below a winch started up its evening complaint.

"What happened to him?" Quentin asked. It wasn't a polite question. It was the question of someone who needed to know what the river did to people who went up it.

"That's the part I can't finish reading." Vess touched the last page without turning fully to it; she knew it by heart anyway. "Entry forty-one, dated the ninth of October, 1961. He writes that he's reached the white rock shelf, that the gorge above it is 'a throat,' that the local guides have all turned back except one, a man named Dele, and that Dele says the source chamber floods from below—that the spring breathes, that it inhales and exhales on its own schedule, and that the old people timed their visits to the exhale." She looked up. "Your cave breathes with the tide. I've read your survey notes, or as much as the office would let me see. Tidal resonance in a karst system miles inland doesn't happen unless the whole formation is connected. One lung. His source and your cave are the same organ."

"And entry forty-two?"

Vess turned to it. Half a page. The handwriting deteriorating—not from haste, she'd decided long ago, but from cold, or altitude, or the particular fatigue of a man writing by bad light in a place with no good light. It read: *Dele says we are close enough to hear it. The sound is not water. Tomorrow we go in on the exhale. If the old stories are true the door opens from—*

Mid-word. Mid-sentence. *From.*

"Opens from where?" Quentin said.

"That's the word." Vess closed the journal gently, the way you close a coffin. "Eleven years, and that's the wall I keep hitting. Every theory I have ends at *from*. From the source side. From below. From within. From the beginning, maybe, whatever that means, because he also wrote that the source is not the beginning, which implies the beginning is somewhere else, which implies—" She stopped herself. She could hear herself accelerating; she'd been told before that when she talked about the journal she sounded like a woman falling downstairs.

But Quentin wasn't looking at her like that. Quentin was looking at the closed journal like a person looking at a locked door they'd suddenly realized they owned the key to.

"The ledgers," Quentin said slowly. "In the cave. The carved walls. I couldn't read them. Nobody can read them—the script's not in any corpus, it's been undeciphered for a hundred and fifty years." A pause, and then, with the reluctance of someone handing over a weapon: "Except the counting marks are consistent. Base-twelve, positional, with a repeating glyph at intervals I took for section breaks. But if the sections aren't sections—if they're *tides*—if the whole wall is a timetable—"

"Then it's not an archive," Vess breathed. "It's a schedule."

"It's a breathing schedule." Quentin's voice had changed, dropped its guard a full register. "The door doesn't open from the sea side because it isn't a door from the sea side. It's an *outflow*. Whatever's upstream breathes down through the karst, and once a cycle—once a great tide, maybe once a generation—the pressure drops far enough that the passage runs dry and you can walk in." They looked at her. "The lowest tide in living memory, Vess. It didn't expose the cave. It *exhaled*."

The windmill creaked above them. Down in the harbor, the water was pulling back off the flats again, exposing black rock, and Vess thought of her grandfather's last line—*tomorrow we go in on the exhale*—and felt the old grief and the new understanding collide somewhere behind her sternum.

He hadn't vanished because the river took him. Or not only. He'd gone in on the exhale, and either the inhale had come early, or—

No. She put it down. Some hypotheses were too heavy to carry uphill.

"There's a route," she said instead, and opened the journal to the traverse map again, flattening it on the millstone between them. "Falls, elbow bend, drowned ferry town, side canyon, white rock shelf, source. Six landmarks, five days' hard travel if the gorges are passable, longer if they're not. The government never mapped above the falls—it's closed country, treaty land, and the maps that exist are colonial fiction. But he was there. He measured it. This is the only accurate route in existence." She tapped the page. "And I am the only person alive who can read his shorthand."

Quentin studied the map, and Vess watched them weigh it—she could practically see the scales, the invisible life on one pan and this on the other—and she understood, with the particular clarity of a fellow obsessive, that she was not persuading them at all. She was just giving permission.

"Why show me?" Quentin asked finally. "You don't know me. For all you know I sell the location to whoever's been asking."

"Somebody already knows the location," Vess said. "There are men in this town with Meridian Salvage badges who ask questions like they're being polite and take notes like they're being paid. Your invisibility has an expiration date, Quentin. Mine too—I've been made since the ferry terminal." She shrugged. "I'm not showing you the map because I trust you. I'm showing you because two people who won't talk survive longer than one."

Something moved through Quentin's face then—recognition, or relief, or the exhaustion of a swimmer who'd been treading water alone and finally sees the second head above the surface.

"One condition," Quentin said.

"Name it."

"When we go—and I haven't said we're going—you don't read from the journal out loud at night. Sound carries in gorges. Everything about us stays boring. Boring is armor."

Vess almost laughed. "My grandfather would have hated you."

"My mother would have hated you," Quentin said, and then went very still, having said it, having let it out of the room where it lived.

Vess picked up the journal, weighed it in her hands, and made the decision she'd been circling for eleven years.

"Then we should probably talk about your mother," she said. "Because there's a letter addressed to you sitting at the chandlery counter, and the return address is the Institute, and I'd bet everything left of my inheritance that the handwriting belongs to Dr. Marion Kavanagh."

Below them, the tide kept going out, and out, and out, past anything the tables promised, and the black rock lay bare all the way to the dark mouth of the cave, breathing.
