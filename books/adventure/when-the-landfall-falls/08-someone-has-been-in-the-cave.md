## 8. Someone Has Been in the Cave

Quentin told themselves it was the tide that brought them back.

That was the official version, anyway—the one they'd write in the survey log if anyone ever asked: *routine monitoring of intertidal exposure following anomalous low-water event.* Bureaucratic language was good armor. It had kept them invisible for ten years, and it almost worked now, padding across the shingle at midnight with a headlamp dialed down to a red glow, telling themselves this was science.

But the truth was simpler and uglier. They hadn't slept. They'd lain in the rented room above the chandlery in Sallow's Reach listening to the harbor bells count hours, seeing the spiral sigil on the stone door every time they closed their eyes, seeing their mother's pendant—*her* pendant, the one she'd worn like a second wedding ring—and around three in the morning they'd given up on sleep, packed the dry bag, and gone out into the wet dark before the coward in them could wake up properly and argue.

The tide was coming back in. That was the thing about this coast: the sea gave you a window and then it took it back, and you never knew which window would be your last. The fisherman's words from that first morning still sat in Quentin's chest like a swallowed stone. *Some holes are meant to stay shut.*

The cave mouth showed as a black seam in the black rock, breathing out cold air that smelled of salt and old stone and something else—something mineral and sweet, like the inside of a geode. Quentin stood at the threshold for a long moment, letting their eyes adjust, listening.

The sea said nothing. The wind said nothing.

Inside, something had been saying plenty.

They smelled it before they saw it. Stone dust—that fine, chalky sweetness of freshly cut limestone, unmistakable, impossible to fake. Then the headlamp beam swept the first ledger wall and Quentin's stomach dropped through the floor of the world.

Chiseled. The carvings were *chiseled.*

Whoever had come had not been careful. They had not been scholars. The rows of incised characters—the tight, patient record-keeping of people who had counted cargo and tides and births and deaths on these walls for generations—had been attacked with something blunt and electric, a hammer chisel run off a battery pack by someone in a hurry. Whole panels were gouged to rubble. Others had been cut out entirely, neat rectangles sawn free of the living rock, the edges still sharp, the pale scars behind them glowing white as bone where the sea had never touched.

Quentin's hand rose without permission and hovered over one of the scars. The dust came away on their fingers like ash.

"Okay," they said aloud, because the silence needed breaking or it was going to crush them. "Okay."

The jars were next. They'd left them exactly as found—two dozen sealed vessels tucked into niches along the eastern passage, resin-black, intact, each one holding whatever the Riverfolk had trusted to the dark. Now four of the niches were empty. Two more jars lay smashed on the passage floor, stamped apart with a boot heel, their contents scattered: dried resin flakes, fragments of woven fiber gone brittle as cicada wings, and small clay tokens stamped with the spiral sigil, dozens of them, rolling under Quentin's light like startled beetles.

They hadn't even taken the tokens. Hadn't catalogued, hadn't photographed, hadn't done anything a human being with a conscience would do. This was a search. Someone had torn the place apart looking for something specific, and when they didn't find it here—

Quentin crouched among the broken jars and made themselves breathe. In through the nose. Out slow. The technique their mother had taught them at nine years old, kneeling beside a collapsed trench wall in some dig she'd dragged them to, her hands steady on their shoulders while everything around them fell down. *Panic is just data arriving too fast, Q. Slow the stream.*

They hated that the technique still worked. They hated that it was hers.

They worked the rest of the cave methodically, because methodical was all they had. The central chamber, with its great stone door, was where the violation got worse—or got stranger, depending on how you read it.

The door itself stood untouched. That massive slab of basalt, seamless except for the spiral sigil carved deep at its center, looked exactly as it had the day Quentin first stood before it. But the rock around it—no. Not quite around it. Below it, at the base, where the door met its sill, there were fresh drill marks. Three of them, spaced with professional precision, core-bit scars the diameter of a wrist, drilled deep and then—Quentin ran their light close—*plugged.* Filled back with crushed limestone and epoxy, tinted to match, a repair job any quarry foreman would have signed off on.

Someone had drilled into the door's base, sampled what was behind it or tested its thickness, and then erased the evidence.

Which meant two things, and both of them landed in Quentin's gut like ballast.

First: Meridian Salvage Group had been here. Corliss Hale's polite men with their polite questions in Sallow's Reach—they'd known Quentin's name before Quentin had ever spoken to them, and now they'd known the cave too, or found it on their own, and they'd come with tools and no reverence and they'd stripped what they could carry and cored the door to find out what it was hiding.

Second, and worse: the drilling had told them something, and then they'd plugged the holes and left the door alone. You don't walk away from a sealed archive unless one of two things is true. Either you can't open it—

Or you've realized you don't need to. Not here.

Quentin sat down on the cold basalt in front of the door, cross-legged, the way they used to sit in front of exam papers they couldn't crack, and let the red light play over the spiral sigil. The carving was old work, older than anything else in the cave—worn soft at the edges, re-cut once, long ago, by hands that wanted it legible forever. A spiral. Not a decoration. Quentin had stared at enough river charts in ten years of hydrographic surveys to know a symbol when one kept recurring, and this spiral wasn't a mark. It was a diagram.

It wound inward, yes. But it also wound *outward*, if you let your eye travel the other way—and at its outermost turn, where the line finally broke free of the coil, there was a tiny secondary glyph. Quentin had noticed it the first day and filed it as ornament. Sitting here now, with the smell of stone dust in their throat and the smashed jars at their back, they looked at it properly for the first time.

It was a water glyph. The same convention used on the old shipping ledgers upstairs in the port museum—a wavy line under a triangle, the sign for *upstream*, for *the source*. Every Riverfolk text fragment Quentin had ever seen used it the same way: as a direction.

The spiral didn't say *here.* The spiral said *follow me back.*

"Oh," Quentin said quietly. "Oh, you clever, stubborn people."

Because that was the thing about the door—it had never been a door in the sense Hale's crew understood. It was a terminus. An outflow. The ledgers, the jars, the records: this whole cave was an *outpost*, a downstream annex, a library branch. And libraries had sources. Archives had originals. Somewhere upriver—in country closed by law and legend both, in the headwaters no survey boat was licensed to enter—there was another chamber. The true one. And this door, this beautiful useless door, only mattered from the other side.

You couldn't open it from here. Quentin had pressed every joint, sounded every seam, mapped the thing with a laser level on their second visit. The basalt was a single piece, set into the limestone like a tooth in a jaw, and behind it—according to the core samples Hale's people had so helpfully taken and plugged—the rock continued solid for at least two meters. There was nothing to open *into*, not from this side. The Riverfolk hadn't built a vault door. They'd built a message: *what you're looking for is upstream.*

And Meridian had read the same message. That was why the holes were plugged. That was why the door stood untouched. Hale's people had cored it, understood it, and left—not defeated, but redirected. Already gone. Possibly already moving upriver.

Quentin's skin went cold despite the damp.

* * *

They spent another hour documenting the damage, and this time they photographed everything—every gouged panel, every empty niche, every boot print in the mud (size eleven, ribbed sole, industrial), every drill scar. If the cave was going to be violated, the violation would at least be on the record. It felt like a small, stubborn act of witness, and Quentin clung to it the way you cling to anything when the larger shape of events has stopped making sense.

It was near four in the morning when they found the last thing.

Tucked into the corner of the central chamber, half-hidden behind the fallen slab Quentin had noted on their first visit, was a length of orange poly rope. Marine grade. Fresh ends, machine-cut. And knotted into it, at intervals, a series of loops—climber's loops, tied fast and clean, the kind of knots you learn hauling equipment out of flooded quarries.

Quentin knew those knots. Had tied those exact knots, in that exact style, for six years, working river surveys for contractors who bid on Meridian subcontracts. Somebody had rigged this chamber for heavy lifting. Somebody had planned to haul the door itself, or slabs cut from around it, out through the cave mouth on a rising tide.

They'd measured the tide windows, too. Quentin checked the rope against the tidal tables in their head and did the arithmetic and felt the answer arrive like ice water: the next window deep enough for a heavy extraction was five days out. Six at most.

Then monsoon season closed the coast entirely—for months.

Everything was converging on the same narrow gap in time. The syndicate's extraction window. The landfall forecast. Whatever Marion Kavanagh was doing right now, somewhere on the water, sailing toward a daughter who'd spent a decade pretending not to exist.

Quentin gathered their gear with hands that were steadier than they deserved to be. At the cave mouth they paused, turned back, and took one last look at the spiral sigil by full lamplight—no more red-filter caution, no more hiding. Let it be seen. Let it be witnessed.

*Follow me back,* the spiral said. *Upstream. To the source.*

The sea was sighing against the threshold now, the tide reclaiming the approach, and Quentin waded out through knee-deep water into the gray beginning of dawn, and the thought that had been forming all night finally assembled itself into a sentence:

Hiding had failed. Invisibility had failed. The cave had been found, stripped, and read by men who would own the region's water history if they reached the source first—and the only route upstream ran through gorges that ate expeditions alive, through villages that remembered, through country where Quentin's mother's thirty-year theory might finally be proven or buried, and Quentin could not let Hale get there first, which meant going themselves, which meant—

Which meant they couldn't do it alone.

There was a historian in Sallow's Reach with a grandfather's lost expedition and a journal full of gorge routes. There was a woman on a ferry with a pendant and a lifetime of unfinished work. And there was a version of Quentin Kavanagh who had spent ten years perfecting the art of being nobody, standing waist-deep in a rising tide, understanding that nobody was exactly who the river would drown first.

Back at the room, Quentin dug Vess Odumbe's card out from under the mattress where they'd hidden it like contraband. Then they pulled the survey chart of the Karst Coast from their kit, unrolled it, and drew one line with a grease pencil—from the cave mouth, upriver, past the last marked sounding, into the blank paper where the map simply gave up.

The line pointed at everything they'd spent a decade avoiding.

The ferry from the south docked at seven-fifteen. Quentin intended to be on the pier when it arrived.
