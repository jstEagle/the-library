## 9. Upstream Is the Only Way

The ledgers did not want to be read at night.

That was Marion's theory, delivered at two in the morning over the folding table in Quentin's rented room above the chandlery, her reading glasses pushed up into gray hair she no longer bothered to dye. "Ledger walls are lit by firelight or nothing," she said, tapping the printout of Quentin's photographs—the ones they'd finally admitted to taking, three days after swearing they'd taken none. "Whoever carved these expected to read them by lamp. We're reading them under an LED strip like butchers reading a carcass."

"Then read them like a butcher," Quentin said. "What do you see?"

Marion had been in Sallow's Reach for six days. Six days of the same argument wearing different clothes: *you should have called me, you should have told me, you should never have gone in alone.* And underneath it, quieter, something Quentin kept refusing to name: *she's good at this.* Watching Marion work was like watching someone else remember their own childhood—every gesture familiar and foreign at once, the way she tilted her head before a breakthrough, the way she hummed four notes of nothing when a pattern started to resolve.

Tonight the humming stopped.

"It's not a ledger," Marion said. "I've been reading it wrong. We both have. It's not a record of what they stored here." Her finger moved down the column of spiral-framed glyphs on the printout, the ones Quentin had catalogued as inventory marks. "It's a record of what they *removed*."

Quentin pulled a chair around backward and sat on it the wrong way, arms folded across the backrest, close enough to smell the coffee going cold between them. "Removed where?"

"That's the question, isn't it." Marion spread three more printouts across the table, overlapping them until the columns ran together into one long river of glyphs. "Look at the verbs. The Riverfolk script uses directional affixes—we know this from the Kesh fragments, the ones I spent thirty years being laughed at about." She said it without bitterness now, or with so much old bitterness it had worn smooth as sea glass. "*Sent down.* *Carried down.* *Given to the water.* Every entry. This isn't a warehouse manifest. It's a shipping log."

"For a cave?"

"For an outflow." Marion looked up, and for a moment the duel was gone from her face and there was just the scholar, lit up, dangerous. "The tide opens this cave twice a year at most. Nobody stores an archive in a place you can reach eleven days out of a thousand. You store it somewhere safe and you put a *copy*—a working copy, a lending library—at the mouth of the road. This is the lending library. Everything here was sent *down* from somewhere upstream."

"The source," Quentin said.

"The source."

They sat with it. Outside, the harbor slapped against pilings, and somewhere below the window a drunk was singing a song about a woman who'd left him for a ferry captain, which struck Quentin as the most honest accounting anyone in this town had produced all week.

* * *

By dawn they had the shape of it, and the shape of it was worse than either of them had guessed.

The ledger's final section wasn't glyphs at all—it was a map, rendered in the Riverfolk's maddening convention where north was whatever direction the water came from, which meant every map was also a confession of where home was. Quentin taped the pages to the wall above the desk and stood looking at them while Marion slept badly in the other bed, and slowly the convention resolved: the spirals marked confluences, the broken lines marked gorges, and at the top of everything, drawn small and dense as a knot, was a dome.

A limestone dome. A spring beneath it. The same dome that appeared in exactly one other place Quentin had ever seen it: the pendant.

Their mother's pendant. The one she'd worn every day of Quentin's childhood, black stone with a white spiral, turning at the end of its cord when she leaned over a dig table. The one she'd stopped wearing the year Quentin left, though Quentin only knew that because Vess Odumbe had shown them a conference photograph from nine years ago, Marion bare-throated at a podium, and asked, casually, too casually, *when did she stop wearing it?*

Quentin hadn't answered her then. They weren't sure they could answer anything now.

The country above the dome was closed. That wasn't legend; that was law. The headwaters sat inside the Karst Interior Reserve, a designation older than most nations' conservation statutes—a blanket closure signed after some colonial-era atrocity nobody wrote down, protecting a watershed nobody mapped, on behalf of a people everybody assumed had ceased to exist. Permits were issued once a decade, if that. Meridian Salvage would have lawyers working the angle already. Academics would write letters. And the river itself would do what rivers do, which is say no.

Eleven days. That was the number the radio kept giving them, updated each morning with the false cheer of forecasters who knew better: monsoon landfall, eleven days out, tracking slightly east, slightly early. After landfall the gorges would run brown and lethal for weeks. Whatever window existed would close like a door.

Quentin stood at the wall until the light came up gray through the salt-hazed window, and understood, with the flat clarity of a man reading his own obituary, that hiding was over. Not because the choice had been taken from them—because it had been made, sometime, without ceremony. Maybe the night they'd entered the cave alone. Maybe ten years ago, boarding a river survey boat with a name shortened and a past folded away. The river had been waiting the whole time. Rivers are patient like that.

* * *

Vess arrived at seven with bread and a look on her face that meant bad news delivered gently.

"Meridian chartered the *Corvina* yesterday," she said, setting the loaf on the table like a paperweight on a runaway document. "Fuel, dive gear, a barge crane. Hale's not outfitting for salvage anymore. He's outfitting for expedition."

"He doesn't know where to go."

"He knows you know." Vess poured herself coffee without asking, an intimacy Quentin resented and appreciated in equal measure. "And he knows your mother's here, and my grandfather's journal is missing from my room, which we can discuss later when I'm less furious, and every fixer on this coast knows the reserve permits office opens at nine. He'll be legal within the week. Illegal within two if he has to be."

Marion emerged from the curtained alcove with her hair flattened on one side and her eyes already fully awake, which had always been obscene. "Then we go first."

"Mother." Quentin heard their own voice come out flat. "The reserve is closed. Closed means closed. There's a patrol post at the gorge mouth, there's a permit process measured in years, and there's a monsoon coming. Three reasons. Pick any one."

"I picked all three thirty years ago," Marion said. "That's why I never went. That's why the theory stayed a theory and everyone got to laugh at me at conferences while I smiled and drank warm wine." She crossed to the wall of printouts and stood before the little knotted dome at the top of everything. "I let the law decide for me because the law was a respectable way of being afraid. I'm done being respectable. I have maybe fifteen good field years left and I intend to spend one of them being right."

"And the patrol post?"

"Ama Sefu," Vess said quietly.

Both of them turned. She was holding her recovered journal—Hale's people had returned it, eventually, after Quentin's lie at the rapids had bought time rather than safety, and the pages smelled faintly of someone else's hands. "Kesh-Taru sits on the reserve boundary. The elders have walked those gorges longer than the patrol post has had a radio. If the Riverfolk take us upriver, the law becomes a formality. If they don't—" she shrugged, "—then we were trespassers with good intentions, which is how my grandfather died, so I'd rather not repeat the experiment."

"You want to go back and ask them."

"I want to go back and ask them *properly*. Not with a camera and a sample kit. With our hands open." She looked at Quentin, and there was something in her face that wasn't quite challenge and wasn't quite plea. "You met them. You carried the tablet out. They'll listen to you."

"They listened to me because I gave something back. That's not a key, Vess. It's a debt. Debts don't reopen."

"No," Marion said, still facing the wall. "But they're a reason to knock."

* * *

They spent the day preparing, and preparation meant decisions, and decisions meant the last hours of Quentin's invisible life ticking away like water out of a hull.

There was no version of this that stayed quiet. Going upstream meant filing a route plan with the harbor authority, which meant a name on a document, which meant *Kavanagh* on a document, because Quentin had discovered somewhere in the last week that lying to officials required a fluency they'd lost and lying to themselves had never worked at all. Corliss Hale would read that name within a day. Everyone would. The decade of being nobody would end not with a bang but with a customs form.

Quentin filed it anyway, at 4:40 in the afternoon, in handwriting they hadn't used since graduate school.

The boat was Vess's idea—a shallow-draft river longboat out of the fishing fleet, half price because its owner believed the headwaters were haunted and wanted the ghost-risk priced in. They bought rope, resin-sealed dry bags, a secondhand water tester, and antibiotics from a pharmacist who asked no questions, which in Sallow's Reach was a professional courtesy rather than a virtue. Marion argued about the rope gauge. Quentin caught themselves enjoying the argument and made a private note of alarm about that.

At dusk, Vess went to send word to Kesh-Taru through the fisherman—the same one from the first day, the one who'd warned that some holes are meant to stay shut—who turned out to have a nephew married into the village and a canoe that ran the boundary stretch twice a month regardless of law, legend, or weather. Some inheritances, Quentin thought, watching him load a sealed message tube, aren't written down at all.

Which left the night, and the wall of printouts, and the thing Quentin had been circling since dawn.

Marion was repacking her kit with the compulsive precision of a woman who'd once lost a trowel and never forgiven the world. Quentin watched her zip, buckle, label. Then: "Why did you stop wearing the pendant?"

Her hands stopped. For a long moment the only sound was the harbor and the drunk, who had graduated to a song about drowning.

"Because I lost it," she said finally. "Or I told myself I lost it. The clasp was failing and I put it in a drawer for repair and then—" she made a small gesture that contained a hospital corridor, a dormitory emptied of one occupant, ten years of silence, none of which she named. "Drawers are easy. You think you're postponing. You're actually deciding."

Quentin looked at the dome on the wall, small and knotted, the spring beneath it holding a civilization's memory behind stone.

"I'm coming with you," Marion added, misreading or refusing to misread the silence. "Upstream. Don't argue. You'll need someone who can read the affixes when the ledgers get wet."

"I wasn't going to argue."

That surprised her enough that she turned around fully, and for a moment mother and child looked at each other across the packing debris like two archaeologists at opposite ends of the same trench, each realizing the strata ran continuous between them.

* * *

They left on the morning tide, which was wrong—expeditions leave at dawn, in stories—but the fisherman said the gorge current favored a mid-morning start and the river didn't care about stories. The longboat sat low in the gray water with its cargo of rope and ambition. Ama Sefu's answer had come back overnight in the fisherman's nephew's careful hand: *Come to the boundary. Bring the one who returned the tablet. The river will decide the rest.*

Not yes. Not no. A door, unlatched.

As they cast off, Quentin looked back once at Sallow's Reach—the chandlery, the rented room with its wall of paper, the whole small architecture of a hidden life—and felt not grief but something closer to relief, the way a held breath feels when it finally goes. Somewhere in that town, Hale's people were reading a customs form and recalculating. Let them. The race was real now, and honest, and the river would settle it the way rivers settle everything.

Eleven days to landfall. Four to the boundary. Two, if Ama Sefu's people agreed to guide them, to the gorges proper, where the maps broke into broken lines and the ledger's little knotted dome waited at the top of everything, holding whatever the Riverfolk had thought worth sending down for three hundred years—and whatever they had thought worth keeping up, behind stone, forever.

Quentin took the tiller, found the channel, and pointed the bow upstream.

The river, patient as ever, began to carry them home.
