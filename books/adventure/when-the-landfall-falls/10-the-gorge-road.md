## 10. The Gorge Road

The river changed its mind about us at Kesh-Taru's northern bend.

Below the village it had been wide and brown and patient, a working river with barge traffic and fish traps and the lazy confidence of water that knows where it's going. Above the village it narrowed, quickened, turned the color of old glass. The Karst Coast had been limestone and salt. This was granite and cold, walls rising on either side of us like the pages of a book someone had slammed shut.

Ama Sefu's grandson piloted us as far as his grandfather's authority ran—a day upstream to a landing called the Weir Stones, where three leaning monoliths marked the edge of what the Riverfolk considered navigable. He wouldn't go past them. He wouldn't say why, either; he just touched the middle stone with two fingers, said something in the old tongue that sounded like an apology, and left us with the boat, two paddles, and a look of genuine pity.

"Cheerful fellow," Marion said.

"He thinks we're going to die," Vess said.

"He thinks we're *going somewhere*," Marion corrected. "There's a difference. Dying is just the toll."

I didn't say anything. I was watching the far bank, where the tree line ended and the scree began, because for the last ten minutes I'd been fairly sure we were being watched back.

Meridian's crew had kept pace since dawn—two fiberglass skiffs running dark hulls, no flags, staying exactly far enough upstream that binoculars became guesswork. Corliss Hale wasn't stupid enough to close the distance in Riverfolk water. But he was patient enough to let the gorge do his work for him. Gorges funnel people. That's their whole job.

We loaded the packs, and I took the bow, because I'd spent a decade learning rivers and I wanted first sight of whatever the water decided to throw at us.

The first day was almost kind. The gorge opened into a slot of green light, terraced gardens gone feral on the high banks—the ghost-farms Vess's grandfather had described, stone walls still standing after sixty years of nobody tending them. Vess read from the journal as we walked the towpath, her finger tracing his handwriting like braille.

*"Passed the hanging gardens at midday. The porters refuse the next reach. They say the river eats boats here. Paid double."*

"And then?" Marion asked.

Vess turned the page. "And then there are four days of entries about weather."

Four days of weather, and then nothing. The journal went blank at the exact point where the gorge pinched to its narrowest. Vess had stopped mentioning this out loud sometime around noon, but I'd seen her checking the blank pages the way you check a wound.

We made camp that night on a gravel bar beneath an overhang, and I took first watch, which meant I sat facing downstream listening to the river and pretending not to hear my mother reorganizing her pack by headlamp thirty feet away. She did everything precisely, quietly, the way she catalogued sherds. Even her breathing sounded peer-reviewed.

"You're staring at the water like it owes you money," she said, without looking up.

"I'm staring at the water like it's going to try something."

"Mm." She snapped a strap tight. "Your grandmother used to say that about the sea. That it was always trying something. She was right, of course. It took her brother."

I knew that story. I knew every story she'd ever told me about everyone except herself. "Get some sleep, Marion."

A pause. Ten years ago that pause would have become a fight. Now she just said, "Quentin," in a tone I refused to examine, and lay down facing away from me.

* * *

The second day the gorge quit being polite.

It narrowed until the walls overhung, until we were walking in a corridor of shadow with a strip of sky overhead like a scar. The towpath crumbled into nothing and we took to the water again, poling through rapids that the boat handled badly and my shoulders handled worse. Marion, to her credit, worked without complaint, hauling lines with hands that were seventy years old and stubborn as rigging.

It was Vess who spotted the smoke.

Not real smoke—the haze of it, a blue smudge above the eastern rim where no camp should be. Meridian had climbed. While we fought the current below, they'd portaged the rim trail and were pacing us from above, and now they could see everything: our route, our camps, our pace. Hale didn't need to catch us. He just needed to know where we'd be.

"They're herding us," Vess said.

"No," Marion said, squinting up. "They're *timing* us. There's a difference. Herding is what you do to animals. Timing is what you do to a schedule." She lowered the glasses. "They want to let the river do the expensive part and meet us at the source with fresh crews and full supplies. Which means our only advantage is arriving angry, exhausted, and early."

"That's not an advantage," I said.

"It's the only one on offer."

We pushed through the afternoon in silence broken only by water. And it was late—gold light going gray at the bottom of the gorge, the air already cooling fast—when the rock came down.

No warning. No rumble first, or none that reached us under the river's noise. Just a crack like a rifle shot from the southern wall, and then the whole face of it letting go.

Later, I would reconstruct it in pieces: the white plume of dust, the boulders coming down in long bouncing arcs, Vess screaming and diving behind a fallen slab. Marion was fifteen meters behind me on the path, hauling the supply line, and the slide caught her in the open with a boulder the size of a truck arcing toward the shelf of rock she stood on.

I don't remember deciding. I remember the line going taut in my hands, and I remember throwing myself backward off the path onto the lower rocks, using my whole weight against hers, and the world turning into impact and dust and the sound of stone ending things.

Then quiet. Dust like fog. My shoulder screaming where the line had nearly taken my arm out of its socket.

Marion was on her back three meters from where she'd been standing, on ground that no longer existed where she'd been standing. A boulder sat in the river where the path had been. She was coughing, gray-faced, gray-covered, alive.

For a second neither of us moved. Then she said, hoarse, "You could have been killed."

"So could you."

"Yes, but I've had a full life. You've barely had half of one, and most of it spent hiding from me." She sat up, wincing, and started brushing dust off her sleeves with those precise little motions. "That was very reckless."

"That was Tuesday," I said. "Come on. We need to move before the rest of the wall decides to follow it down."

She looked at me then—really looked, dust in her hair, blood starting at her hairline where a stone chip had caught her—and for one unguarded moment there was something in her face that had nothing to do with archaeology. Something old and frightened and grateful, all jammed together behind her eyes like sediment layers.

"Thank you," she said.

I picked up the supply line and started walking. "Don't mention it."

She didn't. Neither of us mentioned it for the rest of the day, and that, somehow, was its own kind of conversation—the first honest one we'd had in ten years. Some things you can only say by refusing to say them. She knew it. I knew it. The river roared underneath us, indifferent, carrying on.

* * *

We camped that night higher than I liked, on a ledge above the flood line, because the slide had rearranged the gorge and I no longer trusted the water's manners. Vess slept like the dead—she'd earned it. Marion didn't sleep. I heard her moving around at midnight, and found her sitting at the ledge's edge with her knees drawn up, looking down at the black ribbon of the river.

"Can't sleep?" I asked.

"I'm inventorying." She patted the rock beside her. When I didn't sit, she continued anyway. "Do you know what I was doing when your father called me? The night you were born? I was in a trench in the Karst interior, and we'd just hit a sealed chamber floor, and I told him I'd come when the season ended. Six weeks. I told him six weeks, Quentin. You were six weeks old the first time I held you."

I sat down. Not because I forgave her. Because my legs were tired.

"The ledgers," she said. "In the sea cave. Did you notice the dating system? They counted years by flood events. Every major flood got a mark, and every mark got a record of what the people did next. Rebuilt. Moved. Mourned. The archive isn't a treasure, it's a *response pattern*. Three hundred years of a civilization deciding, over and over, to stay by the water." She was quiet a moment. "I spent thirty years thinking I was chasing a library. I think I've been chasing a mirror."

The river slid past below us, dark and continuous.

"When the wall came down today," she said, "you didn't hesitate."

"Don't read anything into it."

"Oh, I intend to read everything into it. I'm an archaeologist. Reading into things is the entire profession." But she said it softly, and then, quieter still: "You have her hands. Your grandmother's. Steady in bad water."

I should have said something. I had ten years of things saved up, a whole archive of my own, sealed and dated and never opened. Instead I said, "Sleep, Marion. Dawn's in five hours and the gorge gets worse tomorrow."

She laughed—one short breath of it, surprised out of her—and lay back on her pad. "Yes, sir," she said, and for the first time since the ferry, it didn't sound like a duel.

* * *

Dawn brought company.

I smelled woodsmoke before I saw them, and came up over the ledge lip with the glasses to find a fire burning on the gravel bar below us—our gravel bar, the one we'd abandoned—and four figures around it, cooking breakfast with the unhurried ease of people who have never once been chased. One of them looked up, found me instantly, and raised a hand. Not a wave. An acknowledgment. *We see you. We're not hurrying.*

Hale wasn't among them. That was worse. Hale stayed with the boats, saving himself for the endgame, sending his patience ahead of him like scouts.

Vess joined me at the lip, following my gaze. "They're between us and the next landing."

"They're not between us and anything. They're *behind* schedule and showing us." I passed her the glasses. "Look at their packs. Full expedition loads. Ropes, drills—" I stopped. "Drills."

Vess found them too. Leaning against a boulder, casual as fishing rods. Rock drills, the long-barreled pneumatic kind, and beside them something wrapped in canvas that had the proportions of explosives.

"They're not following us to the source," Vess said slowly. "They're following the *river*. If we seal it, if we get there first and Ama Sefu's people assert rights—they'll want another way in. From above. Through the dome itself."

Marion had come up behind us without either of us hearing, which was becoming a habit. "Then we have a problem of arithmetic," she said. "They climb the rim, we hold the gorge floor. Two routes to the same room. And only one of them is willing to put holes in a grave to get there."

Below, the men doused their fire, shouldered their packs, and started up the rim trail without a backward glance—moving faster than us now, fresh, supplied, and pointed at the same horizon.

Vess checked her grandfather's journal, then the sky, then the journal again. "Weather entry," she said. "Last one he wrote. Listen." She read aloud, her voice flattening in the morning air:

*"'The river is louder today. The porters say it is singing. I have begun to hear words in it.'"*

She closed the book. Above us, far up the gorge, thunder rolled though the sky was clean and blue.

"That's not thunder," I said.

Three hundred years of flood marks in the ledger walls. Every one of them a decision to stay. Ama Sefu's voice came back to me across the firelight in Kesh-Taru: *the source is a grave, not a vault.* And graves, I thought, watching the clean blue sky hold its breath, have ways of telling you when someone is digging.

The river was singing.
