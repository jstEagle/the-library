## 11. The Village That Remembers

Vess had been dreaming of her grandfather's last entry for three nights running, and the dream always ended the same way: not with his handwriting, but with the blank space after it. The page where *we are close* trailed off mid-sentence, the ink feathering where water or time or something worse had gotten to it, and then nothing. Sixty years of nothing.

She woke on the boat deck with the journal open on her chest, a habit she couldn't break even now, even here, where the river ran green-brown and wide as a lake and the gorges behind them had finally loosened their grip. Quentin was already up, crouched at the bow with binoculars, and Marion sat wrapped in a blanket reading tide tables like they were scripture. Ama Sefu stood at the stern beside the pilot, pointing once, twice, and the pilot cut the engine.

"Kesh-Taru," the old woman said, in the tone other people used for names of the dead.

The village appeared out of the morning haze the way things appear in dreams: all at once, complete, as if it had been waiting. It sat on a bluff above a wide bend, stilt houses with tin and thatch roofs, drying racks hung with fish, a temple structure at the center built of dark wood so old it had gone silver. Terraced gardens climbed the hillside behind it. And everywhere, carved into lintels and doorposts and the prows of the moored boats, Vess saw it—the spiral.

Not the way Meridian's people would see it, she thought. Not as a logo, a brand, a thing to be claimed. Here it was worn smooth by hands touching it, generations of hands, the grooves polished dark.

"You knew," Marion said quietly, coming to stand beside Vess. "You knew this place existed."

"I knew my grandfather passed through country where people remembered," Ama Sefu said. She was small, straight-backed, her hair white and cropped close, her eyes the color of river stones. "I did not know if anyone still lived who kept what needed keeping. There is a difference between remembering and keeping, Dr. Kavanagh. Most of the world only remembers."

* * *

They were not welcomed so much as assessed.

The whole village seemed to turn out along the bluff path—children first, bold and laughing, then adults whose faces held the particular patience of people who had watched outsiders come and go for centuries. A man in a faded uniform shirt introduced himself as Toban, headman's son, and asked questions in a voice too mild to refuse: Where from. How far upriver. Who else knows you came.

Quentin answered each one flatly and completely, and Vess watched something shift in the crowd's posture—not warmth, exactly, but a recalibration. Liars got turned around at the landing. This one didn't lie; this one just didn't offer more than was asked.

It was Marion who made the mistake. Of course it was Marion.

"The cave on the Karst Coast," she said, when Toban asked what brought them. "Ledger walls. Sealed jars. A stone door with the spiral sigil. I've spent thirty years building a theory about the Riverfolk archive, and that cave is the proof that—"

"Ama Sefu will hear this," Toban said, "or she will not."

He walked away. Just like that, mid-sentence, leaving Marion standing there with thirty years of scholarship hanging unfinished in the humid air. Vess saw her mother-in-all-but-blood—the woman whose books she'd slept with under her pillow at fifteen—go pale, then set her jaw, then say nothing at all. It was the nothing that told Vess how much it cost her.

They waited two hours in a guest house that smelled of woodsmoke and dried fish. Nobody offered them tea until Quentin fixed a child's broken kite string without being asked, and then suddenly there was tea, and breadfruit, and a girl of maybe ten staring at Vess's grandfather's journal with an expression Vess recognized from mirrors: hunger.

"She can't read it," Vess said to the girl, gently. "Nobody can. That's why we're here."

"That's why you're lost," the girl said, and ran off laughing.

* * *

Ama Sefu received them at dusk in the temple, alone, seated on a woven mat with a single oil lamp between them. Up close Vess could see the age in her hands, spotted and strong, and the way her fingers rested on her knees in a position that looked less like sitting and more like bracing.

"Show me the sigil," she said.

Marion produced photographs—of course she'd printed them, of course she carried them like relics—and laid them before the elder. Ama Sefu looked for a long time. Her thumb moved toward the spiral and stopped just short of touching it, hovering, the way you hover over a sleeping child.

"My grandmother's grandmother was born in the year the traders stopped coming," she said. "She lived to be older than I am now, and she taught me the counting songs, and the songs are ledgers. Did you know that? What your scholars call decoration, we call bookkeeping." She looked up. "You found the outflow chamber. The tide room. We have songs about the tide room."

"You know about the cave," Quentin said.

"We know about everything the river keeps." Ama Sefu folded her hands. "Now ask me the question you actually came to ask."

Vess felt her own heartbeat in her throat. Marion glanced at her—*you're the historian, ask*—and Quentin simply waited, patient as stone, and somehow it was Vess the old woman's eyes settled on.

"The archive," Vess said. "The source. My grandfather went upriver in 1961 looking for it and never came back. Everyone treats it like a vault. A treasure. Something to be opened."

"And what do you want it to be?"

"I want to know what happened to him."

Something moved across Ama Sefu's face—there and gone, like a fish under brown water. "Then you are the only one of the three of you asking an honest question." She rose, stiffly, and beckoned them deeper into the temple, past screens into a chamber where the air was cool and smelled of resin and old smoke. On the wall, in the lamplight, spirals climbed in columns. Ledger walls. The same hand, the same script, sixty years and four hundred kilometers apart.

"This is not a vault," Ama Sefu said. "I will tell you what my line has kept, and then you will decide whether you still want to go. The source spring is where the Riverfolk sealed their dead."

* * *

She told it plainly, without drama, which somehow made it worse.

The trading people hadn't vanished. They had ended themselves—as a nation, as a diaspora, deliberately—when the great dams and wars of the lowland kingdoms came for their river. Rather than let their records be looted, their names rewritten by conquerors, they carried everything upstream to the headwater spring, the place their songs called the First Mouth, and they sealed it. Not hiding it. Burying it. The way you bury a person.

"The archive is a grave," Ama Sefu said. "Every tablet in it is a grave-good. Every chamber is a tomb. Your mother—" a nod at Marion, "—has spent thirty years trying to dig up a cemetery and call it a discovery."

Vess heard Marion breathe in sharply and held her own breath waiting for the rebuttal, the footnote, the scholarly correction. It didn't come. In the lamplight Marion Kavanagh looked, for the first time since Vess had known her, genuinely unarmored.

"I didn't know," Marion said. "There was no way to know."

"There was a way to ask," Ama Sefu said. "No one asked. That is also a kind of history, Dr. Kavanagh. The history of people who don't ask."

Quentin spoke from the shadows near the door, voice level. "And my grandfather?" Vess started—*my*, but the old woman answered as if the slip were natural, as if grief pooled and everyone drew from the same well.

"Two men came upriver in 1961. One was your grandfather. They reached the gorges above the drowned ferry town and the river took one of them. The other turned back and never spoke of it. We sang for the one the river took. We sing for the dead of the river every year. He is not lost, Vess Odumbe. He is kept."

Vess discovered she was crying without having decided to, silently, and that no one in the room thought less of her for it. Sixty years of blank space on a page, and the answer had been a song, sung annually, by people she'd never met, for a man who died chasing the same obsession that had carried her here.

"Why help us at all, then?" Marion asked. "If it's a grave, why not send us away?"

"Because the sea cave has been chiseled," Ama Sefu said, and Vess saw Quentin go very still. "Because men with machines came through last month asking about the First Mouth, and they did not ask politely. Because when the monsoon comes early—and it is coming early—the river will rise and take its secrets back down to the salt, and whatever happens next will happen whether we guide you or not." She looked at each of them in turn. "So tomorrow I will tell you my price. Tonight, sleep. And understand this: you may be the ones who find the source. But you will not be the ones who found it first. We never lost it. We are its keepers. You are guests, or you are trespassers, and which you are depends entirely on what you do at the spring."

* * *

Outside, the night was thick with frogsong and river sound. Vess sat on the guest house steps with the journal open on her knees, not reading it, just holding it, feeling the weight of a dead man's handwriting and a living woman's song.

Marion came out and stood beside her, not sitting, arms crossed against the damp.

"He's kept," Vess said. "Sixty years, and he's been kept the whole time."

"Yes."

"I spent my life angry at a blank page. And it was never a mystery. It was a funeral I wasn't invited to."

Marion was quiet a long moment. "That's the cruelest thing about archives," she said finally. "They make grief look like ignorance." Then, softer, almost to herself: "I built a career on not knowing the difference."

From inside, Quentin's silhouette crossed the window, pausing over the lamp, and Vess watched Marion watch it with an expression she had no name for yet.

In the morning, Ama Sefu named her price—and it was Marion who hesitated, and Quentin who signed first, and Vess understood, watching the pen move, that the expedition had just changed owners.
