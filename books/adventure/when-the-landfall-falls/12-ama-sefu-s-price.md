## 12. Ama Sefu's Price

The elder's name was Ama Sefu, and she did not offer them chairs.

They stood in the long house at the center of Kesh-Taru, a hall built of black ironwood on stilts older than the ferry town downstream, older than the dam, older than anything Marion had spent thirty years theorizing about. Smoke from the central hearth rose through a roof vent and hung there, a grey ceiling that never quite left. Riverfolk children watched them from the shadows of the side galleries, and Quentin had the distinct, uncomfortable sense of being an exhibit—three strangers arranged in someone else's museum.

Ama Sefu sat on a low stool wrapped in river-cane, her hands folded over a walking stick carved end to end with spirals. She was small, and she was not frail. When she looked at you, you understood that she had outlasted priests, surveyors, soldiers, and hydrologists, and that she intended to outlast you too.

"You showed the sigil," she said. Her trade-tongue was accented but precise. "In the village square. In front of everyone."

"I'm sorry," Marion began. "I didn't realize it was—"

"You did realize. That is why you showed it." Ama Sefu's voice didn't rise. It didn't need to. "The spiral is not a decoration, Doctor Kavanagh. It is a key, and keys are shown only to doors, or to people who intend to open them. Which are you?"

Marion's mouth opened and closed. Quentin watched her—the great Dr. Marion Kavanagh, who could hold a lecture hall of three hundred in her palm—searching for a sentence and finding none. It was the first time in Quentin's life they had seen their mother genuinely without words.

"The cave on the coast," Quentin said. "It has walls of carved ledgers. Jars sealed with resin. A stone door with that sigil. We didn't open it. Someone else tried."

"Someone else always tries." Ama Sefu turned her head slightly, and one of the children in the gallery came forward—a girl of maybe twelve—and knelt beside her stool. The elder rested a hand on the child's shoulder. "The river keeps its memory, and every few generations someone comes to take it. The company men with their machines. The professors with their permissions. The ones who say *for science*, the ones who say *for the nation*, the ones who say *for everyone*, which means for them. You are which one?"

"I'm not—" Vess started.

"You are the granddaughter of a man who came here in 1961 with a compass and a theory." Ama Sefu looked at Vess, and something in her face softened by a single degree. "He asked. That is more than most. He asked, and we told him the source is a grave, and he went anyway, and the river kept him. Now you carry his book. So. You are grief. That is a kind of honesty, at least."

Vess's jaw tightened. She didn't deny it. Quentin was beginning to understand that Vess never denied anything; she just held things until they were useful.

"And you two." Ama Sefu's gaze came back to them, mother and child standing a careful meter apart, the distance of a decade measured in floorboards. "The mother who spent her life chasing the Riverfolk archive. The child who changed their name so the papers wouldn't find them. You think we don't know who walks into our village? We have been watching the river for two thousand years. We know what a shadow looks like when it's pretending to be a person."

Heat climbed Quentin's neck. Marion said, quietly, "Then you understand why we came. The theory—"

"I understand what you came for." Ama Sefu lifted the walking stick and tapped the floor twice, a sound like a door closing. "I do not yet know what you will leave with. That depends on a price."

* * *

The price was not money.

She made that clear before Marion could open the leather satchel where she kept, Quentin knew, a fold of grant letters and one very old checkbook for exactly these moments. Ama Sefu watched the satchel with an expression that might have been amusement in a younger face.

"You will write it down," she said. "All of you. Because the river does not honor memory alone. Memory floods. Paper can be held."

The girl—the elder called her Nadi, and Quentin gathered from the way the other children deferred that she was Ama Sefu's heir in some sense, blood or otherwise—brought a board of pale wood and a stick of charcoal, and laid them on the floor between the visitors and the elder.

"Anything found at the source," Ama Sefu said, each word placed like a stone in a weir, "belongs to the Riverfolk. To the heirs of the people who made it. Not to a museum in the capital. Not to a company with a salvage license. Not to a university that will put it in a drawer and charge admission to look at it. Not to a mother who wants it to prove her life's work. To *us*. You may look. You may read. You may carry what must be carried out of the water to save it from the water. And then you give it back, and you say thank you, and you go."

Silence. The hearth cracked. Somewhere outside, the river made its low continuous argument against the banks.

Marion crouched slowly, stiffly, and looked at the board. Quentin saw her do the arithmetic—the same arithmetic Quentin had watched her do over thirty years of grant panels and excavation rights and ministry negotiations. *If I sign, I lose the archive. If I don't sign, I lose the source.* Thirty years of theory, a career built on the promise that she would be the one to hold the Riverfolk's memory up to the light, and here was a small woman with a carved stick asking her to sign it all away before she'd seen a single glyph.

"Dr. Kavanagh," Ama Sefu said, almost gently. "You are calculating. That is honest. Take the time."

"I'm not—" Marion started, and stopped, because she was, and everyone in the room knew it.

Quentin looked at their mother crouched over a blank board, and understood suddenly, with terrible clarity, that this was the whole of their childhood in one image. Every excavation. Every summer shipped off to a field station while Marion negotiated access. Every dinner where the dig was the family and Quentin was the equipment. *Take the time. Calculate. What can be kept, what can be claimed, what can be published.* Quentin had spent ten years building a life whose entire architecture was *not that*. Remote surveys, borrowed names, rivers nobody famous would ever come to.

And now here was the fork, actually in the road, actually real.

Marion's hand hovered over the charcoal.

Quentin took it first.

The stick was light and slightly damp. Quentin knelt on the ironwood floor of a stranger's long house and wrote, in a hand that shook only a little:

*Anything found at the source belongs to the Riverfolk and their heirs. Not to museums. Not to companies. Not to universities. Not to mothers. Not to me. We will look, we will read, we will save what can be saved from the flood, and we will give it back. Signed, Quentin Kavanagh.*

They used the name. Their real name, the one they'd spent a decade burying, written in front of the whole village because Ama Sefu had said paper can be held and Quentin understood, in that moment, that this was the first document of their life that was worth holding.

Marion stared. "Quentin—"

"You were calculating," Quentin said. It came out quieter than they meant, and harder. "I've watched you calculate my whole life. Somebody should just—sign."

Marion flinched. It was small, and she buried it fast, but Quentin had spent a childhood learning to read that face, and it landed. For a moment the long house was very quiet, and Marion Kavanagh knelt on the floor of a village her discipline had ignored for a century, looking at her child's signature on a contract that gave away everything she had ever wanted.

Then she took the charcoal, and wrote beneath Quentin's name: *I agree to these terms. Marion Kavanagh.* Her handwriting was as beautiful and controlled as it had been on a hundred excavation permits. Her hand was not steady.

Vess signed last, fast, like someone tearing off a bandage. Then she added a line of her own: *And the journal of Tomas Odumbe, recovered from Meridian Salvage, is to be returned to his granddaughter.* Quentin hadn't known Vess could want something that visibly. It looked almost like pain.

Ama Sefu read all three signatures without touching the board. Then she looked up at Quentin, and there was something in her face that hadn't been there before—not warmth, exactly. Recognition.

"You signed before your mother," she said.

"Someone had to."

"No." Ama Sefu shook her head slowly. "That is not why you did it. I have watched men sign before their mothers, before their wives, before their gods. They do it to be first. You did it to be *free*. There is a difference, and the river knows the difference." She tapped the floor once with the stick. "You will guide us, then. Nadi will go with you as far as the gorge mouth. And you will learn one thing before you sleep tonight, because the water will not wait for your education."

* * *

They learned it in the square, at dusk, with the whole village gathered.

Ama Sefu stood before a flat stone that generations of feet had polished, and Nadi chalked the spiral sigil at its center. Around it, the elder drew—quick, sure strokes—the river's course as the Riverfolk mapped it: not a line but a body. A mouth at the sea. A throat of gorges. And at the head, a chamber drawn as a closed eye.

"The cave on your coast is the tear duct," Ama Sefu said. "The eye weeps ledgers. But the eye itself is at the spring, under the dome of stone, and the door of the eye does not open from the sea side. It never did. Your syndicate men can drill for a hundred years." She looked at Quentin. "It opens from the source. From inside. From the hand of someone the archive will recognize."

"Recognize how?" Marion asked.

Ama Sefu smiled, and it was not entirely a comforting sight. "That is the second thing you will learn. But not tonight. Tonight you sleep, because at first light you go upstream, and the monsoon is coming faster than your radios know." She turned to go, then paused, and said, without looking back:

"The one who signed first walks second. Behind me, always. The river tests the one who gives things away."

Quentin lay that night on a woven mat in a guest house that smelled of woodsmoke and river, listening to the water rise in its sleep, and understood that somewhere behind them, down the dark gorges, Corliss Hale was already moving.

And that in eleven days—maybe fewer—the landfall would come, and the eye at the source would open for someone, or close for everyone.

The river had already chosen its test.
