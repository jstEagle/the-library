## 13. Meridian Closes In

The rapids announced themselves a full minute before we saw them—a low grinding under the river's voice, like something enormous turning over in its sleep. Ama Sefu stood at the bow of the longboat with one hand on the gunwale and said nothing, which from her was the loudest warning yet.

"Portage or run?" I asked.

"Run the first chute," she said. "Walk the rest. The river narrows past the bend, and narrow water is honest water. It tells everyone who passes."

I didn't understand her until an hour later, when we came around the bend and found Meridian's aluminum-hulled boat wedged across the channel like a fish spine, three crewmen standing in it with the casual posture of men who had been waiting all morning and expected to be thanked for it.

Corliss Hale stood on the gravel shoal downstream, hands in his pockets, watching the water with the patience of a man who owned the patience and everything it was pointed at. He'd shaved. That was the detail that frightened me most. Somewhere between the coast and the gorges, between the offer I'd refused and the cave I'd found chiseled open, he had decided this stopped being a negotiation and become a project. Projects got shaved for. Projects got photographed.

Marion was already reaching for her satchel—the reflex of a woman who believed documents were armor—and I put my hand flat on it without looking at her. She withdrew. We had been learning each other's languages again, my mother and I, the way you learn a river you thought you knew and find it has moved in the night.

Vess went white when she saw the boat. Not at the men—at the dry bag lashed under the thwart, the one with her grandfather's journal in it. The 1961 route through the gorges, the fragmentary map, the last words of a man who had walked into this same country and never walked out. She'd read from it aloud two nights ago by headlamp, her voice going strange on certain pages, and I had understood then that she wasn't carrying a document. She was carrying a hand.

"Stay in the boat," I said. "All of you. Let me do the talking."

"Quentin." Marion's voice, low.

"I said let me."

I stepped onto the shoal and walked down to Hale with my hands visible and my pulse doing its best to betray me.

"Kavanagh." He smiled like a door closing. "You made good time."

"You've been following us since the second gorge."

"Since the coast, actually. You just didn't check your wake." He turned and gestured at the river with an open palm, a real-estate agent's gesture, generous and hollow. "Beautiful country. Closed country. You know what the permit situation is up here? Because I do. It's nothing. Nobody's permitted to be here. Which means nobody can report us, and nobody can rescue you. I want you to sit with that for a second. Not as a threat. As geography."

Behind me I heard the crewmen boarding our longboat. Not rushing. They lifted out the fuel drums, the food boxes, the med kit, the satellite phone Marion had argued we couldn't afford and I had argued we couldn't survive without. They worked with the efficiency of men doing a job they'd done before, in places with other names.

One of them lifted Vess's dry bag, and I heard her make a sound I'd never heard a person make—half word, half animal—and start over the gunwale, and Ama Sefu's hand closed on her arm, and Vess stopped, shaking, and the bag went across the water into Meridian's boat.

Hale didn't even watch it happen. He was looking at me.

"That journal," he said. "Old paper. Sixties expedition, right? We've been through it. Interesting man, the grandfather. Got close, apparently. Closer than anyone with a map." He patted his chest pocket, where a folded shape sat. "You want it back, that's a conversation. But first, the conversation I came for."

"Hale—"

"Corliss. Please. We've known each other long enough."

"You've known my name long enough. It's not the same thing."

He laughed, genuinely, and for a moment the charm was real and that was worse. "Here's the last deal, Kavanagh, and I mean last, because after this I stop needing you. You bring me to the source. You walk me to the door, whatever's there, you show me how it opens. In exchange: the journal goes back to your historian, the supplies go back in your boat, my crew forgets your mother's name, and everyone walks out of this valley before the landfall. Everyone. Including my guys, including the old woman, including you." He spread his hands. "I'm not a monster. I'm a contractor. Monsters don't have deadlines."

"And if I say no?"

"Then we take the boats, and you walk. Downstream's four days. The landfall's three. I'd do the math for you but you're a surveyor, it'd be insulting."

The river ground away behind him. Somewhere upstream, three days' hard travel, the spring waited under its limestone dome with a civilization's memory sealed in resin, and I thought about the ledger walls in the sea cave, the chiseled scars, the drill marks on the stone door—Meridian's patience already written into the rock. He didn't need me. He'd said so himself. What he needed was speed, and I was the only speed he could buy.

I looked at his face and I lied to it.

"Fine," I said.

He blinked. I felt the lie leave me like a stone leaving the hand—clean, weightless, committed to its arc.

"Fine," I said again, and made my shoulders drop, made my voice go tired, made myself into the man he'd decided I was: someone who'd held out for pride and could be bought with mercy. "You want the source. I can get you to it. But not like this. Not with your crew breathing down our necks the whole way—the old woman won't lead us past the third bend with guns behind her, and she's the only one who knows the approach. You want me to bring you to the source, you give us room. Half a day's separation. We go ahead, you follow our marks."

"And you don't just walk into the hills?"

"You'd find us. You found us everywhere else." I shrugged, and the shrug cost me something real, because it was the first piece of the performance that wasn't a performance. "I'm not going to die in this valley over your water rights, Hale. I did the math too."

He studied me for a long moment. I let him. I let him see the exhaustion, the salt-stained jacket, the hands I kept from shaking by thinking about something else entirely—the exact gray of the water over the shoal, the way light came off it like hammered tin.

"Half a day," he said. "You mark the route. If we lose your trail, the deal's dead and I stop being polite." He pulled the journal from his pocket and weighed it in his hand, and I watched Vess on the boat watch it, absolutely still, a woman holding her breath with her whole body. "You get this at the source. Not before. Insurance."

"That wasn't the deal."

"The deal was my offer. This is my counter." He smiled again, the closing door. "You're learning how this works, Kavanagh. That's growth."

They gave back half the supplies—the fuel, half the food, nothing with a motor in it except our own boat's engine, which they'd disabled anyway, and left disabled. They kept the satellite phone. They kept the med kit. Then they pushed off downstream to wait at the third bend, and the river took them, and I stood on the shoal until the sound of their engine faded into the sound of the water, which had never once stopped, which would still be making this exact sound when every one of us was gone.

Marion came down the bank first. "Quentin. What did you just do?"

"What he wanted."

"That's what frightens me." She looked at me the way she looked at a test pit that had come back wrong—careful, interested, unwilling to say the wrong thing out loud. "You lied to him."

"Of course I lied to him."

"No. I mean—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "You've never lied well in your life. You got that from me and it was the worst thing I gave you. You go red. You over-explain. I watched you do it at fourteen, at nineteen, at your father's funeral." Her eyes were wet and fierce. "That wasn't that. That was something else. That was me."

I didn't answer, because the answer was yes, and because I wasn't sure yet whether I'd inherited her talent or her sickness.

Ama Sefu stood at the waterline, and when I turned to her she was already watching me with an expression I couldn't read—until I could. It was the expression of someone who has watched many travelers meet the river's honest water, and who is waiting to see which kind this one turns out to be.

"You gave him the river," she said.

"I gave him a river. Not this one."

"Good." She nodded slowly. "Because the third bend is not where we're going. And the marks you leave—" she almost smiled, the first time I'd seen it— "should be very beautiful, and very wrong."

Vess came last, hollow-eyed, her arms wrapped around herself where the dry bag used to be. "He read it," she said. "He read my grandfather's journal. He held it." Her voice cracked and she hated it, I could see her hating it. "Sixty years my family chased this. Sixty years. And it fit in his chest pocket."

"No," I said. "It didn't."

She looked at me.

"Your grandfather's route is in your head. It's been in your head since the night you read it to us by the fire. The paper was always just paper." I held her eyes. "He took the map. He didn't take the mapmaker. That's the only thing he's ever gotten wrong, and in about half a day it's going to be the most expensive mistake of his life."

Behind us, upstream, the gorges waited, and somewhere above them the storm was assembling itself over the sea, days early, patient as everything else in this country. Three days. Maybe less.

I turned the boat's nose toward the narrows and started making plans that I would only tell the river.

* * *

We ran the disabled engine's dead weight through the first honest water in silence, all four of us poling where we could, hauling where we couldn't, and by late afternoon we reached the place where Ama Sefu finally let us stop—a side channel behind a screen of fallen limestone, deep enough to hide the boat, high enough to sleep above the waterline.

I built a small fire, because we needed to boil water, and because a small fire reads as a smaller party. Then I took Vess's pencil and a sheet of my survey paper and began to draw.

Not our route. Not the gorge road. A route. One that bent east where we'd bend west, that crossed the river where we'd stay on this bank, that climbed a ridge no guide would take and died, politely, at a box canyon a day's march short of anything at all. I made it beautiful. I made it the kind of route a competent, exhausted, frightened surveyor would draw for the man holding his mother's life as collateral. I put a false camp on it. I put a false spring on it. I even, after a moment's cold thought, put a false spiral sigil on it, copied from the sea cave door, because Hale had never seen the real one and a lie with a familiar shape in it is a lie that gets believed.

Marion watched me work for a long time. "You've done this before," she said. Not accusing. Almost wondering.

"Surveyors fake data all the time," I said. "Usually for money. This is the first time it's for anything."

"And when he finds the box canyon and knows?"

"Then he finds it three days from now, in the rain, with the river up, and by then—" I looked at the water, already starting to swell at its edges, already tasting the storm coming down from the coast. "By then the source will have made its own decision about who it belongs to."

Ama Sefu sat with her back against the limestone, hands folded, listening to all of it with her eyes closed like a woman hearing weather. When I finished the false map she opened her eyes and looked at it and said, "The river will rise faster than any forecast. Faster than his boats. Faster than your lies." She said it without cruelty, the way you state a depth. "So whatever you are planning, child—plan it for the water, not for the man."

I folded the map into my jacket, next to my heart, where lies apparently lived now.

Upstream, three days out, the spring waited. Behind us, at the third bend, Hale's crew waited too, watching for marks that would start tomorrow morning, beautiful and wrong.

And somewhere over the coast, the monsoon turned toward us, early, and I understood that the clock we were racing had never been Hale's at all.
