## 14. The Buried Ferry Town

Marion Kavanagh had spent forty years reading landscapes the way other people read faces, and the drowned town of Pell's Crossing told her everything before she saw a single wall.

They came to it on the third day upstream, where the gorge widened without warning into a long grey lake that shouldn't exist. Ama Sefu called it *the still water* and refused to look at it directly, which Marion noted and did not comment on. Vess consulted her grandfather's journal and read aloud: *"Pell's Crossing, last ferry before the narrows. If the dam holds, we cross at dawn."* The dam had held for sixty years. The town beneath it had not been so lucky.

The water was the color of steeped tea, opaque with silt, and utterly motionless. No current. No birds. The cliffs on either side leaned in like men at a funeral.

"We portage here," Quentin said, already unloading the boats. "The old road runs along the north bank. Ama Sefu says it comes out above the narrows by tomorrow night."

"I want an hour," Marion said.

Quentin looked up. Not hostile—worse than that. *Assessing.* Her child had become a person who assessed her, the way Marion herself assessed a trench wall before trusting it.

"An hour for what?"

"The town." Marion nodded at the water. "When they flooded it, they didn't move everything. They never do. There'll be a church bell down there, probably. A school register. Ferry ledgers." She heard her own voice shift into lecture cadence and hated it, and kept going anyway, because the cadence was the only armor she'd ever had. "Ferry ledgers matter. Crossings mean traffic, traffic means tariffs, tariffs mean—"

"Records," Quentin said. "I know. You taught me."

The words landed somewhere between gift and accusation. Marion shouldered her pack. "One hour."

* * *

The town surfaced in pieces as they walked the drowned shoreline.

A chimney first, standing alone in six inches of water like a man up to his ankles waiting for a bus that would never come. Then a fence line, its posts soft as cheese. Then the ferry slip itself—a broad stone ramp descending into the tea-dark water, and at its foot, visible through two feet of astonishing clarity near the edge, the ferry itself: a flat-bottomed barge lying on its side, cables still attached to the winch house, preserved by cold and depth and the particular mercy of mud.

Vess crouched at the waterline, photographing nothing, just looking, and Marion recognized the gesture because it was Quentin's gesture too—the discipline of witnesses who had learned that documentation attracts vultures.

Ama Sefu stood apart, facing upstream. When Marion drifted over, the old woman spoke without turning.

"My grandmother crossed here. Before the water." A pause. "She said the ferryman sang so the river would know him. After the dam, no one sang. The river forgot this place, and the place forgot itself." She looked at Marion then, eyes like wet slate. "You people write things down so you will not forget. We sing so the river will not forget. Understand the difference before you go further."

"I'm trying to," Marion said, and was surprised to find she meant it.

Then Quentin appeared at her elbow, and Ama Sefu moved away toward the portage trail with the unhurried dignity of someone who had already said what needed saying, leaving the two of them alone on the drowned street of a town that had died politely, underwater, in the dark.

* * *

They found the schoolhouse by accident.

It stood on slightly higher ground, half-emerged, its roofline broken but its walls intact, and through a window gap Marion could see desks still ranked in rows, silt drifting across them like grey snow. She waded in without deciding to. The water was knee-deep and cold enough to make her gasp, sixty-two years old and suddenly aware of every one of them.

"Careful," Quentin said from the doorway. Not *Mother, careful.* Just *careful.* The voice of a colleague. Marion had spent a decade being a colleague to her own child and had never once figured out how to feel about it.

"There's a register," she said. Her hand found the headmaster's desk, and beneath a slab of collapsed shelving, swollen into a single fused brick of pulp, the school's records. She lifted them with both hands, reverent, useless. Nothing readable. Sixty years of children's names dissolved into one grey block.

"They're gone," Quentin said.

"Everything dissolves eventually." Marion set the block back down. "That's the whole argument of my career, if you want the truth. Everything dissolves, so you dig. You get there first. You—" She stopped.

Quentin waited. Quentin was very good at waiting; Marion had taught her that too, though she'd taught it by example, by always being elsewhere when waiting might have been required.

"You could have let me drown in that rockslide yesterday," Marion said. It wasn't what she'd meant to say. It was adjacent to what she'd meant to say. "You didn't say a word. You just pulled me out and kept walking."

"It needed doing."

"That's my sentence. That's exactly how I talk. When did you—" Marion laughed, short and joyless, and sat down on a drowned desk, water slopping against her thighs, dignity abandoned somewhere back at the ferry slip. "God. Listen to me. Sitting in a flooded schoolhouse lecturing my adult child about sediment."

"Why did you come, Mother?"

Not *why are you here.* *Why did you come.* Quentin had always known the difference between geography and intent.

Marion looked at the desks. Thirty of them, maybe thirty-five. Small chairs. A chalk rail still bolted to the wall, blank, the slate long since dissolved or stolen or simply tired.

"In 1989," she said, "I was offered the Karst survey. The one that became the theory. The spiral sigils, the trade routes, all of it. It was a thirty-year project and I knew it, I knew it the way you know a marriage is going to be hard and worth it." She turned her hands over, looked at them—archivist's hands, nail-bitten, ink-stained, older than her face somehow. "And your father was already gone two years, and you were eleven, and you were—"

"Don't," Quentin said.

"—and in March of that year you were sick. Badly. The kind of sick where the doctors use words like *monitoring* and *protocol* and don't meet your eyes." The words came out of Marion now with the terrible smoothness of something long rehearsed in silence, never aloud. "Your aunt took you to the hospital. I went to the airport."

The water dripped somewhere. A slow metronome.

"Six weeks," Marion said. "The dig was six weeks. I told myself I'd be back before you were discharged. I told myself presence is not the same as love, that love is provision, that I was building something you'd inherit that was worth more than—" She stopped again. Started again, quieter. "You coded on a Tuesday. Did you know that? Your aunt called the site office and the message took nine days to reach me because the satellite link was down, and when I finally got to a phone, you were already better. You'd already decided. I could hear it in your voice. Eleven years old and you'd already decided I wasn't coming, and you were right, because by then I'd found the second chamber and the second chamber was—"

"*More interesting,*" Quentin said. Flat. Not cruel. That was the worst part—the total absence of cruelty, just the clean surgical accuracy of a wound located precisely where it had always been.

"Yes," Marion said. "More interesting. And I have built a life on that sentence and I deserve every floor of it." She looked up. "I'm not asking you to forgive me. I've watched you refuse to ask me for anything for fifteen years and I understood it completely. I'm telling you because Ama Sefu asked me a question on the shore and I couldn't answer it, and because we're walking into a gorge tomorrow that may kill us, and because you pulled me out of that rockslide without a word and I need you to know that I know what that cost you. To reach for me. After."

Quentin stood in the doorway of the drowned schoolhouse, backlit by grey light, and for a long moment said nothing, and Marion let the nothing stand, because she had earned the nothing, she had purchased the nothing fair and square over decades of installments.

Then Quentin waded forward, past the desks, and stopped an arm's length away—the exact distance of colleagues, of strangers on a train—and said:

"The pendant. The spiral. You wore it every day until I was fourteen and then one day it was gone and you never explained. Was it the sigil? Is that why you came? Is any of this about me at all, or am I just the nearest pair of hands to your life's work?"

And Marion, who had confessed everything else with rehearsed fluency, found this question had no rehearsal behind it at all.

"It was your grandmother's," she said. "From the Karst villages. She traded for it when she was young—it was currency once, before it was jewelry. I gave it to you to wear and then I took it back when—" Her breath caught. "When I left for the second chamber. I told myself I'd keep it safe for you. I told myself a great many things."

"You kept it thirty years."

"I kept it thirty years."

"And now the same spiral is carved on a door in a cave, and you need me to open it, and you can't tell whether you came here for the door or for me." Quentin's voice didn't rise. "Neither can I. That's the problem, Mother. Neither of us knows."

The honesty of it went through Marion like groundwater through limestone—silent, patient, structural. She reached out and touched the drowned desk beside her, the wood gone soft as skin.

"No," she said. "But I know which answer I want it to be. That's new. That's thirty years late, but it's new."

Quentin held her gaze for three seconds—Marion counted them, she counted everything—and then turned for the doorway.

"Portage starts in twenty minutes," Quentin said. "Eat something. You look terrible."

It was, Marion understood, the closest thing to absolution on offer, and it was not absolution at all, and she took it anyway, the way you take shelter in a ruin—grateful, unbelonging, alive.

* * *

They were halfway up the portage trail when Vess came running back down it, journal in hand, face drained white under the sweat and spray.

"The narrows," she said, fighting for breath. "My grandfather wrote about the narrows—but he wrote about them wrong, or the river changed, or—" She thrust the open page at them. "Look. He drew the gorge profile. See the notch, here, the side channel? Ama Sefu says there is no side channel. Says it closed in a flood before he was born. But someone has cut it open again. Recently. Fresh stone, fresh tool marks."

Marion studied the sketch, then the distant white seam of the rapids, and felt the old cold certainty settle over her, the one that had never once been wrong about Meridian Salvage Group.

"Hale isn't following us anymore," she said quietly. "He's ahead of us. He's known the route the whole time—he was never chasing the cave. He was chasing the source, and we've been his map." She looked at Quentin. "He let us live at the rapids because we're still useful. The moment we stop being useful—"

Above them, far off, thunder rolled—not from the sky, which was clear, but from the direction of the narrows, a deep percussive boom rolling up the gorge like a door slamming shut somewhere ahead of them.

Quentin was already running.
