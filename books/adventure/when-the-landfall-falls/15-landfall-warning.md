## 15. Landfall Warning

The radio woke them an hour before dawn, hissing static into the grey light of the camp under the gorge wall. Quentin had slept badly—everyone had, since the rapids, since Hale's crew took the journal and half their food—and they lay listening to the voice crackle through Vess's battered receiver like a man shouting from the bottom of a well.

*"...revised track for Cyclone System Nine. Landfall now projected within seventy-two hours. All river traffic advised to clear the upper gorge system immediately. Repeat: seventy-two hours..."*

Vess sat up in her tarp, hair matted, and turned the dial with two fingers as if she could tune the storm away. The voice repeated itself, patient and mechanical, and then dissolved back into static.

"Three days," she said.

"It was eleven yesterday." Marion was already on her feet, pulling her boots on without untying them first, shoving her foot into the leather by force of habit and stubbornness. "It was nine this morning when we checked at Kesh-Taru."

"Weather doesn't negotiate," Vess said. "It just amends its terms."

Quentin said nothing. They were watching the river.

That was the thing about the Karst headwaters that no forecast accounted for, the thing Quentin had learned in ten years of reading water for a living: the river here didn't rise the way coastal rivers rose. It didn't swell gradually, brown and apologetic. It *arrived*. The gorges funneled everything—the rain falling on a hundred tributary valleys, the runoff from slopes that couldn't hold another drop—and sent it downstream all at once, a wall of water moving faster than any boat could outrun once it started. The old survey charts called it pulse-flooding. Ama Sefu called it something else entirely.

She was awake too, of course. She was always awake first, sitting on the flat stone at the edge of camp with her walking stick across her knees, facing upstream toward the source as if she could hear it coming. When Quentin crossed to her, she didn't turn around.

"You heard," Quentin said.

"I heard before your machine did." Her voice was dry as riverbed gravel. "The birds went quiet last night. Not hiding—*listening*. And the water in the pools has gone cold. Cold water comes from high country. It means the sky up there is already full."

"The forecast says three days."

Ama Sefu finally looked at them, and her eyes held the particular patience of someone who had watched outsiders trust the wrong things their whole life. "Your forecast measures the wind over the sea. I am telling you about the river. The river will not wait for your three days. When the landfall falls, the gorges will fill in hours. Hours, surveyor. Maybe less. The river does not read forecasts either."

Quentin looked upstream. Through the morning haze the gorge walls narrowed toward a bend they hadn't yet rounded, and beyond that bend, somewhere, was the spring beneath the limestone dome—the archive, whole and waiting, and Hale's crew somewhere between them and it with a stolen journal and better equipment.

Three days. Maybe less. Maybe much less.

*

They broke camp in twenty minutes, which was fifteen minutes longer than Ama Sefu thought acceptable. She stood at the bank while they loaded the boat, making small corrections to nothing, and when Marion asked her how far to the source she answered the way she answered most questions about distance.

"As far as it is."

"That's not an answer."

"It is the only honest one." Ama Sefu lowered herself into the bow with the dignity of someone boarding a throne. "The maps your grandfather drew, historian—" she nodded at Vess, who had told her about the 1961 expedition two nights ago, hunched over the recovered journal pages by firelight— "they end where the truth begins. Past the drowned town, past the narrows, the river stops being a place you travel and becomes a place you are permitted. We will see whether the river permits us."

Marion shot Quentin a look over the outboard motor—the look that said *she knows more than she's saying*—and Quentin returned the look that said *of course she does, she's known since the village, let it go.* Ten days ago those looks would have been arguments. Now they were almost a language. Almost.

They ran the river hard through the morning. The water was lower than it should have been—Quentin noticed it first, then couldn't stop noticing it, the way you notice a held breath. Every side channel was dry. Every sandbar sat exposed and pale. The tributaries that should have been feeding the main stem were trickling or silent.

"It's sucking back," Quentin shouted to Marion over the engine. "Everything upstream is holding. Like the whole watershed is inhaling."

Marion understood immediately; Quentin could see it land in her face. Thirty years of theory had made her fluent in the same grammar. "Pressure system. The storm offshore is pulling—"

"—pulling everything toward itself. Including the river." Quentin cut the throttle as the gorge bent northward and the current went strange, sluggish, the surface glassy in a way that made their skin crawl. A river should never look calm. Calm was what rivers did right before they lied to you.

They passed the drowned ferry town at midday. From the boat, with the water drawn down so far, the old dam works showed like bones—pilings, a rusted winch housing, the top course of the ferry landing wall breaking the surface. Marion turned her face to it and didn't speak, and Quentin let her have the silence. Some places you excavate. Some places excavate you.

By early afternoon the sky ahead had changed character. It wasn't darker exactly—it was *thicker*, stacked cloud building over the high country in towers that seemed to grow while you watched, purple-black at their bases, lit from inside by heat lightning that flickered without sound. The air had gone heavy enough to swim in.

Vess stood in the stern with the receiver pressed to her ear, hunting bands. At one point she caught a fragment—a port authority bulletin, a fishing cooperative advisory, something in rapid-fire official language—and translated the gist for the others: all traffic recalled, the upper gorge closed, anyone still above the third checkpoint was being written off until after the season.

"Written off," Marion repeated.

"That's what it says."

"So if we're up there when it hits—"

"Nobody's coming for us," Quentin finished. "Not for months."

Nobody said the obvious next part, which was that Hale's crew was also above the third checkpoint, also written off, also racing the same clock with fewer scruples about what happened to the people in their way.

*

They made camp that night in a slot canyon Ama Sefu chose without hesitation, forty feet above the current waterline, on a shelf of rock behind a natural baffle of fallen stone. When Quentin asked why there, she tapped the walls with her stick.

"My grandmother's people marked the flood heights here." She pointed, and Quentin raised the lantern, and saw them: grooves cut into the limestone, dozens of them, ascending the wall in a ragged ladder. Each groove bore a spiral beside it. "Every great flood, someone climbed up and cut the line. Look where they stop."

Quentin followed the marks upward. The highest ones were far above their heads—absurdly, impossibly far above, thirty meters up the canyon wall, near the lip of the gorge itself.

"How long ago was the highest?"

Ama Sefu considered. "Before my grandmother's grandmother. But the stories say the water has touched the rim twice in living memory of memory. Your instruments would call it a thousand-year event. My people call it Tuesday, eventually." She settled onto her bedroll. "Sleep high. Sleep light. Tomorrow we walk—the narrows will not take a boat, and after tomorrow, they may not take anything."

Quentin lay awake a long time, listening to the river run low and quiet below them, and found they weren't afraid of the storm. They were afraid of the arithmetic. Three days of travel compressed into two, maybe one and a half, with Meridian somewhere ahead cutting corners they wouldn't dare cut. The clock wasn't ticking anymore. It was running.

And underneath the fear, quieter but persistent, was the other thing—the thing they'd been carrying since the village, since Ama Sefu's price, since signing their name to a promise that whatever waited at the source belonged to someone else. Ten years Quentin had built a life out of not wanting things. It was easy to be free of ambition when you never reached for anything. But now they wanted to see the archive—not to own it, not to publish it, just to *see* it, one time, before the water took it back down—and the wanting felt dangerous, like the exposed tide flats, like the cave door, like every crack in the world where something true got in.

They slept, finally, and dreamed of rising water that parted politely around them, which was the kind of dream that meant nothing good.

*

Dawn came the color of wet slate, and with it came the sound.

At first Quentin thought it was thunder. Then they realized the thunder was inland, behind the cloud towers, continuous, a rolling drumroll that never resolved. And underneath it, closer, was a new sound in the river itself—a deep, rhythmic knocking, like something enormous turning over in its sleep downstream.

Ama Sefu was already standing at the canyon mouth, facing south, toward the sea they couldn't see.

"It has touched the coast," she said simply. "Landfall. You feel it in the ground before you hear it in the sky."

Vess's receiver confirmed it in fragments between bursts of static: landfall confirmed, category revised upward, the eye tracking directly up the river valley—as if the storm had studied the same maps everyone else had and decided to follow the road.

"The eye follows the gorge," Marion said quietly. "It'll funnel straight up the valley. That's not a storm anymore, that's a delivery system."

"Then we stop talking about weather." Quentin shouldered their pack and checked the guide rope coils, the carabiners, the spare line. "Two options. We push for the source today, fast, and hope we beat both the storm and Hale. Or we turn around and spend a year explaining to a salvage syndicate why we gave up."

Marion looked at them for a long moment. "You know that's not really two options."

"No," Quentin agreed. "It's one option wearing a disguise. Let's move."

They climbed out of the slot canyon into a world gone strange. The light was greenish and sourceless, filtering through cloud so thick it might have been dusk at eight in the morning. The air tasted metallic. Ahead, the gorge walls leaned together, and the narrows began—walls barely a boat's width apart at the waterline, polished smooth by ten thousand years of flash floods, marked at intervals with the Riverfolk's spiral signs cut high and desperate into the stone.

Ama Sefu led them along the ancient path above the narrows, a trail worn into the rock face by feet long vanished, and Quentin walked behind her and understood, finally, what the ledgers had been trying to say. This whole river was a document. The flood marks, the spirals, the trail itself—ten thousand years of a people writing *we were here, we measured, we remembered* into the only paper that would last.

Halfway through the narrows, the river spoke.

There was no mistaking it this time. A sound came down the gorge from behind them—south, downstream, toward the sea—a long, rising roar like a freight train entering a tunnel, and the water in the narrows below began to climb. Not gradually. In surges, each wave higher than the last, brown and muscular, carrying whole trees sideways.

"Pulse flood!" Quentin grabbed Vess's arm. "First surge off the headwater valleys—it's not even the main event, it's the *warning shot*! Higher ground, now!"

They scrambled. The trail gained altitude in switchbacks cut by hands centuries dead, and Quentin drove the others up them with a hand at each back, counting heads—Vess, Marion, Ama Sefu climbing with a terrifying sure-footedness, her stick striking sparks off the stone. Below them the narrows filled. In less than four minutes, the channel they'd have been floating through was thirty feet underwater and boiling.

When they reached the high shelf, all of them stood gasping, watching the river eat its own banks.

"That was hours early," Vess said hoarsely. "That was *days* early."

"The river keeps its own calendar." Ama Sefu didn't sound triumphant. She sounded sad, and certain. "One more day, perhaps two, and then the high country closes. Whatever happens at the source happens now."

Quentin looked upstream. Somewhere past that next bend, past the last checkpoints of law and legend, the dome waited over the spring, and beneath the dome, the memory of a civilization sat sealed in resin and stone—and somewhere between here and there, Corliss Hale was moving with the same deadline and none of the same reasons.

The storm rolled its drumroll overhead. The first fat drops hit the stone around them, huge and warm, spaced like artillery ranging in.

Quentin tightened the straps on their pack.

"One day," they said. "Then we run."
