## 16. The Forbidden Source

They climbed the last stretch in the dark, because Ama Sefu said the spring showed itself only to people who arrived before the sun did.

Quentin thought it was ceremony. Then they understood it was engineering.

The gorge narrowed all night until the walls pressed so close the boats had to be abandoned, hauled onto gravel shelves and lashed with rope that Vess had bought in Kesh-Taru from a woman who laughed at the price and doubled it anyway. After that there was only the riverbed itself, dry in this season, the stones rounded and pale as knuckles. Ama Sefu walked ahead with a storm lantern turned down to a coal, and the six of them—Quentin, Marion, Vess, two of the elder's grandnephews, and the silence everyone carried—picked their way up a channel that no map had ever bothered to name.

The monsoon had moved the schedule. Everyone knew it. Nobody said it. The radio at Kesh-Taru had crackled its warning two days ago—landfall in three, maybe less—and after that the words between them had gone short and practical. Water. Rope. Who carried what. Grief and history could wait; the river would not.

Quentin counted their own breathing. In through the nose, out through the mouth, the way you did when the dark was total and the footing was wet limestone and one of the people behind you was your mother, sixty-one years old, refusing all offers of a hand.

"Here," Ama Sefu said.

The lantern stopped. Quentin nearly walked into the elder's back, then looked up and forgot how to breathe.

The gorge ended. Not in a wall, not in a scree slope, but in a dome—a hemisphere of limestone rising out of the riverbed like the back of a drowned whale, a hundred feet across at least, its surface furred with mineral lace and threaded with veins of white calcite that caught the lantern's coal and held it. Water came out of it. That was the thing Quentin's surveyor's mind kept snagging on: the spring emerged not from a crack at the base but from the crown, a slow silver rope of it spilling down the curve of the dome and spreading across the stones in a fan so wide and so shallow it made no sound at all. The headwater. The beginning of everything they'd followed for nine days. It came out of the top of the world like the mountain was pouring it.

"It's a grave," Ama Sefu said quietly, in the way of someone repeating something they'd already said and expected not to be heard. "I told you."

"You said it was a grave," Marion said. Her voice had a crack in it Quentin had never heard before, and would think about later. "You didn't say it was *intact*."

She was already moving forward, and Quentin's whole body flinched before their brain caught up—twenty years of reflex, the child who knew what happened when Marion Kavanagh saw a site. But the elder's grandnephews had taken her elbows, gently, and steered her left, along a path in the fan of water that was clearly a path, worn smooth, deliberately maintained.

"Walk where we walk," Ama Sefu said. "Always, here. The floor remembers."

*The floor remembers.* Quentin filed it with everything else the old woman had said that had turned out to be instructions rather than poetry.

The path curved around the dome's flank and into shadow, and there—the lantern lifted—the entrance.

Not a cave mouth. A door. Or the idea of one: a slot in the limestone, tall and narrow, its edges dressed with a precision that made Quentin's professional pride sit up and take notice, because dressed stone this clean meant tools, meant workshops, meant a tradition of masonry sustained over generations. Above the lintel, cut deep and rubbed with something dark that had survived centuries of river damp, was the spiral.

Quentin had seen it twice now. Once on a stone door at the bottom of the sea, in a cave the tide had opened for a single impossible morning. Once on a pendant their mother had worn every day of Quentin's childhood until the day she'd taken it off, folded it into a handkerchief, and put it in a drawer, and neither of them had ever spoken about it again.

"Resin," Marion breathed. She had stopped at the threshold and was looking at the door's surface, at the amber sheen over the stone. "The whole face is sealed in resin. They *waterproofed* it—Quentin, look at the—"

"I see it."

"Three thousand years. Maybe older. And it's still—"

"Ama Sefu," Quentin said. "Please."

The elder smiled, or something like it, and produced from the folds of her coat a small object: a disc of river stone, bored through the center, its face carved with a spiral so like the lintel's that the two might have been cut the same week. A key, or a counterweight, or a credential. She pressed it into a recess beside the door, turned it a quarter-turn, and the slot exhaled.

That was the only word Quentin ever found for it. The door moved inward an inch and the air that came out was cool and dry and smelled faintly of cedar and something sweeter underneath, and it moved with a sound like a long-held breath finally let go.

* * *

Inside was a civilization.

Quentin had prepared themselves for chambers, for shelves, for the archive-as-library their imagination had been building since the tide went out that first morning. What they had not prepared for was scale, or tenderness, or the fact that the first thing the lantern light found was a child's handwriting.

The passage opened into a gallery that followed the curve of the dome—chambers within chambers, the limestone hollowed and faced, and every surface that could hold a record held one. Not carved walls here. Tablets. Thousands of them, thin as roof slates, stacked in cedar racks that stood in rows like the pews of a church, each tablet sleeved in a casing of resin poured and hardened until the writing floated inside it like a fly in amber. The racks rose twelve feet. The gallery ran back into the dark farther than the light would go.

And the tablets were *labeled*. Little tags of bone wired to each slot, incised with the same script Vess had spent three years learning to half-read from her grandfather's photographs. Vess was already at the first rack, hands hovering an inch from the resin, not touching, making a sound that Quentin at first took for a cough and then understood was her crying without deciding to.

"It's a census," she managed. "This row—it's a census. Names, births—Quentin, these are *households*. There are children's practice tablets, look, the same character written over and over, they were *teaching them to write*—"

"A whole people's memory," Marion said. She had gone very still in the way she went still before the important sentences, the lecture-hall stillness, and for once Quentin didn't resent it. "Not a treasury. Not a temple storehouse. They sealed their *records* here. Who they were. Who they married. What they owed each other. What they—" She stopped, and Quentin watched her read a tag, and watched thirty years of theory become fact behind her eyes. "What they promised."

"The flood came," Ama Sefu said. "The long water. They knew it would take the coast, the towns, the trade roads. So they carried everything that mattered up the river to the source and they sealed it where the water begins, because—" she said something in her own language, four syllables, and translated it without being asked— "*where the water is born, the water keeps it.*"

Quentin walked the rows slowly, hands in pockets, touching nothing. A decade of training in not being seen, and here was a people who had solved the opposite problem: how to be seen forever, by no one, until the right someone came. The resin caught the light and gave it back warm. A tablet at eye level held a column of figures and, at the bottom, in a different, hastier hand, a single extra line that even untranslated looked like a joke—like someone had added a note to a ledger the way you'd add *and the dog ate the invoice*—and Quentin felt their throat tighten over something that had no business being moving, a bookkeeper's marginalia three thousand years dead.

"They were people," they said, not meaning to say it aloud.

"Of course they were people," Marion said. But she said it gently, and when Quentin glanced at her she was looking at the child's practice tablets, not at Quentin, and her face had the expression of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like.

* * *

It was Vess who heard them first.

She'd drifted deep into the gallery, reading tags by lantern light, cataloguing out loud in a whisper—her grandfather's expedition had died somewhere on this river, in 1961, and Quentin had watched her understand, chamber by chamber, that he had gotten close, so close, maybe close enough to hear this door breathe—and then her whisper stopped, and she said, "Lights."

Quentin killed their lantern. The dark came down complete and total, and in it, far below, faint through the stone, came a sound they'd been listening for in their nightmares since the rapids: engines. More than one. And under the engines, thinner, a sound Quentin knew from the cave at the coast, from the chiseled walls and the broken jars and the fresh drill scars on the door Meridian hadn't managed to open.

Generators.

"They followed the riverbed," one of Ama Sefu's grandnephews whispered. He was young, and his voice was furious. "The boats. We heard nothing because the water makes no sound here."

Quentin's mind did the surveyor's math without being asked. The gorge below, the gravel shelf, the climb—two hours for them, exhausted, in the dark, moving carefully. Hale's crew had engines, winches, and the particular patience of men who were paid by results. Call it ninety minutes. And the door they'd come through was the only entrance, and it was open, and it breathed amber light out into the gorge like a lit window in a country where no one was supposed to live.

"Racks," Marion whispered. "There are side chambers—"

"No." Ama Sefu's voice in the dark was not loud and it did not need to be. "We do not hide in the grave. We stand in it. They must see what this is, and who it belongs to, and they must see us seeing it." A pause. "And Quentin."

Quentin found the old woman's hand on their arm in the black.

"When the man with the guns asks who has claim here," Ama Sefu said, "you will be the one to answer. Not your mother. Not the historian. You. You signed the paper at my village. Your name is the one the river knows."

Quentin wanted to say something—wanted to say *I'm not the one who should*, wanted to say *I've spent ten years making sure no one knows my name*—and instead heard themselves say, "All right."

The light in the doorway bloomed.

It came up fast—halogens, battery packs, the flat white glare of people who worked at night on purpose—and with it came silhouettes, four, then six, then a number Quentin stopped counting because counting didn't change anything. Rifles slung, not raised. Yet. And in front of them, unhurried, hands in the pockets of a coat too good for a river gorge, Corliss Hale walked into the archive like a man walking into a bank he already owned.

His light found the racks. The resin. The rows of amber tablets going back into the dark. Quentin watched his face do something complicated—want, mostly, want so large it briefly broke through the professionalism—and then watched him put it away, because he was very good at his job.

"Well," Hale said. His voice echoed off the dome and came back down changed. "Nine days up a river that doesn't want visitors, and it turns out the rumors undersold it."

He turned. His light found Quentin first, because Quentin was closest to the door, because Ama Sefu had put them there.

"Kavanagh," he said, and smiled. "You didn't take the money. I respect that. It makes this simpler, actually."

Behind him, at the threshold, one of his men unslung a rifle and rested it across his forearms, casual as a farmer leaning on a fence, and looked up—past the racks, past the gallery, past all of it—to the crown of the dome, where the spring came through the limestone, and began, very quietly, to study it.
