## 17. Standoff at the Spring

The dome was singing.

That's what it sounded like, anyway—the headwater spring pushing up through the limestone floor with a low, continuous note, like a wet finger circling the rim of a glass. Quentin had been listening to it for an hour without realizing. The chamber beneath the dome was bigger than the sea cave had ever hinted at: a cathedral of pale stone ribbed with veins of calcite, and along every wall, tier upon tier of resin-sealed niches, each one holding tablets stacked like loaves in a baker's window. Thousands of them. A civilization's memory, filed by hands that had been dust for six hundred years.

Dawn light came through a fissure near the dome's crown, thin and gray, catching the spring where it welled up in a pool so clear it looked solid. Ama Sefu had knelt at its edge when they arrived and touched the water to her forehead, her lips, her chest. She hadn't spoken since. Vess stood among the niches with her grandfather's journal open against her forearm, turning in slow circles, tears running freely down her face and no interest whatsoever in wiping them away. Marion had not moved from the central aisle. She stood with her hands clasped behind her back—the posture she'd used in every lecture hall Quentin had ever sneaked into—and stared upward at the tiers as if reading them from across the room.

Quentin should have been watching the tunnel.

They knew that. They'd known it since the night before, when Ama Sefu's grandson had run ahead to warn them that strangers with machines were two valleys back. But the archive did something to vigilance. It dissolved it. You walked into a room where an entire people had decided to remember themselves forever, and your own small fears felt like litter to be swept out.

So when the voices came echoing up the approach tunnel—boots on stone, the clank of gear, Hale's voice pitched in that reasonable baritone he used for threats—it landed on all four of them like a dropped ceiling.

"Positions," Marion said quietly, and it was strange, Quentin thought, how thirty years of field command could still live in a woman's voice even after the field had let her go.

There was nowhere good to be. The chamber was a single open bowl; the only cover was the tiers themselves, and hiding among them would mean putting their backs to the enemy. Quentin made the calculation fast and hated every part of it: stand in the open, center floor, visible. Let him see you're not afraid before you decide whether you are.

Hale came up out of the tunnel mouth into the gray light with six men behind him and stopped, and for one unguarded second, Corliss Hale forgot how to be a fixer. His eyes went around the chamber the way Vess's had, the way Quentin's had, the way anyone's would. Even men who come to take things are briefly capable of awe. Then he closed it off, and the moment passed, and Quentin understood that this was the most dangerous thing about him—not cruelty, but the speed with which he could put wonder away.

"There it is," Hale said softly. "The source." He looked at Quentin. "You know, I half expected you to lie to me again."

"I did lie to you again," Quentin said. "I told you we were going to the ferry town."

"You told me the river bends east above the falls." Hale smiled. "It does. Everything I said was true too. That's usually how lying works between professionals." He gestured, and two of his men began moving along the wall with handheld scanners, pinging the niches. "Meridian will want a full inventory before extraction. We have maybe sixty hours before landfall makes this whole chamber a swimming pool, so I'd appreciate everyone's cooperation."

"You don't have a legal claim to any of this," Vess said. She'd stepped forward, journal still open, voice steady in the way of someone who has rehearsed being brave in smaller rooms her whole life. "This site sits within the Kesh-Taru customary territory. Under the 2019 heritage accord, subsurface finds revert to recognized descendant communities. I've documented provenance continuously since Sallow's Reach. There are filings. There is a paper trail, Mr. Hale, and it leads directly to a court that will eat you alive."

Hale considered her with something almost like respect. "Ms. Odumbe. Your grandfather's expedition was unlicensed, unfunded, and vanished. You think the ministry that lost his paperwork for sixty years is going to ride to yours?" He turned back to Quentin. "But by all means, keep the lawyers coming. It passes the time."

Marion moved then. Not toward Hale—toward the nearest niche. She reached up, unhurried, and lifted down a tablet with both hands, the way you'd lift an infant, and held it to the fissure light.

"Do you know what resin sealing is?" she said, conversational, terrible. "Of course you do; your scanners told you. What they can't tell you is that Riverfolk resin is a living lacquer—tree sap cut with mineral oil and cured over years. It stays microscopically permeable. These tablets have been breathing for six centuries, Mr. Hale. Slowly drying. Equilibrated. If you move them—fast, warm, into sealed crates, down to a humid coast—they will fracture. Not some of them. All of them. Every word your syndicate just paid to steal will flake into powder inside a month." She set the tablet back with a click that echoed. "I've spent thirty years proving this archive exists. Do you imagine I'd help you destroy it? Or that I'm the only one here who knows enough to make sure you do?"

It was a stall dressed as scholarship, and everyone knew it, and it worked anyway, because Hale's eyes flicked to his scanner man, and the man shrugged: *maybe*.

"Ama Sefu," Hale said, pivoting, spreading his hands. "Ma'am. I'll say plainly what these others won't. Meridian will fund Kesh-Taru for a generation. Schools, clinic, flood defenses. All I need is acknowledgment that the material is surrendered voluntarily. A signature. Yours would carry weight."

Ama Sefu looked at him for a long time. When she spoke, her voice was dry as the old ledgers.

"My grandmother's grandmother carried these words up from the drowning," she said. "She did not sign them away. She hid them. That was her price, and her gift, and her grief. You offer me schools built on the bones of her hiding place." She shook her head once. "No. I am not witness to your sale. I am witness *against* it. And the river knows my voice."

Something moved through Hale's crew then—unease, the superstitious kind, the kind money doesn't fix. Quentin filed it. Ammunition.

"Hale," Quentin said. "Listen to me. The chamber floods from below. Look at the spring." They pointed at the pool, which had risen—hadn't it?—a finger's width since dawn. "When the monsoon hits, the water table drives straight up through that dome. Your extraction window isn't sixty hours. It might be twelve. You cannot win this by standing here negotiating."

"No," Hale agreed pleasantly. "Which is why I'm not negotiating." He nodded at the far end of his line. "Ruz."

The explosives man stepped forward.

He was a small, neat man with a rucksack and the calm of someone who has done one job many times, and he didn't go for the archive at all. He went for the base of the dome.

Quentin understood in a single cold flash what Hale had known all along: the tablets were negotiable, breakable, contestable. The dome was none of those things. Blast the fissure at the crown wide, drop charges at the spring's throat, and the monsoon becomes a tool instead of a deadline—a controlled release, a dewatering operation, Meridian pumping the source dry with permits forged in three jurisdictions. The archive would survive as rubble in a quarry pit. Ownership would survive as fait accompli.

"No—" Vess started forward.

"Stop," Hale said, and two rifles came up, not aimed, just *present*, and everyone stopped, because that's what rifles are for.

Ruz knelt at the dome's base and opened his rucksack with the tenderness of a man unpacking eggs.

Quentin's mind went very quiet and very fast.

There was no play here. No stall left. Marion's scholarship was spent, Vess's law was spent, Ama Sefu's witness had raised doubt but not doubt enough. In perhaps ninety seconds Ruz would set his first charge, and after that every outcome ended with the dome breached.

Except the one nobody was looking at.

Because Quentin had spent eleven days coming up this river, and Hale had spent eleven days following them, and there was one difference between them, and it wasn't guns or money or nerve. It was that Quentin had read the ledgers. All of them. Including the ones about the dome—including the passage Vess had translated on the gorge road, half-asleep, laughing at the absurdity of it: *the mountain door answers only to the hand that knows the falling stone.* The Riverfolk had built this chamber to be sealable. From inside. There was a gallery—a ledge, a mechanism, a counterweighted plug of limestone the size of a house—somewhere above the tunnel mouth, designed to drop and seal the approach against exactly this: raiders, flood, empire. The engineers of a drowned people had foreseen Corliss Hale six hundred years early.

The problem was simple and enormous: the mechanism was up there, past the tiers, reachable only by a climb Quentin had eyeballed once and hated. And the drop would seal the tunnel—with everyone inside. Both sides. All of them, together, with a rising spring and a storm coming and one uncertain sump out, described in glyphs Ama Sefu could read and Quentin could barely parse.

Irreversible. That was the word. Every other option kept doors open; this one closed the only big one, on friend and enemy alike, on Marion, on Vess, on six armed men who would then be trapped animals.

Quentin looked at Marion. Marion, impossibly, was already looking at them—had been, Quentin realized, since Ruz knelt. And she gave the smallest nod. Not encouragement. Authorization. The same nod she'd given dig crews for thirty years: *I've assessed it. Proceed.*

Fine, Quentin thought. One inheritance I'll take.

They moved.

Not toward the tunnel—toward the tiers, left-hand side, walking fast and casual, and Hale called out, "Kavanagh, where are you—" and Quentin answered truthfully, because the best lies were true: "Checking the water line!" and put the first tier between themselves and the rifles and started climbing.

The tiers were built as shelves, but the Riverfolk had also built them as stairs—of course they had; everything here was two things at once—and Quentin went up them three at a time, fingers finding carved holds worn smooth by centuries of maintenance hands. Behind them the chamber erupted: Hale shouting, boots, Vess's voice rising in furious legalese as pure cover, God bless her, buying seconds with citations. Quentin climbed into the fissure light, onto the gallery ledge, and there it was.

A capstan. Black stone, waist-high, grooved for rope long since rotted, connected to a chain that ran into a slot in the wall and vanished. Beside it, the glyph: the spiral, and beneath the spiral, the falling-stone mark. And beside *that*, a warning glyph Ama Sefu had taught them on the gorge road, the one that meant *do not turn back*, meaning both directions of the phrase.

The chain was stone. Not metal—fused stone links, flexible the way the resin was living, impossible, six hundred years and still holding. Quentin gripped the capstan. It was sized for two people. It would not move for one. It was not going to matter.

Below, Ruz stood, first charge in hand, adhesive already mixed.

Quentin threw their whole body against the capstan.

Nothing. Tendons screaming. Nothing. Below, Hale had spotted them, was pointing, shouting words that got lost under the spring's rising note—and then other words arrived, deeper, from everywhere: a crack, a groan, the sound the dome made when the mountain shifted its weight, and whether it was the monsoon's pressure or the capstan's age or plain luck, the chain took up slack with a shudder, and the counterweight began to fall.

The plug came down like a verdict.

It filled the tunnel mouth entirely—a slab of raw limestone taller than two men, dropping on guides with a grinding boom that Quentin felt in their teeth, in their sternum, in the floor. Dust went up in a wall. Somewhere below it, muffled, a rifle discharged once, wildly, and then nothing, and then the sound of six men and three allies understanding at the same moment that the geography of their lives had just changed.

The echo died. The spring sang on.

Quentin stayed crouched at the capstan, breathing hard, hands bleeding lightly where the grooves had bitten, looking down at the chamber: the tiers, the pool, the small figures staring up—Vess white-faced, Ama Sefu serene, Marion with her chin lifted, and Hale, motionless, doing arithmetic.

Everyone inside. Everyone together. The way out a submerged sump only the dead had mapped.

"Well," Quentin said, to no one, to the mountain, to their mother's nod. "Now we swim."

Below, the spring rose another finger's width—and outside, faint through six hundred years of stone, the first rain of the landfall began to fall.
