## 19. What Quentin Carries Out

The sump was a throat of black water at the back of the third chamber, and the glyphs above it said what Ama Sefu had been saying all morning in her low, unhurried voice: *the river goes down to come up again.*

Quentin floated on their stomach at the lip of it, regulator hissing, headlamp beam swallowed three feet into the murmur of the spring-fed dark. Behind them, the archive breathed—resin-sealed chambers stacked like honeycomb along the dome's inner curve, ten thousand tablets sleeping in their wax and cedar, a civilization's whole memory pressed into stone that had kept it dry for six hundred years.

And rising. The storm had found them even here. Water that had lapped at Quentin's boots when they'd first entered now climbed the lowest shelf of tablets, an inch an hour, maybe more. The monsoon was still two days from landfall by the radio's guess, but the river had stopped listening to forecasts somewhere up in the drowned country. It was coming home early, and it wanted its archive back.

*One dive,* Vess had said, chalk marks on her slate, hands shaking only a little. *Guide line through the sump, tie off on the far side, confirm it opens into the air channel the glyphs describe. Then we ferry them out in relays until the water wins.*

*How long do we have?*

Ama Sefu had answered that one, watching the water climb with an expression that wasn't fear so much as recognition. *The river does not negotiate.*

So: one dive first. Quentin spat into their mask, rinsed it, settled it against their face. Marion stood at the chamber's edge with her arms crossed—not blocking, never blocking anymore, just present—and said nothing, which between the two of them had become its own kind of language.

Quentin went under.

* * *

The sump was eleven meters of narrowing limestone, cold enough to ache through the wetsuit, black enough that the lamp showed nothing but suspended silt turning slowly like weather. Quentin moved by feel and by the line paying out behind them, elbow brushing stone on the left, counting kicks the way they'd taught themselves in survey work a decade ago—counting being the thing you did instead of panicking.

At meter seven the ceiling dipped and for four body-lengths there was no room to turn around, no room to think about turning around, only the next kick and the next, the regulator rasping, the dark pressing on the mask like a thumb. Quentin's heart slammed. They counted anyway. Nine. Ten. Eleven—

—and the ceiling opened like a hand unclenching, and they rose into air.

Not open air. A chimney, a vertical slot breathing with its own slow wind, and above it, impossibly far, a gray thread of daylight where the dome's shell thinned toward the surface world. But the water here was thigh-deep over a gravel shoal, and the air tasted of stone and rain, and it was *air*, and Quentin hung onto a rock knob and laughed once, a short ugly bark of relief that echoed up the chimney and came back sounding like someone else.

They tied off the line, set a second marker, checked the depth gauge. Eleven minutes down. Enough to look around. Not enough to want anything.

That was the thought that followed them back through the sump, hand over hand along the line, silt blooming: *not enough to want anything.* Because they had seen, in the thirty seconds of lamplight before surfacing, what lay on the far shoal. Tablets. Dozens of them, loose, unsealed—the working records, the ones the Riverfolk must have carried back and forth through this very passage, left where a swimmer could reach them. And among them, if the ledgers' index glyphs meant what Marion believed they meant, the master ledger itself. The root record. Every name, every trade, every treaty, every flood-year of a vanished people, in one place.

The proof. The fame. The end of being nobody.

It sat in Quentin's chest on the swim back like a second regulator, something breathing inside the breathing.

* * *

They surfaced in the third chamber to faces. Vess first, hungry for the report; Ama Sefu, unreadable; Marion, whose eyes did the thing they'd always done—measured, catalogued, loved in a dialect made entirely of data.

"It opens," Quentin said, pulling the mouthpiece. "Air channel, gravel shoal, room to work. We can move them."

Vess was already chalking a relay schedule on her slate. Marion was already talking load limits and resin-seal handling, voice clipped, competent, alive in the way she only got near a problem. Ama Sefu said nothing. She was looking at Quentin the way she'd looked at the rising water.

Later—there was no later, there was only the work, but later in the way that hours compress—Quentin went back through with the first relay line, and then again, and the archive began its migration: tablet by tablet, wrapped in waxed canvas, passed hand to hand through eleven meters of black water to the gravel shoal and up the chimney to the light. Marion worked the packing. Vess logged everything, provenance in real time, her grandfather's expedition finally getting its ending written properly. Hale's crew was sealed on the wrong side of Quentin's collapse, shouting through forty meters of rubble some of the time and silent the rest, and the silence was worse, because silence meant they were finding another way, or waiting, or both.

On the fourth run, alone, Quentin surfaced on the far shoal with empty hands and the master ledger eight feet away.

It lay on a flat stone apart from the others, as if placed. Cedar case gone soft with age, wax seal cracked but whole, and across the lid the spiral sigil—the same spiral that had been on the cave door, the same spiral that had hung against their mother's collarbone for Quentin's entire childhood, the pendant they'd once grabbed at age nine and been slapped for touching, though not hard, though more startled than angry, though Quentin had spent twenty years deciding which version of that memory was true.

Eight feet. They swam the eight feet without deciding to.

Up close, the ledger was smaller than legend. That was the joke of it, the joke of every famous thing Quentin had ever stood beside: reduced to human scale the moment you got your hands on it. It weighed perhaps four kilos. It would fit in a survey pack. It would fit in a life, easily—a career, a name, a chair at an institute with Kavanagh carved over the door. One dive out. Nobody would know. Vess hadn't seen the far shoal yet; Marion was on the other side of the sump; Ama Sefu was wherever Ama Sefu was, and the old woman's price had been clear—*what is found belongs to the heirs*—but prices were words, and words were negotiable, and hadn't Quentin learned that lesson twice over from people with better lawyers?

Their hands were on the case. Their breath echoed off the chimney walls.

Here was the arithmetic they'd been doing their whole life without writing it down: be somebody, and somebody gets to hurt you. Be nobody, and nobody asks your name. The ledger was the opposite of nobody. The ledger was Quentin Kavanagh, discoverer, in letters a meter tall, forever. Their mother's theory completed, their mother's name restored, their mother's—

No.

Not their mother's either. That was the trap, the second door behind the first. Take it for yourself and you were still doing it *at* her, the whole rescue still a duel. Give it to a museum and you were doing it to Ama Sefu. There was no version of taking this thing out of the ground that didn't make it a weapon in somebody's hand, and Quentin was so tired of holding weapons.

The water was climbing. They could see it now, a wet seam creeping up the shoal's stones since their last pass, the river keeping its own schedule, patient as arithmetic.

Quentin knelt in the shallows and opened the case.

Inside, the master ledger was exactly what the ledgers at the coast had promised: dense, beautiful, columns of glyphs marching in resin-preserved ink, the whole grammar of a people who had measured their lives in river-heights and tide-tables and debts honestly kept. Quentin read the opening lines the way Marion had taught them to read, slowly, aloud to the empty air, and understood maybe one glyph in fifty, and understood the shape of it completely. It was not a treasure. It was a household. Someone had written *here is how we kept the water fair*, and beneath it, in a different hand, generations later, *here is how we kept it fair again after the bad years.* Stewardship, entry after entry. Inheritance as a job you did, not a thing you owned.

A child could have carried it. A child had probably once been taught to carry it.

Quentin closed the case.

Then they turned to the loose tablets scattered along the shoal's edge—the working records, the everyday ones—and began, deliberately, to look through them. Not for value. For names. For lineage-marks. Ama Sefu had spoken hers often enough in the last days, tracing her descent the way riverfolk traced anything: downstream, generation by generation. Quentin matched the glyph patterns to memory, discarding tablet after tablet, until they found a small one, palm-sized, its edges worn round by small hands. A practice piece. A child's recording tablet, the kind a student would use to copy their first trade-entry, their first flood-observation, their first name. The glyphs on it were clumsy, overwritten, corrected in a second childish hand.

It belonged to a lineage. The lineage belonged to Ama Sefu. And it was small enough to matter and small enough to carry, and it proved nothing except that a child who lived six hundred years ago had once tried hard to write neatly, and been allowed to try again.

Quentin wrapped it in waxed canvas, tucked it inside their suit against the cold, and left the master ledger on its stone, squared to the others, sealed case down against the rising water for whatever protection that offered.

They took one photograph. Just one—of the ledger where it lay, in situ, untouched—for Vess's provenance record, so that no one could ever say it had been taken and lost, or taken and hidden. Then they put the camera away, found the guide line, and dove.

* * *

Marion met them at the chamber's edge, reaching for the canvas bundle, and Quentin watched her hands stop mid-reach when she saw the size of it.

"That's not—" she started.

"No," Quentin said.

"Quentin." Her voice did the thing it did when she was assembling an argument, the fieldwork voice, the voice that had won her every grant and lost her every family dinner. "The master ledger is the keystone. Without it the collection is fragments. With it—" She caught herself. Quentin watched her catch herself, watched thirty years of instinct collide with eleven days of Ama Sefu's price, and lose, barely. "With it, the descendants hold something no institution can argue with. I know. I *know*. I'm telling you why it hurts, not why you're wrong."

Quentin unwrapped the child's tablet and put it in her hands.

Marion looked at it for a long time. The clumsy glyphs. The corrections. Whatever she saw in it—and she saw more than most, that had always been the truth under everything, she saw more than anyone and had never once figured out how to say so—moved through her face like weather.

"A practice piece," she said finally.

"Somebody's first try at writing their name."

Marion's thumb moved over the worn edge. "Mine kept a journal at that age," she said, to no one, to the tablet. "I burned it at nineteen. Thought it was embarrassing." She handed it back with both hands, formally, the way you return something borrowed. "This is the right one to carry out. If you're going to carry out one thing."

"I'm carrying out all of them," Quentin said. "Or the river keeps them. Those are the options."

She nodded, and turned back to the packing, and that was the whole conversation, and it was the closest they had come in twenty years to saying the actual thing, and Quentin found they could live on that. It was not absolution. It was better. It was a shared reading of a text, which in their family was love.

* * *

They worked until the water took the choice away.

Relay after relay, the chimney echoing with rope-creak and Vess's count and Ama Sefu's low recitation of the glyphs that told them which chambers could be safely emptied and which had to be left—*the grave-shelves stay*, she said, and no one argued, because by now they all understood the source was a grave wearing a vault's clothes. The water climbed past the first chamber floor. Past the second. Hale's crew had gone quiet on the far side of the collapse, and the quiet had a direction to it, and everyone pretended it didn't.

On the last pass, the sump's entrance was already underwater, and Quentin went through it as a diver proper for the first time—no air pocket at meter seven, just stone and line and count—and came up the chimney into a shoal shrinking to the size of a table, rain beginning to fall in fat drops through the thread of daylight above.

The last tablets went into the last canvas. The master ledger Quentin left where it lay, on its high stone, case down, and watched the first clean sheet of river water slide across the shoal toward it like a hand drawing a cover over a sleeping face.

Ama Sefu was already at the top of the chimney when Quentin climbed out, hauling the line up behind them, coiling it with hands that shook now that there was nothing left to do with them. The old woman looked down into the closing well of the spring for a long moment.

"You left the big one," she said.

"It was never mine to move."

"No." Ama Sefu turned, and her eyes went to the small bundle against Quentin's chest, and something in her face eased that Quentin had not seen ease in eleven days of gorges and rapids and gunmetal men. "You took the right one, though."

"How do you know?"

"Because my grandmother's grandmother's grandmother wrote that tablet," Ama Sefu said. "And because you are the first outsider in six hundred years to leave the gold and carry the homework." She almost smiled. "The river will remember which one you chose. The river remembers everything. That is rather the point of it."

Below them, the spring swallowed the shoal, and the chimney filled, and the archive went dark under brown water—gone, intact, entrusted, exactly as it had been for six centuries before any of them arrived. Somewhere beyond the dome, muffled by stone and storm, a sound carried down: engines. More than one. Coming up the gorge road through the rising river, toward a source that no longer held anything worth seizing and a team that had nothing left to steal except a child's homework and each other.

Vess appeared at the rim, slate under her arm, hair plastered flat. "Landfall's moved up again," she said. "Radio says tomorrow night. And Quentin—" she pointed downriver, where the gray sky over the gorge had begun to organize itself into something with intent, "—that's not the radio talking anymore. That's the actual sky."

Quentin shouldered the pack with the tablet in it, and looked at the water where the archive slept, and felt lighter than they had in twenty years, and terrified, and ready.

"Then we go down to meet it," they said.
