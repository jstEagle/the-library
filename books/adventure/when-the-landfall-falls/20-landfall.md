## 20. Landfall

The river told them first.

Quentin felt it through the hull before they heard it—a low vibration in the aluminum, like something enormous turning over in its sleep far upstream. Ama Sefu had been right about that too. The forecasts said landfall was still a day out, but the river didn't read forecasts. The river read the mountains, and the mountains had already made their decision.

"Off the water," Ama Sefu said. "Now."

They ran the boat into the last sheltered eddy below the final gorge, where the cliff wall leaned out over the current like a brow over an eye, and hauled everything up a goat track that Vess had spotted on the way down—Vess, who hadn't let go of the recovered journal since the sump, who carried it now strapped to her chest like a child. Quentin took the stern line. Marion took the bow. Nobody spoke about how slow Marion's hands had gotten since the rockslide, or how she'd stopped pretending otherwise somewhere around the third day of the descent.

The rain came while they were still climbing.

Not gradually. Not the way rain was supposed to arrive, building from a spatter to a drum. It came down all at once, a gray curtain dropped across the gorge, and the temperature fell ten degrees in a minute, and Quentin's shirt went from dry to plastered to useless in the space of three breaths. Above them the cliffs vanished. Below them the river changed its voice—from the long steady roar they'd lived inside for two weeks to something higher, thinner, almost panicked.

"That's not rainwater," Marion shouted, close enough that her mouth was at Quentin's ear. "That's the source letting go. The dome—" She didn't finish it. She didn't have to. They'd sealed themselves in with the spring, blown the approach tunnel, and then climbed out through the Riverfolk's sump with the archive on their backs, and every hour since, Quentin had been doing arithmetic about water pressure against collapsed limestone. The math had never been good. Now the mountain was showing its work.

They reached the shelf near the top of the gorge as full dark came down—or what passed for dark under a sky strobing with sheet lightning. There was an overhang there, old flood debris packed beneath it, and Ama Sefu moved through the blackness with the unhurried certainty of someone who had been coming to this country since before she could name it. She got a fire going in a hollow of stone where the smoke would crawl out sideways instead of rising. She fed them dried fish and a paste of pounded grain that tasted like nothing and felt like everything. Then she sat with her back to the rock, wrapped in her oilcloth, and closed her eyes as if the end of the world were a scheduled event she'd planned around.

Quentin couldn't sit.

They stood at the lip of the overhang instead, watching the valley perform its own erasure. The river had come up six meters in an hour. Seven. The rapids they'd run four days ago—the ones where Hale's crew had boarded them, taken the journal, taken the supplies, taken Vess's dignity and Quentin's last good lie—were gone. Not hidden. Gone. In their place was a single brown muscle flexing from wall to wall, carrying whole trees end-over-end, carrying what might have been a roof, carrying the unmistakable pale shapes of the supply drums Meridian had cached on the low bank, bobbing once and swallowed.

Hale's people had been down there when the river turned.

Quentin thought about Corliss Hale's face in the lightning flashes of that morning—two days ago? Three? Time had gone soft since the spring—and the particular calm he'd worn like a tailored coat even while his expedition came apart around him. *Bring me the source and everyone walks away.* And Quentin had looked him in the eye and given him coordinates that were true and useless: the drowned ferry town, the buried channel, a route that only existed on a map fifty years out of date. Hale had believed it because he needed to believe it, because a man who'd spent nine figures chasing a legend could not afford to believe a nineteen-year-old survey tech had out-read him. He'd taken his crew up the dead channel with the storm clock running.

And the storm had caught them there, in a canyon with one exit, under a dam that hadn't held water since before Quentin was born but apparently still knew how to fail.

"You're watching for bodies," Marion said. She'd come up beside Quentin without a sound, which should have been impossible for a woman her age on wet scree, and yet here she was, exactly where Quentin's mother always was: at your shoulder, seeing straight through you.

"I'm watching the river."

"You're watching for bodies." Lightning lit her face, and in the flash Quentin saw that she wasn't accusing. She was asking. Genuinely asking, the way she used to ask about sediment cores, back when asking was the only language they'd shared. "I need to know which answer you want. Because I've watched you for twenty years, Quentin, and you've never once wanted the easy thing, and I can't tell if this is mercy or arithmetic."

Quentin watched another tree cartwheel past, roots first, like a drowning thing reaching.

"Both," they said finally. "Is that terrible?"

"It's honest." Marion pulled her jacket tighter. "Hale knew the risk. He read the same gauges we did. He chose the dead channel because it was fast, and fast was worth more to him than safe." A pause. Rain filled it. "That's not a eulogy. That's just the ledger."

*The ledger.* Everything came back to ledgers now. The carved walls in the sea cave. The master record at the source, left behind in its resin-sealed chamber, sinking by degrees into the rising spring. The child's tablet riding dry in Quentin's pack, the one thing they'd carried out for someone else. Somewhere in the last month Quentin had stopped thinking of inheritance as a weight and started thinking of it as a relay, and the difference between those two things was the difference between drowning and swimming.

"He'll live," Quentin said. "Hale. Men like him float. They're mostly blubber and grievance." They heard Marion laugh—one short, surprised note, quickly smothered, the laugh of a woman remembering she wasn't supposed to find her child funny anymore. "But his claim drowns with the equipment. Every core sample, every photogrammetry rig, every drone—he logged all of it under Meridian's survey permits, filed against coordinates in the dead channel. When the ministry comes to assess the damage, the paper trail ends underwater. Vess will bury him in provenance law before the water even recedes."

"And us?"

"We filed nothing," Quentin said. "We were never there. Officially the source is unrecorded, unreachable, and—after tonight—inaccessible. Which is exactly how Ama Sefu's people want it written. Or not written."

Marion was quiet for a long time. Below them the river ate another meter of bank with a sound like tearing cloth.

"Thirty years," she said at last. "I built my whole career toward that chamber. The theory, the grants, the fights with the ministry over access permits. I had the publication drafted in my head before you were born—I used to compose paragraphs during your school recitals." She said it without flinching, which was its own kind of confession; the flinching had happened in the drowned ferry town, days ago, in the hospital-room story, and there was no flinch left to spend. "And now the entire archive is dissolving behind us and I feel—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "Lighter. I feel lighter, and I don't know what that makes me."

"It makes you someone the Riverfolk trusted," Quentin said. "Ama Sefu doesn't hand her grandmother's glyphs to people who are going to steal. You read them aloud. You kept the count. You're the reason the sump line went in straight." They hesitated, then said the harder thing, because the ferry town had taught them both that the hard thing said now cost less than the hard thing said never. "You could have grabbed the master ledger on the last dive. You were closest. Nobody would have known."

"I would have known." Marion looked out at the brown chaos. "That's the disease, Quentin. Knowing. You get it from me. I'm sorry about that part. Of all the parts, I'm sorry about that one most."

Quentin didn't answer, because there was no answer that wouldn't either reopen the wound or falsely close it, and they had spent three weeks learning that some things between them were load-bearing walls you walked around, not through. Instead they stood together at the edge of the overhang, mother and child, and watched the valley disappear.

* * *

The storm owned the night. Around two in the morning a section of cliff across the gorge let go with a crack like the sky splitting, and the whole overhang shuddered, and Vess woke screaming a word in her grandfather's language, and for ten minutes the four of them pressed against the inner wall listening to the mountain rearrange itself. Afterward nobody slept. They sat in the firelight and drank tea Ama Sefu brewed from needles she stripped off a stunted pine growing out of the scree, and Vess, hands still shaking, opened the journal to a dry page and began, for no reason anyone needed, to read.

Her grandfather's handwriting was small and slanted, a surveyor's hand, economical even in fear. The entries from 1961 were water-spotted and half of them trailed off mid-sentence, but the last legible one was dated eleven days before the expedition's radio went silent, and it described the headwater spring in words so close to what Quentin had seen with their own eyes that the hair rose on their arms.

*The dome is real. The light through the water is green, like being inside a bottle of the river itself. I understand now why they sealed it. Not to hide it. To keep it company. A people who write everything down must have known that writing is a kind of leaving, and they wanted to leave gently.*

Vess stopped reading. Her thumb stayed on the page.

"He got this far," she said. "He stood where we stood. And then—" She shrugged, and the shrug contained sixty years. "The journal was found downstream. Just the journal. My grandmother never said the word 'widow' in the house. We said 'the year Papa went upriver.'"

"The flood of '61," Ama Sefu said. She had not opened her eyes. "My mother's generation. The river took the low villages. It took strangers too, sometimes. The river does not check papers." She opened her eyes then and looked at Vess, and something passed between them that Quentin recognized and deliberately did not examine: the look of two people from the same long story, one who'd left it and one who'd stayed. "Your grandfather reached the spring. I believe it. The glyphs say visitors came twice in the old times, and both times the river rose after, and both times the visitors left the records and went home. He may have been trying to do the third thing correctly."

"Did he?" Vess asked. "Do you think he did it correctly?"

Ama Sefu considered this with the gravity she brought to everything, as though each question were a stone to be turned over and inspected on all sides.

"The journal came back down the river," she said. "That is more than most men manage. Yes. I think he did it correctly."

Vess closed the book and held it against her chest again, and turned her face away toward the storm, and Quentin looked at the fire and gave her the privacy of not being watched.

* * *

Dawn came the color of dishwater and showed them a new world.

Or rather, showed them no world at all where the old one had been. The valley floor was gone. The river had spread until it touched both gorge walls, a single brown sea moving with the slow irresistible confidence of something that had all the time in geology. The rapids were a memory. The banks were a memory. Halfway down the drowned reach, the roof of the old ferry customs house—the one they'd camped in on the way up, the one with the 1950s ministry stamp still legible on its lintel—broke the surface like a whale's back, tilted, slid under, and did not come back.

Quentin counted heads without meaning to. Four on the shelf. Downstream, snagged on a gravel bar that was itself shrinking by the minute, a cluster of orange: Meridian's dry bags, maybe forty thousand dollars of technical gear, abandoned or lost. No boats. No people. Either Hale's crew had gotten out ahead of the rise—which meant they'd abandoned the gear and run, which meant Hale had finally chosen safe over fast—or they hadn't, and the river was keeping its own counsel on the matter.

"Hale will spin it," Marion said, reading Quentin's thoughts the way she always had, the family curse working in both directions. "Failed expedition, force majeure, tragic loss of equipment. Insurance will pay. But the claim—" She shook her head with something close to satisfaction. "You can't assert prior discovery of a site you can't locate, on a permit filed against coordinates that are now forty feet underwater, using instruments at the bottom of a gorge. By the time the water drops, the legal window closes. Vess will make sure of it. And the ministry will reclassify the whole headwater catchment as culturally protected within the year, because after this flood, nobody sane wants to argue with the river."

"Ama Sefu's people have argued with the river for four hundred years," Quentin said. "They'll argue with the ministry fine."

"Yes," Marion said. "They will. And they'll win, and it will take them fifteen years, and none of us will be famous for any of it." She glanced sideways at Quentin. "How does that sit?"

Quentin thought about it honestly, because the woman deserved honesty after the ferry town, after the dive, after thirty years of composing paragraphs during recitals.

"Like a door left as found," they said.

Marion looked at them for a moment, and whatever she saw made her nod slowly and turn back to the water, and neither of them said anything else, because the light was changing and the river was still rising and there would be years ahead for the rest of it—if there were years. On a shelf of rock above a drowned valley, in the first gray light after landfall, that was enough.

Below them, the last visible corner of the old world—the customs house roof, stubborn, absurd—finally went under. The brown water closed over it without a ripple worth the name.

Vess came and stood at Quentin's shoulder. She was holding the journal. She was also, Quentin noticed, holding a second object: a waterproof case, Meridian-stamped, salvaged from the scree during the night climb. Inside it, when she flipped it open, was Hale's satellite phone, cracked screen, one bar of signal, blinking with queued messages.

"It washed up on the bar with the bags," Vess said. "It's still pinging." She turned the screen toward Quentin. "There's a message thread. From a number in the capital. Ministry prefix."

Quentin took the phone. The messages were short, bureaucratic, and damning—coordinates requested, permits expedited, a payment reference, and a final message sent twelve hours ago, as the storm made landfall: *Confirm site secured before access review. This closes tomorrow regardless of weather.*

Hale hadn't been working alone. Someone in the ministry had been selling the door before anyone had even proven there was a door.

Quentin looked at the phone, then at Vess, then down at the drowned valley where a syndicate's ambition lay scattered on a shrinking gravel bar, and felt the exhaustion in their body transmute, slowly, into something harder and cleaner.

"Copy everything," they said. "Then we take it to Kesh-Taru. Ama Sefu's council decides who sees it first."

Behind them, Ama Sefu was already rolling the oilcloth, reading the river's mood in the tilt of the rain.

"We walk today," she said. "While the water is high, the soldiers stay low. Come. The elders will want to hear what the storm brought them."
