## 21. Giving It Back

The rain came to Kesh-Taru the way it came everywhere now—sideways, insistent, as if the monsoon had decided to stay and raise a family. It drummed on the new tin roof of the council house and turned the red clay of the village square into a shallow lake that children crossed by running, shrieking, arms out like herons.

Quentin stood under the eave of the old meeting hall and watched them play in the floodwater without fear. That was the thing about this village, Ama Sefu had said more than once: we have never once pretended the river was not coming back.

Three weeks since the source. Three weeks since the dive, the sump, the line of rope Quentin's hands still remembered knot for knot. Three weeks since they'd surfaced into gray light with a child's recording tablet wrapped in oilcloth against their chest, and nothing else, and found Ama Sefu waiting at the water's edge with a lamp held high like she'd known exactly which one of them would come up carrying something small.

The tablets had come out over four days after that. Vess organized the recovery the way she did everything—obsessively, tenderly, with a provenance log she'd started before anyone agreed to anything. Every resin-sealed chamber catalogued in place first, photographed, measured, then lifted by Riverfolk hands, because Ama Sefu had been clear about that too: *your rope, your lungs, our ancestors.* Quentin dove the sump eleven times. Marion, sixty-one years old and half-broken from the rockslide, sat at the air line and counted breaths aloud into the mask, her voice steady as a metronome, and neither of them ever mentioned it afterward.

Now the whole village had come out for the return, and the rain hadn't discouraged a single one of them.

* * *

They'd built a long table of river timber in the center of the square, and on it, under a canopy strung between two poles, lay the recovered archive—not all of it, not yet; the deep chambers were still being brought down the gorge in stages—but enough. Forty-one tablets. The child's recording tablet sat among them, its spiral sigil freshly cleaned, its clay surface holding the pressed marks of a hand much smaller than Quentin's own.

Vess stood at the table with a ledger open, reading names. Not her grandfather's name, though he was there too—in the journal pages she'd copied out and bound herself, the 1961 route annotated in three colors of ink, the last entry transcribed beside the place where his handwriting stopped. She read the names of the Riverfolk dead instead. The keepers of the spring. The families who had carried the archive upstream when the lowland towns drowned. She read until the crowd went quiet in the way crowds do when someone is saying true things slowly.

"Provenance," Vess said, and her voice cracked only slightly, "is just a word for who loved a thing before you did." She closed the ledger. "This has always belonged to you. We were only couriers."

An elder took the ledger from her hands and pressed it shut with both palms, the way you seal a jar.

Then it was Marion's turn.

Quentin watched their mother walk to the table through the rain-shadow of the canopy, and thought, not for the first time, how strange it was that a person could be so familiar and so unrecognizable at once. She wore the same field jacket she'd worn on the gorge road, patched at the elbow where the rockslide had torn it. Her hair was shorter than Quentin remembered from any photograph. She moved like someone who had recently learned how heavy her own body was and had made peace with it.

She didn't give a speech. Quentin should have known she wouldn't. Marion Kavanagh had spent thirty years building a theory about the Riverfolk archive, and the world had expected her, at the end of it, to plant a flag. Instead she set a single sheet of paper on the table, weighted it with a stone from the source itself, and signed it in front of everyone—her name in quick, decisive strokes, the same hand that had written the letter Quentin burned in Sallow's Reach.

"I hereby relinquish," she read aloud, flat and clear, "all claim, scholarly or otherwise, to the Riverfolk archive, its contents, its interpretation, and its publication." A pause. Rain on tin. "I spent my career believing this memory was lost. It wasn't lost. It was kept. There's a difference, and I was too proud, for too long, to learn it." She looked up then, and her eyes crossed the square and found Quentin standing apart under the eave, exactly where Quentin had put themselves. "Kept is better."

She stepped back from the table. The elders received the paper. Someone began a low song, and others joined, and the sound rose under the rain like water finding its level.

Neither of them apologized. Not there, not later. Quentin understood now that they never would, and that this was not the failure it would have seemed a year ago. Some things you don't excavate. You leave them where they lie and build on top of them carefully, and the building holds.

* * *

Afterward, the village fed everyone, because of course it did. Fish and river greens and the dense fermented bread Ama Sefu called *the food of people who plan ahead*. Quentin ate sitting on the steps of the meeting hall, half-listening to Vess argue happily with two teenagers about whether her grandfather had actually reached the second gorge or only dreamed he had, and did not notice Marion sit down beside them until the bench shifted.

For a while neither spoke. The square emptied by degrees. The children had gone home; the rain softened to a fine drifting mist that caught the lamplight.

"You signed fast," Quentin said finally.

"I've been drafting it since the rockslide." Marion turned her cup in her hands. "Lying under that rock, thinking I was going to die in a gorge my daughter—" she corrected herself, evenly, "—my child pulled me out of, I did the arithmetic. Thirty years of theory. One life, still breathing next to me. The theory came out badly behind." She drank. "It usually does. I just never ran the numbers before."

"That's not an apology."

"No. It's an explanation, and explanations are what I do instead." She said it without flinching, and Quentin felt something in their chest loosen—not forgiveness exactly, but the end of waiting for a shape of words that was never going to come. "You burned my letter."

"You knew?"

"The ashes were in your stove when I got to your survey station. You'd left the door open. You always left doors open, even when you were trying to disappear." A dry almost-smile. "You were never very good at invisible, Quentin. You were good at quiet. Those are different things."

Quentin looked out at the dark square, the drowned lamplight, the river somewhere below the village talking to itself in the rain. "When did you figure that out?"

"On the ferry, probably. Watching you pretend not to watch me." She set the cup down. "Or maybe thirty years ago, and I refused to know it, because knowing it meant the dig wasn't the only thing I'd chosen wrong."

There it was. The hospital room. The buried ferry town. The confession Marion had made in the drowned streets of Old Ferrow with the water dripping through the collapsed roof, telling Quentin why she'd boarded a plane to a dig site instead of staying at a bedside—and Quentin listening, saying nothing, offering nothing, because some debts don't get paid in words.

"I'm not going to say it's fine," Quentin said.

"I'd be worried if you did."

"But I'm going to stay. For a while. The council wants the tide instruments read properly, and apparently I'm the only person on this coast who can be trusted not to sell the readings." Quentin shrugged. "And the cave—the sea cave—it needs watching. The door stays as found. Somebody has to make sure it stays as found."

Marion nodded slowly. Then, carefully, the way she handled artifacts: "And me?"

"You can stay too. If you want. There's a room above the survey station. It smells like mildew and bad decisions."

"It sounds perfect." And for the first time in Quentin's living memory, Marion Kavanagh laughed—a short, rusty, real laugh, quickly covered, but real. "Mildew and bad decisions. That's practically the family motto."

* * *

Ama Sefu found them there near dusk, moving through the mist with her stick and her unhurried certainty. She stood before them a moment, looking from one to the other, and whatever she saw made her nod once, satisfied, as if confirming a measurement.

"The council meets tomorrow," she said. "About the headwaters. About the water rights the syndicate thinks it buried with its lawyers." Her mouth tightened. Hale's crew had scattered downstream, but Meridian's paper claims survived the flood better than men did; Vess had already found three filings in the regional registry that needed contesting. "You will both be there. Not as guests." She fixed Quentin with the look she used when she wanted no argument and knew she'd get none anyway. "As readers. The instruments are yours to tend. The record is ours. That is the bargain, and bargains are the only inheritance worth having."

She turned to go, then paused.

"One more thing. The child's tablet—the one you carried out." She didn't look back. "The council read it last night. It's a school exercise. A girl practicing her keeper-marks, over and over, the way children do." Now she did turn, and her eyes were bright and unreadable. "Her name was Sefu. My grandmother's grandmother. The council says the lineage of readers runs through her, and that the person who carried her home should hear the rest tomorrow, when the whole record is opened."

She left them in the mist.

Marion raised an eyebrow. "A recording tablet. Out of everything in that chamber, you chose a child's homework."

"It was the only thing in there that wasn't finished," Quentin said. "Everything else was a record of what people had done. That one was a record of somebody learning how to begin." They watched the lamplight waver in the flooded square. "Felt like the thing worth carrying."

Marion was quiet a moment. "Your father used to say something like that. About beginnings." She said it lightly, testing, and Quentin let it stand without bracing, and the silence that followed was not an old wound reopening but just a silence, shared, almost comfortable.

Below the village, the river rose and rose, patient as inheritance, and the rain fell on Kesh-Taru like a blessing that intended to keep falling whether anyone thanked it or not.
