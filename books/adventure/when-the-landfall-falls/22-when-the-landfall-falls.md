## 22. When the Landfall Falls

The tide gauge at Sallow's Reach had a hairline crack in its housing that whistled when the wind came off the water from the northeast. Quentin had learned to read the whistle the way other people read instruments—a low moan meant pressure falling, a thin keening meant the barometer was lying to them, and silence meant nothing at all was wrong, which on this coast was its own kind of warning.

A year ago, Quentin would have noted the whistle in a survey log nobody would read and moved on. Today they knelt beside the gauge with a sealant gun and a Riverfolk council work order clipped to their jacket, and the work order bore a signature: Ama Sefu's grandson, Deni, who ran the council's records office now and signed his name with the spiral drawn small beneath it, like a period.

*Repair tide instrument, Station 4. Report readings to council weekly. The river remembers; help us remember too.*

Quentin pressed sealant into the crack and thought about how strange it was to be hired. Not contracted through some anonymized consultancy, not paid out of a grant with someone else's name on it—hired. By name. By people who knew exactly whose child they were and had decided it didn't matter, or mattered differently than Quentin had spent ten years believing.

The monsoon season was six weeks out. The long-range forecasts had been recalibrated after last year—the meteorological service now ran river models built partly on the ledger data Vess had spent the winter translating, and the models said what Ama Sefu's people had always said: the landfall would come early, and the river would rise faster than any forecast. The council wanted every station on the coast reading true before then.

Quentin finished the sealant, wiped the nozzle, and sat back on their heels to watch the water.

It was the lowest spring tide of the year today—not the impossible drop of that first morning, but low enough. Out past the breakwater, black rock showed its shoulders above the swell, wet and glistening, the way it had shown them the cave. Somewhere under that swell, behind the stone door they had left exactly as found, the sea breathed in and out of the chamber where the ledgers had stood for four hundred years. Empty now. The jars were in Kesh-Taru, in a climate-controlled room the council had built with settlement money that had taken eleven months of legal wrangling to extract from Meridian's insurers. Hale's company had dissolved in the spring, its claim drowned as thoroughly as the approach tunnel. Vess said there was still a lawyer somewhere drafting appeals. Nobody worried about him.

Quentin checked the gauge, logged the reading in the council's format—which required noting not just height but *character*, a word the old ledgers used for water, meaning the way a tide moved rather than how high—and started down the shingle toward the boat.

That was when they saw the figure standing at the tideline.

For one bad second, the old reflex fired: *someone asking questions*. Then the figure turned, and the light caught grey hair cut short, and a coat that had once been good and was now just durable, and Quentin's chest did the complicated thing it always did.

Marion Kavanagh stood at the edge of the water like a woman waiting for a ferry that might never come.

* * *

She didn't turn around when Quentin's boots crunched up behind her. She was looking at the black rock, or at the water over it, her hands jammed in her coat pockets against the wind.

"Deni told me you'd be at Station 4," she said. "He also told me not to surprise you, and then he gave me your location anyway. I think he finds us both funny."

"He finds everyone funny. It's genetic." Quentin stopped an arm's length away. Close enough to talk. Far enough that neither of them had to decide about anything else. "You didn't say you were coming."

"I didn't know I was coming until Tuesday." Marion finally turned. She looked older than she had on the gorge rim a year ago—not frailer, but settled, the way sediment settles. There were new lines around her eyes and she'd stopped dyeing whatever she used to dye. "I finished the book."

Quentin blinked. "What book?"

"The one I've been threatening the world with for thirty years." A dry almost-smile. "*The Riverfolk Archive: Memory and Water on the Karst Coast.* Cambridge wanted it. I told them no. It's being printed in Kesh-Taru, by the council press, with Vess doing the provenance notes and Ama Sefu writing the foreword. My name is third on the title page."

"Whose are first?"

"Ama Sefu's. Second is 'the people of the source.'" Marion looked back at the water. "It took me four drafts to be able to write that sentence without my hands shaking. Thirty years I thought the archive was mine. Mine to find, mine to explain, mine to hand to the world with my name on the spine. And then I stood in that dome while the water rose, and I understood that I had never once asked what the Riverfolk would have wanted. You asked. On the gorge road, before I did. Before anyone."

"You signed the agreement," Quentin said. "At Kesh-Taru. Publicly."

"I signed it because you signed it first, and I couldn't stand the idea of my child being more honest than me." She said it flatly, without self-pity, which was the only reason Quentin could hear it. "I've been trying to earn the signature since. It's slow work. I'm not naturally good at it."

"No," Quentin said. "You're really not."

Marion laughed—a short, startled sound, as if the laugh had escaped custody. The wind pushed between them and filled the silence, and Quentin found, examining themselves carefully the way they'd examine a stratigraphy, that the silence wasn't angry anymore. It was just silence. Two people standing on a beach. It was astonishing how much could be excavated from that.

"I have something for you," Marion said. "If you want it. If you don't, I'll put it back in my pocket and we'll talk about tides, and I'll take the evening ferry, and that will also be fine."

"What is it?"

She drew her hand from her pocket slowly. In her palm lay a pendant on a worn leather cord—the spiral sigil, carved in dark stone, the twin of the mark on the cave door, the mark Quentin had seen around her neck across a childhood of dig sites and hotel rooms and one terrible hospital corridor.

"I thought I lost this," Marion said. "In the collapse, at the tunnel. I tore the whole camp apart looking for it. Then last month I found it sewn into the lining of my field jacket, which means at some point—I don't even remember doing it—at some point I hid it from myself." Her voice stayed level, but her thumb moved over the spiral the way it used to when Quentin was small and she was explaining stratigraphy at a kitchen table, drawing layers in spilled sugar. "I've decided that's the whole story of my life in one object. Carrying the thing while pretending I'd lost it."

"Why give it to me?"

"Because it was never mine either." She held it out. "It came down through my mother, who took it from a market stall in Sallow's Reach in 1953 and told everyone it was decorative. It belongs here. And—" she paused, and Quentin watched her choose the next word with the care she gave to dating a fragile shard, "—and I would like it to be carried by someone who chose to stay. That's all. No conditions. No inheritance attached. Just a stone and a cord."

Quentin looked at the pendant for a long time.

A year ago—two years ago—they would have refused. Refusing had been the whole architecture of their life: refuse the name, refuse the shadow, refuse the letters, refuse the woman. They had gotten so good at refusing that they'd refused their own reflection half the time.

But the council work order was still clipped to their jacket. *Report readings to council weekly.* Their name on a payroll. Their hands trusted with the river's memory. Belonging, it turned out, wasn't something that happened to you when you finally got small enough to slip inside it unnoticed. It was something you walked into with your eyes open, on purpose, signing things.

Quentin took the pendant.

It was lighter than they remembered. Cold from Marion's pocket, already warming.

"Thank you," they said. It came out rough. They tried again. "Thank you."

Marion nodded once, briskly, blinking hard at the water, granting them both the fiction that she wasn't crying. "There's a boat at six," she said. "I'm staying two days. Vess arrives tomorrow with the page proofs, and Deni wants to show me the new reading room, and I intend to be extremely useful and extremely quiet, in that order." She glanced sideways. "Unless that's too much. Say so, and I'll keep to the guest house."

Quentin considered it. Genuinely considered it—the old escape hatch, still hanging there, still open, still theirs if they wanted it.

"It's not too much," they said. "Come look at the station. The whistle's fixed. I want to show you what character the tide has today."

* * *

They walked the shingle together, not quite in step, not quite out of it, and Quentin talked the way they had never talked with anyone—about gauge calibrations, about the council's plan to build three new stations upriver before landfall, about the school in Kesh-Taru where children were learning the ledger glyphs and one girl had already found an error in Vess's translation of the flood-year tables. Marion listened the way she listened to data: completely, without interruption, filing everything. Once, when Quentin described the submerged sump route they'd dived at the source, she said quietly, "You set the guide line yourself. Ama Sefu told me. You went first into black water on a dead reckoning from a four-hundred-year-old poem." And Quentin said, "Someone had to," and Marion said, "Yes. Someone did. It was you," and let it stand.

At Station 4, Quentin showed her the repaired gauge, the logbook with the council stamp, the little brass plaque Deni had installed without asking anyone: *Kavanagh Survey Station — maintained for the people of the river.* Marion read the name twice.

"They didn't ask which Kavanagh," she observed.

"No. Deni says it covers both of us now." Quentin crouched and checked the float mechanism, mostly to have something to do with their hands. "I fought it for a week. Then I realized I'd spent ten years making sure no one knew my name, and these people had gone and put it on a building, and the world hadn't ended. Nobody asked me to be her. Nobody asked me to not be her. They just needed the tides read."

"That's the whole trick of it, isn't it," Marion said softly. "Being somebody's child. At some point you have to let it mean less than what you do."

The tide was turning. Out past the breakwater, the black rock began to go under, shoulder by shoulder, the sea folding itself back over the cave with the patience of something that had done this ten thousand times and would do it ten thousand more. Quentin stood and watched it, the pendant cool against their sternum under their shirt, and felt the water's character change the way the old ledgers described it—not rising so much as returning, taking back what it had lent, keeping its own accounts.

Behind them, up the coast road, the evening ferry sounded its horn, right on schedule, carrying passengers who had no idea what had been found and lost and given back on this shore. Ahead, six weeks out, the monsoon was assembling itself over warm water, and the river was already dreaming of the rise.

Quentin picked up the tool bag and turned back toward the town, toward the guest house where their mother was waiting, toward the work that was theirs.

The tide gauge whistled once, low and steady, and fell silent.

Everything, for once, was exactly as deep as it should be.
