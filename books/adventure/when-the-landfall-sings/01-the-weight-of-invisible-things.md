## 1. The Weight of Invisible Things

The trick to being no one, Casper Galloway had learned, was to be interesting to no one and useful to exactly one person at a time.

Right now that person was a smuggler named Ferro Dann, who was currently three switchbacks below, wheezing like a bellows with a hole in it, and who had hired Casper for one purpose: carry the good pack, walk the bad passes, and ask no questions. The good pack weighed forty pounds and contained, as far as Casper cared to know, tea. It was certainly not tea. But the Sable Passes did not reward curiosity. They rewarded quiet feet, a low profile against the skyline, and the humility to be, at all times, the least remarkable thing on the mountain.

Casper had gotten very good at being unremarkable. Six years of practice. There had been a time—another life, another name spoken in rooms full of people who mattered—when Casper Galloway had been the most remarkable thing on any mountain they stood on. *The best route-finder on the coast,* people used to say. *Reads rock like scripture.* Now they said nothing at all, which was the point.

They paused on the shoulder of the third pass and let the wind inspect them. Wind made a better scout than most scouts; it found everything worth finding and reported it to no one. Below, the trail dropped into a fold of grey scree where it vanished entirely, which was why this route worked. Three days from the coast, two days from the border stones, and for the middle stretch a traveler simply ceased to exist. No road, no cairns, no smoke. Just stone and lichen and the occasional marmot whistling its disapproval.

Casper liked the marmots. They were honest about their opinions.

"You're stopping," Ferro announced, arriving red-faced and aggrieved, as though the mountain had personally insulted him.

"I'm listening."

"To what? There's nothing here."

"Exactly." Casper crouched and pressed two fingers to the scree. "Someone moved through here yesterday. Four legs, heavy load. Mule train, maybe six animals."

Ferro squinted at the ground, which offered him nothing, because the ground only spoke to people who had spent enough years listening to go slightly deaf to everything else. "So?"

"So we're not the only ones using the quiet way." Casper rose, shouldered the pack, and adjusted the strap with the small habitual tug that kept forty pounds of definitely-not-tea from becoming a spine problem. "Which means someone's paying for silence on both sides of the border, and neither of them knows about us. Let's keep it that way. We cut east at the split and take the goat track."

"The goat track is a cliff with ambitions."

"The goat track is a cliff with excellent manners, if you don't argue with it." Casper started walking. "Argue with it and it drops you into the ravine, and then I have to explain to your buyers why their tea is scattered across four hundred feet of scree."

Ferro muttered something about guides and their god complexes and followed anyway, because that was the arrangement: Casper knew the way, Ferro knew the buyer, and neither of them asked about the other's half. It was a clean arrangement. Clean arrangements were all Casper had anymore, and they treasured each one the way other people treasured heirlooms.

* * *

They made the goat track by late afternoon, and it was every bit as charming as advertised—a shelf of broken limestone stitched to the cliff face, wide enough for one cautious person or one very confident goat. Casper went first, reading the rock the way other people read weather. Here, a slab tilted wrong; weight on the uphill edge. There, a seam of softer stone that frost had been quietly eating for a century. And there—the kind of detail that separated guides from hikers—a handhold that looked perfect and would come away in your grip like a rotten tooth.

"Left foot high, stay off the white hold," Casper called back, without turning around. "Trust the dark one."

"Why?"

"Because the white one is lying to you."

Ferro, to his credit, obeyed, and lived, and complained about it the entire way, which Casper considered a fair price for survival.

This was the part of the work Casper still loved, if love was the right word for a feeling that lived somewhere between craft and prayer. The mountain didn't care who you had been. It didn't care about tribunals or blacklists or the rescue that went wrong on the Vantar Shelf, the one with the names attached to it, the ones Casper still recited sometimes at night like a penance: four souls, one storm, one decision made in ninety seconds that took six years to stop relitigating. The mountain only cared whether you read it honestly. Rock was the last honest thing left in Casper's world, and so Casper had arranged a life with as much rock in it as possible and as few people.

It was working, more or less. That was the bitter joke. Invisibility worked. It just cost everything.

* * *

They camped that night in a hollow below the saddle, out of the wind, and Casper slept in the shallow, unsatisfying way of people who have trained themselves to wake at anything. Which was why, an hour before dawn, when a pebble clicked somewhere up-slope, Casper was already awake and already counting.

Click. Pause. Click-click.

Boots. One person, moving carefully, descending from the saddle. Not a mule train. Not a goat. A scout, then—and coming down the exact line Casper would have chosen, because it was the only sensible line, because Casper had chosen it first.

Casper lay still and did arithmetic. Ferro snored like a landslide, bless him, loud enough to cover a retreat. The fire was ash, cold for hours. The packs were staged by the hollow's mouth, ready. The descent line ran along the eastern rim, thirty feet of easy ground if you knew where the drop-off wasn't.

The boots came closer. And then, in the grey pre-light, the scout stopped at the top of the hollow and looked down, and Casper looked up, and for one suspended moment the two of them regarded each other across twenty feet of nothing.

He was young. Border service, by the cut of his coat—Sable garrison, probably, the ones paid in salt and grudges. He had a lantern unlit in his hand and a face that was doing rapid, visible work: *smugglers, two of them, one old and fat, one—*

And then the work stopped. Because Casper had made the mistake, the first mistake in six years, of looking back too long. Of looking back *like themselves*. Most people, caught in the dark by a scout, froze or fled or groveled. Casper had done none of those things. Casper had assessed him—weight, reach, the angle of the slope behind him, the two seconds it would take to close the distance—and the boy had felt himself being measured by someone who could do it faster than he could, and something in his face changed.

"That's—" the scout said, and his voice cracked with something worse than suspicion. Recognition. "You're Galloway. You're *Galloway*, off the Vantar Shelf. They said you were dead. They said—"

Casper was already moving.

No drama to it—that was the whole art of it. Ferro got a boot to the ribs and a hissed *"up, now, run"* while Casper swept the packs onto their shoulders, and then they were both flowing up the eastern rim in the dark, along ground Casper had memorized by touch the evening before, because a guide who stopped memorizing exits was a guide who died surprised. Behind them the scout shouted, once, twice, his voice bouncing off the saddle, and somewhere far below a dog began to bark, and Casper did not look back again.

Looking back was how you'd been caught. Looking back was a luxury for people with names.

* * *

They lost the pursuit in the fog fields above the tree line, where the world ended at arm's length and even dogs went quiet and miserable. By midmorning they were on the far side of the border stones, and Ferro had recovered his breath and, with it, his voice.

"Galloway." He said the name like it was a debt. "That scout knew you. Knew your *name*."

"He knew a story about someone," Casper said. "Stories get around."

"Don't do that. Don't do the flat voice." Ferro planted himself in the trail, which for a man of his build was an act of real courage. "Six years I've carried you on my runs. Six years you've been the best porter I ever had and the worst conversationalist, and I've never asked what you're hiding from, because that was the deal. But if the Sable garrison knows Galloway walks my routes, then my routes are burned, and my neck is next, and I'd like to know how much trouble is walking beside me."

Casper looked at him. Ferro Dann, sweating, frightened, loyal in the limited way of men whose loyalty has a load rating—and underneath it, genuinely worried, which was almost touching.

"A little trouble," Casper said. "The kind that walks away."

"And if it doesn't walk away?"

"Then I'll be gone before it arrives. You know that's true. It's the one thing you've never had to worry about." Casper shifted the pack. "I'm nobody, Ferro. That's the whole product. You bought nobody, and nobody delivered."

Ferro held the stare for a long moment, then blew out his cheeks and surrendered, the way big men do, all at once. "Nobody," he grumbled, starting downhill. "Nobody with a famous name and a bounty face. Fine. Fine. But we winter in Greyharbor, and Greyharbor's full of sailors, and sailors talk, and if your story follows you there—"

"It won't follow me there." Casper fell into step behind him, eyes already moving over the trail, reading it, dismissing it. "Greyharbor doesn't know my name."

Greyharbor didn't know anything about Casper Galloway. That was the plan. Be no one, carry someone's tea, sleep shallow, keep moving. Six years of it, and it had worked, and today it had cracked—one look, one name, one boy on a ridge with recognition blooming across his face like a stain—and Casper had felt the crack go all the way through, down to the old question that lived under everything:

*A place in the world costs a name. A name costs everything else. So what are you actually carrying all this silence for?*

No answer. There was never an answer. There was just the next pass, and the next, and the weight of invisible things.

They camped that night in a ruined waystation on the flats below the passes—an old tide-flats shelter, walls of stacked stone, roof half gone, smelling of salt and old smoke. Ferro was asleep before the fire settled. Casper sat up, keeping the shallow watch, watching the moon drag the sea back from the mudflats in long silver sheets.

And out of the dark, from the direction of the water, came a sound Casper knew too well and had hoped never to hear again: a human voice, thin with pain, calling for help.

Casper closed their eyes. Every instinct said *no one, keep being no one, walk away before it has a name.*

But the voice had a name already. It carried names the way wounded things do.

Casper picked up the lantern and went down toward the flats.
