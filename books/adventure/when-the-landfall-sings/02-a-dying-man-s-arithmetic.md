## 2. A Dying Man's Arithmetic

The voice led Casper down the shingle and out onto the flats, where the mud held the lantern light like a bowl holds milk.

"I'm here," the voice said, before Casper could announce themselves. "I heard your boots. You walk like a man trying to apologize to the ground."

Casper stopped. "I walk like a porter."

"You walk like a guide pretending to be a porter. There's a difference. The porter watches his feet. The guide watches everything else." The voice belonged to a woman lying on her side in the lee of an overturned skiff, one arm folded beneath her head with a terrible casualness, as though she were napping on a beach instead of bleeding into it. She was sixty if she was a day, weathered to the color of old rope, with a prospector's canvas coat and a smile that had clearly been used to talk its way out of better situations than this one.

Casper crouched at the edge of the lantern's reach. Habit said stay back. Habit had kept them alive for six years, and Casper respected it the way you respect a dog that bites strangers: gratefully, and from a distance.

"Let me see."

"Bold. Most men introduce themselves first." But she shifted, and the coat fell open, and Casper saw the wound low in her belly — a ragged hole, dark in the lamplight, already doing the arithmetic that wounds do when they've been left alone too long.

Casper did their own arithmetic. It came out badly.

"That's a bullet," Casper said.

"That's a bullet. Very good. I told them you were good." She laughed, which cost her; Casper watched the laugh turn into something wetter and quieter. "Name's Vey. Ilsa Vey. And before you ask — yes, there were three of them, no, I didn't start it, and yes, I got the worse end of it because I'm old and slow and they were young and fast, which is how most of history gets made."

"Who's *they*?"

"Men who wanted what I'm carrying." Her eyes went past Casper, up the slope toward the waystation, sharp as a gull's despite everything else going soft in her. "You alone?"

"For my sins."

"Good answer. Bad theology." She patted the ground beside her, an absurd little gesture, hostess to the last. "Sit. You're making me crane my neck, and I've decided not to spend what's left of me on neck muscles."

Casper didn't sit. Casper knelt, because kneeling meant hands free, and opened the satchel they carried for exactly this — the bandages, the needle, the bitter-smelling tincture that smugglers called *honesty* because after it, people told you anything. The work was familiar. That was the cruelty of it. Six years since the mountain, six years of being no one, and the hands still remembered how to be someone's only hope.

Ilsa Vey watched them work without flinching, which told Casper more about her than the coat or the accent or the prospector's calluses. Flinching was for people who still expected the world to be kind.

"You're wasting the good cloth," she observed.

"I'm wasting nothing."

"You're wasting the good cloth on a gut shot. We both know where this goes. I've dug enough holes to know when I'm standing in one." She said it lightly, the way you'd complain about the weather, and Casper hated her a little for it, and admired her more.

"The tincture will hold the pain down."

"The tincture will hold the pain down," she agreed solemnly. "And the tide will come up around four, and I'll have a very peaceful evening listening to the water, and by morning the flats will have me, and some other traveler will find a coat full of bones and think, well, that's the coast for you." She paused. "Unless."

"There's always an unless."

"*Especially* with me. I've made a career of unlesses." She fumbled inside the coat — Casper stilled her hand, gently, and did the reaching instead, and found what she'd been patting for: a packet wrapped in oilcloth, and inside the oilcloth, a roll of hide-paper sealed shut with a blob of black wax pressed with a thumbprint. It was heavier than paper should be. There was something stiff sewn between the layers, like a slat of wood, like a spine.

"What is it?"

"A map."

Casper nearly laughed. "Every dying stranger on this coast has a map. Half of them are drawings of fish."

"This one isn't drawings of fish." Ilsa's eyes had gone bright and feverish, and behind the brightness was something else — fear, real fear, the kind that doesn't come from the wound. "This one's the Meridian."

The name landed in Casper's chest like a dropped anchor. Everyone on this coast knew the Meridian — the salvage ship that went down off the Teeth nine years ago with a king's fortune in gold dust and salvage rights aboard, and with it, supposedly, papers, manifests, cargo lists, all the small bureaucratic ghosts that sink with a ship and never quite stop mattering. Salvage fleets had been picking at those waters for years. Fortunes had been spent finding nothing. Men had died for rumors of her coordinates.

"You're joking," Casper said.

"Do I look like a woman who jokes about maps? Look at my face. This is a serious face. This is a face that has been punched for less." She took the packet back — took it the way you take back a child, both hands, close to the body. "I found her two seasons ago. Dived her eleven times. I know what's in the hold, and I know what's in the strongbox, and I know why three men with fleet tattoos tried to take this off me tonight."

"Fleet tattoos." Casper's hands went still over the bandages. "Salvage fleet?"

"Calloway fleet. Marchand's colors." Ilsa watched Casper's face as she said the name, and whatever she saw there made her nod slowly, like a woman confirming a suspicion she'd been carrying since the boots first crunched the shingle. "There it is. You know her."

"Everyone knows Odile Marchand."

"No," Ilsa said. "*Everyone* knows *of* her. You know *her*. There's a difference, and the difference just walked across your face wearing its Sunday clothes." She winced, waved off Casper's hands. "Never mind. Not my business. My business is arithmetic, and mine has come out very simple: I am dying, you are here, and this map needs to go somewhere before I do."

"Then sell it. There are brokers in Greyharbor who'd—"

"Don't say that word."

"Which word?"

"Sell." She said it like a curse, spitting it toward the water. "That's the whole trap, don't you see? The moment this becomes a thing that's sold, it becomes a thing that's bought, and everything it knows dies in some rich man's safe. The gold they can have. Let Marchand have the gold, let her choke on it. It's the *papers* that matter. The manifest. The list of names that went down with her." Her grip closed on Casper's wrist, and for a dying woman her strength was astonishing and awful. "Names, porter. Names of officials who signed things they shouldn't have. Names that could pull a person out of the pit they've been thrown into."

Casper went very carefully still.

Six years. Six years of forged papers and borrowed names, of walking through border towns with their chin down and their story rehearsed, of being no one so thoroughly that sometimes, in the grey hour before dawn, Casper had to lie still and remember their own name like a password. All of it built on a rescue gone wrong on a mountain in the north, on a report filed by men Casper had never met, on a verdict handed down by officials whose faces Casper had only seen on warrants.

"You don't know what you're saying," Casper said quietly.

"I know exactly what I'm saying. I've been watching you since you picked up the lantern. You looked at my wound the way a guide looks at a route — measuring, deciding, taking responsibility before anyone asked you to. Guides don't stop being guides. They just get very tired." She loosened her grip, patted Casper's wrist almost apologetically. "And you flinched at Marchand's name like it owed you money."

"It does owe me money, as it happens."

"Ha." The laugh turned into coughing, and the coughing turned into a long, bad silence, and when she spoke again the performance had thinned and what was underneath was just an old woman, cold, on a beach, running out of time. "Listen to me. Wren. My daughter. Wren Vey, Greyharbor, keeps a chandlery off Rope Walk — the one with the blue door, you can't miss it, she painted it herself and repainted it every spring because salt eats paint and she refuses to lose to salt." Something moved through her face then, brief and enormous, grief and love tangled so tightly Casper couldn't tell which was holding which up. "She'll want to slam the door in your face. Let her slam it once. Then give her this, and tell her—" Ilsa stopped. Started again. "Tell her it wasn't the sea that kept me away."

"What was?"

"That's between me and her. If she asks, you tell her to read the map first. Everything I couldn't say is stitched into where I chose to go." She pushed the packet against Casper's chest. "One promise, porter. Two parts. Bring it to her. Never sell it. That's the whole contract. No gold for you, no cut, no finder's fee. Just the promise."

"And if I say no?"

"Then you're a kinder person than you pretend, because you'll sit with me until the tide comes, and I'll die easier than I deserve, and the map goes in the fire before I go." She smiled, and it was a real smile, tired and crooked. "But you won't say no."

"You don't know that."

"I know that you knelt. Nobody kneels anymore. They crouch. Crouching means ready to run. You knelt." She tapped the wax seal with one cracked fingernail. "Kneelers make promises. It's a whole condition."

Casper looked at the packet. Black wax, thumbprint, oilcloth, weight. Somewhere north of here, a salvage flagship was presumably riding at anchor, and somewhere south, a girl with a blue door was repainting it against the salt, hating her mother, and neither of them knew yet that a dying stranger on the flats had just drafted a third party into the war between them.

Everything in Casper said *no one, keep being no one*. Maps got people killed. Promises got people hunted. The last time Casper had taken responsibility for a stranger's life, it had ended two careers, one village's trust, and any hope of a fixed address.

But the woman was looking at Casper with the particular patience of someone who has already done all the hard parts — found the wreck, dived it, survived the ambush, crawled the flats — and is now simply waiting for the world to catch up to her plan.

"One promise," Casper said. "Two parts. I bring it to her. I don't sell it. I'm not promising she'll listen, and I'm not promising I'll succeed, and if Marchand's people come for this map, I will throw it in the sea before I let them have it."

"Done," Ilsa said instantly, "done, done — oh, you fool, thank you, you absolute fool," and she laughed again, and this time the laugh didn't hurt her, or she didn't let it show, and she pressed the packet into Casper's hands and held them shut around it with both of hers, cold as the shingle.

"Now," she said, settling back against the overturned skiff, "be a dear and fetch me the rest of that tincture, and while you're at it, sit down properly. I've got perhaps an hour of sense left in me, and I intend to spend it telling you everything you'll need to survive what you've just agreed to. Starting with the tide tables." Her eyes glittered. "Because the Meridian, my dear fool, only shows herself six times a year — and the first of those six tides comes in three days."

Far out on the dark water, right on schedule, the sea began to sigh and gather itself, and somewhere beyond the horizon, Casper would learn soon enough, a fleet was already turning toward the same arithmetic.

* * *

An hour later, Ilsa Vey's breathing slowed to the rhythm of the incoming tide, and Casper sat beside her in the lantern light with a sealed map against their ribs and a promise sitting on their chest like ballast, heavier than any cargo they'd ever carried, and far less willing to be put down.
