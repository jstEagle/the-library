## 3. Six Tides

The map did not look like much.

That was the first thing Casper noticed about it, sitting in the waystation loft with a stub of tallow candle and a mug of tea gone bitter from being forgotten twice. Wax seals cracked, vellum unrolled: a mess of ink lines, depth soundings written in a hand that trembled toward the end, and a set of numbers along the margin that meant nothing at all until they meant everything.

Ilsa Vey had died an hour after dawn, quiet as a tide going out. The waystation keeper, a round woman named Bess who had seen enough dying to stop making a ceremony of it, had covered the body with sailcloth and asked no questions beyond whether Casper wanted breakfast.

"You look like a man who forgot his own breakfast," Bess said now, appearing at the top of the ladder with bread and hard cheese and the expression of someone who intended to be obeyed.

"I'm not—"

"Don't argue with me. I've buried three husbands and a mule, and the mule argued less."

Casper took the plate. It seemed wise.

* * *

By full dark, Casper had the map spread flat on the floorboards, weighted at the corners with a boot, a tin cup, a coil of rope, and Bess's cat, who had opinions about being moved and won.

The ink lines were coastlines. Casper knew them without trying — the Hook of Saltmere, the drowned forest off Crag's Toe, the long gray shingle where the land gave up pretending to be land and became sea instead. Ilsa Vey had drawn her coast the way old sailors drew their wives: with too much detail in the places she loved and none at all where she'd stopped looking.

The numbers along the margin were tide tables. Not this season's — last spring's, worked forward by hand, each figure corrected in a different ink, as if she'd recalculated every year on the anniversary of something.

Casper counted. Six dates. Six low tides, spaced across the autumn like stones across a river, each one lower than the one before. A spring-tide sequence, the kind that came maybe once a year, when the moon pulled the sea back far enough to show it what it was hiding.

And there, at the center of the map, marked with an X so small and so careful it looked almost shy: *the Meridian*. Forty fathoms of salvage ship, sunk nine years ago with a hold full of gold and a passenger list nobody had ever recovered — except, apparently, a prospector with a grudge against forgetting.

"Six tides," Casper said aloud, to the cat, because the cat was the only audience available and had already heard worse.

Six chances. After the sixth, the moon would swing wide, the tides would shrink back into their ordinary modesty, and the wreck would stay down for another year. Possibly forever, if Odile Marchand got there first with her cranes and her chains and her talent for turning other people's discoveries into her property.

Casper sat back on their heels and did the arithmetic every guide learns young: distance, weather, daylight, luck. Greyharbor lay two days east, if Wren Vey could be found and persuaded to listen, which the afternoon's attempt suggested was roughly as likely as persuading the sea to listen. Port Calloway lay four days south, where the salvage fleet wintered. From Calloway to the wreck: three days' sail, fair winds.

Which meant—

Casper's stomach performed a slow, cold roll, like a hull taking water.

Which meant the fleet might already be out.

* * *

Bess kept a wireless set behind the bar, ostensibly for weather reports and officially for nothing else, and she let Casper use it for the price of helping haul a barrel of salt fish up from the cellar, which was a trade Casper would have made at double the price just to escape the smell.

"—repeat, all vessels, all vessels. Salvage fleet *Corvane*, *Brackish Maid*, and tender out of Port Calloway, bearing south-southeast, working the spring sequence. Charter holders advised the Meridian grounds are claimed under Marchand license. Repeat: claimed. Any independent parties on those grounds will be considered poachers and treated accordingly."

The voice was clipped, official, and faintly bored, the voice of a man reading somebody else's threats off a card. But underneath the boredom Casper heard the shape of the thing clearly enough: Odile had run the numbers faster. Odile always ran the numbers faster. It was, along with a gift for holding grudges like fine wine, one of her two great talents.

"Bad news?" Bess asked, polishing a glass that did not need it.

"The worst kind," Casper said. "Predictable."

* * *

There was exactly one person on this stretch of coast who might know more, and reaching him meant walking half the night to a herring station that smelled like a herring station, which is to say like money rotting politely.

Dov Halloway — no relation, though he'd spent years claiming otherwise whenever free drinks were available — ran the listening post at Gullneck Point. He'd been junior signalman on Casper's last crew, back when Casper still had a crew, a reputation, and a name that opened doors instead of closing them. Dov had been twenty-two and mostly elbows then. He'd be twenty-eight and entirely elbows now.

The station door opened before Casper knocked, which meant Dov had spotted them through the glass and spent a full minute deciding whether to pretend not to be home.

"Casper Galloway," Dov said, in the tone of a man greeting a ghost he owed money to. "They said you were dead."

"People say a lot of things about me. Most of them are wishes."

"They're also mostly true." Dov looked up and down the dark path behind Casper, then stepped aside. "Get in before the fog gets sentimental."

Inside smelled of lamp oil, wet wool, and herring, in ascending order of commitment. Dov poured tea from a pot that had clearly been brewed during some previous administration and pushed a cup across.

"You're here about the fleet," he said.

"You've been listening."

"I listen for a living. It's the only part of me that ever got promoted." He leaned back, chair creaking. "Three ships out of Calloway, running the spring sequence south. That's Marchand's flagship leading, which means it's not a survey. Surveys get tenders. Flagships get results."

"How long out?"

"Two days. Maybe three, if the wind's been contrary." Dov studied Casper over the rim of his cup with the frank, unhurried stare of a man who had once watched Casper talk a panicking climber off a ledge using nothing but a sandwich and a lie about the weather. "You're going out there, aren't you."

"I'm going to Greyharbor."

"That's not what I asked."

"No," Casper agreed. "It isn't."

Dov set the cup down. For a while the only sound was the wireless muttering to itself in the corner, listing bearings and barometric pressures to an audience of nobody.

"There's something else," Dov said finally, and Casper heard it coming the way you hear a rockfall start — a small sound that means a large one. "Fleet traffic's been chatty this week. Unusually chatty. And one message came through yesterday, priority-flagged, addressed direct to the *Corvane*. I shouldn't have copied it. I copied it."

He slid a slip of paper across the table. Casper read it twice, because the first time the words refused to arrange themselves into anything survivable.

*TO MARCHAND, CORVANE. CONFIRMED SIGHTING, SABLE PASSES. SUBJECT IDENTIFIED AS C. GALLOWAY, FORMER CREW. ADVISE SUBJECT BELIEVES SELF UNOBSERVED. AWAITING INSTRUCTION.*

The border scout. The one whose lantern Casper had dodged in the pass, whose whistle had chased them down the scree. Casper had told themselves it was routine — a scout doing a scout's job, a stranger passing through, forgettable, invisible, no one.

Odile knew.

"She knows you're alive," Dov said quietly. "She's known since the passes. And she hasn't turned back, and she hasn't sent anyone after you, which—" he tapped the paper, "—means she doesn't want you caught. She wants you followed. Straight to whatever you're carrying."

The candle-gutter of the room seemed very loud. Casper thought of Ilsa Vey's hand pressing the map into theirs, the wax seal warm from her grip, the promise made mostly to quiet a dying woman and now grown roots and teeth.

"She thinks I'll lead her to it," Casper said.

"She trained you. She knows how you think. She's betting you can't resist a treasure map any more than she could." Dov shrugged, apologetic, as if the universe's cruelty were somehow his fault. "Are you going to tell me what's on the map?"

"No."

"Didn't think so. You always were terrible at asking for help and magnificent at needing it." Dov stood, went to a locker, and came back with a canvas packet, which he dropped on the table with a thump. "Flares, dry rations, a chart of the southern approaches with my own corrections, which are better than the admiralty's, and a spare battery for your lamp. If you're going to do something stupid, do it equipped."

"Dov—"

"Don't." He held up a hand. "You carried me off the Kestrel Face when I froze. Everyone said you were mad. You said—" his voice did a small, rough imitation, "'Nobody freezes alone on my mountain.' Well. Nobody sails stupid alone on my coast either. Take the packet, take the tide tables, and take one piece of actual advice."

Casper waited.

"Odile Marchand doesn't chase things," Dov said. "She corners them. Whatever route you're planning, plan the one she'd never expect — the one you'd have called reckless six years ago." He almost smiled. "Lucky for you, six years of being no one teaches tricks the legends never learned."

* * *

Casper walked back along the cliff path in the small hours, the map sealed in oilskin against their chest, the packet on their shoulder, the sea below black and patient as a held breath.

Six tides. Two already gone — wasted on grief and hesitation and a girl in Greyharbor who had shut the chandlery door like a verdict. Four left, and a fleet ahead of them, captained by the only person alive who knew every trick Casper had ever learned, plus several Casper hadn't.

A sensible person would burn the map. A sensible person would go north, change their face, find a new coast, keep being no one. Being no one had kept Casper alive for six years. Being no one was warm and small and safe as a pocket.

But Ilsa Vey had died holding their wrist, and her daughter had shut a door, and somewhere south a ship named for a carrion bird was sailing toward a wreck that held a fortune and a truth, and Casper Galloway — blacklisted, broke, and, apparently, no longer invisible — found that dread had done something strange overnight.

It had sharpened into purpose.

"One promise," Casper said to the dark, to the sea, to the ghost riding in their chest pocket. "You could have picked anything."

The fog closed in around them, and Casper turned south, toward the tides, and began to walk.
