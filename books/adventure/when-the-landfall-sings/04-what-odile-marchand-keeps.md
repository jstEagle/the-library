## 4. What Odile Marchand Keeps

The *Charity's Reward* was named as a joke, and Odile Marchand had never once laughed at it.

She stood at the stern rail in the grey hour before dawn, watching Port Calloway shrink behind them into a smear of lamplight and fish-smoke, and she did her arithmetic the way she did everything: without mercy. Six tides until the Meridian showed her bones. Three days' hard sail to the Sable coast. One map, somewhere ahead of her, in the hands of a dead woman's courier.

And one name she had not spoken aloud in six years, except to herself, in the dark, where it could not hear her say it wrong.

"Captain." Brannoc, her first mate, appeared at her elbow with two cups of chicory and the expression of a man who had learned that offering opinions was safer than offering advice. "There's tea. Well. There's something pretending to be tea."

"You're a comfort, Brannoc."

"I'm paid to be. Badly, but I'm paid."

She took the cup. It was terrible. She drank it anyway, because a captain who flinched at bad chicory would flinch at worse, and there was always worse.

"The fleet's formed up?" she asked.

"Four hulls behind us. The *Kestrel* lost a spar in the harbor mouth and is grumbling about it. Halvard wants to put in at Gullwatch for repairs."

"Halvard wants to put in anywhere with a warm stove and a woman who'll listen to him complain. Tell him he can grumble all the way to the Sables."

Brannoc grinned and went. Odile stayed at the rail.

Below her feet, through three decks of timber and tar, the hold of the *Charity's Reward* carried chains, grapnels, diving bells, and forty men who believed they were sailing after gold. That was fine. Gold was a good story. Gold kept men rowing. But Odile had learned long ago — from a student too clever by half, a student with steady hands and a habit of asking *why* when everyone else asked *how much* — that the best cargo a ship could carry was a secret.

The Meridian carried both.

* * *

Her cabin was small by choice. Captains who wanted big cabins, she'd told Casper once, years ago, on another ship in another life, were captains advertising that they'd already stopped believing they deserved the sea. She'd said it while teaching a knot — the double bowline, the one that never slips when wet — and Casper had tied it wrong four times out of stubbornness and right on the fifth try, and then looked up with that face, that infuriating face that was always half apology and half challenge, and said:

"So what do you advertise, Captain?"

Odile had thrown a chalk eraser at them. It was one of her fondest memories, which told her most of what she needed to know about how her life had gone since.

Now she sat at her desk and unrolled the chart of the Sable coast, weighting the corners with the brass instruments she trusted more than people. The wreck lay off the Teeth — those black fangs of rock where the tide ran like a spilled secret. Six tides a year, the water drew back far enough to show the Meridian's spine. Ilsa Vey had found it. Ilsa Vey had died of it. And between the dead prospector and the drowned ship stood one person in all the world whom Odile Marchand had never managed to stop thinking about.

Casper Galloway.

She pressed her thumb flat against the chart, over the coastline, as if she could pin the past down the way you pin a map.

Here was what the harbors said: that Casper Galloway led a rescue party onto the Kestrel Ice in a whiteout, that the party broke apart, that three clients died and a guide's reputation died with them, and that Odile Marchand — the great Marchand, the woman whose name was stitched into the guild charter — had been ruined by association. Blacklisted in two countries. Stripped of her charter. Forced to sell her skills to salvage fleets, because the sea didn't check references.

That was the story. Odile knew it by heart. She'd helped tell it, in the worst weeks afterward, when grief made a weapon of every word.

Here was what Odile actually remembered, alone, at night, with the lamp turned low:

A whiteout so total that up and down were rumors. Casper's voice, calm as ballast, calling the count. Her own decision — *hers*, not Casper's — to push for the ridge instead of sheltering, because the weather window was closing and she was thirty minutes from the summit fee that would have saved her charter. And Casper, at the rope's end, saying the thing she still heard when the wind came up: *"We should go down, Captain. The mountain doesn't care what we're owed."*

She hadn't listened. She was the captain. Captains don't listen; they decide.

Three ropes went. Three clients. And afterward, in the inquiry tent, with the guild elders looking at her like a ledger with a hole in it, Odile had heard herself say: *the junior guide pressed the ascent.* Heard herself say it and felt, even then, the small cold horror of watching her own mouth commit treason.

It had worked. The blacklist fell on Casper instead of her — mostly. Enough. And Casper had vanished into the borderlands and become a rumor, and Odile had become a salvage captain with a flagship named after a joke, and neither of them had ever said the true sentence out loud.

*I sent you down the mountain so I wouldn't have to be the one who fell.*

She rolled up the chart. Her hands were steady. They were always steady. That was the other thing nobody understood about her — that the steadiness wasn't courage. It was just what was left when you'd spent everything else.

* * *

They raised the Sable coast at dusk on the second day, and by midnight the *Charity's Reward* stood off a fishing village called Lowmark, which consisted of eleven houses, one chapel, one dock, and a population that Odile estimated at "enough to know things."

She took the gig ashore herself with six men. Not because she distrusted her crew — she did, professionally, the way you distrust any tool you depend on — but because some work you do with your own hands or it isn't done.

The village woke to lantern light on its dock and a tall woman in a salt-stiff coat standing very still in the middle of it, hands folded, voice pleasant.

"Good evening," she said, to the fishermen gathering in their doorways with the wariness of people who had met the fleet before. "I'm looking for a traveler. Passed through here recently, likely within the fortnight. A porter, probably traveling alone. Carries themselves like someone trying to be furniture." She smiled. It reached exactly halfway up her face. "You'll have seen them. Everyone sees them. Nobody remembers them. That's rather the point of them."

Silence. Somewhere a dog decided against barking.

An old man near the chapel steps spat thoughtfully into the dark. "Don't know anyone like that."

"No," Odile agreed. "You wouldn't. Which is why I'm going to make this easy." She nodded to Brannoc, who handed her the storm lantern, and she set it down gently on the dock planks, right beside a coil of tarred rope, and stepped back. "This village smells of herring and honesty, and I respect both. So: the person who tells me which road my traveler took keeps their dock. The person who lies to me loses it. And everyone else loses nothing, provided they're quick about choosing sides."

More silence. Then a boy — fourteen, maybe, all elbows and bad judgment — said, "You're Marchand. The salvage witch."

"Salvage *captain*," Odile corrected mildly. "Witch implies I enjoy this."

"Do you?"

She considered him. He had Casper's kind of face, the kind that asked questions at the worst possible moments. "Not yet," she said. "Ask me again in five minutes."

Nobody talked. Of course nobody talked. Fishing villages were fortresses of silence; it was the only wall they had. Odile had burned her first dock at nineteen, under a different captain, and had cried about it, and had been told that the crying was the part to fix.

She picked up the lantern.

"Last chance," she said, and meant it, and hated that she meant it, and lit the rope anyway.

Tar takes flame like a confession. Fire ran the length of the dock in a bright hissing line, and the planks began to blacken and curl, and the villagers shouted and formed a bucket line with the speed of long practice, and Odile stood aside and watched the orange light climb the pilings and thought, not for the first time, that she had become a woman who solved problems by making them impossible to ignore.

It was the old man who cracked. Not from fear — from fury. He came at her shaking his hat at her like a weapon. "The pass road," he snarled. "Toward Greyharbor junction, two nights back, moving fast. There. Are you happy? Burn your own soul next, it's worth less than my dock."

"Thank you," Odile said, and meant that too, which was somehow worse.

Greyharbor junction. Which meant the courier wasn't running straight for the wreck. Which meant a detour — and only one reason a stranger with a dead woman's map would detour to Greyharbor.

Delivering something. Or delivering themselves.

Odile walked back to the gig through the smoke and the bucket-brigade chaos, and Brannoc fell in beside her, and for a while neither spoke. Then he said, carefully, "The traveler. You knew them. Before."

The firelight was dying behind them. Ahead, the *Charity's Reward* rode at anchor, lamps lit along her rail like a string of patient teeth.

"I trained them," Odile said. "Best student I ever had. Better than me, if we're honest, and we're not, so let's leave it there."

"And now they've got your wreck."

"Our wreck," Odile said. "That's the trouble with Casper Galloway, Brannoc. Everything was always ours — the routes, the risks, the blame. Right up until the moment it was time to share the fall." She stepped into the gig and took the tiller. "Then somehow it was only ever mine."

Brannoc rowed. The water was black glass. Behind them, Lowmark's dock smoldered down to a red skeleton, and the villagers' voices carried thin across the bay — anger, mostly, and under it, faintly, a child laughing at the spectacle, because children will laugh at anything.

"Captain," Brannoc said at last. "If it comes to it, on the shingle, at the wreck — what do we do with them? Your student."

Odile watched the flagship grow larger, and thought about a whiteout, and a rope's end, and a voice saying *we should go down*, and the inquiry tent, and the sentence she had said instead of the true one.

"We bring them aboard alive," she said. "I want to look at them when I ask my question."

"What question?"

"The one I've been carrying six years." She adjusted the tiller. "Set course for Greyharbor junction. All sail. If my arithmetic's right, my student is about to knock on a door they think is closed — and I intend to be standing behind it."

Above them the stars were coming out, indifferent and exact, keeping their own ledger of who owed what to whom. Odile Marchand sailed north under them, steady-handed, advertising nothing at all.
