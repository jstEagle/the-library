## 5. Greyharbor, and the Girl Who Wouldn't Grieve

Greyharbor smelled of tar and herring and the particular stubbornness of a town that had decided, sometime in the last century, that it would not be moved by weather, empire, or common sense.

Casper came down off the coast road in the grey hour before dawn, when the fishing boats were already out and the streets belonged to gulls and the occasional insomniac cat. It was a detour. That was the word Casper kept using for it — *detour* — as though the map strapped against their ribs were simply going to wait politely while they did an errand. Every mile toward Greyharbor was a mile the salvage fleet might be gaining. Odile Marchand did not detour. Odile went through things, the way water goes through a hull.

But Ilsa Vey had died holding Casper's wrist with a grip that outlasted her heartbeat by a good minute, and her last coherent words had been *Wren*, and *Greyharbor*, and *don't sell it* — three instructions delivered in descending order of urgency, which told Casper exactly where the woman's priorities had lain.

So: Greyharbor. A detour. An errand.

The chandlery was easy to find, because there was only one, and because its sign — VEY'S, in paint so old the Y had given up and become a crooked V — was the kind of thing a daughter either repainted every spring or refused to touch on principle. From the state of it, Casper guessed principle.

The shop sat wedged between a net-mender's and a tavern that advertised itself with a wooden whale missing one eye. Inside, lamplight fell across coils of rope, barrels of pitch, stacks of oilcloth, and a young woman up a ladder, re-shelving lanterns with the brisk violence of someone who had opinions about where lanterns belonged.

Casper stood in the doorway a moment too long, doing arithmetic they were bad at: Ilsa Vey had been fifty-some, hollowed and wind-scoured. This girl was maybe twenty-two, broad-shouldered, dark-haired, with her sleeves rolled to the elbow and ink stains on her fingers that no chandler could explain. But the jaw was the same. The set of the mouth when it decided to be stubborn — that was the same, exactly the same, and seeing it landed in Casper's chest like a dropped anchor.

"You're blocking my door," the woman said, without turning around.

"Am I."

"Statistically. It's my door."

Casper stepped inside and let the door swing shut. "Nice shop."

"It's adequate." She came down the ladder, lantern in each hand, and looked Casper over with the frank inventory of someone who sized up customers for a living: boots, coat, the road dust of two hundred miles, the particular lean look of a person who hadn't eaten well lately. Her gaze snagged briefly on the knife at Casper's belt and moved on without alarm, which meant she'd grown up around worse. "Rope, pitch, lamp oil, or you're lost."

"Lamp oil," Casper said, because it was true, and because buying something made lying easier.

She set the lanterns down and drew oil from the barrel into a flask, and Casper watched her hands — quick, sure, scarred across the knuckles — and thought about how Ilsa Vey's hands had shaken too badly at the end to hold a map flat.

"Wren," Casper said.

The flask stopped filling. One beat. Two. Then it finished filling, and Wren Vey corked it and set it on the counter and said, in a voice gone carefully flat, "That's four coppers."

"That's not what I—"

"Four coppers," she repeated, "for the oil. And if the next thing out of your mouth is about my mother, it'll be six, because I charge extra for that."

Casper paid the four coppers. It seemed wise to establish credit before running up debt.

* * *

The trouble with Wren Vey, Casper discovered over the following hour, was that she was not grieving, and had no intention of starting.

She waited on two more customers while Casper lingered — a fisherman buying hooks who called her "Vey" like a title, and a boy of eight buying a single candle with coins counted out one at a time from a sock, whom she charged half price and lied about it fluently. When the shop emptied she turned to Casper, arms crossed, and said, "Well?"

"I knew your mother."

"No," Wren said. "You knew *of* my mother. Everyone in the tide-flats knew of Ilsa Vey. She prospected half the coast and drank in the other half. What you want is to tell me she talked about me at the end. They always want to tell me that." She said it without bitterness, which was somehow worse — the way you'd recite a shipping schedule. "It's been eleven years. You're the fourth this year. In spring it was a man who claimed she'd left me a claim stake. He wanted travel money to go collect it."

"Did you give him any?"

"Two coppers," Wren said. "For the entertainment value." She leaned against the counter. "Look. Whoever you are — and you have the look of someone whose name costs money to know, so don't tell me — she walked out when I was eleven. Left a note saying she'd send for me when she'd struck it rich. I got one letter, three years later, postmarked nowhere, saying she was close. *Close.* To what, she didn't say. So unless you've come to finish the sentence, we're done."

"She said she was sorry," Casper said. "At the end."

Something crossed Wren's face — fast, quickly shuttered, like a bird past a window. "People are sorry at the end," she said. "It's cheap. Dying makes everyone honest for about a day, and then everyone else has to live with it." She began restacking oilcloth bolts that did not need restacking. "Did she suffer?"

"Yes."

"Good," said Wren, and then, quieter, almost to herself: "No. Not good. I don't know what I mean by that. Did she say anything that wasn't sorry?"

Casper's hand rose halfway to their ribs, to the map, and stopped. Here was the whole problem laid out plain: *bring her the truth.* But the truth was a wax-sealed chart worth a fortune, hunted by a fleet, and telling this sharp-eyed stranger anything real meant painting a target on the chandlery door. Casper had survived six years by being no one. Being no one did not include standing in shops admitting things.

"She said—" Casper started, and found the sentence full of traps. *She said she loved you* was a lie; Ilsa hadn't said it. *She said nothing* was crueler than needed. What Ilsa had actually said, in the gaps between the pain, was: *Tell her the wreck is real. Tell her I wasn't chasing nothing all those years. Tell her the letter wasn't a lie.*

"She said she was close," Casper said finally. "And that it wasn't a lie."

Wren set down the oilcloth very carefully, the way people set things down when throwing them would feel better but cost more. "That's it? That's the message? Eleven years, and the estate sends a courier with *it wasn't a lie*?"

"I'm not a courier."

"No," Wren agreed, looking at Casper's boots, their knife, their face. "You're something else. Something tired." And then, because she was her mother's daughter down to the bone, she did the last thing Casper expected: she reached under the counter, came up with bread and hard cheese and a jar of pickled something, and put them on the counter between them. "Eat. You look like a strong wind would file a claim on you. Then you can tell me again, slower, and this time I'll know if you're lying, because I'm good at that. My mother taught me nothing useful except how to spot it."

* * *

Casper ate. It would have been rude not to, and besides, the cheese was good, and Casper had been living on ship's biscuit and paranoia for nine days.

They sat among the rope coils in the back of the shop, and Wren asked questions in a flat, methodical way — Where did she die. Was anyone with her. Did she have her ring, the tin one with the green stone. (Yes. No. Yes.) Each answer she took like a coin being counted into a purse, and Casper understood that she was building something out of them, some small structure to stand in, and that she'd do it whether or not Casper helped.

What Casper could not do was explain why a stranger had crossed two hundred miles of bad coast to deliver one sentence. Every honest answer led back to the map. The map led to the Meridian. The Meridian led to gold, and to Odile Marchand, and to men who burned docks to warm up before breakfast. Casper had seen what happened to people adjacent to treasure. There was a village dock on the coast road that was currently smoke and nails because of it.

So Casper did the thing they were best at, the thing six years of being no one had honed fine: they edited.

They told Wren her mother had hired them as a guide, weeks ago, on a prospecting run. That she'd been hurt in an accident — rockfall, nothing anyone did. That she'd asked Casper to carry a message south, and paid them, and that the payment was why Casper had come so far, and that the message was the whole of it: *close, and it wasn't a lie.*

Every word was nearly true. That was the trick of a good lie; it was just the truth with the expensive parts cut out. Casper had once believed they'd never be good at this. It turned out you could get good at anything if the alternative was dying.

Wren listened with her eyes half-closed, and when it was done she was quiet a long moment.

"Rockfall," she said.

"Rockfall."

"And she sent you instead of coming herself. Or sending word ahead. Eleven years of nothing and then a stranger with a message and no letter." Wren picked a bit of thread off her sleeve. "My mother couldn't write a letter?"

"The accident—" Casper began.

"Don't." Wren held up a hand. "Don't build me another excuse. I'm good at spotting those too." She stood, brushed off her skirt, and Casper braced for the door, the end of it, being shown out into the gull-haunted morning with half a promise kept and half a lie sitting in their stomach like swallowed ballast.

Instead Wren said: "You'll stay till tomorrow. There's a room above the net-mender's, I'll square it with Marta. And you'll take a proper meal, because whatever you are, you're a bad liar and a worse eater, and my mother always did collect strays." She paused at the counter, back turned. "I'm not forgiving her. Understand that. Whatever she was close to, whatever it wasn't — she still chose it over coming back. But I'll hear the message. Once. Properly. Because someone should."

She lit the lamps along the front window, one by one, and the shop filled with small warm lights, and Casper sat in the rope-smelling dark behind her feeling like the worst kind of thief — the kind who steals from people who leave the door open.

* * *

It was near midnight when Casper gave up on sleep, dressed, and slipped down to the harbor wall to breathe air that didn't smell of guilt and pickles.

The tide was out far and low, farther than it had any business being, dragging the harbor mud bare and silvered under a half moon. Casper stood looking at it with a cup of cold tea going colder in their hands, and it took an embarrassingly long time for the professional part of their brain to wake up and start screaming.

That low. On this coast. At this phase of the moon.

Casper had spent nine days memorizing a wax-sealed chart. Six tides. The third was two days away, and the wreck lay a hard day's sail east of Greyharbor, and here was the sea pulling its skirts up early, showing ankle, showing everything, like a hint dropped by a world that had gotten tired of Casper's detours.

Down the quay, a boat was tying up that hadn't been there at dusk — a long black hull, running dark, taking the harbor wall with the silent economy of a vessel that had done it many times and preferred not to be asked questions. Figures moved on her deck. One of them raised a lantern, briefly, and the light caught a face Casper knew, and had hoped never to see outside of nightmares: Sef Calloway, Odile Marchand's harpooner, here, in Greyharbor, asking a night-watchman something with a paper in his hand.

Asking, Casper would have bet the map, about strangers.

Casper set down the tea, very quietly, and went to wake Wren Vey after all — because the lie was about to get much bigger, or end entirely, and either way the girl who wouldn't grieve deserved to decide how much of it she'd survive.
