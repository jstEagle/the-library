## 6. The Third Tide Turns Against Us

The cliff road above Cutter's Reach was not a road. That was the point of it.

Casper had learned it years ago, back when they still had a name worth putting on a ledger, back when Odile Marchand used to say, *if the map says there's no path, Galloway, then you've found the only one worth taking.* The route threaded along a shelf of black stone no wider than a coffin, forty feet above water that came in gray and left in white, and it existed only for people who knew to look for the cairn shaped like a kneeling shepherd, and only in weather that wasn't trying to kill them.

The weather was trying to kill them.

Casper pressed their back to the cliff face and let the wind shove, testing it the way you'd test ice. It shoved back with opinions. Behind them, three days south, Odile's fleet would be rounding the Harrow Light by now, and ahead, two tides north, the wreck of the *Meridian* waited in its window of exposed seabed like a secret told too loud.

*Third tide,* Casper thought. *You're losing.*

They moved. Hand, boot, hand, boot—the old rhythm, the one the body remembered even when the world had forgotten the name attached to it. Below, the sea sucked at the rocks with a sound like a crowd deciding something.

The storm came in off the water an hour before noon, which was rude of it, because Casper had planned to be past the Surge Gap by then. Instead they were still on the coffin shelf when the first wave hit the cliff and came *up*—not spray, not spindrift, but a full arm of green water reaching over the ledge and closing its fist.

Casper heard it before they saw it, that deep intake like the world clearing its throat, and they flattened and gripped and the wave went over them anyway, a cold that wasn't temperature so much as *opinion*, and when the water let go, the pack was gone.

Their pack. The one with the rope, the lamp oil, the dried fish, the wax-sealed map in its oilskin sleeve—

No. Not the map. Casper had moved the map to their coat three nights ago, after the dream where Ilsa Vey stood at the foot of the bed doing her dying arithmetic out loud. The map was safe, pressed flat against their ribs like a second heartbeat.

Everything else was in the sea.

Casper sat on the wet stone and took inventory of their new life: one knife, one flint, half a loaf of bread gone soggy, the coat on their back, and boots that had been resoled twice by a man in Port Calloway who'd charged them double for not asking questions.

"Well," Casper said to the ocean.

The ocean did not apologize.

* * *

The problem with being no one, Casper had learned, was that no one helped you. Invisibility was a skill, and like all skills it had a cost, and the cost was that when you sat in the lee of a sea cave at dusk, shivering, chewing soggy bread, nobody walking the coast path looked twice. A fire would be visible. Asking would be visible. Existing, at this particular moment, was proving inconveniently visible, and Casper resented every minute of it.

That was when the boy fell out of the gorse bush.

There was no gentler way to describe it. One moment the gorse was gorse; the next it made a sound like a dropped sack of turnips and produced a child—fourteen, maybe fifteen, all elbows and salt-cracked lips, with a coil of stolen fishing line over one shoulder and an expression of pure, catastrophic guilt.

They stared at each other.

"I wasn't following you," the boy said immediately.

"You were following me."

"I was going the same direction. Independently."

Casper looked at him. Wind-burned, soaked to the knee, a bruise blooming on his shin where the gorse had won. He'd been following them since the Surge Gap—Casper had noticed the small clever footprints overlapping their own an hour back and had assumed it was one of Odile's scouts and had spent a miserable hour planning three separate escapes, all of which now seemed excessive.

"You fell out of a bush," Casper said.

"I *emerged* from cover."

"Into a turnip-sack noise."

The boy's ears went red. "You're Casper Galloway."

And there it was. Casper felt the old flinch, the one that lived in the shoulders. "You're mistaken."

"You're Casper Galloway," the boy repeated, with the terrible confidence of the young, "the guide who took the Kestrel Pass in whiteout with a broken arm and brought eleven miners down alive. The one who read the tide tables at Skerrow Point so well the harbormaster thought he was cheating. The one who—" he faltered, and his face did something complicated, "—the one from the *Wrenwater* crossing."

The *Wrenwater*. Six years ago. The rescue that went wrong. The name that had been dragged through two countries' worth of harbors until it meant *the guide who let them die*, and Casper had buried it and grown a new life on top of the grave, and here was a boy in a gorse bush holding it up like a lamp.

"That crossing," Casper said carefully, "is why I'm no one now."

"That crossing," the boy said, "is why I'm *here*." He stuck out a hand, which was a strange thing to do to a person you'd been stalking. "Tam. I stowed out of Greyharbor on a cod boat, worked my way up the coast, and I've been trying to catch you since the Surge Gap because—" he stopped, restarted, the bravado cracking to show something earnest and enormous underneath, "—because I've read everything about you. The real things, not the harbor-talk. And I figured, if I'm going to be a guide, I should learn from the best one there ever was, and the best one there ever was is sitting in a cave eating wet bread."

"I'm not a guide anymore."

"You're sitting on a cliff route that isn't on any map I've ever seen."

"That's not guiding. That's loitering at altitude."

Tam grinned like Casper had handed him a medal.

* * *

They made a fire after all—small, in the cave's throat, where the smoke went up a crevice the way smoke was supposed to, and Casper told themselves this was a tactical decision and not loneliness. Tam produced, from somewhere in his improbable coat, a tin of sardines, a stub of candle, and a fishing hook, and Casper thought: *the boy is better provisioned than I am, and I've been doing this for six years.*

That was the arithmetic that stung. Not the storm, not the lost pack. The fact that warmth had arrived uninvited, carrying sardines, and Casper's first instinct had been to send it away because warmth was visible and visible was dead.

"Where are you going?" Tam asked, around a sardine. "The real answer. I'll know if it's a lie, I've been lied to by professionals."

Casper weighed it. Every instinct said: give him nothing, stay smoke, stay rumor. But the boy had walked three days of coast to fall out of a bush at them, and the map against Casper's ribs felt heavier than it had that morning.

"North," Casper said. "To a wreck. On the sixth tide."

Tam's eyes went wide and round as moons. "The *Meridian*."

Casper went very still. "Where did you hear that name?"

"Everywhere," Tam said, "for a week. The fleet's been buying up rope and lamp oil in every port from here to the Harrow. Everyone says Captain Marchand's after a wreck nobody can find, and everyone says—" he hesitated, and for the first time looked almost afraid, "—everyone says she's got a list of people she's looking for, too. Guides. Old crew. People who might know the water up there." He looked up, and there was no hero-worship in his face now, only a plain, stubborn care that was somehow much harder to bear. "Is she looking for you?"

Casper fed the fire a stick and didn't answer, which was an answer.

"Right," Tam said. He stood, brushed off his knees, and slung his fishing line over his shoulder with the air of a soldier taking up a post. "Then you'll need someone to watch the sea while you sleep. And someone to carry things. And someone who knows which harbors ask questions, because I've been dodging harbor-masters since I was eleven and I'm *very* good at it."

"I work alone."

"You work *wet*," Tam said, gesturing at the entirety of Casper—soaked, packless, sardine-less. "You lost everything to that wave. I watched it from the gorse. It was very impressive, the wave, less so the pack."

Casper opened their mouth to explain, again, the doctrine of invisibility—the whole hard-won philosophy of being no one—and found that the words had gone strange in their mouth. Because the boy was right. The wave had taken the pack, but what it had really taken was the illusion that staying hidden was the same as staying safe. Odile knew Casper was alive. The fleet was on the water. The old tricks were spent, and every mile of silence from here on was just a slower way of drowning.

Invisibility had a cost, and Casper had been paying it for six years, and the bill had just come due in the form of a fifteen-year-old with sardines.

"Conditions," Casper said at last. "One: you do what I say on the rock, always, even if it looks stupid. *Especially* if it looks stupid. Two: if I tell you to run, you run, and you don't come back."

"That's two conditions."

"Three: you never call me 'the best guide there ever was' where anyone can hear."

Tam's whole face lit up like the Harrow Light. "So it's *yes*."

"It's a trial period."

"It's a *yes*." The boy stuck out his hand again, and this time Casper took it, and the boy's grip was fierce and cold and absolutely certain, and Casper thought: *Ilsa, you'd have laughed at this. You'd have said something about arithmetic.*

"Sleep," Casper said. "We move at first light. And Tam—"

"Aye?"

"Next time you follow someone for three days, emerge *before* they've planned your funeral."

"No promises," Tam said, grinning, and curled up against the cave wall, and within a minute was asleep with the boneless trust of someone who believed the person beside him was a legend.

Casper sat up and kept the watch, map against their ribs, fire low, and looked north into the dark where the water was already pulling back from the wreck of the *Meridian*—and where, somewhere on that same dark water, lanterns were burning on the decks of Odile Marchand's fleet.

Two tides left. One boy asleep against the wall. One name, spoken aloud for the first time in six years, still warm in the cold air of the cave.

Casper found, to their considerable surprise, that they didn't want to take it back.
