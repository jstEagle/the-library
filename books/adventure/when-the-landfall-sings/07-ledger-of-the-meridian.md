## 7. Ledger of the Meridian

The informant's name was Pellwick, and he wept the way some men sang—earnestly, at length, and with no discernible talent for it.

Odile Marchand let him cry. She had learned long ago that tears were like weather on the open water: you didn't fight them, you simply waited them out and noted what they left behind. She sat across the hold of the *Charity's Teeth* with a cup of tea going lukewarm in her hands and watched Pellwick drip onto her deck planking.

"You're ruining my floor," she said mildly.

"I'm sorry—I'm sorry, Captain, I—" He blew his nose into a rag that had seen better decades.

"Don't apologize to me. Apologize to the carpenter. He's very sensitive about his work." She sipped her tea. "Now. Start again from the part where you followed my porter through Sable Pass, and this time leave out the parts where you're brave. They slow us down."

Pellwick was one of hers—or had been, until six years ago, when he'd taken his cut of a salvage run and vanished into the tide-flats towns to drink it thin. She'd found him in a Greyharbor gutter with a bounty notice in his pocket and Casper Galloway's face inked on it, which meant someone else was also looking, and that interested Odile far more than Pellwick himself did.

"Marchand," he said—it was the only name he could manage without crying harder—"the guide you're chasing. The one from the manifest."

"There is no manifest."

"The old woman said—"

"The old woman is dead, Pellwick, and dead women say a great many things." Odile set down her cup. "Tell me what she gave him."

"A map." His voice dropped to a whisper, as though the word itself might be overheard by the sea. "A wax-sealed chart to the Meridian. And Captain—the wreck isn't just coin. I heard her talking before she died. I was in the next bunk at the waystation, playing dead, because I owed you money and I thought maybe there'd be something in it for me if I—"

"Get to it."

"She said the ship carried a sealed case. Official wax. Not cargo-manifest sealed—*government* sealed. A ledger of names." Pellwick's eyes were red-rimmed and shining with the particular light of a man who has finally found the sentence worth surviving for. "Names of port officials. Customs men. Two magistrates out of Calloway. And—" he swallowed— "and the ones who wrote up the inquiry after the Kestrel Pass rescue. The ones who signed the report that got your guide blacklisted."

The hold went very quiet. Somewhere above them, a crewman hauled on a line, and the rigging groaned its usual complaint.

Odile did not move. This was a skill, like reading water: stillness as instrument. Inside her, something turned over slowly, the way a hull turns in a swell.

"That's a story," she said.

"It's what I heard."

"Dying women embellish. It makes the dying more interesting."

"Captain." Pellwick leaned forward, chains clinking—he wasn't chained, actually; Odile had never believed in chaining informants, it made them lie worse—but he *sounded* chained, which was nearly as useful. "I sold the rest of what I know already. To a broker working for the Consortium. That's why I ran. That's why you found me running."

"And what did the Consortium pay for?"

"For confirmation. For the names." He licked his lips. "They're not sending boats to raise gold, Captain. Gold sinks and waits; gold keeps. They're sending boats because whatever's in that sealed case can hang officials—and whoever holds it owns the harbor boards from here to Calloway. The Consortium wants it *burned*. Unread. They told their captains: recover the case intact, deliver it sealed, take no copies. You don't burn paper that's only worth money."

Odile looked at him for a long moment.

Then she stood, crossed the hold, and poured him a cup of tea herself, which frightened him considerably more than shouting would have.

"Pellwick," she said, pressing the cup into his shaking hands, "you are going to tell me everything the broker said, in order, including the parts you think make you look clever. And then you are going to have dinner, and a bunk, and in the morning my bosun will put you ashore at Ternholt with enough coin to stay bought. Do you understand?"

"Yes, Captain." He stared into the tea. "Why be kind to me?"

"Because kindness is cheaper than rope and lasts longer in the memory." She turned for the ladder. "And because you've just told me the most valuable thing anyone's said aboard this ship in six years, and I don't reward that with drowning."

She climbed into the grey afternoon light feeling the deck tilt under her in a way that had nothing to do with the sea.

* * *

Her cabin was small and fiercely organized, the way cabins of people who grew up with nothing always are. Charts rolled in oilcloth by region. Instruments wrapped in wool. On the desk, weighted under a brass compass, a single sheet of paper she had read so many times the creases had gone soft as cloth.

The inquiry report. Kestrel Pass. Six years old.

She had gotten a copy through means she did not examine too closely, back when getting it had felt like an act of loyalty—proof, she'd told herself, that the world had wronged them both. She had read it a hundred times looking for the flaw, the forged line, the smoking gun that would clear Casper Galloway's name and, by extension, her own, because the same report had ended her survey contracts and closed every legitimate harbor to the *Charity's Teeth*.

And she had never found it. Not because the document was clean.

Because she had stopped looking around page four.

Page four listed the signatories. Page four named the officials who had weighed the evidence, and among those names were men who now sat on the harbor board that licensed her fleet, who set the salvage rights she bid on, whose discretion determined whether the *Charity's Teeth* was a business or a pirate ship depending on the season.

She had read page four and understood, with the cold clarity of a person raised on tide-flats, exactly why the inquiry had gone the way it went. And she had folded the report away and told herself: *the truth doesn't float. Build anyway.*

That had been her creed for six years. Truth doesn't float. Build anyway. Take the blacklisted name, take the gray flags, take the work no honest fleet wanted, and build something that couldn't be taken twice.

And now a weeping man in her hold was telling her that the truth had been sitting in a drowned ship the whole time, sealed in government wax, waiting for a low tide and a stubborn porter to come collect it.

Odile laughed—one short, unlovely bark—and then pressed her hand over her mouth as if the sound might escape and be reported.

* * *

The trouble, she thought, pacing the length of her cabin while the lamplight swung, was that she knew exactly how this went. She had watched it happen to other captains. A captain finds leverage—a manifest, a ledger, a name—and for one bright season they own the harbor boards. And then the boards find a bigger lever, or a younger captain, or a rope, and the leverage becomes a noose with better upholstery. Power borrowed from corrupt men is not power. It is debt.

But oh, the things she could do with one season.

She could get the fleet's papers legitimized. She could buy out the two Consortium contracts that strangled her margins. She could—her mind supplied this image unbidden, treacherous as a rogue wave—walk into a harbor office under her own name, the name from before, Marta Marchand of the tide-flats, and watch a clerk look up at her without fear or calculation.

No. She shut that door. That name was dead and deserved to be; it had been soft, and softness was what the pass rescue had punished. Casper had been soft, once. That was the whole tragedy of Casper Galloway in a single word, and Odile had spent six years being grateful she'd been cured of it early.

Except.

Except that if Pellwick was right—if the Consortium wanted the case burned unread—then the Consortium already knew what was in it. Which meant the officials named in it knew. Which meant the men who framed a mountain guide to bury whatever the Kestrel Pass expedition had actually found were the same men who fed her fleet its contracts, and every coin she'd earned in six years had passed through the hands of the people who ruined—

Who ruined *Casper*.

She stopped pacing.

There it was, the thing she'd been circling all evening like a gull over offal. Her anger had always had a strange architecture: fury at Casper for the choices on the pass, fury at the inquiry for the verdict, and underneath both, load-bearing and unexamined, a furious certainty that the verdict was *wrong*, that the report read like a document written backward from a conclusion, that Casper Galloway—cautious, maddening, rule-quoting Casper, who checked knots twice and counted heads three times—had not done what the report said Casper did.

She had buried that certainty because it was useless. Useless things got crews killed.

It was not useless anymore. Now it was worth a harbor board.

Odile sat down at her desk and pulled the report from beneath the compass and read page four again, properly this time, the way she should have six years ago—not as a wound but as a chart. Names. Positions. Signatures. Two of the signatories held seats on the Consortium's governing ring. One of them had personally approved her last three salvage licenses.

They weren't sending ships to sink a map. They were sending ships to drown the past before it learned to swim.

And her orders—she took them from the drawer, heavy cream paper, the Consortium seal in green wax—said, in that bland bureaucratic dialect she'd come to loathe: *recover sealed case intact, deliver unopened, no copies, no exceptions.*

Unopened. As if she wouldn't look. As if she were the kind of captain who hauled treasure across six tides and never once peeked under the wax.

"They think I'm obedient," she said aloud to the empty cabin, and the cabin, wisely, agreed.

* * *

Near midnight she went up to the stern rail, as she did when the thinking got too loud below decks. The sea was black glass under a half moon. Behind them, far off, the lights of Port Calloway had finally sunk below the horizon curve, and ahead—she checked the chart in her head, third time tonight—ahead lay the coast road, the cliff routes, the drowned shelf where the Meridian waited with its lungs full of sand.

Somewhere along that coast was Casper Galloway. Alive. Moving. Carrying a dead woman's map toward a wreck that held either a fortune or a gallows, depending on which page you read first.

Odile had planned this chase like a hunt: intercept the porter, take the map, raise the case, collect the fee. Clean arithmetic. But arithmetic required knowing what you were counting, and until tonight she had been counting the wrong thing.

If she delivered the case sealed, she stayed owned. If she opened it, she became the most dangerous woman on the coast—which was flattering, briefly, and then fatal, historically. If she burned it, she did the Consortium's work for free and spent the rest of her life wondering what her silence had purchased.

Or.

Or she could do the thing no one had ordered, the thing with no column in any ledger: she could find Casper Galloway first, and ask the question she had carried since Kestrel Pass, the one that had nothing to do with gold or manifests.

*What really happened on the pass?*

Because Odile had begun to suspect—standing at the rail with the wind pulling at her coat—that the answer mattered to her more than the money, and that this suspicion was the most dangerous thing aboard her ship.

"Bosun," she called.

A shape detached itself from the shadows by the wheel. "Captain?"

"Change our heading. Northeast by the cliff lights. And wake the fast boat's crew—we go quiet from here."

"Quiet, Captain? We expecting company?"

Odile looked north, toward the dark coastline where a guide who checked knots twice was surely moving through the night with a dead woman's promise in their pocket.

"No," she said. "We're paying a visit."

Behind them, the wake curled away like a signature on a page that hadn't dried yet.
