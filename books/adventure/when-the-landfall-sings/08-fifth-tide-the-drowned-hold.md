## 8. Fifth Tide: The Drowned Hold

The sea gave them forty minutes.

Casper had counted it three times the night before, chalk marks on the slate in the fisherman's hut they'd borrowed above the cove, and three times the arithmetic came out the same: slack water at the fifth low tide would hold the Meridian's spine just clear of the swell for a little less than an hour, and then the ocean would take her back and keep her for one more tide. One more tide was all Odile Marchand needed to arrive with everything she had.

"You're making that face again," Tam said. He was sitting on the shingle with his boots off, mending a torn strap with fishing line, because Casper had learned that if you gave the boy a task he stopped asking questions for almost four minutes at a stretch. "The face like you're doing sums nobody wants the answer to."

"I'm always doing sums."

"Yeah, but usually they come out. This one's got that look of long division." Tam bit the line clean and held up his work. "There. Good as new. Better, probably. I've improved it."

"It's a strap."

"I've *improved* it."

Casper almost smiled. That was the trouble with Tam — he made smiling feel possible, and possible things were dangerous when you'd spent six years training yourself out of them. Casper checked the dive belt a third time, though every buckle was already seated, and looked down at the cove where the wreck's black ribs broke the surface like the fingers of something that had given up waving.

"Rules," Casper said. "You stay on the beach. You don't come into the water. If any boat comes around the headland — any boat, even one with a friendly flag, especially one with a friendly flag — you take the pack and you go up the goat track and you don't wait for me."

"And if you don't come back?"

"Then you deliver yourself somewhere with walls and a stove and you stop following guides around."

Tam looked at the sea, then at Casper, and did not say anything, which from Tam was a whole speech.

* * *

The water was colder than the map had promised, or maybe Casper had just gotten soft in the years since the last time they'd gone down into a drowned thing. The Meridian lay on her starboard side, half-buried in sand, her hull furred green with six years of weed. Fish scattered from the shadow of Casper's body like startled coins. The guide-lines from the old salvage charts still hung in places, rotted to threads, and Casper followed the memory of them instead — down past the broken rail, along the deck that now stood vertical as a wall, into the dark of the hold where the light went from grey to nothing.

Ilsa Vey had drawn the strongbox location from memory, and dying people's memories are either perfect or worthless with no middle ground. Casper felt along the bulkhead by touch, counting frames the way Ilsa's map had counted them — seven, eight, nine — and there, wedged behind a collapsed crate of what had once been tinned peaches, the iron box, small enough to carry, heavy enough to mean something.

It took ten minutes and both hands and a length of pipe for leverage to work it free. When it finally came loose, Casper floated there in the black water hugging it, heart hammering, and thought: *this is either my name back or the end of me, and the box doesn't care which.*

Above, through thirty feet of green-lit water, the surface had begun to look wrong.

* * *

Casper broke the surface gasping and knew before their eyes cleared. The light on the water had changed — gone hard and metallic — and there were two boats rounding the southern headland, low, black-hulled, running without lights in broad daylight because they didn't intend to be seen until it no longer mattered.

Salvage fleet hulls. Casper knew their lines the way you know the handwriting of someone who taught you to write.

"TAM!" The shout tore across the cove. "Go! Now!"

But the boy wasn't moving. He was standing at the tideline, pack forgotten at his feet, staring up the beach — where a third boat had already landed, beached neatly on the shingle, and three figures were walking down toward him with the unhurried confidence of people who have never once been told no by the sea.

Casper swam for it, dragging the box, which pulled like a regret. By the time their feet found gravel, the tallest of the three had a hand on Tam's shoulder, and Tam — fool, brave idiot — had gone rigid rather than run, because running would leave Casper alone here, and that was the arithmetic the boy had done without being asked.

"Ah," said the tall man, pleasantly. "There she is. Or he. Or whatever we're calling it this year." He smiled at Casper with the specific warmth of a knife being shown its sheath. "Captain said you'd come at the fifth tide. She knows you, Galloway. Says you count like she counts."

"Let the boy go."

"He's not the one holding anything worth letting him go over." The man nodded at the strongbox under Casper's arm, seawater streaming off it onto the shingle. "That, though. Captain wants that. And Captain Marchand gets what Captain Marchand wants, or else she burns down whatever's nearest to it." He glanced at Tam. "Present company included, eventually, if we have to wait."

Tam's jaw was set. His eyes found Casper's, and there was no fear in them, which was worse than fear — there was *trust*, vast and unearned and entirely misplaced, the trust of someone who believed legends could swim.

Six years. Six years of being no one, of crossing borders like smoke, of keeping every promise small enough to keep. And here was the whole ledger of it come due at once: the box in one arm, the boy in the other man's grip, and the tide already beginning to turn, the sea sliding back in over the wreck's ribs with the patience of something that has never lost an argument.

Casper had exactly one thing Odile wanted. Odile had exactly one thing Casper couldn't lose.

The mathematics were ugly and simple, and Casper hated them, and did them anyway.

"Take your hand off him," Casper said, and waded up the shingle, and held out the map — wax-sealed, salt-stained, Ilsa Vey's dying handwriting folded inside it like a heart in a ribcage. "The chart. The original. It's yours. Let him walk away first."

The tall man's smile widened. He took the map with two fingers, delicately, the way you'd accept a bribe. Behind him, one of the others whistled a signal toward the headland, and far out, answering, a ship's horn sounded — deep, patient, familiar in a way that made Casper's stomach drop through the soles of their boots.

She hadn't sent boats. She had come herself.

The man released Tam's shoulder with a little push, almost kind. Tam stumbled back and stood there breathing hard, glaring at the box in Casper's arm with naked betrayal, because of course he'd seen it, of course he understood instantly what had been traded and what hadn't.

"You kept the box," he said.

"The map was the price of you."

"But the box is the *gold*—"

"The box," Casper said quietly, "is mine to open. Go get the pack."

And Tam went, muttering, alive, furious, safe — and Casper stood on the shingle watching the flagship come around the headland with Odile Marchand's colors at her mast, and understood that the choice hadn't saved anything yet. It had only bought the next hour. Somewhere inside that iron box was a manifest that could clear a name six years buried, and somewhere aboard that ship was the only person alive who remembered who Casper Galloway had been before the world decided otherwise.

The tide slid in over the Meridian's ribs, right on schedule, and closed over the drowned hold like a book shutting.

Odile's launch touched the beach.

She stepped out before it grounded, boots hitting the shallows, and looked at Casper across twenty feet of wet gravel with an expression that contained, in equal measure, six years of grievance and something older and more dangerous than grievance.

"Casper," she said. "You look terrible."

"You look rich."

"Only one of us can fix that today." Her eyes dropped to the strongbox. "Open it."

"Not here. Not for you."

"Casper." Almost gently. "I burned a dock in Corvane for a rumor of that map. What do you think I'll do for the thing itself?"

Behind Casper, Tam had gone very still by the pack. The tide kept coming in. And Casper, arms wrapped around the iron box, felt the strangest thing rise up through the fear — not courage exactly, but its poor cousin, stubbornness, the same muscle that had carried them across six tides and two countries and one impossible promise.

"Then we'd better talk," Casper said, "before the sixth tide comes in. Because what's in this box isn't gold, Odile. It's names. And one of them is yours."
