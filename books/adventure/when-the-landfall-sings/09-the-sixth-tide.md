## 9. The Sixth Tide

*Odile*

The sea gave up the Meridian like a miser parting with a coin it had counted for years.

Odile Marchand stood at the rail of the *Cormorant's Pride* and watched the black ribs of her surface through the retreating water—first the masts, snapped and leaning like drunks against a fence, then the deckhouse, then the long green-slicked spine of the hull itself. Six tides she had chased this wreck across two hundred miles of coast. Six tides of bad weather, worse informants, and one ghost in guide's boots who kept slipping through country Odile had taught him to read.

She did not think of Casper Galloway as a ghost. Ghosts were dead things. Casper was something more irritating: a debt.

"Shingle's clear," called Brannic from the launch, wading the last few yards with the strongbox line over his shoulder. "No sign of the boy. No sign of anyone."

"No sign," Odile repeated softly. That was what bothered her. A beach with no sign of anyone was either empty or very carefully cleaned, and only one kind of person cleaned a beach.

The strongbox came up dripping, crusted in barnacle and six years of cold patience. It was smaller than the informant's description had promised, which meant either the description lied or the box had been opened before. Odile ran her thumb along the seam and found the answer waiting there like a knife left hilt-out: the wax seal broken, cleanly, recently, by someone who knew locks the way priests knew psalms.

Her crew gathered on the drowned deck, boots squelching, breath steaming in the gray air. They expected gold. She could feel their expectation the way you feel heat off a stove—the whole fleet's wages, the whole rotten year, sitting in a salt-crusted box at her feet.

Odile lifted the lid.

Inside: not gold. Not coin, not bar, not the glittering arithmetic that had bought her backers' patience and Brannic's loyalty and six tides of pursuit. Inside was paper—a single oilskin-wrapped packet, gone—and beneath where the packet had lain, weighted down with a piece of green sea-glass, a note in a hand she recognized the way you recognize your own reflection in someone else's face.

*The truth was the treasure,* it said. *You always taught me to check the manifest first.*

Below it, smaller, almost gentle: *The tide turns in forty minutes. I'll be on the old pilot stairs if you'd rather talk than shoot.*

Odile read it twice. Then she laughed—one short, ugly bark that made her crew step back—and folded the note into her coat, into the pocket where she kept the things she couldn't decide about.

"Captain?" Brannic said. "Where's the gold?"

"The gold," Odile said, "is being carried up a cliff by the only person alive who knows these rocks better than I do." She looked out at the water, already creeping back over the wreck's bones, patient as everything else in the world. "Get the boats ready. And Brannic—if anyone asks what we found, they found nothing. Nothing is a thing we can sell."

*Casper*

The pilot stairs were older than Greyharbor, older than the Meridian, older probably than the idea of harbors—forty-one steps cut into the cliff by hands that had never heard of insurance or salvage law. Casper climbed them slowly, because there was no reason to hurry anymore, and sat down on the top step with the manifest wrapped against their chest and the wind coming off the water like a rumor.

Forty minutes. Maybe less. Maybe Odile would send bullets instead of conversation; that had always been an option between them, though in the old days it had been a joke first.

Tam appeared out of the gorse, breathless, sandy, gloriously un-kidnapped. "They didn't see me! I went to ground under the fishing rack like you said, and Brannic walked right past me, close enough I could've tied his bootlaces together."

"Tempting?"

"I thought about it." Tam dropped down beside them, legs dangling over the drop, entirely too comfortable for a boy who'd been a hostage four hours ago. "Is it true? What they're saying below—that the box was empty?"

"It wasn't empty. It just wasn't full of what they wanted." Casper patted the oilskin packet. "It was full of this."

"What is it?"

"A list of names." Casper watched the boats below, small and busy as beetles. "Some of them are mine. Some of them belong to people who decided six years ago that a mountain rescue gone wrong needed a villain, and picked me because I was cheap and far from home. This list proves I didn't do what they say I did." A pause. "It also proves some other things. About some other people. People whose names I'm guessing Odile Marchand would very much like to own instead of explain."

Tam was quiet for a moment, which for Tam was roughly equivalent to a monastic vow of silence. Then: "Are you going to give it to her?"

"That depends," Casper said, "on whether she comes up those stairs holding a pistol or a memory."

"And if it's both?"

Casper smiled despite themselves. "Then we'll negotiate the order."

Footsteps on stone. Not hurrying. Odile Marchand had never once hurried in her life; she considered hurry a form of lying. She came up the last steps with her coat collar turned up and her hands visible—empty, deliberately, theatrically empty—and stopped three steps down, which put her eyes exactly level with Casper's, which Casper suspected was not an accident.

"You checked the manifest first," she said.

"You wrote the manual."

"I wrote *dozens* of rules. You broke most of them. It's oddly fitting that you'd keep that one." She looked past Casper at the horizon, at the water swallowing the wreck again. "Six years. I spent six years telling myself you were dead, because the alternative was admitting my best student was out there being better at hiding than I ever was at finding."

"I had good teachers."

"Don't flatter me, Galloway. It makes me want to shoot you and I've promised myself I won't do that before lunch." But there was no gun in her voice, or if there was, it was pointed somewhere internal. She sat down on the step above Tam, who scooted over with the wariness of a boy who'd heard stories. "The fleet wants that packet burned. My backers want it burned. There are men in Port Calloway who would pay me a year's takings to watch the smoke."

"And you want leverage," Casper said. "I know. You told me once: information is the only cargo that never sinks."

"I also told you once that the mountain doesn't care who's right. Only who comes down." Odile held out her hand—not grabbing, offering. "Give me the page with my name on it, and I'll let the rest go to Greyharbor. To the daughter. To whatever it is you think you're doing that isn't selling."

Casper looked at the hand. Small, scarred across the knuckles from a rope burn Casper had personally witnessed, eleven years ago, on a route neither of them should have survived. The hand that had pulled them up.

"If I give you your page," Casper said slowly, "you'll still be able to bury mine. Burn the rest. Tell the fleet nothing was found."

"Yes."

"And you'll spend the rest of your life knowing I have proof you were part of the frame, and I'll spend mine knowing you chose not to use yours."

Odile's mouth twitched. "That's generally how truces work. Badly, and with paperwork."

"Mom used to say something like that," Tam offered.

Both of them looked at him. He shrugged. "What? She did."

A long moment passed, filled with wind and the sound of the sixth tide coming home. Then Odile laughed—really laughed, the rusty, reluctant laugh that Casper had heard maybe five times in eleven years and had privately missed more than any coastline.

"You trained the boy to interrupt captains. That alone deserves hanging." She flexed her fingers. "Well, Galloway? The tide won't wait and neither will my crew's patience, which is shorter than the tide and considerably more dangerous."

Casper unwrapped the oilskin. The pages inside were soft with damp but legible, every name standing in its ink like a passenger waiting to disembark. Casper found the one near the bottom—*O. Marchand, witness attestation, dated the winter after*—and tore along the fold, clean, the way you tear anything when you've finally decided it needs tearing.

"One page," Casper said. "For one silence. And Odile—" they met her eyes, and for a moment the cliff, the fleet, the six tides all fell away and it was just two people on a mountain that had become a sea, "—the rescue. Whatever you remember happening up there. It isn't what happened. Someday, when you can stand hearing it, I'll tell you what actually did. Not for the blacklist. Just so one of us remembers it right."

Odile took the page. She read it once, her face doing something complicated and private, and then she folded it away into the same pocket as the note.

"Someday," she said, in a voice that meant *not today, and possibly never, but I heard you.* She stood, brushed off her coat, and became Captain Marchand again the way a stage becomes a world. "My crew believes the hold was empty. Let's keep them believing it. You have until the tide covers the stairs—after that, I hunt you properly, because reputation." She paused at the top step, back turned. "Galloway. The girl in Greyharbor. Her mother died believing you'd come."

"I know," Casper said. "That's why I'm going."

Odile nodded once, sharply, and went down the stairs without looking back—which, Casper thought, watching her go, was the closest thing to a blessing she knew how to give.

Tam let out a breath he'd apparently been holding since the fishing rack. "So... we won?"

Casper tucked the manifest back against their chest and stood, knees cracking, feeling every one of the six tides in their bones—and feeling, underneath the exhaustion, something unfamiliar and warm, like a lamp lit in a room they'd thought was empty.

"We survived," Casper said. "Winning's in Greyharbor. Come on—we've got a promise to finish keeping, and I intend to arrive with the truth, the whole truth, including the parts that don't make me look brave."

Tam grinned and scrambled ahead. Below them, the sea closed over the Meridian for the last time, keeping its gold, letting its names go free.
