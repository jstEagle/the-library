## 10. Landfall

The sixth tide came in like a guest who had been kept waiting too long.

Casper stood on the drowned deck of the *Meridian*, water to their knees and rising, and watched Odile Marchand decide whether to kill them.

She held the manifest in one hand—water-stained, salt-bled, the ink of six years of official rot still legible where it mattered—and her knife in the other, though the knife hung loose, the way a tool hangs when its owner has already put it away in her head. Behind her, on the shingle, Tam sat wrapped in a salvage tarp between two of her crew, alive, furious, and loudly negotiating for a better seat. The boy had a gift for that. Casper intended to encourage it.

"You read it," Odile said. Not a question. The wind off the flats pulled at her grey braid, and for a moment she looked less like the woman who burned docks and more like the woman who had once taught a nineteen-year-old how to read a snowfield before dawn: patient, exacting, tired in a way she'd never admit.

"I read it," Casper said. "Your name's on page four."

"I know what's on page four." Her jaw worked. "I've known since Port Calloway. Why do you think my backers wanted this thing at the bottom of the sea?"

"Because it names them."

"Because it names *me*." She said it flatly, without drama, the way you'd report a depth sounding. "Marchand, O., vessel's officer of record, complicit in falsification of the Galloway incident report. I signed it, Casper. I was told to sign it, and I did, because the alternative was losing the fleet, and I chose the fleet." She looked out at the water climbing the deck seams. "I've spent eleven years telling myself that was arithmetic."

"It wasn't," Casper said. "It was fear with better bookkeeping."

Odile laughed—a short, startled sound, as if the laugh had escaped without clearance. Then the knife came up, and Casper's heart performed a small undignified maneuver.

But she didn't point it. She turned it in her hand and offered it hilt-first, which from Odile Marchand was roughly equivalent to weeping.

"Page four," she said. "Tear it out. Give it to me. You keep the rest—the names, the truth, all of it. In exchange, I never saw you. My fleet never saw you. The boy goes home dry. And when the harbormasters ask why the *Vigilant's Wake* turned back to port with an empty hold, they'll get a story so boring they'll apologize for asking."

Casper took the knife. Tore the page along the old fold, careful, the way you tear anything that has waited a long time. Handed it over.

Odile folded it small and put it inside her coat, against the chest, where people keep the things they can't explain. "You could have sold the whole manifest," she said. "Names like these? Greyharbor would have paid you a house. Two houses."

"A dying woman asked me for something cheaper than that."

"Ilsa Vey." Odile said the name like a word she'd been chewing for years. "The prospector. She's the one who gave you the map."

"She traded it. For a promise."

"And you kept it." Odile shook her head slowly. "Eleven years ago you made one promise—to bring those climbers home—and the mountain broke it for you, and two countries decided you were the kind of person who breaks promises. And now you've rowed through hell to keep one made by a stranger bleeding out on tide-flats." She stepped back as the water reached her thighs. "Do you know what I'm going to do with your page, Galloway?"

"No."

"Neither do I." She turned toward her boat. "That's the first honest thing I've felt in a decade. Try not to drown."

Tam whooped from the shingle—actually whooped, no sense of occasion whatsoever—as Odile's crew shoved the skiff free, and then the boy was wading out to Casper through the rising tide, holding the tarp like a cape, talking before his feet touched the deck.

"She let us GO. Did she—wait, did you give her something? What did you give her? Was it gold? It wasn't gold, there's no gold, I checked the strongbox twice—"

"Tam."

"—because if there WAS gold and you gave it away I want you to know I support you emotionally but I also have questions—"

"*Tam.*" Casper put a hand on the boy's shoulder, and the boy went quiet, glowing. "Help me bail the boat. We're going to Greyharbor."

"To deliver the map's truth," Tam said solemnly, as if reciting scripture he'd memorized without permission.

"To tell a daughter about her mother." Casper looked south, where the coast bent toward Greyharbor's lights. "And yes. That too."

* * *

They reached Greyharbor on the evening of the seventh day, smelling of salt and honest labor, and Casper stood outside Vey Chandlery for the better part of an hour rehearsing sentences and discarding all of them.

The shop smelled of tar and hemp and lamp oil. Wren Vey was behind the counter coiling rope with the brisk violence of someone who had opinions about knots, and she was—Ilsa's face, thirty years younger, with the same stubborn set to the mouth. She saw Casper and her eyes narrowed.

"You again. The porter who wouldn't take payment." She dropped the coil. "You said you had a message. You said half a sentence and left. I've had nine days to imagine every terrible version of it, so whatever it is, say it, because the waiting is worse than anything you've got."

"It isn't good news," Casper said. "Your mother is dead."

Wren didn't move. The lamp behind her hissed. "I'd worked that out," she said finally, quieter. "You had her look. People who are lying about being sorry don't look like that."

"She was gut-shot. Prospecting accident, or the men who follow prospectors—it was hard to tell, and she didn't linger on it. She had maybe an hour. She spent it giving me a map and making me promise." Casper set the wax-sealed packet on the counter between them. "Not to sell it. To bring it to you. I promised a dying stranger, which is either the stupidest contract I've ever signed or the only honest one."

Wren didn't touch it. "She abandoned me when I was twelve. Whatever's in there, it doesn't change that."

"No," Casper agreed. "It doesn't. But I'm not here to pretend it does, and I'm not here to make her noble, because she wasn't, entirely, and neither am I. So here's the whole truth, ugly parts included, because you deserve the ugly parts more than anyone."

And Casper told it. All of it. The waystation and the blood on the floorboards; the six tides; the salvage fleet and the woman who had trained them and betrayed them both in different decades; the storm surge and the boy stowaway; the drowned hold and the choice on the shingle beach—how Casper had handed over the map to save a child they'd known four days, and torn one page from the manifest for a woman who needed it more than the truth needed completeness.

"The manifest named officials," Casper said. "Men who framed me eleven years ago and paid your mother's world to stay quiet about it. There's a fortune's worth of leverage in those pages. I could have bought back my name twice over. Instead—" they tapped the packet "—this is yours. All of it. Because a woman who was bad at loving you was, apparently, very good at trusting a stranger, and I've decided to be flattered instead of insulted."

Wren stared at the packet for a long time. When she finally picked it up, her hands weren't steady.

"She used to say I'd understand when I was older," Wren said. "I decided that was a coward's excuse. Now I find out she was running from men who burn docks." Her thumb moved over the broken wax seal. "She sent it to me. Not to a buyer, not to some official who'd have paid her. To me. She knew what it was worth, and she sent it to the one person she thought wouldn't sell it."

"Is she right?"

Wren looked up, and for the first time her mother's stubborn mouth almost smiled. "Ask me tomorrow. Tonight I'm going to sit in the back room and read every word and cry into a rag like a respectable chandler's daughter." She set the packet down and squared her shoulders. "But I'll tell you now what I already know. She meant something by it. Maybe she was a coward and a deserter and a dozen other things I've got a right to be angry about forever. But she meant *something*, and she trusted it to the right hands, because you're standing here having given away a fortune to keep a promise to a dead woman." She stuck out her hand. "Wren Vey. Apparently I have a mother's estate to go through. You're going to help."

"That wasn't part of the—"

"It's part of this one. Shake."

Casper shook. It was, they reflected later, the second-best contract they ever signed.

* * *

Spring came to Greyharbor the way spring comes to coast towns: sideways, with complaints.

The manifest did its slow work. Names surfaced in the harbor gazette, then in the capital papers; two harbormasters resigned with great dignity and no explanation; a third did not resign, which told everyone everything. Casper's name appeared in none of it directly—Wren had insisted on that, redacting with a chandler's precision—but enough of the frame showed through the gaps that the Sable Passes guide registry quietly, without ceremony or apology, un-blacklisted one C. Galloway. The letter informing them was three sentences long and managed to convey regret without admitting error, which Casper framed anyway, mostly to annoy Tam.

Because Tam had stayed. Ostensibly as Wren's shop apprentice, actually as Casper's shadow, asking questions from dawn until the moment sleep ambushed him mid-sentence. He had a head for routes and a stomach for weather and a talent for finding trouble that Casper recognized with the weary affection of a person looking into a mirror twenty years deep.

So when the letter came from the coastal survey office—*seeking licensed guide, cliff and tidewater routes, references required*—Casper signed it **Casper Galloway** in full, in ink, for the first time in eleven years, and walked it down to the harbor office past the fish stalls and the rope sellers and the gulls conducting their eternal racket.

"You're grinning," Tam said, falling into step. "People will talk."

"Let them."

"You're *whistling*. Casper Galloway is whistling in public. I'm writing this down for posterity."

"Posterity can mind its own business." Casper handed the letter across the harbor office counter, watched the clerk read the name, glance up, and—here it was, the moment, the small terrifying hinge of it—simply nod. No flinch. No whispered conference. Just a nod, and a stamp, and *"Start Monday, the season's short."*

Outside, the tide was turning, the flats shining like hammered tin all the way to the horizon. Somewhere out under that water lay a wreck with an empty strongbox and a story that belonged to other people now. Somewhere north, a woman with a torn page against her chest was deciding, tide by tide, who she wanted to be when the fleet stopped needing her. Casper found they wished her well, which was new, and meant to keep it.

"Well?" Tam was bouncing on his heels. "First job under your own name. Where are we guiding?"

Casper looked at the boy, at the water, at the town that had, without ceremony, made room.

"Anywhere you like," they said. "We're on the chart now."
