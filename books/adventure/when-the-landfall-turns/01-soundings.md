## 1. Soundings

The river told you things in the dry season that it kept to itself the rest of the year.

Wren Galloway stood at the wheel of the *Marguerite* with one boot hooked on the rail bracket and both hands loose on the spokes, reading the water the way other people read a morning paper. Low water was honest water. It showed you its bones — the sandbars it had been hiding all spring, the drowned fence posts of a farm nobody remembered, the pale shoulder of rock off Cutter's Bend that could open a hull like a tin of sardines if you got greedy with your line. In March you could run the Serrano half asleep. In September you ran it like a prayer, or you didn't run it at all.

Wren ran it like arithmetic.

*Six inches over the bar at Miller's Shoal,* they thought, watching the current wrinkle brown and smooth over a spot where last April a barge twice their tonnage had grounded for two days. *Drop to the middle channel past the willow snag, then hug the east bank where the clay holds the depth.* Ten years on this river and the numbers lived somewhere below thought, in the hands and the eyes, the way a musician's fingers know a scale without being asked.

"Sandbar's moved again," Tam called from the bow, where he stood with a sounding pole like a man playing an instrument only he could hear. He was nineteen, all elbows and opinions, and he'd learned the pole from Wren himself, which meant he now announced every finding as though he'd invented the practice. "Two boat-lengths south of where the chart says."

"Charts are rumors," Wren said. "The river's the truth. Call it."

"Four feet," Tam said, driving the pole down. "Five. Six." He looked back at the wheelhouse, grinning. "We could take her through at full steam."

"We could also walk into Port Calloway wearing our good boots through a hog pen. Doesn't mean we do it." Wren eased the throttle back a notch and felt the old diesel settle into its workaday rhythm, a sound so familiar it had stopped registering years ago, the way you stop hearing your own heartbeat. "Half steam till we're past the shoal. Then you can brag to Bex."

"I'm going to brag to Bex anyway."

"You're going to help Bex peel potatoes is what you're going to do, once we make the bend."

Tam went back to his pole, muttering something about captains and tyranny, and Wren let the smile come and go before anyone could see it. That was the arrangement, more or less. The crew teased; the captain pretended not to be pleased; the river carried them all forward without comment.

The *Marguerite* was sixty feet of riveted stubbornness, built before the war for a timber company that no longer existed, bought by Wren eight years ago with a loan that had taken five more years to kill. She hauled freight when there was freight, passengers when there were passengers, and in the low-water season, when the big boats tied up and waited, she did the runs nobody else would attempt — mail to the upriver settlements, medicine to the mission at Two Forks, whatever odd cargo the delta towns needed moved while the Serrano lay thin as a rope left out in the sun.

It wasn't glory. Glory didn't pay for engine parts. But it was steady, and steady was the thing Wren trusted above all else, because steady was the thing the river taught.

* * *

Odile found them at noon, which meant she'd climbed out of the engine room, which meant either the engine was fine or it was fine enough to leave alone for ten minutes, and either way she came up wiping her hands on a rag that had been black so long it might have started life as a different color entirely.

"She's running hot on the starboard bank," Odile said. She was fifty-something and built like the machinery she loved, compact and dense, with forearms that could turn a seized bolt by hand and a face that gave away nothing except when the engine talked to her, and then it gave away everything. "Nothing to worry about today. Something to worry about by November."

"How much something?"

"A new water pump, maybe. Maybe just gaskets, if I baby her." She squinted astern, as if the problem might be visible in the wake. "You know what I'd rather have?"

"A new engine."

"A new engine," Odile agreed. "Or a new boat. Or a winning lottery ticket. I'm flexible."

Wren had heard this conversation, in one form or another, every season for six years. The *Marguerite*'s engine was older than any of them and had opinions accordingly. Every year Wren put money aside for the replacement that never quite arrived, because every year something smaller and more urgent ate the fund — a patched hull, a new anchor chain, Tam's winter coat, Bex's doctor bills for her mother down in the delta. The money went where the need was. That was how a boat worked. That was how a family worked, though nobody aboard used that word, and Wren least of all.

"Baby her," Wren said. "We'll get through the season."

"We always get through the season." Odile said it without complaint, which somehow made it worse. She lingered at the rail a moment, looking at the banks sliding past — the high clay cuts gone cracked and pale, the exposed roots of trees that spent most of the year standing in water. "Lowest I've seen it in four years," she said. "Old man Ferro at the relay station says the monsoon's coming late but heavy. Says the mountains already have snowmelt backed up."

"Ferro says a lot of things."

"Ferro's been right more than the almanac has." She shrugged and headed back below, and Wren watched her go and thought, not for the first time, that Odile deserved an engine that didn't talk back, and that Wren Galloway deserved — 

And there the thought stopped, the way it always stopped, at the edge of the hole where a sentence should have been. *Deserved what?* A house? Wren hadn't slept three consecutive nights ashore in a decade. People? Wren had people; they were currently peeling potatoes and arguing with a water pump. What was the word for the thing that was missing when nothing was missing?

Wren turned back to the wheel and didn't finish the thought. There was a sandbar ahead that needed finishing instead.

* * *

Bex served dinner on the aft deck because the galley was too small for four people and one of them was Tam, who ate the way a woodstove burns — constantly, messily, with visible satisfaction. Rice and river fish and the pickled vegetables Bex made in jars that lined the passageway like ammunition. She was small and quick and had come aboard three years ago fleeing a restaurant job in Port Calloway that she described, variously, as "a crime scene" and "a marriage I barely survived," and she cooked for a crew of four the way some people pray: daily, sincerely, and with an assumption that someone was listening.

"Tam says we nearly died today," Bex said, ladling out fish.

"Tam says a lot of things."

"There was a sandbar," Tam said, wounded. "A living, breathing sandbar, right in the channel, and the captain threaded it like — "

"The sandbar was exactly where sandbars are," Wren said. "In the water."

"Like a knife through butter," Tam finished, undeterred.

Bex laughed and sat down cross-legged on the deck plating with her own plate, and for a while there was just the sound of eating and the river talking to itself along the hull, that low continuous commentary it never stopped making, even at anchor, even asleep. The sun went down behind the western bluffs in a hurry, the way it did this time of year, and the first stars came out over the water like sparks off a grinder.

It was, Wren thought, a good evening. It was, Wren thought, the same evening they'd had four hundred times, and would have four hundred more, and the thought should have been comfortable and instead sat in the chest like a stone swallowed wrong.

Ten years of miles. Wren had tried once, drunk on New Year's rum, to add them up — the round trips, the detours, the distance covered hauling other people's cargo past other people's houses with lights in the windows. The number had come out enormous and meaningless, like counting the grains in a sandbar. Miles weren't years. Miles didn't accumulate into anything. You couldn't stand on a pile of miles and look back and see a life; you just saw more river.

"Captain's brooding," Tam announced.

"Captain's navigating tomorrow's route in advance," Wren said.

"In your head?"

"It's a big head."

Bex snorted into her rice. Odile, who had come up for air and food, said, "Leave them alone. Brooding's free. Everything else on this boat costs money."

They laughed, and Wren laughed too, and the stone in the chest shifted but didn't move, and the *Marguerite* ran on down the darkening river toward Port Calloway with her running lights doubled up against the dark, because low-water season was also smuggler season, and trust was a cargo you didn't carry after dark.

* * *

Port Calloway smelled the way it always smelled — diesel and fish sauce and the green rot of the market district — and sounded the way it always sounded, which was louder than any place had a right to be at six in the morning. Wren took the *Marguerite* in on the ebb tide, eased her against the municipal dock between a fuel barge and a shrimp trawler whose captain waved with the particular friendliness of a man who owed Wren money, and sent Tam to the harbormaster's office with the manifest while Bex went marketing and Odile disappeared into the engine supply shops on a quest for gaskets that would probably end, as such quests did, in compromise.

Which left Wren with the telegram.

It had come in overnight, forwarded through the harbormaster's office, and the boy who delivered it to the dock found Wren checking mooring lines and handed it over with the expression of someone passing along a live coal. Wren read it standing in the early light with the harbor noise washing around them.

HALCYON GEOGRAPHICAL SURVEY REQUIRES IMMEDIATE RIVER TRANSPORT STOP RESUPPLY AND PERSONNEL RETRIEVAL UPRIVER SERRANO HEADWATERS STOP TRIPLE STANDARD RATES PLUS HAZARD BONUS STOP RESPOND OFFICE FOUR CALLOWAY EXCHANGE TODAY STOP V.

Wren read it twice. Triple rates. Hazard bonus. The Halcyon Survey — Wren knew the name the way everyone on the river knew it: the expedition that had come through in the spring with chartered launches and crates of instruments and a leader who'd asked sharp questions about the headwaters and tipped like a woman performing generosity rather than feeling it. They'd gone upriver in June, toward the flooded terraces country where the maps went soft and the settlements thinned out, and that was the last the river had heard of them.

Three weeks of silence, if the harbor gossip was anything. And harbor gossip was usually everything.

Triple rates. Wren did the arithmetic without wanting to. One run at triple rates plus hazard paid off the last of the refit loan. Two runs bought the new water pump, the new rigging, the winter overhaul done properly instead of lovingly improvised. Three runs and Wren Galloway owned the *Marguerite* outright — no bank, no notes, no monthly appointment with dread at the harbormaster's window. Owned her the way a person owns their own hands.

The paper trembled slightly in the morning breeze off the water. Wren folded it along its creases and put it in a shirt pocket, over the heart, which was where important papers went whether or not you'd decided to believe them yet.

Office Four, Calloway Exchange. Today.

Wren looked up the river, past the rooftops and the crane arms and the smoke, toward the haze where the Serrano narrowed and climbed into country that low water made dangerous and high water made impossible. Somewhere up there, a survey team had gone quiet. Somewhere up there, somebody wanted them fetched out badly enough to pay triple, and wanted it handled quietly enough to put it in writing as *immediate* and sign it with a single letter.

Instinct said one thing. The ledger said another. Wren had spent ten years learning to trust instinct about water and ledger about everything else, and the two had never disagreed this loudly before.

"Captain!" Tam came jogging down the dock with the stamped manifest under his arm and his hair full of harbor wind. "Harbormaster says there's a lady been asking after us. Well-dressed. Been waiting since dawn." He grinned. "Says she's expecting you."

Wren touched the telegram through the cloth of the pocket, once, like a man touching a scar to confirm it still hurts.

"Yeah," Wren said, and cast one more glance upriver, where the water ran thin and the truth ran deep. "I expect she is."
