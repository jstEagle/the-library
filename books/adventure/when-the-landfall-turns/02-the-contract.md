## 2. The Contract

Port Calloway smelled the way it always did in late dry season: diesel, dried fish, and the sweet rot of fruit nobody had gotten to the market fast enough. Wren Galloway walked the quay with their hands in their pockets and their eyes on the waterline of every hull they passed, because that was how you read a town — not by its buildings but by what floated at them.

The Marguerite sat where they'd left her, tied off at Berth Nine with Tam aboard keeping watch. She rode low. That was fine. Low meant cargo, cargo meant money, money meant another season. Wren had been doing that arithmetic for ten years now, and it never came out to more than one season ahead. They'd stopped expecting it to.

The telegram had come two days ago, forwarded through the pilot's office like everything else. *EMERGENCY CHARTER STOP HALCYON SURVEY RESUPPLY AND RETRIEVAL STOP TRIPLE STANDARD RATE STOP INQUIRE PORT CALLOWAY HARBORMASTER STOP ASK FOR VOSS.*

Triple rate. Wren had read it four times on the walk over, looking for the trick in it. There was always a trick.

The harbormaster's office occupied a room above the fuel depot, all louvred windows and a ceiling fan turning with the enthusiasm of something that had given up years ago. The woman behind the desk was not the harbormaster. She stood when Wren came in, which told Wren she'd been waiting, and she was dressed wrong for Calloway — linen the color of bone, pressed, boots that had never seen mud. Tall. Gray at the temples, cut short and practical. Eyes the pale brown of river water in flood, carrying silt, carrying everything.

"Captain Galloway." Not a question. "Dr. Imara Voss."

"You're sitting at the harbormaster's desk," Wren said.

"I'm borrowing it. He's at lunch." A small smile, unapologetic. "I find borrowed rooms make better first impressions than hotel lobbies. Fewer distractions."

Wren didn't sit. "You sent the telegram."

"I did. Sit, please. This won't take long, and I'd rather you hear all of it before you decide anything."

Wren sat. Partly because standing made a point, and Wren had learned long ago that points were cheap; partly because the chair was there.

Voss opened a leather folder on the desk — real leather, good stitching, the kind of thing that outlasted its owner. Inside, papers. Wren could read upside down; ten years of contracts signed across chart tables had taught them that much. Manifests. A map. A photograph clipped to the top sheet showing a camp: canvas structures, an antenna mast, a generator shed, and behind it all a wall of green so dense it looked painted.

"The Halcyon Survey," Voss said. "Eleven personnel. Hydrology, geology, archaeology support. Commissioned by the Corvane Institute, funded privately. They went upriver in March to map the headwater terraces above the Serrano narrows."

"I know the terraces," Wren said.

Something flickered across Voss's face — interest, quickly banked. "Do you."

"Everyone who runs this stretch knows them. High water covers most of it. Some years you can see stone under the surface near the second bend. Old work, people say. Nobody's ever gone looking."

"People say," Voss repeated softly, as if filing the phrase somewhere. "Three weeks ago the Survey reported in. Their transmission was brief. They described — " she glanced at the page, though Wren suspected she knew it by heart — " 'a significant discovery requiring reassessment of the expedition's scope.' Then nothing. Three weeks of silence on their scheduled frequency. The relay station at Two Rivers confirms they haven't come back on air."

"Radios fail."

"Theirs has a backup generator and a trained operator. Dr. Ansel Reyes is meticulous about check-ins. He hasn't missed one in fourteen months." Voss closed the folder. "I want you to run supplies up to their last known position and bring them out. All of them, or whoever remains. Whatever state they're in."

"That's a long run for late dry season. Upper Serrano's already dropping. Another three weeks and there are channels I can't promise."

"I'm aware of the season. It's why I'm paying triple."

"There it is," Wren said.

Voss tilted her head. "There what is?"

"The number. Triple rate doesn't buy urgency, Doctor. Urgency is free — anybody scared enough will pay stupid money. Triple rate buys discretion. Somebody wants this trip quiet."

For a moment the fan ticked and neither of them spoke. Downstairs, someone was arguing about fuel prices in three languages at once.

"You're more careful than your reputation suggests," Voss said.

"My reputation says I get cargoes where they're going. It doesn't say anything else, because I don't give people anything else." Wren leaned back. "So let's have it straight. What am I hauling besides rice and radio batteries?"

"Nothing. Standard resupply plus passenger capacity for eleven."

"And the secrecy clause."

Voss's eyebrows rose a fraction. "You've heard about the clause."

"It's in your contract draft. Third page. I read it while you were deciding whether to be impressed by me." Wren nodded at the folder. "No communication regarding the charter's purpose, destination, or findings. No disclosure of cargo manifests. No statements to press, authorities, or downstream settlements. Penalty clause runs to five times the fee." Wren let that sit. "Five times. For a supply run."

"For a *retrieval*," Voss corrected. "Captain, I'll be direct with you, since you clearly prefer it. The Halcyon Survey is backed by private money. Private money attracts thieves, opportunists, and journalists, in roughly that order of destructiveness. If word gets out that a well-funded expedition has gone silent upstream, every fortune-hunter between here and the delta will put a boat in the water. My scientists would be a footnote by the time anyone legitimate arrived. The secrecy clause protects them."

It was a good answer. It was rehearsed, and it was a good answer, and Wren had spent enough years listening to the river lie smoothly about sandbars to know the difference between a truth and a polished one.

"Maybe," Wren said. "Or maybe whatever they found is worth more than eleven salaries, and you don't want witnesses."

Voss smiled again, and this time it reached her eyes, which was somehow worse. "Both things can be true, Captain. The world is rarely tidy. What matters to you is simpler: are my terms honest? They are. Triple rate, half on signature, half on delivery of personnel to Port Calloway. Fuel and provisions billed to the Institute. And the clause, which costs you nothing unless you talk."

"It costs me my crew's judgment. Tam reads manifests. Odile listens to frequencies. Bex talks to everyone at every landing we make. You're asking me to blindfold the people whose lives ride on my decisions."

"I'm asking you to keep a confidence. There's a difference, and captains understand it better than most."

Wren looked at the photograph again. The camp, the antenna, the green wall. Somewhere up there, eleven people hadn't called home in three weeks, and the woman across the desk wanted them brought down quietly, and was willing to pay enough to buy the Marguerite outright.

Because that was the arithmetic underneath everything, and both of them knew it. Ten years of seasons. An engine Odile coaxed along with prayer and scavenged parts. A hull that needed plating next haul-out, no argument, no maybe. The triple fee covered all of it and left change — enough to own the boat free and clear for the first time since Wren had signed for her. Enough to stop counting seasons. Enough to stop being one bad monsoon away from nothing.

Wren thought about that the way a drowning person thinks about air, briefly and with shame.

"Conditions," Wren said.

Voss's expression sharpened. "Go on."

"One. My crew knows the destination and the mission. Resupply and retrieval, that's it. They don't need your findings, but they're not sailing deaf into the headwaters. Blind crews die."

"Acceptable."

"Two. If we find people alive up there, they come down whether they want to or not, and if any of them tell me something's wrong, I listen. I'm not a hired hand for whatever politics are running your Institute."

Voss considered that longer than she'd considered the first condition. "Scientists under stress say many things, Captain. I won't bind myself to act on every alarm."

"You don't have to act. I just have to hear it. That's mine."

"Accepted," she said finally. "And your third?"

"Three. We turn back if the river says turn back. Not if you do. Triple rate doesn't buy the weather."

That almost got a laugh out of her — a breath through the nose, gone fast. "Every captain says that until the money's spent."

"I've spent it before. I'm still here."

Voss drew a pen from inside the folder — heavy, black, engraved — and slid the contract across the desk, turned to face Wren. She'd already signed. Her name sat at the bottom in a hand that was precise without warmth, each letter built rather than written.

*Imara Voss.* Underneath, smaller: *Corvane Institute, Headwaters Division.*

Wren read the whole thing. Every line, twice. It was clean — cleaner than most contracts Wren had signed, honestly, no hidden escalators, no weasel clauses buried in paragraph nine. Except the third page. Except the silence priced at five times the fee, which wasn't hidden at all. It was right there in the open, the way a submerged snag is right there in the open, if you know to look for the ripple.

*What the water hides, it shows you the shape of,* Wren's mother used to say. Wren had been reading ripples since before they could read words.

They picked up the pen.

"Captain." Voss's voice had changed — quieter, and for the first time not entirely controlled. "Before you sign. I want you to understand what's at stake for me personally. The Halcyon team includes people I recruited. People I promised I'd see safe. I have spent three weeks watching a frequency that doesn't answer, and every day that passes makes it likelier that what's waiting upriver isn't a discovery anymore. It's a body count." She held Wren's gaze. "Whatever you think of my backers, think of that."

It was well done. Wren couldn't tell how much of it was true, and that itself was information: a person telling only truths doesn't need to be skilled at it.

Wren signed. The pen scratched loud in the little room.

"Half payment to the Marguerite's account by morning," Voss said, retrieving the contract. "We provision tomorrow. I'll come aboard at first light with my security chief and two crates of equipment. I travel light."

"How many is 'we'?"

"Two. Myself and Mr. Mercer."

"A scientist and a security chief." Wren stood. "For a survey that went quiet."

"Headwaters are unsettled this time of year," Voss said mildly. "Refugee traffic, bandits. Mr. Mercer is a precaution, like your engine."

Everything with her was a precaution. Everything was reasonable. Wren walked to the door and stopped with a hand on the frame, looking back at the woman who was already squaring the contract into her folder, already moving on to the next item in a mind that ran like a ledger.

"Doctor. The message they sent. 'A significant discovery requiring reassessment of scope.' You've read it a hundred times. What do you think they found?"

Voss didn't look up. "When I know that, Captain, I'll know why they stopped answering. And then, perhaps, I'll tell you — if the clause permits."

She smiled at her own joke. Wren didn't.

Outside, the heat hit like a hand. Down on Berth Nine, Tam was leaning over the rail talking to a fish-seller, and the sound of ordinary haggling drifted up the quay, and Wren stood in it a moment, letting it wash against the strange cold feeling the contract had left behind.

Triple rate. Half paid. The boat bought outright by autumn, if the river allowed.

Wren flexed the hand that had held the pen and started walking toward the water, already composing in their head the speech that would land on the crew like a dropped crate: *we're going upriver, we leave tomorrow, and nobody asks the doctor what she's really after.*

Tam saw them coming and straightened, reading Wren's face the way Wren read waterlines.

"Oh no," Tam called down. "What did you sign?"
