## 3. Upriver Traffic

Tam Okafor had been with the Marguerite long enough to know the boat's moods by sound. There was the good idle, a low contented thrum through the deck plates, and the bad idle, which had a hitch in it like a cough at the end of a sentence. Right now, tied up at the Calloway fuel dock with the sun going orange behind the customs house, she was making neither. She was just sitting there, ticking as she cooled, and Tam sat on the cargo hatch with a mug of Bex's terrible ginger tea and listened to the argument already happening in the wheelhouse.

He could hear it through the open window. Everyone in Port Calloway could probably hear it.

"—triple pay is not a number, Wren, it's a dare." That was Odile, the engineer, her accent going thick the way it did when she was angry, the vowels flattening out like she'd left the coast a long time ago and meant to say so. "You don't pay triple for a milk run. Triple means the last three people who said yes didn't come back."

"The last three people who said yes didn't have us," Wren said.

Tam smiled into his tea. That was the captain all over. Wren Galloway could stand at the wheel in weather that peeled the paint off the pilothouse and say things like that with a straight face, and the worst part was that half the time it was true.

"And the engine," Odile went on. "You want to talk about the engine? She's thirty-one years old. I have rebuilt that injector pump twice. The second time with parts I machined myself because nobody makes them anymore. You want to take her into the flooded headwaters in the back half of the dry season, you are not hiring a boat, you are hiring a miracle, and miracles charge extra."

"Triple," Wren said.

"That's not funny."

"It's a little funny."

Tam took a slow sip and let the argument wash over him. This was the liturgy. He'd heard versions of it before the Kessuba run, before the winter they'd gone up the Anselm with ice on the rails, before the time Odile had actually quit for eleven hours and come back because, as she put it, somebody competent had to keep the captain alive. The arguing was how this crew said the thing none of them said. He'd figured that out in his first month, when he was nineteen and homesick and ready to jump ship at every dock, and Bex had handed him a bowl of stew and said, *They fight because they're staying.* It had taken him two more years to understand what she meant.

He heard the wheelhouse door bang and Odile came down the steps with her tool roll under one arm and her jaw set like a cleat. She was a short, dense woman built like something designed to survive pressure, and she glared at Tam on principle as she passed.

"You heard all that."

"Most of it."

"Then you know it's a bad idea."

"I know the captain thinks it's a good idea," Tam said, "which is different."

Odile stopped. Looked at him. Something in her face shifted, the anger banking down to something more like worry, which from Odile was worse. "Three weeks of silence, Tam. A survey team with a radio and a satellite phone and three weeks of nothing. And a doctor who pays triple and won't say why." She hefted her tool roll. "I've rebuilt that pump twice. I can't rebuild us."

She disappeared into the engine room, and the hatch clanged down behind her, and Tam sat with that for a while. *I can't rebuild us.* Leave it to Odile to put the real objection on the table while everyone else was arguing about money and machinery.

Because that was the thing. The Marguerite was four people and a hull, and the hull was the least of it. Tam had come aboard six years ago with nothing but a duffel and a deckhand's certificate he'd half earned, and Wren had looked at him for about four seconds and said, *Can you tie a bowline in the dark?* and Tam had said, *Yes,* and it had been a lie, and Wren had known it was a lie, and had hired him anyway. He'd learned the bowline that night by lantern light, practicing until his fingers bled, and nobody ever mentioned it again. That was the family. Nobody mentioned it again. They just made room.

Footsteps on the dock, and Bex came up the gangway with two cloth bags of market greens and an expression Tam had learned to read like weather. Bex was the oldest of them, sixty-something and built like a soft answer to a hard question, and she had a way of arriving at exactly the moment a conversation needed rerouting.

"Odile's against it," Tam said.

"Odile's always against it. That's her job. Wren's job is to be for it. Your job is to stand in the middle and look sensible, and my job is to feed everyone until they stop being idiots." She set the bags down. "So. Are we going?"

"Has anyone actually voted?"

Bex laughed, a big unbothered sound that sent a heron flapping off the pilings. "Child, nobody on this boat has ever voted in their life. Wren decides, Odile objects, you worry, and I pack extra rice. That's the constitution." She started down toward the galley with her bags, then paused. "For the record. I'm packing extra rice."

Which was Bex's way of voting yes.

* * *

They gathered in the wheelhouse after supper, which was how the real decision always got made — not over the contract but over the dishes, in the low warm light, with the river going dark outside the glass. Wren stood at the chart table with Voss's papers squared in front of them, and Tam leaned in the doorway, and Odile sat on the chart bench with her arms crossed, radiating principled opposition, and Bex occupied the one chair like a magistrate.

"Walk me through it," Wren said. "Tam. Monsoon timing."

Tam had been dreading this and had also, secretly, prepared for it, because that was his role: the one who worried professionally. He came to the chart and put his finger on the headwaters, that sprawl of blue threads above the last marked reach.

"Landfall historically opens the second week of the wet month," he said. "Sometimes earlier. The Serrano headwaters flood fast because the catchment's all steep country — rain up high is down on us in a day, day and a half. We're what, six days' run to the survey camp in good water?" He looked at Wren. "Five, if you push her. That's eleven or twelve days round trip if everything goes right, and we'd be coming back down with the first of it. Doable. Barely. But if we get held up even three days — a breakdown, a delay at the camp, weather — then we're not running ahead of the rain anymore. We're running inside it."

"And inside it means?"

"Means the channels move. Means debris in the water at night. Means sandbars where the charts say channel and channel where the charts say sandbar." He kept his voice even. "I've seen the river eat boats in landfall, Captain. Good boats. Boats with crews who knew what they were doing."

Wren nodded, slow, taking it in the way they took in everything — no argument, no reassurance, just the fact of it absorbed into whatever arithmetic they ran behind those gray eyes. "Odile."

"Engine's sound for six days," Odile said. "Sound for twelve, probably. Sound for fifteen if I baby her, and I'll baby her, that's not the question. The question is what happens when you're up in the flooded headwaters and something lets go, and the nearest machine shop is a hundred and forty miles downstream, and there's a survey team that's been silent for three weeks and a doctor who pays triple and won't say why, and you're asking a thirty-one-year-old engine to be the only thing between this crew and swimming home." She uncrossed her arms. "I'll keep her running. I want that on record. But I want it on record that I said the odds are bad."

"Recorded," Wren said. "Bex."

"Provisions for fourteen days, and I'll stretch it to twenty if we eat the pickled things." Bex said *pickled things* the way other people said *hardship.* "And I'm bringing the good coffee, because if we're going to be frightened, we'll be frightened caffeinated."

Tam almost laughed. Almost. Then Wren looked at him, last of the four, and the wheelhouse went quiet in the particular way it did when the captain wanted the truth and not the liturgy.

"You've been quiet," Wren said.

"I'm the worrier. Worrying's quiet."

"Tam."

He looked at the chart. The blue threads of the headwaters. The little printed note in faded ink — *surveyed 1911, unreliable above the narrows* — which was the river's way of admitting it hadn't been told everything either.

"I think we're going," he said finally. "I think you decided before the telegram was cold, and I think we all decided somewhere before that, because that's what we do. I just want it said out loud, once, so it's not sitting under everything: this isn't a milk run, and we're not going for the money. Not really. Triple pay is the story we tell the harbor master." He shrugged, feeling his ears go hot. "We're going because somebody up there stopped answering, and we're the ones who go."

Nobody spoke for a second. Then Bex said, softly, "Well. There's the vote," and Odile snorted and got up to check the fuel lines, which was Odile for *I love you all and I'm frightened,* and Wren just looked at Tam for a long moment with something that wasn't quite a smile but lived next door to one.

"Cast off at first light," Wren said. "Tam, you have the deck. Bex, feed the winch crew. Odile —"

"I know," she said. "The engine. I know."

* * *

They cast off into a morning the color of hammered tin, and the river took them like it had been waiting.

Tam had the deck, as promised, and he worked her out of Calloway with the spring lines and the fenders and the particular choreography of a boat leaving home, and by the time they cleared the breakwater the town had shrunk to a brown smudge and the wheelhouse had settled into watch routine, two hours on, two hours off, the Marguerite's heartbeat. Odile had the engine singing the good idle. Bex had already produced mid-morning tea. And Wren stood at the wheel with the contract papers folded away and the river ahead, and Tam, watching from the bow, thought: *this is the good part. This is always the good part.* The part where the boat is pointed the right way and everything is still possible.

They passed the first refugee barge an hour short of the Kessuba junction.

Tam saw it first — a low shape coming down with the current, riding deep, too deep for cargo. He signaled the wheelhouse and Wren slowed, because that was the law of the river if not the law of the books: you slow for anything riding deep and low. As it came abreast he could see it was a settlement barge, the kind the upriver villages lashed together when the water or the trouble got bad — a raft of logs and oil drums with a tin-roofed shack on it, and people. Dozens of people. Women with bundles, old men, kids staring at the Marguerite with the flat unblinking look of children who had stopped being surprised by anything.

Wren brought them alongside, careful, and hailed them through the megaphone, and a man with a gray beard and a government blanket over his shoulders came to the edge and shouted back across the water.

Tam stood at the rail and caught the pieces. Flood coming early, the man said. Whole valley moving down. The water was already up at the second terrace — *the second terrace, and it's the dry season, that's not right, that has never been right* — and there were lights at night, up where the river forked, lights out over the water where there was nothing to light. Green-white, he said, low along the surface, like something under the water shining up through it. His people had left the headwater settlements three days ago. He looked at Wren with an expression Tam had seen on a lot of faces in six years and had never gotten used to: the look of someone telling you a true thing and knowing you won't believe it.

"You're going up?" the man shouted.

"Resupply run," Wren called back.

The old man looked at the boat, at the four of them, at the deep-water line on the Marguerite's hull. Then he made a gesture — quick, small, almost involuntary — that Tam had never seen before: he touched the water with two fingers and drew them up across his lips, like a man sealing a letter.

"The river's waking up," he shouted. "Go careful. Go careful, or don't go."

Then the current took them, and the barge slid away downstream with its tin roof and its bundles and its children, and the Marguerite stood on upriver alone.

Nobody said anything for a while. Bex came out of the galley and stood at the rail with her tea going cold. Odile's engine kept its steady heart-beat under the deck plates. Finally Wren turned the wheel a spoke and said, to nobody in particular, "Terraces," and Tam heard the word land somewhere deep in the captain's mind and stay there.

It was near dusk, Tam at the wheel for the evening watch, when he saw it himself.

They were well above the junction, into country the charts gave up on, the banks closing in and the water gone the deep tea-color of the upper river. And off the starboard bow, maybe half a mile out where the channel widened into drowned forest, there was a light. Low. Green-white. It didn't flicker like fire and didn't sweep like a lamp. It just sat there on the black water, steady as a mooring light, in a place where there was nothing to moor and nothing to light and no settlement for sixty miles.

He watched it for a full minute. It watched back, or seemed to. Then it went out — not faded, *out*, like a lid closing — and the dark came back in like water filling a hole.

Tam's hands were steady on the wheel, because his hands were always steady on the wheel; that was the deal he'd made with himself years ago. But he rang the engine telegraph down to half, and when Wren came up the steps a minute later, drawn by the change in the boat's voice, Tam didn't dress it up.

"There was a light," he said. "Out there. Off the bow. The old man's lights."

Wren stood beside him at the window and looked out at the black water and the black trees and the first stars coming up over the ridgeline, and for a long moment said nothing at all. Then: "Keep her at half. Double watch tonight. I'll take midnight."

"Captain." Tam hesitated. "The old man's gesture. The water and the lips. You know it?"

"I know it," Wren said, and turned back down the steps, and that was all.

Tam stood his watch. The river ran dark and quiet around them, and somewhere behind, far downstream, Port Calloway was a memory, and somewhere ahead, up in the flooded threads of the headwaters, three weeks of silence was waiting with its reasons. The Marguerite pushed on, steady, four people and a hull, a family that had never once said the word out loud, running up a river that was — if the old man was right, if the light was right — already awake.

At midnight, when Wren relieved him, the light came back.

It was closer.
