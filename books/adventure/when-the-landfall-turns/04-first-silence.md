## 4. First Silence

The relay station at Kilometre 212 was a tin roof, a diesel generator, and a man named Prosper who had been the river's voice for longer than Odile had been its engine's keeper. She knew him from years of fuel stops — he kept a cat with half an ear and a shelf of spare parts he traded like a miser, and he always had coffee going no matter what hour the Marguerite came up out of the dark.

Tonight the coffee was there. The welcome wasn't.

Odile felt it before she understood it, the way she felt a bearing run dry before it seized: something in the rhythm of the place gone wrong. Prosper stood in his doorway with the lamp behind him, and instead of calling out a greeting to Wren on the wheelhouse deck, he just looked at the boat coming in like a man watching weather.

"Take us alongside," Wren said quietly, and Tam threw the line, and the Marguerite kissed the pilings soft as a mouth closing.

The generator clattered. The cat didn't come down off the roof. That was the second wrong thing. That cat would walk across hot coals for fish scraps.

"Prosper." Wren came down the ladder two rungs at a time. "You look like you've seen the survey team's ghost."

"I've heard worse than ghosts." Prosper's voice was low, pitched under the generator noise, and his eyes went past Wren to where Dr. Voss stood at the rail of the main deck, talking quietly with Mercer. "Inside," he said. "Not all of you."

Wren glanced back. "Bex, keep the galley warm. Odile—"

"Engine check," Odile said, which everyone present understood to mean *I'm coming*.

* * *

Inside the relay shack smelled of kerosene and old paper and forty years of cigarette smoke soaked into the walls. Prosper poured three cups without asking and set the fourth aside for the cat, who did not come for it either.

"The Halcyon Survey," Wren said. "When did you last hear them?"

Prosper sat down heavily on his stool, as if the question were weight he'd been carrying alone too long. "Three weeks they've been dark. You know that or you wouldn't be here."

"We know they're dark. We want to know how they went dark."

"They didn't go dark." Prosper wrapped both hands around his cup like the night had turned cold, though it hadn't; the air through the screen door was thick and warm and smelled of the flooded terraces upstream, green rot and stone. "They went quiet. There's a difference, Captain. Dark is when your set dies or your batteries do. Quiet is when somebody decides not to talk."

Odile leaned against the doorframe, arms crossed, listening with the part of her brain that listened to engines — the part that heard what a machine was doing underneath what it was supposed to be doing.

"Tell it straight," Wren said.

"Nightly schedule, twenty-one hundred, relay through me to Port Calloway. Standard position reports, water levels, supply needs. Then, nineteen days ago, they come up early. Off-schedule. And Dr. Voss's man — Reyes, the geologist — he's not doing a position report. He's excited. I could hear it in his voice even through the static. He says, *relay this priority, we've made a find, repeat, a find.*" Prosper drank. His hands weren't steady. "That word twice. Like a code word. Like something they'd agreed meant more than it said."

"What kind of find?"

"He never said. Next night, twenty-one hundred comes and goes. Nothing. Night after, nothing. I called them every six hours for four days. Nothing. Then on the fifth day, one transmission. Ten seconds. No call sign, no voice. Just—" He stopped.

"Just what?" Odile asked.

"A tone. One long tone, like somebody leaning on the key. Then nothing since." Prosper looked up at Wren. "Captain, I've carried this river's voices for thirty years. Drowning men, lost boats, births, deaths, all of it. A tone like that isn't equipment failing. Equipment fails messy. That tone was somebody holding a switch down on purpose. That was a message that said *we can't say anything else*."

The generator thudded outside. Somewhere on the boat, faintly, Bex was singing to herself over the galley stove, something tuneless and cheerful, and the sound of it made the shack feel colder.

Wren was quiet a long moment. "Does anyone else know about the find?"

"Port Calloway knows the survey reported one. Whether they know what it was—" Prosper shrugged. "Your passenger would know better than me."

"My charter," Wren corrected, but the word sat wrong in the air, and Odile saw Wren hear it sit wrong.

"One more thing." Prosper got up and crossed to the radio bench, all brass and bakelite, lovingly maintained. He tapped a logbook bound in oilcloth. "I log everything. Every carrier wave, every squelch burst, even the ones that aren't words. Last ten nights, there's been traffic on the survey's frequency that isn't the survey. Short transmissions. Encrypted or coded, I can't tell. Coming from somewhere between here and their camp." He turned the logbook around so Wren could see the columns of times and frequencies in his careful hand. "Somebody else is upriver, Captain. Somebody with a radio and a reason to whisper."

Odile watched Wren read the entries. Watched the stillness come into Wren's face — not calm, never calm, but the particular stillness of a pilot reading water that has begun to move wrong.

"Thank you, Prosper."

"Captain." He caught Wren's sleeve at the door. "Whatever your contract says, remember the river doesn't honor contracts. It only honors attention."

* * *

Back aboard, the boat had gone to its night arrangement: Voss and Mercer aft in the chartered cabin, the scientists' gear lashed amidships, the crew forward where they belonged. Odile ran her evening engine checks by feel and lamplight — oil, belts, bilge, the fuel lines she'd replaced twice in ten years and trusted like family.

Tam found her in the engine room, crouched by the port fuel line.

"Well?"

Odile told him. All of it. Tam listened the way he did everything, still and total, his young face unreadable until it wasn't, and then it was troubled.

"Somebody else is up there," Tam said. "With radios."

"Somebody with radios and a reason to hide."

"You think Voss knows?"

"I think," Odile said, wiping her hands slow, "that Voss hired a security chief for a resupply run. You don't bring a man like Mercer to carry crates of flour."

Tam looked toward the engine room bulkhead, as if he could see through it, aft, to the chartered cabin. "You think he's carrying something else?"

"I think we're about to find out what."

They found out at two in the morning.

* * *

Odile slept badly in low water season anyway — some habit of the engine's rhythms lived in her chest, and any change in the boat's breathing woke her. At 0210 the Marguerite breathed wrong. Not much. A shift of weight forward, a fractional change in how the hull took the current against the mooring lines. Most people would have slept through it. Odile came up out of her bunk with her heart already working.

She moved through the forward passage without lighting a lamp. She knew the boat's bones in the dark the way she knew her own hands.

The cargo hold hatch was open.

It should not have been. Wren dogged that hatch every night, every night for ten years, rain or shine, drunk or sober — a ritual so fixed that Odile could have set a clock by it. Now it stood open six inches, and from inside came the small sounds of a careful man being very careful: canvas shifted, rope coiled back, a crate lid eased down onto its slats.

Odile stood in the dark and did the arithmetic. Mercer was a big man but not a quiet one. This was someone smaller. Someone who knew boats well enough to move on one without waking the engine room's keeper — which meant crew, or someone who'd studied them.

She reached past the hatch and hit the hold light.

The man in the crate aisle flinched like a hooked fish. He was young, narrow, dressed in expedition khaki with the sleeves rolled, and he had one of Voss's sealed dispatch cases open on top of Crate Fourteen, papers fanned across it. He blinked in the sudden light, and Odile saw him decide, in real time, between three stories.

"Don't," she said. "I maintain this boat. I know every lie it can hold. Try another one."

He put his hands up slowly. "Dr. Okafor," he said. "Junior hydrologist. I was looking for—"

"Survey instruments are stowed aft with the rest of the science kit. Labeled. In your own handwriting, I'd guess, since you packed them." She stepped into the hold and turned the papers on the case toward herself. Charts. Copies of charts — hand-traced from originals, showing the drowned terraces, depth soundings, and a series of marked cavern entrances beneath the third terrace line. And on the top sheet, in a different hand, angular and hard: a list. Not supplies. Objects. Categories. *Tablets, sealed vessels, textile fragments, metalwork — condition grades — estimated transport mass.*

An inventory. Somebody was inventorying things nobody had admitted finding.

"Where did these come from?" Odile asked.

Okafor's throat worked. "Dr. Voss received a packet at the last settlement. Before we boarded."

"From whom?"

"I don't know. Mercer collected it. But I trace the charts for the doctor, and these aren't mine, and the soundings on them are recent. Weeks old at most. Which means somebody has already been to the terraces and measured them." His voice dropped. "Before the survey ever reported anything. Before the 'find.' They knew."

The river slapped softly against the hull. Upstream, invisible in the dark, the flooded terraces waited under their high water, and somewhere between here and there, someone was leaning on a radio key in the night.

"Put it back exactly as it was," Odile said. "Then get off my boat's hold and don't come into it again without the captain."

"And if I tell Dr. Voss you found me?"

"Tell her." Odile held his eyes. "And tell her the engine room logs every hour of every watch, and I'll know next time too. There's always a next time with men like Mercer. Ask me how I know."

He repacked the case with shaking hands. At the hatch he paused. "The list," he said quietly. "Condition grades. Transport mass. That's not a rescue inventory, is it? That's a removal manifest."

"No," Odile said. "It isn't a rescue inventory."

He went. She stood a long time in the lit hold, among the honest crates of flour and fuel and medicine, and thought about the difference between dark and quiet, and about a single long tone on a dead frequency, and about what kind of person sends a removal manifest upriver ahead of the discovery it removes.

* * *

She woke Wren. Not by knocking — Wren slept like a heron, one eye on the world — she simply stood in the wheelhouse doorway until the shape in the bunk rolled upright.

"Talk."

Odile talked. Prosper's log, the encrypted bursts, the packet, the traced charts, the manifest. Wren listened without moving, and when she finished, the silence went on long enough that the river filled it.

"Voss knew about the archive before the survey announced it," Wren said at last. "Or her backers did. The survey found what they sent people to find — and then went quiet because they found out what the finding was *for*."

"That's my read."

"Wren Galloway's read too." Wren swung out of the bunk, barefoot on the deck plates, and stood at the wheelhouse window looking upstream into the black. The running lights of the Marguerite made a small trembling world on the water, and beyond it the dark was total. "Ten years I've run this river. I know every lie it can hold, same as you. Right now it's holding something big enough that men are whispering over it in the night."

"The monsoon's close," Odile said. "Tam will want to talk windows."

"Let him sleep. Tomorrow we run hard and I get answers out of Dr. Voss face to face." Wren's jaw set in the lamplight. "One thing first."

"Captain?"

"Nobody dogs the hold tonight but me. And tomorrow, we count everything aboard — everything — before breakfast. If Mercer's people put anything on my boat, I want to know what it is before it's part of my boat." Wren turned, and for a moment the tiredness showed through, the ten-years-of-miles tiredness, and under it something harder and older than tiredness. "I signed a contract to carry supplies and bring people home. That's the whole contract as far as I'm concerned. Whatever else is riding up this river with us—"

The radio crackled.

Both of them froze. The set was tuned to the survey's frequency — Wren's habit, keeping a channel open for a voice that hadn't spoken in nineteen days. Static washed and washed. Then, threading through it, thin as wire:

A tone. One long tone. Held, deliberate, human.

Then a second sound beneath it, so faint Odile might have imagined it — a voice, or the ghost of one, buried under the tone like a face under floodwater. Two syllables, maybe three. Gone.

Wren's hand closed on the set's edge hard enough to whiten knuckles. "Did you hear—"

"Yes."

Upstream, in the dark, somebody was still trying to talk.
