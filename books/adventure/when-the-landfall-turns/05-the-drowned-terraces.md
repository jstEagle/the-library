## 5. The Drowned Terraces

The river changed its mind on the third morning above the relay station.

Wren felt it before they saw it — a softening in the current's grip on the wheel, a slackness where there should have been argument. Ten years of running the Serrano had taught them that the river spoke first through the hands. By the time Odile called up from the engine room that the water temperature had dropped two degrees, Wren already had the Marguerite eased toward the cutbank, watching the color of the channel go from tea-brown to something older, greener, like glass over moss.

"Snowmelt's coming down early," Tam said, appearing at the rail with a mug of Bex's coffee and no invitation, which was how Tam did everything.

"That's not snowmelt." Wren took the mug anyway. "That's headwater. Something upriver opened up."

"The flood lakes?"

"Maybe." Wren drank. The coffee was terrible and hot and exactly right. "Or maybe the whole basin's filling at once and nobody downstream has noticed yet."

Tam looked at the water the way Tam looked at everything — sideways, suspicious, like it owed them money. "Voss know?"

"She knows." Voss had been on deck since dawn, standing at the bow rail with her field notebook open against the wind, watching the banks slide past with an expression Wren couldn't read and didn't trust themselves to try. "She's been counting terraces since we cleared the narrows."

"Terraces?"

"You'll see."

They saw by midmorning.

The Serrano in high water was a different animal than the river Wren knew in the dry months. Channels they'd threaded a hundred times vanished under brown flood; sandbars became suggestions; whole islands went under and left only the tops of trees standing like drowned men waving for help. Wren ran it from memory and instinct both, reading the surface the way other people read print — the smooth tongue of a deep channel, the wrinkled frown of a bar, the V-wake of a snag just under the skin of the water.

And rising out of all that motion, on the eastern bank where the valley widened into a long green amphitheater, were the steps.

Wren had run this stretch maybe forty times. In low water the bank here was a steep clay slope stitched with bamboo, unremarkable, the kind of country you forgot before you finished passing it. But the flood had come up six meters and more, and what it revealed was not slope but structure: broad horizontal shelves of stone, one above another, descending into the water in regular courses like the seats of some vast open-air theater. Water sheeted off each lip in thin bright curtains. Between the curtains, the stone showed dark and wet and carved.

Not eroded. Carved.

Wren brought the Marguerite down to half throttle without deciding to. The boat slowed, the engine note dropping to that idle rumble Odile swore was bad for the injectors, and for a while nobody on deck said anything at all.

The carvings ran along every terrace face in continuous bands — geometric at first glance, then not. Spirals nested inside spirals. Rows of figures, small as fingerprints from this distance, arranged in processions. And everywhere, repeated with a discipline that made Wren's neck prickle, a single motif: three wavy lines stacked over a circle. Water over sun. Water over sun. Water over sun, hundreds of times, the way a person might repeat a name so it wouldn't be forgotten.

"That's masonry," Ansel Reyes said quietly. He'd come up from the aft cabin where he'd spent most of the voyage sleeping badly, and he stood at the rail now with his hands gripping it like a man holding onto a moving train. "That is dressed stone. That is *coursed* stone." He turned to look at Wren, and his face had gone strange — lit up and frightened at once, like a man watching a door he'd been knocking on for years finally start to open. "Do you understand what I'm telling you? This isn't a ruin. Ruins are broken things. This is *architecture*. Somebody built this to be seen from the water."

"Somebody built it to be flooded," Wren said.

Ansel blinked. "What?"

Wren nodded at the terraces, at the way each level sat precisely at a height the river reached in wet years, at the spillways cut clean through the lowest courses so the water could climb them without tearing them apart. "Look how it takes the water. It doesn't fight it. It's built for this. Every wet season, the river comes up those steps like it's climbing home." They heard their own voice and didn't recognize it. "Whoever built this wasn't afraid of the flood. They planned around it. Planned *with* it."

Behind them, a camera shutter clicked.

Voss stood ten feet down the rail, photographing the terraces in methodical overlapping frames, her notebook balanced on her forearm, Mercer a step behind her with his arms crossed and his eyes doing what his eyes always did — measuring, weighing, pricing. Wren had watched him do it to the crew, to the cargo, to the river itself. Everything Mercer looked at came out of the looking worth less than it had been.

"Captain Galloway." Voss didn't look up from her viewfinder. "How much draft does your boat draw?"

"Four feet loaded. Three and change light."

"And the channel along this bank — the one you're hugging now. How far does it run?"

Wren considered lying. Considered saying the channel shoaled out in a hundred yards, that the terraces were unreachable, that the whole question was academic. It would have been easy. It might even have been believed; Voss was a scientist, not a pilot, and the flooded river kept its own counsel.

But the Marguerite's hull was currently sliding over water that belonged to somebody — had belonged to somebody for longer than anyone aboard could reckon — and Wren found they didn't want to start lying to it this early.

"It runs," Wren said. "All the way to the head of the valley, if you trust me to keep us in it."

"I trust competence," Voss said, and finally lowered the camera. She looked at the terraces — really looked, for the first time, Wren thought — and what moved across her face was not awe. Wren had seen awe before, on passengers' faces, on Ansel's face right now, on their own face in mirrors after certain nights. Awe was a kind of surrender. What moved across Imara Voss's face was closer to appetite. She looked at the drowned architecture the way a speculator looks at timber: already felled, already milled, already money.

"We'll stop here," she said. "Half a day. I want survey photographs from every angle, and I want Reyes to take measurements of the carving bands."

"Half a day puts us at the Survey camp a day late," Wren said. "You told me those people have been silent three weeks."

Something flickered behind Voss's eyes — fast, controlled, gone. "The camp will keep. This may not." She turned back to the rail before Wren could answer, which was itself an answer, and raised her voice just enough to carry. "Mercer. Have the men ready to put ashore at the second terrace. And see that our equipment stays aboard."

*Our equipment.* Not the scientists'. Not the team's. Wren filed the phrase away with the others Voss had been dropping for days like coins down a well — *my expedition, my find, my timeline* — and watched Mercer nod and move off, and watched him pause at the companionway to say something low to the two men he'd brought aboard in Port Calloway who wore plain canvas jackets and carried their duffels like they weighed nothing because they were mostly webbing and steel.

* * *

They tied off to a stone bollard that shouldn't have existed.

It protruded from the second terrace's edge, waist-high, carved in the shape of a coiled serpent or eel — Wren couldn't tell which, the features worn smooth — and the mooring line went around it with a familiarity that unsettled everyone. As if the stone had been waiting a thousand years for exactly this size of rope.

Bex touched it once, flat-palmed, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal. "This rock's been handled," Bex said. "Feel it. That's not weathering, that's wear. People have been tying off here for a long, long time."

"Longer than us," Tam said. "Longer than anybody's us."

The landing party went ashore — Voss, Ansel, two of the junior scientists whose names Wren was ashamed to still be fuzzy on, and Mercer with his two quiet men, ostensibly as porters. Wren stayed with the boat. It was policy and it was instinct: never leave the Marguerite unwatched in strange water, and never leave her unwatched with strangers aboard, and Voss's people were both.

For an hour Wren stood at the wheel and watched the terraces work.

The flood climbed them in slow pulses, each swell pushing a little higher, spilling over one course and then the next, and the carvings changed as the water rose. Bands that had been dry emerged from meaning into nonsense and back again — no. The other way. Wren watched a procession of figures disappear under a swell and reappear, and understood suddenly that the sequence only read correctly at certain water levels. The river was the missing piece. At low water the story broke apart; at high water it completed. Whoever carved these steps had composed them for the flood, had used the river itself as the hand that finished the sentence.

Wren's chest did something complicated about that. Ten years on this water and they had thought they knew what intimacy with a river meant — every bar and bend memorized, every season's mood catalogued. This was older and stranger. Someone had loved this water hard enough to build it a staircase and write it a letter in stone, knowing full well the letter would only be legible a few weeks out of every year, to whoever happened to be floating past at the right moment.

To whoever happened to be floating past.

Wren looked up sharply at the bank, at the bamboo line above the highest terrace, and found nothing — birdsong, wind, the drip of the flood. But the feeling stayed, the one from the relay station, the one from the night someone had gone through the hold: *watched*. Not hostile. Attentive. Like the river itself had lifted its head.

"Captain." Odile's voice, from the engine room hatch, pitched low. "You want to see something."

Odile only said *you want to see something* when the answer was yes, so Wren went down.

The engine room was warm and loud and smelled of diesel and Bex's coffee, because Bex had a habit of leaving mugs on the workbench and Odile had a habit of drinking them. Odile pointed at the raw-water intake gauge, then at the strainer housing, and said nothing, because Odile believed words were for people who hadn't learned to look.

The strainer basket — which they'd cleaned at the relay station two days ago — was packed with fibers. Long, fine, parallel fibers, pressed into a mat. Not weed. Not root. Wren pulled a strand free and held it up: pale gold, uniform, almost threadlike, with a faint waxy feel.

"Bamboo?" Wren said.

"Bamboo's got nodes. This hasn't got anything. It's processed." Odile wiped her hands on a rag that was cleaner than her hands. "Somebody upstream is putting fiber into the water. Deliberately. On purpose. Enough of it that it's fouling my intake in forty-eight hours."

"Why would anybody—"

"Marking," Odile said. "Downcountry people mark channels with fiber bundles when the water changes. My grandmother's people did it on the coastal lagoons. You lay a line of fiber along a safe channel and everybody who reads water knows to follow it." She met Wren's eyes. "We've been following marked channels since the relay station, Captain. Somebody's been showing us the way up."

Wren stood very still in the engine room noise, holding the golden thread, feeling the Marguerite's hull thrum beneath their boots like a held breath.

Someone was guiding them. Or herding them.

* * *

Voss came back aboard at dusk with mud to the knees and a look on her face that Wren had learned to dread the way sailors dread a particular shade of sky.

The survey party had photographed everything, measured everything, taken wax impressions of the carving bands. Ansel came up the ladder talking — actually talking, words tumbling, the first time Wren had heard him sound anything but exhausted — about hydraulic engineering, about flood-cycle calendars encoded in the procession friezes, about a civilization that had treated the monsoon not as disaster but as appointment. Voss let him talk for ninety seconds. Then she said, "Thank you, Dr. Reyes. Write it up when we're back at the camp," and turned away, and the light in Ansel's face guttered like a lamp in wind.

Wren caught the tail end of it later, from the shadow of the wheelhouse, in the blue hour when the river goes the color of a bruise.

Voss and Mercer stood at the stern rail, heads together, voices pitched under the engine idle. Wren didn't mean to listen. Listening was simply what the wheelhouse was for; sound carried off water whether you wanted it or not.

"—the terraces are secondary," Voss was saying. "Impressive, publishable, useful as corroboration. But the source is what matters. Reyes thinks the complex continues upslope. There are sealed openings above the flood line — I counted four. Where there's an archive, there's an entrance, and where there's an entrance—"

"There's a way to move it," Mercer said. His voice was flat as a ledger line. "My concern is weight and schedule. If the material is stone, we'll need rigging. If it's perishable, we'll need packing and climate control. Either way, I need to know tonnage before the storm window closes, or none of the rest matters."

"Reyes will give us tonnage."

"Reyes will give you resistance. He's been giving it since Port Calloway. So will the boat crew, if it comes to it." Mercer said it without heat, the way a man reads a supply list. "I can manage the scientists. I can't manage a captain who owns the only boat."

A silence. The river slid past, black now, carrying its golden threads somewhere in the dark.

"Then we make sure it doesn't come to it," Voss said. "Galloway is a contractor. Contractors respond to incentives. Triple the fee, a bonus on delivery, and a confidentiality rider strong enough to scare them straight. Nobody walks away from that kind of money over *rocks*, Mercer."

"No," Mercer agreed. "Nobody smart does."

Wren stepped back from the wheelhouse door, quiet on the boards, heart going slow and heavy as a pump working against a clogged line.

*Rocks.* Ten years, and Wren had been called a lot of things — smuggler, fool, widow of the current, best pilot on the Serrano by people who wanted something and worst by people who'd had it — but they had never once thought of the river as rocks. Of any of it as rocks. The terraces climbing out of the flood with their thousand repetitions of water-over-sun; the golden fibers laid down by unseen hands to guide strangers home; the bollard serpent worn smooth by centuries of rope — these weren't rocks. These were a voice. And Imara Voss, standing at the rail of Wren's boat in the last light, had just priced the voice by the ton.

Above the highest terrace, in the bamboo dark, something shifted. A bird, probably. A monkey. The wind.

Probably.

Wren rang down for full ahead, and the Marguerite swung her nose back into the marked channel, following the golden thread upriver into the gathering night — toward the silent camp, toward whatever Voss had really hired them to carry, toward the thing the water was trying, patiently, in its old carved language, to tell them.

The first drop of rain hit the wheelhouse glass before Wren even saw the cloud.
