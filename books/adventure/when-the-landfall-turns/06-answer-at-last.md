## 6. Answer at Last

Ansel Reyes heard the boat before he saw it — a low diesel heartbeat coming up through the water the way sound traveled in the flooded terraces, arriving from everywhere at once. He was on the eastern palisade with a mug of cold coffee going stale in his hand, and for three full seconds he did not move. He just listened.

Three weeks of silence. Three weeks of Mercer's radio discipline and Voss's locked manifest cases and the slow, careful subtraction of people from camp until only eleven of them remained out of twenty-two. Three weeks of telling himself that a boat on the river was a good thing.

"Reyes." Okafor's voice from below, low and urgent. "You seeing this?"

"I'm seeing it."

The Marguerite came around the drowned willow line at half throttle, riding high in the water, her hull patched in places with plates that had been welded by someone who loved her rather than someone who was paid to. A riverboat. An actual working riverboat, with a wheelhouse and cargo tarps and a woman at the rail who raised one hand — not waving, exactly. Signaling. *I see you. I see what you've built here.*

Ansel set down the coffee because his hands had started to shake, and he didn't want Okafor to see it, and he didn't want to see it himself.

*They came,* he thought. *Someone finally came.*

And then, immediately behind that thought, arriving like its shadow: *God help us, they came.*

* * *

He went down to the landing because there was no protocol for this except the old one, the human one, and because Junie was already running for the dock with her arms out like a child, and someone had to be the adult. The palisade they'd built — sharpened pilings, watch platforms, the generator cage wrapped in chain — looked obscene from the water. He knew it looked obscene. He'd helped build it, and every day of it had felt like helping to build a lie.

The boat slid alongside the landing planks with a competence that made something in his chest ache. Not a military approach. Not a barge pilot's brute docking. This was a person who knew the river the way Ansel knew strata — by touch, by habit, by ten thousand small corrections no one else would ever notice.

"You're a long way from Port Calloway," Ansel said, when the gangplank went down.

The captain — Galloway, according to the manifest Voss kept locked in her quarters, though Ansel wasn't supposed to know he'd read it — looked at him for a long moment. Brown eyes, weathered face, the particular stillness of someone who spent their life reading moving water. "So are you," Galloway said. "Eleven tents. I counted nine people."

"Ten." Ansel heard his own voice come out flat. "Dr. Chen is in the medical tent. She shouldn't be counted yet."

"Shouldn't be counted yet," Galloway repeated, not as a question but as a weighing. Their gaze went past Ansel's shoulder, up the slope, taking in the palisade, the watch platform, the crates stacked under tarps near the cavern entrance — the ones Mercer had sealed with numbered straps. "We brought resupply. Fuel, food, medical. Contract said retrieve personnel if conditions warranted."

"Conditions warrant," Ansel said quickly. Too quickly. He heard it land wrong and couldn't fix it.

Galloway's expression didn't change, but something behind it did — a door closing partway, not locking. "Then let's talk about getting your people aboard."

"Not yet." Ansel stepped sideways, off the plank, blocking the view up the slope without making it obvious he was doing it. "There are things you need to understand first."

"Understand," said Galloway. Behind them on the deck, a young deckhand — Tam, the tag on his jacket said — had gone very still, watching the treeline the way prey animals watch treelines. "Ma'am— Captain. There's someone in the rocks there. Left side. Been there since we rounded the bend."

Ansel closed his eyes. "That would be Mr. Mercer."

* * *

Mercer came down to the landing at his own pace, which was the point of coming down at his own pace. He carried his rifle slung, muzzle down, which was also the point. Everything about the man was a message written in body language, and Ansel had grown fluent in it over three weeks the way you grow fluent in a language you hate.

"Captain Galloway." Mercer's smile was a professional product, applied evenly. "Imara will be glad the run went through. We've been radio-dark by design — site security. You understand."

"Do I," said Galloway.

"The survey has proprietary findings. Our backers take a dim view of leaks." Mercer's eyes moved across the Marguerite's deck with an inventorying smoothness that made Ansel's stomach turn, because he knew what Mercer was actually counting: crew, armament, cargo capacity, fuel drums. He was pricing the boat. "I'll need to inspect your manifest before anything comes ashore."

"It's a supply run," Galloway said. "Food and diesel don't leak."

"Everything leaks, Captain. That's rather my job description." Mercer turned the smile on Ansel, and it changed temperature completely. "Doctor Reyes. Good news travels fast, doesn't it. Rescue arrived."

*Rescue.* Ansel held the word up to the light and examined it, the way he'd learned to examine everything Mercer said. *Rescue arrived.* As if the boat were a delivery. As if the eleven missing scientists — the ones who'd left, the ones who'd been *walked out* overland in the night, the ones whose names Mercer had struck from the log with a straightedge and a steady hand — had simply been collected.

"Yes," Ansel heard himself say. "Wonderful news."

Junie was crying quietly at the far end of the dock, hugging a crate of oranges someone had handed her, and Mercer glanced at her the way a man glances at weather.

* * *

Voss received them in the main tent at dusk, and she was magnificent, and Ansel hated how easy it was to forget, for whole minutes at a time, what she was.

She'd changed clothes. That was the first thing. Out of field kit into a pressed shirt, hair tied back, standing beside a table laid with the good coffee she'd hoarded since week one — the coffee she'd told them was gone. She shook Galloway's hand with both of hers. She asked after the river, the season, the engine, by name. She had the Marguerite's specifications memorized, and she deployed them like gifts: *Odile Marchetti's refit, isn't it? The 1948 hull? Remarkable vessel.*

Ansel watched Galloway receive all of it politely and give almost nothing back, and he thought: *this person cannot be bought with charm, and Voss doesn't know that yet.*

"We're ahead of schedule," Voss said. "Ahead of any schedule. What Dr. Reyes and his team have documented in the lower galleries is — I'll be honest with you, Captain, because I value honesty — beyond anything in the literature. A complete civic archive. Pre-dating every known flood-layer culture in the basin by centuries. Records. Instruments. Art." Her hands moved when she talked about it, and Ansel recognized the gesture; it was the same one she'd used in the first weeks, when the wonder had still been real, or had still looked real. "The backers want it handled properly. Conserved, studied, published under controlled conditions."

"And the palisade?" Galloway asked mildly. "Part of proper handling?"

"Site security." Voss waved it off. "Remote locations attract opportunists. You passed refugee barges coming up, I imagine. Desperate people do desperate things."

Ansel kept his face still. The barges had been desperate people, yes. They'd also been the last outside contact before the radios went dark, and two of the men Mercer had walked out of camp in the night had gone toward those barges, and neither had reached them. Ansel knew this because Junie had seen the lights on the water at 3 a.m. and because Okafor had found a torn pack strap on the north trail, and because some part of him had known since then and had refused, week after week, to finish the thought.

Until now. Until a riverboat sat at their dock and rescue had a name and a contract and a secrecy clause, and Ansel could finally finish the thought, and the thought was:

*This isn't a rescue. This is a pickup.*

* * *

He caught Galloway alone afterward, at the rail of the Marguerite, where the captain stood looking upstream at the drowned terraces with an expression Ansel recognized from mirrors — the look of a person doing arithmetic they don't like.

"Walk with me," Ansel said. "Not toward the tents."

Galloway came. They walked the landing planks to the end, past the mooring lines, to where the water lapped against the pilings and the camp's lanterns couldn't quite reach.

"Talk," Galloway said.

Ansel had rehearsed this. He'd rehearsed it for three weeks, in fact, lying in his bunk listening to the generator and Mercer's boots on the gravel, composing speeches to whoever eventually came. Every version had been eloquent. All of them dissolved now, at the actual moment, and what came out instead was raw and badly ordered and true.

"Half my team is gone. Eleven people. Voss says they were rotated out for safety, that they went downriver overland with guides. There were no guides. There was one truck that came up the logging cut in the second week, at night, and it left heavy, and Mercer logged it as a supply exchange." His voice cracked on the last word and he pushed through it. "Chen found something in the deep gallery three weeks ago — that's when the radios went quiet, that's the real reason, not security. She found evidence that the archive isn't abandoned. That there are living communities downstream who descend from the people who built it. Oral traditions. Matching glyphs on their boats. She wanted to open contact, document properly, bring the descendants in as partners." He stopped. Made himself breathe. "Two days later the truck came."

Galloway hadn't moved. Hadn't blinked much, either. "And Chen?"

"In the medical tent. She fell. That's the official record." Ansel looked out at the black water. "She fell down a staircase, Captain, in a cavern with no loose stone anywhere on it, and she hasn't woken properly since, and yesterday Mercer asked me — casually, over dinner — whether head injuries like hers tend to affect memory."

The river said nothing. It never did. That was the thing about rivers; they took everything and testified to nothing, and Ansel had loved that about them his whole life, and right now he hated it.

"Why tell me?" Galloway said at last. Quiet. Not unkind. "You've got eleven witnesses to a crime and you're briefing the ferry pilot."

"Because you're the first person in three weeks who isn't on the payroll." Ansel turned. "Because your crew looked at our palisade like it was a wound. Because when I said *conditions warrant*, you heard me lie, and you let me finish anyway, which means you wanted to hear the rest." He swallowed. "And because I'm begging you, Captain. Take your supplies, take your payment, and leave. Tonight. Leave us off your log, leave this reach off your charts, and go home, and if anyone asks, tell them you found nothing but a fortified camp and a rude geologist."

Galloway watched him for a long moment. "You want me to leave. Without you."

"I want you to leave while leaving is still possible." Ansel heard his own voice drop, and hated how much it sounded like pleading, and kept going anyway. "Monsoon's coming. When it does, this whole reach drowns — the landing, the trails, the logging cut. After landfall, nobody leaves. And Voss knows it. She's not resupplying a survey anymore, Captain. She's waiting out a calendar. Whatever leaves this site leaves on your boat or not at all — and she intends for it to leave on your boat. All of it. The crates by the cavern mouth aren't samples. They're packed for transport. Numbered, strapped, crated. That's not conservation. That's loading."

For the first time, Galloway looked away — back up the slope, toward the tarp-covered stacks, toward the palisade silhouetted against the last grey light. Ansel watched the captain's jaw work once.

"And your people," Galloway said. "The ten. Junie. Okafor. Chen."

"What about them?"

"If I leave tonight, I leave them."

The question landed in Ansel's chest like a core sample, clean and deep, and everything it pulled up was ugly. Because that was the trap, wasn't it? That was the whole architecture of it. Leave, and abandon ten frightened people to a woman who'd already disappeared eleven. Stay, and become the boat that hauled it all out — complicit, paid, useful. There was no clean exit. Voss had designed it that way, or lucked into it, and Ansel suspected Voss didn't believe in luck.

"I'm asking you to save yourself," he said, "because I don't have the right to ask more. And I'm telling you the truth so that whatever you decide, you decide it knowing."

Galloway was quiet a long time. Somewhere upslope, Mercer's boots crossed the gravel, unhurried, and both of them tracked the sound without turning their heads, like animals.

"There's a monsoon ledger in my wheelhouse," Galloway said finally. "My engineer keeps it. Days to landfall, days of margin, days of lies we can afford." They looked at Ansel, and in the lantern-light from the dock their eyes were very steady. "I haven't read tonight's page yet. But I'll tell you what I already know, Doctor Reyes. I don't leave people. It's the one rule I've got, and I've broken every other rule I ever wrote down except that one."

Ansel's throat tightened. "Then you should know the rest of it. All of it. What's down there. What she's really taking."

"I intend to," Galloway said. "First light. Show me the archive."

"No." Ansel shook his head before the word was even fully out. "No — you don't understand, you can't go down there, if Mercer sees you in the galleries—"

"He won't," said Galloway. "Because I'm not going through the front." They nodded upstream, toward the drowned terraces, where the floodwater lay black and seamless over stone that had waited under the river for five hundred years. "Your cavern breathes, Doctor. I watched it from the wheelhouse an hour before we docked — cold air pushing out of the waterline along the third terrace seam, steady as an exhaust. Which means somewhere below, your archive connects to the river itself." A ghost of something that wasn't quite a smile. "I've been reading this water since before your expedition was born. If there's a way in that Mercer doesn't own, the river knows it. And so do I."

Ansel stared at them. Relief and terror arrived together, inseparable, twins — because a captain who could find a hidden door was exactly what hope required, and exactly what would get everyone killed if Voss ever saw it coming.

"Tomorrow," Ansel managed. "Before the camp wakes. I'll show you what the water keeps."

Above them, on the palisade, a lantern swung — Mercer's nightly round, beginning early. And down in the medical tent, faint through the canvas, Junie began to sing to Chen, some half-remembered song from her grandmother's village downstream, in a language none of the expedition spoke and every glyph in the archive did.

Galloway heard it too. Ansel saw them go still, listening, and saw the moment the captain understood — really understood — that the song and the stones and the water were all one sentence, and that Voss meant to tear the sentence apart and sell it by the letter.

"First light," Galloway said again, and turned back to their boat, and the Marguerite's deck lights came on one by one like a held breath finally let go.
