## 7. What the Water Keeps

Ansel Reyes came for me an hour before dawn, when the mist still lay on the water like a held breath.

"Wren." His voice was low, urgent, worn down to the wire. "You wanted to see the find. Now's the time. Before Voss is up."

I'd been sleeping on the Marguerite's deck, as I do in the low country where the night heat sits on you like a wet coat. Up here the air had teeth. I'd heard the camp all night — the generator's stutter, Mercer's men moving crates, the geologists' tents flapping — and I'd thought, more than once, that a river this old shouldn't feel this nervous.

I followed him past the survey tents, past the fortified mess hall with its sandbagged windows, down a goat track toward the terraces. He carried a storm lantern and said nothing, and I let him have the silence. A man who's been frightened long enough gets so he hoards his words like coins.

The terraces at first light were something I'll carry to my grave. The flood had pulled back just enough to bare the top courses of stone — steps, wide as a road, descending into the brown water in perfect rhythm. Not natural. Nothing natural keeps time like that. The stone was cut with channels, and in the channels ran water, clear as glass, fed from springs I couldn't see, spilling terrace to terrace down into the river like a stairway of small waterfalls.

"How long has this been under?" I asked.

"Centuries. Maybe a thousand years." Ansel crouched and touched the carved lip of a channel the way you'd touch a sleeping animal. "The high water this season is unusual. The river gave it back to us." He looked at me sideways. "That's how Voss sees it. A gift she intends to collect."

He led me down the steps to where the lowest terrace met the waterline, and there — half-drowned, hidden behind a curtain of hanging roots — was an opening in the rock. A doorway, cut square and deliberate, with a lintel stone carved in spirals that reminded me of nothing so much as the way eddies curl behind a snag.

"It floods twice a year," Ansel said. "And everything inside is still dry."

* * *

The passage sloped down and then, impossibly, the water did not follow. Ansel explained it as we walked — the cavern sat on a lens of denser stone, a natural basin the old builders had found and improved, sealed with clay and cut channels that drained the percolation away. They'd engineered the water, he said. They'd lived with it so long they'd learned to build *with* it instead of against it, the way the settlements downstream still raised their houses on poles and moved their markets with the seasons.

The lantern light found the walls first.

Carvings, everywhere, in bands that followed the rock's grain. Rivers of figures. Boats — long boats, pole-driven, not so different from the barges I'd passed coming upriver. Planting scenes. Flood scenes. A great spiral motif repeated like a signature, and at the center of each spiral, a shape I can only call a knot — water tied into itself.

Then the passage opened, and I stopped walking.

The cavern was the size of the Marguerite's whole length, twice over, and the lantern barely reached the far wall. But it reached enough. Rows of shelving cut into the living rock, and on the shelves, objects. Bundles of what looked like bark-paper, flat and sealed in wax. Tools of stone and bronze and something darker that Ansel said might be early iron. Nets. Weights. A child's toy boat, no bigger than my hand, carved with the spiral on its sail.

And along one whole wall, a frieze maybe forty feet long: the river itself, rendered from source to mouth, every tributary named — named in glyphs, hundreds of them — and every settlement marked, and at the delta, a city rendered in such detail that I could see the mooring posts along its waterfront.

"They recorded everything," Ansel said quietly. "Every flood year. Every harvest. Every treaty between the settlements. It's an archive, Wren. A living record of a civilization that read the river the way we read a page." His voice cracked. "There are flood annals in there going back further than anything in the coastal libraries. There are songs. There are maps of channels that don't exist anymore — and some that do. I've checked. Some that *do*."

I stood in front of the toy boat for a long time.

You spend ten years on a river and you think you know what a river is. Water looking for the low ground. A road that moves. A thing you read by the color of the current and the smell of the air before rain. But standing in that cavern I understood I'd been reading a single page of a very long book, and the book had been written by people who had loved this water for a thousand years before my kind ever drew a chart.

"They knew the river would keep it," I said.

Ansel nodded. "That's the theory. The archive was sealed as a dedication. Not hidden — *entrusted*. There's an inscription over the entrance. We've only partially translated it." He recited it slowly, the way you'd recite something learned by heart: *'What the water keeps, the water remembers. What the water remembers, the people are.'*

I looked at him. "The people are?"

"The descendants are still downriver. The settlements. Mama Ilo's people, if the names hold. They're the same river folk, Wren. A thousand years, same water." He spread his hands. "This doesn't belong to a university. It doesn't belong to Voss's backers. It belongs to them — or it belongs to the river, which may be the same thing."

* * *

We were climbing back toward the entrance when the light changed above us.

Voss stood at the doorway, silhouetted against the grey morning, Mercer a step behind her with his arms crossed. I don't know how long they'd been there. Long enough.

"Dr. Reyes," Voss said, and her voice was pleasant the way a contract's fine print is pleasant. "You're giving the boat captain a tour."

"Captain Galloway asked about the site," Ansel said. "It seemed reasonable to—"

"It seemed reasonable to brief the crew on operational matters, yes." She came down the steps into the cavern, her boots precise on the wet stone, and I watched her face as the lantern light reached the shelves. I've seen that expression before — on salvage men looking at a wreck, on buyers looking at a herd. It's the look of arithmetic.

"Incredible," she murmured. "Better than the preliminary survey suggested." She turned to me, and the arithmetic became charm. "Captain. Do you understand what you're looking at? The oldest continuous written record on this side of the world. The backers will endow a permanent institute. Every piece catalogued, preserved, published. This is rescue, not removal."

"Rescue," I said. "From the river."

"From the *flood*, Captain. The monsoon will drown this cavern to a depth of six meters. Everything here will be silt and mush by autumn. We're saving it." She ran her gloved hand along the frieze wall, not quite touching. "The extraction begins tomorrow. I'll need your Marguerite for the first heavy load — crates to Port Calloway ahead of the storm. There's substantial compensation in it for you."

I thought about the contract money. I thought about the Marguerite's engine, Odile's lists of parts she needed, the hull that was due for a full re-plating. A single run could buy all of it. I'd have been a liar to say the number didn't sit in my chest like a warm coal.

But I looked at the toy boat. And I looked at the frieze, at the mooring posts of the drowned city, and I thought about a thousand years of river folk reading the water, and I thought about the inscription. *What the water remembers, the people are.* You don't rescue a people's memory by selling it to the highest bidder before the people even know it's been found.

"The flood comes every year," I said. "It's come a thousand times. The archive's still here."

"The archive is here because no one *found* it," Voss said. "That luck has ended. Captain, I'm not asking your opinion. I'm informing you of the schedule." She turned for the steps, then paused. "And Doctor — in the future, my site, my personnel, my disclosures. Am I understood?"

Ansel said nothing. His jaw was tight enough to crack stone.

They climbed out. The morning light went with them, and the cavern dimmed, and I stood there in the dark among the shelves with the water talking somewhere below us, patient as it's always been.

Ansel touched my elbow. "Now you've seen it," he whispered. "Now you understand why I begged you to leave. Because once Voss starts loading, she doesn't stop. And once it's in Port Calloway, it's gone — into private vaults, into backer collections. The river's memory, locked in the dark where no river folk will ever see it."

I looked back once at the toy boat on its shelf, its little spiral sail, waiting a thousand years in the dark for someone to come back down the river.

"Then we'd best think fast," I said. "Because I don't intend to be the ferryman who carries it away."

Above us, faint through the entrance, I heard Mercer's voice giving orders, and the sound of hammers building crates.
