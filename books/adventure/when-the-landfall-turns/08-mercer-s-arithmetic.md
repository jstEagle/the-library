## 8. Mercer's Arithmetic

I learned to count from my grandmother, who kept a stall of dried fish at the Port Calloway market and could weigh a kilo by hand within a gram. She taught me that numbers were honest the way rivers are honest — they don't care what you want them to say. You can shout at a ledger all day. It stays true.

That's why I knew something was wrong with Mercer before I ever saw him touch anything.

It was the way he counted.

* * *

The morning after Wren came up out of the cavern looking like someone had shown them their own funeral, Mercer set up his inventory station on the second terrace — a folding table, a canvas awning, a battery lamp even in daylight because he liked the shadows under people's eyes to be visible. He had two of Voss's security men with him, Okonkwo and the quiet one everyone called Deacon because he never spoke, only nodded. Between them they'd built a little kingdom of clipboards and stenciled crates, and they'd drafted four scientists to work it: Ansel, a botanist named Ferro whose hands shook so badly she'd started dropping things, a young sedimentologist called Priya Nair who couldn't have been more than twenty-five, and old Halvorsen, the surveyor, who moved like a man carrying his own headstone.

The system was simple, which is how you know it was designed to be cruel. Every artifact that came up the ladder from the cavern got logged twice — once in Mercer's ledger, once on a manifest card tied to the crate. The numbers were supposed to match. But Mercer's ledger lived on his side of the table, and the manifest cards lived on the scientists' side, and every hour or so he'd call out a discrepancy in a voice pitched exactly loud enough for the whole camp to hear.

"Nair. Item forty-one. Your card says basalt, my entry says schist."

"It's basalt," Priya said. "I held it. It's—"

"Then correct your card." He didn't look up. "Or would you rather explain to Dr. Voss why her manifests can't be reconciled?"

Priya corrected her card. The stone was basalt. It stayed basalt. But now the record said whatever Mercer wanted it to say, and Priya's name was attached to the correction, and everybody standing there understood the transaction perfectly: the truth was available, but it cost more than any of them could pay.

I watched this from the Marguerite's rail, maybe sixty meters off, where I'd been sent to inventory our own cargo — which was rich, when you think about it, the thief making the crew count their spoons while he stole a civilization. Bex stood beside me pretending to help, which meant handing me rope coils and narrating.

"He's not even cataloguing," Bex said. "Look. He doesn't write descriptions. Just counts. Weight, quantity, condition grade. That's not science, that's a shipping manifest."

"That's an auction house," I said.

"Same thing with better lighting."

Ferro dropped a clay vessel that morning. Not far — knee height onto packed earth — and it survived, a chip out of the rim, nothing. Mercer made her stand at attention and recite the loss protocol back to him while he wrote her name in a column, and the way he wrote it, slow, savoring, told you the column wasn't about losses. It was about leverage. By noon he had nine names in it. By evening, fourteen. He never raised his voice. He never had to. He'd found the exact weight each of those people could carry and he added grams daily, patient as water finding the crack in a hull.

Odile watched from the engine room hatch, arms crossed, and said one thing all day: "In the merchant marine we had a word for officers like that."

"What word?"

"We didn't use words. We used the bilge pump. Things fall overboard. Very sad. Very slippery deck."

"Bex is already planning something," I said. "Don't give him ideas."

"I'm giving him *techniques*," Odile said, and went back below.

* * *

Here's what nobody outside the Survey camp understood yet, and what I want to set down plainly, because it matters to everything that came after: Mercer wasn't packing artifacts. He was packing a *story*. The crates were being built in sequence — I checked, I'm good at sequences, my grandmother's fish stall ran on sequence — and the first crates filled were the ones nearest the ladder, the ones that would come out first if the monsoon schedule collapsed. He wasn't trying to save everything. He was building a load-out where the most valuable, most portable, most *deniable* pieces left first, and everything bulky or fragile or hard to explain got abandoned to the flood as collateral damage.

He was doing arithmetic. That's all it was. Value divided by weight, times days remaining, minus risk. My grandmother would have recognized the method instantly. She'd have also spat at his feet, because in her arithmetic there was always a column he didn't have: what the thing itself would say, if things could talk.

These things could talk. That was the whole problem. I'd been down in the cavern once by then — Wren took me, second descent, mostly because I was small enough for the narrow gallery where the shelf-niches ran along the walls like a library built by patient hands. Thousands of records. Bundles of treated fiber, tablets of some pressed clay-pitch composite, carved tallies, painted bark. A river people who'd spent centuries writing down what the water did, year over year, flood over flood. Floods going back further than any nation I've ever heard named.

And Mercer's ledger had a column for none of it. His column headers were: ITEM. QTY. WT. GRADE. DEST.

DEST. Destination. As in, where does it go when it leaves here forever.

I stood in that gallery with a lamp in my hand and thought about my grandmother weighing fish, and I felt something in my chest go from hot to cold, which is, I've since learned, what resolve feels like when it finishes setting. Anger is hot. Resolve is cold. Anger wants to shout. Resolve starts counting.

So I counted.

* * *

The radio log falsification happened on the fourth night, and I caught it by accident, which embarrasses me — I'd like to say I was running some clever surveillance operation, but really I'd gone up to the wheelhouse to steal an hour with Wren's charts because I'd been teaching myself the upper channels off the soundings, and the radio shack key was hanging on its hook unattended.

Context, for anyone reading this who wasn't there: the Marguerite carried a long-range set, but the Survey camp had its own relay rig, a proper mast and a licensed frequency, because the Halcyon backers wanted daily position reports filed downstream to the River Authority. Standard practice for expeditions in flood season. The Authority logs were public record. Any patrol boat, any settlement station, any family with a receiver could see where the Halcyon Survey was and that it was fine.

The log book sat open on the desk. Three weeks of entries in Ansel's careful hand, then Voss's initials taking over after the silence began, then — for the last six days — entries in a third hand. Blocky, mechanical, no flourishes. Mercer's.

I read them the way you'd proof a manifest, because that's what I do, and the discrepancy walked right up and introduced itself.

Every entry reported the same coordinates. 4°41'S, 61°12'W — grid square K-9, the lower bend, thirty-some kilometers downriver of where we actually lay. And every entry said the same thing in different words: SURVEY PROCEEDING. CAMP SECURE. NO ANOMALIES.

No anomalies. Six days of no anomalies, filed from a position thirty kilometers from the truth, while Mercer's men built crates on the terraces above a drowned archive and a wounded scientist lay fevered in the medical tent.

I stood there holding the log book and did the math my grandmother taught me, and the math said: this man is erasing us. Not hiding us — *erasing* us. Because a false position isn't just concealment. It's a trap. If anything went wrong — if the storm hit early, if the camp flooded, if Mercer decided witnesses were a liability — the Authority would send rescue boats to grid square K-9, to empty water, and by the time anyone worked out the error, there'd be nothing left to find and no names left to find it. The log would say the survey was proceeding calmly at a location where no survey existed. Paper survives floods better than people do. He knew that.

Footsteps on the deck plates. I put the log down exactly where it had been, squared to the desk edge, and stepped into the chart locker shadow, and Deacon came in, took the log, initialed the night's entry — 2200 HRS, ALL QUIET, POSITION CONFIRMED — and hung the key back on its hook, and left, and never knew I was three feet away breathing through my mouth.

I waited until his footsteps faded, then I went and woke Wren.

* * *

Wren listened the way they always listen, which is completely still, eyes somewhere past you, like they're reading a river surface for what's underneath. When I finished, they were quiet long enough that I started talking again, faster, and they held up one hand.

"Say the coordinates again."

I said them. Wren closed their eyes. "K-9 is the Sallow Bend. Shallows, deadfall, a sandbar that migrates every season. Nobody stations a survey there. It's not even a plausible lie — any Authority hydrographer will look at that grid and know it's wrong."

"So he doesn't care if it's plausible."

"No," Wren said. "He cares that it's *consistent*. A plausible lie changes. A consistent lie holds shape. He's not fooling the Authority. He's building a paper trail for whoever comes asking later — his backers, lawyers, an inquiry. 'The survey reported its position daily and it never varied.' It's armor, not camouflage." They opened their eyes. "Which means he expects questions. Eventually. From somebody with standing."

"An inquiry," I said. "Like the kind that happens when an expedition goes silent and boats get seized and scientists stop sending letters home."

"Exactly like that." Wren rubbed their jaw. "Tam. Did anyone see you in the shack?"

"Deacon came in. I hid."

"And the log?"

"Untouched. Exactly as it was."

"Good." Wren stood, went to the window, looked out at the camp — the generator hut humming, the crate stacks growing on the terrace, the mast light blinking its idiot reassurance downriver to a world being lied to. "We don't touch the log. We don't confront him. If we tip him now, he burns the evidence and accelerates. We copy it instead. Every page. Dates, times, initials, coordinates. And starting tonight, we keep our own log — real positions, real events, witnessed, dated. Paper against paper. When this breaks, and it's going to break, we want to be the ones holding the honest ledger."

"You want me to run a counter-log."

"I want you to run the truth," Wren said, "in a format nobody can argue with. Can you do that?"

My grandmother weighed fish by hand within a gram. I said, "I've been doing it since the day we arrived. I just didn't have permission to write it down."

Wren almost smiled. "Permission granted."

* * *

We aligned quietly, the way cargo shifts in a hold — no single crate moves enough to notice, but the center of gravity changes, and eventually the whole boat lists, and by then it's too late to stop.

Odile, told about the log, said only: "Generator fuel. I count the drums every morning. If the number ever changes without my counting it, I'll know whose arithmetic it is." Which was Odile for *I'm in, and I have my own ledger.*

Bex, told over the galley stove at midnight, stirred a pot that didn't need stirring and said, "Deacon eats first at every meal. Always first in line. You know what that means?" I said no. "Means he trusts the food least. Means he watches everything, including me. So I'll feed him extra, and smile, and he'll watch me smiling, and meanwhile you'll be copying papers. Misdirection is just hospitality pointed the wrong way." Which was Bex for *I'm in, and I've already started.*

Ansel, told at dawn on the terrace while Mercer's men hammered crates below us, went gray and then went steady. "Six days of false reports," he whispered. "The Authority thinks we're at Sallow Bend. Tam, if the storm—" He stopped. Looked down at the hammering. Looked at me. And I watched the same transition happen in him that had happened in me in the cavern gallery: heat going to cold, anger becoming arithmetic. "I have the original field notebooks," he said. "Three weeks, my hand, real positions, real dates. They're hidden. They stay hidden."

"Copy them anyway," I said. "Two copies. Different places."

He looked at me with something like relief — the relief of a man discovering he's not the only one keeping books. "You're the deckhand," he said.

"I'm the one who counts," I said.

* * *

On the fifth day, Mercer came down to the Marguerite.

Not to the wheelhouse, not to inspect — he came to the gangway where I was coiling line, and he stood there a moment in his neat field jacket with his clipboard under his arm, and he smiled, and it was the worst smile I've ever been given, because it was warm the way a snare is warm, baited and patient.

"Tam, isn't it? Deckhand." He consulted the clipboard theatrically. "You've been very thorough with the boat's stores. Dr. Voss appreciates order."

"We aim to please."

"Mm." He let that sit. Then, lightly, conversationally, watching my face the whole time: "Funny thing. Radio logs. You'd be surprised how often they get handled by people who shouldn't. Oils from the skin, you know. Smudges. Pages slightly out of square." He tapped the clipboard. "I notice things like that. It's my job to notice things like that."

The river moved under the hull. Somewhere up-terrace, a hammer struck, paused, struck.

"If someone was curious about the radio schedule," Mercer went on, "the correct procedure would be to ask. Dr. Voss runs an open expedition. Transparency is one of our values." He smiled again. "Curiosity handled any other way tends to end up in the loss column. You've seen the loss column. Names in it tend to lose privileges. Shore leave. Rations beyond standard. Access." He glanced meaningfully toward the wheelhouse, where Wren's silhouette stood at the window. "Even contracts can develop discrepancies. Contracts are just logs, when you think about it. Entries and corrections. And corrections can always be made."

I looked at him. I thought about my grandmother's scale, the honest needle, the gram. I thought about Priya correcting a true card to match a false ledger, and Ferro reciting protocols for a chipped pot, and six nights of ALL QUIET filed from a sandbar where nobody was.

"I'll remember the procedure," I said.

"I know you will." He tucked the clipboard away. "That's what I'm counting on."

He walked up the gangway, and I stood there with the line in my hands, and my hands were steady, which surprised me, because inside I was colder than I'd ever been on any water this river had ever thrown at us.

That night I opened the counter-log — a plain notebook from Bex's galley stores, unremarkable, kept wrapped in oilcloth inside the flour bin, because nobody audits a flour bin — and I wrote the date, the time, the true coordinates, and the entry:

*Mercer aware of observation. Threat delivered re: contracts. No action taken. Watching.*

Below it, the running tally I'd started keeping, the real inventory, the one with different column headers than his. Where his said ITEM, QTY, WT, GRADE, DEST, mine said:

*Names protected. Truths copied. Days remaining. What the water keeps.*

Four columns. Growing daily.

And on the sixth morning, when I went up to check the horizon the way Tam-checks-things, the way my grandmother checked weather off the market roof — I saw the clouds stacking over the western cordillera in a wall the color of wet slate, building fast, much too fast, five days ahead of any schedule Odile had drawn.

I went and found Wren at the wheel and said, "The arithmetic just changed. Somebody moved the deadline."

Above the terraces, faint and steady, the hammers kept driving nails into crates that were never going to make it down this river — and behind me, in the flour bin, the true ledger waited, gaining weight by the hour, heavier than anything Mercer would ever lift.
