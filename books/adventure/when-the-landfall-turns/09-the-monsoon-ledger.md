## 9. The Monsoon Ledger

Odile Ferro had a superstition about numbers: they never lied, but they always waited until the worst moment to tell you the truth. She'd learned it in the engine rooms of coastal freighters, where a bearing that "felt fine" would give her a vibration reading three days before it seized, and the reading never came on a calm morning. It came at night. It came when there was no port within reach.

So she did not trust the feeling in her gut as she sat in the Marguerite's engine room at dawn, grease pencil in hand, a fresh page of the logbook open on her knee. She trusted the arithmetic instead.

The barometer had been falling for thirty-six hours — slowly, stubbornly, the way a man descends stairs he doesn't want to go down. The radio operator at the relay station, back when they'd still been allowed clear signal, had read out the synoptic report twice: a depression forming over the high plateau, tracking southwest, strengthening. Monsoon season didn't arrive like weather. It arrived like a decision someone else had already made.

Odile drew the line. That was the first number: the barometer's slope, extrapolated forward, crossing the threshold where the Serrano's headwater channels went from difficult to suicidal. Landfall, best estimate, nine days. Give or take a day either way, and she wrote *give* and *take* in the margin because she refused to pretend precision she didn't have.

Second number: the run downstream. Two hundred and eleven river miles from the Survey camp to the last safe anchorage above the delta narrows. At full steam, with the water rising behind them — which helped, up to a point, and then very much didn't — four days if nothing broke. Five if something did. Something always did.

Third number: loading time. Crates, personnel, the wounded scientist who couldn't climb a ladder unassisted. Two days, minimum, done carefully. Done under Voss's supervision, with Mercer counting every crate like a man tallying sins, call it three.

Nine minus five minus three left one day of slack. One day. On a river that moved its sandbars in the dark.

Odile looked at the number for a long time. Then she closed the logbook, wiped her hands on a rag that was cleaner than her hands, and went to find Wren.

She found them at the rail, watching the water. That was the thing about Wren Galloway that Odile had understood within a month of signing aboard, ten years ago now, and had never once said aloud: Wren didn't watch the river the way other people watched scenery. They watched it the way a person watches a sleeping animal they love. Total attention. A kind of tenderness with a knife hidden in it.

"Nine days," Odile said.

Wren didn't turn around. "I felt it come off the water this morning." A pause. "Nine?"

"Give or take."

"Then we tell Voss today. We start staging cargo tomorrow. We're on the water by day six at the latest."

"That's what I wanted to hear." Odile leaned on the rail beside them. Below, the brown current shouldered past the hull, heavy with silt from country they'd never see. "Wren. There's a second part to the arithmetic."

"I know."

"You don't know yet. You know the shape of it. The second part is that Voss isn't going to want us to leave on day six. She's going to want us to leave when *she's* finished, and she is nowhere near finished. Have you seen what they're doing up at the terraces? They've built a tramway. A *tramway*, Wren. You don't build a tramway for a two-day load-out."

Wren was quiet. Downriver, a heron stood in the shallows with the patience of something that had never once needed to hurry.

"Then we make it plain," Wren said finally. "Contract says resupply and retrieval. It says nothing about becoming a warehouse barge."

"The contract also has a clause in it that says we follow the expedition leader's directives regarding safety and security." Odile had read the whole thing twice, lying in her bunk with a lamp, because she was the kind of woman who read contracts the way other people read horoscopes — looking for the sentence that would get them killed. "I'm telling you how she'll use it. 'For safety.' Those exact words. Watch."

* * *

Voss received them in the survey tent at midmorning, and Odile watched her do it exactly as predicted, which gave Odile no satisfaction at all and confirmed everything.

"Nine days," Voss repeated. She stood over a table of charts with her sleeves rolled, and she had the particular energy of a woman who had been awake since four and considered this a virtue. "Your estimate, Engineer Ferro?"

"Barometric trend, upstream reports, and the color of the water," Odile said. "I'd stake the boat on it. In fact I am staking the boat on it. We need to begin loading by tomorrow and cast off no later than day six."

"Day six." Voss turned to the chart, ran one finger along the river as though measuring it against her own priorities. "Dr. Reyes's documentation won't be complete by then. Half the archive hasn't been catalogued. If we abandon it now—"

"We're not abandoning anything," Wren said. "We're coming back after landfall season. The water will keep it. That's what water does."

Something crossed Voss's face — quick, quickly smoothed — and Odile thought: *there*. Not disappointment. Calculation. As if Wren had said something useful without meaning to.

"Here's my concern," Voss said, and her voice changed registers, became reasonable, became almost warm. "If the Marguerite leaves and the storm shifts north — storms do shift, Captain, you know this better than anyone — and we're stranded here with a skeleton crew and no functioning radio relay, then everything your crew has risked coming upriver for is wasted. Worse than wasted. People die in landfall season waiting for boats that can't run." She laid both hands flat on the chart. "I'm ordering the Marguerite held at this camp through the loading period. For safety. Yours and ours."

"There's no storm here yet," Tam said from the tent flap, where he'd materialized the way he did, silent as river fog.

"And there's no argument yet, either," Voss said pleasantly. "But there will be both, and I'd rather have neither. Captain, your contract's section eleven. I'll have a copy sent to your cabin so we're all working from the same text."

She smiled at them then, all of them, the smile of a hostess seeing guests to the door, and Odile walked back down to the boat with her jaw tight enough to ache.

"For safety," she muttered.

"Told you," she added, to Wren.

"You told me." Wren stood at the gangplank, looking up at their own boat as if seeing it from outside for the first time — moored to a bank that wasn't hers, under orders that weren't hers, fifty feet of steel and oak that had been home longer than any address Odile had ever heard Wren admit to. "Section eleven," Wren said quietly. "I remember signing it. I remember thinking it was boilerplate."

"Everything's boilerplate until somebody reads it out loud with soldiers standing behind her."

Mercer was on the bank, ostensibly supervising crate stowage, actually watching the Marguerite. He watched her the way a man watches a horse he intends to buy cheap. Odile made a note of it, because she made notes of everything now: times, faces, which guards walked which perimeter, which scientists flinched. She kept the notes written small, in engine-room shorthand, folded into the back of the vibration log where no civilian would ever look. A ledger of her own.

* * *

That evening the claustrophobia set in properly, the way it does on rivers when the banks draw close and the sky goes the color of wet slate.

It wasn't the camp that pressed. It was the stillness. The Marguerite had not been tied up for more than a night at a stretch in ten years — Odile knew this because she maintained the engine, and an engine tells you everything about how a boat lives. The Marguerite lived moving. Her shafts seated right when she ran daily; her fuel stayed sweet when it turned over; her hull breathed. Tied to a bank in dead water, she began, within days, to feel wrong underfoot — a faint sluggishness in the deck plates, a different note in the bilge pump. Boats were like people that way. Kept still too long, they started to sour.

And Wren — Odile watched Wren the way she watched gauges, because Wren would never report their own readings honestly.

On the third evening of the hold, Wren stood at the wheel long after there was any reason to stand there. Hands on the spokes. Eyes on the channel ahead, on water they weren't going to cross, on a bend they weren't going to round. Just standing, holding the boat's course toward a place they could not go.

Odile climbed to the wheelhouse with two cups of Bex's terrible coffee and handed one over without asking.

"They've got me checking Mercer's inventory manifests now," she said. "Against the actual crates. He wants discrepancies reported to him directly, not to Voss. Which means either he doesn't trust her bookkeeping or he doesn't want her knowing his."

"Which do you think?"

"I think a man who falsifies things wants company." Odile blew on the coffee. "Wren. Talk to me about the real problem."

Wren's hands flexed on the wheel spokes, once, like a heartbeat.

"She's taking the boat away from me without touching it," Wren said. "That's the real problem. I keep standing here thinking — if she ordered us off the Marguerite, I'd know what to do. That's a fight. But she's done it sideways. Paper and permission and *for safety*. And every morning I wake up and the river's right there and I can't go to it, and I catch myself being angry at the *river*, Odile. At the water. For holding still." A short laugh with nothing in it. "Ten years I've measured my life in miles. Right now I have zero miles, and I don't know who I am at zero."

Odile drank her coffee and let the silence sit, because she was an engineer and she knew that some machinery you don't touch while it's running hot.

"Here's mine, then," she said at last. "Since we're trading. My real problem is that nine days is now six, and the loading hasn't started, and Voss knows the arithmetic better than she pretends to. She's not slow because she's disorganized. She's slow because every day she holds us here is a day our option gets worse. She's spending our clock, Wren. And when the clock runs low enough, she's going to offer you a choice that looks like no choice at all — run her cargo out ahead of the storm, or lose the window entirely and explain to the inquiry why you let the monsoon strand a scientific expedition."

"She wouldn't strand her own people."

"Wouldn't she?" Odile nodded toward the shore, toward the tents, toward the half-empty camp where the missing scientists' cots still stood made up. "Half her team's already gone somewhere, and nobody will say where. I count crates for a living, Wren. I know when the manifest doesn't match the hold."

Below them, along the bank, the lanterns of the Survey camp came alight one by one, and beyond them the terraces rose black against a sky bruising toward rain. Somewhere up there, under the floodwater that would come, lay the archive — whatever it truly was — and somewhere between the archive and the river stood Mercer's tramway, and Mercer's men, and Mercer's lists.

Wren looked down at the wheel, at their own hands on it, and Odile saw something settle in them — not peace, nothing like peace, but the specific stillness of a decision assembling itself out of parts.

"Keep your ledger," Wren said. "Every discrepancy. Every hour of that clock. When I ask you for the numbers, I want them true."

"They're always true," Odile said. "That's the trouble with numbers."

She was halfway down the wheelhouse ladder when Tam's whistle cut across the deck — two short, one long, the signal they'd invented years ago for *come look at this, quietly*. She found him crouched at the radio locker, where the Survey's spare set had been lashed for the return trip, and he didn't say anything at all. He just tilted the logbook into the lamplight and put his finger on the entry.

The position fix. Logged in Mercer's hand, timed two nights ago. And wrong — wrong by forty miles south, deep into country where no relief launch would think to search, where any distress call from the Marguerite would be answered by nobody at all.

Odile looked at the false coordinates for a long moment, and then at the falling glass, and then at the six days remaining on her ledger, and understood that the clock she'd been counting had never been the only clock in play.

"Wake Wren," she said.
