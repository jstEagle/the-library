## 10. Downstream Kin

Mama Ilo had dreamed of the water speaking for nine nights running, and on the ninth night she woke her grandson and told him to ready the boat.

The dream was always the same. A stairway of stone going down into green light, and on every step a bowl filled with words that no one alive could read — and then the water rising over the bowls, patient as an old woman filling jars at dawn, saying: *not yet, not yet, but soon someone will come who does not understand what they are carrying.*

She was seventy-one years old and she had been listening to the Serrano since before she had teeth. The river did not speak in words. It spoke in timing — in when the fish ran shallow, in which year the reeds came up thick along the banks, in how the fog sat on the water some mornings like it was waiting for something. Her grandmother had taught her to read it, and her grandmother's grandmother before that, all the way back to women whose names were kept in songs instead of ledgers. The river people kept their history that way: sung, not written. Water remembers better than paper anyway. Paper burns.

But lately the river had been singing wrong.

It started with the survey boats, months back — the ones with the tall antennas and the polite men who asked questions about the terraces above the narrows, about whether anyone fished there, about whether anyone *went* there. Ilo had told them what she always told outsiders: the high terraces belonged to the old stories, and the old stories said leave them be. The men smiled and wrote things down and paid her in salt and lamp oil, and she took it because refusing gifts was its own kind of insult, and because she knew already that men who write things down do not stop because an old woman tells them to.

Then the boats stopped coming back down. And the river began to sing wrong.

* * *

The boat that carried them upriver was her nephew Doss's, flat-bottomed and slow, with a canopy of woven palm against the sun. There were six of them: Ilo; Doss; her granddaughter Sefi, who was seventeen and thought dreams were superstition until the dreams started coming to *her*; old Kessu, who remembered the songs best of anyone living; and two men from the far settlements below the delta who had come because rumor traveled faster than any boat — rumor of lights under the water at night, of strangers hauling things out of the earth above the flood line, of a silence on the radio bands where the relay station used to chatter.

They went upriver by day and tied off by night, and each evening Kessu sang the traveling verses, the ones meant to keep the water friendly to a hull. And each evening the water felt less friendly. Not hostile — Ilo wanted to be clear about that, even inside her own head. The river was never hostile. But it was *loud*, the way a house is loud when the family is arguing behind a closed door.

On the fourth day they smelled woodsmoke where no settlement should be, and on the fifth day they rounded the bend below the drowned terraces and saw the camp.

Ilo had imagined tents and instruments. What she saw was a fort. Sandbagged positions. A generator shed with a wire fence around it. Men with slung rifles walking a perimeter like the jungle itself might file a claim. And moored at the bank, riding low with cargo, a riverboat — white wheelhouse, working lines coiled like a sailor actually loved her, a name on the bow half worn away: *Marguerite*.

"There," said Sefi, and pointed up at the cliff face above the camp, where the high water had pulled back early this season and exposed stone that should have stayed drowned. Even from the river Ilo could see the carving — long lines of it, regular as stitching, running along the terrace edge like a hem sewn into the world.

Kessu went very still. Then he began, very quietly, to sing.

It was not one of the traveling verses. It was older — the opening of the Lament of the First Landing, the song they only sang at funerals and at the turning of the century, and even then only the first three lines, because the rest was considered too heavy to carry casually. His voice cracked on the second line and he stopped, pressing his hand flat against the gunwale as if steadying himself against the boat or the boat against himself.

"You know it," Ilo said. It wasn't a question.

"I know where it comes from," Kessu said. "My teacher's teacher said these stones were the first page. We thought it was a story to explain why we sing. It isn't a story." He looked at the carved line above the camp with something close to grief. "It's a *remainder*. Everything else washed away except this."

Doss brought the boat alongside the bank below the camp, and almost immediately a man came down the slope to meet them — big, gray at the temples, moving with the unhurried economy of someone who had done violence before and found it dull. He had a rifle slung across his back and a clipboard in his hand, which Ilo thought was the most honest thing she'd seen in years: the weapon and the paperwork together, the way power actually travels.

"You can't land here," he said. "This is a restricted scientific site."

"We are not scientists," Ilo said. "We are the people of this river."

"Then you'll want to go back downstream. There's nothing here for you."

"Everything here is ours," Ilo said mildly. "You are standing on it."

Something flickered across his face — irritation, mostly, but underneath it something quicker, like a man checking a lock. "The site is under lease from the regional authority," he said. "Dr. Voss has the permits."

"Permits," Ilo repeated, tasting the word. She turned to Doss. "Did you hear? The river has been permitted."

"Mama," Doss said carefully, "maybe we should—"

"No." She hadn't come four days upriver to be turned around by a man with a clipboard. "Tell your Dr. Voss that elders of the river settlements have come to see the place our grandmothers sang about. Tell her we will wait at the bank. Tell her that if she will not come down to us, we will still be here tomorrow, and the day after. We are river people. Waiting is what we do best."

The man with the clipboard looked at her for a long moment. Whatever he saw in her face made him turn and climb back up without another word.

* * *

They waited through the heat of the afternoon. Toward evening, a woman came down alone.

She was tall, silver-haired, moving like someone accustomed to being obeyed at a distance. She stopped a careful ten paces from the boat — Ilo noted the distance, noted that it was exactly the range at which a rifle from the perimeter would still be effective — and introduced herself as Dr. Imara Voss.

"You've come a long way," Voss said.

"The river is short," Ilo said. "Only people make it long."

Voss smiled the smile of a woman who had been charmed by river people before and had learned to price it. "I understand you have... traditions connected to this area. I respect that. But this is an active archaeological operation. There are unstable structures, deep water, equipment. It genuinely isn't safe for visitors."

"Unstable," Ilo said. "The stones have held the river for a thousand years. They became unstable when you arrived?"

"They became *accessible* when the drought pulled the water back," Voss said, and there it was — the first crack in the courtesy, a flash of the true thing underneath. "Which is why the work is happening now, and why it can't be interrupted. In a few weeks the monsoon closes the whole headwaters. If we don't recover the materials this season, they're lost to everyone."

"Lost to everyone," Ilo said softly. "Or found by everyone. Those are different losses, Doctor. You have chosen which one frightens you."

Voss's jaw tightened. For a moment neither woman spoke, and the river spoke for both of them, sliding past the hull with that low continuous voice it had, the one that never took sides.

"I'm offering you a courtesy," Voss said finally. "Most operations wouldn't bother. You can't stay here. If your people want compensation for — cultural disruption, or whatever term applies — my backers have a fund for community relations. Reasonable sums. I can arrange it through the delta office."

And there it was, Ilo thought. The whole machinery of the thing laid out plain as a fish trap: the fund, the office, the reasonable sum. Money flowing downstream so silence could flow upstream. Her grandmother had seen the timber men do it. Her mother had seen the dredging companies do it. Every generation, someone arrived with a ledger and discovered that the river people had a price, and every generation the discovery was wrong.

"We will take no money," Ilo said. "We will not go away. And we will remember, Doctor, exactly how you spoke to us today, on the day when the water takes back everything you've packed into those crates."

For the first time, Voss looked genuinely unsettled — not by the threat, Ilo suspected, but by the certainty behind it. "The monsoon is a scheduling problem," she said. "Not a judgment."

"Everything is a judgment," Ilo said. "The river has just decided more slowly than you."

Voss stood another moment, then turned and walked back up the slope without goodbye. Halfway up she paused and spoke to the big man with the clipboard, low, and he nodded, and Ilo watched him glance back down at the boat twice before disappearing into the camp.

"They'll move us along tonight if they can," Doss said quietly.

"Yes," Ilo said. "So we won't be here tonight."

* * *

They shifted the boat a mile downriver, into a channel mouth choked with fallen bamboo where no patrol would bother looking, and tied off among the roots. Sefi cooked rice and dried fish on a small fire screened by the canopy, and they ate as the light went copper and then violet over the water, and Kessu taught Sefi the second verse of the Lament — the one about the bowls full of words — because, he said, if the stones really were the first page, then somebody from the singing side of the family ought to read them before the writing side burned them.

Ilo was half asleep against the gunwale when she heard the oars.

Not an engine. Oars — muffled, wrapped, the stroke of someone who knew water well enough to be ashamed of the noise they made. She put her hand on Sefi's arm and the girl went silent mid-verse, and they all listened as a shape came up the channel against the last gray light: a small skiff, two figures in it, one rowing and one sitting forward with a lantern shuttered to a coin of glow.

The skiff eased alongside. The rower shipped the oars and said, quietly, in accented river-speech: "Mama Ilo? The captain sent me. Wren Galloway, of the *Marguerite*. She couldn't come herself — Mercer counts heads at dark. But she heard what you said to Voss on the bank, and she wanted you to know—" the figure hesitated, "—she wanted to meet you. Properly. Away from the fences."

"And you are?" Ilo asked.

"Tam. Deckhand." A pause. "Runaway, originally. The river took me in before any crew did. So I suppose I'm yours too, a little."

Ilo studied the shape of the young one in the dark — the careful hands, the way they sat balanced in a skiff like the water was furniture. "A little is how belonging starts," she said. "Come aboard. Mind the pot."

* * *

They met an hour later, in the deepest fold of the channel, Wren Galloway's skiff tied to Doss's boat and lanterns lit at last because pretending further would have been its own insult. Ilo had expected the captain of a contract boat to look like the men with clipboards. Instead she found a person with river-gray eyes and rope-callused hands and the particular tiredness of someone who has spent ten years being competent so no one would ask them anything else.

Wren sat across from her with a cup of Sefi's tea held in both hands like something that might still be saved, and told it plainly: the contract, the secrecy clause, the silence on the radio, the drowned archive beneath the terraces, the crates, the crane, Mercer's inventory lists, the orders Bex had found — extract, document, and burn what remained. Wren said *burn* the way most people say a word they've been chewing for days without swallowing.

"I don't hold the deed to any of this," Wren finished. "I hold a boat and a contract. Voss holds the permits and the guns. You hold—" Wren stopped, and looked at Kessu, and at Sefi, and back. "You hold whatever it is that makes those stones yours. I've watched you sit on this water for four days without asking for anything. That's not how the people with ledgers operate. So I came to ask what you want. Not what you'll settle for. What you want."

Ilo let the question sit. Around them the channel breathed, the bamboo creaking, the current worrying the hulls — the sound of a house where the family talks all night.

"I will tell you what my grandmother told me," she said at last. "When the timber men came, she asked her own mother what to do, and her mother said: *the river does not keep things. It carries them.* Everything the water loves, it eventually takes downstream and gives to the sea, and the sea gives it to the rain, and the rain gives it back to the hills. Nothing is owned. Everything is *entrusted*, for a while, to whoever is strong enough to carry it gently." She set down her cup. "Those stones under the water were entrusted to us. Not to me — to us, to all the settlements, to the children not born yet. Our job was never to dig them up. Our job was to be the reason no one else did, until the water wanted them back."

"And now?" Wren asked.

"Now the water is taking them back early, because someone pried the door open." Ilo looked toward the north, where the sky over the headwaters had gone the bruised color it got before the monsoon truly committed. "Doctor Voss thinks the flood is her deadline. It is not. It is the river collecting its loan." She turned back to Wren, and held the captain's gaze the way you hold a rope — firmly, but ready to pay out. "What I want is simple and impossible. I want the archive to stay with the water. I want the people Voss trapped up there to come home alive. And I want to know, Captain Galloway, which of those two things you will choose when you can no longer choose both."

Wren didn't answer right away. Wren looked down into the tea, and Ilo saw the loneliness there, ten years deep, miles stacked on miles like sandbags against a flood that never comes — and saw, too, the exact moment the captain understood that the flood had come, and it was this, and it was now.

"When I was young," Wren said finally, "an old pilot told me a boat isn't the hull. It's the wake. It's what you leave behind you on the water." Wren set the cup down. "I haven't always left much worth leaving. But I'm telling you now, Mama Ilo, so you can hold me to it: I didn't come up this river to haul away a dead civilization's bones for men with ledgers. I came to bring people home. All of them. Yours and Voss's both, if it comes to that — though I suspect Voss would rather sink than be rescued by me."

"Then you will need help," Ilo said. "And you will need it soon. The storm is five days out, maybe less — the birds went quiet this morning, and the ants moved their eggs uphill, and my knees, which have never once lied to me, ache like drums." She reached across and gripped Wren's forearm, river-fashion, the grip that meant *witnessed*. "Bring me your wounded, your frightened, your scientists who no longer believe in their doctor. My settlements will shelter every one. And when the time comes — and it is coming, child, sooner than you think — you will know what to do with the archive. Because you already know what I know."

"What's that?" Wren asked.

"That belonging cannot be charted like miles." Ilo released the grip. "Now go, before Mercer counts heads and finds one missing. And send me that runaway Tam again sometime. Anyone the river adopted that young should learn the third verse of the Lament before the season turns."

Wren laughed — short, surprised, real — and rose, and paused at the rail of the skiff.

"Mama Ilo. If I do this — if I break the contract, if I cross Voss — there may be inquiries afterward. Lawyers. People with clipboards of their own. They'll ask me where the site is, and I'll have to decide what to say."

"Say what the river says," Ilo told her.

"Which is?"

"Nothing," she said. "Loudly, forever."

Wren held her eyes a moment longer, and something passed between them that needed no verse and no permit — the recognition, old as boats, of one keeper acknowledging another. Then the captain dropped into the skiff, and Tam took up the muffled oars, and the two shapes slid away down the black channel toward the fenced camp and the crates and the counting of heads.

Sefi came to stand beside her grandmother at the rail.

"You asked him what he wants," the girl said. "He never answered."

"He answered," Ilo said. "You weren't listening to the words." She looked north, where the first far thunder was clearing its throat beyond the hills, and smiled, old and certain and unafraid. "Go sleep, child. Tomorrow we send runners to every settlement. If the storm gives us five days, we will use every one of them — because that captain is going to need somewhere to bring everybody home to. And I intend to have the fires lit."

Above the drowned terraces, faint through the night air, Kessu began to sing the third verse.
