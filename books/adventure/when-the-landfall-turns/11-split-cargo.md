## 11. Split Cargo

Ansel Reyes had spent eleven years learning to read stone, and in all that time he had never once been able to lie to it. Rock told the truth the way water did — slowly, patiently, without any interest in what you wanted to hear. So he supposed it was fitting that he'd learned to lie best of all on a riverboat, standing over a crate of four-thousand-year-old clay tablets while pretending they were ballast.

The crate was real enough. So were the forty-one others stacked under tarps at the edge of the Survey camp, each one packed by Mercer's men with a speed that made Ansel's teeth ache. Four millennia of a civilization's memory, wrapped in straw and nailed shut in an afternoon.

He watched Voss cross the compound toward the Marguerite's mooring, her stride unhurried, her rain jacket already buttoned against a sky that hadn't broken yet but would. She moved like a woman who had already won something. That was the thing about Imara Voss that Ansel had never managed to reconcile — she wasn't a villain out of a cheap serial. She was brilliant. She had found the terraces when three governments' worth of surveys had missed them. She had sat with him for six hours once, early on, translating the first recovered glyph sequence, and her mind had worked the symbols like light through a prism. He had liked her then. That was the trap of it. You didn't get betrayed by people you distrusted.

Now she stepped aboard the Marguerite without asking permission, because in her arithmetic she already owned the boat, and Wren Galloway met her at the rail with that flat river look that gave away nothing.

Ansel drifted closer, close enough to hear, close enough to be ignored. He'd gotten good at being furniture.

"Captain," Voss said. "I have a revised offer."

"You have a storm coming," Wren said. "That's the only revision I care about."

"The storm is my concern now." She said it mildly, the way you'd correct a clerk. "Five days, maybe less. Which means the first load goes downriver ahead of it or not at all. I'm prepared to make that worth your while."

She unfolded a paper — actual paper, which struck Ansel as almost quaint, a contract in an age when everything could be erased with a keystroke. But paper left a record, and records were what Voss feared and what she used.

"Triple the original rate," she said. "Plus a completion bonus. Enough, Captain, that you never take another contract you don't want. Enough to retire the *Marguerite* to whatever quiet slough she likes, with you sitting on her deck drinking something better than river coffee."

Ansel saw it land. He hated that he saw it. Wren's hand did not move, their face did not move, but something behind the eyes shifted — the way a current shifts under still water. Ansel knew what that money meant. He'd heard Odile talk about the engine, about parts that hadn't been manufactured in twenty years, about the sound the starboard cylinder made on cold mornings that no one said aloud. Triple rate plus bonus wasn't a bribe. It was a life. It was ten years of miles bought back all at once.

"And the cargo?" Wren asked.

"Crate one through twenty-four. The priority selections."

*Priority selections.* Ansel kept his breathing even. He knew those crates. He'd catalogued what went into them before Mercer's men took over the packing: the glyph tablets from the upper gallery, the bronze river-gauge — a working instrument, four thousand years old, that had measured flood seasons with an accuracy that would embarrass half the modern hydrology papers Ansel had published — the painted ceramics from the burial shelf, the woven reed scrolls that crumbled if you looked at them wrong and that Mercer's packers had handled like firewood. The things that would headline an auction. The things that would make the backers' names whispered in rooms Ansel would never be invited into.

"They're fragile," Ansel said, before he could stop himself.

Voss turned to him with polite surprise, as though a chair had spoken. "Dr. Reyes. Your concern for the material is noted."

"It's not the material I'm concerned about."

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "It isn't. That's why your opinion isn't part of this transaction." She turned back to Wren. "Captain?"

Wren looked at the contract a long moment. Then they picked it up, held it to the light like checking a bill for counterfeit, and set it down on the rail between them.

"I'll need to check my draft marks," Wren said. "Twenty-four crates is weight. Weight is draft. Draft is channels, and the channels up here are already lying to me. Give me until evening."

"Evening," Voss repeated.

"Storm moves the schedule, not me. If I ground her in the narrows with your priority selections aboard, that's your fortune on the bottom of the Serrano. You can pay triple for a salvage crew too, if you like."

Something flickered across Voss's face — the first crack Ansel had seen in it, thin as a hairline fracture in core sample. She did not like waiting. Waiting was the one currency she couldn't buy.

"Evening," she said again, and left the contract on the rail, and walked back across the compound with her stride just slightly less unhurried than before.

Wren stood at the rail watching her go. Then, without turning, without moving at all except the words: "Doc. You still got that camera?"

Ansel's heart went off like a depth charge. "What camera?"

"The one you've been hiding in the fuel locker since Tuesday. Odile found it when she was looking for a gasket. She didn't tell Mercer." Now Wren turned, and there was something in their face that wasn't quite a smile but lived in the same neighborhood. "You've been photographing the manifest. Every crate, every seal number."

"It's documentation," Ansel said. "Standard practice. Provenance records—"

"Doc." Wren's voice stayed level, kind, almost. "I've run contraband and I've run missionaries and I've run a circus troupe up this river in '09, and I know what smuggling looks like from both sides of it. You're not documenting. You're building evidence. And you're doing it slow, because you think we've only got days." They tapped the contract on the rail. "So don't waste the time I just bought you."

* * *

The afternoon went long and gold and false, the way afternoons do before weather. Ansel worked the fuel locker with the door cracked, shooting the manifest pages in flat light, then the crates themselves — seal numbers, stenciled weights, the orientation arrows Mercer's men had slapped on without checking which end of a tablet case was up. His hands were steady. His hands were always steady; it was the rest of him that shook.

Because here was the vertigo of it, the thing he kept circling and couldn't land: the money was *right*. Not the bribe — the logic. Twenty-four crates, photographed, sealed, documented, ferried downriver ahead of the storm to some bonded warehouse where they'd be safe from the flood that would otherwise drown the whole site anyway. Wasn't that rescue? Wasn't that stewardship? Mama Ilo's people had no museum, no climate control, no armed perimeter. The water would take everything in five days whether anyone signed a contract or not. Maybe Voss was right. Maybe extraction was salvation wearing a bad suit.

And then he thought of the gauge. The bronze river-gauge, calibrated to a flood rhythm so old it predated every settlement on the Serrano, and he thought of Mama Ilo standing at the waterline saying, in her voice like driftwood: *the river does not keep its dead in warehouses.* He thought of the junior scientists hauling crates at gunpoint last week, and of Priya Chandra — twenty-six years old, three months into her first field season — who had cried silently over a reed scroll while packing it, and who had looked at Ansel afterward with a question in her face he still hadn't answered.

The question was: *which side are you on?*

He'd been dodging it since the cavern. Hiding behind documentation, behind procedure, behind the comfortable fiction that recording a crime was different from stopping one. But nobody downstream was coming. He'd falsified a relay message himself to buy silence; there was no cavalry. There was a riverboat, a crew that kept choosing each other, and a woman with a contract who thought patience was a resource to be extracted along with everything else.

His camera clicked. Seal number 17-C. Orientation arrow pointing down.

*Which side are you on, Dr. Reyes?*

He shot the next frame.

* * *

He found Mercer at the north tarp line near dusk, and understood within four seconds that he'd found him too late.

Mercer had Priya Chandra by the arm. Not dramatically — Mercer didn't do dramatic; violence from him came in the register of bookkeeping, of inventory, of a man correcting an entry. He held her forearm the way he'd hold a mislabeled crate, and he was speaking quietly, and Priya's face had gone the color of the fog off the morning water.

"—the tally sheet says fourteen tablets, case seven. I count thirteen." A small shake of the arm, precise, almost gentle. "So either you recount, or you tell me where the fourteenth is."

"I didn't—" Priya's voice cracked and rebuilt itself. "Dr. Reyes authorized a condition check. One tablet, photographed and returned—"

"Returned." Mercer smiled. It was the worst thing Ansel had ever seen him do, because it was patient. "Nothing gets returned, Ms. Chandra. That's the entire point of a collection." His eyes lifted and found Ansel across the tarp line, and the smile didn't change at all. "Doctor. Good. You can settle this. Did you authorize removal of an item from case seven?"

Ansel walked forward. Each step took a year. He was aware of Tam at the far end of the tarp line, pausing with a coil of line in his hands, watching. Aware of the light going amber and low. Aware that Mercer's grip on Priya's arm had not loosened and would not, because letting go was a concession and Mercer did not concede.

"One tablet," Ansel said. "Condition assessment. Standard conservation protocol. It goes back tonight."

"See, that's interesting." Mercer released Priya — shoved, really, a small precise shove that sent her half a step into the mud — and turned fully to Ansel, and now the smile was gone and what was underneath was worse, because it was nothing, it was arithmetic. "Because I inventoried that case myself this morning. Thirteen tablets. And this afternoon I watched you come out of the archive gallery with your bag heavier than you went in with." He tilted his head. "Where is it, Doctor?"

The truth was that it was in Ansel's bunk, wrapped in his spare shirt, and that he had taken it not to hide it but because it was the tablet — the one with the flood ledger, the one whose final glyphs he and Voss had translated together in the early weeks, back when liking her was still possible. The last line of it read, as near as they could render it: *we wrote this so the water would remember us kindly.*

He had taken it because someone had to decide what the water remembered. And now Mercer was asking him to account for it like missing stock.

"It's being conserved," Ansel said. "You'll have it back when the assessment concludes."

"When the—" Mercer laughed once, short, no humor in it anywhere. "Doctor, let me simplify your job. Everything in this camp is either cargo or obstruction. Cargo gets loaded. Obstruction gets—" he glanced at Priya, at Tam, at the darkening sky, doing sums Ansel could almost see — "handled. You've been very useful with your little camera. Keep being useful. But the inventory closes tomorrow at dawn, and anything not on it doesn't exist." He picked up his tally clipboard from a crate lid. "Anything that doesn't exist can't be missed. Do you understand me?"

Ansel understood him completely. It was the clearest communication of his professional life.

"Give the tablet back," Priya said. Her voice was shaking and she said it anyway, and Ansel loved her for it in a way that frightened him. "It's not yours. None of it is yours. It belongs to—"

"To whom?" Mercer asked, genuinely curious, genuinely waiting, and that was the horror of him: he wasn't cruel for pleasure, he was cruel for accuracy. "Name the owner, Ms. Chandra. Show me the deed. Four thousand years, and show me the deed." He tucked the clipboard under his arm. "That's what I thought. Unowned property gets claimed. That's not villainy. That's physics."

He walked away down the tarp line, boots squelching, counting things.

Priya stood in the mud with her arms wrapped around herself. Ansel reached out and steadied her elbow, and she flinched and then didn't.

"He's right," she whispered. "About the deed. There isn't one. So how do we say it's theirs? How do we *prove*—"

"We don't prove it," Ansel said. "We witness it. Those aren't the same thing, and it turns out that's the whole difference." He looked east, where the sky had gone the color of a bruise coming up, and felt the pressure drop against his ears like the river inhaling. "Get your kit inside. Storm's early."

* * *

Full dark, and the Marguerite's deck lamp burned in a cone of rain-smelling air, and Wren Galloway sat at the rail with Voss's contract beside them and a cup of Bex's coffee going cold, and Ansel climbed aboard because he couldn't stand his own bunk another minute.

Wren didn't look up. "Draft marks checked?"

"Have you decided?"

"I asked first."

Ansel sat down across from them. Down the bank, the tarps snapped in a wind that hadn't existed an hour ago. Somewhere upstream, thunder cleared its throat.

"She'll give you the money," Ansel said. "She'll actually do it. And it would fix everything — the engine, the boat, the years. You could walk away from contracts forever." He turned his cold coffee in his hands. "I've been running the numbers myself all day. Different numbers, same math. Hand over the first load, the site drowns clean, no confrontation, everybody lives. It's the rational play. It's the play I'd defend in any journal."

Wren looked at him then, and waited, because Wren Galloway had the patience of moving water.

"But," Ansel said.

"But," Wren agreed.

"But the tablets say *so the water would remember us kindly.* And if we load those crates, we're deciding what the water remembers. Us. We'd be the ones it remembers." He exhaled. "I keep thinking there's a version where I'm a good scientist and a good person and they're the same version. I haven't found it yet. Have you?"

Wren picked up the contract. Held it to the lamp light again, that same counterfeit-checker's gesture. Then, deliberately, in front of Ansel, they did not sign it. They folded it once, twice, and slid it into their coat pocket, unread, unsigned, a debt deferred rather than refused.

"Evening's over," Wren said. "She'll want her answer by dawn." They stood, and looked east at the bruised horizon where the lightning was starting to stitch itself into the cloud base, closer now, patient as Mercer. "Get some sleep, Doc. Tomorrow we find out what the *Marguerite*'s worth."

"And what's she worth?" Ansel asked.

Wren paused at the wheelhouse door, one hand on the frame, the lamplight catching the river-lines around their eyes.

"More than twenty-four crates," they said, and went inside, and the first fat drops of the early storm began ticking against the deck boards like something counting down.
