## 12. Sabotage

The generator died at 3:14 in the morning, and Bex knew it before she was fully awake, the way you know a pot boiling over in another room — by the silence where the hum should be.

She'd been sleeping in the galley again. Not because her bunk aboard the *Marguerite* was bad — Odile had rebuilt the ventilation last spring and it was better than most beds Bex had known on land — but because the Survey camp's cook tent had no stove worth the name, and Bex had taken to doing her prep work at night when the heat broke, and sometimes she just didn't make the walk back down the boardwalk. So she slept with her ear against the bulkhead, and the generator's drone had become, over eleven days at this cursed camp, the sound of the world still turning.

When it stopped, the silence had weight. It pressed on her eardrums like water.

She sat up in the dark, listening. Rain on the canvas overhead — the leading edge of something bigger, the sky already rehearsing. Wind in the drowned terraces below the camp, moving through the stone channels with that low flute-note the whole site made after dark, which Ansel said was air moving through flooded galleries and which Tam said was the place breathing. Bex didn't hold with either explanation particularly. She held with: the generator should be running, and it wasn't, and in eleven days it hadn't so much as hiccupped, because Mercer's men maintained it like it was made of gold, since everything in this operation ran through it — the floodlights over the excavation, the pumps keeping the lower galleries dry, the refrigeration for the film and paper records, the radio.

The radio.

Bex was moving before she finished the thought, bare feet on cold deck boards, out into the rain-smell of the camp.

* * *

The Survey camp sat on a shelf of cut stone above the terraces, a scatter of tents and prefab shelters strung along a boardwalk that Mercer's crew had fortified without ever quite admitting they were fortifying it — sandbags stacked as windbreaks, a watch post at the landing, wire strung low along the treeline that everyone pretended was for animals. Eleven days ago it had looked like science. Now it looked like what it was becoming, whatever that was, and nobody wanted to say the word out loud.

The generator shed stood apart from the main cluster, downwind of the kitchens, its tin roof drumming under the rain. Bex reached it and stopped, because there were voices inside, and light — a lantern, guttering, held by someone whose hands were shaking hard enough to make the shadows jump.

"—not fuel, look at the line, somebody *cut* the fuel line—"

"Nobody cut anything. It chafed through. It happens."

"It doesn't happen. I check that line every—"

"Then you checked it wrong."

Bex knew both voices. The first belonged to Dr. Okonkwo, the hydrologist, a careful man who catalogued his own pencils. The second belonged to one of Mercer's security men, a broad silent type called Dree who had once, Bex had noticed, watched her chop onions for ten full minutes with an expression she couldn't read and hadn't liked.

She leaned into the doorway. "Evening, gentlemen."

Dree turned, and the lantern swung, and in its light Bex saw what he'd been trying to stand in front of: the generator's fuel line, and not a chafe mark on it but a clean slice, knife-work, angled neat as a butcher's cut. And beside the generator, a coil of copper wire stripped from somewhere, and a handful of fine grit scattered across the housing — sand, or crushed stone, worked into every vent and seam.

That wasn't sabotage to disable. That was sabotage to destroy. Grit in the works like that would grind the engine to death even if you got it running again.

"Camp problem," Dree said. "Go back to your boat."

"Boat's my kitchen," Bex said. "Kitchen's my business." She looked at the line, at the grit, letting him see her see it. "Somebody did that with a blade and a bag of sand. Somebody who wanted it dead past repair."

Okonkwo was staring at the grit too, and she watched the arithmetic move behind his face — the same arithmetic she was doing. The pumps. The galleries flooding. The paper archive down in the dark, swelling and rotting if the dehumidifiers died.

"Voss will want a report," Okonkwo started.

"Voss," said Dree, "will hear it was mechanical failure." He picked up the lantern. "And she'll hear who was seen walking around the camp at odd hours, too. Your boat people. Wandering."

Bex felt it go through her, cold under the skin, ahead of any thought: *he was ready for this.* Ready with the story, ready with the blame, standing guard over a wrecked engine at three in the morning with his account already polished. She thought about saying all that out loud. She thought about the way Mercer's men carried sidearms now, openly, since the elders' boat had been turned away from the landing — and about the fact that she was five foot two and armed with opinions.

"Sure," she said instead. "Mechanical failure. Terrible thing." She smiled at him the way she smiled at inspectors. "I'll let the captain know we're confined to quarters, shall I? Save you the walk."

Dree's jaw tightened. He hadn't said confined yet. Now he knew she knew it was coming.

By noon it was official. Voss stood the assembled camp — scientists, boat crew, all of them — in front of the mess shelter and announced that the generator had been sabotaged, that the guilty party would be found, and that until then, movement between the *Marguerite* and the camp was restricted to escorted parties. Her voice was level, reasonable, almost gentle, which was somehow worse than shouting. She talked about shared purpose and the pressures of the season and how fear made people do strange things.

She never once looked at Wren while she said it.

Wren stood at the edge of the group with their arms crossed, weathered face giving back nothing, and Bex, watching them both, understood that Voss had decided something overnight. This wasn't suspicion. This was positioning. You blamed a boat crew because a boat crew could be marooned; you couldn't maroon your own security chief.

* * *

Confinement, it turned out, meant the *Marguerite*, mostly — Mercer's logic being that a boat at anchor was easier to watch than people in tents. Which suited Bex fine, because it put her back in her galley with her stores and her knives and, crucially, with time.

Time was the one thing the camp had too much of now. No generator meant no floodlights, which meant no night work in the galleries, which meant the extraction schedule — Mercer's precious loading manifest, crates tallied by the hour — had slipped. The scientists milled around in daylight doing careful, deliberate, useless-looking things. Odile spent two days visibly tinkering with the *Marguerite*'s engine where everyone could see her, muttering about injector fouling, and Bex, who had slept within earshot of that engine for six years, recognized theater when she heard it. Whatever Odile was actually doing under the cowling, it wasn't injector work.

Tam took to fishing off the stern with the patience of a saint and the eyesight of a hawk, and reported, in the flat tone he used for reporting river traffic, exactly who walked where along the shore, and when, and carrying what.

And Bex cooked. Confinement meant Mercer's men ate aboard now too, four of them at a stretch, taking their meals in shifts in her galley because the camp mess had gone cold-storage-only and morale ashore was souring. They came down her gangway with their rifles slung and their boots wet and they sat at her table and ate her food, and none of them, apparently, had ever considered that a cook sees everything.

A cook sees who takes seconds. A cook sees who won't meet anyone's eye. A cook hears what men say when they think the person feeding them is furniture.

On the fourth day of confinement, the big quiet one — not Dree, the other one, Callas, younger, homesick, forever pushing rice around his plate — sat eating late with one of the camp guards, and Bex was scrubbing pots with her back half-turned, and she heard him say:

"—burn orders, though. That's what I don't get. Why haul it up here if you're going to—"

"Shut up."

"—I'm saying, the manifest says extract, but the sealed envelope says—"

"*Callas.*"

Silence. Then the scrape of a chair, and boots, and Callas went up the gangway into the rain without finishing his food, which in Bex's long professional experience was the single most reliable tell of a man carrying something he shouldn't.

A sealed envelope. Burn orders.

She kept scrubbing the pot. Her hands were steady. Her heart was going like a paddlewheel.

* * *

Finding the envelope took her two more days, and she was not proud of how she did it, and she was proud anyway.

Callas smoked. He smoked on the foredeck at dusk, always the same spot, always alone, and he kept his cigarettes in a tin that lived in his jacket, and his jacket — because confinement meant the boat crew stayed aboard and Mercer's men rotated through watches that left gaps — hung in the forward cabin during evening meals, on a hook, among six identical jackets, because Mercer's men dressed like they were afraid color might give away their position.

Bex had fed men for thirty years. She knew jackets the way other people knew faces.

She waited until the boat was in its evening rhythm — Tam on the stern, Odile loudly failing to fix things in the engine room, Wren up in the wheelhouse with the chart lamp burning, visible, a deliberate beacon of innocence — and she went forward, and she went through the pockets of the jacket that smelled of cheap tobacco and gun oil.

Passport, folded maps of nothing interesting, a folding knife, and a oilskin packet, rubber-banded, with a wax-sealed envelope inside and a typed card clipped to it. Orders. She read them twice by the light of the passage lamp, holding the page close so the glow wouldn't spill.

The card was a letterhead she didn't recognize — some foundation, some trust, names that meant nothing except that they were the kind of names printed on heavy stock. The text beneath was short. It used words like *asset recovery* and *deniability* and *site remediation*. Bex had cooked for mining camps and salvage crews and once, briefly, for a man who smuggled antiquities inside frozen fish, and she had learned to translate. *Asset recovery* meant take everything portable. *Deniability* meant the backers had never heard of any of this. *Site remediation* —

*Site remediation* meant fire. Meant charges in the terraces, meant the galleries collapsed and flooded past finding, meant every record, every carving, every trace that anyone had ever been paid to come up here and strip a nation's memory out of its bed — burned, blasted, drowned. Meant the elders Mama Ilo had sent downriver, turned away from the landing at gunpoint, waking someday in a world where the place their grandmothers sang about had never existed at all.

Meant that Voss hadn't come for profit. Profit you could tax, trace, sue over. Voss had come to make the archive *unprovable.*

Bex put the packet back exactly as she'd found it, down to the angle of the rubber band, and stood in the passage with her hand flat against the bulkhead, and discovered that she was shaking.

Not from fear. Or not only. It took her a moment to name the other thing, because she'd felt it so rarely it came in foreign: fury, clean and total, rising in her chest like floodwater, and underneath it a grief so sharp it felt like anger's twin. Because she'd been telling herself for eleven days that this was a bad contract, greedy people, the ordinary crookedness of money. Money she understood. Money had rules.

This wasn't money. This was erasure. This was men with rifles planning to unmake a people's proof that they had ever been, and then go home to their heavy letterhead and their clean hands, and the river would keep the secret because the river would be all that was left.

She thought of Mama Ilo's people poling away from the landing, uncounted, uncounted-for. She thought of Ansel Reyes documenting the galleries by lamplight, racing a clock he didn't know had moved. She thought of the grit ground into the generator's vents — and suddenly she wondered, cold and clear, whether the generator had died on its own schedule after all, or whether someone had killed it precisely to slow the loading, and whether that someone was one of Mercer's own, a Callas with a conscience, or whether—

It didn't matter tonight. What mattered was that Wren needed to know, and Odile needed to know, and Ansel needed to know, and they needed to know tonight, because the storm clock was running and men who planned to burn evidence did not wait politely for monsoons.

She climbed to the wheelhouse. The chart lamp burned yellow. Wren looked up from the Serrano charts, pencil behind one ear, and saw her face, and set the pencil down.

"Bex," they said. "What is it."

"Captain," she said, and her voice came out steady, which surprised her, "we need to talk about what we're carrying. And then we need to talk about getting word to Ansel Reyes before somebody burns the whole river's memory down to hide the receipts."

Outside, the rain thickened against the windows, and far upstream, thunder rolled along the terraces like something answering.
