## 13. Night Crossing

The generator coughed its last at dusk, and by full dark the Survey camp ran on lanterns and lies. Voss had posted Mercer's men at the landing with orders to log every departure, which meant the Marguerite sat moored like a prisoner with a view of the water.

Wren stood in the wheelhouse with the lights doused, watching the camp through the glass. Rain came and went in thin veils. Somewhere behind the mess tent, a junior scientist named Cora Vasch was running a fever that had climbed past the point where willpower could hold it down. Ansel had said *sepsis* quietly, the way you say a word you don't want the river to hear.

"She won't last three days on camp rations and Mercer's temper," Ansel had told them that afternoon, crouched in the shadow of the fuel drums. "And she won't last one if Voss decides sick cargo isn't worth feeding."

So they would move her tonight. Mama Ilo's settlement lay six hours downstream through channels that drowned and re-formed with every season — channels no chart showed, because the charts were drawn in low water and the river was now writing its own.

Wren checked the watch. Eleven-forty. Tam was already below, easing the engine room hatch open with a cloth wrapped around the latch so it wouldn't sing. Odile stood at the galley door pretending to smoke, which she never did, which was the signal.

* * *

They went out over the stern, not the gangway. Wren had spent ten years learning where a boat's weight lived, and the Marguerite's stern sat close enough to the water that a man carrying a stretcher could step down into the skiff without a splash worth naming. Bex handed the stretcher across the gap, Cora wrapped in oilcloth against the drizzle, her eyes half-open and unfocused. Ansel followed, then Tam took the painter, and Wren came last, casting off the stern line with two fingers so it wouldn't slap the cleat.

Mercer's sentry at the landing had built himself a shelter from a tarp and a crate, and inside that shelter he was asleep. Wren had watched him for an hour to be sure. Men who thought they were guarding a boat guarded the gangway. Nobody guarded the river side of a boat, because nobody believed the river belonged to anybody but the boat.

The skiff slid out past the Marguerite's shadow and into the open dark.

For the first hundred yards Wren let the current do the work, tiller feathered, engine cold. The rain thickened, then stopped, and the clouds tore open along the eastern horizon just enough to spill starlight onto the water — not much, but enough. Enough for someone who'd learned this river the way other people learned their own hands.

"Where are we going?" Ansel whispered. He had one hand on Cora's stretcher, steadying it against the skiff's gentle roll.

"Through the Serrano's back teeth." Wren nodded toward a wall of drowned forest ahead, black trees standing in black water. "Main channel runs east of that island. We run west."

"West is flooded."

"West is flooded *for people who need to see where they're going.*"

Tam snorted softly from the bow. "Captain means we're going to feel our way."

"I mean," Wren said, "that the flood did us a favor. Every sandbar between here and the settlement is under four feet of water. No bars to strike. Just trees. And the trees don't move."

"The trees don't move," Tam repeated, in the tone of a man filing that away as either wisdom or a eulogy.

Wren started the engine at idle — the little auxiliary, nearly silent, the one Odile had rebuilt twice and once rebuilt again out of spite. It puttered like something content. They slipped into the drowned forest.

* * *

It was hard to explain to people who hadn't grown up on moving water what flooded timber does to your sense of the world. The eye wants edges. The eye wants banks. Out here there were none — only tree trunks rising out of still water, hundreds of them, thousands, each one throwing a long wakeless shadow, and between them the river moved anyway, slow and deep and patient, going wherever it had decided to go before anyone asked its opinion.

Wren steered by memory and by the stars that came through the gaps in the canopy, and by something older than both, which was the feel of the current against the hull. You couldn't see current. But you could hear it — a soft change in the sound of water against wood when the flow shifted, a faint seam of noise where two bodies of water disagreed. Ten years of miles had taught Wren to hear those seams the way a musician hears a wrong note.

"Starboard," Wren murmured, and eased the tiller. A trunk slid past close enough to touch, its bark slick and silvered. Then another. Then open water for thirty yards, then timber again.

"How do you know this?" Ansel asked. Not accusingly. Wonderingly.

"I don't. Not this exact water. Nobody knows this exact water; it didn't exist last month." Wren kept their eyes moving, trunk to trunk, reading the dark. "But I know what the river does when it rises here. It fills the old oxbows first, then cuts through the low ground between them. The oxbows all trend southwest. So if I keep the current coming off my left shoulder, I'm in a cut, and cuts lead somewhere."

"And if you're wrong?"

"Then we spend the night tied to a tree, which is not the worst thing that can happen to a person."

Tam laughed under his breath. "He says that like he's done it."

"He has," Wren said. "Twice."

Cora stirred on the stretcher and moaned, and the mood in the skiff changed instantly, banter folding itself away like a chart no longer needed. Ansel bent over her, wet cloth in hand, murmuring something — not words so much as sounds, the kind you make to tell a body it isn't alone.

"Hour to the first landmark," Wren said. "A dead kapok leaning east, alone in the water. After that, the channel narrows and we go by ear."

* * *

They found the kapok exactly where the memory of it lived, though the flood had risen until only its topmost limbs broke the surface, clawing at the sky. Wren brought the skiff alongside it and killed the engine, and for a moment all four of them — five counting Cora — simply floated in a silence so complete it had texture.

That was when Wren heard it: the seam. Two currents arguing, off the port bow.

"There." A whisper. "Hear it? That hiss, under the wind? That's the cut. The main channel's pushing through the timber on our left, and the slack water on our right is the old oxbow backing up. We take the seam."

"You can navigate by *sound*?" Ansel's voice cracked with something between disbelief and awe.

"You can navigate by anything the river gives you. Tonight it's giving sound. Tomorrow it might give smell — mud has a different voice after rain. The trick is asking instead of telling."

Tam, at the bow, had gone quiet in the way he went quiet when he was learning something he intended to keep. Wren saw him tilt his head, listening, and knew the boy was hearing the seam now too.

They threaded it. The current took the skiff's stern and swung it gently, helpfully, into the narrow water, and for the next hour the river did most of the steering while Wren made small corrections, the way you steady a child learning to walk. Twice the channel split and Wren chose by the depth of the dark — deeper water reflects less light, holds the stars more cleanly, and the shallows show a faint gray breathing where the bottom comes near. Once a caiman's eyes burned red-green in the beam-less dark and slid away without interest.

Cora slept, or something near it. Ansel held her hand.

* * *

Near the fourth hour the sky closed again and the stars went out, and the rain returned with intent. This was the bad stretch — the last mile before the settlement, where the flood spread widest and the landmarks drowned entirely. Wren slowed to a crawl and worked by the third method, the oldest one: counting.

There was a submerged levee, ancient, pre-dating any map, that ran a quarter mile along this reach. In low water it was a mud ridge with grass. In flood it was a shallow place, and shallow places make a water sound nothing else makes — a fine, tight whisper, like silk torn slowly. Wren counted strokes of the current against the hull, listened for the whisper to begin, and steered parallel to it, keeping it exactly at the edge of hearing.

"What is that?" Tam breathed. "I hear it — like —"

"Like the river talking in its sleep. Stay with it. If it gets louder, we're drifting over the levee. If it dies, we've drifted into the deep."

For twenty minutes nobody spoke. The rain fell. The whisper sang its one note. And Wren held the skiff on that knife-edge of sound with hands that did not shake, thinking, absurdly, of Voss — of her charts and her backers and her arithmetic, of a woman who looked at this water and saw only an obstacle between herself and a payday. She would run this reach with a searchlight and a depth finder and a crew shouting numbers, and she would hit the levee, because she would be telling the river what it was instead of asking.

The whisper faded. Past the levee. Ahead, faint through the rain, a single lamp swung on a post — Mama Ilo's landing, hung with a storm lantern that never went out during high water, a beacon for exactly the kind of night this was.

Someone had been watching for them.

* * *

Figures came down to the water with poles and planks, and hands lifted Cora's stretcher up the flooded ramp as gently as if it were made of eggshell. Mama Ilo herself stood at the top in an oilskin the color of river clay, and when Wren climbed the last few feet she took Wren's hands in both of hers and held them a moment longer than greeting required.

"You came by the timber," she said. "In the dark."

"There wasn't another way."

"There is always another way. Most of them are worse." She looked past Wren at the skiff, at Tam and Ansel climbing out, at the rain falling on all of them equally. "You'll take tea. Your boy will eat. The doctor-woman's patient goes to my daughter, who has hands that pull fever. Then you'll go back before first light, yes?"

"Yes. Voss counts heads at dawn."

Mama Ilo nodded slowly, and something in her face made Wren stop before turning away.

"When you come again," she said, "— and you will come again, captain, the river has already decided that about you — I will ask you a question. Think about it on the water. It is about what you carry home."

"I'm carrying a scientist and a debt."

"No." She smiled, and the lantern light caught the deep lines around her eyes. "Tonight you carried a person. Think about the difference. Go drink your tea."

Inside the longhouse, steam rose off tin cups, and Cora was already being unwrapped by women who moved with the unhurried certainty of people who had pulled fevers out of bodies for a hundred generations. Ansel sat beside her holding the cup she couldn't yet lift, and Tam ate like the drowning breathe, and Wren stood at the doorway looking out at the rain and the black water and the lantern on its post.

Six hours back. Dawn head-counts. Mercer's arithmetic. Voss waiting with a contract and a fortune and a plan to haul a civilization out of its grave in crates.

Wren drank the tea, which tasted of something green and bitter and old, and understood that Mama Ilo's question had already begun asking itself.

What do you carry home?

Not crates. Not miles. Wren looked at Tam laughing at something Ansel said, at the fever-light fading in Cora's face, at the river running past the landing in the dark, going where it had always intended to go.

The answer was sitting in the room. It just hadn't signed anything yet.

By first gray light, the skiff was back on the water, running the seam home — and on the Marguerite's deck, waiting in the drizzle with a clipboard and a smile that didn't reach her eyes, stood Dr. Imara Voss.

"We need to talk, Captain," she said. "About cargo."
