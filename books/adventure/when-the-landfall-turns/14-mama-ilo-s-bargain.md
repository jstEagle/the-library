## 14. Mama Ilo's Bargain

Mama Ilo had dreamed of the water rising for three nights before the boat came.

In the first dream, the river stood up out of its bed and walked through the village on legs of brown glass, and every house it touched remembered being a boat. In the second, her grandmother — dead sixty years, a woman Ilo had known only as a voice in the dark of the sleeping room — sat at the foot of her mat and said, *They are opening the doors that were closed for a reason.* In the third dream there were no images at all, only a sound like a great many small voices reading aloud in unison, in a language Ilo's body knew and her mouth had forgotten.

She woke from the third dream with salt on her lips, though she lived two days' paddle from any sea, and she went out onto her stilted porch and looked upriver into the dark and knew.

The old ones would have called it hearing the archive turn over in its sleep. The mission school had called it superstition. Ilo had buried enough of both to hold her own counsel. She sent word downriver by her grandson's fastest skiff: *The water is speaking. Come if you remember how to listen.*

Then she waited, because waiting was also a skill, and hers was better than most.

* * *

They came at dusk on the second day, poling out of the drowned channels north of the terraces in a long shallow boat that rode so low Ilo's first thought was *sinking*, and her second was *no — loaded*. A woman stood at the stern with a pole, and even at distance Ilo read her the way you read weather: the set of the shoulders, the way the weight stayed in the hips no matter what the current argued. That was a river person, whatever papers she carried or didn't. You could not fake that stance. You could not buy it.

Behind the pole-woman, huddled under tarps, were others — a thin man cradling another man's bandaged arm, a young deckhand with river-mud to his knees, and a scientist by her glasses and her terrible posture, the kind of person who looked at water like it was a document to be translated rather than a relative to be greeted.

Ilo counted them twice. Then she called her people to the landing.

The boat came alongside with a competence that pleased her more than she showed. The pole-woman stepped off first, offered a hand back to help the wounded man, and only then turned to face the crowd gathered on the planks.

"Captain Wren Galloway," she said. "Marguerite, out of Port Calloway. We're asking shelter. There's a man hurt — fever in the wound — and we've got scientists upstream who'll need a place when the storm closes the river."

"You're asking," Ilo said. "That's already more than most boats do. Most boats take."

Something moved behind the captain's eyes — not surprise exactly. Recognition of being seen. "We'd pay in fuel, in medicine, in labor—"

"We'll speak about payment after your sick man is lying down." Ilo turned and gave the orders that needed giving, and her people moved, because they always moved; forty years of floods and funerals had made a machine of them that ran on grief and competence. Two young men took the stretcher. Her daughter-in-law Sefi took one look at the wound and began issuing instructions of her own, which was correct. The scientist hovered until Ilo put one finger against her elbow and steered her toward the cookfire like a stray dog that might bolt.

Only when the wounded man was indoors and the tarps were stacked did Ilo sit across from the captain on the landing planks, where the water slapped underneath like a slow heart.

"Now," she said. "Tell me what you found up there. And don't tell me what you think an old woman wants to hear. Tell me true."

Wren Galloway looked at her for a long moment. River people learned to measure silence; this one measured it well, letting it stretch without flinching, which told Ilo the answer mattered more than comfort.

"A cavern under the terraces," the captain said finally. "Dry inside. Full of things — records, tools, art. A whole civilization's memory, kept above the flood line by people who built for ten thousand years." A pause. "There's a doctor up there who means to take all of it out and sell it. And a man named Mercer who means to burn what can't be sold."

Ilo had expected the words. She had dreamed them, in a way. But expecting a blow does not keep it from landing.

"The doors," she said quietly. "My grandmother used to say the doors were closed for a reason. Not hidden. Closed. There's a difference." She looked upriver, where the last light was going copper on the flooded forest. "Hidden things want finding. Closed things want respecting. Do you understand me, Captain?"

"I understand that I don't fully understand," Wren said. "And that I'm standing in the middle of something older than my job."

That, at least, was honest. Ilo found herself liking the honesty the way you like dry wood in wet season — not beautiful, but sound.

* * *

They fed everyone that night — rice and smoked fish and the bitter greens that grew wild along the levee, and Sefi's pepper sauce that made the young deckhand, Tam, cough and then ask proudly for more. Ilo watched her household absorb strangers the way a river absorbs rain, and she watched the captain watch it too, with an expression Ilo recognized because she wore it herself sometimes: hunger for a thing you have decided you don't deserve.

Afterward, while the settlement settled into its noises — dogs, babies, the generator Sefi's husband refused to let die — Ilo took the captain out in a small canoe, alone. Some conversations could not happen on land. Land made people perform. Water made them tell the truth, or at least made their lies heavier, easier to hear sinking.

She paddled them out past the fish traps to where the channel widened and the stars came down to meet the current, and there she shipped the paddles and let the boat drift.

"My grandmother's grandmother came from the terraces," Ilo said. "Before the last great flood, before the people scattered downriver. She carried nothing. That was the whole point. Everything worth keeping was already kept — in the stone, under the water, behind the doors." She turned. "You've seen it. Tell me. Did it feel like a treasure?"

Wren was quiet a moment. "It felt like a library," she said. "Like walking into a house where everyone just stepped out and might come back."

"Yes." Ilo felt the truth of it land in her chest like a struck bell. "A house, not a vault. That's what the doctor up there doesn't understand, and what her backers will never understand, because understanding it would cost them everything they came for. You cannot own a house whose owners only stepped out. You can only be its guest or its thief."

"And Mercer means to burn it."

"To erase the evidence of the theft. Yes." Ilo let the canoe swing slowly under the stars. "Captain. I'm going to ask you for something now, and I want you to listen to all of it before you answer, because I am not going to accept the first thing that comes out of your mouth. First answers are always either fear or flattery."

Wren's mouth twitched — almost a smile. "Fair warning taken."

"I ask you to make sure the archive stays with the water." Ilo raised a hand before the captain could draw breath. "Not yet. Hear it through. I know what I'm asking. I know there's a woman up there with guns and a contract and nine kinds of leverage, and a storm coming that will close the river, and a boat full of people whose lives are in your hands. I know that if you promise me and fail, you'll carry it. And I know that if you promise me and succeed, it may cost you the boat, the money, maybe worse." She held the captain's eyes. "So I'm not asking for a promise tonight. I'm asking you to go back up that river knowing what's wanted, and to choose — freely, with your eyes open — whether you're the kind of keeper this needs. My grandmother said the doors would open again someday, and that whoever stood there when they opened would decide what our grandchildren inherit. A memory, or a hole in the ground where a memory used to be."

The drift carried them a long way in silence. Ilo let it. On the water, silence was not empty; it was full, the way a held breath is full.

Finally Wren spoke, and her voice was low and roughened, like a rope taking strain.

"I won't promise you tonight. You're right that a fast yes would be cheap. But I'll tell you what I can give you." The captain's hands flexed once on the gunwale. "Ten years I've run this river measuring my life in miles, because miles are simple and people aren't. Up there I watched a woman try to chart something that can't be charted, and I watched good people start doing her arithmetic because she fed them and paid them and made the math feel inevitable. I don't want to be someone the math happens to." A breath. "So here's my word, and it's not a promise, it's a heading: I will not help load that archive. Whatever else I do or fail to do — I won't be the hands that carry it out. Whether I can stop it entirely, with guns at the camp and a storm coming — I honestly don't know. I won't lie to you and say I do."

Ilo studied her for a long time. The stars moved. Somewhere downstream a fish jumped, the slap of it loud as a door closing.

It was not the answer she had dreamed of. Dreams never accounted for guns, or storms, or the ordinary weakness of human hearts. But it was an answer given in the manner her grandmother had described across sixty years of absence: weighted, honest, unwilling to float on flattery.

"A heading, not a promise," Ilo said at last. "On the river, that may be worth more. A promise is a chain, Captain, and chains snap under strain and then everyone drowns tangled in them. A heading — that you can hold in the worst water, if you're the kind of pilot who holds headings." She picked up her paddle. "I think you might be. But I've been wrong about pilots before, and the river has corrected me."

She turned the canoe toward home, and as the strokes bit and the settlement lights grew, she spoke once more, not turning around, giving the words to the water between them the way you give bread to a current:

"But understand this much, Wren Galloway. If those crates leave the terraces — if even one of them reaches a buyer's warehouse — then everything my family kept safe through twenty generations of floods ends in a glass case in a city that has never once heard the river speak. I am an old woman. I can wait to be angry. But I will be angry a very long time, and I will tell my grandchildren's children exactly who stood at the doors when they opened, and what that person chose." Now she did turn, and let the old woman show through, the one the mission school had failed to iron flat. "Do you want to be remembered as a guest of that house, or its thief? Sleep on it. The storm gives us all a few days to become who we're going to be."

* * *

Ilo did not sleep. She sat on her porch through the small hours with her grandmother's voice in her ears and the river talking underneath everything, as it always had, patient and enormous and keeping its own accounts.

Near dawn, Tam — the young deckhand, the one who liked the pepper sauce — came padding out barefoot, unable to sleep either, and sat on the porch steps below her without asking. Young people always thought their sleeplessness was private.

"Your captain," Ilo said, after a while. "How long have you served with her?"

Tam considered. "Three years. Feels longer. Good-longer." A pause. "She's not like other captains. She learns the river instead of just using it. Odile says she talks to the Marguerite like it's crew, not equipment." Another pause, longer. "She'd die before she let anyone drown. I've seen her burn her own cargo to make weight in a flood. So if you're wondering whether she'll do the right thing—" Tam shrugged, uncomfortable. "I don't wonder. I just worry the right thing and the survivable thing aren't the same shape this time. They usually are. This time they're not."

"No," Ilo agreed softly. "This time they're not. That's why I asked for a heading instead of a promise."

Tam glanced up, puzzled, and Ilo let it stay puzzled. Some things a young person should learn from the river directly.

The sky over the eastern levee had gone the color of hammered tin. Too early for dawn. Ilo rose stiffly, joints announcing themselves, and looked at that wrong-colored sky with the attention of a woman who had survived eleven named storms and buried neighbors from three of them.

"Sefi!" she called into the house. "Wake everyone. Look at the east. The storm's changed its mind about timing — it's coming faster than anyone's arithmetic says." She was already moving toward the storehouse where the tarps and the spare line lived, her body remembering urgency the way the river remembered rain. "And send someone to wake the captain. Tell her Mama Ilo says the water's given them less time than they think — and whatever she meant to decide upstream, she'd best decide it today."

Downriver, faint under the tin-colored dawn, the river went on talking. Ilo listened while she worked, and tried to hear whether it sounded like a house whose owners were stepping back in — or a house about to be locked forever.
