## 15. Landfall Warning

Odile heard the storm before anyone told her about it.

She was in the engine room, elbows-deep in the cooling system, when the pressure in her ears changed — that faint, subterranean shift she'd felt a hundred times in thirty years of running boats, the way the air itself seemed to lean downriver. She sat back on her heels, wiped her hands on a rag that had stopped being gray sometime last year, and listened. The river had gone quiet in the wrong way. Not calm. Holding its breath.

By the time she climbed into the daylight, Tam was already on the foredeck with the barometer, and Wren was standing beside him, and neither of them was saying anything, which told her everything.

"How bad?" she asked.

Wren held out a hand without turning. Tam passed the barometer over. Wren studied it the way other people studied love letters.

"Fell twelve points since dawn," Wren said. "And it's still falling."

"That's not our storm," Tam said. "The monsoon line was supposed to sit south of the confluence for another week. I've been tracking the reports off the relay. It's moved."

"Moved where?"

"North. Straight at us." Tam's jaw worked. "Odile, I ran the numbers twice. Landfall in five days. Maybe less."

Five days. Odile did her own arithmetic, fast and ugly. They'd had nine. Nine had been tight — enough to load, enough to run the descent at a careful pace, enough to argue with the river a little and win. Five days meant no arguing. Five days meant the Marguerite would be running ahead of the rain with her stern already wet, and every hour Voss spent packing crates was an hour the river took back with interest.

"Does Voss know?" she asked.

"She knows," Wren said. "Her weather wire came in an hour ago. She's been in the command tent since."

"And?"

"And she's accelerating." Wren's voice was flat, which was how Wren sounded when the truth was worst. "Everything moves today. Everything."

Odile looked upriver, toward the terraces, toward the cavern mouth where the water had been lowering all week like a held curtain, and felt the old engine-room dread settle into her chest, familiar as a worn bearing.

* * *

By midmorning the camp had turned into something Odile didn't have a clean word for.

Voss had every crate that could be crated staged on the lower terrace, ranked in rows like ammunition. The crane — a battered lattice-boom unit the Survey had hauled up in sections two seasons ago, the kind of machine Odile would have condemned in any yard she ran — swung its hook over the water, lowering loads onto the Marguerite's cargo deck in a rhythm that had no patience left in it.

And the scientists were hauling.

Odile watched from the wheelhouse roof, a wrench in her hand she had no use for, and watched Dr. Ferro — a small, precise man who catalogued pottery shards with cotton gloves — dragging a crate across the stone with his shoulder, his gloves gone, his hands bleeding where the rope burned them. Two of Mercer's men stood over the loading line with rifles held in the casual way of men who wanted to be seen holding them. Not pointed. Just present. The distinction was supposed to be a courtesy. It wasn't.

"They're not even pretending anymore," Bex said, coming up beside her with a thermos neither of them drank from.

"No."

"Ansel tried to argue. This morning. Said the load order was destroying stratigraphy, that half those crates were packed wrong, that some of the pieces would come apart before they ever saw a museum." Bex's laugh had no humor in it. "Mercer told him the stratigraphy could testify at the inquiry too, if it felt like walking."

Odile's grip tightened on the wrench. She was not a violent woman. She had spent a lifetime fixing things that other people broke, and it had taught her a specific and durable patience: everything could be mended, given time and the right hands. But watching Ferro stumble under a crate that two grown men should have been carrying, while a man with a rifle shifted his weight like he was bored — something in her that was usually a bearing began instead to grind.

"How many crates fit?" Bex asked. "Realistically. If we run heavy."

"If we run heavy and the river runs kind? Forty. Fifty." Odile watched the hook swing down again. "She's staging two hundred."

"She knows that."

"Course she knows that. She's not loading a boat. She's loading a claim. Whatever gets out proves the backers were here first and best. The rest—" Odile nodded at the terrace, at the rows of numbered crates, at the cavern mouth behind them. "The rest is for the insurance file. Or the fire."

Bex went still. "You think she'd burn it."

"I think a woman who points rifles at geologists will do whatever her arithmetic tells her." Odile handed back the thermos. "And her arithmetic just got five days shorter."

* * *

Odile spent the afternoon doing the one thing nobody had ordered her to do: inspecting the crane.

It was plausible work. She was the engineer; the crane was a machine; machines on her boat's loading line were, by ancient maritime custom and by the specific terms of the resupply contract, her responsibility. When Mercer's man at the terrace edge asked what she was doing, she told him the truth in a voice bored enough to be a wall: checking the boom welds before they hung two hundred crates over her deck in a storm front. He looked at her, looked at the rifles' worth of authority he carried, and decided a greasy woman with a flashlight was beneath his attention.

She started at the base and worked up.

The crane was a liar, the way old machines are liars — it had been telling everyone it was fine for years, and everyone had wanted to believe it. The slew ring was scored. The boom's third section had a weld that some previous season's hurry had laid down cold, and the metal around it was crazed with hairline fractures like frost on a window. The main hoist wire was serviceable. The load block was serviceable. The brake band had maybe a season left.

A machine like this would work, Odile thought, right up until it didn't. And it wouldn't give you a warning you'd recognize unless you were the kind of person who listened to metal the way Wren listened to water.

She sat on the boom footing with her flashlight and her rag and let herself think about it plainly, because she was fifty-eight years old and had run out of patience for thinking about things sideways.

Voss was going to overload this crane. Voss was going to run it in wind, with exhausted people, at a pace that had no respect in it, because her five days were shorter than everyone else's. And when it failed — not if, when — it would fail over the Marguerite's deck, or over the loading line where Ferro and his bleeding hands were standing, and the failure would be called weather, or sabotage, or an act of the crew, and the people under it would still be under it.

Odile had spent her whole life keeping machines honest.

She took out her tools.

* * *

She was careful about it. That was the whole craft of it — sabotage, she was discovering, was just maintenance run in reverse, and she was very good at maintenance.

The cold weld on the third boom section didn't need help. She simply stopped helping it. There were two shims taking load at the section joint that a more conscientious rigger would have replaced years ago; she loosened their set screws a quarter turn each, enough that vibration would walk them loose over a day of hard swinging. She didn't touch the hoist wire — that would kill someone, and the line between what she'd do and what she wouldn't was the last clean thing she had. Instead she went after the crane's obedience.

The limit switch that stopped the boom at maximum slew was a cheap electromechanical unit, and its housing was held by four screws gone soft with corrosion. She backed two of them out entirely and left the other two finger-tight, then dressed the contacts with a film of grease that would insulate them beautifully until the day it mattered. In wind, under load, with the boom worked hard, the switch would chatter and fail. The boom would swing past its stop. Not catastrophically — the mechanical stop would catch it, eventually, with a violence the cold weld would have opinions about.

She sat back and looked at her work and felt the strangest thing, which was pride. Thirty years of keeping things running, and the finest piece of engineering she'd ever done was a machine she was teaching to fall down.

"You're smiling," said a voice above her. "That worries me more than the rifle men."

Wren stood at the terrace edge, hands in pockets, watching her with the particular stillness that Wren used instead of questions.

"Boom's tired," Odile said. "I gave it a diagnosis."

"Uh-huh."

"Limit switch is worn. Slew ring's scored. Third-section weld is cold work — somebody rushed it, seasons back." She wiped her hands, slow and deliberate. "Machine like that, you push it the way she's pushing it, it's going to come apart. I'd put money on the third section, under a heavy swing, in a gust. Over the water, if we're lucky. Over the crates, more likely."

Wren crouched down to her level. In the low light the wheelhouse lamp threw, Wren's face was unreadable, which was its own language between them — Wren asking without asking.

"You did something," Wren said.

"I did nothing. I did less than nothing. There's a difference, and it's the whole difference." Odile held the look. "I'm not going to lie to you on your own boat. But I'm not going to write it in the log, either. Some repairs are between me and the machine."

Wren was quiet a long moment. Down at the loading line, the hook came down, the rope crew swarmed, Ferro's thin shoulders took a crate's weight, and the rifle man shifted his bored weight from one foot to the other.

"If it comes apart," Wren said finally, "people could die. Ferro. Tam. Anyone on the deck."

"I know where it'll come apart. I've known for three hours. That's the point of doing it yourself instead of letting the machine surprise you." Odile stood, knees cracking, and looked her captain full in the face. "Nobody dies. I'll make sure of it. But half those crates go in the water, and Voss loses her claim, and she can't prove it was anything but an old crane in a storm. Because it *is* an old crane in a storm, Wren. I just stopped lying to it on her behalf."

Wren looked at her for a long time. Then, quietly: "The log says the limit switch is worn."

"The log says exactly that."

"Then we understand each other." Wren turned toward the loading line, then paused. "Odile. When this is over — whatever's over — I want you to know I saw the arithmetic you're doing. The kind where you spend yourself to stop something worse. I just want you to spend carefully."

"I always spend carefully," Odile said. "That's why it took me thirty years to buy anything worth spending on."

* * *

Evening came down the valley like a lid.

The wind had turned. All day it had run upriver, the ordinary valley draft, and now it came down from the heights instead, carrying a smell Odile knew from her childhood on the coast — green rot and ozone, the smell of a storm still over the horizon and already in the air. The barometer fell another six points. Tam taped a fresh track of the storm reports to the wheelhouse bulkhead and stood looking at it the way sailors look at things they can't argue with.

Voss came down to the boat at dusk.

Odile was in the engine room and heard the conversation through the deck plates, Voss's voice pitched to carry, Wren's pitched not to. Voss wanted a night shift. Lights on the terrace, the crane running under floodlamps, loading until the crates ran out or the sky did. Wren said the deck crew was exhausted. Voss said exhaustion was a scheduling problem. Wren said something Odile couldn't hear, in the voice Wren saved for the last word of an argument, and the deck plates carried the silence that followed, which was louder than the argument.

Then boots on the gangway, and Mercer's voice, low and flat, giving the terrace orders, and the floodlamps stuttered on against the early dark, and the crane began to move at night.

Odile climbed up on deck and watched it.

The crane under floodlight was a skeleton of hard shadows, swinging its loads through the dark, and the scientists moved beneath it like people in a bad dream, and the wind came down the valley in longer and longer pushes, and every swing of the boom walked her shims a little looser, and she stood there counting.

Not the crates. The swings. She'd estimated the third-section weld had perhaps four hundred hard cycles left under load, fewer in gusts, and she stood at the rail with her hands folded and counted the boom's rhythm like a rosary, like a fuse burning down.

Three hundred and ten. Three hundred and eleven.

Tam came to stand beside her. Neither of them spoke. Below, Ferro stumbled, caught himself, took the weight again. The floodlamps buzzed. The wind pushed.

"Five days," Tam said at last.

"Four and a half," Odile said. "And about three hundred swings."

Tam looked at her. Odile looked at the crane. Understanding moved between them the way it moved between old crew, without freight or ceremony.

"Three hundred," Tam said. "Then what?"

"Then," Odile said, as the boom swung another loaded crate out over the black water, "the machine tells the truth."
