## 16. The Crane Falls

Tam had spent three days watching the crane the way you watch a snake you've decided not to trust.

It was a survey crane, bolted to a concrete pad Voss's people had poured in the first week — a lattice boom on a slew ring, a diesel winch with a cable that sang when the wind came off the water. It was built for lifting instrument crates and outboard motors. It was not built for what Voss was asking of it now: ton after ton of sealed archive crates, hauled up from the cavern mouth by sweating scientists and slung into the Marguerite's hold like so much cargo grain.

Tam knew cranes the way Odile knew engines — not by schooling but by proximity, by years of watching things fail. And Tam could see the failure coming. The slew ring bolts were weeping rust at two corners. The boom's third panel had been patched once with a weld that looked like candle wax. Every time the winch took a heavy load, the whole rig leaned a half-degree to starboard and then shuddered back, like a man deciding whether to stay standing.

Odile had weakened it further. Tam had helped.

Not much. A file taken to one pin on the night shift, a shim loosened where no inspector would ever look, a turnbuckle backed off a quarter-turn that read as settling. Small work. Deniable work. Tam had done it with their heart going like a drum and their hands steady as rope, because Bex had come below decks two nights ago with Mercer's orders copied out in her careful cook's handwriting, and the words *burn the site* had changed something in all of them that could not be changed back.

You didn't argue with a man who intended arson. You didn't petition him. You broke his tools.

Now the sky over the terraces was the color of wet slate, and the wind had a taste to it — green, vegetal, the smell of rain still hours away but already thinking about them. Voss stood on the loading platform with a clipboard she didn't need, calling weights to her people, and Mercer walked the line between the scientists and the crate stacks with a rifle slung across his back the way other men wore a towel. Casual. Deliberate. Seen.

"Next load," Voss called. "Reyes, your team is slow."

Ansel Reyes came up the ramp with six others, all of them gray-faced, all of them moving like men carrying furniture they hated. They'd been hauling since dawn. The junior one — Pell, the cartographer whose wrist Mercer had grabbed yesterday hard enough to bruise — stumbled on the ramp and caught himself on the rail, and Mercer's head turned toward the sound with the smooth attention of a bird.

"Load it," Mercer said.

They loaded it. Four crates this time, strapped into a sling, heavier than the last set because Ansel had argued for the stone tablets and won — won, Tam thought, in the way that mattered only if the tablets survived the day. The winch took the strain. The cable sang. The boom leaned its familiar half-degree to starboard.

And then, for the first time in three days, it kept leaning.

Tam saw it from the Marguerite's deck, twenty meters off the pad, coiling a line that didn't need coiling. Saw the lean go past the point where it always stopped. Saw Odile, down in the engine well, look up through the deck hatch with an expression Tam had never seen on her face before — not satisfaction, nothing so clean as that. Something closer to grief. The face of a person who has set a thing in motion and must now watch it land.

The slew ring let go at the two corners where the rust wept.

It didn't explode. That was the terrible part, how quiet it was. Two bolts sheared with a sound like knuckles cracking, and the whole upper works of the crane — boom, winch, cable, sling, four crates of a drowned civilization's memory — rotated slowly, almost politely, past its designed stop, and then gravity remembered it, and the boom came down across the loading platform like the arm of God swatting a table.

The crash went through the ground and up through the Marguerite's hull and into Tam's teeth. Dust boiled up white. The four crates hit the concrete and burst — Tam heard wood go, heard stone inside it go worse — and half the packed inventory of three weeks' labor became rubble and shards in the space of four seconds.

Someone was screaming. It took Tam a moment to find the voice in the dust, and when they did, it was worse than screaming would have been.

Mercer lay under the boom's second panel, pinned from hip to knee, and he wasn't screaming. He was making a low, rhythmic sound, like a man trying to breathe through a folded throat, and his rifle had skittered eight meters away across the pad, and every scientist within sight of him had gone absolutely still, the way animals go still around a wounded predator.

* * *

Voss crossed the platform in ten strides.

Tam watched her do it and understood, in that moment, that everything until now had been rehearsal. Voss didn't run to Mercer the way you run to a hurt person. She moved like an officer moving to a fallen standard — fast, upright, unafraid, making sure everyone saw her arrive. She knelt, said something too low to carry, and Mercer's rhythmic sound changed pitch. Then she stood, and turned, and found the Marguerite with her eyes across twenty meters of gray air, and Tam felt those eyes land on them like a hand on the back of the neck.

"You." Voss's voice carried without effort. It always had; it was a voice built for lecture halls and boardrooms, and now it was built for this. "Galloway. Get over here."

Wren Galloway came down the gangway unhurried, which Tam recognized as either great courage or great practice, and possibly both. Ten years on the river had taught Tam that Wren's slowness was a weapon. People mistook it for calm and told them things.

"The crane failed," Wren said.

"The crane was sabotaged." Voss's voice didn't rise. That was the frightening thing about it. It got quieter and more precise, the way water gets faster in a narrow channel. "My engineer inspected that rig two days ago. Rated for double this load. So either your engineer knows something about fatigue I don't—" her eyes flicked toward the Marguerite, toward Odile's empty hatch, "—or someone aboard your boat has been doing carpentry."

"Or," Wren said, "your rig was never rated for what you asked of it, and you killed it with arithmetic."

For a heartbeat Tam thought Voss might actually strike them. The whole platform seemed to lean toward the possibility — scientists frozen mid-step, dust still sifting down through the light, Mercer's breathing rasping under the boom like a broken bellows. Voss's hand closed around her clipboard until the cardboard creaked.

Then she smiled, and it was worse.

"Search the boat," she said, to two of her security men. "Hold the crew on deck. All of them." She turned back to Wren, and now her voice dropped into something almost intimate, almost kind, which was the most dangerous register it had. "Captain, you and I are going to have a conversation about your contract. On your deck. Where your people can hear it."

* * *

They put the crew together on the Marguerite's foredeck like livestock at a fence.

Two riflemen aft, one amidships, Mercer's third man up on the wheelhouse roof with a view of everything. The scientists had been ordered back to camp — ordered, not asked — and Ansel had gone with them, but not before catching Tam's eye across the water with a look that said, plainly, *hold on*. Tam held on.

Bex stood close enough that her shoulder touched Tam's arm. She hadn't spoken since the crane fell. Her hands were flour-white still, from the morning's bread, and they were shaking, and she kept them folded so nobody would see, except Tam saw, because that was what family was for.

Odile came up from the engine well last, wiping her hands on a rag with tremendous deliberation, and Tam watched the riflemen watch her, and watched her give them nothing. Her face was a locked door. Only Tam, who had shared a fo'c'sle with her for six years, could see the cost of keeping it locked — the small muscle working in her jaw, the way her eyes went once, quickly, to the sky and then away, doing the arithmetic of clouds.

Five days. Maybe less. Odile had said nine yesterday morning. The sky had eaten four of them while everyone argued about crates.

Voss came up the gangway with Wren beside her, and stopped at the foredeck's edge, and looked at the four of them — Wren, Odile, Tam, Bex — arranged against the rail with the darkening river behind them, and Tam realized she was counting them. Weighing them. Deciding what each one was worth and what each one would cost.

"I'm going to say this once," Voss said, "and then we're going to work. Three days ago I offered Captain Galloway a fortune to move the first shipment ahead of the storm. The captain chose to stall me. Yesterday my security chief intercepted a falsified radio log entry in this crew's handwriting. Today my crane falls down." She let that sit. The wind pushed at her coat. Somewhere behind her, faintly, Mercer was being dug out, and the sound of the digging was the only sound on the river. "I have spent my career being told that people like you are the honest ones. Boat people. River people. Salt-of-the-earth people who'd never steal, never lie, never sabotage a piece of equipment with men standing under it." Her voice cracked, just once, on *men*, and Tam understood that whatever Mercer was to her, it wasn't only payroll. "So here is what happens now. You will load what remains. You will run it downriver ahead of the rain. And when the season turns, you will be paid — paid honestly, paid in full — and you will never see any of us again. Or—"

"Or," Wren said quietly.

"There is no or," Voss said. "That's what I came to tell you. There was an or when this was a business arrangement. There isn't one anymore. My backers have invested too much, the window is closing, and I am not going to stand on this river and explain to eleven shareholders that a cook and a deckhand out-argued me." She looked at each of them in turn, and Tam made themselves meet it, made themselves be a post, a piling, something driven deep. "You're crew. Act like crew. Load the boat."

Nobody moved.

The silence stretched until Tam could hear the river against the hull, patient, enormous, taking notes. Bex's shoulder pressed harder against Tam's arm. Odile's rag had stopped moving. And Wren Galloway stood at the rail with the wind pulling at their collar and said, in the voice they used for negotiating sandbars and nothing else, mild and absolute:

"No."

Voss's jaw tightened. "Captain—"

"You want to know what happened to your crane?" Wren said. "I'll tell you what I know, which is what the river knows. You asked a survey rig to lift stone. Stone is heavy. Heavy things fall. That's not sabotage, Doctor, that's physics, and physics doesn't take contracts." They stepped forward, one pace, and Tam's heart went into their throat, because there were rifles right there, right there, and Wren walked toward them the way they walked toward a shoal they'd decided to thread. "But here's what I think actually happened. I think your chief there—" a nod toward the platform, toward the men still levering at the boom, "—has been running loads you never signed off on. Night shifts. Extra weight. Because somebody upstream wants numbers, and Mercer makes numbers happen. Check his tally sheets. Check what left the cavern at two in the morning. Your crane didn't fail, Doctor. It was *used*."

It was a lie. It was a beautiful, seamless lie, built in four seconds out of true materials, and Tam watched it land because it gave Voss somewhere to look besides at them. Doubt was a door, and Wren had just opened it in the middle of her certainty, and Tam saw her hesitate — saw the clipboard lower a centimeter, saw her glance toward the platform, toward the tally sheets that did exist, that Mercer did keep, that were falsified in ways even Voss might not know about.

Then Mercer screamed from under the boom — not pain this time, words, torn and furious: "*Imara. Don't. Listen to me, don't you dare listen—*" and the doubt died in Voss's face like a lamp going out.

She straightened. When she spoke again, the boardroom was gone from her voice entirely, and what was underneath it was colder and older and much more honest.

"Bex," she said.

Bex flinched. Tam felt it through the shoulder contact like a current.

"Your galley stores were inventoried Monday," Voss said. "Flour, salt, fuel, medical kit. Wednesday morning the medical kit is missing forty ampules of morphine, and Wednesday afternoon my chief finds the wrappers burned in your stove." She hadn't moved, hadn't raised her voice; she was laying cards down one at a time on a table only she could see. "Somebody on this boat has been dosing my sentries' coffee for two nights. Somebody wanted my watch rotation soft and sleepy. I wonder who that could be." Her eyes rested on Bex with a terrible gentleness. "I could put you ashore for that. With the tents. With the radio that doesn't reach anywhere. In monsoon season."

The wind came off the water then, hard, the first real shove of it, and the Marguerite leaned at her mooring lines, and every halyard and stay on the old boat began to sing at once — that low multi-string note Tam had known since childhood, the sound the boat made when weather was coming, the sound Wren called her singing and nobody else was allowed to call anything.

"So," Voss said, over the singing. "We understand each other now. No more pretense. No more contracts. You load my cargo, you run my river, and you do it because I am holding your boat, your licenses, your names, and your cook. And if anything else on this expedition breaks, falls, burns, or goes quiet—" she paused, and finished it simply, "—I will stop needing any of you alive."

She turned and went down the gangway. The riflemen stayed.

For a long moment nobody on the foredeck spoke. The boat sang. The sky came down another shade darker, and far upriver, beyond the drowned terraces, thunder cleared its throat and considered.

Tam exhaled, and found their voice worked, barely. "Wren," they said, low, under the wind. "The morphine. How does she—"

"Doesn't matter." Wren was looking at the sky, doing Odile's arithmetic, and their face in that gray light was the face of a person stepping off a known chart. "What matters is she's right about one thing. There's no pretending anymore. Whatever we do next, we do it in the open."

Odile folded her rag with great care, tucked it in her belt, and said, "Then we'd better do it tonight. Storm moves up the valley by tomorrow noon. After that, nobody runs this river — not her, not us, not God."

Above the wheelhouse, the boat sang on, and Tam looked at the riflemen, and the riflemen looked at the darkening water, and somewhere between them the last hour of legality drained away like water finding a crack.

That night, Tam would remember later, the rain arrived early — and with it, Wren Galloway put a hand on the mooring cleat and asked the crew, quietly, in the dark, one question:

"How fast can we cut loose?"
