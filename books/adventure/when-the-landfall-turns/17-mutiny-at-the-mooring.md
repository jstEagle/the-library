## 17. Mutiny at the Mooring

The rain came first as a smell — green rot and hot metal rolling down from the high country — and then as a sound on the tarps over the crates, a thousand small drums tuning up for the storm that was no longer five days out but three, maybe two.

Wren stood at the Marguerite's rail and watched Voss's people work the loading line in the gray half-light. Mercer had a rifle slung across his back now instead of hidden under his coat, and he walked the line like a man counting money he hadn't earned yet. The scientists moved crates with their heads down. One of them — the young botanist, Ferro, who'd cried into his coffee the night Bex fed him real food — stumbled on the wet planking and went to one knee under a crate of sealed stone tablets.

"Up," Mercer said. Not loud. That was the worst of it. He never needed to be loud anymore.

Ferro got up. He picked up the crate. And Ansel Reyes stepped out of the loading line and put his hand flat on top of it.

"No," Ansel said.

The word was small against the rain-drum, but it carried. It carried because everyone on that bank had been waiting three days for someone to say it.

Wren's hands were already moving before their mind caught up — unhooking the gangway latch, stepping down onto the mud-slick shore. Odile appeared at the wheelhouse window behind them, and Tam came up the deck from the stern lines, and Bex set down the crate she'd been pretending to carry. Nobody spoke. Ten years on this river had taught Wren that a crew didn't need orders when the moment arrived; they only needed to have been paying attention all along.

"Dr. Reyes." Voss came around the end of the crane truck, umbrella held by one of her aides, her voice pitched for reasonableness the way Mercer's wasn't. "You're exhausted. Everyone is. This is not the moment—"

"It's exactly the moment," Ansel said. His glasses were fogged and he didn't wipe them. "I've documented eleven thousand items. I've photographed, catalogued, cross-referenced. What you're loading right now — those crates you're pointing at — are the burial records. The names. Do you understand? These are *names*. People wrote them so the water would remember them, and you're shipping them to a warehouse in a country none of them ever saw."

"They'll be preserved—"

"They'll be *owned*." Ansel's voice cracked, and he let it. "There's a difference. I spent six years learning it."

Mercer shifted his weight. Wren watched his hand drift toward the sling of the rifle, casual as a man scratching an itch, and something cold settled in their chest — not fear exactly, but the old river-knowledge that some currents look calm right up until they take your boat sideways.

"Dr. Voss," Wren said, and stepped between them. "Contract says I ferry cargo. Contract doesn't say I ferry it at gunpoint, and it sure doesn't say my boat becomes a barge for stolen graves."

"The contract," Voss said, "says triple rate for whatever I designate as priority freight. You signed it, Captain. In Port Calloway. With witnesses."

"I did." Wren kept their voice level, kept their eyes on hers. "And I've been doing arithmetic since. Triple pay buys me a new engine, a new hull, maybe a new life. It doesn't buy me looking at myself in the mirror of a morning. You want to know what that costs? More than you've got."

For a heartbeat — one long heartbeat, with the rain coming harder and the crane cable creaking overhead — Wren thought she might actually hear it. There was something human in Dr. Imara Voss somewhere; they'd seen it flicker once, watching the terraces come up out of the floodwater, before she'd turned away and started measuring.

Then Mercer said, "Captain, step away from the doctor," and the moment closed like water over a drowned thing.

* * *

It happened fast after that, the way bad weather does.

Voss ordered the loading resumed. Ansel sat down on the crate of names and four other scientists sat down beside him, arms linked, and Mercer grabbed Ferro by the collar and hauled him up and Ferro — thin, frightened, weeping Ferro — spat in his face.

The rifle came off Mercer's shoulder.

Wren had spent ten years learning to read water: the way a current lies about its depth, the way a sandbar moves a foot a night without anyone seeing it go. Reading men was the same skill wearing different clothes. They read Mercer now — read the coiled readiness in him, read the fact that he wanted this, had been maneuvering toward it for days, because a man with a gun pointed at civilians could do anything and call it security.

They also read the ground. Mud, ankle-deep, churned by three days of hauling. A man braced to shoot someone standing in that muck was a man about to find out how fast footing fails.

"Mercer." Wren didn't raise their voice. "Before you do something the inquiry will hang you for — look at your feet."

He glanced down. Half a second. It was enough for Tam.

Tam had been easing along the Marguerite's rail since the standoff began, and now he swung down onto the mooring cleat with a knife already open and cut the bowline clean through. Then the stern. Two strokes, quick as gull-wings, and the Marguerite began to slide off the bank into the brown flood-current with the whole negotiation still standing on the shore.

"What—" Voss spun. "Captain! Captain, what are you *doing*—"

Wren was already moving. Three steps through the mud, a grab for the trailing stern line as the gap widened — Odile's hand locked around their wrist, hauling, and then the deck was under their boots and the bank was falling away behind them and the river had them.

"Odile," Wren said, "get us twenty meters off and hold station."

"Twenty meters, holding." The engine turned over with its old arthritic cough and then caught, and the Marguerite swung her nose into the current and hung there, forty tons of steel riding the flood like a held breath.

On the shore, chaos. Mercer with the rifle half-raised and nowhere to point it — shooting the captain wouldn't get the boat back, and even he could do that math. Voss shouting orders nobody could execute. The scientists on their feet now, all of them, and Ansel laughing — actually laughing, a wild cracked sound — while the rain finally broke loose in earnest and the whole world went silver.

Wren stood at the rail and looked back at the woman whose contract they were tearing up with every meter of water between them, and found, to their surprise, that it hurt. Not because she deserved better. Because Wren Galloway had never broken a contract in ten years, not in low water, not in flood, not when the pay went bad or the cargo went rotten. A captain's word was the only chart that mattered. They had just torn it up in front of witnesses.

"You all right?" Tam said quietly, at their shoulder.

"No," Wren said. "But I'm right. Those aren't always the same thing, but today they are."

Tam nodded slowly. "Mama Ilo used to say — belonging isn't a thing you sign for."

"She said that to you?"

"She said it to everybody. Took me till now to hear it." He looked back at the shrinking shore, at the tiny furious figure of Voss, at the archive entrance hidden somewhere behind the terraces where the flood was already rising to keep its own counsel. "We going back for the rest of them?"

"We're going back for all of them," Wren said. "Every soul on that bank who wants to leave. Cargo stays."

* * *

They brought the Marguerite in again at dusk, stern-first, using the flooded side channel Wren had memorized on the way up — the one Voss's people couldn't see from camp because a wall of willow and rising water stood between. Rain hammered the canopy. Under it, the scientists came down to the bank in ones and twos, carrying not crates but packs, notebooks, each other.

Ansel came last, wading out to the gunwale with water to his thighs, and gripped Wren's forearm hard enough to bruise.

"Eleven thousand items documented," he said. "Coordinates encrypted, copies split three ways — mine, Ferro's, and one I'm trusting to Mama Ilo. If Voss burns the site, the record survives. If she takes nothing, the site survives. Either way the backers lose."

"And if she comes after us?"

Ansel looked back at the darkening camp, where a single generator light burned and a radio antenna stood black against the sky. "She's got a chartered launch moored upstream. Fast one. She brought it for artifact runs."

Wren felt the arithmetic turn over in their chest, cold and clear. A launch could run eight knots in flood-water. The Marguerite, loaded heavy with people instead of profit, could make five on her best day and her oldest engine.

"Then we don't outrun her," Wren said. "We out-*know* her. Get aboard, Ansel. Everybody aboard?"

"Everybody who's coming. Two stayed with her. Kessler and Ndiaye — they believe the science needs protecting more than the site does." His jaw tightened. "Their choice."

"Their choice," Wren agreed. "River remembers choices too."

They cast off into full dark, running lightless, Odile reading the flood by the feel of the wheel and Wren calling channels from a memory ten years deep. Behind them the camp light shrank to a coal, then a spark, then nothing — and somewhere back there, Wren knew, a radio was warming up, and a woman who had measured everything was discovering the one thing she couldn't: that the river had already decided whose side it was on.

Ahead, downstream, the narrows waited — and past the narrows, the elders' settlement, and past that, a hundred miles of rising water between them and home.

"Hold on to something," Wren told the wheelhouse. "Rain's here early. We're going to be faster than we've ever been."

The Marguerite answered the way she always did — a shudder through the hull, a deepening of the engine note, as if the old boat had been waiting ten years for someone to ask this of her.
