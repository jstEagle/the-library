## 18. Running Ahead of the Rain

The Marguerite left the terraces behind at first light, and Wren did not look back.

They had sealed the cavern mouth at dawn — Ansel's idea, Mama Ilo's people doing the work with stones and rope and a patience that made Wren's chest ache. No blasting, no concrete. Just the river's own flood, invited in. By the time the monsoon arrived, the water would do what it had done for a thousand years: keep what it kept, and tell no one.

"Wheel's yours, Captain," Tam said, and there was something in his voice Wren hadn't heard since Port Calloway. Not fear. Readiness. The whole crew had that look — Odile in the engine hatch with her sleeves rolled, Bex lashing the last of the cargo down, the scientists crowded on the middeck like passengers on a ferry that had never been built to carry so many.

Because she wasn't. The Marguerite was a supply boat, eighty feet of shallow-draft steel built for crates and fuel drums, and now she carried nineteen souls: seven scientists, four of Mama Ilo's people who had come upriver to help and could not go back the way they'd come, and the wounded junior scientist — Ferro, his name was Ferro — laid out on a pallet of life vests in the shade of the wheelhouse, his broken arm splinted with a survey stave.

"Sandbar at the first bend's moved," Wren said. "It always moves after the first big rain. I want eyes on the water, everyone. The river's not the river we came up."

Odile's head appeared from the hatch, grease to her elbows. "She's not either. I've got her running hot as she'll tolerate. If you ask for more than that, she'll give it to us and then die."

"Then I won't ask."

The first rain came as a gray curtain two miles downstream, visible and patient, like something waiting at a door. Wren eased the throttle forward and the Marguerite leaned into the current, and the run began.

* * *

The first hour was the sandbar at the first bend, exactly where Wren had said, except it wasn't — it had migrated a hundred yards east and grown a hump that broke the surface like a whale's back, brown water peeling off it in long V-wakes.

"Hard to starboard," Wren called, and Tam threw his weight against the wheel, and the hull shuddered as they shaved the bar's shoulder — a grinding kiss, half a second, then free.

"That's going to leave a mark on the paint," Bex said from the rail.

"That's going to leave a mark on the plate," Odile shouted up. "But she'll hold."

They held. The second bend drowned entirely — a cutbank Wren had steered around a hundred times in low water was now simply gone, a wide brown sheet where the channel should have been, and Wren had to read the current the old way, the way their father had taught them before there was a father to teach it anymore: by the texture of the water. Smooth water lies. The boils and seams and long dark tongues of faster current — that's where the channel lives. Wren stood at the wheel with the rain starting to spit and read the river like a page, and the Marguerite followed.

"You know this water," Ansel said quietly. He'd wedged himself into the corner of the wheelhouse, notebook in a plastic sleeve, watching.

"I know this water the way it was," Wren said. "Right now I'm guessing. There's a difference."

"You're very good at guessing."

"Ten years of miles." Wren heard the old dryness in their own voice and was surprised to find it had gone soft somewhere in the last three weeks. "Ansel. The archive. You're sure about the seal?"

"Sure as anything is. The flood does the rest. In a season, in a year, nobody finds it who isn't meant to." He looked out at the drowned cutbank. "Mama Ilo's people have known where it is for a hundred generations. They never sold it, never mined it, never wrote it on a map. That's the whole argument, isn't it? That's the whole case. It was never lost. It was kept."

Wren turned the wheel two degrees and the boat answered, and below them the drowned terraces slid away under brown water, and Wren did not look back.

* * *

At midday the river turned against them in earnest.

The storm's leading edge had come down the eastern tributaries overnight, and every feeder creek was running chocolate, throwing debris — whole trees, fence rails, a dead cow turning slowly — into the main channel. Tam stood at the bow rail with a boat hook, fending off the worst of it, and once a floating log the size of a canoe came at them beam-on and he couldn't reach it and everyone held their breath and the Marguerite took it on the bow quarter with a crack that Odile swore about for a full minute.

"Plate's dented, not opened," she reported finally. "But we're done being gentle."

"We were never gentle," Wren said.

The narrows were the problem. Wren had been saving the thought of them like a stone in the shoe. Eleven miles of rock-walled channel, the river's throat, where in low water there were three bends and in flood there was no water at all — just current, a single smooth brown muscle pouring through, no seams to read, no boils, nothing to steer by except memory and the tops of the rocks that used to be islands.

"We could tie off and wait it out," Tam said.

"Wait for what? Higher water?" Wren shook their head. "We run it now, in daylight, while I can still see the rock teeth. Tomorrow it's a waterfall."

Nobody argued. That was the thing about the crew — Wren had spent ten years telling themselves the silence aboard the Marguerite was loneliness, and only now, running a flooded river with nineteen people trusting them absolutely, did they understand it had never been silence at all. It had been a language. Odile didn't argue because Odile had already done the arithmetic on the engine. Tam didn't argue because Tam had already tied off the life vests. Bex didn't argue because Bex had already put coffee in everyone's hands, because that was how Bex fought fear.

"Everybody below or clipped in," Wren said. "Ferro strapped double. Bex, sit on him."

Bex sat on him.

Wren took the Marguerite into the narrows at half throttle, which was madness, and then full throttle, which was the only thing that worked, because a boat steers on water moving past her rudder and in the narrows the whole world was moving. The walls came alive on either side, rock streaked with falling water, and the channel was a rumor, and Wren steered by the one rock that never moved — the Split Tooth, a black fang in the center where the current divided — and hung the boat off it so close that Tam, clipped at the rail, could have touched it.

They came through the last bend sideways and straightened out into the long drowned valley below, and the whole boat exhaled at once.

"Captain," Ferro said from under Bex, pale and grinning, "I have written papers about rivers. I would like to formally retract all of them."

* * *

They took on Mama Ilo's people at the settlement landing at dusk, in the rain, in the dark.

It hadn't been the plan. The plan had been to drop the scientists at the settlement and run the Marguerite down to Port Calloway light and fast. But the settlement's own boats were gone — lent downriver to villages that had flooded worse — and the storm was coming a day early, and Mama Ilo stood on the landing with the rain running off her chin and did not ask for anything.

That was the thing Wren understood about her by then. She never asked. She stood where she stood, and told the truth, and let you do the arithmetic yourself.

"The low village will be under by morning," she said. "Twelve of us. The old ones, the small ones. The rest go up the ridge to the mission house."

"Twelve," Wren said. "Plus the nineteen we've got."

"Plus the nineteen you've got."

Odile, appearing at Wren's shoulder, said nothing, which said everything: the boat would float. The boat would float with forty on her if the water was kind, and the water was not going to be kind.

"Get them aboard," Wren said.

They came up the landing ladder in the rain — grandmothers and a boy of six with a rooster under his arm, because Wren supposed if you were saving one thing from a flood, a rooster was as good as anything — and Bex fed them all from the galley in shifts while the Marguerite sat tied to the landing and the river rose around her hull, visible in inches, in the lamplight, the mooring lines creeping up the pilings.

Wren stood at the rail beside Mama Ilo and watched the water come.

"You could have taken the offer," she said. It wasn't a question. Voss's money, Wren's fortune, the Marguerite bought outright and then some. Everyone on the boat knew the number had been named.

"The offer was to carry the archive out," Wren said. "I'm carrying people."

"Mm." Mama Ilo watched the rain. "My grandmother said the river keeps two ledgers. One for what floats, one for what sinks. She said most people spend their whole lives trying to get on the wrong one."

"Which one's wrong?"

"She never said. That was the lesson." She turned and looked at Wren, and her eyes in the lamplight were the exact color of the water. "You're on the floating one tonight, Captain. Don't spend the whole run worrying about it."

* * *

They ran through the night.

Wren took the wheel and gave it up twice — to Tam, to Odile, who knew the boat's voice as well as anyone — and took it back, and the rain came down in ropes and the river climbed the banks and the world became a gray corridor with the Marguerite running down the middle of it at full throttle, running ahead of the landfall the way a mouse runs ahead of its own shadow.

There was no exhilaration in it, Wren told themselves, and then there was, and then there wasn't, in waves, like the river itself. There was the wheel alive under their hands and the engine roaring and the whole boat leaning into the current with forty souls aboard, and Wren's blood sang with it — ten years of miles and never a run like this, never a run that mattered — and in the same breath there was the cavern behind them filling with black water, ten thousand years of somebody's careful hands, sealed now, maybe forever, and the grief of it sat in Wren's chest like ballast.

Saving what can be saved. That was the whole job. That had always been the whole job, and they'd never known it until now.

"Sandbar," Tam called at three in the morning, and there it was, a new one, born overnight, right in the mouth of the channel, and Wren took them around it through water so shallow the mud boiled up astern, and the Marguerite never even slowed.

* * *

The delta opened around them at dawn like a hand unclenching.

The rain was still falling but the wind had shifted, gone soft, and the channels braided out into a hundred silver threads through the drowned grass, and somewhere ahead was the delta settlement and high ground and the storm cellars Bex had been describing for two days like a woman describing heaven.

And behind them, faint under the engine noise, a sound Wren had been dreading: a second engine. High-pitched. A launch.

Tam heard it too. He came up the wheelhouse steps two at a time, rain streaming off him. "Tell me that's a pump."

"It's not a pump."

"Voss had a radio. You gave her a radio."

"I gave her a radio," Wren said, "because leaving a person with nothing at all is a different crime than the ones she committed." They watched the gray water astern. The sound was faint, still miles back, but the chartered launch from Port Calloway — if that's what it was — was fast, faster than the Marguerite, and the flooded river didn't care how fast you were if you didn't know where the new sandbars had grown overnight.

"Can we lose her?" Tam asked.

Wren looked at the braided channels ahead, the drowned grass, the water that had rearranged itself overnight into a maze that existed nowhere on any chart. They thought about the narrows. They thought about the Split Tooth. They thought about a channel they'd found once, years ago, at the bottom of a low-water season, a thread of deep water behind a drowned levee that no survey had ever marked because no survey had ever run this river in the rain.

"Maybe," Wren said.

And then the radio crackled, and Voss's voice came out of the storm.
