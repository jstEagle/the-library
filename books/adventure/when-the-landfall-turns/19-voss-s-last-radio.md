## 19. Voss's Last Radio

Ansel Reyes had spent eleven years learning to read stone, and in all that time he had never once been able to stop reading it — the grain of a cliff face at breakfast, the bedding planes in a riverbank while he waited for coffee. So it was fitting, he supposed, that on the second morning of the run downriver, with the Marguerite riding high and heavy with people instead of cargo, what he noticed first was not the water or the sky but the cut bank sliding past on the starboard side: a clean exposure of grey siltstone, cross-bedded, recent. The river was already rewriting itself. Somewhere behind them, everything they had left was being buried under a season's worth of new chapters.

He stood at the rail with his hands wrapped around a tin cup Bex had pressed on him — chicory, terrible, hot — and tried to feel like a man who had won something.

It wasn't working.

* * *

The radio had started at dawn.

Ansel had been sleeping on the deck, folded into the space between the cargo winch and the rail where Tam had wedged him a hammock, and the voice came out of the wheelhouse speaker and into his dreams before he understood it was real. Voss's voice. He knew it the way you know a sound that has governed your days for months — clipped, precise, each consonant placed like a specimen pin.

*"Marguerite, this is Halcyon actual. Do you copy."*

Not a question. A summons.

Ansel sat up so fast the hammock dumped him half onto the deck planks, and by the time he'd untangled himself Wren was already in the wheelhouse, one hand on the wheel, the other nowhere near the radio. Odile stood at the engine hatch below, listening up through the deck boards. Tam crouched on the roof of the wheelhouse like a heron, watching the channel ahead.

The launch had come into view an hour after sunrise — a chartered patrol boat out of Port Calloway, white hull, twin engines, flying nothing. It hung back at the edge of visibility, maybe two kilometers astern, close enough to be a statement and far enough to be a lie about its intentions. Ansel had watched it through Tam's binoculars until his arms shook. Two figures on its flybridge. One of them raised a hand once, slowly, the way you wave to someone across a church aisle.

Now Voss's voice filled the boat again, and this time there was static under it, and under the static something that might have been wind.

*"Wren Galloway. You are in violation of a signed charter agreement. You have stolen contracted vessel time, abandoned contracted personnel, and interfered with a licensed scientific operation. I have your name in front of me. I have your registration number. When we reach Calloway, there will be consequences that will follow you for the rest of your life."*

A pause. The river ran. Somewhere off the port bow a kingfisher went off like a struck match.

*"You are carrying my people, Galloway. Dr. Reyes is my employee. Half those scientists signed their contracts with my expedition. Every hour you run downriver without authorization, you are making their positions worse. Think about that. Think about whose names you're writing your little mutiny across."*

That one landed. Ansel felt it land in himself — the old reflex, the contract-brain, the part of him that had spent three weeks telling himself that staying loyal to Voss was the same thing as staying loyal to science. He looked at the other scientists crowded along the rail: Priya with her burned forearm wrapped in a wet cloth, young Okafor who hadn't stopped shaking since the crane fell, Marta holding her field notebooks against her chest like a child. None of them looked like employees. They looked like people who had finally exhaled.

Wren said nothing. Wren stood at the wheel with the particular stillness Ansel had come to recognize as attention — the whole body become an ear.

Voss's voice came again, and this time it was different, and everyone on the boat heard it change.

*"Galloway. Please."*

The word sat in the air like a dropped tool.

*"The storm has turned. You know it's turned. My radio operator says five days, maybe less, and this launch draws too much to run the upper channels when they flood. If you don't answer, if you just keep going—" A breath, ragged at the edges now, the precision coming apart seam by seam. "—then at least take the scientists to safety. That's all I'm asking. Take them and go. Leave the rest to me."*

Tam swung down from the wheelhouse roof and landed soft on the deck. "She knows," Tam said quietly. "About the narrows. She knows she can't follow us past them."

"She knows," Odile agreed from below, not looking up from her engine. Her voice carried anyway; it always did. "Question is whether she means the plea or the threat. Could be both. People are usually both."

Ansel found himself beside the wheelhouse door without having decided to walk there. "Captain," he said. "For what it's worth — the last time Imara Voss asked anyone to leave someone behind 'for their own good,' it was me asking her, and she ordered Mercer to lock the infirmary."

"I know," Wren said.

"You're not going to answer her?"

Wren's eyes stayed on the channel. The water ahead had gone the color of strong tea, swollen with the storm's first outriders, and the current had picked up enough that the Marguerite's wake curled away at an angle instead of trailing straight. "I'm going to answer her once," Wren said. "When it'll do the most good and the least harm. Not yet."

"When?"

Wren glanced at him then, and Ansel saw the thing he'd been trying to see since the night crossing — the thing Mama Ilo must have seen in the dark of her meeting with them. Not hardness. Something more expensive than hardness. "When she asks the right question," Wren said, and turned back to the river.

* * *

They ran all day.

It was not the river Ansel had come up. He kept checking landmarks against his memory and finding them wrong — a bluff he remembered as sheer now shouldered a gravel bar; the island where they'd taken on firewood had grown a tail of new sand pointing downstream like a finger. The monsoon hadn't even arrived yet and the river was already rehearsing. Twice Odile called depth warnings from the bow sounding line, and twice Wren took the Marguerite through water that looked, to Ansel's untrained eye, exactly like the water they'd avoided.

"How do you know?" he asked Wren at midday, standing in the wheelhouse doorway. "How do you know which ripple is a sandbar and which is just wind?"

"Wind ripples move with the gusts," Wren said. "Bars hold still. And bars make the water above them go glassy-smooth right before it goes shallow — the current gets shy." A pause. "My grandmother taught me. She ran mail on this stretch before the survey charts existed."

"You trust memory over charts?"

"I trust memory over charts that were made in a different year's water." Wren adjusted the wheel a degree. "Charts tell you where the river was. Memory tells you how it thinks."

Ansel wrote that down later, in the margin of his notebook, because it seemed like the kind of sentence that would matter someday, though he couldn't have said why.

The launch held its distance all day, patient as a debt. Toward evening it closed to within a kilometer, and through the binoculars Ansel could see that one of the figures on the flybridge was gone, and the other stood very still, facing them, and he understood without wanting to that it was Voss herself, watching the back of the boat that had taken everything from her, or saved everything from her, depending on which ledger you kept.

Bex brought food around at dusk — rice and dried fish, eaten standing up, because nobody wanted to sit. Nobody talked much either. The silence had a shape to it, and the shape was the sky, which had gone the color of a bruise healing wrong, green-black in the west, stacked with cloud that didn't drift so much as accumulate, like something deciding.

Priya touched Ansel's elbow. "If she catches us," she said, low, "what happens to us? Contractually."

"Nothing good," Ansel said. "But she won't catch us."

"You sound sure."

"He sounds sure," Tam said, appearing with a coil of line over one shoulder, "because he watched the captain run the Serrano narrows at night in fog last spring, which I personally would not attempt with a map, a prayer, and a death wish." Tam grinned, but it was a thin grin. "Also because the launch draws a meter and a half and the narrows draw less than that in places only one person alive knows."

"And after the narrows?" Priya persisted. "Then what?"

Tam shrugged. "Then we're ahead of the rain, and being ahead of the rain is the whole job."

* * *

The radio woke them at 3 a.m., and this time it was not Voss.

It was static first — a long, tearing wall of it — and then a voice under the static, and Ansel was on his feet before he recognized it, because the voice was Mercer's, and Mercer was lying ashore with a broken collarbone and a crushed hand under a collapsed crane.

But no. He listened harder, heart hammering. It wasn't Mercer. It was the launch's operator, relaying. Reading, in a flat official cadence, a message composed by someone else.

*"Marguerite, relay from Halcyon actual. Storm track confirmed shifted northwest. Estimated landfall at headwaters: four days. Recommend immediate surrender of vessel at next navigable anchorage. Rescue assets cannot reach you past kilometer marker sixty once landfall begins. Any persons aboard your vessel are thereafter beyond recovery range. This is a safety notice. Acknowledge."*

Four days. Ansel did the arithmetic with cold hands. Four days to the delta settlement, and the delta was still two full running days away, and the channels between here and there were filling even as the message crackled through the speaker.

Odile appeared at the wheelhouse door, hair wild, grease to the elbows. "She's not wrong about the window," Odile said. "She's wrong about everything else, but she's not wrong about the window."

"We're not stopping," Tam said.

"Nobody said stopping." Odile looked at Wren. "I'm saying we run the narrows tonight, in the dark, ahead of the worst of the rise, or we don't run them at all. Once the flood comes down the gorge, that whole section turns into a washing machine. Tonight it's just fast."

Wren was quiet for a long moment. The wheel hummed faintly under their hands. Ahead, the river bent toward a line of black hills, and between the hills, invisible in the dark, lay the gap that every chart marked with a single dismissive word: *rapids.*

"Everyone awake?" Wren said, not loudly.

By the time the words finished, everyone was.

* * *

Before the narrows, Wren answered the radio.

Ansel stood in the wheelhouse and watched it happen, and understood, watching, why Wren had waited. Because now the plea and the threat had both been spent. Now Voss had nothing left but the truth, which was that she was stranded upstream with a broken security chief, a scattered crew, and a rising river, and the person she needed mercy from was the person she had held at gunpoint forty hours ago.

Wren keyed the mic. "Halcyon actual, Marguerite. Copy your safety notice."

Static. Then, faintly, breathlessly: *"Galloway."*

"The scientists are safe. All of them. Fed, sheltered, and free to sign or not sign whatever they want when we reach the delta. That's the last report you get from this boat." Wren paused. Ansel realized he was holding his breath. "Imara. There's a fuel cache at the old logging camp, left bank, three bends below the terraces. Odile can raise it on frequency six if your operator's worth anything. It'll run your launch generator through the storm. After that, I can't help you."

Silence on the channel. Long enough that Ansel thought the transmission had died.

Then Voss's voice, and it was small in a way Ansel had never heard it, stripped of every pin and label: *"Why?"*

And Wren said: "Because the river decides, not me. It decided to give you a cache. What it decides after that is between you and it." Wren set down the mic. "Marguerite out."

Nobody in the wheelhouse said anything for a moment. Then Bex, who had materialized from somewhere with a lantern, snorted softly. "Merciful and mean as a whip," Bex said. "You'd have made a terrible saint and a fine grandparent."

"Both true," Wren said, and put the wheel over.

* * *

The narrows came at them out of the dark like a held breath finally released.

Ansel would spend years failing to describe it afterward. He had the notes — he always had the notes — bearing, current speed estimated at nine knots, gradient drop visible as a roar rather than a sight. But the notes read like a stranger's account of someone else's night. What he actually remembered was sound: the river doubling its voice inside the gorge walls, bouncing off stone he couldn't see, so that the noise seemed to come from everywhere and nowhere, from inside the boat, from inside his own chest. What he remembered was Wren's hands on the wheel, moving constantly, precisely, unhurried in a way that could not possibly have been calm and therefore had to be something better than calm — fluency. What he remembered was Odile at the engine, feeding it exactly as much as it could take, and Tam on the bow with the lantern hooded to a slit, calling depths in a voice pitched to carry and not to frighten:

"Green water! Green — hold — *rock, port side, close!*"

The Marguerite leaned. The whole world leaned. Ansel grabbed the rail and thought, absurdly, of the archive — of the sealed cavern behind them, the ten thousand years of a civilization's careful records sitting dry in the dark while the water rose over their doorstep, keeping them the way the river kept everything, which was neither cruelly nor kindly, but completely. He had promised Mama Ilo's people nothing and everything, and somewhere behind him in the black water the promise was keeping itself.

"Sandbar, mid-channel!" Tam's voice cracked like a whip. "It wasn't there last spring!"

"It's there now," Wren said, and took them left, impossibly left, through a slot between the bar and the gorge wall that Ansel's eyes told him was narrower than the boat, and his eyes were wrong, because the boat fit, because the river let them, because Wren had asked it in the only language it respected.

Then the gorge opened. The roar fell away behind them like a door closing. Stars reappeared overhead, hard and cold between the racing clouds, and the Marguerite settled onto long swells and ran.

Ansel got the binoculars up and swept the darkness astern. For a long minute there was nothing. Then, far back, at the mouth of the narrows, a white running light appeared, hovered, wavered — and turned away. Slowly, deliberately, the way a chess player tips over their own king.

"They're not following," he said.

"They can't," Wren said. "There's a channel through there I've run twice in my life, and I'm not sure I could find it again tomorrow. Nobody charters their way through that." A pause, and then, quieter, almost to themselves: "She'll make the cache. She's too stubborn not to. The river will decide the rest."

Ansel lowered the binoculars. Around him the deck was full of sleeping shapes — scientists, elders' kin, Tam's hammock swinging empty because Tam was already aloft again, watching the sky. Vindication, he thought, was supposed to taste like something. This tasted mostly like chicory and river spray and the strange grief of having been right about a person.

Ahead, far downstream, the clouds had begun to organize themselves into something with intent.

"Captain," Odile called from the engine hatch, her voice carrying over the water. "Barometer's falling faster than the tables say it should. Whatever's coming — it's coming early."

Wren nodded once and opened the throttle, and the Marguerite ran on into the dark, ahead of the rain, carrying everyone it could hold.
