## 20. Landfall

Bex had cooked through hurricanes before, but never one that arrived while the whole world was still wet from the last rain.

The Marguerite came into the delta settlement at gray noon, running ahead of a sky the color of a bruise going bad. Bex stood in the galley doorway with a dish towel over one shoulder and watched the river do something she'd never seen it do: run backward. The tide was coming up to meet the flood coming down, and where they met — out past the last sandbar, out where the water went from brown to the color of old pewter — the surface stood up in long ridges, like the spine of some animal deciding whether to wake.

"Get below," Wren said, passing her on the way to the wheelhouse. Their voice was hoarse. They'd been at the wheel for thirty hours, threading channels that had drowned since the last survey charts were drawn, and they looked like a person who'd been wrung out and hung back up without drying.

"I'm cooking," Bex said. "Forty people on this boat and half of them haven't eaten since yesterday."

Wren looked at her — really looked, the way they did maybe twice a year — and then nodded and kept moving. That was the thing about Wren Galloway that Bex had figured out somewhere around year three: they didn't argue with you when you were right. They just filed it away like a sounding mark on a chart, and if you were wrong, they'd come back for it later, gentle as anything.

She went back into the galley and put her hands to work. Rice first — the big pot, the one she'd bought in Port Calloway with her own money because the boat's original pot had a bottom like a sieve. Beans soaking since dawn. The smoked fish Tam had traded for at the last settlement before the narrows, cut into slivers to make it go far. Forty people. She'd fed twenty-two once, during the flood two seasons back, and thought that was the ceiling of her career. Now she was doing forty with a monsoon on the doorstep, and the strange part — the part she'd never say out loud to anyone but the galley cat, who wasn't real but lived in her head anyway — was that she wasn't scared of the feeding. She was scared of what came after. Of all those people standing on the levee watching the storm come, and nothing left to do with her hands.

Cooking was how Bex prayed. Nobody had ever told her that was allowed, so she'd just started doing it, and no god had complained yet.

* * *

They lashed the Marguerite at the settlement levee with everything the elders had and everything the boat carried: hawsers doubled and tripled, sprung to pilings that Mama Ilo said had been set by her grandmother's grandmother, driven deep into clay that hadn't moved in living memory. Ansel Reyes worked alongside the settlement men without being asked, his shirt off despite the wind, tying knots Bex didn't recognize and some she suspected he'd invented on the spot. Tam ran the winch. Odile stood in the engine room with the hatch open, shouting numbers up through the deck plates, keeping the motor warm in case they needed to fight the mooring in the worst of it.

The scientists came ashore in ones and twos, hollow-eyed, carrying each other. The wounded one — Dr. Ferro, whose arm Mercer had broken back at the camp — was carried down the gangway on a stretcher made from a cargo pallet, and the settlement women took him straight to the big house on stilts at the center of the village, where the floor was woven reed and the walls were hung with river-grass bundles that smelled like camphor and rain.

And then there was nothing left to load, nothing left to tie, nothing left to carry, and the sky opened.

It didn't start as rain. It started as sound — a long low roar from upstream, from the direction of the headwaters, from the direction of everything they'd just run away from — and Mama Ilo, standing on the levee with her shawl whipping around her, turned toward it and said something in the old language, quiet, almost conversational, the way you'd greet a relative getting off a boat.

Then the rain came, and it came sideways, and the world went white.

* * *

They crowded into the big house. All of them — the crew, the scientists, the elders, the children, the dogs, and Bex brought the galley cat too, which was to say she carried an empty basket and let people believe what they wanted. The house shook. Not violently, not yet, but continuously, a deep tremor like the building itself was humming, and the reed walls flexed in and out with the gusts, breathing.

Bex found herself next to Mama Ilo on the long bench by the fire pit, and the old woman pressed a bowl into her hands — some kind of porridge, dark grain, sweetened with something Bex couldn't name.

"You feed people," Mama Ilo said. It wasn't a question. Her voice was low enough that Bex had to lean in under the roar of the roof to hear it. "All day, since you came. Before that too, I think. On the water."

"Somebody has to."

"No." Mama Ilo shook her head slowly. "Somebody has to *want* to. There is a difference. My mother taught me the difference. She said the river has two kinds of people on it — the ones counting what it costs them, and the ones counting what it feeds. You are the second kind."

Bex looked down at the porridge, embarrassed, pleased, furious at herself for being pleased. "I'm just the cook."

Mama Ilo laughed then — a real laugh, cracked and warm, nearly lost under the storm. "Child. When the water takes the boats and the bridges and the roads, who is left? The cook. The cook is always left. The cook is the one who knows how much is enough." She patted Bex's knee with a hand like driftwood. "That is not a small knowledge. That is the oldest knowledge we have."

Across the fire pit, Wren sat with their back against a post, arms loose, eyes half-closed, listening to the house sing. And it did sing — the whole structure, the tied-down world, the hawsers outside groaning in rhythms, the reed walls, the pilings, all of it finding a note and holding it. Odile had said earlier, before the worst hit, that the Marguerite was singing too, her mooring lines tuned like instrument strings, and Bex had thought the engineer had finally cracked. But now, inside the elder-house, she could hear it plainly: the storm had a key, and everything built by patient hands was playing along with it.

Tam was asleep sitting up, mid-sentence, a plate still balanced on one knee. One of the settlement children had draped a blanket over him with tremendous ceremony. Ansel sat writing — of course he was writing, the man wrote like other people breathed — hunched over a oilcloth-wrapped ledger, shielding the pages with his body every time the door flap lifted. Bex caught a glimpse of names. A list of names, written small and careful, and beside them, in a different ink, things that looked like amounts, dates, routes. She looked away. Some cargo you don't read the manifest of.

The night went long. Somewhere past midnight the wind found its true voice and stopped pretending to be weather and became instead a single enormous fact, and the house held, and the levee held, and the Marguerite out on the black water held, and Bex learned that endurance sounds exactly like everyone you love breathing in the same room.

* * *

Morning came gray and dripping and impossibly calm, the way it does after the sky has spent itself. The delta was rearranged — new channels where fields had been, a floating island of torn-up bank drifting past the settlement with a strangler fig still growing on it, upright, unbothered, like a lady riding a barge.

Wren walked the levee with Mama Ilo, checking the hawsers. Bex followed with a thermos of coffee, because someone had to, and heard the tail end of their talk.

"—upstream," Mama Ilo was saying. "The water will decide. It always decides. Whatever she took with her, whatever she tried to take." She gestured at the flooded plain, at the drowned terraces lying somewhere under all that churning brown, hidden again, sealed. "The river keeps its own accounts. We only keep ours."

Wren said nothing. They were looking upstream with an expression Bex couldn't read — not worry exactly, and not relief either. Something between. Like a captain reading water that hasn't decided yet what it's going to be.

Ansel came up the levee path at a jog, oilcloth bundle under his arm, face pale under the windburn.

"It's done," he said. "Sealed. The backers' names, the transfer schedules, the extraction orders, Mercer's standing instructions — everything. Addressed to the inquiry commission at Port Calloway, registered courier, goes out on the first boat after the water drops." He exhaled, long and shaky, like a man setting down something heavier than paper. "Once it's sent, it can't be unsent."

"And Voss?" Tam asked, coming up behind them, hair still flat from sleep.

Nobody answered. The river answered instead, or seemed to — a long slow pulse of debris coming down from the headwaters, branches and reed mats and one overturned crate, stamped with a logo Bex recognized from the Survey camp, spinning slowly in the current and gone.

"Unknown," Wren said finally. "Upstream stays unknown until the water lets us know it."

Bex handed out coffee. Four cups, steam whipping away sideways. Below on the levee, the settlement was already moving — elders pulling storm-felled brush, children hunting stranded fish in the new pools, women re-thatching the big house with the efficiency of people who had done this a hundred times and would do it a hundred more. The scientists were among them, awkward at first, then less awkward, then not awkward at all. Dr. Ferro sat in the sun with his splinted arm and a child showing him, very seriously, how to braid fishing line.

Odile emerged from below decks, wiped her hands on a rag, and looked at the sky, then the water, then the boat.

"She held," Odile said. Meaning the Marguerite. Meaning, possibly, everything else.

"She held," Wren agreed.

Bex looked at them all — her strange, stubborn, uncharted family, salt-crusted and exhausted and standing — and felt the thing she'd been afraid to feel since Port Calloway finally arrive and settle in her chest like ballast: they were going home. All of them. Together.

Then Ansel cleared his throat, and the look on his face said the river wasn't done with them yet.

"There's a boat coming downriver," he said. "Flying no colors. And it's making straight for us."
