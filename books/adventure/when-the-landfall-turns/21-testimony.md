## 21. Testimony

The hearing room in Port Calloway had once been a customs house, and it still smelled like one — tar, old paper, the ghost of tobacco that no amount of whitewash could drown. They'd put a long table up on a dais for the commissioners and a single straight-backed chair down on the floor for whoever was being asked to explain themselves. Wren had sat in worse chairs. Wren had sat in that chair twice before, once for running cargo without manifest and once for a bar fight they hadn't started, and both times the chair had felt like an accusation.

This time it felt like a door.

Three commissioners sat behind the table: a magistrate named Corven with a face like a closed ledger, a hydrographer from the River Authority who kept glancing at charts she'd already memorized, and a thin man introduced as counsel for the Calloway Shipping Consortium, which was how Voss's backers had chosen to appear — not as names, but as a shape wearing a suit.

Wren wore their good coat. Bex had brushed the salt out of it the night before and said nothing about the mended tear at the cuff, which was its own kind of kindness.

"Captain Galloway." Magistrate Corven didn't look up from his papers. "You'll state your name and vessel for the record."

"Wren Galloway. Master of the river steamer *Marguerite*, licensed for the Serrano run, eleven years."

"You were contracted by Dr. Imara Voss of the Halcyon Survey on the fourteenth of the dry season."

"I was."

"For what purpose?"

"Resupply and retrieval of her survey team, silent three weeks at the flooded headwaters."

Corven turned a page. The sound carried in the quiet room like a stone skipped across still water. "And did you complete this contract?"

"No, sir."

The counsel for the Consortium leaned forward slightly, the way a fisherman leans when something touches the line.

* * *

They had rehearsed none of it. That had been Ansel's idea, and Odile's, and finally Wren's own, arrived at separately on the third night after landfall, sitting on the *Marguerite*'s roof while the monsoon hammered the delta and the whole settlement of Mama Ilo's people sang inside against the storm. The truth had a shape, and the shape had edges, and some of those edges belonged to other people. To say everything would be to spend truths that weren't theirs to spend.

So the plan was simple: tell the truth, all of the truth that was lawfully theirs, and not one grain more.

"The contract specified resupply and return," Corven said. "What happened instead?"

"The Survey camp was intact but fortified. Dr. Voss's security chief, a Mr. Mercer, had armed men. Half the scientific staff had been sent away or had left. Those remaining were exhausted and frightened. I judged the situation unsafe for my crew and said so."

"You judged. On whose authority?"

"My own. It's my boat and my river."

A murmur went through the gallery. There was a gallery; Wren hadn't expected one. Tam sat in the second row between Bex and Odile, and behind them sat a dozen faces Wren knew from the delta settlements, and two elders Wren recognized from the boats that had come upstream drawn by dreams. Mama Ilo was not there. She had said the hearing room was a place where words got weighed, and she preferred places where they got lived. But she had sent word through Tam: *Tell it plain. Plain is stronger than clever.*

"Describe the events of the day the vessel left the survey site," Corven said.

Wren told it plain. The loading forced at gunpoint. The crane collapse — they gave Odile's finding honestly, that the rigging had failed under load beyond its rating, and let the commissioners draw whatever conclusions they liked about why the loads had doubled overnight. The standoff on deck. The moment Voss had ordered the mooring lines held and Wren had cut them anyway.

"You severed your own vessel's lines," the Consortium counsel said. His voice was smooth as silt. "While under charter. While your contract stipulated obedience to the chartering party's direction."

"I did."

"That is breach of contract."

"It is."

"You understand that the Consortium intends to pursue damages. Substantial ones."

"I expect so."

He smiled thinly, sensing soft ground. "Then perhaps you can help us recover our property. Our client's expedition equipment remains at the site. Its location—"

"I couldn't say where it is now."

"You were there six weeks ago."

"Flood season came early," Wren said. "The channels have moved since Tuesday. Anything I told you about that stretch of river would be wrong by the time you wrote it down."

The hydrographer almost smiled. She was the only one in the room who understood that this was not evasion. It was geography.

* * *

They broke at midday. In the corridor outside, Tam fell into step beside Wren, hands jammed in pockets, trying to look casual and failing the way Tam always failed at casual.

"They're going to try the money angle this afternoon," Tam said. "I heard the counsel talking to his clerk. They want to offer you a settlement. Take their damages off the table if you'll sign a statement confirming the site's coordinates for their recovery operation."

"Recovery operation."

"That's what he called it."

Wren looked out the corridor's high window. Port Calloway lay below in its dry-season colors, barges stacked at the wharves, the river running brown and patient toward the sea. Somewhere up that river, under forty feet of monsoon water, a civilization's memory sat sealed in the dark, exactly where Mama Ilo's people had always known it to be, exactly where it had waited out ten thousand years of floods and empires and men with clipboards.

"They think everything has a price because everything they've ever wanted has been for sale," Wren said.

"So what do we do?"

"What we planned. Nothing different."

Tam nodded slowly. "Mama Ilo says you're learning."

"Mama Ilo says a lot of things. Half of them are tests."

"Which half is that one?"

Wren grinned despite the knot in their chest. "The half that's true."

* * *

The afternoon session opened with the Consortium counsel standing, adjusting his cuffs, and making his move.

"Magistrate, before further examination of the captain, I'd like to present a proposal. The Consortium is prepared to withdraw its claim for contractual damages — entirely — contingent on one condition. Captain Galloway provides this commission, and our client, with accurate navigational data for the upper Serrano, permitting recovery of abandoned expedition property before the next dry season closes the window."

Corven looked up for the first time. "That's not a withdrawal of claim. That's a purchase."

"It's a resolution, magistrate. Efficient ones save everyone money."

"And if the captain declines?"

"Then the damages proceed, the captain's license comes under review given the admitted breach, and the property sits unclaimed until someone less scrupulous recovers it. I'd think the captain would prefer the river's contents end up in a museum rather than a scrapyard."

The room went very quiet. Wren felt every eye settle on them like weather.

They thought about Voss, then. Not with hate — hate was heavy cargo and the *Marguerite* ran best light — but with a kind of clinical curiosity. Voss had believed the same thing this man believed: that anything underwater was unowned, and anything unowned was available, and anything available was already hers. The whole arithmetic of it. Mercer had called it *the ledger*. Everything entered, everything extracted, nothing left to simply be.

"May I answer that?" Wren said.

"You may," said Corven.

"You're right that the property will sit unclaimed. You're right that someone could go find it, eventually, if the river lets them. Here's what I know that your client's surveys apparently didn't." Wren stood, because some things deserved a level voice and both feet on the floor. "That stretch of the Serrano drowned a village in my lifetime. It drowned a ferry landing the year before that. It moves its channels faster than any recovery operation could chart them, and it does it on purpose, far as I'm concerned. Every person who lives on that river above the narrows — and there are more of them than your client's maps show — knows what's up there and has known for longer than the Consortium has existed. If they wanted it recovered, it would be recovered. It isn't. Draw your own conclusion about whether it wants to be found."

"You're speaking for the river communities?" the counsel said. "On what authority?"

"None. That's the point. They don't need me to speak. They just need people like you to stop talking long enough to hear them."

Corven's pen scratched. The hydrographer wrote something and underlined it twice.

* * *

Ansel testified the next morning.

Wren watched from the gallery as the geologist walked to the chair with his report under his arm — the sealed report, the one that named the backers' representatives, documented the falsified radio logs Tam had caught, recorded Mercer's orders as Bex had found them in the expedition strongbox, and described the extraction-and-burn directive in language so precise it read like an autopsy. Ansel had spent three weeks on it. He'd written it the way he did everything, carefully, completely, without flourish, and then he'd put the backers' names in an appendix and locked the appendix under seal pending the inquiry's disposition.

He answered every question. He declined several others.

"Dr. Reyes," the counsel said, "your report references artifacts of significant cultural and commercial value observed at the site. Yet it specifies no location. As the survey's lead scientist, surely you recorded positions."

"I recorded positions relative to landmarks that no longer exist. The flood reconfigured the entire terrace system. My positional data is void."

"You must have photographs."

"Water-damaged. Unusable."

"You must have notes."

"Sealed with the rest of my field materials, per the commission's own protective order." Ansel's voice never rose. He had the patience of a man who had spent years waiting for rock to tell him things. "I'll note for the record that the protective order was requested by me, in writing, the week after landfall, on grounds that the site may contain culturally sensitive material belonging to living descendant communities. The order was granted. It's binding. Your client's recovery ambitions are, respectfully, illegal under it."

The counsel turned a page he had clearly already read. "The Consortium disputes the validity of that order. The descendant communities you cite have no registered land claims."

"They don't believe in registering," Ansel said. "They believe in remembering. I've found the second system holds up better under flood conditions."

Someone in the gallery laughed — one of the delta elders, a dry sound like wind through reeds — and Corven let it stand.

* * *

It took nine days. Nine days of the chair, of the corridor, of Tam bringing news from the wharves each evening, of Odile tearing down and rebuilding the *Marguerite*'s engine in a rented slip because waiting idle made her itch, of Bex cooking for half the delta's legal witnesses in a boarding house kitchen until the landlady declared them family and stopped charging.

On the ninth day, Corven read the disposition aloud.

The seizure of the *Marguerite* was ruled unlawful detention of a chartered vessel; Wren's cutting of the lines was ruled justified self-recovery, the breach-of-contract damages withdrawn by the commission itself, over the Consortium counsel's visible displeasure. Mercer's conduct was referred to the maritime authority for prosecution. Dr. Voss, unaccounted for since the flood, was listed as missing, status open. And the protective order over the upper Serrano terraces was confirmed, extended, and — at the hydrographer's insistence — expanded to include the entire reach above the narrows, on the grounds that "navigational instability renders the area unsuitable for commercial recovery operations indefinitely."

Indefinitely. Wren wrote the word down in the logbook that night and liked the way it looked.

The Consortium counsel shook Wren's hand on the courthouse steps, because men like him always did, sealing the transaction even in defeat. "You understand this doesn't end it," he said quietly. "My client is patient. Rivers change."

"Rivers change," Wren agreed. "That's rather the point of them."

* * *

That evening the crew gathered on the *Marguerite*'s deck with the engine hatch open and the first cold beers of the new season sweating in Bex's good bucket. Downriver the sun was going out in bands of copper, and the water ran high and clean, carrying the last of the monsoon seaward.

"To indefinite," Odile said, raising her bottle.

"To the hydrographer," said Tam. "Whoever she is. I owe her a pie."

"To Ansel," Bex said, "who lied beautifully. Water damage. I nearly believed it myself."

"He didn't lie," Wren said. "There wasn't a single false word in that room. He just told the truth sideways, the way the river does."

Ansel himself appeared at the gangway then, duffel over his shoulder, a paper contract folded in his jacket pocket — signed that afternoon, witnessed by Corven's own clerk. Deckhand and expedition naturalist, seasonal, wages negotiable, duties including "whatever the captain requires and the river permits."

"Room aboard?" he asked.

"We'll make one," Wren said. "Bex, he eats a lot. Scientists forget meals and then eat like dredges."

"Noted," Bex said. "He bunks amidships. Away from my galley and away from Tam's snoring."

"My snoring is legendary," Tam said proudly.

They drank. The *Marguerite* rocked gently at her moorings, and Wren rested a palm on the rail and felt the current moving underneath, steady, unhurried, going somewhere it had decided long ago.

Upstream, under dark water, the archive slept on, and would sleep on past all of their lifetimes, past the Consortium's patience, past the memory of anyone now living — except the people who remembered without needing to write it down, and the river, which forgot nothing.

Wren looked at the crew laughing in the last light and understood, finally, what ten years of miles had been for. Not distance. Approach.

Spring season opened in six weeks. There was cargo to book, a hull to caulk, and a river to run — home, this time, both directions.
