## 22. Miles, Reconsidered

The ice went out of the Serrano on a Tuesday, and by Thursday the Marguerite was taking on cargo again.

Wren felt the change in the river before they saw it — a different note in the water against the hull, a loosening, like a held breath finally let go. Ten springs they'd watched this same thaw from this same wheelhouse, and every year it had meant the same thing: miles. Miles stacked up ahead like cordwood, waiting to be burned through. Miles as a way of not arriving anywhere.

This spring was different. This spring, Wren stood at the wheel with coffee going cold in one hand and listened to the wheelhouse full of voices that hadn't been there last year.

"Port side clear," Tam called from the rail below, where they were coiling lines with the easy economy of someone who'd stopped counting the seasons until they could leave. "Buoy's moved again — maybe eight meters east."

"I see it." Wren eased the Marguerite past the displaced marker. The flood had rearranged half the lower channel, as floods did, and most pilots would spend a week relearning it with soundings and curses. Wren had spent one evening with a chart and a pencil, and then another evening with Mama Ilo's eldest grandson, Kofi, who drew the new channels from memory because his people had been reading this water since before there were charts.

"Sandbar off Miller's Point is gone entirely," Odile said from the engine room hatch, wiping her hands on a rag that had seen better decades. "Whole thing. River ate it."

"River's allowed," Wren said.

Odile snorted. "That's what you said about my spare fuel pump, and look how that turned out."

* * *

They'd taken on Ansel at Port Calloway three days ago, and he still hadn't found his sea legs, which was a source of quiet delight to Bex, who served him breakfast each morning with the solemnity of a woman administering medicine.

"You'll get them," she told him now, setting down a plate of eggs and fried bread on the chart table. "Everyone gets them eventually. Except Odile. Odile was born with them."

"I was born with an engine room," Odile corrected from the doorway. "Legs came later."

Ansel smiled the careful smile of a man still learning how much weight his jokes could carry. He'd come back thinner than he'd left, and quieter, and with a sealed report in a waterproof case that he kept in the cabin under his bunk like other men kept letters from home. The inquiry had gone the way inquiries go — long, procedural, punctuated by lawyers' coughs. Wren had testified for two hours: the seizure of the boat, the guns on the deck, the storm coming down like a closing fist. They had asked, more than once, what Voss's expedition had actually found up there.

"Water," Wren had said. "Old water. Flooded country."

It wasn't even a lie. It just wasn't all of it. Some truths belonged to the water, and Wren had learned which ones those were the hard way, at gunpoint, in the rain.

The backers had withdrawn their claims quietly, the way money does everything. Mercer's name had surfaced in the report enough times to end his career in anything legitimate. Voss herself — no one knew. Upstream, past the narrows, past the drowned terraces, into country the monsoon had closed like a book. Some nights Wren thought about her out there, and felt nothing so clean as anger. Mostly it was a kind of tired pity, the feeling of watching someone measure a river in cubic meters of salvage and never once put a hand in the current.

* * *

Kofi came up to the wheelhouse mid-morning with his sister Ama, both of them wearing the serious expressions of children granted an important errand. Mama Ilo sent them downriver twice a season now, ostensibly to trade dried fish and river-salt, but everyone understood the fish were an excuse. The grandchildren liked the wheelhouse. They liked the brass instruments and the big wheel and, most of all, they liked Wren, whom they addressed with a formality that cracked around the edges whenever Bex produced food.

"Captain Galloway." Kofi saluted, roughly. "Grandmother says the third channel past the Split will be open by Friday. She says you should take it instead of the eastern run."

"She says that, does she."

"She says the eastern channel will silt in after the next rain." Ama nodded vigorously. "She says the river told her."

Wren looked at the two of them — ten and twelve, maybe, standing in the wheelhouse of a boat their ancestors had probably paddled past when it was forest — and felt the familiar turn in the chest that had started sometime during the monsoon and never quite let go. Belonging, it turned out, wasn't a thing you decided. It was a thing that got extended to you, tentatively, like a line across dark water, and you either took hold or you didn't.

"Tell your grandmother I'll take the third channel," Wren said. "And tell her the Marguerite owes her a run of freight, free, whenever she wants it."

"She knows," Ama said. "She says that's why she tells you things."

* * *

They made the Split by late afternoon, and Wren took the third channel without hesitation, threading water that would have required an hour of soundings a year ago. That was the difference, Wren thought, standing easy at the wheel while Tam lounged against the rail and Ansel pretended not to be seasick. A year ago the knowledge had all lived in one skull, hoarded like cargo, and the skull had been lonely as a lighthouse. Now the knowledge was shared out — Kofi's channels, Odile's engine, Tam's weather eye, Bex's galley arithmetic, Ansel's patient way of making sense of anything broken. The Marguerite ran on more than diesel these days.

The miles still counted. Wren still counted them — some habits were load-bearing. But somewhere between the narrows and the delta, between cutting a boat loose from a madwoman and lashing her down through a hundred-year storm, the miles had changed direction. They used to lead away. Every departure had been a small escape, every horizon a door closing softly behind.

Now the miles led somewhere. There was a settlement that expected them. There were grandchildren who waved from the bank. There was a sealed case under a bunk protecting a truth that a whole valley of people were quietly, communally keeping, and there was a river that remembered everything and forgave most of it.

"Captain?" Ansel, at the rail, pointing upstream. "Smoke. Thin. Right at the tree line above the Split."

Wren raised the glasses. One thread of smoke, pale, deliberate — not a wildfire's column but a signal, the kind river people made. And beneath it, just visible in the long light, a canoe putting out from the bank, moving toward them fast, paddled by someone who clearly knew exactly what the current would do for them.

Tam appeared at the wheelhouse door. "Trouble?"

"Mama Ilo doesn't send smoke for trouble," Wren said slowly. "She sends runners for trouble. Smoke means—" It meant a gathering. It meant something worth telling face to face. It meant, if Wren read it right, that the river had given something back, or wanted something given.

The canoe swung alongside, and the paddler stood, balancing like it was nothing, and called across the water in a voice pitched to carry:

"Captain Galloway! Grandmother says — the water dropped early this year. Past the terraces." A grin, wide and unafraid. "She says the stones are showing. All of them. And she says you should come see what the river kept safe."

Wren lowered the glasses. Looked at the crew, already gathering at the rail without being called. Looked at the open channel ahead, and the miles beyond it, leading — for once — exactly home.

"Odile," Wren said. "How's she run?"

The old engineer grinned. "Like she knows the way."
