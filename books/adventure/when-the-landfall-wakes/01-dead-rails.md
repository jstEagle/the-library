## 1. Dead Rails

The rain came in sideways off the gorge, half of it turning to ice before it landed, and Lena Marchetti stood in the middle of it with her boot on a rail that used to go somewhere and didn't anymore.

"Tell me again," she said.

Odell crouched beside the washout, rain hammering the shoulders of his slicker. He was sixty-one years old and built like something the railroad had retired but never quite gotten rid of, all knuckle and gristle, and he read ground the way other men read timetables. "Culvert let go sometime in the night," he said. "Water took the ballast out from under about forty feet of track. Rail's still hanging, mostly. Tie's gone. Subgrade's soup." He spat into the void where the mountain had been. "Train comes through here at six tomorrow morning carrying forty cars of timber, this is a very short story."

Lena looked at the washout. Forty feet of nothing, dropping eight feet down to a creek that was running brown and fast with everything it had stolen. Above it, the rail hung in the air like a sentence somebody had stopped writing halfway through.

"Options," she said.

"Rebuild the culvert, we're here four days and the railroad fines us for every hour past Thursday. Reroute around the slide slope, we're here two days if the weather holds, which it won't." Odell wiped his face with a gloved hand, which accomplished nothing. "Or we do what you did on the Kestrel Spur in March."

She'd been afraid he was going to say that.

The Kestrel Spur job had taken eleven hours and cost her a week of sleep. It had also been the reason the Cascade Northern's dispatch office now called them first when something broke, which was the only reason they were standing in this particular freezing rain instead of some other freezing rain, and the only reason there would be money in the account Friday if—and it was a large if—they fixed this tonight.

"Teo!" she shouted.

Teo Vasquez came sliding down the embankment with his hardhat crooked and his grin already going, because Teo was twenty-three and hadn't yet learned that enthusiasm was a thing you paid interest on. "Boss."

"You ever run a shoofly?"

"A what?"

"He means a temporary detour," Odell said.

"I know what he means," Teo said, wounded. "I was testing him."

"He means no," Odell said.

"He means yes," Lena said. "He means you're about to learn it in the worst classroom in the world. Get the crew up here. All of them. And get the tarps off the flatbed—we're going to need dry ties or we're going to be setting wet ones all night and watching them float away."

Teo went back up the bank three times faster than he'd come down, shouting names into the rain. Lena heard Priya answer from somewhere near the trucks, and then Marta's voice, lower, and then the diesel cough of the generator catching, and under all of it the creek running loud with its stolen cargo.

She turned back to the gap.

Forty feet. In ordinary weather, ordinary conditions, with a full crew and a full day, a shoofly around a washout like this was a two-day job: cut a new alignment thirty yards upslope, lay two hundred feet of new track on fresh ballast, curve it back onto the mainline, do it all while the railroad's timetable sat on your chest like a stone. In freezing rain, at night, with five people and whatever the flatbed happened to be carrying?

It wasn't possible. She knew that. She'd been doing this work since she was nineteen years old and she knew exactly what was possible, the way a swimmer knows where the bottom is.

But the account was down to four hundred dollars and change. The insurance on the truck was two months behind. Priya's daughter needed a dentist, and not the kind of dentist you put off. And the Cascade Northern's contract had a clause—she'd read it twice, hating every word—that paid a bonus for restoration inside twenty-four hours and stopped paying anything at all past seventy-two.

"We're doing the Kestrel," she said.

Odell looked at her for a long moment. Rain ran off the brim of his hat between them like a curtain. "You sure?"

"No," Lena said. "Let's go."

* * *

They worked by generator light and headlamp, and the rain kept coming, and by midnight the world had shrunk to a circle of mud and steel maybe sixty feet across.

Marta ran the excavator, carving the new bench into the slide slope with a delicacy that always surprised people who hadn't seen her eat. Priya set grade stakes by lamplight, calling corrections over the wind in a voice that never rose and was somehow always the loudest thing on the site. Teo carried things—ties, plates, spikes, the endless heavy vocabulary of track work—from the flatbed to wherever Marta pointed, and learned, as the hours went on, to stop asking why and start watching where.

And Odell laid rail.

That was the thing nobody outside the crew understood, the thing Lena had stopped trying to explain to clients years ago. Track work looked like brute labor from a distance, mud and iron and men swinging mauls. Up close it was closer to surgery. Every tie had to sit at exact depth. Every rail joint had to close within a sixteenth of an inch or the whole string would hunt and wander until it found a way to fail. You couldn't rush it and you couldn't hesitate, and the only people who could do it fast were the people who'd done it so long their hands had stopped needing to be asked.

Odell had been laying rail for forty-two years. His hands didn't ask.

Lena worked the gauge bar beside him, checking his joints, and neither of them spoke much, because they'd been doing this together long enough that speech had become mostly decorative.

At 2 a.m., the temperature dropped through freezing for real, and the rain started glazing everything it touched. The mud went from soup to concrete. The tarps froze stiff. Teo slipped on the ballast slope and went down hard enough that Lena heard the breath leave him, and when he got up he was laughing, which worried her more than crying would have.

"How long?" Priya called from the stakes.

Lena checked her watch. Six hours until the first train. Maybe ninety feet of track left to lay, plus the curve back onto the mainline, plus surfacing, plus tamping, plus pulling the old dead rail off the washout edge so nothing caught a wheel.

"Ask me at dawn," she said.

* * *

At 4:40, with the sky still black and the rain finally thinning to sleet, they spiked the last joint.

Lena walked the whole shoofly twice behind Odell, both of them bent low, reading the new track the way you'd proofread a document written in the dark. The curve was a little tight. The superelevation was a little shy. On a dry day at freight speed it would sing. Tonight, frozen, with a loaded timber drag coming down a mountain grade—

"It'll hold," Odell said.

"I know it'll hold."

"You don't sound like you know."

"I sound like a woman who hasn't slept." She straightened up. Her back had filed a formal complaint somewhere around hour nine and she'd declined it. "Flag board at the top of the curve. Ten-mile-per-hour restriction until we come back and surface this properly in daylight. I'm not giving them a reason to write us up."

"They'll fine us for the restriction."

"They'll fine us less than they'd fine us for a derailment." She pulled the flag kit off her pack and started up the line to set the boards herself, because it was her name on the safety plan and she had never once in eleven years asked anyone to stand where she wouldn't.

The first train came through at 6:12, twelve minutes late, which everyone involved understood to be entirely the weather's fault. Lena stood well clear and watched the locomotive take the curve at walking pace, its headlight swinging across the new rail, and felt the ground carry the weight of it past her boots—the long shudder of forty loaded cars trusting, without knowing it, a piece of track that hadn't existed yesterday.

Weary pride. That was the feeling. It had a taste, kind of metallic, like blood in your mouth after a night of cold air. It was the only flavor success had ever really come in for her, and she'd made her peace with that a long time ago.

Teo whooped from the flatbed. Priya allowed herself one nod, which from Priya was a parade. Marta shut down the excavator and lit a cigarette even though she'd quit two years ago, and nobody mentioned it.

Odell came and stood next to Lena, and for a while they just watched the tail end of the train disappear down the line toward the valley.

"Payroll's covered," he said quietly.

"Barely."

"Barely counts." He took off his hat and shook the ice out of it, an old habit, pointless in this weather. "You know what I keep thinking about?"

"Don't."

"The Kestrel Spur took eleven hours. This took fourteen. We're getting slower."

"We're getting older."

"Speak for yourself." But he smiled when he said it, and then the smile faded into something else, something he'd been carrying around for a few weeks now, and Lena saw it land before he could pack it away. "There's mail in the truck. Came up with the supply run yesterday. One of them's got a letterhead on it."

"Whose letterhead?"

"That's the thing," Odell said. "I don't know the name. But I know the address." He looked at her, and his voice did the thing it almost never did, which was hesitate. "It's the old Cascade district office. The one they closed twenty years ago."

The creek ran on below them, brown and satisfied, holding everything it had taken.

Lena stood very still on the rail she'd just rebuilt, and felt the cold go deeper than the rain had managed all night.

Nobody sent letters from the Cascade district office. Nobody sent letters from above the old Cascade line at all—not to her, not to this crew, not after the oath they'd sworn over bad coffee the winter her father went to prison. No jobs above the line. Not for any money. That rule had held through drought and flood and two years when payroll meant Priya's savings, and it had held because every one of them knew exactly what was buried up there under the snowpack, and it wasn't ore.

"Read it," she said.

"I haven't opened it."

"Why not?"

"Because it's addressed to you," Odell said, "and because I've known you nineteen years, and I figured you'd want to throw it away yourself."

She took the envelope from him. Heavy paper. Expensive. A wax seal, which was either a joke or a threat. Through the cold she could feel the weight of whatever was inside—more than a letter. Pages. Maybe plans.

Above them the clouds were starting to tear open along the ridge, and for one moment the light came down the gorge and touched the high country to the north—the country above the Cascade line, white and silent and sealed, the country she had spent twenty years not looking at.

Lena put the envelope in her coat, unopened, against her heart like something contraband.

"Not here," she said. "Get the crew loaded. We're going home."

"And then?"

She looked north one more time, just once, the way you look at a grave.

"And then," she said, "I guess we find out what money thinks it can buy."
