## 2. The Man with the Permits

The rain had stopped an hour before the man arrived, but the bunkhouse yard still held its water, every rut and bootprint brimming, so that when Cassius Vane stepped out of his black SUV he seemed to walk on a field of small broken mirrors. Lena watched him from the porch with a mug of coffee going cold in her hands and thought, not unkindly, that he dressed like a man who had never once been rained on.

He was tall in the way of things built to be noticed—long charcoal coat, no mud on his shoes despite the quarter mile of gravel between the county road and their gate. Fifty, maybe. Silver at the temples, cut close. He stood a moment surveying the yard the way other men surveyed property they already owned: the rusting flatcars stacked like children's blocks, the fuel bladder under its tarp, Odell's shop with its door propped open on a wedge of scrap timber, the smell of diesel and wet creosote that lived in everything they owned.

Lena set her coffee down. She didn't go out to meet him. That was a rule older than most of her rules: let them come to you, and let them stand in your yard while they do it.

He saw her anyway. Of course he did. Men like him always found the person not coming to greet them.

"Miss Marchetti." His voice carried across the yard without effort, pitched like he'd spent years talking over machinery. "Cassius Vane."

"I know who you are." Everyone in the salvage trade knew. Vane Infrastructure Group held concessions on four hundred miles of closed mountain rail, and Vane himself had a reputation for buying dead lines cheap and reselling them dearer, or turning them into toll roads, or whatever else the permits allowed once the paper was signed. "You're a long way off your own tracks."

"Not as far as you'd think." He crossed the yard, stepping around the puddles rather than through them, which told her something too—a man unwilling to get his feet wet was a man who expected other people to do his wading. He stopped at the base of the porch steps and looked up at her with an expression of frank appraisal, cataloguing her the way she'd catalogued him. Grey-streaked braid. Hands scarred from torch work. Coveralls that had been patched past any dignity. "I'll come to the point, because I know how your time is billed."

"Nobody's billing you yet."

"Then consider this free." From inside his coat he drew a document wallet, leather, soft with use, and held it up without offering it. "Six weeks ago the state reopened the Cascade corridor for concession bids. I hold the primary permit. Engineering access, right-of-way, environmental waivers—all of it. What I don't hold is a crew willing to work it."

"There's a reason for that."

"There are several reasons," Vane said. "Most of them superstition."

"It's not superstition when the mountain actually ate people."

"The mountain eats people everywhere, Miss Marchetti. It's what mountains do. The difference is that everywhere else, someone eventually goes back in." He climbed one step, unhurried, until his eyes were nearly level with hers. Close up she could see the fine map of lines around them, the particular patience of a man accustomed to waiting out opposition. "I want the Cascade Tunnel reopened. Drained, cleared, made passable to the south portal by the first of June. My concession expires July first. If the tunnel isn't certified by then, the state voids the permit, the corridor reverts to wilderness designation, and forty years of my money goes into the ground forever."

"So hire a tunnel crew out of Kalispell."

"They quoted me eleven months and a number with two commas in it." He smiled thinly. "You quoted nine weeks to rebuild the Miller Creek spur last fall. In winter. After the washout took out two hundred yards of grade. The railroad's own engineers said it couldn't be done before spring."

"The railroad's own engineers weren't hungry."

"No," Vane agreed. "They weren't. And that, Miss Marchetti, is the entire basis of my offer." He opened the document wallet and drew out a contract, three pages, clipped, plus a single sheet beneath it that he laid carefully on the porch boards at her feet. A check. She didn't look at the figure. Looking would have been a mistake, and he knew it, which is why he put it down instead of handing it over. "That's the advance. Ten percent, cash, today. The balance on completion, with a bonus clause if you beat the deadline. Equipment costs covered separately. I've already arranged haulage rights on the county road and a fuel allocation through the co-op."

"You've done your homework."

"I always do." He glanced past her, through the window into the bunkhouse kitchen, where Odell sat at the table doing something deliberate with a ledger and not looking out at them at all, which meant he was listening to every word. "Your foreman keeps immaculate books. I respect that in a man."

"He's not my foreman because of his books."

"No. But the books tell me what I need to know." Vane folded his hands. "Here's my arithmetic, plainly stated, since I find plainness saves time. Your crew is seven people. You're carrying debt on the excavator—the yellow one, the 320, against a note from First Cascade, called due in March. You lost the Kettle Falls contract in January when the fishery board rescinded. Your insurance carrier raised your rates after the Miller Creek injury claim. And payroll for February went out late. Not very late. Late enough."

The rain-washed yard was very quiet. Somewhere down the valley a raven called, twice, and quit.

Lena kept her face still. Inside, something cold turned over. She'd known the numbers were bad. She hadn't known they were visible from outside.

"That's a lot of research to spend on a company you haven't hired yet."

"It's not research. It's diligence. I don't invest in operations I can't read." He picked the check up off the boards and held it out to her, level, arm steady. "Take it or don't. But take it tonight, because I'm seeing the Hollis brothers tomorrow morning, and they'll say yes, and then this conversation becomes their problem instead of yours."

The Hollis brothers. Lena almost laughed. The Hollises would take the advance, burn half of it in equipment failures by week two, and leave the tunnel half-dug and the corridor condemned anyway. Vane knew that. Which meant the offer wasn't really aimed at the Hollises at all—it was aimed at her pride, and it had been from the moment he stepped out of his truck.

She looked at the check. She made herself look at it. The figure sat there in neat machine print, more than the crew had seen at one time since the year they rebuilt the trestle at Alder Narrows, and behind it, unspoken, sat all the rest: the note on the excavator, Teo's wages owed from three weeks of weather delays, the fuel bill, the hospital lien Odell never talked about from his wife's last year.

"No," she said.

Vane's expression didn't change. "No to the terms, or no to the job?"

"Just no."

"May I ask why? Genuinely. I'm curious what price the Cascade line carries that beats that number." He said it without mockery, which was somehow worse.

"You couldn't afford it."

"Everything has a price."

"Not everything." Lena came down one step, so they stood eye to eye, and let him see whatever it was he thought he could read in her face. "Twenty years ago they pulled nine cars out of that tunnel and sealed both portals with concrete. There's a man buried in there they never got out—brakeman, name of Ellery Voss—and as far as I know nobody's gone past the bulkhead since. My father worked that line. Half this valley worked that line. We don't dig it up for money, Mr. Vane. Not my money, not yours."

Something moved behind Vane's eyes—not surprise exactly. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man confirming a fact he'd already filed.

"Sentiment," he said quietly. "Very well. I won't pretend to understand it, but I'll respect it, which is more than most financiers would offer." He returned the check to the wallet, unhurried, and buttoned the coat. "For the record, Miss Marchetti: the offer stands for seventy-two hours. If the Hollises disappoint me, I may be back. If they don't—" he spread his hands, "—the mountain keeps its dead, and I keep my losses. Either way, somebody pays."

He walked back across the mirror-field of puddles without breaking stride, got into the SUV, and sat for a moment with the engine running, doing something on a phone or a clipboard, she couldn't tell. Then he backed around and drove down toward the county road, taillights dissolving in the grey.

Lena stayed on the steps until the sound of him was gone entirely.

* * *

Odell came out when the dust settled, which took a while, because the dust was rain and the sky just kept making more of it. He leaned on the porch rail beside her, a big man gone soft at the edges, sixty-two years old with forearms like dock rope and a ledger still in one hand.

"Hollis brothers," he said. "He wasn't lying about that."

"I know."

"Which means either he gets them, or he comes back here. Or—" Odell turned the ledger over in his hands, not opening it, which was how Lena knew what was coming, because Odell only fidgeted with the books when the books were the bad news. "Or neither. And we make payroll Friday."

"We'll make payroll."

"Lena." He said her name the way he said it maybe twice a year, flat and low, the foreman voice, the one that meant stop working and listen. "Run it with me. Excavator note, called in March. Fuel account, thirty days past. Teo's owed three weeks. Insurance premium on the lowboy lapsed Tuesday—I paid it personal, don't argue. And the Kettle Falls money we were counting on for spring is gone, and it isn't coming back, because the fishery board doesn't rescind contracts out of kindness." He looked out at the yard, at the equipment sitting in the wet like patient animals. "We are eleven days from missing payroll. Eleven. And the man who just drove away knows it to the day, because men like that don't guess."

Lena said nothing. The raven called again, farther off.

"I was there," Odell said. "When they sealed it. Nineteen-ten hours, second of October, both portals, concrete trucks running all night. I stood on the north grade with your daddy and watched them pour, and I heard him say—we all heard him say—nobody opens that again. Not for money. Not for anything." He rubbed his jaw. "I've kept that oath twenty years, same as you. I'm not telling you to break it. I'm telling you what it costs to keep it. Because keeping it might mean losing everything else, and I needed you to hear that from me and not from a bank letter."

"And if I sign?"

"Then we go up the gorge, and we pump that tunnel dry, and we find out what twenty years of water left in there." Odell's voice didn't waver, but his hand tightened on the ledger until the cover creaked. "And I'll be honest with you, girl, because nobody else will be: part of me wants to know. Part of me has wanted to know since the night they poured the concrete. Whatever your daddy blamed himself for—whatever they blamed him for—I've carried the shape of it for twenty years without ever seeing the thing itself."

Lena looked down the empty road where the SUV had gone. The mountains stood above the valley with their new snow, white and indifferent, and somewhere up in all that white was a hillside with two concrete scars in it, holding back a lake of black water and everything else the river had kept.

"Seventy-two hours," she said.

"Seventy-two hours," Odell agreed. "And he'll be back before they're up. Men like that always come back, because they've already spent the money in their heads." He pushed off the rail and headed for the shop, then paused at the door. "Supper's at six. Teo fixed the water heater, so it might even be hot."

Inside, later, Lena sat at the kitchen table with the crew's accounts spread out in front of her and Odell's figures copied fair in her own handwriting, and she added the columns twice, and then a third time, hoping arithmetic would turn merciful. It wouldn't. Eleven days. Maybe twelve if she stretched the fuel.

From the bunkroom came the sound of Teo laughing at something on the radio, and Rios arguing with him about it, easy and warm, the sound of people who believed the roof over them was solid. Lena closed the ledger and pressed her palms flat against its cover, as if she could hold the whole enterprise shut by force.

Upstairs in the tin trunk under her bed was a photograph of a man in a railroad cap standing at the north portal, squinting into the sun, one hand resting on the shoulder of a girl of eight. On the back, in her mother's handwriting: *Last good day.*

She had refused Cassius Vane to his face, and it had felt clean, and it had lasted four hours.

At six she washed up and went to supper, and nobody asked her anything, because nobody had to. At nine, Odell knocked once on her door frame and set a piece of paper on the desk without a word: the crew roster, seven names, each with the amount they were owed written beside it in his square engineer's hand.

Lena sat looking at the names for a long time.

Then she got out a pen.
