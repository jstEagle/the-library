## 3. Sworn Off

The contract sat on the bunkhouse table between a tin of spare fuses and Odell's chipped coffee mug, and Lena could not make herself touch it.

Six pages. Vane's lawyer had drafted them in language clean as machined steel: scope of work, schedule of penalties, indemnification clauses, a cash advance figure that made her eyes snag on it twice, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. Reopen the Cascade Tunnel. Six weeks. All equipment, blasting permits, and downstream liability to be borne by the concession holder. All discretion to be exercised by the crew chief.

*Discretion.* That was a laugh. There was no discretion to be had on the Cascade line. There was only the tunnel, and the thing the tunnel had done to her family, and the oath the crew had sworn over a bottle of rotgut the winter her father went to prison—*never above the old line, not while any of us draw breath.*

She'd written that oath herself. She'd made them say it out loud, one by one, because she'd been twenty-two and raw and needed to hear voices other than her own promising that the past would stay buried.

Now Odell stood at the stove with his back to her, stirring a pot of beans he wasn't going to eat, and Teo was out at the trucks checking tie-downs for the third time, and the rain came down on the tin roof like it had a grudge.

"You've read it four times," Odell said without turning. "It doesn't change on the fifth."

"Everything changes on the fifth read. That's how contracts get you."

He grunted. Odell's grunts were a whole vocabulary; this one meant *you're arguing with yourself and losing.* He'd been her father's swamper before he was hers. He'd carried her out of the hearing room on his shoulders when she was eleven and screaming, and he had never once, in twenty years, said a word about it.

"Payroll's Friday," he said.

"I know when payroll is."

"Parts order for the Cat. Fuel. Teo's back wages from the Meridian job, which we still owe because the Meridian job paid us in promises." He turned then, wiping his hands on a rag gone gray with use. "I did the arithmetic while you were sleeping. We're eleven days from done, Lena. Eleven. And that's if nobody's truck throws a rod."

She looked at the contract. The advance figure looked back.

*It's just steel and water,* she told herself. *It's a hole in a mountain full of cold water and dead rail. The past isn't in there. The past is in a courthouse record and a prison file and a grave in the valley cemetery with a headstone that says BELIEVED IN HIS OWN INNOCENCE because she'd paid extra for the carving and never regretted a cent of it.*

But her hands knew better. Her hands remembered.

* * *

The rain that day, twenty years ago, had come down the same way—like it had a grudge—and Lena was eleven years old and standing in the dooryard in her mother's borrowed slicker when the state cars came up the switchback road with their lights turning through the fog.

Three of them. Nobody sent three cars for a conversation.

Her father came out of the shop with grease to his elbows and the day's wages in his pocket, and he stopped on the porch step, and Lena watched something go out of his face that never came back. Not fear. She'd thought it was fear for years, because eleven-year-olds only have a few words for the big things. It wasn't fear. It was the look of a man who has spent his whole life reading country—reading water, reading rail, reading weather—and has just understood that the country has read him first, and decided what he is.

"Marco Marchetti?" the lead officer said, though everyone in the valley had called him March since before Lena was born.

"That's me."

"You'll need to come with us. There's been a derailment on the Cascade line. Four dead."

Lena's mother made a sound like a rail snapping. Lena herself didn't make any sound at all. She just watched her father crouch down to her level, which was what he always did, which was the first thing he always did, and he took her chin in his greasy hand and said, "You listen to me, little bit. I was forty miles from that tunnel when it happened. You know where I was. Mama knows where I was. That's all that matters, and the truth is heavier than any load we ever hauled, and it'll carry us. You hear me?"

She heard him. She believed him the way you believe the sun comes up.

The truth did not carry them. The truth turned out to weigh exactly nothing. The state's case was a timecard with a smudged signature, a dispatcher's log that put him "in the vicinity," and a jury from down-valley that had ridden the Cascade line their whole lives and wanted, more than anything, a name to hate for the four coffins. They got one. Marchetti, Marco. Brakeman. Saboteur. The man who cut the brakes and sent the morning freight into Blackpool Curve at full speed, killing four men and sealing the tunnel behind them—because after the wreck, with the mountain cracked and the river finding new ways through, the railroad had poured the portals full of concrete and written the line off forever.

Her father served nine years of a twenty-year sentence before his heart quit in the infirmary at Deer Lodge. Lena was twenty when the letter came. She read it standing in a bus station, and then she went outside and threw up behind the baggage shed, and then she went and found Odell, who was working a salvage crew out of Milltown, and said, "Teach me everything you know."

He looked at her a long time. "That'll take years."

"We've got years."

They'd had years. And somewhere in the middle of them, on a night when the crew was four strong and flush for once, she'd made them swear the oath. Never above the old line. Not for any money. Not for any reason. Some wounds you leave closed, because opening them doesn't clean them, it just lets the bad air in.

She'd made them swear it because she'd learned, in the years since the trial, that her father had been right about one thing: the truth was heavy. Too heavy. It had crushed him. She wasn't going to go digging for it. She was going to go around it, the way you go around a slide, and keep her crew alive, and that would be the whole of her life's work, and she'd told herself it was enough for so long that she'd almost believed it.

* * *

"You're doing the thing," Odell said.

She blinked. The bunkhouse came back: rain, beans, the contract, the lamp. "What thing."

"The thing where you go somewhere behind your eyes and come back with a decision you haven't told anybody." He sat down across from her, the table creaking under his forearms. He was sixty-eight and built like the ballast he'd spent his life shoveling, and his hands had a tremor in them now that he pretended they didn't. "So tell me. What's the decision?"

"I haven't made it."

"You've made it. You're just waiting for it to feel forgivable." He tapped the contract with one thick finger. "I signed on with your father at nineteen. I hauled ties on the Cascade line for six seasons. I knew the four men who died up there—played cards with two of them. And I'm telling you, Lena, that tunnel doesn't hold anything we need to be afraid of. It holds water and wreck and twenty years of dark. The thing we're afraid of is on this side of the bulkhead. It's been on this side the whole time."

"That's easy to say when it's not your name in the record."

"It's not easy at all," Odell said, quiet now. "I watched them take him off that mountain. I was there. You were screaming and I picked you up and I've never once wished I'd set you down. But I'm going to say the thing nobody's said out loud in twenty years: March didn't do it. Everybody who ever worked with him knew it. And every year we don't touch that line, we're not protecting his memory. We're just agreeing with the jury."

The rain hammered. Somewhere out in the dark, Teo's flashlight swept the yard, checking tie-downs a fourth time.

"The advance clears payroll," Lena said. "The advance clears payroll and the parts and the Meridian wages and puts four months' cushion under us. And if we don't take it, in eleven days I'm selling the Cat to a gravel outfit in Missoula and we're all finding other work, and the crew's done, and you know it as well as I do."

"I know it."

"And the oath—"

"The oath was a promise to a wound," Odell said. "Wounds aren't owed anything. People are. Your father's owed. Teo's owed. You're owed." He pushed the contract an inch toward her. "I'm not telling you to sign. I'm telling you I'll stand next to you either way, and that's been true since you were eleven years old and heavier than you looked."

She laughed, or something near it, and picked up the pen.

*It's just steel and water.* A hole in a mountain. Cold water and dead rail and twenty years of dark, and somewhere behind the bulkhead, a wreck she had no intention of looking at any closer than the job required. She would take the money. She would do the work—pumps and shoring and drainage, engineering, nothing more. She would keep her eyes on the gauges and her crew on schedule, and she would not go looking for timecards or dispatcher's logs or the truth that had already crushed one Marchetti.

She would go in, and she would come out, and she would not open anything that wasn't on the drawings.

The pen scratched. Her signature looked, in the lamplight, like something written by a stranger with her hands.

"There," she said. "Done."

"Done," Odell agreed, and got up to finally eat his beans, and if his hands shook a little more than usual as he ladled them out, neither of them said so.

* * *

Teo came in at midnight, soaked to the collar, and found the contract signed and squared on the table. He read it standing, water dripping off his nose onto the page, his young face doing arithmetic of its own.

"Six weeks," he said. "Up the old line."

"Up the old line," Lena said.

He looked at her then—really looked, the way new hires do before they've learned which questions cost something—and she watched the question form and die behind his eyes, because Odell had briefed him, or the valley had, because the valley briefed everybody. *The Marchetti girl. The Cascade wreck. The oath.*

"Okay," Teo said, and hung his slicker on the peg, and that was all. That was the whole of it. And Lena understood, watching him, that the oath had just quietly stopped existing, and that she had killed it, and that she would carry that too, along with everything else.

She lay awake past two, listening to the river in the dark, high and cold somewhere below the yard, running the way water runs when it's going somewhere it's been before.

The advance would hit the account Thursday. Vane's convoy would stage at the north portal Monday.

Which meant she had five days to figure out how to face a mountain she'd sworn off—and why, when she'd signed her name, the relief she'd felt hadn't felt like relief at all. It had felt like a fuse being lit somewhere far inside her, deep and slow, burning toward something she couldn't see yet.
