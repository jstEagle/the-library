## 4. Up the Gorge

The road to Cascade was never a road. It was an argument the mountain had won, twice, and the county had repaved out of stubbornness and federal money that dried up somewhere around mile nine.

Lena drove point in the crew truck with the window cracked despite the cold, because the air coming off the gorge told her things a windshield couldn't. Ice melt running loud under the gravel. The particular mineral smell of rock that had shifted overnight. Twenty years of running these lines had taught her to read water the way other people read weather reports, and the water this morning was reading angry.

Behind her, the convoy crawled: Odell's flatbed with the compressor chained down like a hostage, Marisol's pickup towing the pump trailer, and at the rear—God help them—the supply truck, forty feet of rented steel hauling six tons of shoring timber, diesel, and chain-link, driven by Teo Vasquez on his fourth day of employment and his first day of mountain driving.

She'd put him there deliberately. Not as punishment. As education. A man who couldn't back a loaded truck up a shelf road wasn't going to survive two months at the portal, and better he learned it with her watching than alone.

"Road's washed at the second switchback," Odell said from the passenger seat. He'd been quiet since the bunkhouse, which for Odell meant he'd been thinking, which for Odell meant trouble was already scheduled. "Crew said it held last fall."

"Crew said a lot of things last fall." She took the curve low and slow, feeling the rear axle hunt for grip. Below them the river ran high and green-white with snowmelt, thirty feet down through a throat of granite. "Everything holds until it doesn't. That's the whole job."

"That the philosophy you're teaching the new kid?"

"That's the philosophy I'm teaching everybody." She glanced at him. "You've got something to say, say it."

Odell turned his coffee thermos over in his big hands. He was sixty-one and built like something the railroad had poured rather than manufactured, and he had been with her father before he'd been with her, which made every silence between them carry freight.

"Two days in," he said finally. "And I keep waiting for you to tell us what Vane actually wants up there besides a tunnel."

"He wants a tunnel."

"Nobody pays hazard rates like that for a tunnel. Nobody pays *advance* like that." He looked at her. "I've watched you sign contracts for eleven years, Lena. You read the penalty clauses first. This time you signed fast."

The switchback came up and she took it, gears growling, and let the climb answer for her. What could she say? That she'd signed fast because payroll was three weeks from bouncing? That she'd signed fast because the number Vane wrote down had more zeroes than any salvage contract she'd ever seen, and zeroes have a way of making a person forget what they swore off?

"I read it," she said.

"You skimmed it."

"I read it *fast*." The road straightened along a shelf cut into the gorge wall, close enough to the edge that the drop felt personal. "There'll be time to figure out his angle once we're standing in front of the work. Everything looks simpler with a wall of rock in front of it."

Odell grunted—the Odell grunt that meant *that's not an answer but I'll bank it*—and went back to his thermos.

In the mirror, she watched Teo's supply truck take the same curve wide, too wide, correcting, catching. The kid was good with machines and bad with fear, which was the exact inverse of most people and exactly why she'd hired him off the yard in Wenatchee when three other crews had passed. Fearless men died stupid deaths. But fearless men who were teachable—

The radio crackled. Marisol: *"Lena. Bridge at Miller Creek. You seeing this?"*

Ahead, where the shelf road crossed the creek's outlet on a span of creosoted timber and rusted stringers, Lena saw it and her stomach dropped through the floorboards.

The bridge was dying.

Not broken—dying. The near abutment had slumped a hand's width, tilting the whole deck downhill toward the far side. Water that should have gone under the span was going *through* it, boiling white through the stringers on the downstream end, and every second of that flow was pulling fill out from under the timbers like a dentist working a bad tooth.

She stopped the truck, set the brake, and got out into air that bit through her jacket. Odell joined her at the edge. Behind them, one by one, the convoy halted, engines idling, exhaust ghosting in the cold.

"We turn around," Marisol said, walking up. "Or we find another way. There isn't another way, so we turn around."

"Turning around costs us the day getting down and the day getting back," Lena said. "We don't have days. Vane's clock started Monday."

"So we cross it?"

"We *assess* it." She walked the bridge slowly, testing each plank with her boot heel, reading the stringers the way she'd read the river. The upstream half was sound—old growth timber, dry, true. The downstream third was the problem. The abutment there had been undercut by the creek shifting course during freeze-thaw, and the outer two bents stood in air where soil used to be.

"It'll hold a truck," Odell said behind her. "One truck. Slow. If the driver keeps his wheels on the sound line."

"One truck each way, spaced out, no stopping on the deck." She turned. "Teo goes last. His load's the heaviest."

Marisol's jaw tightened. "Then let me take the supply truck and he can drive my—"

"No." It came out harder than she intended. She softened it. "He takes the truck because he has to learn this bridge exists. Everybody crosses everything on this mountain eventually. Better here, where we're all standing right here."

She flagged Teo forward. He climbed down from the cab looking younger than his twenty-six years, cheeks red from the cold or the climb or both, and studied the bridge with the frank, unembarrassed attention of a man who didn't yet know enough to be properly afraid.

"It's tilted," he observed.

"It's tilted."

"How tilted is too tilted?"

"Ask me after." She put a hand on his shoulder. "Low gear. First gear. Don't touch the brakes on the deck—if you feel it move, you give it fuel and get across. Momentum is your friend. Hesitation is not. And whatever happens—" she met his eyes, made sure she had them, "—you do not stop on the bridge."

He nodded. Went back to the cab. She heard him take three long breaths through the open window, a habit she recognized because it was hers.

The empty trucks crossed first, one at a time, each crossing punctuated by a groan from the stringers that everyone pretended not to hear. Then Odell's flatbed, light now, creeping across like a cat on ice. Then Marisol.

Then it was Teo.

The supply truck nosed onto the deck and the whole span sighed—a deep structural exhalation, wood and iron settling into a shape they didn't want to hold. Lena stood at the near abutment with her fists closed, watching the truck's progress foot by foot. Halfway. Two-thirds. The rear wheels came onto the sound timber and she allowed herself one breath—

The far abutment let go.

No warning. No crack, no groan, just a soft subterranean shrug as the last of the undercut fill surrendered, and the downstream end of the bridge dropped eight inches, then a foot, the deck racking sideways with a sound like a ship breaking up. The supply truck lurched. Teo did exactly the wrong thing—he hit the brakes—and the truck stopped dead with its rear axle still on the tilting deck and its front wheels hanging over a gap where the far approach had been a moment ago.

"Fuel!" Lena shouted. "Give it fuel! *Go!*"

The engine roared. The wheels spun. The deck beneath the rear tires sagged, tilted further, and the truck began to slide—not forward, *sideways*, the whole forty-foot rig slewing toward the downstream edge where the stringers were peeling away into the creek.

What happened next happened in about four seconds, and Lena would spend years replaying them.

Teo bailed. That was the right call and she'd never fault him for it—he came out the driver's door onto the upstream rail of the bed and jumped for solid ground and missed by six feet, catching the far abutment's broken edge with both hands, body swinging out over the creek, boots kicking at nothing.

And the truck, driverless, sliding, rolled its rear wheels off the failed deck entirely. The front axle caught the gap's lip. The whole rig teetered there, balanced on its front bumper and the far edge of the ruined span, six tons of timber and diesel cantilevered out over white water with Teo hanging below it, directly in its shadow.

Nobody spoke. The river filled the silence.

Lena's mind went cold and clear and fast, the way it only did when everything else was on fire. *Don't look at the truck. Look at the physics.* The truck would go when the lip crumbled—it might be ten seconds, it might be ten minutes, but the fill under that abutment was soup and the math was not complicated. Which meant nobody was climbing down to Teo from above. Which meant—

"The winch," she said, turning. "Marisol, your truck, back it up, cable off the front mount. Odell, anchor points—we need the flatbed as counterweight, chain it nose-to-nose. *Now.*"

They moved. They moved because four years of wrecks and washouts and winter recoveries had built the reflexes, and while Marisol backed her pickup into position and Odell threw chains, Lena lay down flat at the broken edge of the abutment, coat sleeves scraping grit, and looked down at Teo.

His face was gray. His hands were bleeding where the concrete had chewed them. Below his boots the creek thundered through the breach, throwing spray that iced his sleeves.

"Hey," she said. "Look at me. Not the truck. Me."

He looked at her.

"In about ninety seconds there's going to be a cable in front of you. When it comes, you wrap it twice around your forearm, clip the carabiner to your harness, and you do not let go no matter what. Can you do that?"

"My hands are—"

"Your hands are fine. Adrenaline is a painkiller. Use it." She heard the winch motor spin up behind her, felt the cable hum past her shoulder as Marisol paid it out. "Teo. Hey. Look at me. You know what I did my first week on this mountain? Dropped a generator into the Napeequa. Whole thing. Eleven thousand dollars."

"What happened?"

"Odell fished it out and billed me for the rope." She almost smiled. "That's the deal here. We break stuff, we fish each other out, we bill each other for rope. That's the whole culture. Ninety seconds."

The cable swung out over the gap. Too short. "Six more feet!" she yelled, and Marisol fed it, and the hook came penduluming back and this time it passed close enough that Teo lunged for it—one hand leaving the abutment, the world holding its breath—and caught it.

Wrap. Clip. Click.

"Winch!" Lena screamed, and the motor took the strain.

It was not graceful. Winch cables are not designed to lift swinging men off crumbling abutments; the cable sang, the pickup squatted on its rear axle, and for one endless moment Teo swung out over the creek like a wrecking ball with a heartbeat. Then the arc carried him inward, his boots found the broken edge, and Lena and Odell had him by the harness straps and were dragging him up over the lip onto solid ground, where he lay on his back in the gravel, chest heaving, staring at the sky.

Above him, the supply truck chose that moment to finish its decision. The lip crumbled. The rig slid off the shattered abutment and went down the gorge wall in a series of enormous, cartwheeling impacts—timber bursting loose like matchsticks, the diesel tanks rupturing against rock—and hit the river with a boom they felt through their boot soles. It settled in the shallows at a drunken angle, half-submerged, its cargo scattered downstream in a brown wake.

Silence. Six people standing at the edge of a broken bridge, looking at six tons of their supplies drowning thirty feet below.

Teo sat up. Blood was sheeting from a gash above his eyebrow where the jump had cost him, and his hands were shaking so hard the tremor traveled up his arms.

"Boss," he said. His voice came out wrecked. "I stopped on the bridge."

"Yeah," Lena said. "You did."

"I'm sorry. I—"

"Teo." She crouched in front of him, took his shaking wrist, held it steady until the trembling slowed against her grip. "You bailed clean, you clipped on the first pass, and you're sitting here breathing instead of being under that truck. That's the whole report. The rest is equipment, and equipment is replaceable, and tomorrow Marisol teaches you why you never touch the brakes on a failing deck. Right, Mari?"

Marisol, who had gone the color of paper, nodded once.

* * *

They burned the rest of the day and all of the next one on recovery, because Lena Marchetti did not leave six tons of shoring timber and a rented truck on the bottom of a gorge, and because the rental company's insurance rider required the chassis returned or the deposit forfeited, and the deposit was four figures she did not have.

Odell rigged a highline anchor off two living pines above the wreck site and they lowered Marisol down on ropes to strip the cargo—half the timber was lost to the current, but they saved the diesel drums, sealed and floating in their cradle, and most of the chain-link. The truck itself came up in pieces, winched out in sections over eight hours of brutal, freezing labor, everyone taking turns on the ropes because warm bodies tired faster than cold ones and rotation was survival.

Teo worked the whole time. Refused the rest, refused the ride down to the clinic in Skykomish, let Marisol butterfly the gash above his eye with electrical tape and steri-strips because nobody had packed a proper kit. By the second evening he was hauling cable with the best of them, gray-faced and silent, and when the last piece of chassis came over the rim he sat down hard on a stump and put his head in his hands.

Lena sat beside him. Below them the creek ran on, indifferent, carrying the last scraps of their cargo toward the sea.

"You want to quit," she said. "That's normal. Day five, nearly died, boss dragged you up a cliff. Most people quit here."

"I don't want to quit." He lifted his head. There was blood in his eyebrow line and something else in his face—something that hadn't been there Monday. "I want to know why you didn't fire me."

"Because firing you wouldn't teach you anything except that this crew quits on people." She stood, knees cracking, and offered him a hand up. "Everybody pays something to work this mountain. Today you paid. Welcome aboard—for real, now."

He took the hand. His grip was still unsteady, but it didn't let go.

* * *

They lost two days. Two days of Vane's six weeks, spent on a rope, a winch, and a rental chassis that would cost them a fight with an insurance adjuster later. The convoy reached the north portal at dusk on the third evening, headlights sweeping across a sight that shut up every conversation at once.

The bulkhead stood across the tunnel mouth like a wall built to keep something in rather than something out—concrete and steel plate, stained black with twenty years of seepage, water weeping from its seams in long dark tears. Behind it, they all knew, the drowned dark of the Cascade Tunnel waited, and everything the river had kept.

Teo stood beside the truck, staring, his stitched eyebrow catching the headlight glow.

"Boss," he said quietly. "Whose lantern is that?"

Lena followed his gaze. Hanging on a spike driven into the bulkhead's edge, sheltered from the rain, was a miner's flame lantern—old pattern, brass, glass smoked black.

Old pattern. Brass. Glass smoked black.

But the spike it hung on was bright steel, unrusted, and the wick inside the glass was trimmed and dry.

Lena crossed the mud, took the lantern down, and turned it in her gloved hands. No corrosion. Oiled hinges. And stamped into the base, small and clean:

**PROPERTY OF C.V. SURVEYING — 2019**

Five years old. Maybe less.

Someone had been inside the Cascade Tunnel recently. Recently enough to leave their lamp hanging by the door.

She looked up at the bulkhead, at the water weeping through the seams, and felt the first cold thread of the real job slide under her skin—nothing to do with pumps or permits, and everything to do with the fact that Cassius Vane had some serious explaining to do.
