## 5. What the River Keeps

The river had a voice, and it was saying Teo's name.

Not really. He knew that. It was just the acoustics of the gorge, the water bouncing off the granite walls the way sound bounced inside a cistern, so that every rush and gurgle came back doubled, tripled, wearing different voices. But standing alone at the north portal with the light going amber behind the western ridge, it was hard not to take it personally.

*Teo. Teo. Teo.*

"Knock it off," he told the river.

He'd drawn the evening watch fair and square—cards in the cook tent, Odell's rule that nobody complained about a draw they'd shuffled themselves—and now he was walking the perimeter with a thermos of coffee that had gone bitter an hour ago and a headlamp he hadn't switched on yet because the dark wasn't full enough to need it. The portal stood forty yards off, a black rectangle punched into the mountainside, half-swallowed by alder and slide alder and twenty years of everything that grows when nobody's watching.

Lena had told them all the same thing at the morning briefing: *Look, don't touch. Log anything that looks wrong.* That was the whole job for the first week. Survey, log, don't touch. The bulkhead first, then the drainage gallery, then the old pump house downstream. Nobody went past the bulkhead. Nobody even thought about going past the bulkhead.

Teo was good at looking. He'd been the youngest on his last crew, back before, and being youngest meant you learned to watch everything because you were the last to know anything. So he watched.

The bulkhead was a wall of poured concrete maybe eight feet high, set into the portal throat, with a steel access hatch near the top and a row of weep holes along the bottom where the water behind it breathed out in thin silver threads. Except tonight the threads weren't thin. The middle weep holes were running thick, almost roped, and the stain below them spread across the rock apron like something spilled. The water coming out was clear at the top and tea-dark by the time it reached the gravel, and where it pooled it sat black and still as ink, holding the last of the sky in its surface.

Behind that wall, somewhere in the mountain's throat, stood forty years of floodwater. Lena had said it flat, the way she said everything: *The tunnel runs two miles through the shoulder of the mountain. The south end collapsed into the river gorge sometime after the sealing. Everything between here and there is full.*

Full. Two miles of black water pressed against eight inches of concrete, and the concrete was leaking, and the leak was getting louder. Teo put his palm flat against the bulkhead, low, near the biggest weep hole. Cold came through it like the wall was dead, but under the cold there was a tremor—the faintest pulse, rhythmic, patient. Water finding its way. Water always finding its way.

*Teo. Teo.*

"All right," he muttered. "I hear you."

He should have logged it and walked on. The seep rate was up; that was worth a line in the book. But the coffee was bitter and the evening was long and the path curved around the base of the bulkhead toward the old maintenance walk, and Teo had never in his life been good at leaving a thing alone once it started talking to him.

The maintenance walk ran along the rock face above the tailrace channel, a shelf of rotted planking bolted to the stone. Twenty years of needles had drifted onto it, brown and soft, and ferns grew up through the gaps. His boots left prints in the duff, the first prints in a long time, he figured. Nobody came up here. That was the point of a place like this—it stayed closed until somebody paid to open it, and nobody had paid for twenty years.

Except.

He stopped.

There was a patch of duff ahead, maybe ten feet on, where the brown had been disturbed. Not slid—*swept*. Swept sideways in a rough arc, the way you'd clear a spot to set something down or kneel. And in the cleared patch, half tucked behind a slab of spalled concrete, sat a lantern.

It was one of the old railroad kinds, the short-globe hand lanterns with the wire guard, the kind Odell still kept two of hanging in the tool container out of pure sentiment. This one lay on its side, globe cracked, guard bent, and the rust on it was deep—orange bleeding into brown bleeding into black, scale flaking off in plates. Teo crouched over it without touching. His breath fogged. The river said his name.

Twenty years, he thought. Twenty years of rain and freeze and snowmelt dripping off that rock face. A lantern left out here since the sealing would be a lump of oxide. It would have no shape left. It would be a stain with handles.

This one had shape. The hinge worked when he finally made himself touch it—stiff, gritty, but working. The fount inside held a film of old fuel, varnished amber, not evaporated to nothing. The wick was charred. The burner ring was scorched black, and when he tilted it, a fine ash sifted from the chimney and landed on his boot.

Somebody had burned this lantern. Recently enough that the ash hadn't washed away, and the rust—well. Rust lied. Rust was just what weather did to any steel it could reach, fast or slow depending on the year it got. Five wet winters would do this. Five wet winters and a little coal smoke and a little handling.

Five.

He turned it over. Stamped into the fount's base, shallow, mostly eaten: a maker's mark and a date code. Teo had spent two seasons cataloguing artifacts for a historical society job outside Bend; he knew how to read these. His heart did something strange and slow.

The stamp read a year five years back. Give or take a manufacturing lag, give or take a lie in the stamping itself—but the ash didn't lie, and the varnish didn't lie, and the swept patch of ground didn't lie.

Someone had come up this walk, in the last handful of years, with a lit lantern, and knelt here at the sealed north portal of the Cascade Tunnel, and done something. Looked at something. Listened at something. The hatch was six feet up the bulkhead face; below it, at eye level for a kneeling man, the concrete showed a cluster of old chisel marks, faint, deliberate, spaced like someone taking measurements.

Or like someone tapping. Sounding the wall. Listening for what answered.

Teo stood up too fast and his knee popped and the sound went rolling down the gorge and came back twice. He switched on his headlamp, which made everything worse—a hard white circle, shadows crowding in around it—and swept the beam along the walk, up the rock face, across the black pool. Nothing moved. The weep holes threaded their silver. Somewhere upstream the river dropped over a shelf with a sound like a door closing.

He photographed the lantern from four angles, then the chisel marks, then the swept ground, using the crew camera because Lena wanted timestamps on everything. The flash lit the portal mouth once, huge and white, and in that single frozen instant Teo saw the interior of the tunnel beyond the bulkhead's edge—not much, just the first few feet of the arched liner before the dark took over, wet stone gleaming, and—

And something pale, low down, near the invert. A shape. Rounded. Man-made.

The flash died and the dark came back and the shape was gone, if it had ever been there. He raised the camera again and his thumb hovered and he thought about the oath, the story Odell had told his first night around the stove—the derailment, the sealed tunnel, the man they'd never speak of unless work made them. *We don't go up the old Cascade line,* Odell had said, and everyone had nodded, and Teo had nodded too, because he was new and new people nod.

But the lantern was five years old. Which meant the oath had already been broken, by somebody, quietly, without telling a soul—and if somebody else had already crossed this line, then the line wasn't sacred anymore. It was just a line. And lines that aren't sacred are just places where things hide.

He took the second photograph.

The flash bloomed. In the white instant, the pale shape resolved: a drum, maybe, or a float—something buoyant, wedged against the arch, riding high against the waterline stain on the liner stones. Something that floated. Something that had floated *up* to rest there when the water rose, and would float again if the water fell.

Which meant the water had fallen before. Recently. Somebody had lowered it, gone in, come out—or meant to.

Teo lowered the camera. His hands weren't shaking exactly, but they wanted to. The coffee in his thermos had gone fully cold and he drank it anyway, because his mouth needed a job.

He should wake Lena. That was procedure: anything wrong, anything unlogged, wake the boss. But it was nearly dark now, truly dark, and Lena had been asleep maybe ninety minutes after a sixteen-hour day, and the look on her face when she'd signed Vane's contract—that look had followed Teo around for three days like a dog that wouldn't stop circling. She carried this mountain somewhere bad in her. Everyone knew it and nobody said it. Waking her at nine p.m. to say *I found a lantern that isn't as old as it should be* felt like handing her a reason to call the whole job, and the job was payroll, and payroll was why any of them ate.

So instead he did the smaller right thing. He bagged the lantern in a poly sack, tagged it, and set it inside the survey tent where the morning shift would trip over it. He wrote the log entry in block letters: *NORTH PORTAL, MAINT WALK. LANTERN FOUND, BURNED RECENTLY (EST ≤5 YRS). CHISEL MARKS AT BULKHEAD BASE. POSSIBLE PRIOR ENTRY / WATER LEVEL MANIPULATION. PHOTOS 041–042. REQUEST BOSS REVIEW AM.*

Then he stood a moment longer at the bulkhead, close enough to feel the tremor in the concrete, and listened the way the unknown visitor must have listened. Under the river-voice, under the seep-hiss, there was another sound. Faint. Irregular. From behind the wall, from deep in the two miles of drowned dark—a knocking. Soft, arrhythmic, like wood shifting, like something loose bumping stone in a current that shouldn't be moving.

Water doesn't knock. Water hisses, water roars, water threads through weep holes. Water doesn't make a sound like a fist on a door.

Teo backed away. He didn't run—he was proud of that later, that he walked, boots deliberate on the rotten planks—but he walked fast, and he did not look back at the portal, and the river called his name the whole way down the path, and this time he let it.

In the cook tent, Odell was up nursing the stove, reading glasses pushed up into gray curls, and Teo stood in the doorway dripping cold air and said, "Odell. You ever known anybody to go inside the Cascade since it was sealed?"

Odell looked at him over the glasses for a long moment. The stove ticked. Outside, the river kept saying everybody's name, one after another, patient as a ledger.

"No," Odell said slowly. "And I've known everybody who works these mountains."

Teo set the tagged lantern on the table between them, and the char smell of it rose up faint and fresh, like something only just blown out.

"Then somebody's lying," Teo said, "or somebody new is up here. Because whoever it was—" he tapped the fount, the date stamp, the scorched burner ring— "they weren't here twenty years ago."

"They were here five."

* * *

Morning came gray and hard, frost furring the guy-lines, and Lena read the log entry standing very still with her coffee untouched, the way she did when she was putting pieces together behind her eyes and didn't want them rattling. She picked up the lantern, turned it, read the date stamp, and Teo watched her face do something complicated—watch the suspicion arrive and settle in, taking off its coat like it planned to stay.

She looked at the photographs. At photo 042, the pale floating shape against the waterline stain. Then she looked up the gorge, toward the south, where the river came down from the collapsed end, and Teo saw her jaw set.

"Nobody touches the bulkhead today," she said. "Nobody goes near it. I want the seep rates logged every two hours." She handed the lantern back to him like it weighed more than it should. "And Teo—when Vane's helicopter comes up this afternoon, you're standing next to me when it lands."

"Why me?"

"Because," Lena said, and her voice was quiet and flat as the black water behind the wall, "somebody's been inside my tunnel. And I want to watch Cassius Vane's face when I ask him how long he's known."
