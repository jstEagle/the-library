## 6. Vane's Arithmetic

The rain had stopped sometime before dawn, which on the mountain meant only that the water had changed jobs—from falling to seeping. Lena stood at the north portal with the rusted lantern in both hands, turning it in the gray light like a question she didn't want answered.

It was a good lantern. That was the problem. A carbide model, brass-capped, the kind rail crews had used for sixty years and nobody had manufactured for twenty. The body should have been pitted through, the burner fused with corrosion, the whole thing welded into one lump of oxide by two decades of standing water. Instead the hinge moved smooth. The carbide chamber still held a pale residue that smelled faintly of old gas when she worked her thumbnail into it. The rust was skin-deep—a season's rust, maybe two.

Five years. Maybe less.

She set it down on a cable spool and wiped her hands on her thighs as if the thing had left something on them. Behind her, the bulkhead loomed out of the portal's shadow: a wall of poured concrete studded with rebar stubs, built the year she was nine years old by men who wanted the mountain to forget what it had swallowed. Black water stood behind it somewhere—she could feel the pressure of it in her teeth, the way you feel a storm coming. The pumps Odell had rigged ran day and night now, a wet mechanical heartbeat under everything.

Teo came up the grade from camp, coffee thermos swinging, and stopped when he saw her face.

"You told Odell," he said.

"I told Odell."

"And he said?"

"He said I should talk to Vane." She looked back at the lantern. "Odell doesn't say things like that unless he means them."

Teo crouched by the spool, studied the lantern without touching it. He was twenty-three and had been with them eight months, long enough to know the rule but not long enough to have helped make it, and there was still something in him that wanted to believe problems could be solved by looking directly at them. "Maybe somebody dropped it during the original salvage. When they sealed it."

"Sealing crews don't work inside the flood zone. There was nothing inside to salvage—that was the whole point of sealing it." She heard her own voice go flat and careful, the way it did when she was holding something down. "Somebody carried this lantern into a drowned tunnel. Recently."

"Or somebody wants us to think so."

That was the other possibility, and she'd been chewing on it since four in the morning. Either way, the arithmetic had stopped adding up, and Cassius Vane was the only man on this mountain who'd brought his own numbers.

* * *

She found him at the survey tent above the south approach, exactly where she knew he'd be, because Vane kept office hours the way other men kept religion. He sat at a folding table under an awning strung between two alders, laptop open against the damp, hard copies fanned around him in a system only he understood. His coat was dry. His boots were dry. On this mountain, in April, that was its own kind of statement—the man did not get wet, did not get cold, did not get his hands on anything that might leave a mark.

"Miss Marchetti." He didn't look up. "We're forty-one hours behind schedule. If you're here to tell me the pumps need new impellers, I've already authorized them."

"I'm here to show you something." She laid the lantern on the table, on top of his elevation profiles, and watched his hands stop moving.

For half a second—less—he went very still. Not the stillness of surprise. The stillness of a man deciding which version of himself to be. Then he picked the lantern up, turned it with genuine-seeming interest, and frowned at it like a foreman frowning at a bad weld.

"Where was this?"

"North portal. Silt line, six meters inside the bulkhead. Teo found it yesterday." She let that sit. "The hinge works, Mr. Vane. The carbide cup has residue in it. That lantern has been inside that tunnel within the last five years, and the tunnel's been sealed for twenty."

Vane set it down. He aligned it carefully with the edge of a map, square to the paper, a small compulsive courtesy. "Floodwater does strange things. Anaerobic conditions, low oxygen below the silt line—corrosion slows dramatically. You'd know that if you'd done tunnel recovery before."

"I've done three. I know what twenty years of water does to brass." She pulled out the folding chair across from him and sat, uninvited, because sitting made it harder for him to stand over her. "So let's do the arithmetic together. Your concession expires in six weeks. You hired my crew at nearly double rate to reopen a tunnel every contractor in three counties refused to quote. You're paying penalties to yourself, effectively, since you own the deadline. And now we find evidence someone's been inside the sealed tunnel recently—which means someone had access, equipment, and a reason. There are exactly two people on this mountain with keys to the north access road, and one of them is me."

Vane leaned back. Rain dripped off the awning edge between them, patient as a metronome.

"You think I sent divers into a flooded tunnel," he said.

"I think somebody did."

"The tunnel's not flooded. It's *drowned*. There's a difference. Flooded means you can pump it out. Drowned means the mountain made a decision and you live with it." He tapped the table once. "Nobody's been inside, Miss Marchetti. What you found is debris from the original wreck, washed forward by twenty years of current. Brass survives. Ask any diver."

"Washed forward six meters and then set upright on a ledge."

He paused. "Set upright."

"On a flat stone. Burner up. Like someone put it down to work and meant to pick it back up." She held his eyes. "Debris doesn't stand itself up, Mr. Vane."

Something moved behind his face—not guilt, she realized. Calculation. She'd seen men look guilty before; this was different. This was a man running numbers and finding they no longer favored him, and deciding, in real time, how much truth the budget allowed.

"Schedules," he said finally. "You want to talk about arithmetic? Here's mine. Every day this tunnel stays shut costs me eleven thousand dollars in carrying costs and penalties. Six weeks to permit expiry. My engineers' models say the water column drops below working depth in nineteen days if your pumps hold. Yours say twenty-six. I'm budgeting for yours, which means I'm already spending money on your pessimism." He spread his hands. "A lantern is not a schedule item. A lantern is noise. Bring me flow rates, structural readings, pump telemetry. Bring me anything that moves steel, and I'll act on it before you finish the sentence. But I don't pay double rate for ghost stories."

"It's not a ghost story."

"No?" He smiled, thin and professional. "Then what is it?"

Lena had spent twenty years learning the difference between a man who was lying and a man who was hiding, and Cassius Vane was doing neither, exactly. He was *managing*. Treating her suspicion as a cost center, a variance to be explained away in the quarterly report. Which meant either he genuinely didn't know about the lantern—or he knew something worse, something the lantern was only the edge of, and he'd decided she didn't have clearance for it.

Both possibilities were bad. Only one was survivable.

"Fine," she said, and stood. "Schedules. But I want the survey maps. All of them. My crew can't shore a bulkhead against loads we haven't seen."

"They're proprietary."

"They're load data. Odell needs them or I pull my people off the pumps and you can explain nineteen days versus never to your backers."

The smile stayed fixed while everything behind it recalculated. Then he slid a folder across the table—thick, corner-brassed, the kind of document that had been printed by someone who cared about documents. "Hydrology and structural surveys. Initial series. The updated set comes Thursday."

Lena took it and didn't leave. Because the folder was heavy with more than paper, and because Vane had given it up too fast, and because a man like Vane gave you exactly what you asked for when what you asked for was safe.

She opened it right there, flipping past the hydrology charts, the pump curves, the cross-sections of the bore rendered in clean blue lines. And there it was.

Page after page of the tunnel interior, surveyed—she checked the date stamp—eleven months ago. Before the contract. Before her. Someone had been inside the Cascade Tunnel almost a year ago, and had produced drawings of it, and Cassius Vane had those drawings in his tent while telling her nobody had been inside for twenty years.

Her finger stopped on sheet fourteen. A plan view of the bore, 1,400 meters in from the north portal, deep in the drowned section. Most of the drawing was routine: invert profile, lining condition notes, silt depths. But at one station—marked with a red circle and a coordinate block, the only annotation in color in the entire document—someone had flagged a single point. Station 14+62. Offset six meters left of centerline.

Six meters left of centerline was nowhere. It wasn't the track bed, where a derailment would scatter wreckage. It wasn't the drainage channel, where current would carry debris. It was a patch of undisturbed ballast against the far wall, a place nothing would come to rest except by being carried there and put down.

She thought of the lantern, upright on its stone.

"What's at fourteen sixty-two?" she asked, without looking up.

She felt him go still again, that same managed stillness, and this time she understood it completely. He'd known the maps were in the folder. He'd handed them over anyway, which meant either he hadn't remembered what was on sheet fourteen—or he had, and he wanted to see whether she'd find it.

"Survey artifact," Vane said. "Instrument reflection off the lining. We circled anomalies for follow-up."

"Instrument reflections don't get coordinates to the centimeter." She turned the page toward him. "And they don't get circled in red."

"Miss Marchetti." His voice cooled by degrees, the way the tunnel water was cold—still on top, killing underneath. "Your contract covers reopening a bore and making it safe. It does not cover interpreting my engineering. If you have concerns about the integrity of the structure at any station, log them through Odell and my people will review them. That's the process. That's the *arithmetic*." He gathered the loose papers, squared them, tapped them neat. "Now. Forty-one hours behind. Is there anything else?"

There was a great deal else. There was the fact that the red circle sat less than thirty meters from where the wreck of the *Cascades Limited* lay according to every record she'd memorized by age fifteen, in the dark, with a flashlight under her blanket. There was the fact that a man who owned a deadline didn't circle random spots in drowned tunnels for fun. There was the fact that he'd hired *her*—not the cheapest crew, not the closest crew, but a crew whose name was a liability everywhere except, apparently, to him.

But she'd learned from her father's mistakes, and the biggest one had been saying everything he knew the moment he knew it, to people who were counting on him not to.

"No," Lena said. "Nothing else."

She took the folder and walked out into the drip and gray of the morning, and behind her she heard Vane resume typing, steady as rain, a man whose books balanced.

* * *

Odell was waiting at the pump shed, which meant Teo had run ahead, which meant the whole camp would know within the hour. Her foreman stood with his arms crossed, watching the discharge hose pulse brown water into the gorge, and he didn't ask. He just waited, the way he'd waited through eleven years and two bankruptcies and one funeral, until she laid the open folder on the generator housing and put her finger on the red circle.

Odell read it for a long time. He'd been a miner before he was a salvager, down in the Kennecott workings, and he read ground the way other men read weather.

"That's not collapse," he said at last. "Collapse zones, you mark 'em wide. Debris fields, you mark 'em along the fall line." He tapped the circle. "This is a point target. Somebody knows something's at fourteen sixty-two, and it ain't rock."

"Somebody surveyed inside a sealed tunnel eleven months ago."

"Mm." Odell straightened, worked his bad shoulder the way he did when he was thinking. "Eleven months ago, permits for entry into a state-sealed tunnel require a signed waiver from the concessions office." He looked at her. "Who owns the concessions office?"

"Cassius Vane."

"There's your arithmetic, then." He said it without heat, without drama, just laying the number on the table next to the others. "Question is what you want to do with it. We can walk. Contract's got a hardship clause—structural anomaly, crew safety, we exit with sixty percent paid and no penalty. I drafted it myself, back when you signed. Knew you'd need a door."

Lena looked at the red circle. Six meters off centerline, 1,400 meters into the dark, in a tunnel her father had died inside twice—once in the wreck, once in the record. Twenty years she'd kept her crew away from this mountain on a rule written in grief, and the first time she broke it, she'd found a lantern set down carefully by someone who meant to come back, and a map with one spot marked like a treasure on a boy's chart.

If she walked, she'd spend the rest of her life wondering what was at fourteen sixty-two. If she stayed, she was staying inside a man's arithmetic, on a page he'd drawn, toward a number he'd chosen—and every instinct she had about salvage, about mountains, about men like Vane, said that whoever marked that spot expected to reach it, and had planned for everyone between him and it to be expendable.

"We don't walk," she said.

Odell nodded slowly, unsurprised. "Figured. Then we do it smart. Pumps keep running, we log everything, and nobody goes past the bulkhead until I say the water's honest." He closed the folder and handed it back to her. "One more thing, boss. That circle's eleven months old. Whatever Vane's after—he's been coming up this mountain alone for a year, spending his own money, before he ever heard our name." Odell's eyes went toward the portal, toward the black water standing patient behind the concrete. "Man doesn't spend a year chasing something unless somebody told him it was there. Question I'd be asking is—who told him? And how'd they know?"

Lena had no answer. But that night, checking the pump gauges by headlamp, she caught herself doing the thing she swore she'd stopped doing—running the old crash over in her mind, the inquest, the witness list, the names that had been read into the record and buried under it. Her father's notebook had listed every soul aboard the *Limited*. Passengers, crew, and the three names history forgot: the road's inspector, the insurer's adjuster, and a young brakeman's widow who'd signed the manifest as next of kin for a man in car seven.

She couldn't remember the widow's name. But she remembered the adjuster's firm, because her father had cursed it at the kitchen table a hundred times, and because the firm's regional director, twenty years ago, had been a rising young man named—

She stopped, headlamp beam trembling slightly on the gauge glass.

The pumps thudded on in the dark, lowering the water centimeter by centimeter, and somewhere behind the bulkhead, at station fourteen sixty-two, whatever Cassius Vane had circled in red waited with the patience of the drowned.
