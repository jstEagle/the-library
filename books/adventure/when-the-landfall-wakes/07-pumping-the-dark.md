## 7. Pumping the Dark

The pumps started at four in the morning because Odell Brandt had never in his life trusted a machine he hadn't heard running first.

He stood at the north portal with a thermos of coffee gone bitter and watched the discharge hose jump and buck like a live thing, spilling black water down the rock apron into the channel they'd cut for it. Two hundred feet below, the river took it without comment. The river had been taking things without comment up here for a hundred years.

"Flow's good," Teo said beside him, shouting over the diesel racket. The kid had a headlamp on even though dawn was coming up gray behind the ridge—couldn't shake the habit from the first night, when the dark inside the portal had looked back at all of them. "Four hundred gallons a minute. Maybe better."

"Maybe better isn't a number."

Teo grinned. He was twenty-three and everything about him was still negotiable. "Three-eighty to four-twenty, then. Depending on head pressure."

Odell grunted, which the kid had learned to read as approval. Behind them the portal mouth gaped—a steel arch set into cut granite, half its timbers rotted to brown lace, the bulkhead wall beyond it holding back whatever the tunnel had been keeping. You couldn't see the water from here. That was the thing nobody said out loud. You could hear it, though, if the pumps went quiet: a low sound like breathing through a wet cloth, the sound of a mountain with fluid in its lungs.

Six weeks. Vane's contract said six weeks to reopen the line, and that meant getting the water down twenty-two vertical feet so they could cut a working access through the bulkhead, shore it, and run the dewatering long-term while they cleared the bore. On paper it was straightforward. Odell had spent nineteen years underground before his knees voted him to the surface, and straightforward was a word he'd learned to distrust the way other men distrusted strangers.

"Get some breakfast," he told Teo. "You're on second shift with me at noon."

"You're not sleeping?"

"I slept." He hadn't. "Go on."

The kid went. Odell stayed, and drank his bad coffee, and listened to the mountain breathe.

* * *

By seven the sun had cleared the ridge enough to throw a blade of light across the portal apron, and the rest of the crew came up from camp smelling of woodsmoke and oatmeal. Lena arrived last, as she always did—not because she moved slow but because she'd already walked the whole site twice before anyone saw her. She came along the discharge channel with her hood down despite the cold, hair going iron-gray at the temples at forty-one, eyes doing their inventory: hoses, fuel levels, the telltales they'd strung across the approach, the sky.

"Level?" she asked.

"Dropped fourteen inches overnight," Odell said. "If she holds, we're looking at access depth in nine days. Ten if the seepage picks up."

"It'll pick up." She crouched at the edge of the channel, watching the water run. "Snowpack's heavy this year. Every degree of warm weather is money out of our pocket."

"That's the optimistic reading."

She glanced up at him. One corner of her mouth moved—the Marchetti version of a laugh, rationed like wartime sugar. "Give me the pessimistic one tonight. First I want coffee and the morning report."

He gave her the report standing up, which was how he gave everyone everything: pump hours, fuel burn, the cracked fitting on number two they'd swapped out, Teo's flow numbers corrected upward because the kid deserved to have his arithmetic honored even if it wasn't a number yet. Lena listened the way she did everything—completely, without wasting motion—and then she went down the line and put her hands on shoulders and checked knots and said six words to each person, and by eight o'clock the crew was moving like one animal with a dozen limbs.

Odell had worked for bosses who managed by volume and bosses who managed by memo and one memorable lunatic who managed by throwing things. Lena managed by attention. It was cheaper than any of the others and it held better in the rain.

He was thinking about that—about how a crew becomes a family not through sentiment but through the accumulated weight of small competencies—when he made his first trip inside the bulkhead gallery to check the relief valves.

* * *

The gallery was a maintenance adit driven parallel to the main bore behind the bulkhead wall, and it was where the original builders had put their valves and gauges, back when the Cascade Tunnel was a living thing instead of a grave with plumbing. To reach it you went through the personnel lock—a steel tube six feet wide with a door at each end, built so men could service the wall without ever letting the water past them. The near door opened onto the gallery; the far door, if you were a fool or a dead man, opened onto the flood.

They'd rigged work lights on the gallery side. Odell went in alone, because checking valves was a two-minute job and he didn't believe in making two people share a two-minute job's risk.

Inside, the air was cold and mineral, forty degrees and damp as a root cellar. The lights made yellow islands along the concrete. Water sounds came through the far wall—the deep, patient pressure of maybe ninety thousand tons of snowmelt standing in the bore, leaning its whole weight against eleven inches of reinforced concrete and eighty years of silt.

Odell walked the gallery slow, laying his palm flat against the wall every few paces the way he'd done in copper mines in three states. Feeling for weeps. A healthy wall talked to you through your hand—cold, sure, silent. A sick wall wept at the seams, or ticked, or sang.

The wall was talking fine until he got to station marker 4.

There he stopped, and took his hand off the concrete, and crouched down with his lamp low, and felt the bottom drop out of his stomach the way it does in an elevator.

Somebody had been cutting on this wall.

Not recently-cut—rusted, weathered, softened at the edges. But cuts. Kerf lines, straight and deliberate, laid out in a pattern no flood had ever invented: two horizontal channels a hand-span apart, running maybe eight feet along the base of the wall, joined by short vertical kerfs at intervals like the teeth of a comb. And above them, higher up where the light didn't reach well, more of the same—shallower, older, partially spalled over with mineral deposit, but there. You could see where somebody had come at this concrete with a wall saw or a wire rig, scored it deep, and then left it. Scored it and walked away.

Flood damage didn't look like that. Flood damage was spalls and star-cracks, water finding weakness, chaos with a geology degree. This was geometry. This was a man with a plan and a schedule, cutting a wall into removable pieces and leaving the pieces standing because removing them would have released the water before he was ready—or before whoever came after him was ready.

Odell knelt there a long time. His knees complained. He ignored them the way he ignored most of his body's correspondence these days.

He thought about the day the tunnel closed. He'd been thirty-one, working the Marmot lease two ranges over, and the news had come up the mountain on the radio: derailment inside the Cascade bore, cars folded like paper, fire, flood, the railroad sealing both portals within the month rather than dig the wreck out. Eleven dead. And a brakeman named Tomas Marchetti taken away in handcuffs while the ash was still settling, because the company needed a name to hang the disaster on and his was the cheapest one available.

He thought about a girl of maybe fifteen standing at the rail yard fence watching her father loaded into a state car, and about the woman that girl had become, and about the unspoken rule that had governed this crew for longer than some of its members had been alive: *no jobs above the old Cascade line.* Not discussed. Not debated. Just true, the way gravity is true.

And now here was geometry, cut into the wall behind the bulkhead, old enough to rust but nowhere near old enough to be original construction. The builders' tool marks ran perpendicular to these. These ran with intent.

Somebody had prepared this wall for demolition. Somebody had done it years ago—five, ten, he couldn't say without coring samples, but the rust bloom said not twenty. And the only reason to prepare a demolition you don't execute is if you're waiting for something. A permit. A price. A patsy.

A salvage crew with a desperate payroll and a boss whose name opened doors.

Odell stood up. His hands were steady, which surprised him. Underground, panic was a luxury item, and apparently the habit still held.

He climbed back through the personnel lock, dogged the door behind him, and stood in the gray morning light breathing hard—not from the climb.

Then he went to find Lena. Only Lena.

* * *

He caught her at the fuel point, checking the drum levels herself because she never asked anyone to count what she wouldn't count. When she saw his face she set down the dipstick and stepped away from the crew's earshot, back toward the treeline, and waited. Twenty years of working together meant she knew the difference between Odell coming to report and Odell coming to tell her something.

"Show me," she said, before he'd said a word.

He led her back through the lock. She ducked in behind him, headlamp on, and he watched her face in the yellow island light at station marker 4 as she crouched and saw what he'd seen.

She didn't touch the wall. She looked for a long time, moving the lamp slow, tracing each kerf line end to end.

"How old?" she asked finally. Her voice was level. He'd heard that level voice twice before—once over a man's body after a winch failure, once the night the bank called the note on her house. It was the voice she used when feeling would be expensive.

"Rust says five years, give or take. Could be less if the water's been high. Could be more if whoever cut it cleaned up after themselves." He crouched beside her, joints cracking. "But it's not flood, Lena. Flood doesn't cut combs. This is staged demolition. Somebody scored this wall into panels and left it standing, waiting."

"Waiting for what?"

"For somebody to come finish it. Or—" He stopped, because the next part was the part that mattered, and he wanted to say it clean. "Or the cutting is the point. You score a wall like this, you've changed its strength. Those kerfs are stress risers. If the water rises high enough, or the pressure spikes, this section lets go right along those lines instead of holding the way intact concrete would. Whoever cut this built a fuse into the bulkhead. They just haven't lit it yet."

Lena was quiet. Down the gallery, the water leaned against the far side of the wall, patient as arithmetic.

"Vane's survey maps," she said at last. "I told you about the mark. Spot's nowhere near any collapse a train wreck would've left. I couldn't make it fit."

"It fits here." Odell put his finger on the wall, between two vertical kerfs. "This is what he paid us to uncover. Or what somebody paid him to uncover—I'm not saying I know which. But a man doesn't draw a target on a map for a spot he's never seen. Somebody showed him this."

"Five years ago, maybe. When the lantern was new." She was thinking aloud, fast, the way she did. "Teo found that lantern in the debris at the portal mouth. Five years old, maybe less. Someone's been in and out of this tunnel since it was sealed. Through the south portal, probably, before the slide took it. Servicing the cuts. Keeping them open. Keeping the fuse dry."

"And then hiring a crew to lower the water for him." Odell let that sit. "We're doing the dangerous half of somebody else's demolition, Lena. For wages."

She turned and looked at him, and in the lamplight her face was the face of the girl at the rail yard fence, aged twenty years and armored in the living of them.

"Who else knows?"

"Nobody. I came straight to you."

"Keep it that way. For now." She stood, and he stood with her. "If it goes through the crew, it goes through the camp, and if it goes through the camp it goes down the mountain, and I want to know exactly what Vane does when he finds out we know before I decide how much he gets told."

"You think he doesn't already know we'd find it?"

"I think a man who plans five years ahead plans for that too." She killed her lamp, and the gallery went dark except for the work lights and the sound of the water. "Here's what we do. We keep pumping. We keep the schedule he's paying for. But we core a sample out of this wall today—you and me, nobody else—and we photograph everything, and the photos go somewhere that isn't this camp. And when the water's down and we can see what's really behind this bulkhead, we decide together whether we're salvagers or witnesses. Because I don't intend to be the third thing."

"What's the third thing?"

"The people who helped light the fuse."

They came back out through the lock into the morning. The crew was moving freight, hollering, the pumps hammering their steady four-count. Everything looked the same. Nothing was.

Lena stopped at the portal arch and looked back once at the black seam of the personnel lock, and Odell watched her file it away in whatever room she kept the things that hurt.

"Odell," she said quietly. "My father spent twenty years being blamed for a cut brake line. Somebody paying a witness to stay quiet. Somebody arranging a story instead of telling the truth." Her jaw worked. "I signed that contract telling myself it was steel and water. So you tell me straight, because you're the only man on this mountain who was underground long enough to know. What we just looked at—is that the same kind of thing? Is somebody building another story in there?"

Odell looked at the portal, at the dark behind the bulkhead, at the mountain holding its breath around ninety thousand tons of kept water.

"Lena," he said, "I think somebody's been writing in that tunnel for five years. And I think we just got hired to turn the page."

Behind them, faint under the pump noise, the water shifted against the wall—a long groan of pressure settling, or testing, or counting.

Station marker 4 held. For now.
