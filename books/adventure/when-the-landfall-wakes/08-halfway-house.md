## 8. Halfway House

The resupply run was supposed to be Odell's job.

He'd drawn the short straw fair and square—three straws in a coffee can, Teo's hand shaking with cold when he pulled the long one and whooped like a boy at a fair. But two days before the truck was due back up the gorge, Odell's knee went bad on him, the old mining injury that flared whenever the barometer dropped, and the barometer had been dropping for a week straight. He could barely make it from the cook shack to the generator shed without stopping twice to swear at it.

So it was Lena who drove down to Halfway House alone, which suited her fine. Six hours behind the wheel of the crew's battered flatbed, just her and the switchbacks and the radio hissing static between stations, nobody asking her anything, nobody watching her face while she thought about things she didn't want to think about.

The lantern was still bothering her. Five years old, maybe less. Somebody had been inside the Cascade Tunnel recently, and Vane had looked her dead in the eye and lied about it—or worse, hadn't lied at all, had simply talked over the question until it drowned in schedules and penalty clauses. She'd spent three nights turning it over. Every answer she came up with was worse than the last one.

The road down was mud soup after the week's rain. She took the switchbacks slow, chains clanking, the gorge dropping away beside her in gray veils of mist. By the time she rolled into Halfway House—a gas station, a diner, a freight depot, and eleven buildings pretending to be a town—the light was going amber and the mountains behind her had gone blue-black.

Marta Ruiz ran the depot the way other people ran fortresses. Seventy years old, forearms like dock rope, and a memory for freight manifests that had made more than one railroad auditor weep. She met Lena on the loading dock with a clipboard already in hand.

"Marchetti." A nod. "Your crate came up on Tuesday's mixed. Been sitting under canvas so it wouldn't swim."

"You're an angel, Marta."

"I'm a businesswoman. Angel's a different union." She walked Lena down the dock, reading off the manifest as they went. "Diesel filters, two cases. Chain, forty foot. Your coffee—don't thank me yet, half of it's that instant swill because the good stuff's back-ordered. Medical resupply. And mail."

"Mail?"

"Your man Odell put in a forwarding order through the county office. Anything addressed to the crew comes here now." Marta's mouth did something complicated. "There's a box."

Lena stopped walking. "A box."

"A small one. No return address. Postmarked Millbrook." Millbrook was the county seat, ninety miles east, where the courthouse sat and the permit hearings happened and Cassius Vane's lawyers kept offices with brass plaques. "It's addressed to you. Not the crew. You, personally, by name, at a worksite that isn't supposed to exist on any public schedule."

Lena looked at the crate sitting under its tarp. Standard shipping crate, stenciled with the supply house's logo, strapping intact, seals unbroken. Everything exactly as it should be.

Except somebody had known where to send a letter.

* * *

She didn't open it on the dock. Marta watched her load it into the cab and said nothing, which from Marta was a speech. Lena bought diesel and coffee and a hot plate of whatever the diner was calling stew, ate half of it, and drove back up into the clouds with the box riding shotgun like a passenger.

She told herself she'd wait until camp. Tell Odell first. Open it in daylight with witnesses, the way you'd open something suspicious, because that's what it was—suspicious, unsolicited, addressed with a precision that meant someone had done their homework.

Instead she pulled over at the third switchback, where the road widened for logging turnouts and the river showed itself far below, white thread in a black gorge. Engine idling. Rain ticking the windshield. She cut the strapping with her pocketknife and lifted the lid.

Inside: a spare fuel pump gasket she'd actually ordered, wrapped in newspaper to keep it from rattling. And under the newspaper, folded twice, a clipping.

Not original newsprint. A photocopy, slightly skewed, the kind any library or county archive machine would spit out for a dime. The masthead read *Millbrook Courier-Register*, dated twenty-two years back—two years before the derailment—and the headline said:

**RAILROAD INSPECTOR CLEARS CASCADE LINE AHEAD OF HEAVY SPRING SERVICE**

Below it, a photograph. Her father stood on the ballast beside the north portal, younger than she could easily bear, hard hat pushed back, one hand resting on the rail like it was a horse he trusted. The article praised the inspection. Praised *him*. Quoted him at length about brake systems and winter stress on the old rolling stock.

Someone had circled things in red ink. The date of the inspection—eleven days before the wreck. A line in the third paragraph: *Inspector Elias Marchetti flagged concerns regarding brake-line wear on the mountain division's legacy cars, recommending replacement before the spring schedule.*

And at the bottom, in the same red ink, a single circled name from the list of officials quoted in support of the inspection program:

**A. KOWALCZYK — Regional Safety Board**

Lena sat with the engine running and the rain coming down and read the clipping four times. Her father's handwriting wasn't on it. The red ink wasn't his—too heavy, too deliberate, the circles closed with a ruler-straight confidence he never had. This was someone else's annotation. Someone else's research.

Someone knew.

Not guessed. Knew. Knew enough to find a two-decade-old local story about her father, knew enough to send it to her at a work site that existed on no public manifest, knew enough to circle a name she'd never heard and trust that she'd understand why it mattered.

She turned the clipping over. On the back, in pencil, block letters:

**ASK HER WHAT SHE SAW.**

No signature. No threat. Just that, sitting there like a lit fuse.

* * *

She drove the rest of the way up with the clipping in her jacket pocket, against her chest, and hated herself for how carefully she'd folded it, as if it were something precious instead of something aimed.

Camp had settled into its evening rhythm by the time she rolled in—generators humming, the smell of Teo's curry experiment fighting the smell of wet canvas, headlamps bobbing between the pump shed and the portal. The north bulkhead loomed above the worksite, black water standing behind it, patient as a held breath. They'd dropped the level another meter that day. Odell's numbers said nine more days to working clearance inside the adit.

Odell met her at the flatbed, limping, holding out a hand for the manifest. "Road treat you right?"

"Road treated me like road does." She handed over the paperwork, kept the box. "Mail came."

"Anything good?"

"There's a letter. Addressed to me."

Something shifted in his face—not surprise, exactly. More like a man hearing a sound he'd been waiting for. "Bring it to the cook shack," he said. "Teo's making enough food for six and we're only five. Might as well give him an audience."

"It's my mail, Odell."

"It is." He looked at her steadily, rain dripping off the brim of his hat. "And if somebody's mailing things up this mountain with your name on them, I'd take it kindly if I saw it before you decided what to do alone. That's all. Your call after."

That was Odell. He never grabbed the wheel. He just made sure you knew he was in the truck.

* * *

They opened it at the long table under the cook shack's tarp roof, rain drumming overhead, steam rising off Teo's pot. Lena laid the clipping flat where everyone could see it and told them the whole thing in order: the postmark, the no return address, the red circles, the pencil on the back.

Silence, except the rain.

Priya read it twice, lips moving. She was their surveyor, the youngest of the old guard, and she had a mind like a transit—nothing got past her unmeasured. "This is a photocopy," she said finally. "Library quality. County archive keeps the *Courier-Register* on microfilm. Anybody with ten dollars and an afternoon could pull this."

"So it's not a leak from inside the railroad," Teo said. "It's—" He frowned. "What is it? Is it a warning or a help?"

"That's the question, isn't it." Odell turned his mug slowly on the table. "Somebody sends you your own father's name in the paper, circled, with instructions. Could be a friend. Could be bait."

"Bait for what?" Lena asked. "We're already here. We're already doing the job. What's left to catch?"

"Maybe the job isn't what somebody thinks it is," Priya said quietly. "Maybe they think you're here for Vane. Maybe they're hoping you're not."

Lena looked at the circled name again. **A. KOWALCZYK.** Regional Safety Board, twenty-two years ago, quoted supporting an inspection program her father had run with everything he had—two years before the same system buried him under a derailment and a verdict.

Ada Kowalczyk. If she was still alive, she'd be seventy-something. Retired, probably. Somewhere in the valley counties, living a life that had nothing to do with mountain rails.

Unless it did.

"The person who sent this knows why I'm really here," Lena said. "Or thinks they do. Either way, they know something about the wreck we don't. And they sent it to me, not to Vane, not to the crew. Me."

"Which means they know who your father was," Odell said. "Which means they've been paying attention longer than this week."

Teo had gone pale around the mouth. "So somebody's been watching us. Since before we even got up here."

"Since before the contract, maybe." Priya tapped the date on the masthead. "You don't pull a clipping like this on a whim. You pull it when you've been building a file."

The rain picked up, hammering the tarp, and somewhere down the slope the river answered it, louder than it had been all week. Lena noticed Odell noticing too. The river was rising. It was always rising, this time of year. But lately it sounded like it was in a hurry.

"What do we do?" Teo asked.

Lena folded the clipping along its existing creases and put it back in her jacket. "We keep pumping. We keep the schedule. Nobody talks about this outside this table, and nobody mentions it on the radio—assume every frequency is heard." She looked around at them, these people who had followed her onto a job she'd sworn off, and felt the weight of the clipping like a second heartbeat. "And when the water's low enough, I'm going to need a day. There's somebody I have to go see."

Odell nodded once, like a man confirming a bearing he'd already plotted. "Kowalczyk," he said.

"You know the name?"

"I know the Safety Board roster from back then. My father worked the mountain division thirty years." His jaw tightened. "Everybody knew everybody. Ada Kowalczyk signed off on half the safety filings in this corridor." He paused. "Including, if I remember right, some paperwork connected to the Cascade inquest."

The rain came down harder. Down in the dark behind the bulkhead, the water waited, and somewhere below it, in the cold and the black, the wreck sat arranged by hands that had never been identified—hands that might, if Lena read this right, belong to someone still alive, still writing letters, still circling names in red ink.

Someone outside the crew knew why she was here.

And tomorrow, first light, she was going to find out who.
