## 9. The Witness

The clipping said *Ada Kowalczyk, milepost 41.2, homestead above the gorge*, and the clipping had been slipped into a resupply crate by someone who knew things they shouldn't. Lena didn't tell the crew where she was going. She told Odell she was walking the old telegraph line to check for washouts, which was even true, in the way a lie is true when it borrows real boots and a real direction.

She left before dawn with a day pack, the clipping folded in her breast pocket against her heart like a warrant, and her father's old compass that pointed true north and nothing else.

The trail climbed out of the river gorge through timber that hadn't been logged since the wreck. Twenty years of deadfall made the going slow, and the frost made it treacherous, and Lena was glad of both. Glad of anything that slowed her down and gave her time to rehearse what she'd say. She'd started a dozen openings on the switchbacks—*I'm Elias Marchetti's daughter*; *I need to ask you about the night train*; *you were there*—and abandoned every one of them. None of them were right. All of them assumed Ada Kowalczyk owed her something, and Lena had learned the hard way that nobody owed anybody anything up here. The mountain settled its own accounts.

Milepost 41.2 still stood, or half of it did—a cast-iron post leaning out of the alder brush like a drunk leaning on a fence. The telegraph wire ran from it into the trees, snapped decades ago, trailing in the moss. Beyond it, the trail broke out onto a bench of land above the gorge, and there was the homestead: a cabin with a sagging porch, a root cellar built into the hillside, a garden gone to bramble, and a woodpile stacked with a precision that said somebody still lived here and still cared about some things.

Smoke stood straight up from the chimney. No wind this high. The morning was so clear Lena could see the far wall of the gorge and, far downstream, a thread of white water that would be the Cascade outflow—the water they'd spent two weeks pumping out of the dark.

She stood at the edge of the clearing a long moment. A dog should have barked by now. Recluses kept dogs. Then she saw why none came: an old border collie, gray-muzzled, lying on the porch boards, watching her with professional disinterest. It thumped its tail once, decided she wasn't worth the effort, and put its head back down.

"Your dog's lazy," Lena called.

"I know." The voice came from behind the woodpile, dry as bark. "He's earned it."

Ada Kowalczyk came around the split cedar with a hatchet in one hand and a round of birch under her arm. She was smaller than Lena expected, and older—seventy, maybe more—but she moved like someone who'd stopped being afraid of her own body a long time ago. She looked at Lena the way you look at weather coming over a ridge. Taking its measure. Deciding whether to bring in the laundry.

"You're not a hiker," Ada said. "Hikers don't come up the telegraph line. Hikers come up the road until the road quits and then they complain."

"No," Lena agreed.

"Railroad, then." Not a question. Her eyes went to Lena's hands—rope-scarred, diesel-stained—and then to her face, and stayed there. Something moved behind them. Recognition, maybe, or the ghost of it. "You've got his jaw," she said. "Elias Marchetti's daughter."

Lena's rehearsed openings all died at once. "You knew him?"

"I knew his face. Everybody on this line knew his face. He used to stop here when the northbound ran late and I'd give him coffee and he'd pretend my pie wasn't terrible." She set the birch down on the chopping block but kept the hatchet, holding it the way other people hold cigarettes. "I wondered how long it'd take one of his to come up here. Twenty years is longer than I figured. You must have found something."

"Why do you say that?"

"Because nobody walks six hours uphill to visit an old woman out of sentiment." She nodded at the clipping showing in Lena's pocket. "And because somebody sent you. That's the part I don't like. Sit down. Coffee's on. It's terrible too."

* * *

The cabin smelled of woodsmoke and dried herbs and, underneath, faintly, of machine oil—old habits. The walls were papered with maps. Not railroad maps. Weather maps, hand-drawn, decades of them, barometric lines marching across the Cascades in faded ink. Ada poured two cups without asking and sat across the table, and the collie followed them in and lay down between them like a chaperone.

Lena set the clipping on the table, unfolded. The inquest notice. The witness list. One name circled in pencil.

Ada looked at it for a long time. She didn't touch it.

"Somebody circled that," she said finally. "Not you. Your hands were full of rope when you read it—I can tell by how careful you're being with it now." She took a slow breath. "You want to know what I saw."

"I want to know why you weren't at the inquest."

"That's the same question wearing a different coat." Ada wrapped both hands around her cup. Outside, the light climbed the far wall of the gorge, gilding the scree fields one band at a time. When she spoke again, her voice had gone flat and even, the voice of someone reciting something they'd recited alone for twenty years. "Night of the eleventh. I couldn't sleep—bad knee, storm coming. I went out to the porch around eleven. The 9:40 freight was late, which it always was, but I could hear it working up the grade from the tunnel approach. Slow. Straining. And then it stopped."

"Stopped?"

"Braked. Hard enough that I heard the shoes squeal from here. Right about mile forty-one, just below my place. Then nothing. Five minutes of nothing, which is wrong—a freight doesn't sit on a four-percent grade for five minutes. Then I heard voices. Two, maybe three men. Shouting, low. And then a sound I've only heard twice in my life, and the other time was in the war." She looked up. "A wrench on a fitting. Big one. The kind they use on brake rigging. Metal on metal, deliberate, three distinct turns. Then the freight let its brakes off and rolled."

Lena's hands had gone still around her cup. "You heard them cut the brakes."

"I heard them *loosen* them. Cut comes later—that was the story the company told. Sabotage with a cutting torch, dramatic, criminal, blame somebody. But torches are loud and bright and leave evidence. Three turns of a wrench is quiet and leaves nothing, if the man doing it knows his trade." Her mouth twisted. "And then the train rolled down into the tunnel approach and the world ended, and everybody came running, and I stood on my porch with my hand over my mouth and said nothing."

"Why?" It came out harder than Lena intended. "You could have—"

"I know what I could have." Ada's eyes came up, and there was no flinching in them, and that was somehow worse than tears. "Two days later a man from the insurer came up this trail. Gray suit, city shoes, ruined them coming up here, which I enjoyed. He sat where you're sitting and he told me a story. He said the investigation was going to find sabotage regardless of what any witness said, because the company needed it to be sabotage—an act of God or an act of a man, either way, not an act of maintenance budgets and deferred inspections, which is what it actually was. He said if I testified to wrenches and voices, I'd be contradicting the finding, and contradicting the finding would get the statement thrown out and me labeled unreliable, and—" he paused, and here her voice finally cracked, just once, a hairline fracture—"and he mentioned my son."

Lena felt the floor tilt. "You had a son?"

"Had. Stefan worked the section gang out of Cascade. Nineteen years old. His name was on a list the man put on my table—not accused, just *listed*. Men whose employment might become inconvenient during the inquiry. He said testimony from the mother of a listed man carries a certain weight, and the weight could go either way." Ada turned her cup a quarter turn on the table, precisely, like a valve. "Stefan never touched that train. But I didn't know that for certain, did I? That's the genius of it. They don't threaten you. They just make sure you can't be sure. And I had buried a husband already and I could not—" She stopped. Set the cup down. Pressed her palms flat on the table until they stopped shaking. "He left an envelope. Four hundred dollars. In 1953 money. I threw it in the stove and he picked it out again, unhurt, because of course he'd brought two. They always bring two."

"And you took the second one."

"And I took the second one." She said it steadily, but her hands stayed flat on the table, pinned there like specimens. "And I have taken a payment every year since. Anniversary week. Same amount, adjusted for inflation—they're punctual, I'll give them that. A check, no letter, no return address, cashable at the county bank where the teller has instructions not to ask. Twenty years of hush money for five minutes of listening on a porch." She finally looked up, and her eyes were dry and furious and ancient. "So don't you sit there and think I don't know what I am. I know exactly what I am. I'm the reason your father stood in a dock alone while three men walked away with their wrenches."

"They never caught who did it?"

"The inquest didn't want catchers. It wanted a culprit, and your father was convenient—he'd filed a brake inspection report that contradicted the maintenance schedule, which made him either a hero or a saboteur depending on which way the company wanted the wind. They chose saboteur. His report became evidence *against* him. Do you understand that? The man wrote down that the brakes were failing and they used his honesty as the noose." Ada's voice rose and then she mastered it, dragged it back down. "I read the papers. Every year, anniversary week, when the checks came, I read everything about your father. Where they sent him. What it cost him. I kept the clippings in a tin."

"Where's the tin?"

"Burned. Ten years ago. Couldn't stand looking at them." She paused. "That's a lie. It's under the floorboards by the stove. I couldn't stand *not* looking at them."

Lena got up before she knew she was moving. The board came up easy—it had been pried often. The tin inside held clippings, yes, decades of them, yellowed and soft at the folds. And beneath them, flat as a wafer, a leather folder gone stiff with age.

She opened it on the table. A sworn statement, handwritten, dated three days after the derailment. Witnessed by a justice of the peace whose name meant nothing anymore. *I, Adela Kowalczyk, residing at milepost 41.2, do attest...* It was all there. The braking. The five minutes. The voices. The three turns of the wrench. Signed. Witnessed. Never filed.

"You made a legal statement," Lena said. Her voice sounded far away to herself. "You had it witnessed. Why didn't you file it?"

"Because filing it killed Stefan's prospects and maybe Stefan, and because I told myself next year, next year, and then twenty years went by and next year stopped meaning anything." Ada looked at the folder, and her face did something complicated—grief and fury braided so tight you couldn't tell which strand was which. "Then last month a check came early. With a letter. First letter in twenty years. Asking me to confirm, in writing, that I remembered *nothing* about the night of the eleventh. Offering me a great deal of money to sign a statement of non-memory." Her eyes came up sharp. "Somebody is getting ready to make sure the record stays closed, Miss Marchetti. Permanently. And they wanted my silence certified before whatever they're planning happens. So I burned the letter and I did not sign, and I have been sitting up here ever since, waiting to see who they'd send next."

The wind had come up while they talked. It moved through the clearing and rattled the porch boards, and somewhere below, far off, the river ran loud with spring melt.

Lena looked down at the sworn statement, at her father's name in the opening line of the accusation section, at the date. Twenty years of silence, sealed with a signature and a stamp, sitting in a tin under a floorboard while a man rotted in disgrace and died in it.

"Come down the mountain," Lena said. "Come to the hearing. File this."

Ada was quiet a long moment. She reached out and touched the folder, lightly, the way you touch a door you haven't opened in twenty years.

"I'm seventy-three years old," she said. "My knee is bad, my dog is lazy, and I have spent two decades being paid to forget the worst night of my life." She drew the folder toward her, squared its edges on the table, precise as a valve. "Give me one good reason to spend what's left of my courage on your father's ghost."

Lena thought of the bulkhead, and the fresh lantern rust, and the survey map with its mark in an impossible place, and the clipping slipped into a crate by hands unknown.

"Because whoever sent that letter," she said, "is already moving. And when they move, they won't leave witnesses loose—even certified ones. This isn't about courage, Ada. It's about being alive when the truth gets told."

Outside, far down the gorge, a sound rolled up through the trees—deep, percussive, wrong. Not thunder. The sky was clear.

Ada's head came up. The collie was on its feet, hackles raised, staring downriver.

"What," Ada said quietly, "was that?"

Lena was already moving for the door. "That," she said, "is my crew's blasting schedule. And we don't have one today."
