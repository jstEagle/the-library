## 10. Cut Lines

The rain caught them two miles below the witness's cabin and followed them all the way back up the gorge, needling through the truck's rusted roof seams like it had a grudge. Lena drove with the wipers on their fastest setting and still saw the road in slices—a flash of gravel, a flash of drop-off, a flash of the river running high and brown below.

Ada Kowalczyk's words rode in the cab with her, heavier than the mail crate they'd loaded at Halfway House.

*I watched him cut those lines. I was twenty-three years old and I watched him do it, and then a man in a good coat handed me four hundred dollars and told me what silence was worth.*

Twenty years of silence. And now a sworn statement folded in oilcloth inside Lena's jacket, against her ribs, warm as a coal.

She'd stopped at Halfway House on the way down to meet Ada, alone—told the crew she had personal business, told Odell only that it concerned her father. He hadn't asked more. That was the thing about Odell: he knew which questions were load-bearing and left them standing. But when she'd come back out of Ada's cabin with her face doing whatever it was doing, he'd looked at her across the truck bed for a long three seconds and said, "Whatever you found, it'll keep till we're dry."

It would keep. It had kept twenty years. But now there were photographs to take, measurements to log, a notebook to find—and six days left before Vane's next inspection, and water behind the bulkhead dropping an inch a day if the pumps held, and someone outside the crew who already knew why she was really here. Someone had slipped that inquest clipping into the resupply crate with a witness's name circled in grease pencil. Someone wanted her looking. Or wanted her seen looking.

She came around the last switchback above camp as full dark settled, and the first wrong thing hit her before she even understood it was wrong: no lights.

Camp should have been a scatter of yellow—the bunkhouse windows, the string work-lights over the tool shed, the blue pilot glow of the generator shed. Instead there was one light, a headlamp, swinging wild near the pump line, and a voice carrying up the slope in the flat shout people use when shouting is the only tool left.

"*Lena!*"

That was Odell. She killed the headlights and rolled down the grade on engine braking, because if something was wrong down there she didn't want to announce herself to it. The truck bumped onto the camp pad and she was out before it fully stopped, boots skidding in the churned mud.

"Here," she called. "Talk to me."

Odell's headlamp found her, blinding, then dropped. "Generator's dead. Not stalled—dead. Somebody got into the fuel line." His voice was level but his hands weren't; she could see them shaking at his sides, lit by the lamp's spill. "Somebody *cut* it, Lena. Clean cuts. And Teo was in the pump gallery when the lights went."

Her stomach dropped through the mud, through the mountain, all the way to the river.

"How long dark?"

"Forty minutes. We've been feeling our way along the pump line since. He went in to check the intake screen before the shift change—he does it every night, he doesn't wait for—" Odell stopped himself. "He's hurt. Marisol's with him. Gallery floor drops away past the second brace and in the dark he walked off the edge into the sump channel. Maybe eight feet down onto rock."

Lena was already moving. "Flashlights?"

"Two working. Batteries are in the generator shed and I'm not sending anyone in there blind until we know we're alone."

They weren't alone. She knew it the way you know weather coming. The cut fuel line told her that much: this wasn't the mountain turning on them. This was a person, with tools and time and a reason, and the reason had a shape she could almost see.

* * *

Teo was sitting up when they reached the pump gallery, which was the first mercy. Marisol had rigged a tarp over the sump channel mouth and wedged both flashlights so the beams crossed on him like stage lighting, and under that harsh white cross he looked younger than twenty-four and grayer than sixty, one leg stretched out at an angle legs don't go, his coveralls soaked black from the knee down.

"Don't," he said, before anyone spoke. "Don't do the face. It's not broke. Marisol checked, it's—"

"It's dislocated and you're lucky," Marisol said. She was kneeling in the muck with her sleeves shoved up, hands steady, voice not. "Knee's out of the socket, ankle's likely fractured, and he's been sitting in forty-degree water for half an hour, so he's going into shock whether he likes it or not. I need heat, dry clothes, and something better than ibuprofen, in that order."

"The truck has blankets," Lena said. "And the diesel heater in the mess tent runs off a bottle. Get him moved, get him warm. Odell—"

"I'm on the generator." He hesitated. "You should see the cut first."

She should. She did.

In the generator shed, by flashlight, the sabotage was almost elegant. The fuel line from tank to engine had been severed in two places with a tubing cutter—clean, circular, professional—and the loose ends bent back and tucked under the frame so a casual glance showed nothing. Whoever did it had known generators. Had known *their* generator, or taken the time to learn it. On the concrete floor beneath the fuel filter, half wiped away by somebody's boot, was the ghost of a shoe print. Size eleven, deep lug tread. None of the crew wore lugs; the ground here ate them alive and everyone had switched to smooth-soled rubber weeks ago.

Vane's men had smooth-soled boots too. She'd noticed that the first week, the way you notice anything about men who stand too straight around heavy equipment. Which meant either Vane's people had done this—or someone wanted it to look that way.

Or Vane's people hadn't all gone down the mountain when he sent them.

She crouched there a long moment, flashlight beam steady on that print, and let herself think the ugly thought all the way through: *someone is on this mountain right now.* Someone who cut a fuel line knowing the pumps ran off the same circuit, knowing darkness at the north portal meant the water rose unwatched, knowing a man checking an intake screen in the black might walk off an edge. That wasn't theft. Theft doesn't aim. This aimed.

And underneath the fear, cold and clear as spring water, another thought: *Ada's statement is in my jacket, and whoever did this may know exactly what I brought back up the mountain.*

* * *

By midnight the mess tent was a field hospital that smelled of diesel heater and wet wool. Marisol had popped Teo's knee back in with a violence that made even Odell look away, splinted the ankle with a tent pole and duct tape, and buried him in blankets until only his face showed, chalk-white and furious at himself.

"I check that screen every night," he kept saying, to the ceiling, to nobody. "Every night. I know where the edge is. I *know* where the edge is."

"You know where the edge is with the lights on," Marisol said. "Nobody knows it in the dark."

Lena stood at the tent flap with a tin cup of coffee going cold in her hand and watched her crew take inventory of each other. Odell at the stove, feeding it bottle gas, moving with the deliberate calm of a man keeping his hands busy so his mind wouldn't run ahead of him. Marisol charting Teo's pulse on the back of a supply manifest. Ruiz and Chen dripping rainwater by the door, arguing in low voices about whether the cut line could have been stress failure—knowing it couldn't, arguing anyway, because arguing was how they burned off the thing they couldn't say yet.

Finally Chen said it anyway. "So. Are we saying it?"

Silence. The heater ticked.

"Somebody cut that line," Chen said. "Not weather, not wear. A person. A person who's probably still up here, and the nearest town is ninety minutes down a road that eats trucks, and our radio mast is powered by—" he gestured at the dark beyond the canvas, at everything the dead generator fed, "—that. So are we saying it, or are we all going to keep polishing our coffee cups?"

"We're saying it," Lena said.

She set the cup down and stepped into the middle of the tent, into the circle of lamplight, and told them. Not everything—not Ada, not the statement against her ribs—but enough. The clipping in the resupply crate. The witness name circled. The fresh lantern Teo had found at the portal, five years old in a tunnel sealed for twenty. The demolition cuts Odell had shown her in the bulkhead, deliberate as surgery. And now the fuel line, cut clean, tucked away, aimed.

"Someone has been inside that tunnel recently," she said. "Someone has been watching us since before we earned our first paycheck. And tonight they turned the lights off on a man walking a ledge in the dark. I'm not going to dress that up. That's not vandalism and it's not a warning shot. That's an attempt."

Teo, from his nest of blankets, quietly: "It was an attempt."

"It was an attempt," Lena agreed. "And here's what I keep coming back to. Vane wants that tunnel open on a schedule. Sabotage costs him schedule. So either Vane's lost control of his own operation—or the sabotage isn't against the job at all. It's against *us*. Against whatever we're close to finding." She let that sit. She watched Odell's eyes flick toward her jacket and away, giving nothing away, loyal as rebar. "I can't tell you which. I can't tell you who. What I can tell you is that I'm not leaving this mountain without what my father left in it, and I have no right to ask any of you to stay for that."

Ruiz shifted. "Lena—"

"No. Hear me out." Her voice came out rougher than she intended, and she let it. "First light, the truck goes down regardless—Teo needs a hospital, Marisol drives him, that's not negotiable, don't start. But after that, anybody who wants a seat on the return trip gets one. Full pay through the end of the contract. No questions, no hard feelings, and I will personally shake your hand at the bottom and mean it." She looked around the circle, at each of them, making herself hold it. "This started as a job. It isn't one anymore. It's my family's name and maybe my family's grave, and I dragged all of you into it for money we needed, and that's on me. So: free passage down. Anybody. Right now, say it, and it's done."

The heater ticked. Rain worked the canvas overhead.

Marisol spoke first, without looking up from her pulse chart. "I'm driving Teo down because he needs plates and pins, not because I'm leaving. I'll be back Thursday. Somebody save me a bunk."

"You're concussed-adjacent and you're driving a mountain road at dawn," Odell said.

"I'm the only one who hasn't been awake thirty hours."

"That's fair," Odell admitted.

Chen cracked his knuckles, one hand, then the other, a habit that meant he'd decided something. "My sister thinks I work construction in Reno. If I go home now I have to explain what I actually do, and honestly the tunnel is easier." A beat. "I'm staying, boss."

Ruiz shrugged like it cost him nothing, which fooled nobody. "Whoever cut that line used a tubing cutter. I want to see the tool marks up close, because I've got a theory about the bite width, and if I'm right, I know exactly whose kit we're looking for." He glanced at Lena. "Also, nobody leaves me alone in a tent with Chen. Staying."

Then it was Odell. He'd been quiet through all of it, working the stove valve, and now he turned around and wiped his hands slow on his rag like a man closing out a shift.

"Thirty-one years ago I came down out of the Kettle Creek mine with everybody I worked with dead behind me and a company that called it my fault," he said. "You know that. You hired me anyway, and you never once asked me to prove I wasn't what the record said." He folded the rag, laid it on the shelf, precise as a surgeon. "Your father's record says something false. I've known that since the day you signed Vane's contract, because nobody who raised you could've done what they say he did. So no, I'm not taking passage down. I'm staying to help you fix the record. That's the whole speech."

Lena's throat closed. She looked at Teo last, wrapped in blankets, gray-faced, leg splinted, the newest of them and the most recently thrown off a ledge into the dark.

"Teo," she said. "You especially. Nobody would think less—"

"Boss." He said it patiently, like she was the new hire. "I took this job because everywhere else I've worked, when things went bad, everybody scattered and the story ended with everybody scattered. First place I've ever been where the offer to leave got answered by five people staying." He tried to shrug and winced. "Besides. Whoever pushed the lights out on me owes me a knee, and I intend to collect."

Something in Lena's chest unlocked that had been locked since the bunkhouse, since the contract, maybe since the arrest she'd watched through a car window at fourteen. She nodded, once, because anything bigger would have broken her voice.

"All right," she said. "Then here's how we live through this. Watches around the clock, pairs, starting now—nobody walks anywhere alone, including to the latrine, I mean it, Ruiz. Generator's first light priority; Odell, splice that line and we guard the shed like it's payroll. Radio mast comes up second, and the moment it does, I'm calling down for a state police welfare check on Vane's crew roster—I want to know who's accounted for and who isn't. Marisol takes Teo down at dawn and brings back medical supplies, batteries, and a shotgun shell inventory I don't want to discuss in front of the tent."

"And you?" Odell asked quietly.

Lena put her hand flat against her jacket, against the oilcloth and the sworn statement sleeping inside it.

"Me," she said, "I'm going to figure out who else knows what I know. Because that clipping didn't climb a mountain by itself."

* * *

She took first watch herself, alone at the camp pad's edge, which violated her own rule and which she did anyway, because there was one thing she needed to do without witnesses.

Below the pad, the river ran loud in the dark, swollen with the day's rain. Above the north portal, half a mile off, the bulkhead stood invisible, holding back its black lake. Somewhere between the two, a size-eleven boot print was drying in a generator shed, and a man with a tubing cutter was either gone down the mountain or very patient.

Lena took out the oilcloth packet and opened it just enough to confirm what she already knew—that the paper was dry, that the signature was real, that Ada Kowalczyk's careful old handwriting said *I saw the brake lines cut with a hacksaw, and I saw who held the saw,* and gave a name.

A name Lena recognized. A name that appeared on Cassius Vane's own concession paperwork, in the list of original shareholders whose heirs still drew royalties.

The rain thinned. Far up the gorge, faint as a struck match, a light blinked once at the north portal—there, then gone, then there again. Deliberate. Rhythmic.

Three flashes. A pause. Three flashes.

Signal lamps. Someone at the portal was talking to someone else, and the someone else wasn't answering from the direction of camp.

Lena closed the oilcloth, slid it back against her ribs, and started up the slope toward the light, counting her own heartbeats like a fuse burning down.
