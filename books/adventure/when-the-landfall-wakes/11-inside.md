## 11. Inside

The pumps went quiet at 4:17 in the morning, and the silence was worse than the noise.

For nine days the bulkhead had groaned and shuddered under Odell's diesel pumps, spitting black water down the drainage cut in a rope that never seemed to thin. Lena had learned to sleep through it the way you sleep through a river outside your window—by convincing yourself it was permanent. Now it stopped, and the mountain felt like a held breath.

She stood at the north portal with a mug of coffee she wasn't drinking, watching the water line on the bulkhead timbers. It had dropped eleven feet since they'd started. The top third of the steel door stood clear of the flood, weeping rust and mineral stain down its face in long orange streaks like old blood let dry.

"Cutting torch is hot," Odell said behind her. He didn't ask if she was ready. He'd stopped asking on day four, when she'd caught him studying the demolition cuts in the bulkhead frame by lamplight and neither of them had said out loud what both of them were thinking: *somebody did this on purpose, and somebody came back after.*

"Teo?"

"Here." Teo stepped up beside her, arm still in the sling from the generator sabotage, his other hand holding a headlamp he'd already clicked on twice just to be sure. His face was pale in the pre-dawn gray but set. "I'm going in."

"You don't have to."

"I know." He looked at the door. "That's why I'm going."

Lena nodded. That was the whole argument, settled.

They'd chosen the entry team the night before, around the stove, without ceremony. Four people: Lena, Odell, Teo, and Marisol, who ran their winch work and had the steadiest hands of anyone Lena had ever met with a camera. Everyone else would stay topside on the winches and the radio. Nobody had argued about that either, which told Lena more about the crew's mood than any argument would have. They were scared. They were coming anyway—or at least, these four were, because someone had to go first and everyone trusted her to decide who.

*Her name opens doors,* Vane had said, back when she still thought he meant her reputation with rail commissions. She was starting to understand he'd meant something else entirely.

"Rules," she said, turning to face them. The three of them looked back—Odell with his gray stubble and his miner's calm, Marisol rolling her shoulders like before a heavy pull, Teo trying to look like his arm didn't hurt. "Nobody touches anything until Marisol photographs it. Nobody separates from the team. Air's been tested twice but we keep the monitors running anyway—if CO climbs or the oxygen dips, we walk out, no debate. And if I say the word *out*, we are all moving toward the portal before the word finishes leaving my mouth. Agreed?"

Three nods.

"One more thing." She made herself say it plainly. "Whatever's in there—and we know something's wrong in there—it's been twenty years. My father's been dead fourteen. Nothing in that tunnel is going to be what I want it to be. So nobody looks at me like I'm glass, and nobody pretends this is just another job. We see it straight. That's the whole deal."

Odell put one hand briefly on her shoulder, heavy as a coupling iron, and said nothing, which from Odell was a speech.

Lena turned to the bulkhead. "Light it up."

* * *

The cutting torch went through the steel door like a can opener through a tin, and the smell that came out nearly put them all on their knees.

It wasn't just standing water and rust. It was twenty years of drowned things—creosote, diesel, rot-soaked timber, the sweetish chemical reek of batteries that had died in the dark. Marisol gagged once and mastered it. Teo pulled his collar up over his nose. Odell breathed through his mouth with the practiced ease of a man who'd spent thirty years underground and considered bad air a personal acquaintance.

Behind the steel door was a timber bulkhead, and behind that, when they'd braced and pried the planks, was the tunnel itself.

Lena went through first.

The passage beyond was chest-deep empty now where it should have been—the water had drained down-slope toward the south portal, which was why Vane's engineers had insisted they open north first, and why the south end still held six feet of black lake behind its own failing seal. Her boots found gravel and old ties under an inch of slick silt. Her headlamp beam reached maybe forty feet before the tunnel swallowed it, the air so thick with suspended grit that the light seemed to have texture, to have weight.

The Cascade Tunnel ran a mile and a quarter through the shoulder of Mount Corben. She had grown up forty miles from this portal. She had never once stood inside it.

"Air monitor's green," Teo said softly behind her. "CO's fine. O-two's fine."

"Move up slow. Watch your footing—the ballast'll be shifted."

They advanced in a loose diamond, Lena at the point, headlamps swinging. The tunnel walls wept constantly, threads of water finding their way through a century of shot seams, and somewhere ahead water dripped into standing pools with a sound like a clock counting something. The rails ran ahead of them into the dark, twin lines of rusted shine, and walking between them felt like walking down the throat of the mountain itself.

Two hundred yards in, Odell said quietly, "Lena."

She stopped. Followed his beam.

A lantern hung from a bracket bolted to the tunnel wall. Not a carbide lamp, not a railroad lantern—a modern LED work light, the kind you'd buy at any supply house, its plastic housing cracked and furred with mold. Its battery pack lay on the tie below it, split open, cells bloomed white with corrosion.

Five years old, maybe less. Teo had said as much when he'd found the first one near the portal. This one confirmed it: someone had worked here recently, and had lit their way with equipment no salvage crew of twenty years ago ever carried.

"Somebody's been doing tours," Marisol murmured.

"Somebody's been doing something," Lena said. "Keep moving."

But her heart had changed rhythm. It wasn't fear exactly, though there was plenty of that. It was the feeling of approaching a room where you know the furniture has been rearranged—you can't say how you know, but every step closer makes your skin crawl harder.

At four hundred yards, the drip sound changed. Deeper. Echoing off something large.

At five hundred, her headlamp found the train.

* * *

There is a moment, Lena would think later, when awe and violation occupy the same heartbeat, and you cannot tell which one is breaking you.

The wreck filled the tunnel wall to wall.

The *Corben Flyer*, westbound, seven cars plus locomotive, derailed at speed in the dead center of the mountain. Twenty years of floodwater had not scattered it. That was the first wrong thing—she knew wrecks, she'd cleared three of them, and water moved wreckage, tumbled cars, sorted steel like a river sorts stone. These cars lay in a line, nose to tail along the curve of the tunnel, as neatly placed as cars in a yard. The locomotive sat upright on its side, drivers still attached, facing the direction it had been traveling. The first coach rested against it at a careful angle. Behind it, the second coach. Behind that, the baggage car. Each one positioned. Each one *arranged*.

"It's too neat," Odell said. His voice had gone flat, the voice he used for reporting numbers. "Derailments aren't neat. I've seen a hundred. Cars jackknife, they telescope, they pile. This looks—" He stopped.

"Staged," Lena finished.

Marisol was already working, camera clicking, flash strobing off wet steel. "Photographing everything. Don't touch."

Teo's lamp swept across the second coach's windows, and he made a small sound.

The windows were broken—but broken inward, punched deliberately, all eight of them, in a pattern too regular to be chance. And through the nearest opening, in the flashlight wash, Lena could see the interior of the car, and the interior was wrong too. Seats had been removed. Not thrown—removed, unbolted, stacked in a tidy pile at the car's far end. The floor beneath was scoured clean in lanes, as if someone had walked it hundreds of times with a shovel or a detector.

"Someone searched this car," she said. "Thoroughly. More than once."

"The bonds," Teo whispered. Then, catching himself: "Sorry. I just—Vane's maps. The spot he kept marking. It's—"

"I know where it is." She did. She'd done the arithmetic herself two nights ago, lying awake: the X on Vane's survey sheets sat a hundred yards past this wreck, deep in the tunnel, nowhere near any plausible collapse point. He hadn't hired them to reopen a tunnel. He'd hired them to drain a vault.

But that was a problem for later, for daylight, for the surface. Right now the wreck had her, and she let it.

They moved along the line of cars slowly, Marisol shooting everything, Odell reading the steel the way he read everything—with a professional eye that missed nothing. At the third car he crouched at a wheel truck and beckoned her down.

"Look at the flange marks on the rail here." His glove traced the shiny scars on the railhead. "This wheelset left the rail right about here. But Lena—" he moved his lamp fifty feet back up the track, to a section of rail where the spikes had torn free in a long ragged row, "—the actual derailment started back there. See the spike pull? Something took this train off the rail back there, and then it rolled clean a hundred feet before the trucks started coming apart."

"What takes a train off the rail and lets it roll?"

"A broken rail. A spread gauge. Or—" he looked up at her, and his eyes in the lamplight were very tired, "—brakes locked at speed, dragging wheels flat, hammering the rail till something gives. Except locked brakes leave flat spots on the tires, and those tires are round as the day they were turned."

Lena stared at him. "You're saying the brake story doesn't hold."

"I'm saying whatever happened to this train, the physical evidence doesn't match the official story. Which we knew. Which is why your father—" He stopped himself, jaw tightening. "Which is why we're here."

She stood. Her legs felt strange, distant, like borrowed equipment.

Twenty years ago, a commission had ruled that engineer Tomas Marchetti's failure to control his brakes had killed eleven people in the Cascade Tunnel. They had taken his license, his name, and eventually, by slow degrees, his life. Lena had been nine years old. She had watched them lead him away in handcuffs while the neighbors watched from their porches, and she had built her entire adult self on top of that memory like a crew builds a trestle over a burned bridge—never looking down.

And now she stood inside the wound itself, and the wound was telling her the official story was physically impossible.

"Lena," Marisol called from the baggage car doorway. "You need to see this."

* * *

The baggage car had been the mail car, once. The placard holder still showed the ghost outline of RAILWAY MAIL SERVICE stenciling. Its doors stood open, and inside, the scene repeated the coaches' pattern but worse: shelving ripped out, sorting cases toppled, the floor swept into deliberate piles of debris along each wall. Someone had turned this car inside out looking for something.

But in the center of the car, sitting on a folding camp table that had no business existing anywhere in a twenty-year-old wreck, was a wooden crate.

Fresh pine. Clean corners. A shipping stencil on its side, barely faded: **C.V. ENTERPRISES — CASCADE SURVEY**.

"Oh," Teo said quietly. "Oh, that's—"

"Yeah," Lena said.

Vane had been inside. Recently. Recently enough to leave furniture. He'd run his own secret entries during the survey phase last fall, before the permits, before the contract, before he'd ever walked into their bunkhouse with cash in hand—draining water somehow, or entering some other way, or maybe the water level had been different then, and she filed that question away with all the others because right now Marisol's lamp had found the thing sitting on top of the crate.

A ledger. Water-swollen, mildewed, its cover stamped with the railroad's crest.

And beside it, weighted down with a rock, a single sheet of paper in a plastic sleeve, laid out flat, facing the door. Facing whoever came in. Facing, Lena understood with a cold drop in her stomach, *her*.

She picked it up.

It was a photocopy, old, third- or fourth-generation, the text soft with repetition. An inquest transcript page. Witness statement number six. And across the middle of it, in fresh red ink, someone had circled a single line:

*Witness states she observed the brake line coupling on the fourth car to be severed prior to departure, and reported same to yardmaster J. Pruitt, who declined to log the report.*

Below the transcript, in the same red ink, in a tight, precise hand she had never seen before but understood immediately, someone had written four words:

**ASK ADA KOWALCZYK WHY.**

The tunnel dripped. The air monitor beeped its steady green heartbeat. Somewhere far behind them, up the mile of flooded dark toward the south portal, water moved against a failing seal with a sound like slow applause.

Marisol photographed the paper without being asked. Odell read it over her shoulder and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that the paper wasn't already saying: *someone knows. Someone has known. Someone wanted you to find this.*

Not Vane. Vane marked maps and left camp tables; this note was addressed to her specifically, by name, through a witness she'd never heard of. Vane wanted bearer bonds. Whoever wrote this wanted something else entirely—and had used Vane's greed, and her father's shame, and eleven dead people, as the machinery to get it.

"Lena," Teo said. His voice cracked on it. "The air monitor."

She looked. The CO reading had ticked up—not dangerous, not yet, but climbing, a slow creep that meant ventilation somewhere was changing, or water somewhere was rising, or the mountain was simply exhaling after twenty years of held breath.

"We're done for today," she said, and was proud of how level it sounded. "Marisol, finish the crate and the ledger. Odell, mark our route. Teo, watch the gauge. We walk out slow and we walk out together."

They were fifty yards from the portal, the gray daylight of the entrance finally visible ahead, when her radio crackled.

Topside. Ruiz, on winch watch, and she had never heard Ruiz sound like this.

"Lena. You need to come up. Now." A breath. "There's a woman at the trailhead. Old lady, hiking alone, says she's got something to give you. Says her name is Kowalczyk."

Lena stopped in the mouth of the tunnel, half in the mountain's dark, half in the morning light, the photocopy burning in her pocket like a coal.

"And Lena," Ruiz said, "there's a second vehicle behind her. Black SUV. Been sitting there an hour. Plates come back to a law firm in the city."

Behind her, the tunnel dripped. Ahead of her, the world had started moving.

She walked out into the light.
