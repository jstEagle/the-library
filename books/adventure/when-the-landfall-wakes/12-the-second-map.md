## 12. The Second Map

The mail car was the third one in, wedged between a flattened boxcar and the locomotive's tender like a letter slipped into the wrong envelope. You'd never have found it by schedule or by logic—twenty years of water and silt had rearranged the train into something that obeyed no timetable—but Odell found it anyway, because Odell had spent thirty years reading wreckage the way other men read weather.

"Mail car," he said, tapping his knuckle against a rib of corroded steel where the reporting marks had been. Under the grime: *R.M. 4471*. "See the double wall? They armored these for the pay runs. Everything behind this skin should be dry."

It wasn't dry. Nothing on the mountain was dry. But when they cut the access panel with the plasma torch and pried the steel back like the lid of a tin, the air that came out was different—colder, staler, carrying the ghost of paper instead of rust.

Lena went in first. She always went in first; it was the one selfishness she allowed herself.

Inside, the dark was total until her headlamp cut it, and then it was worse than total, because the light showed her everything at once: canvas mail sacks slumped in their racks like sleeping animals, sorting shelves bowed under two decades of load, a clerk's stool tipped on its side, and everywhere the fine gray silt of standing water long gone, settled over every surface like the dust of a snowfall that had fallen indoors.

"Careful where you step," she said, though her voice came out lower than she intended. The space pressed close. It felt less like entering a room than entering a held breath.

Teo crowded in behind her, headlamp swinging. He'd been quiet since the lantern—five years old, not twenty—and quieter still since she'd told him what she'd found in Vane's survey maps: that X mark, dead center in the tunnel, nowhere near any collapse worth surveying. He moved now like a man in a church he suspected had been robbed.

"This is where the records would be," Teo said. "If there were records."

"There are records." Lena ran her glove along the nearest rack. The canvas was stiff as bark but intact. "Railroad kept duplicate manifests in the mail cars on mountain divisions. Insurance requirement. If my father filed anything about those brakes—if he wrote it down anywhere official—it would've traveled through this car."

She hadn't said that part out loud before. Saying it made it real, and real things could be taken away.

Odell's voice came through from outside, muffled by steel. "Water's down another foot. We've got maybe four hours before the seep starts climbing again. Whatever you're doing in there, do it."

* * *

They worked the racks methodically, because methodical was how you stayed alive in collapsed structures and how you kept from tearing something irreplaceable in half. Lena cut each sack open along its seam with a knife rather than ripping, and laid the contents out on a spread tarp: bundles of newspapers gone to pulp, seed catalogs fused into bricks, personal letters swollen soft as overripe fruit. The ink had mostly survived. The paper hadn't. It was like handling the skeletons of words.

Teo read dates aloud as they went. "March eleventh. March ninth. This whole bundle's from the week before—" He stopped himself. Everyone on the crew had learned to stop themselves at that date. *April second.* The day the Cascade Local went into the curve above the north portal doing sixty with no brakes, and the day the railroad needed someone to blame, and the day they found Antonio Marchetti's signature on the maintenance log and decided that was enough.

Lena turned a letter over in her gloved hands. A woman's handwriting, looping and patient. *Dear Tomas, I hope the mountain is treating you better than last winter—*

She set it down gently, face up, so the name wouldn't be pressed into the mud.

It was on the fourth rack, third sack from the end, that her knife hit something that wasn't paper.

Metal. Flat, rectangular, hinged. She drew it out with both hands: an oilskin-wrapped field case, the kind the railroad issued to roadmasters and inspectors, the kind her father had carried for nineteen years before the twentieth killed him. The clasp was green with corrosion but the oilskin had done its work. When she thumbed it open, the notebook inside fell open to a page dated three days before the crash, and her own breath stopped working.

Her father's hand. She'd know it anywhere—the narrow letters, the way he crossed his sevens, the little checkmarks he used instead of underlines. She'd grown up watching him make them at the kitchen table, logging axle counts and rail temps while she did homework across from him.

The entry read:

*3/29 — Ran the Local down from the summit with J. Perrault driving. Dragging brakes on car 6 audible from the yard. Not adjustment — wear. Lining shot through on the lead truck. Flagged for replacement. Perrault agrees. Filed with dispatch.*

Below it, another entry, the next day:

*3/30 — Car 6 lining NOT replaced. Parts requisition denied — cost. Re-flagged. This is not a maybe. If they run this consist heavy over the grade before the swap, it will not stop.*

And below that, in harder strokes, the pen nearly through the paper:

*3/31 — Told Hargrove directly. In writing. His response attached.*

A carbon copy was clipped to the page, and even through the fog of twenty years Lena could read the reply from the division superintendent: *Noted. Proceed as scheduled. Do not file further maintenance objections on revenue consists without regional authorization.*

*Do not file further objections.* Her father had been ordered to shut up about the brakes nine days before nine people died, and then the railroad had used his honest logbook—the logbook that proved he knew, that proved he'd tried—to hang him. Because a man who flagged a fault and got ignored looked negligent. A man who flagged a fault and got ignored looked like the man who should have stopped the train.

"Lena." Teo's voice, very careful. "You're shaking."

"I'm fine." She wasn't. She was twenty-four years old and eight years old at once, standing in her father's grave, holding the proof that he had died shouting into a system designed not to hear him. "Bag it. Every page, photographed before we move anything. Odell's rules."

"He's going to want to see this."

"No." The word came out flat and hard, and she heard herself and couldn't soften it. "Nobody sees this yet. Nobody outside this car. You understand me, Teo? This doesn't go in the report, doesn't go in the camp ledger, doesn't get mentioned at dinner. If Vane learns what this is before I decide what it's worth—"

She stopped. Because saying it made the shape of the trap visible, and she'd been walking inside its outline for six weeks without looking down.

Vane hadn't hired the best salvage crew in three counties because of their reputation. He'd hired *her*. Lena Marchetti, whose name was still on the old incident files, whose family name still opened the archive drawers in the county courthouse that Cassius Vane, outsider, concession-holder, man-with-money-and-deadline, could never open alone. She'd thought the job was the tunnel. The tunnel was the excuse. Somewhere in this wreck—maybe in this very car—was whatever Vane actually wanted, and he'd known she was the key to reaching it.

The X on his survey map. Dead center. Right about here.

"Photograph everything," she said quietly. "Then we find out what else is in this car."

* * *

What else was in the car took them two hours to reach, because the rear third of the mail car had folded inward when the boxcar behind it accordioned, and the only way through was a gap eighteen inches wide between a sorting shelf and the ceiling buckled down to knee height.

Lena went through first again. On the far side, the silt lay undisturbed.

That was the thing she noticed before anything else, and it raised every hair on her arms. The front of the car had been buried, flooded, sifted over—honest damage, honest time. But back here, past the fold, the floor of the car was clean in long swept-looking lanes, and the mail sacks weren't slumped randomly. Three of them had been stacked. Deliberately. Neatly. Against the torn bulkhead like someone had been using them for a workbench.

Someone had been in here. Recently. Working.

"Teo," she called back through the gap. "Light."

He fed his lamp beam through, and it found what she'd already begun to fear: fresh tool marks on a floor hatch set into the car's belly—a baggage-door access, the kind that opened onto the undercarriage space where high-value freight rode on mountain divisions. The padlock was new. Brass, bright, uncorroded. Somebody had locked a hatch inside a sealed tunnel full of floodwater, which meant somebody had pumped that section dry, come in, done their work, locked up, and left.

Five-year-old lantern. Swept floors. Stacked sacks. New brass lock.

"Okay," Teo said, and his voice cracked on it. "Okay, so we're leaving now, right? Tell me we're leaving now."

Lena crouched at the hatch. The lock was a cheap thing, and her bolt cutters were ten feet away through an eighteen-inch gap, and none of that mattered, because her hands had already decided. Her father had been blamed for this wreck. Someone else had staged it—that was the word rising in her now, cold and certain, *staged*—and someone had come back, five years ago or five months ago, and started digging in its guts.

She was reaching for the lock when the light changed.

Not the lamps. The air. A draft, faint and wrong, moving through the car from the direction of the south portal, carrying diesel exhaust and cold river smell and the particular silence of men trying not to be heard.

Teo's headlamp snapped toward the gap. Beyond it, through the torn bulkhead and the flattened boxcar and the long throat of the tunnel, torchlight flickered where no torchlight should be. Voices. At least three. Boots on ballast, unhurried, coming on.

"Lena," Teo whispered. "Somebody's coming."

She closed the notebook, slid it inside her jacket, zipped it to her chin, and put her body between the hatch and the gap.

"How many?" she breathed.

"Can't tell. Three, four." A pause. Then, flat with dread: "One of them's got a big light. That's a surveyor's rig. That's Vane's rig."

Of course it was. Of course he'd come now—now that the water was low enough, now that his crew of hired hands had watched hers break through the bulkhead and start pulling the wreck apart, now that the thing he'd bought six weeks of her life to reach was finally in reach. He hadn't trusted her to find it. He'd let her do the dangerous part and planned to walk in for the prize.

Except she was holding the prize, and it wasn't the one he thought.

The boots grew louder. Light bloomed in the gap, throwing the shadow of the folded bulkhead across the swept floor like bars.

Lena stood up slowly, hands visible, heart hammering against the notebook's hard corners.

"Cassius Vane," she called out, loud enough to carry down the tunnel, steady enough to pass for calm. "You're a long way from your permit maps."

The light stopped. A pause, long enough to mean he was smiling.

"Not as long as you think, Ms. Marchetti," came the answer, echoing off wet steel. "And I'm afraid we're not done here. There's a specific car I need you to open—tonight—and you're going to take me to it."

Lena's hand pressed flat against her jacket, over the notebook, over her father's handwriting, over the truth.

"The way I see it," Vane said, and the light began moving again, closer, "we can do this the easy way or the expensive way. Your crew's payroll is due Friday."

The trap wasn't half-visible anymore. It was closing around the light.
