## 13. Bargaining with Ghosts

The mail car smelled like a drowned library. Lena stood in the doorway a long moment before she went in, headlamp beam cutting through the silt-hung air, and let herself feel the weight of what was in her jacket pocket. Her father's field notebook. Forty pages of his cramped, slanted hand, dated nine days before the wreck. Nine days before they put him on a train to the county jail and never let him ride anything again.

She'd read it twice by lantern light in the crew shack. She'd read it a third time standing at the north portal at dawn, watching the river run brown and fast below the bulkhead, and by then she'd stopped shaking.

Now she waited for Vane.

He came up the line at ten, alone, which was itself an answer. Men like Cassius Vane didn't come anywhere alone unless they wanted something said that couldn't be overheard. His boots were wrong for the mud—city leather, expensive—and he picked his way along the ties with the careful distaste of a man crossing a floor he considered beneath him. Behind him the tunnel mouth yawned, black water still trickling down the shored walls, and ahead of him the wreck sat in its pool of lamplight like something laid out for viewing.

"You found it," he said. Not a question. His eyes went straight to her jacket.

"Found what?" Lena kept her voice flat. "We've been pulling debris for two weeks. There's a lot of it."

"Don't." He stopped six feet from her. "I've watched you work, Marchetti. You don't waste motion and you don't waste words. Don't insult us both."

Somewhere deeper in the tunnel, water dripped off a strut. Counting seconds. Lena made herself breathe slow.

"Then say what you came to say."

Vane took off his gloves, finger by finger, and tucked them into his coat. It was a stalling gesture and she let him have it. When he looked up, something had shifted in his face—the boardroom polish cracked just enough to show whatever was underneath, and what was underneath looked tired.

"The notebook is in your pocket," he said. "I know because I paid a man to watch this portal every night since we broke the bulkhead, and last night you carried it out wrapped in your spare shirt like it was an infant. So we can skip the part where you pretend you haven't read it."

"He flagged the brakes." Her voice came out rougher than she intended. "Nine days before. He wrote to the division superintendent. He wrote to the insurer. He tagged the brake shoes on car eleven himself—there's a sketch, measurements, a work order number. They did nothing. Then when the train went into the river, they said it was *his* negligence." The words were coming faster now, twenty years of them backed up behind her teeth. "You knew. You knew all of it, and you hired me anyway, and you pointed me at this tunnel like a gun."

"I did," Vane said simply.

Lena's hand closed around the notebook through the fabric of her jacket. For one hot second she thought about swinging on him, and she saw him register the thought and stand very slightly straighter to receive it, and that—that refusal to flinch—was the only thing that stopped her. Men who flinched got hit. Men who didn't wanted something.

"Why," she said. "Say it plain."

* * *

He walked past her, into the wreck.

She followed because there was nowhere else to go, and because some part of her had already understood that this conversation was going to happen among the dead whether she liked it or not. Vane moved between the cars with a strange familiarity, ducking under a buckled coupling, stepping over a wheel truck furred with rust, until he reached the third coach back from the locomotive. He put his palm flat against its flank the way you'd touch a horse you were about to buy or put down.

"Do you know what my family does, Marchetti? Did?"

"Railroads."

"Bond houses." He almost smiled. "My grandfather financed half the mountain divisions in this state. My father ran the Cascade concession's paper—the bonds that built this tunnel, the insurance instruments that covered the rolling stock. I grew up reading prospectuses at the dinner table while other boys read adventure serials." He turned to look at her, lamplight catching the hollows under his eyes. "My brother rode this train."

Lena went still.

"Julian. Nineteen years old. Coming home from his first season working the resort hotels up-valley. Third coach." He patted the steel beside his hand. "This one. Or near enough. They never recovered—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "They never recovered everything."

The river said what it always said, low and constant, under everything.

"When the inquiry blamed your father, my parents accepted it," Vane went on. "It was tidy. It was what the railroad wanted and what the insurer wanted and grief doesn't fight paperwork, not when you're drowning. But I was fourteen, and I'd already read enough bond filings to know how numbers get arranged. And years later, when I started pulling the old accounts—legitimately, at first, for a history I was writing—I found something the inquiry never mentioned." He held her eyes. "The payroll car. Car seven. It was carrying a bonded shipment. Bearer instruments, non-registered. Sixty thousand dollars in 1908 terms, moving up-line for a land purchase that never happened. When the train went into the river, those bonds went with it. And bearer bonds, Marchetti, don't die. They just wait."

"You think they're still in there." She looked past him, down the length of the flooded dark toward where the seventh car lay somewhere under two decades of silt and river. "After twenty years underwater."

"Paper in waxed tin, packed in oilcloth, in a locked strongbox. It's been done before. Salvage men have pulled readable documents out of wrecks older than this." His jaw tightened. "I've spent four years buying the concession rights, fighting the state for permits, running surveys. Do you know what every single one of those permits requires? A licensed salvage operator. A crew with a clean record and a name the county trusts." Something bitter moved across his face. "Do you know how many operators would take this contract?"

"All of them. For your money."

"Not with the Cascade line's reputation. Not with the ghost story attached to it. Every crew in three counties has heard about the wreck that killed the Marchetti girl's father. Superstition runs deep in this trade—you know that better than anyone. And the ones who weren't superstitious wanted terms I couldn't give without explaining why I needed a specific car opened first." He spread his hands. "But you. You had debts, a good crew, and a reason of your own to want this tunnel open. Your name got me the county's cooperation. Your father's history got the historical society interested, which got me the preservation waiver I needed to dig at all. You opened doors I'd been beating on for four years."

Lena felt it land, all of it, cold and precise. Every door she'd knocked on these past weeks—the clerk who'd waved her through, the society man who'd signed the waiver with a handshake and a look of sympathy, the whole valley's strange willingness to let *her* crew do what no one else could touch. She'd thought it was respect. It had been a key cut to her shape.

"You used my father," she said. "His disgrace. That's what bought your permits."

"Yes." No flinch. God help her, no flinch at all. "And I told myself it was justice with interest. That clearing his name was worth using it. I even believed that, some days." He looked down at the coach under his hand. "Julian believed in this railroad. He took this job on the trains because he loved them. And the company that owned the bonds let a man take the blame rather than admit their own brake maintenance records were rotten, because admitting it would have devalued the paper. My family's paper. I understand the machine, Marchetti. I was raised inside it. So I became part of it, and I used it, and here we are."

Here we are. Standing in the wreck of both their families, lit by borrowed lamps.

* * *

Lena walked away from him then—not far, just far enough to put the mail car's broken doorframe between them, to have something solid at her back. She pulled the notebook out of her jacket and held it, not offering it, just holding it, feeling the warped cover and the water-swollen pages that had somehow survived because her father had been the kind of man who wrapped things in oilcloth too.

"My father died in 1911," she said. "Three years after they took his license. Heart, the doctor said. I say it was this tunnel wearing him down from the inside. And you want me to believe you brought me up here out of some shared grief."

"I want you to believe I'm telling you the truth now, when I could have kept lying." Vane came toward her slowly, hands visible, palms out—an old gesture, disarming, and she hated how well it worked. "I could have let you find the bonds yourself. I could have taken them and vanished and left you your father's name cleared as payment. That was the plan, if I'm honest. Clear name for clear access. Everyone walks away with what they came for."

"But."

"But you read the notebook before I expected you to find it, and you didn't come to me furious about the bonds. You came up this morning and posted Odell at the south adit and Teo at the portal, and you've been standing guard over a pile of wet paper like it's a grave." He stopped in front of her. "You don't care about the money at all. Which means I can't predict you anymore, and a man who can't be predicted is either a partner or a problem."

"And which am I?"

"That depends entirely on you." He nodded at the notebook. "There are sixty thousand dollars in bearer bonds under forty feet of muck in that seventh car. If they surface, they're mine by right of the concession—I can prove the chain of ownership, I have the original issue records. But I'll trade. Straight trade, in front of witnesses: the bonds for the wreck itself. Full archaeological survey, funded by me, every artifact catalogued and given to the county. The names of everyone who died, carved and filed. Your father's record amended with the notebook as primary evidence." He paused. "And Ada Kowalczyk's statement, if you've found her, entered into the formal inquiry record with legal backing. I retain lawyers who owe me favors. They can make the state reopen a twenty-year-old finding faster than any historian alive."

Lena stared at him. It was a good offer. It was, infuriatingly, nearly the exact outcome she wanted—and that was the thing that made her skin crawl, because Cassius Vane did not offer good deals out of decency. He offered them because they cost him less than the alternative.

"What aren't you telling me?"

For the first time, he hesitated. She watched him weigh it, watched the boardroom polish try to reassemble itself and fail.

"The survey maps," she pressed. "The spot you marked in the north wall, fifty meters past the wreck. There's no collapse there. No plausible cargo. I've been trying to make it make sense for a week and it doesn't."

"It isn't about the wreck," Vane said quietly. "Not entirely. The bonds were logged onto car seven at the Cascade yard. But the transfer manifest—the counterfoil proving they were loaded, the document that makes my claim legally airtight against the insurer's heirs—it was in the conductor's safe. And the conductor's safe wasn't recovered with the body." He met her eyes. "Because the conductor didn't die in the wreck, Marchetti. He died fifty meters further in. Someone got off that train, or someone was already waiting in the tunnel, and the safe went into a niche in the north wall that my survey says exists and every official drawing says doesn't."

The dripping water counted seconds again. Ten. Twenty.

"Someone cut those brake lines," Lena said. The words came out of her like something being extracted. "Ada saw it done. Paid silence. And now you're telling me there was a man in the tunnel that night with a safe."

"I'm telling you the derailment wasn't just negligence and scapegoating." Vane's voice had dropped, though there was no one to hear. "It was a robbery that needed a wreck. And everyone who might have known—the conductor, the brakeman who signed the transfer, the yard clerk who countersigned—they're all dead or scattered, and the only two people left who care are standing in this tunnel arguing about it." He exhaled. "So. Partner or problem, Marchetti. The storm line's coming up the valley—you can smell it on the river already, and when it hits, the south portal goes and everything in here goes with it. I need an answer before the barometer does."

Lena looked down at the notebook in her hands. Her father's handwriting swam in the lamplight: *Brake shoes on #11 worn past limits. Reported to sup't. Will not run this consist again without replacement.* Will not run this consist again. Except they'd made him run it. Or someone had made certain it ran regardless.

Her whole life she'd believed the story had two people in it: her father and the lie. Now it had a robber, a murdered conductor, a silenced witness, and a bond man who grieved like an accountant and bargained like one too. She didn't trust Cassius Vane. She trusted even less the version of events where she walked away and let the river decide what truth survived.

"Partner," she said. "On conditions. The bonds get documented before they move—photographed, witnessed, Odell counts them. The safe comes out through the county, not through your lawyers' back pocket. And nobody touches anything past the wreck until we've surveyed it properly, because if there was a man in this tunnel that night, I want to know what else he left behind."

"Agreed." Too fast. She caught it—he caught her catching it—and something flickered behind his eyes that she chose, for now, to file away instead of name.

Then she heard it.

Under the river's constant voice, a new note. Deeper. A groan of steel from the direction of the south adit, long and structural, the sound a tunnel makes when it changes its mind about standing. Odell's whistle sounded from the camp—two short, one long. All hands.

Vane's head came up. "That's early. The storm's still hours out."

"Then it isn't the storm." Lena was already moving, notebook jammed inside her jacket, boots splashing through the standing water toward the light at the portal's mouth. Behind her, the river rose a half-inch against the bulkhead gauge as she passed it—a half-inch in ten minutes, when it hadn't moved a half-inch in two days.

Somebody, somewhere upstream, had opened something.
