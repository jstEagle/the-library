## 14. Storm Line

The river spoke first.

Lena heard it at three in the morning, a sound like gravel being swallowed whole, and she was out of her bunk before the second swallow. She stood in the doorway of the bunkhouse in her boots and nothing else and listened to the Cascade Fork go from a voice to a shout.

By dawn it was screaming.

The storm had come up the gorge overnight the way storms did up there—no warning worth trusting, just a wall of cloud that had sat on the divide for two days drinking snowmelt and now decided to give it all back at once. Rain came sideways. The gauge Odell had staked at the cut bank above camp read eighteen inches when it should have read six.

"South portal's weeping," Teo reported, coming into the mess trailer with his slicker streaming. "Not seeping—weeping. Water's coming through the arch joints in ropes."

"Through concrete?"

"Through twenty-year-old concrete that's been freezing and thawing since before I was born." He wiped his face with his sleeve, which accomplished nothing. "Odell's up there now. Says the invert's heaving."

Lena pulled on her gear. Outside, the world had narrowed to gray water and gray rock and the drumming of rain on steel. The Fork, which had spent two weeks behaving itself behind its coffer dam, had turned brown and muscular overnight, carrying whole trees past the portal like matchsticks.

She found Odell crouched at the south adit with his palm flat against the lining, the way a doctor palpates a belly. His lips were moving. Counting drips, maybe. Or praying, which he'd deny to his grave.

"Talk to me," she said.

"Invert's lifted maybe three inches since yesterday. There's a crack running crown to springline, new as of this morning." He looked up at her, rain sheeting off his hard hat brim. "That bulkhead north of us is holding back eleven feet of standing water. If the south side goes, the tunnel drains through us."

"How long?"

"Honest answer? I don't know. Storm answer? Not long enough."

Lena stared down the black throat of the adit, where their work lights burned in a receding string of yellow dots, all the way to the wreck they'd spent five weeks uncovering. Five weeks of pumping, shoring, hauling mud by the ton. Five weeks of her father's notebook sitting in a waterproof case in her footlocker, its pages proving what the railroad had spent twenty years burying.

"We pull out," she said. "Everything portable. Equipment first, then records, then people. And we get Vane on the radio, because if his concession is about to drown, that's his problem—but if my crew drowns, it's mine."

* * *

Vane arrived within the hour, which told her he'd already known. He came up the switchback road in a white pickup with chains on all four tires and two of his hired men riding the bed like cargo: Dreele, big and quiet, and a lean one named Sorel who smiled too easily and never reached his eyes. Lena had catalogued them both weeks ago and filed them under *watch*.

"You're evacuating," Vane said. It wasn't a question. He stood in the rain without a hat, letting it run off his jaw, watching the brown river with an expression she couldn't read—grief, or appetite, or some alloy of the two.

"The south portal is failing. Odell gives it hours, not days."

"Then the notebook stays here."

Lena turned slowly. "Say that again."

"Your father's book. The original pages." Vane's voice stayed level, reasonable, the voice of a man discussing freight rates. "It doesn't leave the mountain until I say. That was implicit in our arrangement."

"There was no such arrangement. There was a contract to reopen a tunnel, and there's a man standing in front of me trying to change its terms in a rainstorm because he thinks I'm too busy to notice."

"I think you're smart enough to notice," Vane said, "and pragmatic enough to understand leverage. Those pages are the only thing keeping your crew's fees alive. You walk them off this mountain, you walk away from every dollar I owe you."

Behind him, Sorel shifted his weight from one foot to the other. Dreele didn't move at all, which was worse.

Lena thought about Odell's hands on the heaving concrete. About Teo's arm still in a sling from the generator sabotage. About six paychecks and a fuel bill and the bridge they'd have to rebuild on the way down. She thought about all of it for exactly four seconds.

"No," she said.

Vane exhaled through his nose. For a moment something human crossed his face—not regret exactly, but the shadow of a man who'd hoped not to arrive at this particular door. Then it closed.

"Dreele," he said quietly. "The footlocker in her quarters. Sorel, the survey trailer."

"You're robbing us," Lena said. "In front of me. In front of God and the river."

"I'm securing an asset." But he wouldn't look at her when he said it, and that was the confession.

Dreele started toward the bunkhouse. Lena put herself in his path, and it was absurd—a hundred and forty pounds of woman against a man built like a culvert—but the crew materialized the way crews do. Odell, dripping, planting himself at her shoulder. Teo with a pry bar he'd picked up from God knew where. Marisol from the pump shed, still wearing her ear protection around her neck like a priest's collar.

"Nobody touches that locker," Lena said.

Dreele looked past her at Vane. Vane looked at the river. And the river, with impeccable timing, made its opinion known.

The sound came from the south portal—a crack like a rifle shot, then a longer groan, tectonic, the mountain rearranging its mind. A rope of water burst from the arch joint and hit the ballast in a white fan. The string of work lights inside flickered, steadied, flickered again.

"Adit's going," Odell said flatly. "Everybody who wants to see old age should start walking downhill."

And that was when Sorel ran.

Not toward the trucks. Toward the portal. Toward the dark, where the notebook wasn't—it was in Lena's footlocker, and everyone knew it, and Sorel either didn't know or didn't care, because greed doesn't check its maps. He went through the falling water with his head down and his hand already reaching for the light string, and the mountain chose that moment to shrug.

* * *

Later, in the hearing and the archive and the long years after, Lena would tell it plainly, because plainness was all the truth could bear. The south portal failed while Sorel was forty feet inside. Not dramatically—the way failures actually happen, which is suddenly and completely. A section of lining the size of a garage door let go, and the river that had been leaning on the outside of the mountain for a century leaned a little harder, and the adit filled with noise and water and darkness.

They got him out because Odell refused to consider anything else. Odell went in on a lifeline with Teo paying rope, into water thigh-deep and rising, following Sorel's headlamp glow where it bobbed against the current. They found him wedged against the wreckage of a light stand, one leg pinned by a slab of lining, his pack—empty, useless, holding nothing but a stolen coil of copper wire—still strapped to his back.

He'd gone in for the notebook. He'd grabbed the wrong thing. And the slab had him from knee to hip, and the water was at his waist, and rising.

"Get it off me," Sorel kept saying. "Get it off me, get it off—" Not the slab. The pack. Even pinned under half a ton of mountain, even with the river climbing his ribs, his hands were scrabbling at the buckles of an empty pack, because somewhere in his arithmetic the wire was still worth something, still counted, still an asset to be secured.

"Your leg's under the stone," Odell told him. "Hold still. We'll lever it."

They levered. Teo got a hydraulic bag under the slab edge and inflated it and the mountain gave an inch, and in that inch the water took its payment. It surged through the gap, lifted Sorel bodily, and drove him into the collapse zone where the rest of the arch was peeling away in sheets, and Odell caught his wrist, and held, and the river pried the wrist loose like a child taking a toy from another child.

They recovered him a hundred yards down the adit, snagged on the wreckage of the mail car—the same car where Lena had found her father's notebook, though she would only learn that detail later, and would carry it the rest of her life like a stone in her boot.

Sorel was dead before they got him into daylight. Drowned in twelve inches of moving water, in a tunnel he'd helped drain, killed not by sabotage or malice but by his own grip on a prize that didn't exist. By his own greed, Odell said afterward, sitting on a crate with his head in his hands. By his own goddamn greed.

* * *

Vane stood over the body for a long time. The rain had slackened to a fine cold mist, and the crew had retreated to the high ground by the trucks, and nobody spoke. Dreele had taken off his hard hat, which seemed to be the only eulogy available.

"He wasn't supposed to go in," Vane said finally. His voice had lost its polish; underneath it was something raw. "I said secure the locker. On the surface. That's all."

"You said rob us," Lena said. "He heard the rest himself."

Vane flinched. It was small, but she saw it, and she hated herself a little for cataloguing it even now.

"The portal's gone," Odell said, joining them, wringing river out of his cuffs. "North bulkhead's still holding, but the whole tunnel's flooding from the south end now. Whatever's left inside will be underwater by morning. Including the wreck. Including everything."

Lena looked at the black mouth of the adit, where their five weeks of work was dissolving inch by inch into brown water. Her father's notebook was safe—she'd moved it to the survey trailer's document safe two days ago, after the clipping, after Ada Kowalczyk's statement, after she'd started sleeping with one eye open. Vane didn't know that. Sorel had died for an empty footlocker.

But the wreck itself. The photographs she hadn't finished taking. The brake linkage from the lead coach, still clamped in its jig where they'd documented the cuts. The physical evidence, the thing no sworn statement could fully replace.

"It'll be underwater," she said, "but it won't be destroyed. Steel keeps. The question is whether anyone can ever get back in to prove it—and Vane, your concession expires in nine days."

Something moved behind Vane's eyes. Grief, calculation, the war between them conducted at speed.

"The hearing's in town," he said. "Thursday. Emergency session, extension request, the whole circus." He looked down at Sorel's body, zipped now into a tarp, and his jaw worked once. "I was going to walk in there with those pages and a finished tunnel and make them beg me to stay. Now—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "Now I don't know what I'm walking in there with."

"You're walking in with a dead man and a drowned tunnel and a lie," Lena said. "I'm walking in with the truth. Between us, I like my odds."

For the first time since the bunkhouse, Cassius Vane looked at her straight on. And what she saw in his face wasn't anger. It was the look of a man reading a map and realizing, all at once, that the treasure has been marked in the wrong place—that he'd spent years, and other men's lives, digging toward a spot far from any plausible collapse.

"You knew," he said. "About the safe. You've had it the whole time."

"I've had the truth the whole time," Lena said. "You're the one who kept confusing it with property."

Down in the gorge, the river took another bite of the south portal, and the mountain groaned, and the last of their work lights inside went out—one, then another, then all—like a town going dark for the night.

Thursday was three days away.
