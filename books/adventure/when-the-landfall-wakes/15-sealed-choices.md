## 15. Sealed Choices

The river told us first.

I'd been listening to it my whole life—forty years underground and on the grades above, you learn the difference between water doing its work and water getting ready to do something else. That night it had a new note under the roar. A knocking. Deep down, patient, like a fist testing a door.

I stood at the north portal with my palm flat against the bulkhead timbers and felt it come through the wood into my bones. Behind that wall was forty feet of black standing water, and behind the water was everything we'd bled for: the notebook in its oilcloth, Ada's statement copied three ways, Lena's photographs of a wreck somebody had dressed up like a stage set. All of it sitting in the dark, waiting on us to be smart enough to keep it alive.

Both portals were gone. The south adit had let go at dusk—not all at once, but in a long groaning surrender, half the face slumping into the gorge with a sound I'll hear when I'm dying. The north bulkhead was holding, but holding is not the same as winning. Holding just means you haven't lost yet.

Lena came up the grade with her headlamp bouncing and her jaw set the way it gets when she's already made the decision and is only now telling herself about it.

"Talk to me, Odell."

"Timbers are sound," I said. "That's the good news. Bad news is they're sound because the pressure's distributed wrong. When the south face went, it changed the hydraulics. Water's looking for a new way out, and the only door left is this one." I knocked twice on the planking. "She'll hold tonight. Maybe."

"Maybe isn't a plan."

"No. Plan is we build her a second skin." I'd been running numbers in my head since sundown, the old arithmetic from my mining days, back when men still died of bad shoring and the company called it weather. "We've got the cribbing off the supply truck, the steel plate off Vane's rig, and every bag of quick-set cement between here and the highway. We brace inside, plate the seams, pour what we can against the outside face before the water takes the ground out from under us."

Lena looked past me into the portal mouth, where the black water waited behind its wooden lid. "How long?"

"If we work clean? Dawn. If we don't—" I shrugged. "Then we lose the tunnel, the evidence, and probably Teo's truck."

"We're not losing any of those things." She said it flat, no fire in it, which is how I knew she meant it. Lena doesn't spend words on things she hasn't decided. "Wake everybody up."

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about a crew: it isn't born, it's survived into.

I've run gangs of men my whole working life, and most of them were blood-related to somebody else entirely—a brother here, a cousin there, whole families moving through the camps like weather. And I'll tell you straight: family by blood is the weakest timber in the world. It holds until it doesn't, and when it fails, it fails without warning, because nobody ever chose it. You can't inspect a bond you never built.

What we had on that mountain was different. Every one of them had chosen, at some point, to be there. Teo, who signed on green and scared and stayed anyway after the bridge nearly ate him. Marisol, who could read a pump gauge like scripture and never once asked why an old miner kept his tools wrapped in oilcloth like relics. Lena, who carried her father's ghost up a mountain she'd sworn off and opened the door anyway.

And me. I'd walked away from underground work twenty years ago because I buried two men in a collapse that wasn't their fault or mine, and I swore the same oath Lena did—never again, never up the Cascade line, never into the dark on purpose. Then a woman half my age showed me a contract and said *we need payroll*, and I looked at her tired face and chose the dark all over again. Freely. That's the word. Freely given, freely taken back if you want it. Nobody ever offered me free passage off a job before Lena did, and nobody took it, and that right there is the whole difference between a crew and a family.

You choose your people. Then you show up at two in the morning when the river knocks.

* * *

We worked through the night like our hands belonged to somebody more competent than us.

Marisol ran the pumps and the generator both, keeping the sump level down so the cribbing floor wouldn't float. Teo hauled plate steel on a hand cart, one piece at a time, down a grade slick with spray, humming to himself the way he does when he's terrified—I learned that about him on the bridge day, and I've never told him I know. Lena set the braces, calling angles from memory of her father's field notes, which she'd been reading like a devotional. And I laid the cement, bag after bag into the gap between the old bulkhead and the new plate, mixing by feel in the freezing runoff because the light was too bad to see the color.

Somewhere around three in the morning the knocking got louder.

"Odell." Teo's voice, thin. "It's louder."

"It's supposed to be. Means the water's found the plate instead of the timbers. Plate can take it." I didn't say the rest, which was that plate can take it *until it can't*, and that the seam at the top-left corner was weeping in a way I didn't love. "Keep hauling."

Lena came over and put her shoulder against the last brace beside mine. For a while we just pushed, two people leaning on a twelve-by-twelve, holding back a river with pine and stubbornness.

"My father stood here," she said quietly. "Before all of it. There's a photo—he's got his hand on these same timbers, when they were new."

"He was a good shoring man?"

"The best. That's what kills me. Everybody always talks about the derailment, but he was famous for the *other* thing—for never losing a man underground. Twenty-two years, zero fatalities. And then one bad night and the whole record burns." She pushed harder on the timber. "I used to think the record was the point. Now I think the point is who shows up."

I glanced sideways at her. "Somebody showing up tonight?"

"Somebody always does." She almost smiled. "That's the trick of it. You build a thing where people show up, and then they keep showing up even when the river's at the door."

* * *

At four-forty the top-left seam let go.

Not a failure—a warning. A jet of water thick as my wrist punched through the weeping joint and hit the far wall of the portal with a crack like a rifle. Marisol killed the pumps so we could hear ourselves think, and in the sudden quiet we all heard the same thing: the deep note changing pitch. Rising. The river finding its voice.

"Plate off the bottom rows," I shouted. "Move it up, double-lap the seam, pour everything we've got left behind it!"

Teo moved like I'd never seen him move. That boy—who'd frozen on the bridge, who'd gone white the first time the water spoke—went *into* the spray, dragging sixty-pound plate up a ladder one-handed while the jet tried to peel the bulkhead open beside his head. Marisol fed him bags. Lena held the plate flush with her whole body while I wired it home, my hands shaking too hard to admit to anybody.

And the whole time, in the back of my skull, the old arithmetic ran: *pressure times area, load paths, factor of safety.* And underneath it, older and quieter: *you buried two men once. Not tonight.*

The jet slowed. Thinned. Stopped.

We stood there in the dripping dark, six hands and a pile of wet cement bags, listening to the river knock politely on forty feet of new steel and timber, and get no answer.

Marisol started laughing first—that shaky, hiccupping laugh that means the body's paying a bill the mind ran up. Then Teo sat down flat in the puddle, and then Lena, and then me, because my knees had apparently filed their own notice hours ago and were only now delivering it.

"Dawn in ninety minutes," Lena said finally. "Somebody should check the south side, make sure nothing else—"

"I'll go," Teo said, and was up before she finished, headlamp already swinging toward the grade.

I watched him go and thought: *there it is.* The boy who froze on the bridge, volunteering into the dark. Nobody paid him. Nobody ordered him. He just went, because that's what you do when the thing you're part of matters more than the fear you carry.

That's family. Not the blood. The going.

* * *

Lena stayed behind with me while the others climbed out. We sat on the cement bags with our backs against the new plate, sharing a thermos of coffee that tasted like tin and victory, watching the first gray light come down the portal shaft.

"You know what I keep thinking?" she said. "Vane's men will be back. Storm's not done, and neither is he. Whatever he wants out of that wreck, he's going to come for it hard now that the south face is open."

"Then we'd better be ready."

"We will be." She turned the thermos cup in her hands. "Odell. Last night—you could have told me the bulkhead was hopeless. You're the only one who'd know. You could have saved yourself the work and gotten everyone off the mountain."

"Could have," I agreed.

"Why didn't you?"

I thought about it honestly, the way she deserved. About the two men I'd buried, and the twenty years of oilcloth-wrapped tools, and the oath, and the woman beside me who'd broken hers for better reasons than I broke mine.

"Because you asked," I said finally. "Nobody's asked me to hold a line in twenty years. They've ordered, or begged, or assumed. You *asked*. And when a person asks you straight, you find out what you've actually got in you." I tapped the plate behind us. "Turns out it's enough. Barely. But enough."

She leaned her head against my shoulder for a second, the way a daughter does, though she'd deny the word if you said it out loud. Neither of us needed it said. That was the whole point of us.

Then her radio crackled—Teo, breathless, from the south grade.

"Lena? Odell? You need to see this. Somebody's been at the south portal overnight—and they left something. It's... it's marked up. Survey paint, fresh. And there's a map nailed to the timber." A pause, the wind filling it. "It's got your name on it, boss. Right where the bonds are supposed to be."

Lena stood, and I watched the tiredness fall off her like a coat, replaced by the other thing—the thing she carried up this mountain in the first place.

"Well," she said. "I guess Vane just told us where he's going."

She clicked her radio. "Everybody up. We're done holding the line." A grim little smile, there and gone. "Now we go collect."
