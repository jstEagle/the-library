## 16. Ada Comes Up the Mountain

The rain quit sometime before dawn, and by seven the clouds had torn open enough to show blue sky over the eastern ridge, which felt like an insult after three days of the river trying to eat us alive.

Lena stood at the north portal with a thermos of coffee she wasn't drinking, watching Odell's night crew knock the last timber into the shored bulkhead. They'd worked straight through—eighteen hours of mud and chain hoists and Odell shouting geometry at men too tired to argue—and when the last wedge went home, the water behind the bulkhead had stopped rising. Stopped. That was all anyone could promise. The south portal was a lost cause, a throat full of rock and floodwater, but the north held, and behind it, forty yards in on a shelf they'd cut above the high-water line, sat the evidence: her father's notebook in a waterproof document bag, Ada Kowalczyk's name circled in the margin of page eleven, and every photograph Teo had taken of that wrong, arranged wreck before the storm shut them out.

Everything they needed to burn Vane down was sitting in a mountain that wanted to swallow it.

"Boss." Teo came up the tram path at a jog, hood back, hair plastered to his skull. "Truck on the road. One vehicle, coming slow."

"We expecting anything?"

"No. Resupply isn't due till Thursday."

Lena capped the thermos. After the sabotage, after the generator, after one of Vane's hired hands dying stupid in the flooding adit because he'd rather drown than let go of a satchel he thought held the notebook, she didn't take surprises lightly. "Get Odell. Nobody approaches alone."

But when the truck came around the last switchback—a county-plated pickup with a cracked windshield and a roof rack tied down with what looked like clothesline—it stopped fifty meters short, and the person who climbed out was small, gray-haired, and moving like her knees had opinions about the altitude.

Lena knew her before she got close. She'd studied the face for two days on the hike out to the hollow below Miller's Bend, and again in the photograph from the inquest clipping: younger then, thirty and terrified, standing on the courthouse steps with her hand pressed flat over her heart like she was pledging something. Twenty years later, Ada Kowalczyk walked up the tram road in a man's barn coat and rubber boots, carrying a leather portfolio against her chest with both arms, the way you carry something you've decided not to lose.

"You're a hard woman to find twice," Lena said.

Ada stopped an arm's length away. Up close she was thinner than the photograph, weathered, with a tremor in her left hand she kept closing into a fist. Her eyes went past Lena to the portal, to the bulkhead, to the black seam of the tunnel mouth, and Lena watched twenty years move across her face like weather.

"It's still open," Ada said. Her voice was rough, underused. "I half believed it wouldn't be. I half hoped."

"The state will seal it soon enough. We're just keeping the water honest till then."

"I brought something." Ada lifted the portfolio. Her knuckles were white around it. "And I brought a letter, and I brought myself, and I'll be honest with you, Miss Marchetti—I nearly turned around four times on that road. The last time was right there at the bridge over the gorge. I sat in that truck for ten minutes."

"What changed your mind?"

Ada's jaw worked. "I realized I'd been sitting in that truck for twenty years. Figured it was time to drive."

* * *

They put her in the mess tent because it was the warmest place left—the generator was running again but rationed, heat going only where people slept—and gave her coffee and the chair nearest the stove. The crew drifted in without being asked. Odell filled the doorway with his arms crossed. Teo sat close enough to refill her cup. Marisol leaned against the supply rack, arms folded, watching Ada the way she watched unfamiliar rigging: for the flaw that would tell you when it would fail.

Ada noticed all of it. Lena could see her noticing—the way a guilty person reads a room, cataloguing exits.

"You know who I am," Ada said.

"I hiked six miles through alder brush to find out," Lena said. "You told me to go away. You told me some people bury things so deep even God can't find them, and then you closed the door."

"I did." Ada wrapped both hands around the cup. The tremor was worse now, or maybe she'd stopped fighting it. "I want to tell you why. Not the version I told you at the door. The real one. And then—" she drew a breath that seemed to cost her— "I need to ask you for something, and you're going to have every right to refuse me."

"Ask first," Marisol said quietly. "Then explain."

Ada looked at her. Something passed between them—recognition, maybe, of two women who'd learned not to trust a story until they knew what it was selling.

"All right." Ada set down the cup, opened the portfolio, and took out a single sheet of paper, yellowed soft at the folds, typed on a manual machine with a ribbon going dry—you could see the letters fading halfway down each line. At the bottom, a signature in careful schoolteacher's cursive, and beneath it, a date twenty years and two months old.

"This is my sworn statement," Ada said. "The original. My copy. The railroad's attorney kept the filed one, and what went into the inquest record afterward was—" her mouth twisted— "amended. That's the polite word. I signed this version. What the record shows is not what I said."

The tent was very quiet. Outside, someone swung a sledge against timber, once, twice, rhythmic as a heartbeat.

"My statement said the brake line on the number three car was cut," Ada said. "Not failed. Cut. Clean, angled, with a sharp blade, on the outside of the fitting where a walking inspection from the catwalk wouldn't catch it unless you were looking. I saw it because I was on the platform at Cascade station when the train made its unscheduled stop—there was a hot box warning, they halted to check the journals. I was close. Close enough to see the metal bright where it was freshly cut, no rust, no oil film. I told the first investigator exactly that. He wrote it down. Then a man from the insurer came to my house two days later with a checkbook and a very reasonable voice, and he explained to me what would happen to my sister's kids if I testified about sabotage on a line the railroad was already trying to abandon. There'd been layoffs that spring. Half the town worked for the company or wished it did. He didn't threaten me. He just laid out the arithmetic and let me do the rest myself."

"And you took the money," Odell said. No judgment in it. Just the fact.

"I took the money." Ada's chin came up, defiant and ashamed at once, which Lena recognized because she'd worn the same expression most of her life. "Four hundred dollars. It paid off my sister's medical debt and bought me twenty years of not sleeping well. I recanted. I told the inquest I'd been mistaken—that in the dark, at distance, a corroded brake line can look scored. They wrote it down. They wrote down that the derailment was consistent with excessive speed on the curve and operator error, and they wrote down the name of the man who was driving, and the whole valley read it over breakfast and decided they'd known all along what kind of man Elias Marchetti was."

Lena hadn't moved. She became aware, distantly, that her hands were flat on the table, that she was leaning forward, that everyone else in the tent was carefully not looking at her.

"You told me at your door you never met my father," Lena said.

"I lied." Ada met her eyes. "I rode that line twice a week for nine years. He checked my ticket more times than I can count, and once, when the heater in my car died in January, he gave me his own coat and rode in the cold himself and laughed about it. I knew him. Not well. But I knew him well enough to know that everything the record says about him is a lie, and I helped write it, and I have regretted it every single day since, and regret is a coward's currency—it spends easy and buys nothing." She pushed the yellowed sheet across the table toward Lena with two fingers, slowly, like setting down something heavy. "So here is what I'm asking. Not forgiveness. I don't get to ask for that. I'm asking for a job."

Marisol's eyebrows went up. Odell uncrossed his arms.

"There's a hearing," Ada said. "Vane called it—emergency session, concession extension, day after tomorrow, down in the county chamber. You knew that."

"We knew that."

"He expects a room full of county men who owe their roads and their bridges to the companies he represents. He expects to stand up there with his maps and his schedule and his lawyers and talk about economic development until everyone forgets there's a dead train in that tunnel and a dead man's name on it. He does not expect—" she tapped the statement— "the witness. The living, breathing, un-amended witness, with a letter from an attorney named Ruth Okafor in Cascade Falls, who has spent three weeks reviewing my case pro bono because her father also rode that line, and who tells me that if I testify, if I put this statement on the record under oath, the amended inquest can be formally challenged and reopened. It won't be fast. It won't be clean. But it starts with me standing up in that room and saying what I should have said when I was thirty years old."

"That's not a job," Lena said. "That's a firing squad."

"It is." Ada almost smiled. "I've been standing in front of it for twenty years anyway. This way I at least get to say something first."

* * *

Odell took Ada up to the bunkhouse to thaw out properly and get food in her, and Marisol went with them, which left Lena alone in the mess tent with the yellowed paper and her father's notebook side by side on the table.

She didn't touch either one for a long moment.

Page eleven of the notebook, in Elias Marchetti's cramped, slanted hand: *Brake line #3, scoring inconsistent with wear. Flagged for shop inspection 4/11. Requested replacement. Denied — budget.* Dated five days before the crash. Her father had found the cut line—or suspected it—and reported it, and been refused, and then been blamed for the disaster the refusal made inevitable. And Ada Kowalczyk had stood on a platform and seen the same thing he'd seen, and been paid to unsee it.

Two witnesses. Twenty years apart. Both silenced by the same arithmetic.

Teo came in quietly, set a fresh pot of coffee on the stove, and stood looking at the two documents without touching them. "That's the lady from the clipping," he said. "The circled name."

"That's the lady."

"She drove up here alone. In that truck. On that road." He shook his head slowly. "After everything. After the generator, after the fight in the adit, after a man died in that tunnel two days ago. How'd she even know we were still up here?"

Lena frowned. "I told her where the camp was when I visited. Why?"

"Because I checked the mail crate this morning, before her truck showed up." Teo reached into his jacket and produced a newspaper clipping, folded once, the same inquest article Lena had received weeks ago in the resupply—but this copy was newer, crisper, and someone had written across the top margin in ballpoint pen, in a small precise hand:

*Day after tomorrow. Bring everything. — R.O.*

"R.O.," Lena said. "Ruth Okafor."

"The lawyer. So she sent the clipping before—" Teo stopped. "Wait. If the lawyer mailed this days ago, then Ada didn't decide to come up the mountain yesterday. She decided weeks ago. Same time somebody slipped the first clipping in our crate."

Lena stared at the two clippings, old and new, and felt the shape of the thing assemble itself. Someone had been working this angle from the outside the whole time—not Vane, not his people. Someone who knew why she was really here before she'd admitted it to herself. Someone feeding her evidence piece by piece, patient as a winch operator paying out cable, letting her dig toward the truth at exactly the pace she could survive.

Ada appeared in the tent flap, hair drying, color back in her face, and caught them both looking at the clipping.

"Ah," she said. "You found Ruth's note. She said you would." She came in and sat down heavily, and for the first time since stepping off the truck, she looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with age. "I suppose I should be honest about the rest of it, then. About how Ruth found me. Because it wasn't the clipping that started this, Miss Marchetti. Three weeks ago, a package arrived at my house. No return address. Inside was a photocopy of a field notebook—page eleven—and a note in the same handwriting as that one, saying: *He flagged it five days early. Somebody finally found his book. Are you going to let them finish it alone?*"

Lena's blood went cold and hot at once. "Someone sent you a copy of my father's notebook. Before we ever found it."

"Before *you* say you found it, yes." Ada's eyes were steady. "Which means whoever sent it already knew it was in that tunnel. Already knew where to look. And I've been turning that over on the drive up, and I keep arriving at the same question, and I think you need to hear it out loud before we all go down to that hearing tomorrow." She leaned forward, and her voice dropped, and the tremor in her hand stilled completely.

"If your father's notebook was in the wreck's mail car, and nobody's been inside that tunnel in twenty years—then who besides you knew it was there? And why would they want *me*?"
