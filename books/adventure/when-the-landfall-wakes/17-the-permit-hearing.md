## 17. The Permit Hearing

The county courthouse in Meridian Falls had been built to impress a town that no longer existed. Marble steps worn hollow by boots, a rotunda with a mural of prospectors shaking hands over mountains that had since been strip-mined and abandoned. Lena had walked past it a thousand times without going in. Courthouses were where her father's life had ended—one stamped document at a time—and she'd sworn the same oath about them as she had about the tunnel.

Now she climbed the steps with a canvas satchel against her hip and Ada Kowalczyk on her arm.

Ada wore her good coat, the one with the mended cuff, and she gripped the rail like it might try something. She hadn't spoken much on the drive down. She'd spent twenty years not speaking; another hour wasn't going to break her. But when they reached the doors she stopped, and her hand tightened on Lena's arm hard enough to leave marks.

"Last time I stood in this building," she said, "I lied."

"Then we're even," Lena said. "I'm about to tell the truth for the first time in my life, and I'm terrified."

That got the ghost of a smile out of her. They went in together.

* * *

The hearing room was smaller than the rotunda promised—a committee chamber with folding chairs along the walls and a long table where five members of the Concessions Review Board sat behind nameplates. The public gallery was full. Word had gotten around; half the town wanted to watch Cassius Vane beg for his concession, and the other half wanted to watch him get it so they could keep their jobs on his crews.

Vane sat at the front table alone except for two lawyers who looked like they billed by the syllable. He was dressed like a man who'd already won: charcoal suit, silver tie pin, hands folded. But Lena had spent six weeks reading that man across a work site, and she could see what the tailoring was hiding. His collar was damp at the hairline. His thumb kept moving against his forefinger, counting something that wasn't there.

The board chairman, a heavy woman named Osgood with reading glasses on a chain, called the session to order. She read the agenda in a flat voice: emergency extension of Cascade Corridor Concession 7-C, requested by Vane Development Group, on grounds of force majeure—spring flooding compromising worksite access.

"Mr. Vane," Osgood said. "You have the floor."

Vane rose. And he was good—Lena would give him that forever. He spoke for eleven minutes about hydrology and permit windows and the sunk costs of mobilization, about how abandoning the corridor now would strand equipment worth more than the county's annual road budget. He cited figures from memory. He made the flood sound like an act of God aimed personally at his balance sheet. When he finished, two of the board members were nodding, and a man in the gallery said "hear, hear" before his wife shushed him.

Osgood turned a page. "Is there public comment?"

"There is," Lena said, and stood.

She felt the room find her—the salvage woman, the Marchetti girl, back after twenty years. Somewhere in the gallery someone whispered her surname and someone else whispered *the derailment*, and both whispers reached her the way cold air reaches you under a door.

"I'm Lena Marchetti. I run the crew Vane Development hired to reopen the Cascade Tunnel." She set the satchel on the witness table and didn't open it yet. "Mr. Vane has told you about water. I'd like to tell you about what the water was hiding."

One of Vane's lawyers leaned toward him. Vane shook his head once—a small motion, controlled. Let her talk. What could a salvage rat possibly have?

"The concession application lists the Cascade Tunnel as a collapsed rail tunnel requiring clearance and structural rehabilitation," Lena said. "That's what your board approved. That's what we were contracted to do. But the tunnel isn't collapsed. It's flooded behind a bulkhead, and the bulkhead wasn't built by the railroad in 1904 or patched after the derailment in—" she paused, let the year sit in the room, "—twenty years ago. It was cut. Deliberately. Demolition charges, placed by someone who knew rock, sometime in the last five years. My foreman Odell Brandt spent nineteen years underground before he came to us. He can read a blast face the way you read a ledger. He'll confirm it under any oath you care to name."

A murmur went through the gallery. Osgood's glasses came off.

"You're alleging sabotage of a state-registered structure?"

"I'm alleging worse than that." Lena opened the satchel. "I'm alleging that the wreck inside that tunnel was moved."

She laid the photographs out one at a time, facing the board. Eight-by-tens, printed at the drugstore in Halfway because nowhere else would touch them fast enough. The locomotive lying against the north wall—not crumpled the way a derailing crushes steel, but *placed*, its damage consistent and wrong, like furniture arranged after a burglary. The mail car separated from the train by forty feet of track that showed no scorch, no gouge, no reason. The coupler pins pulled clean and stacked. The cargo doors of the express car standing open on an empty interior, floorboards pried up and left leaning against the wall.

"My father drove that train," Lena said. Her voice held. She'd rehearsed holding it for twenty years. "Elias Marchetti. You can look him up—he's in your records, convicted on the inquest findings, dead eight years now, never lived long enough to see the inside of a prison he was spared by illness instead. The inquest concluded he derailed his own train through negligence. These photographs say otherwise. This wreck was staged. Someone went into that tunnel after the fact and rearranged it to match a story."

"That's a considerable claim," said the board member on the end, a thin man whose nameplate read HOLLIS. "Based on photographs taken by—forgive me—an interested party."

"Based on photographs corroborated by a sworn witness." Lena turned to the gallery. "Ada Kowalczyk."

Ada rose. It took her a moment, and the whole room waited, and Lena watched a seventy-year-old woman decide, one more time, in front of everyone, that she was done being quiet.

Ada didn't go to the witness table. She stayed where she was, which somehow made it worse for everyone else.

"My name is Ada Kowalczyk. In the year of the derailment I worked the signal box at Milepost 41. I saw the brake line on the third wagon bleeding air for two miles before the curve. I wrote it in my log. I gave a statement to the inquest." Her voice was dry and level, like gravel poured slow. "Three days later a man from the railroad's insurer came to my house with an envelope and a suggestion. My husband was sick. The envelope paid for what the insurance wouldn't. I took it. I withdrew my statement. I watched Elias Marchetti carry the blame for a brake line somebody cut, and I carried the envelope, and I have hated myself in a very organized way ever since."

She held up a folder. "This is my original statement. Dated, signed, witnessed by the yardmaster who is also still alive and still willing to countersign it. I never destroyed it. I just never used it. Until today."

She set it on the witness table next to the photographs, and the sound it made—a folder meeting wood—was the loudest thing Lena had ever heard in that building.

* * *

It came apart quickly after that, the way bad structures do.

Vane's lawyers tried to regain footing. One of them argued procedural grounds—that testimony regarding a twenty-year-old inquest fell outside the scope of a concessions hearing. Osgood looked at him over her glasses and said, "Son, this board administers permits for every mile of closed rail in three counties. If there are people cutting bulkheads and staging wrecks inside our corridors, I promise you it is inside our scope."

Then Hollis asked the question Lena had been waiting for. "Ms. Marchetti. You've shown us a staged wreck and a suppressed witness. What you haven't shown us is motive. Why would anyone do this?"

And here it was. The part she'd debated all night in the bunkhouse with Odell, Teo, and the rest of them, passing a bottle of nothing stronger than coffee. How far to push. Whether to burn Vane completely or leave him a door.

She'd decided on the truth, all of it, because partial truths were what had buried her father.

"Six weeks ago," Lena said, "Mr. Vane hired my crew under an emergency contract. Deadline-driven. Penalty clauses. He pushed us toward the tunnel on a schedule that made no engineering sense, and when we were inside, he directed us specifically to the express car—the one with the pried-up floorboards and the empty interior." She let that land. "Historical record says the Cascade run carried a bonded-currency shipment for the Meridian Trust. It was never recovered from the wreck. Mr. Vane, would you like to tell the board what you were looking for?"

For the first time, Vane moved. He started to rise, and his lawyer grabbed his sleeve, and for one unguarded second his face did something complicated—grief and fury and something almost like relief fighting for the same ground.

"I commissioned a recovery operation," Vane said, and his voice was steady, which must have cost him everything. "Private historical recovery. The bonds, if they exist, are a legitimate asset of the corridor's history—"

"They're bearer instruments from a defunct shipment," Osgood said flatly, "recovered from a state-sealed disaster site through unauthorized demolition of a registered structure, using a salvage contractor you deceived about the nature of the work." She took off her glasses entirely. "Mr. Vane, does your legal team want to walk that back, or shall I ask the county attorney to join us? Because he's in the gallery. He's been here since the start."

The county attorney raised a hand without standing. Vane's lead lawyer looked at his client. His client looked at the table. Whatever arithmetic ran behind those eyes—and Lena knew by now that Vane was always running numbers—it came out negative.

"We withdraw the extension request," the lawyer said quietly.

"It won't matter," Osgood said. "Concession 7-C is suspended pending investigation. All worksites sealed. And gentlemen—" this to the lawyers, but her eyes went to Vane, "—the county attorney will want statements. Today."

* * *

The backers folded like wet cardboard.

Lena watched it happen from the witness table while the board conferred in a low huddle. A banker named Pruitt, silver-haired, who'd sat through the whole hearing wearing the expression of a man at a funeral he'd paid for, stood up, straightened his coat, and walked out without looking at Vane. Then a woman Lena recognized from the landfall development brochures. Then two more men whose faces she only knew from newspaper society pages. One by one, Vane's money stood up and left the room, and by the time Osgood rapped the table for order, the front row where his investors had sat was empty except for one elderly man who'd apparently come for the show and was enjoying himself immensely.

Vane didn't watch them go. He sat with his hands flat on the table, staring at the photographs of the wreck he'd spent—what, years? decades?—arranging his life around, and Lena found she couldn't hate him cleanly. Not anymore. Not after the adit, after the storm, after the things he'd said in the dark with the river coming for them all. There was something underneath the scheme that she recognized, because she'd been carrying its twin for twenty years: a person who couldn't stop digging at the same wound.

But recognition wasn't forgiveness, and the hearing wasn't finished.

Osgood called the vote. Suspension of Concession 7-C: unanimous. Referral of the demolition evidence and the Kowalczyk statement to the county attorney and, through him, to the state records office for formal review of the original inquest: unanimous. Lena heard the gavel come down on both and felt something in her chest unlock that she hadn't known was locked.

Her father's name. Moving. After twenty years of sitting under rubble, moving.

* * *

Afterward, the room emptied slowly, the way rooms do after executions. The gallery filed out buzzing. The lawyers packed their leather cases with the brisk misery of men whose invoices were about to become worthless. Odell appeared at Lena's elbow—she hadn't seen him come in; he'd stood at the back the whole time with his cap in his hands like a man in church—and put one heavy hand on her shoulder.

"Your daddy would've liked watching that," he said. "Wouldn't have understood half the permit language. Would've liked the watching."

Teo was in the doorway, still moving stiffly, grinning like a fool. Behind him, half the crew had driven down without telling her, and they filled the corridor outside the chamber doors, dirty-faced and out of place among the marble, waiting to hear.

Ada was slower leaving. She stopped beside Lena at the witness table, where the photographs still lay fanned out, and touched the edge of one with one finger, gently, the way you'd touch a grave marker.

"He looks like you," Ada said, nodding at Elias Marchetti in the picture—though of course there was no picture of him, only the wreck, only the mail car, only the notebook that was even now in an evidence bag in the county attorney's office upstairs. "In the jaw. I remembered that, all these years. That he had a stubborn jaw."

"He got it from his mother," Lena said. Her eyes were burning and she let them. "Thank you. For coming up the mountain. For all of it."

Ada patted her arm once, briskly, the way she probably hadn't been patted in twenty years. "Don't thank me. Go make sure they write it down right. That's the whole job now. Making sure they write it down right."

Then she was gone, mended cuff and all, and Lena stood alone in the emptying chamber with the photographs and the silence.

Through the tall window, down on the platform of the old Meridian Falls station, she could see a lone figure in a charcoal suit standing where the trains used to stop, not moving, not leaving, waiting—with the patience of a man who had finally run out of numbers to count—for her to come down.

She picked up her satchel, shouldered it, and went to find out what Cassius Vane thought was left to say.
