## 19. Closing the Portal

The order came down on a Tuesday, typed on state letterhead and hand-carried up the gorge by a man who looked like he'd rather have mailed it.

Lena read it twice on the portal platform while the courier waited by his truck with the engine running, as if the mountain might change his mind about staying. *Per the findings of the Cascade Tunnel Review Board, all salvage operations are hereby concluded. The Cascade Tunnel is to be permanently sealed at both portals no later than June 1. Demolition may be performed by the permit holder or, at the Board's discretion, by qualified contractors of record.*

"Qualified contractors of record," Odell repeated over her shoulder. "That's us."

"That's us," Lena said.

She'd expected to feel something like relief. The hearing was two weeks behind them now—Vane's backers gone pale in their seats when she laid her father's notebook on the table, Ada Kowalczyk's sworn statement read aloud in a room that had spent twenty years pretending it didn't exist. The truth was in the record. The bonds claim had dissolved like frost on a warm rail. By every measure that mattered, they'd won.

But she stood there holding the state's letter, looking at the north portal—the bulkhead they'd shored through the storm, the steel and timber her crew had bled for—and what she felt wasn't relief. It was closer to standing at a graveside before you've decided what to say.

"You want me to draft the bid?" Odell asked.

"No." She folded the letter along its crease. "I want us to do it ourselves. No subcontractors. Nobody else's hands on this."

Odell studied her the way he did when he already knew the answer and wanted to hear her say it anyway. "Because?"

"Because my father's name is written into the record now because of what we found in there." She nodded toward the dark mouth of the tunnel, where the water had been pumped down to a knee-deep trickle and the wreck sat arranged in its final dishonesty until the state's investigators finished photographing it. "And because whoever sets these charges is going to be the last person to stand where he stood. I'm not giving that job to strangers."

Odell said nothing for a moment. Then he took the letter from her hand, read the deadline again, and tucked it into his jacket. "June first. We'll need blasting permits, a licensed shot-firer, and about four hundred pounds of ANFO we don't have."

"I'll make calls."

"And Teo will want to know if he gets to push the button."

Lena almost smiled. "He gets to ask nicely."

* * *

They spent three days preparing, and it was the strangest work any of them had ever done: dismantling, carefully, everything they'd built.

The pumps came out first, hauled up the switchbacks on flatbeds, their intake hoses coiled like shed skins. Then the shoring timber they could recover without compromising the bulkhead's remaining strength—because the plan, drawn up with a state engineer who'd flown in from the capital and left visibly shaken by what he'd seen inside, called for the north bulkhead itself to come down in a controlled collapse, burying the portal mouth under several hundred tons of deliberately loosened mountainside. The south portal would get the simpler treatment: a concrete plug poured behind a steel door, unglamorous and permanent.

"The north side matters more," the state engineer had said, not unkindly. "It's the side people can reach. It's the side people will try to reach."

Lena understood. Somewhere in the dark behind that bulkhead lay the wreck of the *Cascadia Limited*, car nine still holding the mail sacks her father's notebook had been hidden among, the brake linkage Ada Kowalczyk had watched a man cut with a hacksaw twenty years ago. The state had taken its photographs, pulled its samples, catalogued its evidence. Now the mountain would keep the rest.

She'd expected to fight that harder than she did. For six weeks the tunnel had been an enemy—a thing that swallowed trains and reputations and, nearly, her crew. But standing in the north adit on the last day of entry, running her headlamp across the great silent hulk of the locomotive one final time, she found she didn't hate it anymore. It had kept her father's notebook safe for twenty years. It had held the proof nobody else would look for. Whatever else the tunnel was, it had been patient with the truth.

"All right," she said quietly, to the dark, to the wreck, to the man whose ghost she'd stopped being afraid of somewhere around chapter fourteen of this whole disaster. "We've got it from here."

Teo, at her shoulder, pretended very hard not to have heard her talking to a train.

* * *

The charges went in on the last Friday of May.

Four hundred pounds of explosive, distributed across thirty-two boreholes drilled into the rock crown above the portal and packed into the bulkhead's base—enough to bring the slope down in a single deliberate shrug, sealing the mouth under rubble no shovel would argue with. Odell ran the drilling crew himself, checking each hole depth twice against the shot plan, and Lena walked the line behind him with the firing circuit diagram, initialing each charge as it was tied in.

It was slow, exacting, sacred work, though none of them would have used that word aloud. But she caught the others feeling it. Teo handling the detonators like communion wafers. Marta, who'd been with the crew eight years and joked through everything, saying nothing at all for a full shift. Even Rook, the quietest of them, pausing at the portal mouth on his way past with wire spools, looking into the dark for a long moment before moving on.

They all knew whose ground this was. Not Vane's—not anymore, whatever wreckage he was making of himself down in town. Not the railroad's, not the state's. Tomás Marchetti had stood on this apron of gravel the morning of the last run, twenty-two years old and certain of his brakes, and he'd waved to the yard crew and climbed aboard and never come home except as a headline and a verdict.

The boreholes were placed where the shot plan required. But when Lena marked the final hole herself—the one nearest the portal's northern rib, dead center in the old loading apron—she set her chalk mark exactly where the historical society's photographs showed a man had once stood with a clipboard, checking brake pressures, three days before the crash.

"There," she said. "That one goes there."

Odell looked at the mark, then at her, and didn't ask why. He just handed her the drill template.

* * *

The night before the blast, the whole crew camped at the portal. There was no technical reason for it—the firing was scheduled for ten the next morning, the access road closed, the safety perimeter staked out half a mile down-valley. But nobody suggested driving back to the bunkhouse, and nobody suggested leaving either, so they built a fire in the lee of the equipment trailer and ate Marta's beans and passed a bottle of the good whiskey Odell had been saving for an occasion nobody could have predicted.

"To the *Cascadia*," Teo offered, raising his cup.

"To the forty-one," Marta said—the count of the dead, which the amended record now correctly attributed to cut brake lines and a railroad's purchased silence, not to a young engineer's error.

"To Ada Kowalczyk," Odell added, "who carried that alone for twenty years."

They drank. The fire cracked. Up the valley, the river ran high with spring melt, loud in the dark, but the bulkhead was coming down tomorrow and the water behind it would go with it, released into the channel the state had cut, and after that the tunnel would hold nothing but stone.

Lena sat a little apart, as she often did, and found her father's notebook in her jacket—the original, the one she'd testified with, returned to her by the county archivist with a receipt and a strange formality, as if it were a relic. In a way it was. She turned the pages by firelight: his handwriting, quick and slanted, the brake-pressure readings that fell off day by day, the marginal note in red pencil—*third time this week. Told yards. They say schedule.* He'd known. He'd documented it. He'd done everything right except survive the men who needed him wrong.

"You're doing the thing again," Teo said, dropping onto the gravel beside her.

"What thing."

"The talking-to-a-dead-relative thing. You've got the face."

She huffed. "You've gotten bold since the bridge."

"I nearly died on that bridge. It reorganized my priorities." He was quiet a moment, turning his cup. Then, more seriously: "Tomorrow. When it comes down. Are you going to be okay?"

Lena considered lying, decided he'd earned better. "I don't know. Twenty years I thought that tunnel was where my father's name went to die. Tomorrow it stops existing. And I keep waiting to feel like I'm losing something, and instead I just feel—" She searched for it. "Lighter. Like somebody finally put down a load I forgot I was carrying."

"That's not grief?"

"It might be. Grief and release wear the same coat sometimes." She bumped his shoulder with hers. "Ask me tomorrow."

* * *

The morning of the shot was clear and cold, the kind of high-country morning where the light comes down hard and honest and hides nothing.

They cleared the perimeter by nine. The state engineer observed from the overlook with a stopwatch and a clipboard; a handful of townspeople had driven up as far as the road closure allowed and stood at the barrier line, small figures against the bright gravel—among them, Lena noticed, a woman in her seventies with white hair and a wool coat, standing very straight. Ada Kowalczyk had not announced she was coming. She hadn't needed to.

At 9:55, Lena made the final circuit alone. Every charge tied in. Every circuit tested. The chalk mark at the northern rib long since drilled and loaded, her father's footing now holding four pounds of explosive and a legacy. She put her palm flat against the cold bulkhead steel, just for a second.

"Okay, Dad," she said. "Bring it down clean."

Then she walked back to the firing point, took the exploder box from Teo—who had asked nicely, as promised—and keyed the charge.

The mountain exhaled.

It wasn't the Hollywood roar. It was deeper than sound, a pressure wave that slapped the valley walls and came back doubled, and then the north slope above the portal shrugged exactly as designed, hundreds of tons of rock sliding down in a slow gray avalanche that swallowed the portal mouth, the bulkhead, the apron, the boreholes, the chalk mark—all of it folding into darkness and dust. The dust cloud rolled out over the river and hung there, gold in the sun, and beneath it the tunnel ceased to be a place anyone could enter ever again.

Nobody spoke for a full minute. The echo came back off the far ridge, softer and softer, and then there was only the river.

Ada Kowalczyk took off her hat. After a moment, one by one, so did the rest of them.

* * *

By afternoon the dust had settled and the crew was loading the last of the gear, and that was when the courier truck came back up the valley, faster than seemed wise on that road.

The driver found Lena at the trailer and handed over an envelope—thicker than the last one, and heavier in the hand in a way that had nothing to do with paper. Inside was a letter from the State Historical Preservation Office, and beneath it, a second letter on different letterhead entirely.

The first confirmed the seal: demolition certified complete, site formally closed, case file archived. Routine. She skimmed it.

The second letter took longer to understand, because her eyes kept snagging on the figure in the third paragraph.

*In recognition of the recovery and documentation of materials of significant historical value during your operations at the Cascade Tunnel site—including the Marchetti field notebook and associated evidence—the Board has approved a preservation grant in the amount of $86,000, payable to Marchetti Salvage & Rail Works upon submission of final expedition documentation.*

Eighty-six thousand dollars. She read it three times. She did the arithmetic she couldn't stop herself from doing—payroll arrears, the equipment loans, the bridge insurance deductible, the winter fuel bill—and watched the numbers, for the first time in years, come out on the right side of zero. All of it. Every debt. Cleared, with margin to spare.

Odell appeared at her elbow, reading upside down, because of course he was. He let out a low whistle. "That's payroll."

"That's payroll," Lena agreed. "That's everything."

"Plus interest we don't owe anymore." He straightened up, and there was something in his face she rarely saw there—something almost like ease. "You know what this means."

"It means I need to write a very thorough final report."

"It means," Odell said, "that for the first time since I've known you, you're going to have to decide what this crew does next instead of what it does to survive."

Lena looked up the valley—at the fresh scar of rock over the portal, already browning at the edges, already becoming landscape; at the river running free below it; at her crew scattered down the grade, laughing about something, loading chain like men and women who knew exactly where they belonged.

She folded the grant letter into her jacket, next to the notebook.

"First," she said, "somebody owes me a final report. And then—" she smiled, and it surprised her how easily it came— "then we find out what's north of here."

Down the valley, the northbound freight whistled through the gorge, long and clear, like an answer arriving early.
