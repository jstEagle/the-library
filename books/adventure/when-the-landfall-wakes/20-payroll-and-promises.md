## 20. Payroll and Promises

The envelope from the state salvage office arrived on a Tuesday, thick as a sandwich and smelling faintly of the river it had been fished out of. Lena set it on the bunkhouse table without opening it, the way you'd set down something that might still be alive.

"Open it," Odell said, not looking up from the stove where he was burning eggs with his usual precision.

"You open it."

"I signed nothing. You're the one who filed the claim."

Teo leaned in the doorway, arm still in its sling, hair growing back over the scar the generator darkness had given him. "If it's bad news, I vote we burn the bunkhouse and start fresh somewhere with better weather."

Lena slid her thumb under the flap. Inside: a cover letter, a schedule of fees, and a check made out to Marchetti Salvage & Rail Recovery for an amount with more digits than she'd written on any document in her life. The salvage fees for the pumps, the rail, the winch work, the shoring timber—every hour they'd bled into that mountain, priced out at last by someone in a warm office who had never smelled tunnel water.

She read it twice. Then she read it aloud, because some numbers needed air to be believed.

Odell turned off the burner. Teo sat down hard on the bench like his legs had been unplugged. Nobody said anything for a long moment, and then Odell said, quietly, "That clears us."

"That clears us," Lena agreed. "The bank note. The parts debt at Hollis's yard. All of it."

"And payroll," Teo said. "Actual payroll. With, like, dates attached."

"With dates attached."

He laughed—a short, cracked sound, the laugh of a man who had spent four months being paid in promises and IOUs and Lena's cooking—and put his good hand flat on the table as if to feel the money through the wood.

It wasn't everything. The salvage fees covered the debts and the season's costs and a thin margin besides, but the margin was thin enough that Lena had already done the arithmetic three times, and each time it came up short of comfortable. Comfortable was coming from somewhere else. That letter was still two weeks out, sitting in the inbox of the Cascade County Historical Society, where Lena had mailed a proposal she'd drafted at two in the morning with shaking hands: preservation and documentation of the Cascade Tunnel wreck site, oral histories, recovery of artifacts for exhibit, one lump sum, payable upon completion of the final report.

She hadn't told the crew she'd applied. She wasn't sure yet whether that was prudence or superstition.

* * *

The historical society's answer came on a Friday, delivered not by mail but by a small round woman named Dr. Imogen Hale who drove up the gorge road in a hatchback that had no business surviving the switchbacks, parked it crooked beside the bunkhouse, and stepped out holding a folder against her chest like a hymnal.

"Ms. Marchetti." She looked around at the yard—the idle dozer, the stacked rail, the north portal visible up the track as a gray mouth in the mountainside—and something in her face softened. "I've read your report. All ninety pages of it."

"It ran long."

"It ran honest. That's rarer." She held out the folder. "The board voted unanimously. We can't pay what the bonds would have been worth—thank God nobody got those—but we can pay this. It includes stipends for your crew as consulting labor. Mr. Okonkwo's testimony about the demolition cuts is, frankly, the most valuable mining forensics we've received in a decade. And Ms. Kowalczyk has asked that her honorarium go to the crew fund. She says—" Hale glanced at the page, though she clearly knew it by heart— "she says she took enough off this mountain already."

Lena took the folder. Her hands were steady, which surprised her. Inside was a grant agreement, a check, and a photograph—Ada Kowalczyk at twenty-three, standing on the platform at Cascade station, squinting into sun, alive in a way photographs rarely manage.

"She came to the archive last month," Hale said. "Sat with the amended record for an hour. Didn't say much. Just touched the page where your father's name was corrected." She paused. "You should know the amendment drew press. Small press—a regional paper, a rail history journal. But it drew it. There may be letters. There may be visitors. There may be people who want to talk to you."

"People want to talk to me about a lot of things lately."

"Yes. But these will be the kind worth having."

After Hale's hatchback fishtailed back down toward town, Lena stood in the yard a while with the folder under her arm, doing the new arithmetic. Debts cleared. Grant secured. Payroll funded through winter and past it. For the first time since she'd signed Vane's contract on a night she still dreamed about, the crew's future was not a cliff edge with a rope tied around somebody's waist.

She carried the folder inside and pinned the check to the corkboard above the payroll ledger, next to the old oath—*no jobs above the Cascade line*—which Odell had never taken down, even after they broke it. It hung there between them all season like a scar. Now, looking at it, Lena found she didn't want to take it down either. Some rules you keep on the wall so you remember why you broke the only one that mattered.

* * *

The city job offer arrived the following week, addressed to Teo by name, in an envelope with a skyline embossed on the corner.

Meridian Infrastructure Group. Assistant project engineer, transit division. Salary printed in bold. Benefits in bullet points. A start date three weeks out. Someone at Meridian had read the newspaper coverage of the hearing—everyone had, eventually; the photo of Lena at the witness table with her father's notebook open in front of her had run above the fold—and decided that a young engineer who'd survived the Cascade job was exactly the kind of hire that made a résumé sing.

Teo read it standing in the doorway, his sling making the paper tremble. He read it twice. Then he walked out to the shop where Lena was re-packing a bearing under Odell's supervision and laid the letter on the bench between them.

"Well?" he said. "Say something. Somebody's supposed to tell me I'm an idiot or a genius, and Odell just keeps sanding that block like he's deaf."

"I heard every word," Odell said, sanding.

Lena wiped her hands and picked up the letter. She read it slowly, giving it the respect it deserved, because it *was* respectable—that salary could buy a life with walls and heat and a bed that didn't list to port. "This is real," she said. "This is a good offer. Meridian doesn't make mistakes like this."

"I know."

"You'd be working on things that move people instead of things that rust."

"I know."

"You'd be sleeping somewhere the river can't reach you."

"Lena." He laughed, but it caught. "I know. I did the math. I always do the math. That's my whole thing—you hired me because I do the math fast and I don't flinch at the answer." He sat down on the upturned crate by the bench, suddenly looking very young, which he was, which they all sometimes forgot. "Here's the math. Four months ago I was digging ditches in Kelso for a man who docked my pay when it rained. Then you pulled me out of a gorge with a come-along and a chain, and you didn't yell at me for freezing up, and Odell taught me pump curves at two in the morning because I asked, and when the lights went out in that tunnel and I thought—I genuinely thought—I was going to die in the dark, you stood in the water and talked me through the dark like it was a room you'd been in before." He looked down at his sling. "Nobody at Meridian is going to stand in the water."

Lena said nothing. There was nothing to say that wouldn't cheapen it.

"So the math says," Teo went on, "that the number on this letter is smaller than the number of what I'd be leaving. Which means I'm declining it. Which means I need you to say yes to another season before I lose my nerve, because I've already drafted the decline email in my head four times and each time it gets politer and I trust myself less."

Odell set down his sanding block. "Boy," he said, "you draft emails in your head?"

"All the time. It's how I argue."

"He argues in drafts," Odell told Lena, as if reporting a medical condition. "Sign him."

Lena stuck out her hand. Teo shook it, then ruined the formality by hugging her one-armed, sling and all, and she let him, and over his shoulder she saw Odell smiling at the wall like a man watching weather break.

"One condition," she said into his ear. "Next time a city comes calling, you tell me *before* you've drafted four emails. I don't want to find out I almost lost you from an envelope."

"Deal. Same goes for you. Next time you apply for a grant at two in the morning, wake me up. I do good cover letters."

She pulled back. "How did you know about the grant?"

"Saw the envelope in the trash. You write 'historical society' on things and throw them away like nobody reads garbage." He grinned. "I read garbage. It's how I do the math."

* * *

Odell's decision required no envelope, no letter, no math at all—or rather, the math had been done decades ago, and he simply presented the result.

He did it the way he did everything: without announcement. On the first of the month, when Lena posted the new season's roster on the bunkhouse corkboard, his name was already at the top in his own square carpenter's handwriting, ink dry, position filled. Foreman. Season six. She stared at it for a full minute before she realized what she was looking at.

"You could have asked," she said, finding him in the kitchen.

"You could have offered."

"I was going to offer."

"I know. But you get a look when you're about to ask somebody for something. Like you're braced for them to leave." He cracked two eggs into the pan, which immediately began to burn, because Odell cooked the way he did everything else—competently, and slightly too hot. "Twenty-two years I worked for companies that saw me as a line item. Eleven years I watched you spend yourself like the crew was a debt you owed the world. I'm not signing on because you need a foreman. I'm signing on because for once in my life I want to watch what happens when the books are clean and nobody's scared." He flipped the eggs. They were, miraculously, perfect. "Also the pension situation down in the valley is a disgrace and I refuse on principle."

"Odell Reyes-Marchetti Salvage would collapse without you."

"Don't put my name on the company."

"Too late. Filed the paperwork Tuesday."

He turned, spatula raised, genuinely alarmed. "You did not."

"Hale suggested it. Historical societies love continuity." She was lying about the paperwork and he knew it, and they stood there grinning at each other across the burnt-egg smell like a pair of kids who'd gotten away with something, and neither of them mentioned that *Reyes* was her father's name and that putting it on a company sign was its own kind of amendment to the record.

That evening the whole crew ate together—Teo, Odell, Lena, plus Sable and Ruiz who'd come up from town for the payroll celebration, plus, unexpectedly, Ada Kowalczyk, who had written ahead to say she'd be passing through on the stagecoach line and would "take a plate if there's room." There was room. There was always going to be room.

They ate on the porch because the evening was finally warm, the first true warmth of the spring, the snowline retreating up the peaks like something losing interest. Ada sat in the best chair and accepted it without apology. Somebody produced a bottle of rye that Odell claimed had been "for emergencies." This, everyone agreed, qualified.

"To the crew," Lena said, raising her cup.

"To the record," Ada said, correcting her gently, and they drank to both.

Later, when the others had gone in and the mountain had gone blue with dusk, Ada stayed in her chair with her blanket and her half-cup, and Lena sat on the step below her like a girl at a grandmother's knee, which was, in every way that counted, exactly what she was.

"Do you know what I thought about," Ada said, "for twenty years? Not the crash. Not the money. I thought about the sound the brake line made when it parted. Like a whipcrack, but wrong—lower. I've never heard anything else make that sound. Sometimes a door hinge gets close." She turned the cup in her hands. "And now I think: I kept that sound to myself for twenty years, and all anyone had to do was ask."

"I should have asked sooner."

"You were nine years old, child. The asking was the railroad's job, and the railroad chose not to." She reached down and put her hand on Lena's head, briefly, the way you'd steady something valuable. "Your father used to wave at me from the cab when the morning freight passed my fence. Every day. Never missed. Twenty years I couldn't look at that stretch of track." She looked up the valley now, toward the sealed portal, invisible in the dark but present the way weather is present. "Yesterday I rode past it on the stage and I waved. Silly old woman, waving at a mountain. But it felt right."

"It doesn't sound silly."

"No," Ada said. "It sounds like spring."

* * *

They spent the last week of the month doing the unglamorous resurrection work: restocking the sheds, servicing the dozer, repainting the company name on the truck—*Marchetti*, and beneath it now, smaller, *est. by E. Reyes, 1901*, because if the record could be amended, so could a truck door. Teo repacked every bearing on the rolling stock and labeled every tool, including several he labeled purely to annoy Odell with his penmanship. Money moved through the account in Hollis's yard and came back as parts, and the parts came back as capability, and capability sat in the yard gleaming like a promise kept.

On the last Friday, Lena balanced the books and found a number she had never seen before in her life: surplus. Not much. Enough. She wrote the crew's winter retainers first, then a bonus line for each of them, then sat looking at the remainder until Odell leaned over her shoulder.

"Emergency fund," he said.

"We have an emergency fund."

"We have a coffee tin with forty dollars in it and a note that says 'emergency.' Put it in the actual account. Name it something boring so future-you doesn't raid it."

"'Landfall Reserve,'" Lena said.

"Boring," Odell said approvingly. "Done."

She wired the transfer, closed the ledger, and felt the strangest sensation—not relief, exactly, and not peace, but something adjacent to both. Lightness with mass to it. The feeling of a train running on rail that's been properly gauged: not effortless, never effortless, but *true*. Everything aligned. Everything bearing weight the way it was built to.

Outside, the light was going gold and long, and the high country was opening. Snowmelt roared in the gorge, the passes were clearing one by one, and the northbound freight that ran the low line through the valley would be taking on orders at the Cascade junction come Monday morning.

Lena stepped out onto the porch. Teo was on the roof patching shingles badly and happily. Odell was in the garden arguing with the soil. Down the valley, the rails caught the last of the sun and threw it back in a single bright line, pointing north, pointing up, pointing at all that country waking after its long white sleep.

Her father had loved this season. He used to say the mountains forgave nothing and forgot less, but that every spring they carried you forward anyway, if you asked them right.

Lena stood in the yard a moment longer, listening to the river rise, feeling the pull of the open line like a hand on her collar.

Then she went inside, took the blank order book down from the shelf, and started writing the manifest for a run north.
