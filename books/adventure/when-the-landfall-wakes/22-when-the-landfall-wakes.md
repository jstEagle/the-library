## 22. When the Landfall Wakes

The landfall woke on a Tuesday, the way it always did, without ceremony and without mercy.

Lena heard it from the bunkhouse porch at four in the morning—a deep, grinding groan from up the gorge where the snowfields let go all at once and gave themselves to the river. By dawn the water below the north portal had risen six feet and turned the color of wet concrete, carrying whole trees sideways like matchsticks. The mountains were done holding their breath. Spring had come to the high country, and it came the way everything came up here: all at once, taking what it wanted, leaving the rest rearranged.

She stood with a tin cup of coffee going cold in her hands and watched the river work, and for the first time in twenty years she felt nothing about it but respect. No dread. No old wound opening. Just the clean arithmetic of water finding its level, which was more than most things managed.

Behind her, inside the bunkhouse, Odell was frying eggs and arguing with Teo about whether a man could eat four of them before a twelve-hour ride or whether that was how you ended up sick at the first trestle. Marisol was laughing at both of them. Dree sat by the window with her boots already laced, watching the road like she didn't trust it to stay put, which was fair—the switchback had shed two more slumps since the thaw started, and the county had posted signs nobody read.

"Train's late," Dree said, not turning around.

"Freights are never late," Odell called from the stove. "They're just early for tomorrow."

"It's a work train running special for us. It can be late."

Lena drank her coffee and let them fill the room. This was the sound she'd been listening for her whole life without knowing it: her people, loud and unafraid, packing up a home they'd built out of bad contracts and cold mornings, getting ready to leave it better than they'd found it.

* * *

They'd spent three weeks doing it—leaving it better. The state's sealing order had come down in April, signed by a deputy commissioner who'd read the amended record twice and then shaken Lena's hand like he was afraid of it. The Cascade Tunnel would be closed permanently. Not drowned this time, not buried under a lie, but sealed with engineered bulkheads and a concrete cap and a bronze plaque that said, in language no lawyer could sand down, what had happened there and who had been blamed for it wrongly.

The crew had placed the charges themselves. That had been Odell's idea, and when Lena thought about it now—and she thought about it more than she expected to—it was the thing that had finally let her sleep through a night without dreaming of brake lines.

Her father had stood at that south portal with a drill rig and a surveyor's eye and seen the mountain plain as daylight. He'd written it down in a field notebook with a cracked spine, flagged the faulty brakes, filed his warnings, and been buried under the wreckage of other men's greed. Twenty years later his daughter had stood in the same portal with a charge bag and set the pattern herself—not to erase him, but to close the door behind him properly. To give the tunnel an ending instead of a wound.

The blast had gone off at noon on a clear day. The crew had stood back on the haul road, and the mountain had coughed once, a white plume rising off the south face, and then settled into itself like an old man pulling a blanket up. Teo had cried, quietly, and pretended he hadn't. Marisol had put her hand on Lena's shoulder and left it there until Lena's breathing evened out.

Nobody had said anything about closure. Up here you didn't get closure; you got endings, and you made your own peace with the difference.

* * *

The money had come in pieces, the way money did.

The salvage fees first—Vane's contract honored in full by his creditors' committee, because a contract was a contract even when the man who wrote it was finished. Then the historical society grant, which Dree had chased down with a patience Lena hadn't known she possessed, three months of letters and phone calls until some archivist in the capital finally read Ada Kowalczyk's sworn statement and understood what the crew had pulled out of that wreck. The grant covered the debts. All of them. The parts invoice Odell had been carrying in his breast pocket since November. The winter Lena had floated on credit and stubbornness. The whole ledger, wiped clean.

Teo had gotten a call from a firm in the city—structural assessment, real salary, a desk with a window. He'd taken the call on the porch with the door shut, which fooled nobody, and afterward he'd come inside and eaten two eggs in silence and then said, "I told them I'd think about it," and everyone had nodded like that was a normal thing to say when you'd already decided.

He decided the morning they loaded out. He threw his duffel into the cab of the supply truck, looked at the city-bound branch line diverging south at the junction, and climbed aboard the northbound freight instead without a word. Lena caught his eye across the yard and raised an eyebrow, and he shrugged, and that was the whole conversation. Some men needed to see the city once to know they didn't want it. Teo had seen enough of it in the newspaper clipping business to last him.

Odell had signed on for another season before the ink dried on the grant letter. "Where else am I gonna go," he'd said, "that needs me this bad?" It wasn't sentiment. It was arithmetic, the kind Odell trusted: a crew that had held together through sabotage and flood and a fight in a flooding adit was worth more than any sum on paper, and he knew it, and so did she.

* * *

The freight came up the valley at nine, right on schedule, which proved Dree wrong and made her day.

It was a work extra—two diesels and a string of flatcars and one battered caboose the railroad had dredged up from somewhere out of its own past. The crew had hired it legitimate, with a waybill and everything. Lena had insisted on that. After the Cascade job she was done with gray arrangements, done with favors that curdled into leverage, done with every version of "we'll sort the paperwork later." If they rode north to the new line, they rode there on paper anyone could read.

The new line itself was the part she still couldn't quite believe. A mining concern up past Alder Gap, reopening an old high-country route everyone had written off—except their survey had come back clean, and their permits were real, and their foreman had heard about a salvage crew that had brought every soul home from the worst job in the territory and wanted that crew specifically. The offer had arrived by mail, on letterhead, with numbers attached. No cash advances pressed into palms at bunkhouse doors. No deadlines built to break people.

"You're smiling," Marisol said, coming up beside her at the platform edge.

"I'm not."

"You are. It's terrifying. Do it again."

Lena looked at her. Marisol had a new scar along her forearm from the generator sabotage night, thin and pale now, and she wore it like a rank insignia. They all carried marks from that winter. Teo's ribs ached in the rain where the darkness had dropped a winch hook on him. Odell's hands had never fully forgiven the pump work in the black water. And Lena—Lena had the notebook, her father's notebook, riding in an oilcloth sleeve in her jacket's inner pocket, next to the duplicate pages she'd given away and the ones she'd kept. She carried lighter than she used to. That was the strange part. She'd gone into that tunnel expecting to find a wreck and had come out having found a father instead—one made of ink and field notes and a dead man's careful handwriting, flagging faulty brakes days before the crash, doing his job right in a world that punished him for it.

"Boarding in five," the conductor called, and the crew moved.

* * *

They rode the flats first, through country just turning green, and then the line began to climb and the windows filled with the evidence of the thaw. Water everywhere. Ribbons of melt falling off cut banks. Culverts roaring. In the high meadows the snow still lay deep and blue-shadowed, but the faces of the ridges were bare and streaming, the whole landscape wringing itself out after a long hard winter.

Lena rode in the lead locomotive. The engineer—a railroader named Sato who'd worked the district twenty years and remembered her father distantly, vaguely, the way men remember a good hand from another crew—had offered her the seat without being asked. Word traveled. What had happened at the hearing had traveled further than she liked, honestly; she'd been recognized twice in town by strangers who wanted to shake her hand, and she'd let them, because refusing felt like ingratitude toward something larger than her discomfort.

Now she sat in the right-hand seat with the throttle under her hand and the mountain unspooling ahead, and Sato drank coffee and watched her handle the grade like she'd been born to it, which, in fairness, she had.

"Your dad ran this district," Sato said somewhere past the second summit. "Before my time mostly. But the old heads talked about him. Marchetti, they'd say. Man could read rail like sheet music."

Lena kept her eyes on the curve ahead. "That so."

"That's so." Sato sipped his coffee. "Funny thing about records. They get amended, but talk doesn't need amending. Talk just waits."

She didn't answer. There wasn't anything to answer. But she thought about it for the next ten miles, the way the truth had lived on in the mouths of railroaders all those years her father's file said otherwise—unofficial, unwritten, patient. The archive had fixed the paper. The talk had never needed fixing. Between the two of them, maybe, the man had been kept whole.

* * *

They stopped at the Alder Gap water tower at midday, and Vane was waiting on the platform.

Lena saw him from the cab before anyone else did—a tall figure in a good coat gone shabby at the cuffs, standing apart from the handful of locals, hands in his pockets, watching the train like a man watching weather he couldn't do anything about. Her chest tightened, an old reflex. Then it loosened. Whatever Cassius Vane had left to take from her, he'd lost the means to take it.

She climbed down anyway.

He didn't approach until she was standing on the gravel, and even then he stopped a respectful ten feet off, and up close she could see what ruin actually looked like on a man: not dramatic, just worn. The certainty gone out of his posture. The eyes that used to run arithmetic on everything they saw now just... seeing.

"I'm not here to ask for anything," he said.

"Good. Answer's the same either way."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth—not quite a smile. "Fair." He looked up at the locomotive, at the crew visible in the caboose windows, at the flatcars loaded with their gear. "Northbound."

"Alder Gap line. Reopening."

"Good steel up there, if the reports are true. High work, though. Cold."

"We've had our cold."

He nodded slowly. He shifted his weight, and for a moment she thought he'd leave it there, turn around, walk back into whatever diminished life the hearing had left him. Then he reached into his coat and took out an envelope, creased soft from handling, and held it out.

"What's that?"

"The rest of the duplicate pages. Your copies—I kept two sheets back, that first night, before I returned the notebook. I told myself it was insurance." His jaw worked. "It wasn't insurance. It was me not being able to let go of the last thing I had of him. Of either of them." He said *him* and meant his brother, and they both knew it, and neither dressed it up. "I've read them enough times to have them by heart. They should be with you. With the record."

Lena took the envelope. Inside were two photostats, her father's handwriting crisp under the copying machine's grain: brake-line inspection notes, dated eight days before the derailment. She'd read these words a hundred times. Reading them again, standing on a sunlit platform with the river loud in the gorge behind the town, felt different than reading them in the dark of the mail car. Lighter. Like rereading a letter from someone who'd made it home.

"He'd have liked the crew you kept together," Vane said abruptly. "My brother would have too. He was a brakeman. Third car back." He looked at the mountains. "I spent two years and everything I had trying to buy my way to a truth that was sitting in a filing cabinet the whole time. You spent one winter and dug it out with your hands." He straightened his coat, a small futile gesture. "I don't know what you're supposed to do with that information. I haven't figured it out yet."

"Start smaller," Lena said. "That's what I'm doing."

He studied her for a long moment. "Is it working?"

"Not yet. Check back in a season."

This time the almost-smile made it all the way. He touched his hat brim—an old-fashioned gesture, courtly, from a man who'd learned manners somewhere before he'd learned ruthlessness—and walked away down the platform, unhurried, a ruined man carrying himself like a free one, which maybe, finally, he was.

Lena stood with the envelope in her hand until the conductor's whistle sounded, and then she climbed back up into the cab and put the pages in the oilcloth sleeve with the rest, and closed the pocket over them, and took the throttle.

* * *

The high country opened above Alder Gap in the late afternoon, and it was worth every mile of the climb.

The line ran a shelf cut into the mountainside, the drop falling away sheer on the right into a valley full of moving water and light. Snowfields clung to the cirques up top, brilliant white against rock the color of iron. Somewhere above the clouds, more snow waited to come down and join the rivers. The landfall was awake all around them, working, restless, magnificent, forgiving nothing and forgetting less—and carrying them forward all the same. That was the bargain the mountains offered. It was the only bargain they offered. You took it or you stayed home.

Marisol leaned out the caboose cupola and shouted something into the wind that got torn to pieces before it arrived. Teo stood in the open door with his face turned into the cold air like a man drinking. Odell sat inside at the little desk, already sketching the first week's work plan in pencil, steady as tide. Dree watched the shelf line ahead with a surveyor's squint, cataloguing slumps and culverts, already knowing the route better than the maps did.

Sato glanced over. "Want her the rest of the way?"

"She's yours," Lena said. "I'll take the evening shift if you'll have me."

"Have you? Marchetti—" He caught himself, grinned, corrected. "Reyes. I'd have to fight Odell for the roster."

She laughed—actually laughed, out loud, a sound that surprised her coming out—and settled back while the rails sang under them. Below, the river ran silver and violent and free, doing exactly what it had always done, going exactly where it had always been going, asking nothing of the world except room.

Her father had loved this stretch. She knew that now, from the notebook—from a margin note in his handwriting, a single line she'd read forty times: *Best light on the Gap comes at six. Stop pretending you don't notice.*

She checked her watch. Quarter to six.

"Sato. Slow to restricted through the Gap curves. There's a view."

"There's a view," he agreed, and eased her back.

The train came around the great shoulder of the mountain into the long golden light, and the whole crew was at the windows, and the valley opened below them like a page turning, and Lena Marchetti drove her people north into the rest of their lives at a speed the timetable would have called excessive and the mountains, watching, would have called correct.
