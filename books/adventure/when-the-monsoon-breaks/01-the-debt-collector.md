## 1. The Debt Collector

The rain in Puerto Cobre fell like it had a grudge, and Quentin Vane had learned to live under it the way other men lived under a sentence.

He was drinking cheap rum at the back of the Bar Estrella, where the ceiling fan turned with the enthusiasm of a dying man and the walls wept salt, when Halvard Reeve found him. He'd have smelled the money before he saw it — Reeve wore a linen suit that had never been rained on, which in this town was either sorcery or wealth, and probably both.

"Vane." The man sat down uninvited, setting a leather folder on the table like a doctor laying out instruments. "You're harder to find than your reputation suggests."

"My reputation's easy to find. It just isn't for sale."

"Everything's for sale. That's the whole engine of the world." Reeve signaled the bartender with two fingers — an order, not a request — and waited until two glasses of something amber and expensive arrived before he spoke again. "Meridian Survey Group. Seven people went up the Khasra Gorge eleven weeks ago. Radio silence for nine."

Quentin drank his rum and said nothing. Silence was a currency upriver; he'd spent enough of it to know its exchange rate.

"The gorge closes with the monsoon," Reeve continued. "Three weeks, maybe less this year. After that, no one goes in or comes out until October. If my people are alive, they're alive now. If they're not—" He spread his hands, a small elegant gesture that admitted nothing. "I need to know which."

"So hire someone who hasn't been blacklisted."

"I did. Three of them. Two declined. One is currently in a hospital in Santa Marta with a machete wound he declines to explain." Reeve smiled without warmth. "Which leaves you. The only guide who's run the Khasra source country twice and lived. And the only one who doesn't care what anyone thinks of him anymore, which I'm told makes you cheap."

"I'm not cheap."

"You're broke." Reeve opened the folder. "I know about the debt. The outfitters in Quito. The gear you pawned in Iquitos. The fourteen months of interest compounding like mold." He slid a single sheet across the table. A number was printed on it, followed by several zeros, and beneath the number, in smaller type: *all obligations discharged upon completion.*

Quentin looked at the number for a long time. Not because it tempted him — though it did, the way water tempts a drowning man — but because nothing in this business arrived clean. Money like that came with hooks in it, and hooks were how he'd ended up here, in a border town where the rain never stopped and nobody would shake his hand.

"A client died," Quentin said. "That's why they blacklisted me. You've read the file. You still want me?"

"I want the Khasra Gorge, and you're the only key that fits." Reeve leaned forward. "I don't care what happened on the Serrano climb. Half the people in this industry are liars and the other half are worse. Bring my team out, or bring me proof of what happened to them, and the number is yours."

"And if I say no?"

Reeve gathered his folder, unhurried, a man who had already priced every outcome. "Then in three weeks the monsoon closes the gorge, and whatever's up there stays up there. And you stay here." He glanced around the bar — the weeping walls, the broken fan, the rain hammering the tin roof like applause for no one. "I've seen what staying here does to men, Vane. It's slower than drowning but it arrives at the same place."

* * *

Quentin didn't answer that night. He walked home through the flooded streets instead, past the shuttered chandlery where he used to buy rope on credit, past the hostel where he owed three weeks' rent to a woman who had stopped asking for it because asking had become pointless. His room was a cot, a stove, and a wall map of the Khasra drainage that he'd drawn from memory during his first winter in exile, when he still believed he'd go back.

He stood in front of the map a long time.

The gorge ran northeast off the old survey charts like a crack in the world — forty kilometers of limestone and whitewater between the last government checkpoint and the high plateau where the maps simply stopped, marked *incomplete* in some cartographer's tired hand. He'd run it twice. The first time, young and lucky. The second time, older and luckier still, though he hadn't known it then. Luck felt infinite at thirty. At forty-one you could feel it thinning, like rope worn by use.

There were reasons not to go. He catalogued them the way he'd once catalogued anchors: the blacklist, the debt, the season, the fact that Reeve's money would buy him out of one hole and drop him into a deeper one. Men who took jobs like this didn't come back the same, if they came back. The gorge kept things. Everyone on the river knew that much.

But there was the number. And underneath the number, something he refused to look at directly, the way you refuse to look at the sun — because Elena Marsh's name had been on Reeve's list of the missing, third from the top, printed in a neat bureaucratic column: *E. Marsh, geologist.*

Elena Marsh. Who had stood up in a hotel conference room in Huaraz seven years ago, in front of forty people and a panel of inquiry, and testified in a clear steady voice that Quentin Vane had abandoned his client above the icefall. Who had watched him with those gray eyes while the panel stripped his certification, his bookings, his livelihood, everything but his name, and even the name only survived because it was attached to the disgrace.

He told himself the name on the list meant nothing. People from old lives surfaced in manifests all the time; the industry was small and the world was smaller. He told himself he'd go upriver for the money, for the debt, for the simple animal need to stop being poor in a town that fed on the poor.

He told himself all of it, standing in front of the map, and none of it held, and he knew exactly why, and he poured himself the last of the rum rather than think about it.

* * *

He found Reeve at the Hotel Miramar the next morning, sitting on the veranda with coffee and a satellite phone he wasn't using, watching the river run brown and fast below the town.

"Yes?" Reeve said, without turning.

"I'll take you up the gorge. My terms. I pick the route, I pick the pace, and when I say turn back, we turn back — including you."

"Agreed."

"And I want the full manifest. Names, roles, last known positions. Everything your office has."

Something flickered across Reeve's face then, quick as a fish under ice — gone before Quentin could name it. "Of course," Reeve said, and passed over a second folder, thicker than the first.

Quentin flipped it open right there on the veranda, because he wanted the decision finished before the doubt could catch up. Seven dossiers. Team leader, Tomas Iriarte, logistics specialist, hydrologist. Two porters contracted out of Cobre itself. A medic. A surveyor. A geologist.

*Elena Marsh.* Her photograph was four years old. She looked thinner than he remembered, and harder, and her eyes in the picture had the same expression they'd had in the conference room — steady, certain, absolutely sure she was right.

Seven years he'd carried what she said about him. Seven years of closed doors and lowered voices and the particular silence that follows a man into a room when everyone knows what he did — or what they say he did. And now she was upriver, missing, possibly dead, possibly waiting, and the monsoon was coming to close the door on all of it.

He shut the folder.

"We leave tomorrow," Quentin said. "First light, before the river rises any more than it already has."

"Wise." Reeve rose, extended a hand, and this time Quentin took it — dry, firm, the handshake of a man closing a purchase. "One condition of mine, Vane. Whatever you find up there — whoever you find — you bring it to me first. Before the authorities, before the press, before anyone. That's the deal."

It should have sounded like caution. It didn't. It sounded like a man protecting something, and Quentin filed that away with the rest of what he refused to examine.

"Tomorrow, then," Reeve said, and walked inside out of the rain.

Quentin stayed on the veranda a while longer, watching the brown water tear past the town toward the sea, carrying everything upstream with it — silt, branches, the occasional drowned thing. In three weeks the monsoon would land and the gorge would seal itself like a throat.

He went home and sharpened his knife, checked his rope, and lay awake listening to the rain, telling himself one more time that he was going upriver for the money.

By dawn he'd stopped believing it, and by the time he pushed the boat off the mud landing into the current, with the mist still hanging in the trees and the gorge opening ahead of him like a held breath, he'd stopped saying it at all.
