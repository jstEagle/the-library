## 2. Upstream Silence

The Khasra Gorge did not want him there. Quentin understood that by the second day, when the porters he'd hired in Rethi village turned back at the shrine stones and would not go farther, not for triple pay, not for anything. The eldest of them, a wiry man named Suren with a face like old rope, pressed Quentin's advance back into his hand.

"The river changes up there," Suren said, in the flat tone of a man stating weather. "It stops being water."

Then they were gone down the trail, and Quentin stood alone at the mouth of the upper gorge with six days of food, a climbing rope, and the particular silence of a place that had eaten people.

He went on.

* * *

The first camp was three days upstream, tucked under an overhang where the Meridian Survey's blue tarps still hung, bleached to the color of drowned sky. Abandoned — but not abandoned. Quentin crouched at the edge of it for a long time before he let himself believe what he was seeing.

The tents were struck cleanly. The cook fire was cold but stacked, ready to be lit again. Gear was cached under stone slabs in the careful, deliberate way of people who intended to come back. And the radio — the radio sat on its case in the center of camp, and someone had taken it apart.

Not smashed. Taken apart. The power board was missing, wrapped separately in oilcloth and buried beneath the fire ring, which was where Quentin found it only because he'd spent ten years learning how guides hide things from clients and one bad year learning how investigators hide things from him. He sat back on his heels with the board in his hands and felt the shape of the truth settle over him like the coming rain.

You don't sabotage your own radio because you're lost. You do it so no one can call you, and so no one can trace your signal.

They hadn't vanished. They'd gone dark.

He should have turned around then. That was the honest accounting, later: there was a moment, kneeling in that empty camp with the river talking to itself below, when turning around was still possible, still cheap. Instead he repacked the radio exactly as he'd found it, buried the board again, and climbed higher into the gorge.

* * *

**From the field journal of Elena Marsh, entry dated the 9th:**

*Tomas has ordered the radios stripped. Dev says we've crossed some line we can't uncross, and Tomas looked at him with such patience, such terrible patience, and said: "We crossed it at the source, Dev. Everything since is just bookkeeping."*

*I keep thinking about the assay results. I have them memorized whether I want to or not. The numbers don't behave like numbers. They behave like a door.*

*I asked Marta today if she was afraid. She said she was past afraid, that fear is for things you can still escape. I didn't ask what she meant. I think I already know.*

*There is a man coming up the gorge. Tomas says there isn't. But I saw the smoke from the lower camps this morning, and smoke means either locals or a search party, and the locals stopped coming here generations ago.*

*If it's who I think it might be — God help us all, and God help me most of all.*

* * *

The second camp told him more than the first. It was perched on a shelf two hundred feet above the waterline, defensible, with sightlines down both bends of the gorge — a camp chosen by someone thinking about being found, or about not being found too easily. Scattered among the ordinary debris of a survey team, Quentin found the wrongnesses: a crate of blasting caps, far more than any mineral survey required; a second radio antenna, coiled and hidden; boot prints leading away from camp toward a side ravine, and none returning along the same line.

And pinned under a mess tin, a page torn from a notebook, half-soaked, the ink feathering. He recognized the handwriting before he read a word. He'd spent four years hating that handwriting — the sworn statement, the inquiry, the neat, precise letters that had dismantled his career sentence by sentence.

*Elena Marsh.*

The note was logistical. Caches, bearings, a date. But at the bottom, in smaller script, almost as if she hadn't decided to write it:

*We are not lost. Remember that. Whatever they say later — we are not lost.*

Quentin read it four times. Then he folded it into his jacket, against his chest, where the rain couldn't get at it, and told himself the only reason he kept it was evidence.

The gorge narrowed after that. The walls leaned in until noon was a rumor, and the river ran green-black and fast beneath him, and the air took on a pressure like the whole sky leaning on his shoulders. Monsoon was five days out, maybe four. Reeve's money said find them; the mountains said find them fast.

On the sixth day he smelled sulfur, and above the sulfur something else — a faint metallic tang, like blood in the nose, like ore.

On the seventh day, he found the source.

* * *

**From the field journal of Elena Marsh, entry dated the 11th:**

*Marta cried tonight. Not from fear. From the maps. She spread them out and showed me the seam running under the gorge like a spine, and she said, "Do you understand what this is? This isn't a strike. This is a country changing hands."*

*Tomas has started sleeping with the sample case strapped to his wrist. He smiles more now. That frightens me more than the guns did.*

*Yes — guns. Dev brought them up from the second cache two days ago and nobody asked why a survey team has them. Nobody asks anything anymore. We just do the work and watch the water rise.*

*I wrote once that I believed in telling the truth even when it costs. I testified against Quentin Vane on that belief. I have thought about him every day since, and every day I have been less sure.*

*If he comes up this gorge — if it's him — I don't know whether I'll beg him to take me out of here or beg him to leave before Tomas decides he can't.*

*Both, probably. Both.*

* * *

The source was a wall of rock split by a fall of white water, and above it, hidden in a fold of the cliff where no government map marked so much as a trail, the Meridian Survey had built itself a fortress. Tarps lashed double. A stone windbreak. A cold forge where someone had been assaying samples, and the slag piles glittered when Quentin's headlamp swept across them — glittered wrong, dense and heavy, with a dull silver-green sheen he had no name for and didn't need one. He'd seen men kill over metals worth a tenth of this.

"Stop there."

The voice came out of the dark above him, and with it the click — unmistakable, universal — of a rifle being made ready. Quentin raised his hands slowly and kept them up.

"Quentin Vane," he called. "Reeve sent me. I'm the search party."

A long silence. Then a lantern flared, and Tomas Iriarte stepped into the light — a broad, gray-bearded man with the calm of someone who had already decided how everything would end. Behind him, shadows resolved into faces: a young geologist clutching a clipboard, a woman with red-rimmed eyes, a porter with a bandaged arm. And apart from the others, slightly forward, her hair tied back and her jaw set like a woman walking into a headwind —

Elena Marsh.

She went very still when she saw him. He watched her decide, in real time, what her face was going to do. What it finally did was nothing at all, which told him more than any expression could have.

"You came alone," Iriarte said. It wasn't surprise. It was inventory.

"Reeve's money only stretched that far."

"And the radio traffic? The inquiries?"

"Gorge ate them," Quentin said. "That's why I'm here."

Iriarte smiled, and it was a good smile, warm even, and Quentin felt the dread arrive fully formed, cold in his stomach, because he understood suddenly what his presence meant. Seven people hiding in a hole in the earth, guarding a secret worth a war — and now an outsider had walked into it, a witness, a loose end. Every hour he stayed alive here, he became complicit. Every hour after that, he became a problem.

"You must be exhausted," Iriarte said, lowering the rifle with the ease of a host. "Eat first. We'll talk about going down in the morning."

Behind him, Elena Marsh met Quentin's eyes across the firelight, and gave the smallest shake of her head.

Not *don't trust me.* Not *go back.*

*Don't sleep.*

Far below, the river changed its voice — deeper now, hungrier — and the first fat drops of the monsoon began to fall through the dark.
