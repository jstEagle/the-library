## 3. What the Source Keeps

The water came up in the night.

Quentin heard it before he understood it — a change in the river's voice, from the low mutter of a week's dry season to something deeper, older, like a throat clearing. He was on his feet with his boots half-laced before the thought finished forming: *the monsoon is early.*

The camp at the source was a wound in the gorge wall — three tents, a generator, crates stenciled with Reeve's company mark, all of it wedged into a shelf of rock forty feet above the water. And above it, veined through the cliff face, the gray-green shimmer Iriarte had been guarding like a man guards a wife he intends to sell.

Quentin found Elena at the edge of the shelf, watching the river rise around the drowned remains of their landing beach.

"It's coming up fast," she said. She didn't look at him. She hadn't looked at him properly since he'd walked into camp four days ago and said her name and watched her face do something complicated and then go still.

"How fast?"

"Fast enough that Tomas has stopped pretending we're leaving."

* * *

Elena Marsh had kept journals since she was nine years old. Her father had given her the first one, a red clothbound thing with a broken clasp, and told her: *write down what happened, because memory is a liar.* She had written through university, through her survey certification, through the Khasra expedition. And now, by generator light, in a tent that smelled of wet nylon and diesel, she wrote:

*I have to put this down exactly or I'll talk myself out of it again.*

*Tomas says the samples assay at concentrations no one will believe. He says governments will come for this gorge the way they come for oil fields, and that whoever holds the data holds the only leverage worth having. He says we stay until the buyer is confirmed. He says the boats are "a security risk."*

*He means Quentin Vane is a security risk.*

*And here is what I cannot stop writing, so I will write it:*

*Six years ago I stood in a hearing room in Kathmandu and told eleven men that Quentin Vane had led David Okonkwo above the fixed lines on the Lhotse face against protocol, and that David died because of it. That is what I said. What I did not say is that I was twenty-six years old, that David's family had retained counsel, that the expedition's insurer had made it very clear to me what my career would look like if the finding went the other way, and that I believed it anyway. That's the part I need to be honest about. I believed it. Mostly. The way you believe a thing you've been handed fully assembled, with the screws already tightened.*

*Then last year I read the recovered telemetry. The fixed lines had failed an hour before Quentin moved. David was already off-route when Quentin reached him. Quentin climbed into a wind nobody should have survived, to a man who was already dead, and the report I signed helped bury him for it.*

*I have carried that for fourteen months. I am carrying it now, upriver, in a camp run by a man who would burn the world for a rock sample.*

*Vane is outside. I can hear him re-rigging the rafts Tomas ordered abandoned. If we are going to die in this gorge, it will not be because I stayed quiet twice.*

* * *

Quentin worked the raft frame in the rain, hands numb, and did not hear her come up behind him until she spoke.

"I lied about you."

He kept his hands moving. "You'll have to be more specific. People lie about me professionally."

"The hearing. Lhotse. I testified that you took David above the lines." Rain ran off her hood. "I knew the lines had failed. Part of me knew. I let them tighten the screws on my doubt until there wasn't any left."

Quentin set down the rigging line. Six years he'd imagined this conversation — rehearsed it angry, rehearsed it drunk, rehearsed it once, memorably, as a speech delivered over her grave. None of the rehearsals fit the actual woman standing in front of him, soaked and shaking and offering him the thing he'd wanted most in the world at the exact moment he could least afford to hold it.

"Why tell me now?"

"Because Tomas is going to get us killed for data, and you're the only one here who's ever gotten people out of a place like this alive. Because you deserve to hear it from me and not from a tribunal transcript. And because—" her voice caught, steadied— "because if I die in this gorge having never said it, I'll have spent two lives being a coward instead of one."

Above them, a door banged. Iriarte stood on the rock shelf with a flare pistol in one hand and the sample case chained to his wrist, watching the rafts.

"No," Quentin said, understanding a half-second too late.

Iriarte fired twice. The first flare hit the lead raft's hull; the second found the fuel bladder they'd cached beneath the tarp. The raft went up in a bloom of orange that turned the falling rain to sparks, and the heat of it reached Quentin's face even across the distance, and then the burning rubber slid off the shelf, into the current, and was gone downstream in seconds — a funeral pyre running the river's length.

"There," Iriarte called down, calm as a man closing a ledger. "Now everyone's committed."

* * *

They went on foot at dawn, nine of them — Iriarte, Elena, three surveyors, four porters, Quentin — down the gorge's north bank where the map promised a porter trail that the map had never actually walked. The monsoon made landfall behind them like a door slamming. Rain came not in drops but in volumes, a vertical ocean, and the trail became a stream, and the stream became a chute.

By midday they reached the first gorge narrows and Quentin saw the problem before anyone else did: the trail vanished under a waterfall-fed torrent, and beyond it the bank pinched to nothing. Behind them, the river was climbing the walls.

"We cut high," he shouted over the water. "We traverse above the flood line and drop back in past the narrows. It costs us six hours."

"It costs us the handoff window," Iriarte shouted back. "The satellite window closes tonight. We go through."

"Through is underwater."

Iriarte looked at the torrent, then at Quentin, and Quentin watched him do the arithmetic — watched him arrive at the sum where four porters and three scientists were cheaper than a missed transmission. "Then some of us wait here while others push on."

"You're splitting the group in a flood zone."

"I'm prioritizing the asset." Iriarte touched the case at his wrist. "You were hired to find us, Vane. Consider yourself successful. The rest is weather."

That was when the young porter, Anselm, slipped.

It happened fast — wet rock, a loaded pack, a slope that had been dirt and became grease. He went down the bank toward the torrent and Quentin moved without deciding to, the way he had on Lhotse, the way he always had, and caught the boy's wrist against a root and held him while the water tried to take them both, and the team hauled them back up hand over hand.

When Quentin got his breath back, Iriarte was already walking away uptrail with the case, and two of the surveyors were following him, and Elena was standing over Quentin with her hand still outstretched from the haul line.

"He'll leave us here," she said.

"Yes."

"And you'll still get us out."

Quentin looked up the trail after Iriarte, then down at the river, which had eaten the last of the beach and was working on the trees. Somewhere upstream, the dam of rain that had been building for a week over the high plateau was beginning to move.

"Not all of us," he said. "Not unless we move now."

Behind them, faint under the rain, came a sound like the mountain clearing its throat — the deep, grinding crack of the gorge wall letting go somewhere above the source, sending its first load of water and stone down the channel toward everything they loved, owned, or owed.

Quentin got to his feet. "Run."
