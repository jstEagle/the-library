## 4. The River Gives Back

The gorge stopped being a place and became a throat around midnight.

Quentin felt the change before he heard it — a tremor through the soles of his boots, the rock itself shivering like a struck bell. Then the sound arrived: not the river anymore, but something older and angrier, a freight-train roar filling the canyon wall to wall. The monsoon had made landfall, and the Khasra was collecting everything the sky could give.

"Higher!" he screamed, and the word was swallowed whole. He grabbed the nearest porter — a boy called Devi, seventeen at most, all elbows and terror — and shoved him up the talus slope. Behind him the others strung out along the ledge like beads on a fraying line: the two remaining porters, the surveyor Okafor limping on a wrapped ankle, Iriarte half-dragged between them, and Elena, who had not left his side since the boats went into the flood and did not intend to.

They climbed. The water climbed faster.

By the time they reached the shelf — a fold of limestone forty feet above the channel, barely wide enough for six people to lie flat — the river had erased the route behind them. Quentin looked down at the place where the trail had been and saw brown water moving at the speed of a falling man. Whole trees cartwheeled past, roots first, like drowning giants.

Nobody slept. They pressed against the rock and let the rain hammer them and waited for morning, which came gray and indifferent.

* * *

They moved at first light along the high route — a goat path Quentin had used once, years ago, on a survey that had cost a man his life and Quentin his name. The path was a memory of a path: crumbling, slick, in places no wider than a boot. Below them the river raged. Above them the cliffs wept water in a hundred silver threads.

Devi went down at the second hour.

It happened the way these things always happen — slowly, then all at once. A hold crumbled. The boy slid, caught himself, slid again, and stopped with one leg jammed in a cleft and the rock beneath him giving way grain by grain. His scream was thin against the river's roar.

Quentin was moving before he decided to. He bellied out along the path, rain in his eyes, and got a fist around Devi's harness. The boy's ankle was wrong — folded at an angle that made Quentin's stomach clench — and his weight was dragging them both toward the edge, toward the brown thunder below.

"I have you," Quentin said. "Look at me. Only at me."

Devi looked at him. And Quentin held on, muscles screaming, boots finding purchase by faith alone, until Okafor's hands closed on his collar and they hauled the boy up onto stone that was barely stone at all.

Devi couldn't walk. That was the arithmetic of it. A shattered ankle, six hours of treacherous ground, a storm still unloading itself on the watershed above them. Quentin did the math every guide does and hates: the group's survival against the individual's.

Then he did the other math. The one that had kept him alive these past years — the cold ledger of self-preservation, the weight he'd carried since a client fell on his rope and the world decided it was easier to blame the guide.

He shrugged off his pack and pulled out the evidence case.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth, was Iriarte's strike data — the copies Quentin had made in the source camp while Iriarte slept. Assay results, coordinates, spectral scans. A mineral strike worth a war, and the only proof of it outside Iriarte's head. Quentin's leverage. His ransom. His vindication, if it came to that — the thing he could trade to Reeve, to a government, to anyone, to buy back the name they'd taken from him.

Devi was shivering. Shock was coming for him, and after shock, the river's cold, and after that, nothing.

Quentin unwrapped the oilcloth. He stripped it into bandages, splinted the ankle with a tent pole, and used the case itself — aluminum, waterproof — as a litter harness to bind the boy to Okafor's back. Then he stood at the edge of the path and let the wind take the papers, sheet by sheet, out over the gorge. They didn't flutter. The rain had already turned them to pulp. They simply fell, and the river took them the way it took everything, without malice and without memory.

Elena watched him do it. He felt her eyes on him the whole time and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that the river hadn't already said better.

* * *

They came out of the gorge at dusk on the third day, into a valley where the rain had thinned to a drifting silver mist. The Khasra, spent and swollen, ran wide and slow beside the trail like a beast gorged and drowsy.

Quentin was soaked through, unpaid, unemployed, and — he took inventory, walking — strangely light. The ledger in his chest, the one he'd balanced every day for six years, had gone quiet.

Elena fell into step beside him. She was carrying her own pack, and inside it, he knew, were her journals — the full testimony. Not just what Iriarte had done, but what she had done. What she'd said, years ago, in a hearing room in another country, when they'd put her in a chair and told her what to say about Quentin Vane and she'd said it, believing it, or telling herself she believed it.

"You could have kept them," she said. "The copies. You'd have walked out rich."

"Rich men drown in that river all the time."

"That's not an answer."

He watched the water a while. "I spent six years holding onto a rope that was already cut," he said. "Letting go is just the part where you stop pretending."

She was quiet for a hundred yards. Then: "I'm giving the journals to the commission at Kalapur. All of it. Iriarte's sale, the sabotage, the strike — and my testimony. My real testimony, this time. What they made me say. What I let myself believe."

Quentin stopped walking.

"You understand what that costs you," he said. "Your career. Your name. Everything you built after."

"I understand what it cost *you*," she said. "I've known for three years. I knew before I got on that boat." She met his eyes, and the rain ran down her face like she couldn't be bothered to wipe it away. "I came on this expedition to find the courage you're watching me finally find. That's the ugly truth. I needed to see what a ruined man does with his hands, and you — you spent your proof on a boy you'd known four days."

"He was on my rope."

"Everything was on your rope," she said. "That's the whole point. That's what I got wrong."

The mist was lifting. Somewhere above the valley walls the clouds were tearing open, and a blade of evening light came down the gorge and laid itself across the water, gold on brown, the first honest light in nine days.

They walked. The trail bent south toward Kalapur, toward the commission, toward the hearing that would come and the name that might come back. Quentin didn't know if it would. He knew only that his pack was lighter than it had ever been and that the man walking beside Elena Marsh — not ahead of her, not behind her, beside her — was not the man who'd entered the gorge.

At the trailhead, where the valley opened onto the long road down, she paused.

"They'll ask why you gave it up," she said. "Reeve. The commission. Everyone. They'll ask what you got out of it."

Quentin looked back once at the gorge — dark now, quiet, keeping what it kept.

"Tell them the river gave it back," he said. "Everything I ever lost. It just didn't give it to me the way I asked."
