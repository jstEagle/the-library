## 1. The Weight of Water

The rain came to Karva the way debt came: gradually, then all at once.

Noor Iversen stood at the window of his rented room on the fourth floor of the Meridian Lodging House and watched it arrive. He had felt it coming for two days—a pressure behind his eyes like a thumb pressed into wet clay, a taste of iron in the back of his throat, the gulls flying low and stupid over the fish market. The barometer on the sill, a cheap brass thing he'd bought to replace the one that mattered, had been falling since Tuesday, steady as a confession.

By Thursday morning the harbor was gone. Not empty—*gone*, swallowed whole, the cranes reduced to gray smears, the breakwater a rumor beneath white water. The monsoon had turned its full attention on the coast for the first time that season, and it did so with the particular malice of weather that has been waiting.

He could have told them. He *had* told them.

Noor dressed in the dark because the light hurt—his head always hurt now, in the hours before a storm broke, as if the sky were trying to speak through him and resenting the medium. His shirt had been white once. His boots had resoled twice. In the pocket of his coat, folded into quarters soft as cloth from handling, was a letter from the Harbormaster's Office of Karva, dated eleven days ago:

*Mr. Iversen — The Fleet Protection Contract previously under consideration has been awarded to Voss Meteorological Associates. We thank you for your application and note with regret the concerns raised regarding your professional history.*

Professional history. As if history were something a man could have instead of a past. As if the word didn't carry its own smell, like smoke after a fire you didn't start but couldn't put out either.

He went down the stairs two at a time, out into the rain, and let it hit him. People ran along the quay with sacks over their heads, hauling shutters, screaming at children. Noor walked. There was a strange peace in standing inside a forecast, being the one thing in Karva that was not surprised.

The storm would peak at four in the afternoon. It would drive a surge up the harbor channel that the seawall—built twenty years ago by engineers who consulted no one who actually read the sky—would not hold. The fishing fleet moored in the inner basin, forty-one hulls, most of them family boats older than their owners, would take the surge broadside against the pilings and begin to die around six.

Voss Meteorological Associates had issued an advisory this morning. *Elevated seas expected. Small craft advised to exercise caution.*

Noor had written a different sentence once, in another life, in a report that had ended him: *Surge will exceed seawall capacity by one point three meters. Evacuate the inner basin or lose the fleet.* They had called it fearmongering. They had called it a man chasing relevance. The fleet had stayed, that time too, and the fleet had died, that time too, and Noor's name had become a synonym for crying wolf in a country where the wolf kept eating.

He stood at the mouth of the inner basin until his clothes gave up pretending to be garments and became simply wet weight, and he watched the water do exactly what he knew it would do.

It was not satisfaction he felt. That was the cruelty of it. Anyone imagining the disgraced forecaster gloating over a vindicated prophecy had never watched a boat he'd slept aboard—the *Maren Sille*, old Tomaas's trawler, where Noor had spent a whole lost summer gutting herring for bed and board—lift on a surge and come down across a piling with a sound like a rib breaking. Forty-one hulls. By nightfall there would be thirty-some, and by morning the harbor crews would be pulling splinters out of the water and calling them by name.

The rain ran off his chin. Somewhere behind him a woman was weeping into a radio handset, calling a husband who was out beyond the wall in a boat that should never have left.

You can be right, Noor thought, watching the sea climb the wall, and it will change nothing except how alone you are.

* * *

The Kessler brothers' office occupied the ground floor of a counting house on Salt Lane, behind a door painted green so many times the wood beneath had begun to drink the color. Inside it smelled of ink, damp wool, and the particular staleness of money that has passed through many unhappy hands.

Dov Kessler sat behind the desk. Dov was the younger brother and the face; his brother Bren handled what Dov charmingly referred to as "acquisitions," which meant that when Dov smiled, somewhere else a man was losing his boat, his house, or his fingers' worth of dignity. Today Dov smiled the way a tide comes in—without malice, without hurry, certain of the shore.

"Noor." He spread his hands. "Sit. You're dripping on my floor."

"Your floor has survived worse."

"My floor has survived everything. That's why it's my floor." He slid a ledger across the desk and turned it around. Numbers marched down the page in Dov's neat, terrible hand. "Two hundred and forty thousand marks. Principal, interest, the advance on the survey contract you defaulted on in spring, plus the fee for the collection visit Bren made to your room last month—which, incidentally, you owe me for, since he came back empty."

"He took my radio."

"And sold it for nine marks, which is why there's a *fee*. You see my problem, Noor? Everything you touch turns into an expense."

Noor looked at the number. Two hundred and forty thousand. It had been one hundred and ten when it started, a bridge loan against a chase contract that had evaporated with his reputation. The number grew the way storms grew now—fed by warm water, by delay, by the simple physics of a system designed to consume.

"I'll have work," Noor said. "The season's turning. Someone always needs—"

"Someone needed you in spring. Someone needed you in autumn. What someone needs, Noor, is a forecaster whose name isn't a punchline." Dov laced his fingers. "Here's what happens next. You have until the first snow in the high country—call it three weeks, call it a month—to bring me sixty thousand. Cash. After that, the remainder converts. Your lodging debt becomes a lien. Your father's workshop on Tanner's Row—it's still registered to you, yes?"

Noor said nothing. The workshop had been closed for nine years. But it was the last thing with his father's name on the deed, the last physical fact of the man besides a brass compass, and Dov knew it, and that was why Dov mentioned it, gently, the way you'd touch a bruise to see how deep it went.

"Bren hates liens," Dov continued pleasantly. "Too much paperwork. He prefers to simply sell things. So give me sixty thousand and we stay friends. Fail, and I'm afraid the workshop goes to auction, and then whatever's left of you goes to work for us, and Noor—" he leaned forward, and his smile finally showed its teeth, "—nobody works *for* the Keslers for long. Ask anyone who does."

"Where would they be?" Noor asked. "Anyone who works for you."

Dov laughed, genuinely delighted. "There's the wit. That's what I paid good money for, back when you were worth good money." He waved a hand toward the door. "Sixty thousand. First snow. Don't make me send Bren somewhere you love."

Outside, the storm had begun its long withdrawal, dragging its skirts north along the coast, and the city emerged blinking into a light the color of dishwater. The inner basin was already a graveyard of hulls. Men waded chest-deep between the wrecks, cutting loose the dead ones before they could batter the living. A crowd had gathered at the seawall, silent in the way crowds are silent only when they've watched something they helped happen.

Noor heard his name before he saw the speaker.

"—Iversen! Oy! Iversen!"

A boy of maybe fifteen, soaked through, holding a folded paper above his head like an offering. "Man paid me a mark to find you. Said you'd be at the wall, watching. Said you'd be watching the water." The boy shrugged, apologetic for the stranger's accuracy. "He was right."

The paper was heavy stock, cream-colored, absurdly clean against the drowned day. On it, in a hand so even it might have been typeset:

*Mr. Iversen — I represent parties with a recovery requirement in the Vantaa Range. Government survey equipment, abandoned, value to the right retriever. Fee: seventy-five thousand marks, half on acceptance. The Meridian Lodging House, tonight, eight o'clock. Ask for Corven.*

Seventy-five thousand. Half on acceptance. Noor read it three times, standing in the rain that had ruined forty-one boats, feeling the number work on him the way water works on a hillside—not attacking, just persisting, until the ground gives.

Nobody salvaged government gear out of the Vantaa Range for less than it cost to get there. Nobody offered seventy-five thousand for a walk in the mountains unless the walking was the cheap part of the job.

But sixty thousand was due at first snow, and the first snow was coming—he could feel it already, a faint cold wire running through the back of the storm's departure, weeks out yet, but coming—and a man drowning does not audit the rope.

"Tell your man I'll be there," Noor said.

The boy grinned, pocketed the mark, and ran.

* * *

Corven was not what Noor expected, though afterward Noor could not have said what he had expected. Perhaps someone larger. Men who bought other men were usually large in some way—loud, or rich-looking, or armed. This man was none of those. He was neat. Medium height, medium build, a face you would forget while looking at it, dressed in a traveling suit of good gray wool that had never known weather. He sat in the Meridian's narrow parlor with his hands folded and a cup of tea untouched beside him, and when Noor entered he rose exactly far enough to be polite and no farther.

"Mr. Iversen. Thank you for coming. Sit, please." He waited until Noor sat. "I'll be brief, because you strike me as a man who respects brevity and distrusts charm."

"That's a charm offensive itself."

Something moved at the corner of Corven's mouth—not quite a smile, more the memory of one. "Then I'll dispense with it. Three months ago, a government survey team established a station in the Vantaa Range. Atmospheric instruments, data loggers, the usual expensive apparatus. Six weeks ago, contact ceased. The government declared the project concluded and withdrew support. The equipment remains on the mountain. My clients want it recovered."

"Salvage companies exist."

"Salvage companies file reports. Reports go to registries. Registries go to governments." Corven lifted the teacup, considered it, set it down again without drinking. "My clients prefer discretion. Which is why they prefer you. You have mountaineering experience, meteorological training sufficient to read the instruments and pack them properly, and—" here the not-smile returned, briefly, "—a professional history that makes you unlikely to ask inconvenient questions about paperwork."

"And unlikely to talk. Disgraced men don't testify. Nobody prints what a liar says."

"I wouldn't have used the word *liar*."

"You didn't have to. It was in the contract offer." Noor leaned back. The parlor smelled of mildew and lamp oil. Somewhere upstairs a pipe leaked with the patience of water. "What's actually up there, Corven?"

"Instruments."

"What kind of instruments?"

"The kind worth seventy-five thousand marks to people who don't want to climb for them themselves." Corven produced a folder and laid it on the table between them, square, unhurried. "Route maps. The station coordinates. A supply manifest from the original expedition. Satellite photographs, such as they are—the range eats satellites alive. Everything you need and nothing you don't."

Noor opened the folder. The Vantaa Range stared back at him in gray relief: a spine of peaks running northeast from the monsoon coast into the interior highlands, unloved, unmapped in any detail newer than thirty years, its valleys shaded the deep green-black of country where the rain never fully leaves. The survey station sat high on the western shoulder, above the treeline, above anything sane.

"Recovery window?" he asked, because asking practical questions kept him from hearing his own pulse.

"First snow closes the passes. You have perhaps four weeks."

"And the other half of the fee?"

"On delivery. Equipment verified, packed, and brought down to Solheim, where my people will receive it." Corven finally drank his tea, a small precise sip. "Half now buys your gear and your silence about the arrangement. The rest buys the work done."

Seventy-five thousand. Thirty-seven and a half in his hand tomorrow, enough to push the Keslers back past the horizon, enough to buy time, and the rest enough to buy—what? A clean ledger. A beginning. The word *beginning* had a taste to it, like snowmelt.

He should have asked more questions. Later, in the long white nights of the range, Noor would replay this evening a hundred times and catalog every question he failed to ask: Who were the clients. Why had the government really withdrawn. Why had contact ceased. Why him, why now, why the urgency dressed in patience. But desperation is a kind of noise, and it drowns out the small wrong notes. He heard only the number.

"One condition," Noor said. "Paper contract. Signed. I've been burned by verbal agreements from both directions."

"Of course." Corven produced the contract as if it had been waiting in his breast pocket since birth—three pages, dense, fair-seeming. Noor read the fee schedule, the delivery terms, the penalty clause for failure. His eyes were tired and the lamp was bad and the words swam a little, and on the second page there was a paragraph of legal small print that his gaze slid across the way a boot slides on wet rock, and found no purchase, and moved on.

He signed. Corven countersigned, blotted it, folded it away, and shook Noor's hand with a grip that was dry, cool, and brief as a receipt.

"A pleasure, Mr. Iversen. My courier delivers the advance and the equipment manifests to your lodging at dawn. I'd advise an early start. The mountain," Corven said, gathering his hat, "is not improved by delay."

At the door he paused, half-turned, and for one moment the forgettable face became entirely memorable—not menacing, nothing so crude, merely *attentive*, the way a lens is attentive.

"For what it's worth," Corven said, "I chose you because you're the best climber-meteorologist in this part of the coast who can still be hired. Whatever else they've taken from you, that part is true. Good night."

Then he was gone into the wet street, and Noor stood alone in the parlor with thirty-seven and a half thousand marks arriving at dawn and the sensation—faint, deniable, easy to drown in arithmetic—that he had just been measured for something, and fitted well.

Upstairs, he took the brass compass from its place in his bag. His father's. The needle swung, settled, held its small silver conviction pointing north, indifferent to debts and storms and smooth men in gray wool. Noor held it in his palm and felt the weight of it, which was almost nothing, and the weight of the water on the roof above him, which was everything, and slept badly, dreaming of a green staircase of mountains rising into cloud, and a light moving along the ridge, patient as a tide, waiting for him to begin climbing.
