## 2. Compass and Debt

The compass sat in Noor's palm like a small drowned sun.

Brass, dented at the north shoulder where his father had once dropped it off a sea wall in Karva and fished it out of green water with a boat hook, swearing in two languages. The glass over the dial had a hairline crack running from east to west, so that when the light caught it right, the needle seemed to float on a river of fracture. His father had called that crack *the honest line*. *A compass that's never been broken lies to you*, he used to say, turning the thing over in his rope-scarred hands. *It hasn't learned what it costs to be wrong.*

Noor turned it over now, in the rented room above the chandlery on Karva's north quay, and listened to the rain.

It came down the way it always did this time of year—not falling so much as arriving, a gray vertical country between the clouds and the rooftops, drumming on tin, gurgling in the gutters, filling the harbor with a sound like slow applause. Somewhere below, a fish-gut cart squealed through an alley. Somewhere farther, the Kesslers' men were probably already asking after him. Three days. That was what Dov had said, leaning against the doorframe of the last chase office that would still take Noor's calls, smiling with all the warmth of a ledger. *Three days, Iversen. Then we stop being patient and start being thorough.*

He set the compass down on the sill and began packing.

* * *

His father had been a pilot-boat man, one of those who went out to meet the deep-sea freighters in weather that kept everyone else tied to the dock, and he had trusted instruments the way other men trusted saints—intimately, and with a constant low-grade suspicion that they might abandon him at any moment. He taught Noor to read the sky before he taught him to read anything else. Barometric pressure by the ache in your back teeth. Wind shift by the smell the air put on an hour before it arrived. Rain by the particular hush that fell over water when the atmosphere was stacking itself into something heavy.

*You can lie to a gauge*, his father said. *You can miscalibrate it, you can drop it, you can read it wrong because you want something too badly. But the sky doesn't care what you want. Learn it anyway.*

Noor had learned it. God, how he'd learned it. And for eleven years it had made him the best storm chaser on the coast—contracts with shipping lines, insurance syndicates, the aquaculture barons up the delta, all of them paying good money for the single skill no model could replicate: Noor Iversen standing on a headland or a bridge deck with his face into the wind, saying *not tonight* or *run now*, and being right.

Until the Veyra forecast.

He didn't let himself think about it often, but packing was the kind of work that left the hands busy and the mind unguarded, and the memory came up the way floodwater comes up a dry creek bed—all at once, and carrying everything with it.

Four years ago. The spring the monsoon came early and strange. He'd been under contract to the Veyra Shipping Combine, a good contract, the kind you built a decade on. And for six days he had watched the pressure readings do something they were not supposed to do: climb when they should have fallen, fall when they should have climbed, the whole coastal system breathing backward like a man inhaling underwater. Every official model—the government bureau's, the university's, the combine's own imported forecasting suite—showed a routine depression swinging harmlessly out to sea.

Noor stood on the breakwater at Port Veyra with his face into a wind that smelled wrong—too sweet, too charged, the air stacked heavy in a pattern he had felt maybe twice in his life—and he said: *This is not a depression. This is something being fed. It will turn inland. You have thirty-six hours to clear the roads and pull your fleet behind the headland.*

They had laughed at him. Not cruelly, at first. The operations director had patted him on the shoulder like a horse that had spooked at nothing. The bureau liaison had used the word *anomalous* four times in one sentence. And then, when Noor refused to stand down—when he pulled his own equipment, published his own advisory under his own name, told the fishing cooperatives directly to get their boats out of the water—they stopped laughing.

*Where is your data?* the bureau man asked, in the hearing of everyone who mattered.

And there was the trap, laid flat and neat as a snare across a game trail. Because his data was his body. It was twenty years of standing in weather, cross-referenced against instinct, and it did not fit in a report. He had readings, yes—he'd scrambled to assemble them—but they contradicted every certified source, and a forecast that contradicts every certified source isn't a warning. It's a symptom. They used the word *unstable* about him the way doctors use it about patients. Within a month his license was suspended pending review; within three, quietly revoked. The storm came in on the thirty-sixth hour exactly as he'd called it, turned inland exactly as he'd called it, drowned eleven people and broke the Veyra fleet's back, and the inquiry into the disaster concluded—with great solemnity—that the bureau's models had performed within acceptable parameters, and that unauthorized advisories from unlicensed individuals had contributed to public confusion during the emergency.

*Contributed to public confusion.* He had saved the fishing fleet. Two hundred boats had run for shelter because a disgraced man shouted at them from a dock, and not one word of thanks ever reached him, because gratitude would have meant agreeing with him, and agreeing with him would have meant the models were wrong, and the models being wrong was a door nobody in that room was willing to open.

So they closed it instead. On him.

Noor zipped the pack shut on his sleeping bag and sat back on his heels, breathing the wet-room smell of mildew and kerosene. The rain applauded on. In the corner, the salvage contract lay on the table where Corven's runner had left it, three pages of dense type under a signature line, and beside it the advance: forty percent, cash, in an envelope that currently weighed more than his reputation.

* * *

He allowed himself one drink, poured from the bottle he'd sworn he wouldn't buy, and carried it to the window.

Below, the quay lights smeared themselves across black water. A harbor tug moved through the rain with its running lamps doubled and trembling in the wet, and Noor watched it the way a man watches a woman across a room he's been thrown out of. Four years without a license. Four years of salvage runs and cargo inspections and reading weather privately, off the books, for fishermen who paid in fish and trusted him precisely because the government didn't. Four years of watching lesser forecasters miss systems he'd have called in his sleep, and saying nothing, because saying anything was how you got the Kesslers' attention, and the Kesslers' attention was worse than silence.

The debt hadn't even been his sin, not really. That was the bitter joke of it. A chase boat—his old *Tern*, sold in the first year of the disgrace to pay the lawyers—a boat he'd co-signed on for a younger chaser named Pell, who'd had the talent but not the temperament, and who'd wrecked her in a squall he should never have gone out in, and then skipped the coast entirely, leaving Noor holding paper he'd signed out of loyalty. The Kesslers bought the debt the way they bought everything: cheap, and with intent. Interest compounding like mold in a wet wall. Three years of payments eaten by interest until the number was bigger than when he started, a tide that only came in.

Forty thousand. He'd stopped calculating the exact figure months ago. It was less a number now than a weather system of its own, following him from port to port, darkening whatever sky he stood under.

Corven had appeared ten days ago, in the back booth of a tea house near the customs house, dressed like money pretending to be modesty. No briefcase. No muscle. Just a thin man with a pleasant, forgettable face and eyes that did arithmetic while his mouth made conversation.

*A salvage job,* Corven had said, stirring tea he wasn't drinking. *Government survey team went into the Vantaa Range eight months ago. Atmospheric research. They lost contact; the program was defunded; the site was written off. There's instrumentation up there worth recovering. Sensitive instrumentation.* A pause, delicate as a scalpel laid on cloth. *The people who want it recovered would prefer discretion. Your name came up because you know mountains and you know weather, and because—* here the small smile, the one that made Noor want to put his fist through the man's forgettable face— *you're available.*

*How much?*

*Enough to settle your situation. All of it.* Corven had slid the envelope across first, which was the mark of a man who understood desperation the way surgeons understand anatomy. *Half now. Half on delivery. The gear comes down intact, and you tell no one what you saw up there. Simple.*

Simple. Noor drank and watched the tug vanish into the rain and thought about the word the way his father had taught him to think about a fair-weather sky: *if it looks too easy, it's because you're looking at the front of it, not the back.*

But the envelope was real. The envelope was forty percent of freedom. And the mountain—

The mountain, at least, was honest. That was the one thing he could still say. The Vantaa Range didn't care about his license or his debts or what some bureau man had written about him. It cared about weight and footing and the rate at which warm air rose off the valleys, and it punished error immediately and without committee review. After four years of human weather, Noor wanted a few weeks of the other kind.

He finished the drink. Set the glass down. Picked up the pen.

* * *

The contract was three pages. He read the first page properly—scope of work, delivery terms, the payment schedule matching what Corven had said—and by the second page the rain had worked its way into his mood, a gray fatigue settling over him like ballast, and he found himself skimming. Clause seven, indemnification. Clause nine, liability for loss of equipment. Clause eleven—

Clause eleven ran to a paragraph and a half of small type about exclusivity and information handling, and Noor's eye caught on phrases—*sole and exclusive agent*, *all findings, observations, and materials encountered in the course of the operation remain the property of the contracting party*, *the contractor agrees to make full disclosure*—and his tired mind filed them under *lawyer furniture*, the way a man walking past a church files away the bells. He'd signed contracts like this a hundred times. Chase contracts, salvage contracts, insurance riders. The language was always the same cathedral, and you never read every stone.

Besides. What was he going to find up there? Rusted tripods. Frozen batteries. A dead camp in the clouds. Whatever the survey team had been measuring, it was measuring weather, and Noor Iversen was arguably the last man on the coast who needed to steal weather data. He knew what the sky did. What he didn't know was how to make forty thousand dollars appear out of clean air, and this contract was the only cloud on any horizon raining money.

His father's voice, faint, through four years and one grave: *Read what you sign, boy. A man's word is the only instrument he carries that can't be recalibrated.*

*I know, Papa,* Noor said to the empty room, and signed anyway.

The pen scratched. The needle of the compass on the windowsill trembled—some vibration from the traffic below, or the building's bones, or nothing—and settled again, pointing its cracked-glass heart toward a north Noor would not be needing. The route Corven's maps showed ran south and up, into the Vantaa foothills, along the old survey road to the tree line, and beyond that into country where the monsoon spent itself against stone and the government's maps thinned to guesswork.

He counted out the advance money twice, split it—half into the oilskin pouch against his skin, half into a hollowed space in the bottom of his pack beneath the spare socks, because a man who grew up on pilot boats trusted no strongbox more than his own back—and then sat for a moment with the compass in his hand, weighing it.

He almost left it.

That was the strange truth he admitted only to himself, there in the lamplight with the rain going gray toward dawn: the compass had become a weight he couldn't justify. Brass and glass, ninety grams, useless next to a GPS unit and a barometric altimeter, sentimental cargo, the kind of thing that got men killed because they wouldn't leave their dead behind. Every gram on the Vantaa would argue for itself. And yet his thumb found the dent at the north shoulder, the crack running east to west, the honest line, and he heard his father's voice one more time, clearer than it had been all night:

*It hasn't learned what it costs to be wrong.*

Neither had the mountain, Noor thought. And neither, despite everything, had he.

He wrapped the compass in a wool sock and stowed it in the outer pocket of his pack, where his hand could find it without looking. Then he shouldered the load, checked the stove was cold, and went down the stairs into the last wet dark of the night, past the sleeping chandlery, out onto the quay where the rain met him like a crowd.

The bus for the interior left from the depot at five. By six he was on it, wedged among crates and sleeping herders, watching Karva's lights shrink in the side window until the road bent and took them away for good. Ahead, invisible beneath the cloud, the Vantaa Range waited with its feet in the flooded foothills and its head somewhere above the weather, and riding in the inside pocket of a stranger's coat, a copy of the contract he had signed without reading traveled south along the same road, keeping pace with him like something patient.

At the last stop before the survey road, the driver killed the engine and pointed up through the rain into a green darkness that swallowed the trail whole. Noor stepped down into the mud, adjusted the pack straps until the weight became part of him, and turned his face uphill.

The air smelled of wet stone and crushed vegetation and, very faintly, underneath it all, of something metallic and wrong that he couldn't yet name—an odor like a struck match struck nowhere, like ozone with an intention behind it. He stood listening to the forest breathe, and the hair rose slowly on his forearms, the way it always had, the way it had on the breakwater at Port Veyra four years ago, when the sky was lying to everyone but him.

Somewhere up in that green staircase, eight months of silence were waiting.

Noor tightened his grip on the ice axe and started climbing.
