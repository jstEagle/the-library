## 3. Into the Green Staircase

The rain began before dawn and did not so much fall as arrive, the way a debt arrives—already owed, already everywhere. Noor stood at the treeline where the road gave out and looked up into the Vantaa foothills, and the foothills looked back the way they always did: green, indifferent, dripping.

He had climbed in weather like this before. He had made his name in weather like this. There was a version of himself, four years younger, who would have stood here grinning, tasting ozone on the back of his tongue, reading the sky the way other men read faces. That man was gone. What remained was a debtor with a contract folded in oilcloth against his chest and forty kilograms of salvage gear cutting grooves into his shoulders, and the only thing he still knew how to do—the only thing anyone had ever paid him for, until they stopped—was walk toward weather everyone else was walking away from.

*Recover government survey equipment from the abandoned Vantaa Range expedition,* Corven had said, as if he were describing the retrieval of a lost umbrella. *Instruments, drives, whatever's portable. The team's long gone. Compensation is generous.*

Generous. Noor turned the word over as he stepped off the road and onto the trail, if you could call it that—a seam of mud and root winding up through forest so wet it seemed to be dissolving. Generous was what men said when they wanted something they couldn't name out loud. But the Keslers' deadline was eleven days away, and eleven days was not enough time to find honest work, and honest work wouldn't have covered a tenth of it anyway. So he climbed.

The forest took him the way water takes a stone.

It was monsoon country, true monsoon, not the tourist-brochure kind. The air was a physical thing, warm and thick, pressing on his eardrums, filling every fold of his clothing until he could not tell sweat from rain. Ferns grew to his chest. Moss climbed the trees in green fur, and lianas hung down like the rigging of some drowned ship. Water ran everywhere—not in streams but in threads, dozens of them, silvering down every slope, finding every channel, whispering constantly, insistently, like a crowd murmuring behind a closed door.

Noor walked for six hours and gained perhaps nine hundred meters, and by then he had stopped hearing the whispering as background and started hearing it as speech. That happened sometimes, in the deep wet places. His father had called it *the mountain talking in its sleep*. His father had also believed a brass compass could hold a man's luck, so Noor didn't put much stock in the phrasing. But the feeling was real. The feeling was the oldest instrument he owned.

He ate standing up, rice and dried fish from a foil pouch, chewing mechanically while he watched the cloud deck above the ridgelines. This was the part no one understood about storm work—that most of it was watching, patient and unglamorous, the way a fisherman watches water. The clouds over Vantaa were doing something he didn't like.

They were too organized.

Monsoon cloud should have been chaotic, ragged, piled up and torn apart by competing winds, a drunk argument of vapor. These clouds were stacked. Layered. They moved with a coherence that made the hair on his forearms lift, each bank feeding the next in a slow rotation around a center that sat, stubbornly, over the high range. He'd seen structure like this before. He'd seen it once, four years ago, over the Karva delta, three days before the flood that ended his career—and when he'd told his client to pull his fleet out of harbor, the man had laughed, because every official forecast said clear skies, because every official forecast came from the same bureau, fed by the same models, and what did the disgraced Iversen know?

The fleet had gone down. Eleven boats. Two men drowned. And Noor's name went from *gifted* to *unstable* to *unemployable* in the space of one inquiry, because the official record showed clear skies, and the official record was never wrong, and therefore he must have been.

He chewed the last of the rice and swallowed it dry.

*Coincidence,* he told himself, the word worn smooth as river glass from use. *Weather is weather. Clouds don't conspire.*

But the clouds kept their formation, and he kept climbing.

* * *

Near dusk he crossed the first sign of the survey team, and it was wrong in a way he couldn't immediately name, which bothered him more than an obvious wrongness would have.

A marker flag, orange plastic, zip-tied to a sapling. Standard government issue—he'd seen hundreds on old survey lines. But the tie was new. Not weathered, not sun-bleached, not furred with moss the way everything else in this forest was furred within weeks. Someone had put this flag up recently. Within days. Maybe within hours.

And below it, half-sunk in mud, a boot print. One. Deep-heeled, military tread, large. It pointed uphill.

Noor crouched over it for a long time, rain running off his hood, and told himself the obvious story: a recovery crew had come up ahead of him, marked the route, moved on. Corven hadn't mentioned anyone else. Corven hadn't mentioned much of anything. The story held together. Stories always held together if you didn't squeeze them.

He squeezed anyway. If a recovery crew had come through, there'd be more prints. There'd be trash—cigarette ends, ration wrappers, the shed skin of any group of men moving through wet country. There was nothing. One print, one fresh flag, and a silence around them so complete it felt arranged.

He camped that night in the lee of a granite shoulder, tent pitched low, stove lit inside the vestibule where the flame couldn't be seen from above. He didn't decide to hide the light. His hands decided for him, the way they'd once decided to check anchor bolts twice, the way they'd decided things in the delta. Some part of him had already left the obvious story behind and was moving quietly through the dark on its own.

He slept badly, and dreamed of water running backward.

* * *

The second day the forest began to climb in earnest, and the trail became a staircase—literal in places, steps cut into the slope decades ago by some forgotten crew, now soft with moss, each one a small green cushion that swallowed his boots without complaint. The trees thinned. The air cooled by degrees he could feel on his wrists. Above him, through breaks in the canopy, the ridgelines showed themselves: black rock seamed with white water, and above those, the high country wrapped in cloud, and above even that—visible only in glimpses, like something surfacing to breathe—the shoulders of the Vantaa massif, gray and enormous.

The green staircase. A trekking map he'd bought in Karva had called it that, in cheerful italics, beside a cartoon hiker. Whoever drew the cartoon had never carried forty kilos up it in the rain.

He passed a second flag in the morning. A third at noon. All fresh. All pointing up. And between them, spaced with a regularity that had begun to feel less like navigation and more like invitation, the single boot prints—always one, never two, always uphill, always the same deep heel. As if someone were walking this trail just ahead of him, placing flags, leaving exactly one footprint each time, and taking care to leave no others.

Nobody walked like that. Nobody *could* walk like that, not with a pack, not on ground this soft. Which meant the prints weren't tracks at all. They were messages. Or bait.

By late afternoon he had stopped eating. Not a decision; his stomach simply closed, the way it did before storms, before the delta, before every moment of his life that mattered. His body had always known things before he did. It was the one instrument the inquiry couldn't confiscate.

The cloud deck lowered as he climbed into it, and the world contracted to a gray sphere twenty meters across, dripping. Sound changed too—the constant water-whisper fading as the streams froze higher up, replaced by wind moving through the last of the trees with a sound like slow breathing. He checked the compass more often than he needed to. The needle held steady, northwest, into the heart of the range, and there was a stupid comfort in that, in the little brass-cased certainty of it, his father's voice in metal form: *the needle doesn't care what you're afraid of, boy. It only knows north. Be like the needle.*

The base camp appeared all at once, the way things appear in fog—assembled out of the gray in under a minute, as if it had been waiting for him to get close enough.

Two dome tents, a cook shelter, a equipment rack under tarps, a solar array angled at a sky that hadn't shown sun in days. A flagpole with a government pennant, soaked and hanging like a dead bird. Everything neat. Everything in place. A camp that looked, from thirty meters, like a team that had stepped out for a morning of instruments and would step back in by dark.

Noor stood at the edge of it for five full minutes and did not go in.

Because a camp like this should have been wrecked. Eleven months of abandonment in monsoon country meant collapse—tarps shredded, tents flattened by snowload or bloated with mold, fabric rotted, everything the rats and the rain could take, taken. This camp was intact. Tarps taut. Guy-lines humming faintly in the wind. And—

His eyes found it and his stomach dropped through the mountain.

Smoke. Thin, gray, almost invisible in the fog, rising from the cook shelter's flue pipe.

Someone was here. Someone was here *now*, or had been within the hour, and every animal instinct he possessed screamed at him to turn around, go down, take the loss, let the Keslers have his hands if it came to that. He actually took one step back. His boot crunched on gravel, absurdly loud.

Then he thought about the money. About Dov Kessler's smile, which was worse than his brother's fists. About the letter he'd written his sister promising this was the last job, the turnaround, the bottom of the curve.

He unslung the ice axe from his pack and went in.

* * *

The camp was empty. He confirmed it the way he did everything now—methodically, unhurried, hands steady because steadiness was free and panic cost. Cook shelter first: stove cold, ash damp, but the ash was *fine*, not clumped, not rained-on. Burned within the last day. The smoke he'd seen must have been residual heat drawing moisture off the flue—or he'd missed whoever tended it by minutes, which was somehow worse.

Then the tents. Both empty of people. Sleeping bags rolled. Boots paired neatly at the tent doors, four pairs, sizes mixed, laces tucked in. Nobody leaves boots behind. Not in the mountains. You can lose a tent, a stove, your mind—but you take the boots.

The mess table under the shelter held the thing that finally made his skin crawl, the detail his mind kept returning to like a tongue to a broken tooth.

Meals. Half-eaten meals, two of them, set out on enamel plates. Rice, congealed and gray, fuzzed at the edges with the first bloom of mold. Spoons resting in the food. Mugs beside the plates, tea gone to cold scum. Two settings, side by side, as if two people had sat down to eat together and simply—stopped. Mid-meal. Eleven months ago? Food didn't survive eleven months looking like that. It survived days. A week, maybe, in cold conditions.

But the mold said longer than days. The mold said weeks. And the untouched boots said months. And the warm stove said hours. The camp was arguing with itself, three different timelines laid out on one table, and Noor stood among them with rain dripping off his hood and felt the unease in his chest sharpen, refine itself, harden into something with a name.

Dread. Not fear of what he might find. Fear of what had already been found, by other people, and dealt with.

He forced himself to eat one of the moldy plates' neighbors—a sealed ration from the supply crate, checking the date stamp twice—and while he chewed he searched properly. The equipment rack under its tarp: transit case after transit case, stenciled with the ministry crest and a serial prefix he recognized from Corven's contract. Atmospheric sondes. Barometers. A theodolite in fitted foam. And at the back, a hardened field server the size of a shoebox, its status lights blinking a slow green heartbeat.

Still running. Eleven months, and the instruments were still running. Solar array, he supposed. Automatic logging. Except the logbook was supposed to be with the server, and the logbook was on the mess table, and the logbook was the reason his hands had gone cold despite the gloves.

It was a standard survey log, canvas-bound, the kind with carbon-copy pages. He'd used them himself. He opened it with the care of a man defusing something.

Entries in a neat engineer's hand, dated through the spring and early summer of the team's deployment. Routine stuff. Calibration notes. Wind readings. Barometric trends annotated with small, increasingly frequent exclamation points. Then, in the final third of the book, the handwriting changed—tighter, faster, the letters leaning forward like a runner. Phrases surfaced and sank in the technical shorthand: *anomalous seeding signature*. *Cyclogenesis not consistent with natural forcing*. *Requested verification from bureau — none forthcoming*. *Who authorized the upper-atmosphere releases?*

And then, eleven pages from the end, the entries stopped.

Not trailed off. Stopped. The last entry ended mid-line, mid-word even—*re-checking the 300mb data because if this is right then*—and the page after that was gone. Not torn. Torn leaves ragged edges, and these edges were knife-clean, sliced flush to the spine, one after another, eleven pages in total, removed by someone with a blade and all the time in the world.

Noor sat very still in the cook shelter with the mutilated logbook open on his knee and listened to the camp drip.

Half-eaten meals. Running instruments. Cut pages. Boots by empty tents. Fresh flags on the trail below, and single boot prints that couldn't belong to a living man, and a stove that had been warm today. The pieces wouldn't make a picture. Every arrangement he tried produced something obscene—an expedition that had vanished between bites of dinner; a cleanup crew that tidied sleeping bags but left the tea; a government that recorded the weather dying and then cut the record out with a knife.

Outside, the fog pressed close against the shelter's open front, and somewhere in it, upslope, a sound reached him that might have been wind through rock and might not have been. A rhythm. Regular. Like footsteps on the green staircase, coming down.

He closed the logbook, slid it into his jacket against the oilcloth contract, and killed his stove.

Whatever had eaten this team had not finished with the mountain. And whatever was walking down through the fog toward the camp was about to find out it wasn't empty anymore.
