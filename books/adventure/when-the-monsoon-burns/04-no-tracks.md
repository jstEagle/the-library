## 4. No Tracks

The rain stopped sometime before dawn, and Noor woke to a silence so complete it felt like a held breath.

He lay in his bivouac beneath the overhang of the old survey camp's supply tent, listening to the mountain drip. Water ran off every leaf and ledge in the Vantaa foothills, thousands of small voices that should have made a chorus, and yet the silence underneath them was the loudest thing he'd heard since Karva. It was the silence of a place that had stopped expecting visitors.

He'd slept badly. The camp did that to a man. He had spent the evening cataloguing its wrongness the way he used to catalog pressure gradients—methodically, because method was all that stood between him and panic. Six mess kits, six sleeping rolls, six names stenciled on duffels. A team of six, and every one of them had walked out of this camp in the middle of a meal. One bowl still held oats, gone to a gray slurry that insects had been at for days. A pot of tea sat on the cold stove, the water inside evaporated to a ring of mineral stain. Nobody struck camp. Nobody packed. Nobody even finished eating.

And nobody left a note.

He'd found the logbook in the instrument tent, in a waterproof case that had been opened and not properly closed. The entries ran in a neat surveyor's hand—barometric readings, wind speeds, humidity indices, all of it precise and dull as arithmetic—until the final week, where the entries changed character entirely. The handwriting got faster. The numbers stopped matching the instruments' calibration logs. On the last written page, the surveyor had recorded a pressure anomaly so severe that Noor, reading it by headlamp, had felt his stomach drop with professional recognition. He knew that signature in the data. He had seen it once before, seven years ago, in the readings that ended his career.

The next page was gone. Cut out, not torn—the blade had gone clean through the binding, a surgeon's cut. The three pages after it were gone too.

Someone had wanted the story to stop at the exact place where it became a story.

Noor had sat with the logbook for a long time, listening to the rain, and told himself what he'd been hired to tell himself: that the team had descended in a hurry, that Corven would pay on recovery of the equipment, that the missing pages were water damage or carelessness or anything at all other than what they looked like.

He'd never been good at lying to himself. It was the one skill his profession had never required.

* * *

He broke camp at first light and climbed out of the survey basin heading north-northwest, following the route the team's maps showed in red pencil. The monsoon had left the mountain soft. The trail was a wound in the green, a channel of mud and crushed sedge where six bodies had passed and passed again, hauling instruments up toward the high survey points. For the first two hours the tracks were everywhere—boot prints overlapping boot prints, the deep divots of a heavy equipment sledge, even a childlike handprint where someone had braced themselves against a wet boulder and left the mud behind like a signature.

Then the handprint was the last thing.

It happened between one switchback and the next, at a place where the trail crossed a stretch of open ground beside a stream. The ground there was perfect for holding prints—fine alluvial silt, damp but not flooded, the kind of surface that would keep a boot's story for a week in this climate. Noor's own prints followed him across it like a procession of dark commas.

Ahead of him, the silt was empty.

Not washed-out. Not obscured. Empty. The team's trail simply ended at the edge of the open ground, six sets of prints marching up to an invisible line and stopping, as if the whole survey team had stepped off the edge of the world at the same coordinates. Beyond that line the silt lay smooth and unbroken, silvered with rain, all the way to the treeline on the far side.

Noor stood at the boundary for a long time. He crouched and put his fingers in the mud. He looked for the signs that water might have erased the trail—a debris line, scouring, rearranged stones. There were none. The stream hadn't jumped its banks. The rain, however hard, could not have erased prints selectively, stopping at a neat border like a tide respecting a property line.

He made himself walk it. Forty meters of open ground, each step squelching, each step expecting at any moment to find the prints resume on the far side. They didn't. On the far side of the clearing, where the trail should have continued toward the high survey point, there was nothing. Not the team's prints. Not animal sign. Not even his own coming back to meet him, because he'd walked the same line in and would walk the same line out, and the mountain had already drunk the first set.

No, he corrected himself. Not drunk. He knew what water did to tracks. Water smeared them, filled them, collapsed their walls. These prints hadn't been smeared. They had been *removed*—the silt left smooth and level, as if a hand had drawn a blanket over them.

He stood in the middle of the clearing with the rain beginning again, fine and needling, and let himself think the thought he'd been holding at arm's length since the camp.

People had come back here. After the team left—or after the team was taken—people with equipment and time and a reason had walked this ground and made it look like no one had ever walked it. You didn't erase tracks to hide a rescue. You didn't erase tracks to hide an accident.

You erased tracks to hide a crime. Or to prepare one.

* * *

The gear started appearing an hour later, and it was wrong in a way that took his mind a while to articulate.

The first item was a surveyor's ranging pole, snapped in half, lying ten meters off the trail in a tangle of fern. Noor picked it up and turned it over. The break was old, weathered gray at the fracture. He looked back at the trail, at the slope, at the fern. Nothing up-slope that a falling pole could have rolled from. The pole had been carried to this spot and left.

The second item was a glove. Then a lens cap. Then, most damning of all, a theodolite case, empty, its foam lining cut out in one clean piece—the instrument removed with care, the case discarded with carelessness. No one dropped a theodolite case. It was the single most valuable object a survey team carried, worth more than the truck that brought them in. You strapped it to your body. You slept with it between you and your neighbor.

Noor found six more items over the next two hours, and he began to understand what he was looking at. The objects weren't scattered. They were *placed*—each one off the trail, each one at a distance a searcher's eye would catch, each one positioned to say *here, here is where something happened, look here.* It was a trail of evidence laid down by someone who wanted a search party to find exactly this: signs of struggle, signs of loss, a team that had come apart on the mountain and been swallowed.

It was a story, written in props. And Noor had spent enough years reading the language of landscapes to know when a scene was staged.

The rain thickened as he climbed. By midday he was above the worst of the canopy, the trees shrinking to birch and then to pine twisted by wind, and the Vantaa Range opened around him in long gray shoulders of rock with cloud moving through the cols like slow breath. He checked the drive of his own instruments out of pure habit—pressure falling, humidity pegged, the whole atmosphere wound tight and rising. Another system coming in off the coast, bigger than the last. He had perhaps thirty-six hours of weather window, maybe less.

He ate cold food under a rock overhang and studied the route ahead. The high survey point sat on a spur below the eastern ridge, a day's climb. The team's red-pencil route continued past it toward the pass. Whatever the surveyor had cut out of his logbook, he'd cut it out somewhere on that spur.

Noor packed up and kept climbing.

* * *

He made the spur by late afternoon, in weather that had gone from rain to something colder and more ambiguous, sleet trying to decide what it wanted to be. The high survey point was a flat of wind-scoured granite with an instrument tripod still bolted to the rock, its anemometer turning lazily, its solar panel furred with grime. Dead. But beside it, a second tripod stood with instruments that were not dead—their housings sealed, their status lights burning small and green in the gray afternoon.

Noor frowned. He checked the serial numbers against the manifest Corven had given him. These weren't on it. Government-issue atmospheric sounders, the kind that cost more than Noor's entire salvage fee, still powered, still logging, eleven days—by the dust and weathering, longer—after the team that installed them had vanished.

He crouched beside the nearest sounder and, on impulse, popped its data port cover. There was a port for a field drive, and the port was empty. Cleanly, recently empty. He could see the bright scratch-marks in the dust where something had been plugged in and unplugged, more than once.

Someone had been here. Recently. Someone had been reading these instruments and carrying the data away, and they hadn't erased this the way they'd erased the footprints, because instruments bolted to bedrock were harder to disappear than tracks in silt. But they'd left the machines running. Left them drinking sunlight and writing their observations into internal memory, patient as lichen.

Or someone had left them running so that whoever came looking—salvage, search, cleanup—would find them, and be found finding them.

Noor straightened up slowly and turned a full circle, scanning the ridgelines. The cloud was low and moving fast, tearing across the eastern ridge in rags. Rock, snow patches, the black teeth of the spur. Nothing moved.

He told himself that was a good thing, and did not believe himself.

* * *

He camped that night in a hollow below the spur, out of the wind, with his bivouac strung low and his stove burning small behind a windbreak of stones. He was tired in the way that had stopped being about muscle and started being about the mind—the fatigue of a man who has spent a whole day assembling a picture he doesn't want to look at. Six meals unfinished. Pages cut from a logbook. Tracks erased with a border as neat as a survey line. A trail of planted evidence leading nowhere. Instruments still running, their data ports freshly used.

The mountain was not hiding a tragedy. The mountain was curating one.

He was banking his fire when he saw the light.

It came up on the eastern ridge, a small white point moving against the black of the skyline, and for the first three seconds his mind offered him the comfortable explanations. A star. But stars didn't move along a ridgeline at a walking pace. A headlamp, then—a survivor, one of the six, eleven days out, still moving, still alive. Noor's heart lifted toward that idea the way a balloon lifts, eager and stupid, and then the professional part of him, the part that had read ten thousand storms and been right about every one of them, took the observation away from his hope and looked at it coldly.

A survivor's light does not move like that.

A survivor's light bobs. It sweeps, hesitates, swings back, doubles on itself. It searches. It is the light of a man watching his feet, of a woman testing the ground ahead, of someone whose body is spending everything it has on the next step. Eleven days alone on a mountain would make that light slower, more ragged, more human.

This light was level. It held its height above the ridgeline with a steadiness no exhausted human could manage, and it moved at a pace that never varied—not the pace of someone crossing broken ground, but the pace of someone *patrolling* it. It stopped at intervals, precise as clockwork. Held still for a count. Moved on.

Too steady for a survivor. Too deliberate for a ghost.

Noor killed his stove and sat in the dark with his heart going hard and slow, and watched.

The light worked its way along the ridge for the better part of an hour, north to south, stop and move, stop and move. It passed above the high survey point. It paused there longer than at the other stops—long enough, Noor thought, for someone to check instruments, or to check on the people checking instruments. Then it continued south, descended out of sight into a col, and did not reappear.

He sat for another hour after it was gone, not moving, letting the cold soak through him while his mind did what it did best and worst: it built the system out of the observations. A patrol, moving on a schedule. Instruments left running as bait or as beacons. A scene below, staged for searchers. A logbook with its truth cut out. And somewhere at the center of it, a smooth-voiced man in Karva who had paid a ruined storm chaser's debts just enough to make him climbable, and who had sent him up here to *recover survey equipment* with a manifest that conspicuously did not include the equipment that was still running.

Noor thought about the contract folded in his breast pocket, and about the exclusivity clause he had signed without reading, and about the question he had been refusing to ask since the empty silt clearing, and he asked it now, in the dark, with the sleet ticking softly against his bivouac.

What exactly had Corven hired him to recover—and what was Noor supposed to be, walking this route with a beacon in his pack and a manifest in his pocket, other than the next set of tracks on a mountain where all the other tracks had been erased?

The sleet thickened. Somewhere below him in the black, small and almost tender, a sound carried up the slope: the clink of metal on rock. Once. Then nothing.

Noor lay back and did not sleep. Above him the cloud tore past the stars, and the mountain held its silence around him like a hand, and he understood at last that he was not alone on the Vantaa Range, and never had been.

The mountain was watching him back.
