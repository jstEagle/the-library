## 5. Ilse

Eleven days.

Ilse Brandt had stopped counting in hours on the fourth day, when the hours had begun to feel like a currency she could not afford to spend, and had started counting instead in the small economies of staying alive: three sips of meltwater before dawn, two after; one ration bar broken into quarters, eaten at intervals long enough that her stomach forgot it was being cheated. Eleven days since she had crawled out from under Tomas's body and understood, with a clarity that still visited her at night like a cold hand on the back of the neck, that everyone she had come up this mountain with was gone, and that the only reason she was not gone with them was that she had been down at the river gauge when the men came.

She had heard it before she saw anything. That was the thing no one believed afterward—would never believe now, because there was no afterward for anyone but her. The mountain had a grammar, and she had learned to read it: the tick of stone cooling at dusk, the cough of rockfall in the eastern couloir, the particular hush when snow began to think about falling. On that evening eleven days ago the grammar had been wrong. There had been a rhythm under the wind, mechanical and patient, and then voices—not shouting, not panicked, but *organized*, which was worse—and then the light.

She had watched it from behind the moraine, flat white light sweeping the camp in slow, methodical strokes, like a man reading a page he intended to burn. She had heard Anselm say something in his reasonable voice, the voice he used for funding committees, and then she had heard nothing more from Anselm ever again. She had pressed herself into the scree until the basalt printed its pattern into her cheek, and she had done the arithmetic that survivors do and hate themselves for: one of us might live if I stay hidden. All of us die if I don't.

She had stayed hidden. The light had moved on. And in the morning the camp had been a museum of itself—meals half-eaten, instruments humming, Tomas's mug still balanced on its rock—and Ilse had understood that the emptiness was not an accident. It was a message, or a trap, or both. Someone wanted the camp to look abandoned rather than emptied. Someone wanted whoever came next to conclude that the Vantaa survey team had simply walked off the face of the world, as teams sometimes did, as the mountains were allowed to keep.

So she had taken the drive—the data drive, pulled from the atmospheric array in the ninety seconds of fury and grief she allowed herself—and she had gone up instead of down, because down was where the light came from, and she had made herself a life in the cracks of the mountain. A hollow beneath a leaning slab, lined with the foam pad she'd cut from the mess tent. Snowmelt caught in a ground sheet. Her body burning its own fat with the patience of a banked fire. Each day she had watched the ridge through a gap the width of two fingers, and each night the light had come back, moving along the high line with that same terrible patience, sweeping, sweeping, a lighthouse for a coast that had been deliberately wrecked.

She was not rescued. She was *remaining*. There was a difference, and the difference was everything, and she held onto it the way you hold onto a rope in a whiteout—hand over hand, not thinking about the bottom.

* * *

The stranger arrived on the eleventh day, coming up the green staircase of the foothills with the stumbling, head-down gait of a man who had been sold something.

She saw him first as movement below the old camp—a dark figure threading the boulder field, pausing too often, checking a compass or a map or his own courage. Through the cracked monocular she'd salvaged from Anselm's kit she studied him by increments, the way she'd learned to study weather: not all at once, but in signs. Heavy pack, government-issue frame, the kind you requisitioned, not bought. Boots worn on the outer edge—he favored his right ankle, or favored his right side generally, keeping something between himself and the open slope. No rifle. That registered last and loudest. Everyone on this mountain carried something; this man carried only weight.

He reached the camp in the late gray of afternoon and stopped at its edge the way people stop at the edge of churches, and even at four hundred meters she could see him understand what she had understood eleven days ago: that the camp was intact, and that its intactness was obscene. He stood among the half-eaten meals and the humming instruments and turned slowly, and she read his posture the way she'd once read pressure gradients—shoulders climbing toward his ears, head tilting at the logbook, hands rising to touch his face and dropping again. Grief, maybe. Or recognition. Or the specific horror of a man realizing a story has been written for him and he is standing inside it.

Then he did something that made her breath go thin against the cold stone: he opened the logbook. He sat with it for a long time in the failing light, turning pages, and at some point he went very still, the stillness of a man who has just found pages missing, and Ilse felt the distance between them collapse to nothing. Whoever he was, he had just seen the same wound she had seen. Cut paper has a sound, a clean fibrous whisper, and it does not fade from the ear.

*Rescue,* said a voice in her that sounded like hope and was probably hunger.

*Hunter,* said another voice, older and colder, that sounded like the ridge light sweeping its patient arcs.

She lay in her hollow and let the two voices argue while the dark came down and the temperature fell through freezing with the smoothness of water finding its level. Rescue would mean someone sent to find them—which was possible, which happened, except that rescue parties did not arrive alone, unannounced, without radio checks, eleven days late, and did not stand over cut logbooks looking like men reading their own death warrants. Hunter would mean the light on the ridge had finally sent something down into the valley of the camp, and if that was true then every instinct she had honed for eleven days said: *stay in the stone, stay small, stay nothing.*

But hunters did not sit in dead men's camps looking gutted. Hunters did not check the logbook for missing pages. Hunters knew already.

And there was a third possibility, the one she kept turning over like a stone with something alive under it: he was neither. He was a tool, like she had been a tool of the survey, like they had all been tools of the program whose name she'd found stamped inside the instrument housing in letters she had photographed with her eyes until they burned there—CLEAR SKY, CLEAR SKY—and tools were sent up mountains by other people's hands. Which meant the question was not *what is he* but *whose hands*, and that question she could not answer from four hundred meters in the dark.

What she could answer was this: the drive weighed less than a kilogram and contained the only proof in the world that the monsoon was being bent out of its ancient shape, and proof that stays hidden is just a heavier kind of silence. Eleven days she had told herself she was surviving. The truth was uglier and simpler. She was *storing*. She was a vault with a heartbeat, and a vault that never opens is a grave with better insulation.

Below her, the stranger had made camp in the lee of the supply tent. She watched his stove flame bloom, small and blue and shockingly domestic. He ate sitting upright, facing downslope, the way a man sits when he expects to be approached from behind—or when he hopes. Twice he looked up at the ridge, at the place where the light would come, and twice he looked at the treeline near her hollow, and both times she was stone, she was moss, she was the mountain wearing a woman's coat.

Near midnight the light came.

It swept along the high line exactly as it always did, and she watched the stranger watch it, and what she saw in his silhouette undid her careful categories: he did not duck. He did not hide. He lay flat in the shadow of the tent wall and *observed* it, tracking its arc with his raised hand as though timing it, counting it, learning it—and when it passed he did not relax. He lay awake. She could tell because his breathing, faint on the wind that funneled down the cirque, never found the long slow tide of sleep.

A hunter would know the light's schedule. A rescuer would signal it. This man was doing neither. He was studying it the way she studied storms—with the humility of someone who had been wrong before and paid for it.

Ilse closed her eyes and made herself list the facts, because facts were the only rope she trusted.

One: he had come up the survey route precisely, which meant he had a map, which meant someone had given it to him, and the only holders of that map were the program and the people who funded it.

Two: he had no radio she could detect, and no beacon she could hear—but she had learned on the third night that absence of evidence was the program's favorite costume.

Three: he had looked at the cut logbook like a man looking at a crime scene, not a salvage manifest.

Four: she was dying by increments. Not fast—she had water and enough calories for perhaps six more days, seven if she cheated the cold—but certainly, and certainly was a luxury she could no longer afford to fund. Every day she waited, her body spent interest she could not repay. Every day, the drive's secrets got heavier and her arms got lighter.

Hope, she thought, was not a feeling. It was a calculation with incomplete data. You ran it anyway, because the alternative was to stop running numbers altogether, and that was the one equation she refused.

* * *

In the hour before dawn, when the cold stands at its full height, she moved.

Not toward him. That was the amateur's error, the direction every instinct screamed. She moved *around* him, a wide silent arc through the boulder field she had walked eleven times in the dark and knew the way she knew her own teeth—where the scree sang, where the frost-slick slab lay, where the wind funneling off the glacier covered the small betrayals of a human body. She came down on the far side of the camp, into the jumble of supply crates, and she took from them the thing she had decided on three nights ago and rehearsed in her mind until it was smooth: his pack.

Not him. His pack. A pack was leverage without violence, a conversation opener written in a language even frightened men understood. If he was rescue, he would lose the pack and laugh shakily and be glad to see her. If he was a hunter, he would need the pack more than he needed to kill her quietly, and needing things made men predictable, and predictable was survivable.

She reached the edge of his camp. Ten meters. Five. He was asleep at last—finally, mercifully asleep, his breathing long and tide-drawn, his face slack with an exhaustion so complete it looked almost holy. Up close she could see details the monocular had withheld: frost-nipped fingertips, a week of beard going gray at the chin, and around his neck, on a cord, a brass compass in a scarred leather case, tucked against his chest like something being kept alive rather than carried. People guarded objects that way only when the object was a person who had died.

His pack leaned against the crate beside him. She lifted it with both hands, slow as a tide, feeling its true weight—heavy, heavy with gear and something denser, boxy, deliberate—and she had it clear of the ground, she was one step into her retreat, when his breathing changed.

Not waking. Changing. The way air changes before a pressure fall.

"You can put it down," he said quietly, without opening his eyes, "or we can find out how fast you are."

Ilse stood in the gray pre-light with a stranger's pack in her arms and eleven days of stone-cold caution in her spine, and she heard her own voice come out low and scraped from disuse, steady enough to surprise her.

"I could have," she said. "I've been close enough for two days."

His eyes opened then, and she watched him take her in—the hollowed cheeks, the burned-looking eyes, the improvised clothing, the drive-shaped bulge strapped inside her jacket against her heart—and she watched him arrive, visibly, at the same fork in the road she had been standing at all night.

"Who sent you?" she asked.

And the stranger, lying in the ruins of her team's last camp with the ridge light's schedule fresh in his mind, said the one thing she had not prepared for:

"That's the question I climbed this mountain to ask myself."
