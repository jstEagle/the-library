## 6. The Knife in the Tent

Eleven days of watching had taught Ilse Brandt that patience was not a virtue but a muscle, and hers had grown strong enough to ache.

She lay in the hollow beneath the granite shelf where she'd dragged her sleeping bag on the fourth night, a place no one would find unless they were looking for a person rather than shelter. The rain came down through the trees in long silver threads, and below her, in the clearing that had been Camp Vantaa for six years before it became a grave, the stranger moved through the wreckage of her old life with the careful hands of a man who knew instruments.

He was reading the barometer. She could tell by the way he held it at eye level, tilting it against the light, the way Tomas used to. Her chest did something complicated and painful, and she pressed it flat with breath.

*Rescue or hunter.* The question had been running in her head since dawn, when she'd seen him come up the switchbacks below the tree line — one man, one pack, moving like someone who had done this before but not lately, his rhythm slightly out of true, a musician returning to an instrument after years away. Not military. Military men climbed with their weight forward, hungry for the ground. This man climbed like he was apologizing to it.

But the light on the ridge last night hadn't been an apology. It had been patient and deliberate, sweeping the valley in slow arcs, and she had lain in her hollow with her hand over her mouth until it died, counting her heartbeats like coins she couldn't afford to spend.

So: was this man connected to the light? Sent by whoever cut the logbook pages and left no tracks in ground soft as bread dough? Or was he what he appeared — a salvager, a scavenger, one more animal drawn up the mountain by the smell of abandoned things?

There was only one way to find out, and it required her to stop being a ghost.

She watched him work through the afternoon. He found the cache under the collapsed mess tent — she saw him stiffen when he pulled back the fly and saw the plates, the half-eaten rice gone to gray mush, the mug still standing upright where Anselm had set it down mid-sip. He stood over it for a long time. Then he did something she hadn't expected: he took off his hat.

He stood bareheaded in the rain over the remains of her team, and he said something too quiet for her to hear, and Ilse felt the question in her chest tip, slowly, like a scale finding its weight.

Not a hunter, then. Probably. *Probably* was a word she had learned to live inside these eleven days, the way you learn to live inside a small room.

She waited for full dark. Not because she needed it — she knew this camp better than her own hands now — but because a man surprised in daylight runs, and a man surprised in darkness freezes, and a frozen man listens.

* * *

His tent was the orange dome from the survey stores, pitched where their comms tent used to be, which told her something about how much he knew and how little. He'd built a small fire and boiled water, and the smell of it — actual tea, actual heat — made her stomach clench with a hunger she'd been managing by refusing to name.

She came in low from the boulder field, silent on wet ground because eleven days will teach you the language of wet ground, and she crouched behind his pack where it sat propped against a stone, and she waited for him to turn back to his fire.

When he did, she lifted the pack and stepped backward into the treeline shadow, and said, in English, clearly, the way you speak to a frightened animal:

"Don't."

He turned fast — faster than she'd guessed he could move — and his hand went to his hip where a man carries a knife, and she said "Don't" again, sharper, and raised into the thin light the blade she'd taken from Anselm's kit eleven days ago, the one she'd sharpened every evening on a stone until it could split a hair of rainlight.

"I have your pack," she said. "Everything you need to live is in it. I'm holding your life hostage so we can have a conversation. That's all this is. If it were anything else, you'd already know."

The stranger didn't run. That was the first good sign. He stood very still in the firelight, and she watched him do the arithmetic — the distance to her, the distance to the dark, the odds — and then, remarkably, he stopped doing arithmetic and just looked at her.

"You've been here," he said. His voice was rough, unused. "You're the reason the camp's still—"

"The camp is still exactly as we left it," she said. "Because we didn't leave it. Sit down."

He sat. Slowly, palms visible, the way you sit for a dog or a soldier. Good instincts, she noted. Or good training. She kept the knife loose in her grip and the pack between them like a border.

"What's your name?" she asked.

"Noor. Noor Iversen." A pause. "I was hired to salvage the survey equipment. Government contract, third party, I don't even know who signed the papers. I climb, I collect, I descend. That's the whole job."

"And you found the camp."

"I found the camp." His eyes went to the mess tent, then back to her face. "Meals still out. Instruments still running. Logbook with pages cut out. No bodies. No tracks." He said it evenly, but she heard the thing underneath it, the same thing that had been living under her own skin for eleven days. "I've been telling myself there's an explanation that isn't the obvious one."

"There is an explanation," Ilse said. "It's just worse than the obvious one."

She crouched, keeping the blade angled away from both of them, and set his pack down between them without releasing it. Rain ticked on the tent fabric. Somewhere upslope, water moved through rock with a sound like slow breathing.

"My name is Ilse Brandt," she said. "Atmospheric physics. I was deputy lead on the Vantaa Monsoon Survey. Six years of data. Six years of measuring what the monsoon does when nobody interferes with it." She watched his face. "In June, our readings started coming back wrong. Not noisy — wrong. Consistent. The moisture transport over the range was being shaped. Steered. There were pressure anomalies in the upper valley that had no natural signature. I've spent my career learning what weather looks like when it happens, Mr. Iversen. This wasn't happening. It was being *done*."

Noor had gone very still. Not the stillness of disbelief — she knew that stillness, the slight head-tilt, the mouth starting to form an objection. This was different. This was a man hearing a sound he'd heard before and hoping he was mistaken.

"We flagged it," she continued. "Anselm wanted to flag it quietly, through channels. I argued we should publish. We compromised — we sent the raw data set to the ministry contact who funded us, encrypted, with a cover letter asking one question: *is anyone operating atmospheric modification in the Vantaa corridor?*" She laughed, and it came out ugly. "We got our answer in nine days."

"The light on the ridge," Noor said.

"That started night three. One light, moving the ridgeline in sweeps. Never two. Never approaching. Just... confirming. Like someone checking a trap line." She tightened her grip on the pack strap. "Tomas went down toward the valley on day five to try to reach the relay station. He didn't come back. Anselm went after him on day seven. Neither of them came back, and there were no tracks, Mr. Iversen. Ground you sink into past your ankles, and no tracks. You tell me what that means."

He didn't answer. He didn't have to. She watched him arrive at it himself, watch it land on him like a dropped pack.

"They erased them," he said quietly.

"They erased the *team*. They're going to erase the data. And then they're going to erase anyone who knows the data existed." She looked at him steadily. "Which raises an interesting question about you, doesn't it? Who hired you? What exactly did they ask you to recover?"

"A list of equipment. Serial numbers. Coordinates." He frowned, and she saw him reaching back through memory, pulling the contract up into the light and examining it properly for the first time. "Barometers, theodolite, the data loggers—" He stopped.

"But not the drives," Ilse finished for him. "Nobody hires a salvage crew and forgets to mention the most valuable component. Unless the equipment isn't what they're paying for." She let that sit between them in the firelight. "What else was on the contract?"

He was quiet a moment. When he spoke, his voice had changed — flatter, harder, a man reading his own autopsy. "A clause. Confirmation of site condition. Photographic documentation of... 'any persons encountered.'" His jaw worked. "I signed it without reading past the payment schedule. I was—" He shook his head. "It doesn't matter why. I signed it."

*Desperation,* she thought. *They always pick the desperate ones. Desperate men don't ask questions; they're grateful for the work.* She knew the type because she'd been raised by one — a father who took contracts in the North Sea that broke other men, because the alternative was poverty and poverty, he said, was slower but just as certain.

She reached into her jacket, slowly, letting him see every movement, and drew out the drive.

It was small — smaller than the importance packed inside it, a black rectangle the size of a matchbox, scuffed from her pocket, wrapped in a strip of waterproof cloth. She'd pulled it from the primary logger on the second night, after the light first came, and carried it against her body ever since, next to the skin, where it stayed warm and where she could feel it like a second heartbeat.

"This is six years of baseline data," she said, "plus ninety-one days of readings showing deliberate atmospheric intervention in the Vantaa corridor. Pressure steering. Moisture diversion. Precipitation suppression over three valleys while loading the systems upstream." She turned it in her fingers, and the firelight slid across its black surface. "Whoever is doing this has been running an operational program for months. Not experiments. Operations. And the monsoon is beginning to answer back wrong."

Noor stared at the drive the way men stare at things that are about to change the shape of their lives — the way, she thought, a man stares at a door he can't unopen.

"The program has a name," she said. "I found it in Anselm's correspondence, buried in a funding acknowledgment. One line. CLEAR SKY." She pronounced it carefully, giving each word its full weight. "Clear Sky. As if the goal were blue skies and calm harvests. As if the opposite of what they're doing weren't written into the name itself."

"CLEAR SKY," Noor repeated. Something moved behind his eyes — recognition, or the start of it, some thread connecting to another thread in a pattern she couldn't see. "And you've been sitting on this for eleven days. Alone. With that light on the ridge. Why not go down? Why not take it out yourself?"

"Because the valleys are watched, and I'm a woman alone with no gear and no story that survives questioning. Because whoever erased my team will be watching the trails for exactly that — a survivor, descending, carrying something." She smiled without any warmth in it. "And because I needed someone to come *up*. Someone with a contract, a route, equipment. Someone whose presence on this mountain makes sense to them." She nudged his pack an inch toward him with her boot. "Congratulations, Mr. Iversen. You make sense to them."

Understanding crossed his face in stages, like weather fronting over a ridge. "You want to use my descent. My legitimate, contracted, unsuspicious descent."

"I want to use your legs, your route, and your paper trail. The drive goes down hidden in salvage equipment, in the pack of a licensed contractor with a signed government job. Nobody stops a man carrying out broken instruments." She finally released the pack, pushing it fully into his space, and kept only the knife loose in her lap. "That was the plan, anyway. Before I watched you stand bareheaded over my team's last supper and apologize to them."

He looked down at his own hands. "I didn't know," he said. "On the contract. I swear to you, if I'd known—"

"I believe you," Ilse said, and found that she did, and that believing him cost her something, because belief was a door and doors could be walked through from either side. "But belief isn't the issue. The issue is that the moment you carry this drive, you stop being a salvager. You become a witness. And witnesses, on this mountain, have a life expectancy measured in days." She held his gaze across the fire. "So I'm not going to put it in your pack. I'm going to put it in your hands, and let you choose. Because if you carry it because I forced you, you'll drop it the first time someone offers you money or a knife. It has to be yours."

She held out the drive.

For a long moment he didn't move. The fire cracked. Rain came down through the canopy with its endless soft percussion, and somewhere far above, beyond the cloud deck, the mountain went on doing whatever mountains do in the dark — indifferent, immense, keeping its own records.

Then Noor Iversen reached out and took the drive, and Ilse watched eleven days of solitary terror change hands and become a shared weight, and felt lighter and more afraid in the same instant, the way you feel after finally setting down something heavy in a place with no walls.

"It goes over the pass," he said. "Down to Solheim. There's a radio mast there — old relay, still maintained. From Solheim, this goes everywhere at once. Every open frequency. There's no taking it back once it's broadcast."

"Good," Ilse said. "Everywhere at once is the only safe place for it."

He turned the drive over in his frost-nipped fingers, and his expression shifted — she saw it happen, the moment the burden became real, became personal. "There's a problem," he said slowly. "My contract. Whoever wrote it — Corven, the man who hired me — he'll expect confirmation. Site condition. Persons encountered." He looked up at her, and the firelight made his eyes hard to read. "If I report you alive, they come for you. If I report the site empty, and they find out I lied—"

"Then we have a problem," Ilse agreed quietly.

And as if the mountain had been waiting for the words, as if it had been listening all along the way it had listened for eleven nights, a light bloomed on the ridge above the camp.

Not sweeping this time. Not patient. Fixed — planted on the skyline like a star that had decided to descend, burning steady white through the rain, aimed down into the valley.

Aimed, Ilse realized with a cold that had nothing to do with the altitude, directly at the glow of their fire.

Noor was already moving, killing the flame with a sweep of his hand, and the darkness closed over them like water. In the black, his voice came low and close:

"They're not checking the trap line anymore."
