## 7. What Corven Bought

The fire had burned down to a red eye in the dark, and Noor sat with the contract spread across his knees like a dead bird he was trying to bring back to life.

He had read it once before—skimmed it, really, in Corven's office above the harbor in Karva, while rain crawled down the glass and the Keslers' deadline ticked somewhere behind his ribs. He had signed on the last page because the last page was where signatures went, and because a man drowning does not study the water for its chemistry. He had told himself that salvage contracts were all the same: recover the equipment, log the serial numbers, return what could be returned. Gear. Metal and glass and lithium. Things without heartbeats.

Now, by firelight, with Ilse's data drive sitting in his breast pocket like a second heart, he read it again. All of it. Every clause.

The paper had gone soft at the folds from three days of climbing, and the ink had smeared where sweat had touched it, but the words themselves had not changed. Only their meaning had. That was the trick of contracts, Noor thought. They didn't lie to you. They just waited for you to become someone who could hear them properly.

*Item 4. The Contractor shall locate and confirm the disposition of all government survey assets designated VS-7 through VS-19.*

*Disposition.* Not *recovery*. Not *return*. Disposition—a word from morgues and insurance ledgers, a word that meant *state of being*, that meant *what happened to them*, that meant, if you pressed the word until it confessed: *are they dead*.

*Item 6. The Contractor shall document any evidence of human presence at or beyond the survey sites, including but not limited to remains, shelters, tracks, or signals.*

Any evidence of human presence. He had read that clause the first time as boilerplate, the kind of language lawyers stitched into everything so they'd have something to point at later. But it wasn't boilerplate. It was the job. It was the whole job, laid out in plain sight while he stood there counting the money in his head.

Corven hadn't hired him to carry equipment down a mountain.

Corven had hired him to walk the survey team's route and come back with proof that no one on it was still breathing.

Noor turned the page. His hands were steady, which surprised him. He would have expected shaking. Instead there was a strange cold clarity, the same clarity that came over him in the last minutes before a storm broke—the moment when all the noise fell away and the sky showed you exactly what it intended to do.

*Item 9. In the event the Contractor encounters persons associated with the survey expedition, the Contractor shall make no attempt at rescue, communication, or assistance, and shall withdraw immediately and report via the beacon provided.*

Via the beacon provided.

The beacon. Corven had handed it to him at the dock with a little ceremony, a black cylinder the size of a thermos, saying it was for emergencies, saying it would summon extraction if Noor got into trouble. Noor had carried it strapped to the outside of his pack ever since, a small weight of supposed safety.

But beacons didn't only call for help. Beacons broadcast. And whoever held the other end of the frequency could follow the signal the way a shark follows blood—not to save the bleeding thing, but because the bleeding thing was exactly where it wanted to be.

He thought about the light on the ridge. Three nights now he had seen it, moving with that unhurried deliberation, too steady for a survivor, too patient for a ghost. He had told himself it was a herder, a patrol, a government survey crew coming back for their own gear. He had wanted it to be rescue.

It wasn't rescue. It was an answer. Someone had received Corven's signal—received every step Noor took up this mountain, plotted on some screen in some warm room—and had sent men to close the distance. Not to extract Noor. To verify him. To do the part of Item 9 that Corven's contract phrased so delicately: *make no attempt at rescue.*

Or to do the part nobody wrote down at all. The part where witnesses stopped being witnesses.

The fire popped. Somewhere below the shelf of rock where they'd made camp, meltwater ran in the dark, a sound like whispering that never quite became words. Noor looked across the flames at Ilse Brandt, asleep sitting up against her pack, her chin dropped to her chest, one hand still curled near the knife she never let get far from her. Eleven days alone on this mountain before he found her. Eleven days hiding from the light on the ridge. She had known what he was only beginning to understand—that the mountain had eyes, and the eyes were not friendly.

She stirred. Her eyes opened halfway, that soldier's habit of surfacing instantly, taking inventory: fire, dark, stranger, drive. "You've been reading that thing for an hour," she said. Her voice was rough with sleep. "An hour is a long time to read four pages."

"It's a long time to learn you're not who you thought you were."

She came fully awake at that, unfolding herself with the economy of someone who had learned to waste nothing, not movement, not food, not trust. She crouched opposite him across the coals. "Read it to me."

So he did. He read it aloud in the flat voice he used for forecasts, item by item, and watched her face as the architecture of it assembled itself for her the way it had assembled itself for him. She didn't interrupt. When he finished, she was quiet for a long moment, looking into the fire.

"Confirm the disposition," she said. "That's how they said it. That's how they talked about us. I heard them on the radio, the last morning, before we scattered. A voice asking base camp to *confirm disposition of personnel*. I thought it was bureaucratic nonsense." Her jaw tightened. "It was a checklist."

"Corven sold you," Noor said. "All of you. Before any of you were even missing. He took the contract, and then he needed someone to walk the route, and he went looking for a man desperate enough not to ask questions." He laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, hollow, echoing off the rock. "And he found me. Because my ruin made me cheap. Because everyone in Karva knows Noor Iversen will take any job that clears his ledger. I wasn't a hire. I was a tool with debts."

"Does it change anything?" Ilse asked. Not cruelly. Genuinely. She had a way of asking questions that cut straight past comfort. "You were going to carry the gear out either way. Now you know what the gear meant. Does it change what you do?"

Noor looked at the contract again. At the exclusivity clause on the final page, the one he'd signed without reading, the one that bound him to deliver *all recovered materials and documentation exclusively to C. Corven or designated representatives*, penalty for breach set at a figure that would have bought the whole harbor district. He had signed away not just the gear. Everything he found. Everything he saw. Everything he knew.

Including her. Including the drive. Including eleven days of survival and whatever CLEAR SKY was and however many people had been erased off this mountain.

If he delivered, Corven delivered, and whoever Corven answered to buried the last evidence that the monsoon was being bent out of shape by human hands. If he breached, the penalty ruined him more thoroughly than the Keslers ever could—except the Keslers were already ruined-adjacent, already circling, and what was one more wolf at a carcass?

"I keep thinking about my father's compass," Noor said. "He gave it to me when I got my first chase contract. He said—" He stopped. Started again. "He said a compass doesn't tell you where to go. It tells you where you are. The going is your own business." He turned the contract over, as if something might be written on the back, though he knew there wasn't. "I've spent three years lost. I kept thinking if I could just clear the debt, clear the disgrace, I'd find north again. And the whole time I was walking somebody else's bearing. Following a signal I didn't know I was transmitting."

"The light," Ilse said. "You understand what it means now."

"I understand it's tracking me. Us. This camp." He looked up at the black wall of the ridge, invisible now except where stars cut off sharply along its spine. "Which means they'll come at night, or in weather, when visibility favors them. Adeyemi—if that's who it is—he won't rush. Men who are sure don't rush. They'll wait for us to move, because moving is when people make mistakes, and they'll take us on ground of their choosing."

"You say *they* like you've met them."

"I haven't. But I've read enough storms to know the pattern. Something gathering on the flank, waiting for conditions." He rubbed his eyes. "Here's what I can't figure. If Corven's people wanted us dead, why hasn't the light simply come down here? We're two people, half-equipped, one of us limping. Why watch?"

Ilse was quiet. Then: "Because confirmation isn't killing. Killing is messy. Confirmation is paperwork." Her voice had gone very flat, the way voices go when they are holding something heavy very carefully. "They need to know where the drive is. Who has it. Whether it's been copied, transmitted, whether there are others. They need the whole picture before they act, because acting wrong is how programs like theirs end up in newspapers." She looked at him. "Which means every hour we sit here, we're teaching them. And every hour we move, we're telling them where we think we're going."

"Then we stop being readable."

"That's easier to say than to do. There's one pass. You know that as well as I do. One pass, and they know we have to take it."

Noor pulled the beacon from the outside of his pack and weighed it in his palm. Black metal, cold even by the fire. A small antenna cap. A single green indicator light, dark now, obedient. He thought about hurling it into the gorge, letting it fall into the white water, cutting the thread that connected him to Corven and Corven to the men on the ridge.

But a cut thread told them something too. A silent beacon was information. A moving beacon was information. The only winning move was to make the information false.

"They gave me a tool," he murmured. "Maybe I use it."

Ilse's eyebrows rose. "Explain."

"Beacons transmit position. Fine. Position isn't destiny. I've spent my life predicting where weather systems will be, and the ones that survive are the ones that don't go where the model says." He began, slowly, to smile, and it felt strange on his face, like wearing someone else's coat. "We let the beacon travel honestly for another day. Then we leave it somewhere it can keep lying for us. A ledge. A gully. Somewhere it looks like we camped and moved on. Meanwhile we go quiet and fast and wrong—wrong being anywhere they aren't."

"And the pass?"

"We take it early. Before they expect it. In bad weather, if bad weather comes." He glanced at the sky out of old habit, reading the stars through breaks in the cloud, feeling the pressure in his ears, the taste of the air. "There's weather coming. Two days, maybe three. Something big, sliding down from the northwest. I can feel it the way you feel a train through the floor before you hear it."

"You trust that? Your feeling?"

Noor almost laughed. "That feeling is the reason I'm bankrupt. I called a superstorm three years ago that every official model missed. I told them to pull the fleet. They called me a fearmonger. The storm came, and forty boats went to the bottom, and somehow the story became that I was reckless, that I'd cried wolf, that my forecast was a stunt gone wrong." He flexed his hands, feeling the old ache in the knuckles. "Nobody ever apologized. Nobody ever checked the numbers. They just needed someone to blame, and I was convenient—I was loud, I was half-foreign, I owed money to the wrong people. So yes. I trust the feeling. It's the only thing anyone's ever punished me for that was actually true."

Ilse studied him across the coals, and something in her expression shifted—not warmth exactly, but recognition. One erased person looking at another.

"Then we have something in common," she said. "My team found the truth and died for it. You found the truth and starved for it. Different punishments for the same crime: being right at the wrong time." She reached out and tapped the contract on his knee. "So. What are you going to do about the man who bought you?"

Noor looked at the signature at the bottom of the last page. His own name, in his own hand, looped and confident, a stranger's confidence. *N. Iversen.* He had signed it in Karva with rain on the windows and the Keslers' deadline in his throat, and he had believed he was selling his labor, his legs, his tolerance for cold. He hadn't understood he was selling his eyes. That the job was to see, and to report what he saw, and that what he saw was supposed to be nothing.

Loyalty. He turned the word over. He owed Corven nothing—less than nothing; Corven had used his desperation as a lever and his route as bait. He owed the Keslers money, which was not loyalty, only arithmetic. He owed his father a compass he was failing to honor. He owed the drowned crews of Karva a forecast they'd ignored. And he owed this woman beside him, maybe, the debt of having walked into her hiding place carrying the enemy's beacon in his pack.

What he owed the dead on this mountain was simpler. He owed them the thing Item 9 forbade: assistance. Communication. Rescue, in the only form left to them—which was the truth, carried over the pass, shouted into whatever frequencies were still open.

"Nothing," he said. "I owe him nothing. That's what I'm going to do about him." He folded the contract along its soft creases and fed it, corner first, into the coals. The paper caught, curled, blackened. He watched Corven's careful language blister and lift on the heat, watched Item 9 glow orange and collapse into ash. "He bought a tool. Tools get repurposed."

Ilse watched the fire eat the words. "He'll know. When the beacon starts lying, he'll know."

"He'll suspect. There's a difference, and the difference is time." Noor looked up at the ridge again, at the hard line of stars. "And time is the one thing we can still steal."

The fire settled. Ilse lay back, knife under her hand, and within minutes her breathing slowed into real sleep—the sleep of someone who had decided, finally, that the person keeping watch was safe enough. Noor stayed up. He cleaned the ice axe. He checked the drive's casing, the small battery indicator Ilse had shown him, its amber light already dimmer than yesterday. Dying, she'd said. Days, not weeks. The truth had an expiration date, and it was ticking faster than the debt ever had.

Near dawn, he checked the ridge one last time.

The light was there.

But it had moved. Down from the crest, lower than he'd ever seen it, two-thirds of the way toward the treeline—close enough now that he could tell it wasn't one light but several, traveling in a spaced line, disciplined, unhurried, descending along the exact route Noor would have chosen for himself.

They weren't waiting anymore.

Whatever window he'd imagined had just closed, and the men coming down the mountain knew things about his position that he had only just finished learning himself. Noor woke Ilse with a hand on her shoulder, and when her eyes opened he didn't explain. He just pointed.

She looked at the lights for a long moment. Then she began packing.
