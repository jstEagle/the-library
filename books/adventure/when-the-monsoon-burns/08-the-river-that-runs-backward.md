## 8. The River That Runs Backward

*Ilse*

They had been running for two hours when Ilse heard the water.

Not the polite sound of a mountain stream—the polite sounds had been with them all morning, threading through the trees, chuckling over stones. This was different. This was a sound with weight behind it, a sound that pressed against the ears like a hand. She stopped on the game trail, breath tearing at her throat, and Noor nearly walked into her before he heard it too.

"That's wrong," he said.

"What?"

"The Vantaa streams run east this time of year. Snowmelt season's over. They should be down to a trickle by now." He pushed past her, shouldering through a curtain of wet fern, and Ilse followed because there was nowhere else to follow. The trees opened, and the gorge was there, and the water in it was going the wrong way.

She had seen the survey data. She had spent eleven days with the numbers, sleeping beside them, memorizing them the way a drowning woman memorizes the shape of a plank. She knew what CLEAR SKY was doing to the atmosphere above the range—knew it in the abstract, in the language of barometric curves and precipitation anomalies. But knowing a thing in numbers was different from standing above a gorge and watching a river run backward, west, uphill in spirit if not in fact, swollen to three times its honest width, brown as tea and loud as a crowd.

The seasonal flows had reversed. The data had said so, in its dry and terrible way, and here was the data made flesh.

"Where does it go?" she asked.

Noor didn't answer right away. He was reading the gorge the way she had seen him read the sky all morning—not with his eyes only, but with his whole body, head tilted, lips slightly parted, as though the landscape were speaking a language he'd been raised in and she could only hear the accents.

"There's a ford downstream. Or there was, in the old surveys. Basalt shelf, wide and shallow." He shook his head. "Nothing about this is shallow."

Behind them, far back along the trail, a light blinked through the trees. Once, twice. Deliberate. The patrol was not hurrying. It didn't need to hurry. The mountain was narrowing around them like a closing fist, and both of them knew every trail funneled toward the same few crossings, and the men with the lights knew the crossings better than the maps did.

Ilse looked at the water. The water looked back, the way deep water always does.

"We cross here," she said.

Noor turned to her, and she watched him weigh it—the same calculation she'd watched him make at her old camp, when she'd held his pack hostage with her knife and told him about CLEAR SKY and he'd had to decide, in the space of a breath, whether she was a survivor or a trap. He'd chosen to believe her. He was still choosing, every hour, to believe her.

"Here," he said, "the water's moving at maybe six meters a second. It's carrying boulders the size of your torso. If it takes you, it'll hold you against the far wall and you'll drown in a pocket of air the size of a coffin." He said it without drama, the way a man reads a weather report. "Downstream, the gorge widens. Slower water. Longer swim, but survivable."

"Downstream is where they'll expect us."

"Downstream is where anyone with a brain would go." A ghost of something crossed his face—not quite a smile. "So we don't."

* * *

*Noor*

Noor had grown up in a delta country, in a house his father built on stilts above a river that changed its mind twice a year. Before he could read, he could read water. His father had taught him the grammar of it: the way a current telegraphs its depth in the color of its surface, the way a submerged rock writes its signature in the V of broken water above it, the way a river lies with its smooth places and tells the truth with its rough ones. *The river is always talking*, his father used to say, *most people just never learn to listen.*

The river in the gorge was shouting.

He stood at the lip of the descent with the pack straps biting his shoulders and the drive's weight—Ilse's drive, the weight of eleven dead surveyors and a government's lie—pressing against his spine, and he listened. The main channel ran hard against the far wall, exactly as he'd told her. But forty meters downstream, where a tributary gully dumped its own load of floodwater into the gorge, the two currents collided and canceled, and behind the collision there was an eddy—a slow revolving pool, maybe eight meters across, tucked against the near bank like a room off a corridor.

From the eddy, a swimmer could cross the corridor in one burst. One burst was all a body had in water like this.

"It's a chance," Ilse said when he explained it, crouched beside him with her chin on her knees, watching the water with the flat unblinking attention she gave everything. "A chance isn't a plan."

"A chance is all the mountain ever sells." He pulled the rope from his pack—thirty meters of it, salvaged from her old camp along with everything else they carried of the dead team's gear. "You first or me?"

Something flickered in her face. He'd learned to read it by now, the way he read water: the small contractions around her eyes that meant she was doing arithmetic with trust. Eleven days alone. Eleven days of deciding, over and over, that other people were the danger.

"You first," she said. "If the eddy isn't where you think it is, I'd rather know before I'm in it."

It was the most honest thing she could have said, and he respected her for it. He tied the rope around his own chest, under the arms, and paid it out to her hands. "If I go under and it pulls me into the main channel—let go. Don't be a hero. Rope plus body equals two bodies."

"And if you make it?"

"Then you come across on the rope, and I pull against the current, and we both pretend this was a reasonable thing to do."

She almost smiled. It was the first time he'd seen her face attempt it, and it looked like a door opening in a house that had been shuttered a long time.

He went down the bank on all fours, the spray hitting him like thrown gravel, and the cold was a fact that arrived all at once and never left. He slid into the eddy and the world became noise and pressure and the rope singing behind him, and for four seconds he was nowhere, nothing, a piece of the river—then his hands found the far shelf of rock and he hauled himself out of the corridor of the current into the room of the eddy, gasping, alive, and the rope went taut and Ilse began to come across.

* * *

*Ilse*

The water took her the way she had always privately feared water would: completely, and without negotiation.

She had the rope. She had Noor's voice behind her, shouting something about the eddy, about kicking left, left—and then a log, black and barnacled with moss, came down the tributary gully like a battering ram and struck the rope mid-span.

The jolt went through her arms, through her teeth, through the architecture of her chest. The rope went slack. The current, patient as a creditor, collected her.

Afterward, she would remember it in fragments, the way she remembered the night her team was taken: the white sky wheeling overhead, the taste of silt, the impossible green of the far bank seen from underneath, refracted and wavering like something in a dream. The cold was not cold anymore; it was a hand closing. Her boots filled with the river. Her lungs began composing the first sentence of a scream she would never finish.

But her hands—her hands had spent eleven days learning the mountain's grammar. They found the submerged shelf of basalt Noor had described, the one that made the eddy possible, and they held it, fingers hooked into a seam, while the main channel tore at her body like a dog at a rag.

And then Noor was there.

He came into the water after her—she saw him as a shape, a dark diagonal against the white chaos, roped to the bank, leaning out past all sense—and his hand closed on her wrist with a grip that would leave bruises she would later catch herself pressing, privately, like old letters. He hauled. The seam in the basalt tore free of her fingers. For one endless second they were both in the corridor of the current, both of them nothing, both of them the river's—then the eddy took them, revolving them once, twice, gently, absurdly, like a mother turning a child by the shoulders to look at something, and set them against the near bank among the stones.

He dragged her out. She vomited river water onto the rocks and shook so hard her vision stuttered.

"Left," she managed, when she could speak. "You said kick left."

"You kicked right."

"I kick right when I'm drowning. It's a flaw of mine."

He made a sound that might have been a laugh if laughter could survive drowning, and then he did something she did not expect. He sat down beside her in the spray, shoulder to shoulder, and said nothing. He didn't ask if she was all right. He didn't tell her she'd been brave or foolish. He just sat, and let the shaking move through both of them, and after a while she understood that this was his version of holding her, and that it was, in its way, more intimate than arms would have been.

The drive, she realized. She patted her chest, under the soaked jacket, and felt the hard rectangle still there, strapped against her ribs where it had been since the camp. The river had wanted it too. The river hadn't gotten it.

"Still dry," she said.

Noor looked at her, at the rectangle's outline under her jacket, and something moved through his face that she couldn't name—awe, or dread, or the particular exhaustion of a man realizing the thing in his care is heavier than any pack.

* * *

*Noor*

They crossed the rest of the gorge on the far side, climbing the goat track above the waterline, and Noor kept looking down at the river and thinking about his father.

His father had drowned in that delta river, in the end. Not dramatically—a heart, a misstep, a river that had been a friend for sixty years and asked for one afternoon in return. Noor had been nineteen. He had inherited the house on stilts, the brass compass, and the belief that water could be read, that everything could be read, that the world was a text for those patient enough to learn its alphabet. He had built a career on that belief, and the career had made him famous, and then the career had made him a liar in the world's eyes, and the world had taken everything but the compass and the belief.

And now here was a river running backward because men in an office had decided the sky was a machine they could adjust, and the alphabet his father taught him was the only map anyone had.

"Ilse." She was ahead of him on the track, climbing with the grim economy of someone who has decided exhaustion is a rumor. "The readings you memorized. The flow reversals. When did they start?"

"Fourteen months ago. The first anomalies. The team flagged them as instrument error at first." She didn't stop climbing. "Then the harvests down-valley started failing, and they stopped calling it error and started calling it evidence, and then the light came to the ridge, and they stopped calling it anything at all."

Fourteen months. He turned the number over. Fourteen months of a reversed river, of borrowed weather, of a monsoon being pulled like a taffy by hands that had never touched it. His own ruin—the forecast he'd gotten right, the one that had ended him—sat inside that number somewhere. He'd always believed he'd been punished for being right. Now he wondered if he'd been punished for being early. If his little private catastrophe had been the first tremor of this larger one, and someone, somewhere, had decided that the tremor was easier to erase than the earthquake it foretold.

The trail crested a shelf of rock and the gorge fell away below them, and Noor stopped, because the river below had done something new.

At the confluence where the tributary met the main channel, the water was standing up. Not a wave—a wall, a standing wave two meters high, holding its shape against the current like a held breath, rotating slowly in place. He had seen standing waves in rapids all his life. He had never seen one hold for more than a few seconds. This one had been holding, by the look of the scoured rock around it, for months. The river was not just reversed. It was organized. It was flowing according to a schedule written in the sky by CLEAR SKY's engineers, and it was as wrong as a face with the features rearranged.

Ilse stood beside him and looked at it for a long time.

"My team's last log entry," she said quietly. "The one before the pages were cut out. Brandt wrote: *The weather is no longer arriving. It is being delivered.*"

Behind them, up the trail they'd come by, the light blinked again. Closer now. And below it, faint but unmistakable, carried on the gorge wind: the sound of an engine. Not a vehicle—a generator, or a pump, something mechanical and patient, the sound of men who had stopped chasing and started waiting.

Noor looked upstream, to where the gorge narrowed into a slot canyon the maps called the Throat, the only route toward the pass. He looked at the standing wave, at the reversed river, at the light on the ridge behind them.

"They're not following us," he said. "They're herding us. Every trail from here funnels into the Throat, and they know it." He tightened the pack straps against his shoulders and felt the drive's weight shift against his spine, and heard his father's voice across twenty years and one drowned delta: *the river is always talking, most people just never learn to listen.*

"Then we go through the Throat," Ilse said, "before they finish closing the door."

The first drops of the evening's rain hit the stones around them, fat and warm and wrong—warm, in air cold enough for breath to show—and Noor looked up at a sky that was promising a storm the season could not possibly deliver, and understood that the mountain had one more thing to say, and that it was going to say it loudly.
