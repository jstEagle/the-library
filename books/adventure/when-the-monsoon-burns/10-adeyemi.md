## 10. Adeyemi

Major Tunde Adeyemi had learned long ago that the mountains did not care what you believed. They cared only about pressure gradients, temperature differentials, the arithmetic of air. It was one of the reasons he loved them, and one of the reasons he trusted himself among them more than he trusted himself in rooms full of men.

He stood at the window of the forward operations post—a converted survey station above the tree line, all riveted steel and humming generators—and watched the Vantaa Range assemble its evening. Clouds came up the valley like a slow tide, gray-green with moisture, dragging their bellies along the ridgelines. Somewhere below, in that drowned green country of foothills and gorges, two people were moving through terrain that had already killed six better-prepared souls.

He did not think of them as people. That was the discipline. He thought of them as variables.

"Satellite pass in eleven minutes," said Okafor from the console, not looking up. She was young, precise, and had stopped asking questions sometime around her second deployment, which Adeyemi considered the mark of either wisdom or damage. With Okafor he suspected both.

"Run the seeding schedule again," he said. "Frontal insertion, western approach. I want the closure complete before first light."

"You're sure about the timing, sir? The moisture profile is—" She hesitated. "It's aggressive."

"It's supposed to be aggressive." He turned from the window. "That is the entire point of an engineered storm, Sergeant. A natural one you can only survive. This one we build to specification."

She turned back to her screens. The generators throbbed beneath the floor like a second heartbeat.

* * *

Adeyemi had been a soldier of weather for nineteen years, and in that time he had come to understand that most people misunderstood his work completely. They imagined men like him as villains out of cheap fiction—cackling over levers, drowning villages for profit. The reality was quieter and harder. The reality was famine projections.

He kept them on his personal slate, encrypted, updated quarterly: yield curves for the southern provinces, rainfall deficits plotted against population growth, the slow red thickening of maps where children would starve if the monsoon failed three years running. He had stood in those provinces once, early in his career, as a junior officer distributing grain during the Kess drought. He remembered the weight of a child carried into the aid station—how light she had been, how her mother held her the way you hold something already surrendered. He had been twenty-six years old and he had made a promise to himself in that dust-choked room: never again would he watch the sky fail these people and do nothing.

CLEAR SKY was his answer to that promise. Not a weapon. A tourniquet.

The program's premise was simple enough to explain to a farmer: the monsoon was becoming unreliable, and unreliability at scale meant death by increments. So they steadied it. Seeding flights, thermal injection stations along the coastal shelf, pressure management across the upper basins. They gave the rain a schedule. They made the sky keep its appointments. And for seven years it had worked—the harvests held, the granaries filled, the famine curves on his slate bent downward year after year like a bow being unstrung.

The cost, of course, was elsewhere. Weather was a ledger; nothing was created, only moved. Rain promised to one valley was taken from another. The lowland harvests had begun to stutter. There were whispers in the hill towns—*borrowed weather*, the herders called it, spitting the words like a curse—and whispers had a way of becoming surveys, and surveys had a way of becoming leaks.

Which brought him to the Brandt woman and her team of idealists with their instruments and their certainty.

* * *

He reviewed their file again that night, because he was a thorough man and thoroughness was a form of respect. Ilse Brandt, atmospheric chemist, thirty-four. Her team had gone into the Vantaa Range under a legitimate environmental survey permit—legitimate enough, anyway, issued through channels Corven's employers had spent years cultivating. Six researchers. Eleven weeks planned. Their mandate: baseline monsoon sampling in a high-altitude corridor where CLEAR SKY's manipulation left fingerprints in the isotopic record.

They had found the fingerprints. Of course they had. The data drive they'd pulled from their own instruments contained everything—seeding signatures, thermal anomalies, the whole architecture of the program laid bare in numbers elegant enough to end careers and governments' appetites for the program both.

And then they had died. Or so the report read.

Adeyemi read the incident summary the way he read all such documents: twice, slowly, looking for the seam. *Team lost in flash-flood event, gorge crossing, day sixty-three. Equipment scattered. No bodies recovered.* No bodies recovered. In his experience, no bodies recovered usually meant exactly that—mountains were efficient—but it also meant no confirmation, and confirmation was the one commodity he could never requisition.

He believed they were dead. He wanted to believe it, which was precisely why he checked the belief the way a man checks a rope before trusting it to hold his weight. Six researchers, fit and experienced, caught in a gorge flood—they might die, yes. But all six? Without a single recovered body, without gear distributed in the pattern a flood leaves? And the base camp, when the recovery team reached it: meals half-eaten, instruments still running, logbook with pages cut away. Floods did not cut pages from logbooks. People cut pages from logbooks, and people who cut pages intended to be read later or never found at all.

He set the file down and looked at the window, where the clouds had finished assembling and the first fat drops were ticking against the steel roof.

If the woman lived—if she had found some stray soul to help her carry that drive toward the pass—then everything he had built, everything the dead child in the aid station had demanded of him, hung on the speed of two exhausted people climbing through weather he could control.

So he would control it.

* * *

The storm went up at 0300, and Adeyemi stayed awake to watch it being born.

On the main display, the Vantaa basin rendered itself in false color: cold blues at altitude, the wet green smear of the foothills, and threading through it all the injection corridors where the seeding flights had laid their payload. Okafor worked the console with steady hands, releasing the trigger sequence station by station, and the atmosphere answered like an instrument being tuned. Pressure fell along the western passes. Temperature dropped through the layer where rain becomes something with teeth. Moisture that had been drifting contentedly overhead was now, quite suddenly, in the wrong place at the wrong temperature, and the sky began to do what skies do when you arrange their failure for them.

By dawn it was snowing above four thousand meters, and by midmorning it was not snowing so much as erasing.

Adeyemi stood at the window with tea going cold in his hand and watched the whiteout take the ridgelines one by one. It was beautiful. He had never stopped finding it beautiful—the way a storm closed over a landscape like a hand closing over a candle. Somewhere under that white were two figures, if the figures existed at all, and every hour the storm deepened, their options narrowed from many to few to one, and the one remaining option was to turn back down into the valleys where his patrols could do quietly what the mountain was doing loudly.

"Pass closures confirmed," Okafor reported. "Northern col, eastern saddle, river route. Civilian traffic advisories are broadcasting. The valley towns will assume it's a natural system." A pause. "Solheim's mayor has already filed a complaint about the harvest roads."

"The mayor can complain to the monsoon," Adeyemi said, and immediately regretted the flippance, because it sounded like something Corven would say, and there were days he feared he was becoming the kind of man who hired brokers like Corven.

He drank the cold tea anyway. Discipline.

* * *

Corven called at noon, his voice arriving through the static-scrubbed channel smooth as poured oil. "Major. I hear the weather's turned."

"The weather does that."

"My client is pleased. Closure by storm reads as act of God. Very clean." A pause with a smile in it somewhere. "Though my client wonders—two sets of tracks were reported at the old survey camp. A local tracker's rumor. Probably nothing."

"Probably," Adeyemi said.

"But if it's something, I have a man positioned near the waystation who could confirm. For a fee. My client's fee structure is, I'm afraid, creative."

Adeyemi looked out at the whiteness swallowing the world. He understood what Corven was: a man who sold other men's desperation by the kilogram. He despised him with the clean, uncomplicated hatred of a man who still believed his own hands were clean—or told himself he did.

"I don't purchase rumors, Corven. If there are survivors on my mountain, I will find them myself."

"And do what, Major?" The oiliness thinned, just slightly, and something sharper showed underneath—the question of a man who genuinely wanted to know what happened to loose ends. "Your orders are containment. Containment of data. But data doesn't freeze to death. Data doesn't fall into a gorge. Only people do that. So I'll ask plainly, since we're professionals: if the Brandt woman is alive up there, what happens to her?"

Adeyemi was quiet for a moment. Snow ticked against the glass.

"That," he said finally, "is above your rate."

He cut the channel and stood alone with the question, because Corven, for all his vileness, asked the correct questions. What did happen to survivors? He was not a murderer. He needed that to be true the way other men needed God. His orders spoke of containment, of asset denial, of euphemisms designed by committee so that no one ever had to write the word *kill*. But orders were weather too—they moved things without touching them. A closed pass. A delayed rescue. A patrol that arrived just late. The machinery of plausible deniability, in which men like Adeyemi turned cranks and men like Brandt froze, and no signature anywhere read as murder.

He had signed off on the incident report that read *no survivors expected*. He had known, signing it, what *expected* purchased: the right to make it true.

He had done it believing in the famine curves. Believing that a child's weight in his arms nineteen years ago outweighed six researchers' lives now. That arithmetic had carried him through every operation, every closed valley, every borrowed harvest. Conviction was load-bearing. He had built his whole self on it.

But lately—in the last months, since the lowland failures had stacked from anomaly into pattern—he had noticed something in the models that he had not reported upward. The stabilization algorithms were oscillating. The corrections required each season were larger than the last. The system was not settling into the new equilibrium the engineers had promised; it was hunting, overshooting, the monsoon swinging wider each year like a pendulum someone kept pushing. CLEAR SKY had been designed as a tourniquet. Tourniquets, left on too long, killed the limb.

He had not told anyone this. Not because he was hiding it, but because saying it aloud would make it real, and if it became real, then the arithmetic changed, and if the arithmetic changed, then everything he had done—all of it, the closed files and the erased teams and the storms built to specification—would need to be weighed again on scales he was afraid to look at.

* * *

Evening came down blue and absolute. The storm had settled into its long grind, the engineered heart of it beating on schedule, dumping snow across every route off the high country. Okafor brought him the day's summary and he read it standing up, the way he read bad news.

Patrol reports: negative. Thermal imaging: masked by precipitation. The beacon Corven's people had seeded on the ridge still pulsed its idiot rhythm, guiding no one, or guiding whoever was fool enough to follow it.

"Sir," Okafor said, gathering her notes, hesitating at the door in the way she did perhaps twice a year. "The Brandt team. Before. Did you know any of them?"

He considered lying, or deflecting, or the dozen professional answers available to him.

"I met Dr. Brandt once," he said. "At a conference, three years ago. She presented on isotopic drift in high-altitude precipitation." He almost smiled. "Her data was already showing our fingerprints. She didn't know what she was looking at. She thought it was industrial contamination." He set the empty cup down. "She was very good. Rigorous. The kind of scientist who checks her own work three times before she trusts it."

"So she'd have double-checked whatever she found up there."

"Yes, Sergeant. She would."

Okafor nodded slowly, and he saw her arrive at the same place he had been living for days: that rigorous people did not publish rumors, and that whatever was on that drive, if it existed, was not a mistake.

"Get some sleep," he said. "We're above the snowline now. All of us."

She left. He stood at the window a long time, watching the storm he had made devour the passes, feeling the strange twin weights he always felt on nights like this—the rightness of it, the famine curves holding their shape in his mind like scripture, and beneath the rightness, faint but persistent, a sound like ice cracking under load.

Somewhere on that mountain, if she lived, Ilse Brandt was checking her work a third time.

And far below, in the last green folds of the foothills before the white country began, a lamp flickered in the window of a herder's hut where two travelers had stopped to warm themselves—two travelers whose presence had just registered on a patrol drone's thermal sweep, a faint heat signature in a doorway, logged, flagged, and queued for morning review.

Adeyemi did not see the report yet. But the mountain had begun, in its patient way, to give up its secrets—and the first of them was glowing amber on a screen in a hut full of sleeping strangers, waiting for dawn.
