## 12. White Silence

The storm did not arrive the way storms were supposed to arrive.

Ilse had grown up on the Baltic coast, where weather came honestly—out of the west, announced by gulls going quiet and a greenish bruise along the horizon, giving you hours at least to pull in nets and batten hatches. Even in the Vantaa foothills, during the survey's first weeks, the monsoon had kept its old manners: building all afternoon, breaking at dusk, spending itself by morning. Weather you could read. Weather that kept its appointments.

This was different. This came down out of a sky with no face.

She felt it first as a pressure behind her eyes, an ache like the onset of a fever, and then as a wrongness in the sound of the wind—a note held too long, too steady, the way a machine holds a note rather than a lung. She stopped on the slope and turned her hooded face toward Noor, who was three meters below her, cutting steps into the crusted snow with the mechanical patience of a man who had done nothing else for two days.

"Noor." Her voice came out flattened by the wind, stripped of everything but volume. "Listen."

He didn't stop. His hands—she'd been watching them all day with a dread she hadn't let herself name—moved in their stiff, half-curled way, swinging the axe, packing the step. He had stopped being able to feel his fingertips sometime yesterday, she was almost certain, because he'd started testing each hold twice before trusting it, and he'd stopped taking off his gloves even inside the tent, even to eat, as if the air itself might finish what the cold had begun.

"Noor! The wind!"

He looked up then, and she saw him hear it—the held note, the too-even roar. Something moved across his face that she recognized from her own mirror over eleven days alone on this mountain: not fear exactly, but the cold arithmetic of a man counting what he had against what was coming.

"That's not natural," he said. It wasn't a question. He'd said something similar two nights ago, staring at the pressure gauge on Maret's wall, at the barometric line falling in a curve so smooth it looked printed. *Weather doesn't do straight lines,* he'd told her. *Weather is a drunk walking home. This is a man marching.*

"Can we outrun it?" she shouted.

He turned his head slowly, reading the slope above them—the col still a gray notch between black towers, impossibly far—and then the slope below, and then the sky, which had begun to do something she had no vocabulary for. The clouds weren't moving across the valley. They were descending into it, pouring over the ridgelines like milk over the lip of a bowl, erasing the peaks one by one from the top down.

"No," he said. "But there's rock ahead. A band of schist, maybe four hundred meters up. If we can get under an overhang—"

The first snow hit them sideways.

* * *

It wasn't snow, not really. Snow fell; this arrived. It came in horizontal sheets so dense that Ilse lost sight of Noor three times in the first ten minutes, finding him again only by the pale ghost of his pack frame and the rhythm of his axe. The world contracted to a sphere perhaps five meters wide, white walls pressing in, and inside that sphere there was only the slope, the rope between them taut and humming, and the sound—that held, mechanical note, rising now, filling her skull the way water fills a bell.

She had survived eleven days alone on this mountain before Noor came, and she had thought that had taught her what cold was. She had been wrong. That cold had been patient, a slow thief working through the nights while she slept in the wrecked survey tent. This cold was violent. It wanted her immediately. It got into the seams of her clothing within minutes, found the damp places where sweat had gathered during the climb, and turned them to iron bands around her ribs, her wrists, the small of her back. Her exhaled breath froze in her eyelashes until the world appeared through a fringe of white needles.

The drive rode against her sternum, inside her jacket, in the pouch she'd sewn from the survey tent's canvas. She could feel its small hard weight with every heartbeat, and she had taken, without quite admitting it, to timing her pulse against it. *You are warmer than I am,* she thought at it, absurdly, somewhere in the white blindness. *You are the warmest thing I'm carrying. Don't waste it.*

Noor's voice reached her once, torn nearly to nothing: "—rock! Left! Follow the rope!"

She followed the rope. The slope steepened, then broke into the schist band he'd promised—dark stone standing out of the whiteness like the bones of a drowned ship. And there, where the rock had fractured along some ancient seam, an overhang: not large, not comfortable, a shelf of stone jutting over a hollow barely deep enough for two people to sit pressed together, but it faced away from the wind, and it was not snow, and after six hours inside the storm's mouth it seemed to Ilse like the interior of a cathedral.

They crawled into it and lay side by side on stone, gasping, while the world outside ceased to exist.

* * *

The tent died forty minutes later.

They heard it happen—or rather they heard the absence happen, the particular silence where the flapping of nylon should have been. Noor had anchored it badly, or well, Ilse would never know which; he'd strung it in the lee of the schist with the last of his strength, and the storm simply took it, peeling it off the mountain whole, a bright orange shape tumbling end over end down into the white void like a leaf off a bonfire. Gone. Along with it went the stove, the fuel, the spare rope coil lashed beneath it, and roughly half the food—the dried fish, most of the flatbread, the sugar tin that Maret had filled from his own stores and refused payment for.

Ilse watched Noor watch it go. In the gray light under the overhang his face was very still, and she understood that he was doing the same arithmetic she was, and arriving at the same number.

"We have what's in our pockets," she said. "And what's in my pack."

"And the hut is two days behind us." He said it flatly, factually, the way he said everything now, as if emotion were another kind of weight he couldn't afford to carry uphill. "And the pass is—" He stopped. Recalculated. "I don't know how far the pass is anymore. Without the route."

Without the route. The words sat between them. Their map had gone with the tent—not the paper map, which Noor still carried folded against his chest next to the compass, but the route itself, the lived knowledge of it: the safe line across the icefield, the place where the cornice overhung, the sequence of landmarks that had made the pass a destination rather than a rumor. Two days of visibility had built that knowledge, and the storm had deleted it in an afternoon.

Outside, the white silence roared.

That was the thing about it, Ilse thought, pressing her shoulder against Noor's for warmth neither of them would mention. It wasn't silence at all. It was the loudest silence she had ever heard—a sound with no direction, no variation, no information in it whatsoever. Real weather spoke. Real weather told you things if you knew how to listen: where it had come from, what it intended, when it would tire. This sound told you nothing. It was a wall of noise erected specifically to make listening useless.

Adeyemi's work, she thought. Somewhere below them, or above them, or inside some warm room full of screens, a man had ordered this the way other men ordered artillery, and the sky had obeyed.

"Ilse." Noor's voice was strange. Thick. "Your hands. Let me see your hands."

"It's my hands we should be discussing?"

"Your hands."

She extended them reluctantly into the gray light. Her fingers were bad—stiff, bloodless at the tips, the nails gone a bruised lavender—but she'd had worse during her eleven days alone, and she said so. Then she took his hands instead, because he'd offered his as if in trade, and she made the mistake of looking at them properly for the first time in a day.

They were worse than bad. The frostbite had climbed past his knuckles overnight. The fingers were swollen, waxy, mottled white and angry red, and when she tried to close his right hand around an imaginary compass grip, the fingers moved like something borrowed, like tools operated by someone else.

"Noor." She kept her voice level. She had learned, in eleven days alone, that panic was a luxury purchased with calories, and she was no longer rich enough for it. "Can you hold the compass?"

He was quiet for a moment. Under the overhang, with the storm sealing them in, the question hung in the dark like smoke.

"I can hold it," he said finally. "Whether I can read it—" He flexed the ruined fingers, and she saw him fail to hide the pain of it. "My father used to say a compass isn't in the hand. It's here." He tapped his temple with the back of his wrist, the only gesture the fingers allowed. "Bearings, drift, the feel of a slope under your boots. The brass is just a check. A way of arguing with yourself."

"And if you argue wrong?"

"Then we walk off the mountain," he said, "in whichever direction feels like south."

* * *

They ate half a ration bar each and shared the last of the water, melted against their stomachs in the metal flask because there was no fire and would be no fire. Ilse chewed slowly, mechanically, making herself swallow, and it was while she was chewing that the thought arrived—the one she had been circling for two days without landing, the way a bird circles before committing.

The battery.

She pulled the drive from its pouch and held it in her cupped palms. It was a survey-grade unit, military-hardened, built to survive exactly this kind of abuse, and it had been built, she remembered now, turning it over in the gray light, with a sealed internal cell rated for field operation. She had checked the charge the night they left Maret's hut. Ninety-one percent. Enough, she'd calculated, for the broadcast in Solheim, for the transfer to whatever transmitter the town's radio mast carried, with margin to spare.

But that calculation had assumed ordinary cold.

She pressed the edge of the drive and woke its status light. The little diode blinked amber—the color it blinked when the cell dropped below reserve threshold—and the percentage readout, when she navigated to it with a thumbnail she could barely feel, said thirty-eight percent.

Thirty-eight. From ninety-one, in two days.

"What?" Noor leaned close, his breath fogging in the space between them.

"The cold." She heard her own voice go thin. "Lithium cells don't die in the cold, but they sleep—they lose capacity, permanently, if they spend too long below freezing at low charge. And this has spent eleven days in my jacket at altitude, and now—" She stopped. Made herself say it plainly, because plainness was all either of them could afford. "The data has an expiration date, Noor. The drive will hold the files forever. But it won't be able to give them up. Not to the mast, not to anything. In—" She ran the numbers, and hated them. "In three days, maybe four, if this cold holds. After that, broadcasting it would be like trying to pour a river out of a frozen bottle."

The white silence roared outside their stone shelter, indifferent, total.

Noor was quiet for a long time. When he spoke, his voice had changed—she heard it change, the flatness cracking along some fault line she couldn't see.

"Three days." He laughed, or something adjacent to laughter, brief and terrible. "Three days ago we had a route, a tent, a stove, and ten days of food. Now we have no route, no tent, no stove, two days of food, and a clock." He looked at his hands, lying in his lap like something washed ashore. "The mountain is collecting its toll. Everything we brought up here, it's taking back piece by piece. And it hasn't even started on us yet."

Ilse put the drive back in its pouch, against her skin, where her heartbeat could keep it company. And she found—this surprised her, even in the moment—that she was not afraid. Or not only afraid. Beneath the fear was something harder and stranger, something that had been growing in her since the day she'd cut the pages out of the logbook and buried her team's names under a cairn: the certainty that the toll was worth paying. That some prices were not losses but payments. Her team had paid. Maret was paying, feeding strangers from a failing larder and asking only to be remembered. Even Dov, the debt collector with the dead nephew, had paid.

"We'll make it," she said. "You'll navigate by whatever it is you navigate by. I'll keep this warm. And in Solheim there's a mast, and on that mast there's a transmitter, and through that transmitter there are—" she gestured vaguely at the stone above them, at the whole listening world beyond the storm "—people. Millions of people who've been told the weather is natural. Who've been told their harvests failed honestly."

Noor looked at her. In the gray light his eyes were the only warm thing left, and even they were rimmed with frost-scald.

"You sound like a believer," he said.

"I am one." She settled back against the stone, shoulder to shoulder with him, and closed her eyes. "I watched what happens when nobody believes anything. Eleven days of it. Never again."

* * *

She slept in fragments, surfacing each time to check two things: the drive's amber blink against her chest, and the sound of Noor's breathing beside her. Between fragments, the storm went on speaking its one wordless word, and the temperature inside the overhang continued its slow descent, and somewhere in the smallest hours of the night she woke to a sound that did not belong to the storm at all.

Voices.

Faint, shredded by wind, coming from downslope—from the direction of the route they had abandoned, the direction of Maret's hut, the direction of everything behind them. More than one voice. And threaded through them, unmistakable even at this distance, a sound she had last heard on the ridge eleven days ago, the sound that had taught her to hide:

The steady electronic chirp of a tracking beacon.

She put her hand over Noor's mouth and felt him come awake instantly, wholly, the way soldiers wake. In the dark under the stone she brought her lips close to his ear.

"Someone's climbing," she breathed. "Behind us. With a beacon."

His eyes opened in the gray gloom, and she watched him listen—really listen, head tilted, the old skill moving through his broken body like current through a dead wire. When he answered, his whisper was almost lost under the storm.

"That's not a patrol rotation. Patrols don't climb in this." A pause. The voices came again, closer, or the wind shifted and let them through. "That's someone who *has* to climb in this. Someone whose money's on the other side of this pass."

Ilse pressed the drive tighter against her heart, feeling its fading warmth, its shrinking hours.

"Then whoever it is," she whispered, "we're racing the same clock. And they know exactly where we are."
