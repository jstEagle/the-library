## 13. Dov's Bargain

The storm had spent itself by morning, and what it left behind was a world made of glass. Every branch on the treeline below the hut wore a sheath of ice; every rock glittered like something quarried from a jeweler's dream. The sun came up the color of old bone, and Noor stood in the doorway of Maret's hut with his hands wrapped in strips of blanket, watching the light move across the slope and thinking that beauty, at this altitude, was just another way the mountain lied to you.

Behind him, Ilse slept the thin, rationed sleep of the wounded. The graze along her ribs had stopped weeping, but the skin around it had gone the gray-white of frostbitten fruit, and when she moved in her sleep her breath came out in small, careful sounds, as if even unconsciousness required negotiation. The drive sat between them all night like a third sleeper, its dying battery light pulsing amber every few seconds—a heartbeat counting down.

He had tried to warm his hands over Maret's stove until the feeling came back, and what came back instead was pain, bright and total, the nerve-endings waking like men pulled from a flood. He'd bitten down on a leather strap while they throbbed. His father would have said: *the body keeps honest books, Noor. It bills you for everything.* His father had said a great many things, most of them now sealed inside a brass case somewhere under forty feet of glacier ice.

Maret was outside doing something to the goat pen, moving with the unhurried economy of a man who had survived sixty winters by never spending more effort than a task deserved. The old woman had fed them barley and dried fish and asked nothing, and Noor kept turning that over in his mind the way he used to turn a pressure chart—looking for the low center, the place where the kindness would curve inward and become a debt. He found nothing. It unsettled him more than the patrol lights had.

The wind came down off the col in long, clean gusts, carrying the smell of snow and stone and, faintly, of woodsmoke that wasn't theirs.

Noor smelled it before he saw anything. Woodsmoke, and under it something chemical and wrong—the sharp ghost of stove fuel burning too rich, the smell of someone running a camp stove hot because they were too cold to care about the waste.

He turned downslope and saw him coming.

Dov Kessler climbed out of the treeline like a man being audited by the mountain itself. He was without a pack. His coat was crusted white across the shoulders, his beard a mass of ice, and he walked with the stiff, deliberate gait of someone whose feet had stopped reporting to him an hour ago. When he was twenty meters out he stopped, raised both hands—empty—and stood there swaying slightly, letting Noor look at him.

"You're a long way from your ledger," Noor said.

Dov's lips were split and dark. "You going to shoot me?"

"I don't have a gun."

"Then I'll take my chances." He took another step and his knee buckled, caught, held. "Water. Please. I've been walking since the storm broke and I think I gave myself to it a little."

Noor should have shut the door. Every calculation he owned said *shut the door*—this was the Keslers' advance man, the polite knife who had offered to erase a debt in exchange for a data drive, who had tracked them through a waystation and a gorge and half a mountain. But Noor looked at the man's hands, blue at the fingertips, and at the tracks behind him—uneven, staggering, no second set of prints anywhere in the snow—and he thought about how the weather didn't care whose side you were on. It was the one lesson of his whole ruined career that had never once been wrong.

He brought him water and sat him by the stove, and Ilse woke and watched from the bunk with her hand resting near the knife under the blanket, saying nothing, missing nothing.

* * *

Dov drank three cups before he talked, and when he talked, he talked to the fire.

"The storm," he said. "Adeyemi's storm. You know what it did below the snowline? It wasn't supposed to come down. That's the whole design—everything stays above the passes, a wall of weather, nothing spills." He turned his cup in his cracked hands. "It spilled."

Noor waited. He had learned, in the years when forecasting was still his trade, that people told you the shape of a thing faster if you let the silence do the asking.

"My sister's boy," Dov said. "Tomas. Eleven years old. They live in the lowlands past Solheim, where the river bends. When the engineered front collapsed off the range it didn't behave like weather. Weather has edges. This thing came down like a decision." His voice stayed flat, which was worse than breaking. "Flash flood off a hillside that hasn't flooded in living memory. Took the house. Tomas and two other kids from the bend. They found—" He stopped. Started again. "They didn't find all of him."

The stove ticked. Outside, the ice on the branches caught the light and threw it back in pieces.

"I've worked collections eleven years," Dov went on. "You tell yourself stories so you can sleep. The people we squeeze signed papers. The paper is the truth. Everything else is sentiment." He looked up then, and his eyes were red-rimmed and terribly clear. "There's no story that makes a river take an eleven-year-old because some major wanted a clean wall of cloud. I sat in that valley two days helping dig. Then I came up here."

"To warn us?" Ilse said from the bunk. Her voice was rough with sleep and suspicion in equal measure. "Or to finish the job yourself, since your brothers' advance man froze?"

Dov flinched at the name Kessler the way a man flinches at his own reflection in a broken mirror. "My brothers don't know where I am. My brothers are in Karva drinking Corven's money and calling it investment." He reached slowly into his coat—Ilse's knife came out from under the blanket—and drew out not a weapon but a folded sheet of paper gone soft at the creases, and laid it on the floor between himself and the stove.

"The patrol rotation," he said. "I copied it off the waystation board before Adeyemi locked the passes. Two-man sweeps on the high line, dawn and dusk. They hold the col from midday—that's when they change shifts and get lazy. And there's a blind spot on the north shoulder where the ridge shadow kills their visibility for about ninety minutes after first light. If you're going over, you go at first light, north shoulder, and you pray the shadow holds."

Noor crouched and read it. The handwriting was precise, bureaucratic, the penmanship of a man who had spent his life making other people's debts legible. "Why give us this?"

"Because you're the only ones trying to get the truth over that pass, and I'm done being on the side that buries it." Dov's jaw worked. "And because if I go home and face my sister without having done one true thing, I won't survive the look on her face. Debt I understand. That look, I couldn't pay."

* * *

Ilse made him eat before she made him leave—hard bread and a strip of dried fish, pushed into his hands with the wordless authority of someone who had been hungry long enough to have opinions about it. While Dov chewed, Noor stepped out onto the shelf of stone before the hut and studied the sky.

It was the first time since the storm that he'd let himself really read it, and reading it hurt, the way using a burned hand hurts. But the sky was still there, still legible, still speaking the language he'd been exiled for knowing. High cirrus unspooling from the northwest, thin as pulled wool—moisture riding in above the frozen layer. A lenticular cap sitting on the far peak like a coin placed on a grave, meaning wind up there that could peel a man off the ridge. And underneath it all, a wrongness in the pressure that he felt in his teeth rather than measured: the atmosphere hadn't settled after Adeyemi's storm. It was reloading.

"That doesn't look good," Dov said beside him. Noor hadn't heard him come out. "I grew up around boats. I know enough to be frightened."

"It's not finished," Noor said. "What hit the lowlands—that was the program losing its grip on the edge of the system. The middle's still coiled. Adeyemi built a wall of weather and the wall is cracking, and everyone up here is standing in the crack." He paused. "Including whoever comes up after us."

Dov was quiet a moment. Then: "That's the other thing I came to tell you."

Noor turned.

"Corven's coming up the mountain himself." Dov said it flatly, the way you report a change in tide. "I passed his party two days ago, below the tree line, before the storm closed in. Six men, climbing gear, radios. He's not sending anyone this time. He's coming to collect in person."

"Why?" The question came out before Noor could stop it, and the answer assembled itself in his head even as he asked—Corven's contract, the exclusivity clause, the smooth voice in Karva saying *salvage*, saying *equipment*. Corven had hired Noor because Noor was cheap and desperate and deniable. Now the deniability was gone. Now there was a survivor, a data drive, a dead advance man's loyalty, and a fixer's storm sealing the exits. The line item had grown complications, and Corven was a man who handled complications personally, the way a banker handles a forged signature.

"Because you've become expensive," Noor answered himself. "And he doesn't trust anyone else to carry something this valuable down without keeping it."

"He trusts you least of all," Dov said. "He told his men you were a weatherman who'd sell his own name for fuel. He picked you because ruin makes a man predictable." He wiped his mouth with the back of his hand. "I believed him, once. Ruin does make you predictable. Until it doesn't."

The wind shifted, and with it came the smell of stove fuel again—but fainter now, and from the wrong direction. From below. From the treeline Dov had climbed out of.

Noor went very still, the way he used to go still on the chase truck when the instruments and his gut disagreed. "How far behind you were they?"

Dov's face changed. The clarity in it curdled into something older and more practical—the expression of a collections man realizing the account has come due. "I cut their trail at the river fork. Broke my own track in the water. Bought myself a day, maybe less." He looked at the sky, at the cirrus, at the coin of cloud on the far peak, and Noor watched him arrive at the same arithmetic Noor had already done. "But if they're reading sign half as well as I read yours at the waystation—"

"They're not a day behind," Noor said. "They're hours. The storm slowed them, and the storm ended, and Corven didn't climb this far to stop at the tree line."

Inside the hut, the drive pulsed its amber heartbeat against the dark. Ilse appeared in the doorway, knife held loose at her side, eyes moving from Noor to Dov to the smoke-smell on the wind, reading the whole scene in a glance the way she once must have read instrument arrays.

"How long do we have?" she asked.

Noor looked north, toward the shoulder of the ridge where the morning shadow would fall like a curtain, toward the col beyond it where the pass waited with its ice bridge and its patrols and whatever Adeyemi had decided they deserved. Then he looked at the sky again—at the coiled, reloading weight of it—and felt the old, disgraced, exiled certainty rise in him like water finding its level.

"Less than the weather will give us," he said. "We walk at first light. North shoulder, in the shadow. And we pray the mountain stays deaf a few hours longer—because Corven isn't bringing six men up here to talk."

Below them, deep in the trees, a stove flame flared once against the dusk, small and orange and unmistakable, like a match struck in a dark room where everyone had pretended to be asleep.
