## 14. Corven's Ascent

The mountain did not want him. Corven understood this the way he understood contracts—not as a feeling but as a clause, written in scree and ice and the particular silence of a place that had never needed to lie to anyone. He had spent thirty years among liars, and the honesty of the Vantaa Range unsettled him more than any threat Adeyemi's people had ever made.

He climbed anyway.

Below him, the line of mercenaries moved with the economy of men paid by the day, not the hour. Six of them. Vasik out front with the route map, breathing through his mouth the way heavy men did at altitude. Two more behind him with the radio sled, its runners hissing over a crust of old snow. Corven hung in the middle of the column, where a broker belonged—close enough to the front to see what was coming, far enough back to watch how the men at the rear held up. You could learn everything about a team from its tail. The tail was where doubt lived.

He had dressed for the mountain the way he dressed for everything: well enough to be respected, not well enough to be robbed. A grey shell jacket with the logos stripped off. Boots that had climbed three ranges before this one and looked embarrassed about none of them. The only concession to sentiment was the thin wool scarf his mother had knitted the year before she died, wrapped twice around his throat beneath the collar, where no one could see it and he could pretend he'd forgotten it was there.

*Noor Iversen is a line item,* he told himself, and the telling had the flat taste of a sentence read aloud in a language he no longer spoke fluently.

*Line item seven. Retrieval, confirmatory, no witnesses. Fee: debt extinguishment plus ten percent.*

That was how the contract read. He had written it himself, in the clean administrative language that made terrible things sound like inventory. He had learned that language young, in the back offices of Karva's shipping houses, where men ruined each other over coffee and never raised their voices. The trick—the only trick, really—was to never let the nouns touch the verbs. You didn't kill a man; you *resolved a liability*. You didn't sell a friend; you *monetized an existing relationship*. The mountain, of course, had no such language. The mountain would say what it meant.

* * *

The debt had a name, and the name was not Kessler.

The Keslers were weather to Corven—pressure, a front, something that moved money around the lowlands and broke weaker structures. He respected them the way a farmer respects hail. But the debt that had brought him up this mountain was older and quieter and wore better clothes. It lived in a portfolio managed by a firm called Halvard & Senn, and Halvard & Senn managed portfolios for people who did not appear on paper at all. People whose money smelled faintly of government canteens and whose meetings were scheduled by aides with military haircuts. People who had funded a program called CLEAR SKY and needed, above all things, for that name to remain a rumor.

Corven owed them two hundred and forty thousand. He had owed it for four years. The principal had been a loan against his brokerage—the legitimate one, the one with a brass plaque and a receptionist who watered the ferns—and the interest had been structured in a way he had not fully understood until it was too late to understand anything, which was, he suspected, the entire point of the structure.

They had never threatened him. That was the elegant part. They had simply *recalibrated*. Each year the number grew, and each year the terms of repayment shifted slightly, and each year the work they asked of him in lieu of payment grew a little less deniable. He had started with introductions. Introductions became logistics. Logistics became this: a man in his fifties climbing a mountain in the teeth of an engineered storm to retrieve a data drive and confirm that the eleven people who had generated it were beyond the reach of subpoenas.

*Beyond the reach,* he thought. *That's good. I should use that.*

He took out the little notebook he kept in his breast pocket—the waterproof one, the one no one had ever seen—and wrote it down. *Beyond the reach of subpoenas.* His handwriting had gone small and precise over the years, the script of a man who expected everything he wrote to be read by someone he feared.

* * *

They made the treeline by midafternoon, and Corven called the halt himself, because Vasik would have pushed on and Vasik's judgment ended at his own endurance, which was considerable and irrelevant. A team was not a body; it was a rate. You climbed at the rate of your slowest essential man, and today the slowest essential man was Corven, and he was not ashamed of that. Shame was for people who still believed they would be judged by something other than the outcome.

The forest here was wrong. He had read Iversen's route notes—he had read everything Iversen had ever written, going back six years—and the notes described a wet green world, moss to the waist, water running in every direction like a house with the taps left on. What Corven saw was a forest holding its breath. The moss was grey at the tips. The streams ran thin. The air smelled less like rain than like the memory of rain, the way a hospital smells faintly of the people who have left it.

CLEAR SKY had done this. He understood that now, in the dumb animal way one understands weather. The program pulled the monsoon north and west, wrung it out over the grain provinces, and left the Vantaa watershed drinking from an empty glass. Three failed harvests in the valleys below. He'd seen the numbers. He'd signed off on the cover memos explaining the numbers. *Climatic variability within historical norms.*

The phrase had a rhythm to it, almost musical. He had written it eleven times. He no longer believed it the way he no longer believed in the God his mother had knitted scarves under, but disbelief, Corven had learned, was not an obstacle. It was merely a weight you carried. You could carry a great deal of weight if you packed it properly.

* * *

At the halt, Vasik came back down the line and crouched beside him, and Corven watched the man decide how much to say.

"You knew him," Vasik said. It wasn't a question. Vasik was not clever, but he was observant in the narrow way of soldiers, and Corven had made the mistake, three days ago in the waystation at Karva, of knowing how Iversen took his coffee.

"I hired him," Corven said. "Once."

"For what?"

"For a forecast. A monsoon onset, six years ago. He called it to the day. Called the flood window too, and the flood came, and it drowned forty-one people because the port authority believed my client's meteorologist instead of him." Corven turned his water bottle in his hands. "My client's meteorologist had a better suit."

"And that ruined him."

"That ruined him." Corven looked up the slope, where the light was going the color of old tin. "Do you know what I did after? I wrote a memo. A very good memo, explaining that forecast verification is a probabilistic discipline and that no single event validates or invalidates a model. I distributed it widely. It was, technically, true. And it was the most dishonest thing I have ever written, and I have written for these people for four years."

Vasik said nothing. Vasik was paid to say nothing. It was one of the reasons Corven had hired him, and one of the reasons he sometimes could not stand to be in the same tent with him.

"Why him?" Vasik asked, finally. "For this. Why a ruined man?"

And there it was—the question Corven had been circling for a month, the question he answered differently every time he caught it, and every answer was true, and every true answer made him smaller.

"Because a ruined man is legible," Corven said. "A successful man has options. A successful man, finding a survivor on that mountain, weighs the survivor against his career and might decide the survivor is worth more. A ruined man—" he stopped. Started again, in the clean administrative language, because the other language hurt. "A ruined man weighs the survivor against his debts. The debts are larger. The arithmetic is reliable. Iversen would sell the drive, take the payout, and never once look back, because looking back is a luxury and his credit is exhausted. I chose him the way you choose a bridge. Not because it's beautiful. Because it holds a known load."

"You keep saying *would*," Vasik said.

Corven looked at him.

"Past tense," Vasik said, and got up, and went back down the line to check the radio sled's lashings, which did not need checking.

* * *

That was the trouble with mercenaries. They were illiterate in every way that mattered and then, without warning, they read you like a billboard.

Corven sat with his back against a trunk whose bark had gone soft with some slow rot, and let himself do the thing he never let himself do on the clock, which was to remember.

He remembered Noor Iversen at thirty, before the disgrace, standing in the shipping house in Karva with rain still on his shoulders, arguing a forecast to a room of men who owned the ships the forecast would save. He had been magnificent. That was the word, and Corven did not use it lightly. Not eloquent—eloquence was cheap—but *magnificent*, in the old sense: great-souled. He had cared about the people behind the numbers with a ferocity that made the owners uncomfortable, the way an open flame makes a man in a paper suit uncomfortable. He had been right, and he had been right *loudly*, and Corven had sat in the corner of that room and thought: *this man will die poor or famous, and the odds are on poor.*

Then the flood came, and the port authority needed a scapegoat more than it needed a forecaster, and Corven had been handed the memo assignment, and he had written it, and it had been the best work of his career, and it had taken a magnificent man and folded him down into something small and desperate and legible.

He had told himself, for six years, that he hadn't done it. That the port authority would have found another pen. That the memo merely summarized a consensus. These were true statements in the way that bones are true: structurally, load-bearing, and never spoken of at dinner.

And then, four years ago, Halvard & Senn had called him in—a quiet office, a view of the harbor, a portfolio of obligations—and he had understood, sitting in that chair, that the pen was a career. That there were men who spent their whole lives being the second signature. That the second signature was what the whole apparatus ran on: not monsters, not believers, just men who could make terrible things sound like inventory, and who were paid precisely because they could.

He had signed. The interest had been structured. The ferns at his receptionist's desk needed watering twice a week, and he had noticed, at some point, that he had stopped noticing them.

* * *

They climbed through the evening and made the high camp below the col at last light, and the storm that Adeyemi had built came down off the summits like a debt being called in.

Corven stood at the tent flap and watched it come, and for the first time on the mountain he felt something he would have to admit, in the privacy of the waterproof notebook, was fear. Not of the storm. Of the *specificity* of it. He had read the program's specifications—he had helped redact them—and he knew what an engineered depression looked like on a chart, and this was one. A wall of weather with a purpose behind it, closing the passes, sealing the high country, sweeping the board clean. Somewhere below or above, Major Adeyemi was playing god with a spreadsheet, and Corven was inside the god's spreadsheet, a cell in a column, and the column was labeled, he was almost certain, *liabilities*.

He wrote in the notebook by the blue flame of the stove, while Vasik's men slept in the breathless way of the truly tired.

*Day 3. Weather: engineered. Morale: adequate. Iversen: 2 days ahead, injured per Dov's last report, carrying the drive and the woman. Corven's assessment: he will not sell. Recalculate.*

He looked at the words a long time. Then he wrote the thing underneath, the true thing, the one he had been not-writing for six years.

*Note: I built him to be predictable. It appears I built him well. It appears I built him better than he knows. A ruined man is legible, yes. But I chose the ruin. I wrote the memo that made him legible to me. So the arithmetic that says he will sell—that arithmetic is mine. I invented the debt. I invented the man who would pay it. And if he climbs this mountain instead, if he carries that drive over the pass for nothing, for free, for the truth—then the flaw is not in the man. The flaw is in the model. And I do not know what Halvard & Senn do to men whose models fail.*

He closed the notebook. Outside, the storm found its voice, and the tent walls leaned in like creditors.

Vasik unzipped the flap, letting in a knife of cold air and snow. "Signal from the ridge," he said. "Adeyemi's patrol pulled out at dusk. All of them. Down-valley."

"Down-valley," Corven repeated. "They're not chasing Iversen anymore?"

"No. They're letting him through." Vasik's face, in the stove-light, was the face of a man reporting something he didn't have the vocabulary to distrust. "And there's this. A waystation keeper down at the treeline says a second man came through yesterday. Paid in cash. Kessler money. Heading up the same route." He paused. "Says he looked like a debt collector."

Corven sat very still, and the storm pressed against the fabric, and somewhere above them in the white dark, a man he had ruined was climbing toward a pass with a dead man's data and a living woman's trust, and Dov Kessler's man was behind him, and the pass ahead was open for the first time in days, and Corven understood, with the clarity that comes only at altitude and only at night, that the mountain was not the most honest thing on it.

He reached for his boots.

"We move at first light," he said. "Full speed. If Iversen reaches Solheim before I do, every one of us is a line item in someone else's column."
