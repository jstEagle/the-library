## 15. The Ice Bridge

The storm broke sometime in the night, the way a fever breaks—not with mercy but with exhaustion, the wind finally lying down in the col like a beast that had spent everything it had. Noor crawled out of the snow trench they'd dug against the lee side of a serac and looked up at a sky so clean it felt like an accusation.

Stars. Hundreds of them, hard and white as nailheads driven into black iron. He hadn't seen stars in nine days. The monsoon had owned the heavens since Karva, and now, above six thousand meters, the air had been scoured so thoroughly it hurt to breathe it—thin, glittering, cold enough to make his teeth sing.

Beside him, Ilse stirred in the shared sleeping bag, her bandaged arm tucked against her chest. The graze from the col had stopped weeping, but the skin around it had gone the wrong color, a bruised yellow spreading toward her elbow that Noor didn't like and couldn't do anything about. She'd stopped complaining about the cold two days ago. That frightened him more than the wound.

"Light," she said, without opening her eyes. Her voice came out sanded flat. "Is it light?"

"Almost." He watched the east, where the great shoulder of the Vantaa massif was turning from black to a deep, bruised indigo. Below them the glacier fell away in frozen cascades, a river of ice poured down the mountain's throat and stopped mid-pour. "We should have moved an hour ago."

"Then move me." She sat up with the economy of motion that still amazed him—the survey team had starved her and frozen her and shot her, and she still rose from the snow like a blade coming out of a drawer.

He handed her the last of the fuel-heated water, half a cup each, and they ate the final ration bar split down to the wrapper's foil. Noor chewed slowly, doing the arithmetic he'd been avoiding. One day of food. The pass was, at Dov's estimate, four hours' hard travel—if the route Dov had described still existed. If the ice bridge hadn't gone the way of everything else Adeyemi's storm had touched.

The drive sat against Ilse's chest in its wrap of insulation, and every few hours she pressed two fingers to its casing the way a mother checks an infant's temperature. The battery indicator was a single green diode that had turned amber yesterday and now pulsed like something breathing. Dying, but not dead. The data had an expiration date, and they were racing it across the roof of the world.

Noor took out the compass.

He did it without thinking, the way he'd done it every morning since he was nine years old—his father's brass compass, the case worn to a soft gold shine by forty years of a forecaster's hands, the needle trembling inside its glass window as if it were alive and cold and glad to see him. It was the only thing he'd carried out of his old life intact. Not his reputation, not his career, not the name that had once opened doors in three countries. Just this. His father had pressed it into his palm at the trailhead outside Karva, twenty-two years ago, and said: *A man can lose everything else and still know which way is north. Remember that.*

He'd thought about it a great deal in the last nine days. Which way was north. Which way was true. Whether the two had ever been the same direction at all.

The needle swung, settled, pointed toward the pass. Toward Solheim, and the radio mast, and whatever was waiting there—Corven, the Keslers, the end of the road. Noor closed the lid with a click that sounded enormous in the silence.

"Show me this bridge," he said.

* * *

They reached it an hour after first light, and Noor stopped so abruptly that Ilse walked into his back.

Below their boots the glacier made a right-angle turn around a granite spur, and where the ice bent, it had torn. The crevasse was not a crack but a chasm—a blue-black gash forty feet across, deep enough that their headlamps, when Noor swept his down into it, found no bottom. Only walls. Walls of ice compressed into a color Noor had no name for, a blue so pure it seemed to generate its own light, layered like the pages of a book a hundred thousand years thick.

And across the chasm, spanning it like a fallen sentence, was the bridge.

It had been a snow bridge once—winter's accumulation drifted across the gap and welded into a single arched slab. Adeyemi's engineered storm had loaded it with meters of new snow, then the warm pulse behind the front had rotted it from beneath, and now what remained was a spine of ice with the architecture eaten out of it. Noor could see through it in places. He could see the chasm *through* the thing he was supposed to walk on.

"That's not a bridge," Ilse said. "That's a rumor of a bridge."

"Dov said it held for the patrol." Noor knelt at the lip, probed the near edge with his axe. The ice rang solid there, dense and old. But eight feet out, where the arch began its span, the surface was different—dulled, sugared, the texture of compacted snow that had been through too many melt-freeze cycles. "For the patrol, at their weight, in their boots, three days ago. Before the last thaw."

"Before the last thaw," she repeated. "Which was when?"

"Yesterday afternoon. While we were digging the trench."

She was quiet a moment. Wind moved along the crevasse, a low moan through the gap like breath across a bottle's mouth. Somewhere far below, deep in the glacier's plumbing, ice settled with a sound like a door closing in an empty house.

"Ilse." He turned to look at her. Her face was wind-scoured gray, lips cracked, the bandage on her arm spotted with old blood, and her eyes were the clearest thing on this whole mountain. "I weigh thirty kilos more than you. Maybe more, with the gear. The bridge held for a patrol man. It will not hold the same for me."

"I know." She said it simply, without drama, and that simplicity was worse than any argument. "I've been doing the same arithmetic since Dov told us the route."

"So we don't both cross."

"We both cross." She crouched beside him at the lip, close enough that he could hear the rattle of her breath in the cold. "Because the drive goes with me, and I am not giving you the drive, and you are not letting me cross alone while you find another route down a mountain that has already tried to kill us twice. So we both cross. One at a time. Roped."

He looked at the rope coiled at his hip. Thirty meters of it, half their length already spent on the descent from the col. Enough to tie them together across forty feet of nothing.

"If it goes under you," he said, "I hold. I anchor and I hold, and I pull you out, and then we find another way for me. Agreed?"

"And if it goes under you—"

"It won't."

"Noor." She said his name the way she'd said it in the tent the first night, flat and precise, a woman who had spent eleven days alone on a mountain learning that euphemism was a luxury that got people killed. "If it goes under you, I weigh half what you weigh. The rope comes taut, and my hundred and ten pounds is not going to arrest your fall. You know this. I know this. So we do it properly. You cross last, unroped, and I anchor from the far side with everything we have."

"Unroped." He tasted the word. "Over that."

"You crossed the Karva flood channel in spate to pull a boy out. Dov told me. Everyone in the lowlands knows the story." Her eyes didn't soften, but something in her voice did, a degree, a fraction. "You know how to read moving water. This is moving water that forgot how to move. Read it."

* * *

He read it.

That was the truth of what he did for the next hour, kneeling at the crevasse lip while the sun climbed and the light moved across the bridge's surface, changing it, telling on it. It was the old knowledge, the exiled knowledge—the thing he'd been born with and trained and then crucified for, the storm-sense that had told him the Karva fleet was sailing into a wall of water while every official model said otherwise. He'd spent nine days on this mountain using it to stay alive. Now he used it the way a doctor uses a stethoscope: laid flat against the ice, listening.

The bridge had a sound. Everything under load has a sound, and ice under load sings if you know how to listen. He pressed his palm flat to the surface at the near anchor and had Ilse walk out onto the first third of the span, slowly, and he listened with his hand and with his boots and with some older organ he had no name for.

Under her weight the bridge spoke. A low tone, deep and round, the sound of a structure that was carrying what it was built to carry. When she reached the middle—where the arch was thinnest, where he could see daylight through the rotten lattice beneath her boots—the tone rose. Sharpened. A wire being tuned toward its breaking note.

But it held. She crossed the middle, and the tone came back down, and she stepped onto the far lip with her boots on old dense ice, and she turned and looked at him across forty feet of murdered glacier with the drive strapped to her chest, and she nodded once.

"Your turn," she called. "Slow. Flat feet. And Noor—" the wind took her words, and she had to raise her voice, and it cracked across the gap like a whip, "—*do not stop in the middle. Whatever you hear, do not stop.*"

He anchored the rope for her sake, though they both knew what it was worth, and he left his pack—stripped now to almost nothing—on the near side to be hauled across by rope afterward. He kept the axe. He kept the compass in his chest pocket, because he had kept it there every day for twenty-two years, and because a man does not think clearly about which of his own superstitions are load-bearing until the moment he must.

He stepped out onto the bridge.

The first eight feet were honest ice. It took his weight with a solid, granular crunch, and the tone beneath was deep and certain, and he allowed himself one breath of something almost like relief.

Then the span began.

The surface changed under his boots from ice to the sugared rot he'd seen from the lip, and the tone rose to meet him, and it was higher than it had been under Ilse. Higher by an interval he could feel in his back teeth. He kept moving. Flat feet, slow, weight spread, the way he'd once walked out onto river ice as a boy behind his father, who had taught him that fear travels through the body faster than sound and that the trick is to be slower than your fear.

Halfway.

The middle of the span was a cathedral of ruin. Beneath his boots he could see the lattice the melt had carved, arches within arches, a honeycomb of blue going down and down into the dark, and through the gaps he could see the far wall of the crevasse and feel the enormous patient depth of the place, the way you feel depth in your inner ear, in the roots of your hair.

The tone under his feet climbed.

And stopped.

Not faded. *Stopped.* The way a clock stops. The way a heart stops. The silence that followed was the worst sound he had ever heard, because he knew what it meant—it meant the ice had gone past singing, past complaint, past the whole vocabulary of warning, into that final structural quiet that comes half a second before everything lets go.

He didn't stop. He couldn't make his legs obey, so he *threw* them—one lurching stride, another, the bridge flexing under him now with a motion that was no longer tone but torque, the whole arch beginning to fold—

The world cracked.

It came up through his boots and into his spine, a sound like the mountain clearing its throat, and the surface tilted, and the far third of the span simply *left*—a whole slab of it shearing away in silence and dropping into the blue, and Noor was sliding, sliding down the tilt of the collapsing arch with the chasm opening beneath him like a mouth—

His axe was in his hand. He didn't remember drawing it. He swung it down and the pick bit the last sound ice at the lip of the span and held, his full weight on the shaft, his boots swinging over forty feet of nothing, his breath coming out of him in a white shout.

And then the lip began to break too.

He felt it through the shaft—a fine trembling, a spiderweb of failures racing through the ice around the axe head. The anchor was going. In a second, maybe two, the pick would tear free and he would go down into the blue book of the glacier and the story would end there, unwritten, and the drive would cross with Ilse, and that would have to be enough.

His other hand was at his chest. He didn't decide this either. His father's compass was in his chest pocket, and his hand had gone to it the way a drowning man's hand goes to anything, and through the brass case he felt the ice's trembling, and the compass—*his father's compass, the last clean thing, the thing that knew which way was north*—was directly over the only sound ice left, the deep old ice of the true lip, four inches beyond the reach of his axe.

Four inches.

The axe tore loose.

He was falling, and in the falling there was time—there is always more time than you think—one full breath in which he saw the glass window and the trembling needle and his father's hands at a trailhead twenty-two years ago saying *remember that*, and he understood, with a clarity that felt like the cold itself, what the compass had become. Not a gift. A weight. An anchor dropped not into ice but into the past, into the disgrace and the debt and the man he'd been before the mountain, and he had been carrying it across every bridge of his life, and this bridge was asking him, at last, the only question that mattered:

What are you willing to put down to keep going?

He drove the axe through the compass case and into the ice.

The brass crumpled like paper around the pick. The needle's housing shattered. He felt it die through the shaft—a small, precise destruction, forty years of his father's hands ending in half a second—and then the pick was in old ice, deep ice, honest ice, and his fall stopped with a jolt that nearly tore his shoulder from its socket, and Noor Iversen hung over the blue chasm of the Vantaa glacier, screaming, alive.

* * *

It took him eleven minutes to climb out. Ilse lay flat at the lip with her arm hooked through the rope's anchor, talking to him the whole way in a low, steady voice—not encouragement, he realized later, but *coordinates*. Left boot one span higher. Crack at two o'clock. She was navigating him out of the ice the way he'd navigated her through the flood, and neither of them said so.

He came over the lip and lay on the far side of the crevasse with his face in the snow, and his whole body shook, and after a while Ilse knelt beside him and turned him over with careful hands.

The compass case was ruined. The brass had folded around the axe pick like a crushed flower, the glass window burst, the needle bent double against its card. Noor held it in his frostbitten hands and turned it over and over, and there was nothing left in it that could point anywhere, ever again.

"I'm sorry," Ilse said quietly.

"Don't be." His voice came out wrecked. He cleared his throat and tried again. "He told me a man can lose everything else and still know which way is north." He closed his ruined fist around the empty case. "I spent twenty years thinking he meant the instrument."

He stood. He left the broken compass where it lay, on the far side of the bridge that no longer existed, in the country of the past. Then he shouldered the rope, and they hauled his pack across, and together they turned toward the pass.

The col was visible now, two hours' climb above them—a notch in the great white shoulder of the range, sharp against a sky that had begun, very quietly, to fill with cloud again from the south.

Noor watched the cloud build, and read it, and his stomach dropped for a second time that morning.

"That's not weather," he said. "That's a wall. Someone's building a wall between us and Solheim."

Ilse followed his gaze, and her hand went to the drive against her chest, and its amber light pulsed once, like a heartbeat skipping.

"Then we climb faster," she said.
