## 16. Ambush at the Col

*Ilse*

The col announced itself by its silence.

For eleven days on the mountain Ilse had learned the Vantaa's vocabulary—the groan of settling snowpack, the tick of rime building on stone, the wind's many dialects as it came over the ridgelines from the north. But the col was a place where all of it stopped. The wind that had screamed at their backs for six hours simply ended, cut off like a door closing, and they stepped through into a bowl of white so still she could hear her own pulse in her ears.

Noor stopped two paces ahead of her, a shape more than a man, crusted head to foot in ice. He raised one hand—wait—and stood listening with his whole body, the way he did when he was reading weather no one else could feel.

"We're not alone," he said.

She had known it too. Not because of anything she heard, but because of what she didn't: Dov had been behind them since dawn, a dark smudge in the whiteout, keeping a distance that was neither pursuit nor company. Now the smudge was gone.

"Dov?" she called softly.

Nothing. The wind chose that moment to return, sliding down from the northern ridge in a long exhale, and beneath it—faint, rhythmic, wrong—came the crunch of boots. More than one set. Coming down the slope ahead of them, not behind.

They had been herded. The whole day's climb, the route Dov had given them, the rotation gaps he'd promised—it had funneled them here, into this bowl between the peaks, where the walls rose steep on three sides and there was exactly one way forward and none back.

"Noor." Her voice came out flat and calm, the voice she'd used in the survey camp when the instruments first showed the impossible, when panic would have cost her everything. "How many?"

He tilted his head. She watched him count footsteps the way other men counted money.

"Four," he said. "Maybe five. Spread out. They're walking like they already know where we are."

The drive hung against her chest inside her jacket, strapped there with cord, warm from her body heat. Its battery indicator had blinked red for two days now. Hours, Noor had said. Not days. And here, at the top of the world, with the pass a hundred meters beyond the bowl's far lip, someone had finally come to collect them.

The whiteout thickened. Visibility dropped to twenty meters, then fifteen. The figures resolved out of it slowly, like photographs developing—dark parkas, goggles, rifles carried low and ready. Four of them, fanning into an arc. And behind them, smaller, moving with none of their economy, a fifth figure in a city coat that had no business above the snowline.

Corven.

Even at this distance she recognized the way he held himself, hands loose at his sides, head cocked, as if he'd wandered onto the mountain by accident and was mildly inconvenienced to find it occupied. Eleven days ago she might have believed it. Eleven days ago she hadn't read the contract hidden in his pack's false bottom, or seen the second set of initials beside Noor's name on the payment schedule.

"Mr. Iversen!" Corven's voice carried strangely in the dead air of the col, bright and reasonable. "There you are. You've made this very difficult, you know."

* * *

*Noor*

Corven looked exactly as he had in Karva—pressed, pleasant, faintly amused—as though the mountain were a hotel lobby and the mercenaries merely staff. That was the thing about the man Noor had never let himself see clearly until now: he didn't perform menace because he didn't need to. Menace was outsourced.

"You signed a salvage contract," Corven called across the snow. "I paid you to recover equipment. Instead you've destroyed equipment, assaulted my associates' interests, and—" he gestured vaguely at Ilse, at the drive's outline under her jacket, "—taken inventory that doesn't belong to you."

"The survey team belonged to nobody," Ilse said. "You saw to that."

"I saw to nothing. I'm a broker, Miss Brandt. I move goods. What happened to your colleagues is—" he sighed, and the sigh was almost convincing, "—regrettable. Adeyemi's people are thorough. I only learned the scope of it after I'd committed funds." He took a step closer. One of the mercenaries shifted to keep him covered. "But here's what I can't commit: my own solvency. There are men behind this program who've advanced me a great deal against delivery. If the data reaches Solheim, I don't lose a commission. I lose everything. So you understand—I have no choice. None of us ever does. That's rather the point of the whole arrangement."

Noor found his voice came out steadier than he felt. "The Keslers. You sold my route to them. My debt. You used it as a leash."

"I used what was available. Your ruin made you predictable, Noor—that's why I chose you. A desperate man climbs fast and asks few questions." Corven smiled, and for one unguarded instant something moved behind the smile, something tired and old. "I did hope you wouldn't make me do this."

"And Dov?" Noor scanned the whiteout. The advance man was nowhere. "Was he part of the arrangement too?"

Something flickered across Corven's face—the first honest expression Noor had ever seen him wear. It lasted less than a second.

"Dov is being handled," Corven said. "Gentlemen."

The mercenaries raised their rifles.

And the mountain answered.

It came without warning—a crack like the sky splitting, then a deep rolling boom from the northern wall, and the whole slope above the col began to move. Adeyemi's engineered storm had loaded those slopes for days; everyone on this mountain had been climbing under a debt of snow, and the rifle reports, sharp as they were, were simply the coin that paid it.

"DOWN!" Noor grabbed Ilse's arm and hauled her toward the eastern wall as the white world tore itself apart.

* * *

*Ilse*

The avalanche missed them by the width of a prayer. It took the western side of the col in a single swallowing motion, a white tongue fifty meters wide, and where two of the mercenaries had stood there was suddenly only moving snow and, briefly, one black glove rising and closing and gone.

The sound went on forever. Then it stopped, and the silence afterward was worse—total, pressurized, ringing.

Ilse lay against the rock wall with Noor's weight half across her, both of them plastered in powder. Somewhere to her left a man was screaming, high and continuous, the third mercenary, buried to the chest. The fourth had been on the far arc; she lost him in the churned fog.

Then a rifle spoke from below the col—from the route they'd climbed, from behind them—and the screaming stopped.

Dov rose out of the whiteout like something exhumed. His face was a mask of frost and blood, one goggle shattered, and he was carrying his rifle the way Noor carried the compass case: like the last true thing he owned.

"Corven!" His voice was ruined, gravel and grief. "My nephew died in Halvard when your weather broke the river. You knew. You sat in that waystation and you knew what the program was doing, and you sold us all anyway."

"Dov." Corven had backed against the rocks, hands up, and for the first time his voice cracked its polish. "Dov, think about what you're—"

"I thought about nothing else for nine days."

The shot went wide—whether from Dov's shaking or the wind, Ilse never knew—and then the fourth mercenary came out of the fog at a sprint, tackling Dov into the snow, and the col dissolved into chaos: bodies, muzzle flash blooming orange through the white, Corven scrambling along the rock line toward the pass with his city coat flapping like a broken wing.

Noor moved. Later, Ilse could never reconstruct how—he was simply *there*, crossing the churned snow in four strides, driving his shoulder into the mercenary's back. All three men went down. A rifle discharged into the air. Dov got an elbow free and swung it with a lifetime of dockside fury behind it, and the mercenary rolled away clutching his face, scrambled up, and fled downslope into the whiteout, alive and wanting nothing more to do with any of them.

Which left Dov kneeling in the snow, and blood spreading beneath him, black on white, far too much of it.

* * *

*Noor*

He reached Dov in three steps and caught him as he tipped sideways. The bullet had gone in under the ribs, low, ugly. Dov's breath came in short wet pulls, and his eyes—frost-rimmed, furious, frightened—found Noor's and held them with terrible clarity.

"Rotation," Dov rasped. "Told you the truth. Wasn't bait. Corven followed you himself—used my rotation to time it. I led you straight into—" A cough, red-flecked. "Damn fool. Both of us."

"Don't talk."

"Listen." His hand closed on Noor's wrist with shocking strength. "Corven's got a radio beacon. Calls the patrol. He'll light it up at the pass unless—" another cough, longer, "—unless the storm takes the signal. Watch the sky. When it goes green-black before the strike, that's your window. Everything metal sings first. You know that. Storm man. You know that."

Noor knew it. Had known it since he was nine years old, standing on a seawall with his father while the air turned the color of bruised fruit and every hair on his arms stood up. The knowledge that had ruined him. The knowledge that had gotten him here.

"I know," he said.

Dov's grip loosened. His gaze drifted past Noor's shoulder, up, toward the pass, and something in his face eased—not peace exactly, but the end of a long argument.

"Nephew's name was Tam," he said. "Remember it. Somebody should."

Then his hand went slack in the snow, and the col took him the way it took everything—silently, completely, without malice.

* * *

*Ilse*

She knelt beside Noor in the settling powder and pressed both palms hard against the wound, though she knew, and he knew, and Dov had known, that pressure was a formality now. The cold had already begun its work. Above them the whiteout was thinning, tearing into ragged streamers, and through the gaps she could see the pass—two hundred meters of rising snowfield, wind-scoured, empty.

Corven was gone over it. She'd seen him go, small and quick, a dark stitch in the white seam of the skyline. Running, not fighting. Corven always ran toward the exit.

"He'll trigger the beacon," she said. "Bring Adeyemi's whole patrol down on us."

"Not if the storm breaks first." Noor was looking at the sky with an expression she'd only seen once before, in the flooded gorge—total attention, total surrender to something larger than himself. "Dov said watch for the green-black. If a front's coming off the northern ice, we might have minutes. Maybe less."

"Or maybe hours," she said, "and Corven calls the patrol, and we die on this col next to a man who died for us."

Noor didn't answer. He was doing the arithmetic she refused to do: the drive's dying battery, the hours to Solheim, the wound in her arm she hadn't yet told him about.

Because there was a wound. She'd taken it during the scramble—she wasn't even sure when, a blow of heat and force along the outside of her left arm as she'd thrown herself behind the rocks, and only now, in the strange calm after the guns, did the pain arrive, bright and insistent. She peeled back her sleeve. A furrow ran from elbow to shoulder, shallow, seeping, the skin burned gray at its edges. A graze. A bullet had touched her and decided, for whatever reason, not to stay.

"Hurt?" Noor asked, without looking.

"A little."

"Liar."

"Yes," she admitted. "A lot. But I'll walk."

He tore a strip from the hem of his frozen shirt—she heard the fabric crack like paper—and bound the arm with hands that shook from frostbite and something else. Grief, maybe. She was learning to read him the way she'd learned to read the mountain: not by what he said but by what he couldn't.

"He gave us the rotation," Noor said quietly. "Real numbers. Real risk. And Corven used it to set the trap, and Dov knew it, and he came up that slope anyway knowing what it would cost." He tied off the bandage. "Why? He didn't owe us anything. We were just the job."

Ilse looked north, where the storm was gathering itself behind the peaks—a bruise-colored wall rising over the ice fields, silent for now, patient. Below them Dov lay under a thin shroud of blowing snow, and somewhere under that shroud was a man who had spent nine days thinking about a river and a boy named Tam.

"Because somebody has to remember," she said. "That's all Maret asked. That's all any of this is. People deciding what gets remembered."

Noor pulled the compass case from his jacket—the empty case, the brass shell with the axe-gouge through its heart—and closed Dov's fingers around it. It fit there as if it had always meant to.

"For Tam," Noor said. "Somebody will."

* * *

They climbed.

The pass rose before them in the clearing air, and behind it the sky was doing the thing Dov had described: darkening from the north in a color that had no name, green shading into black, the light going strange and metallic. Every rock on the ridgeline seemed to hum. The fine hairs on Ilse's good arm lifted. The drive against her chest felt suddenly loud, as if it too could feel the charge building in the air.

Two hundred meters. Then one hundred. The wind returned in gusts that tasted of ozone. At the crest of the pass, just visible against the boiling sky, she saw it—a tripod, a small antenna, a figure crouched over it with his back to the wind.

Corven, setting up his beacon, calling down the patrol, selling them one final time.

"Fifty meters," Noor breathed beside her. "Ilse. Look at the sky. Look at the *color*."

She looked. Green-black, bruise-deep, the whole northern heaven charged like a capacitor. And every hair on her body standing, and the drive humming faintly against her sternum, and Noor's eyes lit with the terrible gift that had cost him everything and was about to be worth all of it.

"How long?" she asked.

Noor smiled, and it was not a kind smile.

"Exactly long enough," he said, "for one broadcast."
