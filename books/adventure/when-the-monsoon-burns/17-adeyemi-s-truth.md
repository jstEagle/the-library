## 17. Adeyemi's Truth

The storm had a seam in it. Adeyemi had known it would, because he had asked for one.

He stood at the col with his hands in his coat pockets and the wind pulling at him like a debt collector, watching the two figures come up out of the whiteout the way water comes up through a crack—slowly, then all at once. The man first, broad-shouldered, moving with that peculiar economy of someone who has stopped trusting his own legs and started trusting the air instead. Then the woman, half-carried, her weight slung across his back, her blood already freezing to rust on his shoulder where the graze had soaked through.

Adeyemi had ordered his patrol to hold at the lower ridge. He had told them the col was unstable, which was true, and that he would handle the intercept personally, which was also true, though not in the way they understood it. He had come up alone because some conversations could not be witnessed. Not by soldiers. Not by satellites. Perhaps not even by God, though Adeyemi had stopped believing God was watching this valley sometime around the third failed harvest.

The wind here sang through the rocks in a register he had never heard anywhere else—a thin, keening note, like a wire under too much tension. For eleven years he had worked in rooms full of instruments that measured the atmosphere's every breath, and still nothing had taught him as much about pressure as this pass did: the way it made a man feel held together only temporarily.

They saw him at forty meters. The man—Iversen—stopped so completely that the snow seemed to close around him like a fist. Adeyemi watched him shift Ilse Brandt's weight, watched his free hand find the ice axe at his hip, and understood exactly what he was looking at: a man doing arithmetic with his own life. One against one, but the woman was wounded and the man was frostbitten and neither of them had eaten properly in days. Adeyemi had read their file. All of it. He knew what Iversen had lost in Karva, knew about the Keslers, knew about Corven. Knowing a man's debts was a kind of intimacy; Adeyemi had never been comfortable with how good he'd gotten at it.

"Major," Iversen said. His voice was wrecked—wind-scoured, half gone—but steady underneath, the way a house is steady while it burns.

"You know who I am."

"I know what you are." A pause. "I don't know yet if those are different things."

Adeyemi almost smiled. Almost. "That's the most honest thing anyone's said to me on this mountain."

He walked forward—not slowly, because slowness would read as threat, but evenly, deliberately, the way you approach a spooked animal or an unexploded shell. He stopped ten paces away, close enough to see the frost in Iversen's beard, the raw skin of his hands where the gloves had worn through, far enough that the ice axe would have to travel before it mattered.

"I'm going to take something out of my coat," he said. "Slowly. It's paper, not a weapon. Though I suppose paper has been a weapon longer than anything else we've made."

Iversen said nothing. The wind filled the silence with its wire-song.

Adeyemi drew out the folder—waterproofed, government-issue grey—and opened it against his thigh so the pages wouldn't tear away into the sky. He held it up so Iversen could see the top sheet: a map of three provinces, overlaid with rainfall projections, the numbers printed in a red so dark it was nearly brown.

"Do you know what CLEAR SKY was built to prevent?" he asked.

"I know what it did." Iversen's eyes were on the folder, then on Adeyemi's face, then back. "Three harvests. Maret's valley. The river that runs backward. I've walked through what it did."

"Yes." Adeyemi turned a page. "And do you know what it was *for*?"

* * *

He had rehearsed this conversation for six hundred kilometers of marching, and every rehearsal had ended differently. That was the thing about truth, he had learned late: it wasn't a fixed point you could navigate toward. It drifted. It had weather of its own.

"In 2041," he said, "the southern provinces lost everything. You remember the famine year?"

"I was nineteen," Iversen said. "I remember."

"Nineteen. Good. Then you remember the roads. You remember what people looked like by the end of it." Adeyemi kept his voice level, the way he'd been trained to deliver casualty figures. "Forty-one thousand dead in eight months. Not from war. From rain that didn't come. I was there. I carried bodies. And when the program was proposed—when the scientists came to us with models showing we could steer the monsoon, buy back the rain, push the moisture where it was needed—I signed the authorization in eleven days. Eleven days. People called it reckless. I called it the fastest moral arithmetic I've ever done. Forty-one thousand dead against a few degrees of altered weather. There was no version of me that hesitated."

Ilse Brandt stirred against Iversen's shoulder. Her face was grey with cold and blood loss, but her eyes were clear, and they were fixed on Adeyemi with an expression he recognized because he had seen it in mirrors during the worst months of the program: the look of someone measuring whether a man can be reached.

"The survey team," she said. Her voice was thin. "Dr. Halvorsen's team. They found the same thing your own instruments must have found. That the steering wasn't holding. That the corrections were compounding."

"They found it," Adeyemi agreed. "Eleven months ago. Their data showed the model diverging—feedback loops in the upper convection, energy accumulating where the program couldn't bleed it off. The monsoon wasn't being managed anymore. It was being *wound*. Like a spring." He closed the folder partway. "I know because I commissioned their survey. I chose their route. I picked the ridge where they camped."

Iversen went very still. Even the wind seemed to lean in.

"And then someone cut pages out of their logbook," Brandt said.

"No." Adeyemi shook his head once, a small precise motion. "I didn't order that either. I ordered the team extracted. Safely. Quietly, because the program's existence was classified, but *safely*—that word was in my orders twice, underlined. What happened on that ridge was done by people further up than me, using channels I wasn't given access to, and by the time I understood what had been done, the passes were closed and the story was 'vanished in a storm.'" He looked out past them, down the long white fall of the mountain toward the valleys where the snow was just beginning to mean something. "I have spent eleven months telling myself they died of the storm. That the extraction went wrong. That no one decided anything. Because the alternative was to admit that I built the machine whose gears ground them up, and that I didn't hear them go."

* * *

The wind shifted. Adeyemi felt it change against his left cheek before any instrument would have registered it—the old bodily knowledge, the kind soldiers and sailors and storm chasers share, the knowledge that lives below language. Pressure falling from the northwest. The engineered system he had sealed the valley with was beginning to eat itself, exactly as the models had predicted it would, exactly as Halvorsen's data had warned.

He took out the second document. This one was a single page, printed that morning at the forward station, and his hand was not quite steady as he held it up.

"This is yesterday's model run," he said. "My model. The one CLEAR SKY runs on. Look at the divergence curve."

Iversen leaned forward despite himself. Adeyemi saw the moment the man's eyes caught the shape of the line—that upward sweep, gentle at first, then climbing like smoke.

"That's not noise," Iversen said quietly. It wasn't a question. He was reading the graph the way Adeyemi himself read terrain: instinctively, bodily. "That's a system leaving its basin. Once it does that—" He stopped. Swallowed. "Once it does that, you can't steer it back. You can't steer it at all. Every correction you make adds energy. You're not flying the plane anymore. You're adding weight to a plane that's already falling."

"Yes," Adeyemi said. "That is precisely what it is. And I have known for—" He calculated, and hated the number. "For seventy-three days. Seventy-three days I've filed reports saying the divergence was within tolerance. Seventy-three days of buying the program time it doesn't deserve, because I kept thinking: one more season. One more correction cycle. We can damp it. We built this thing, surely we can unbuild it." He laughed, and there was no humor in it, only the sound a man makes when a structure inside him gives way. "But you can't unbuild weather, Mr. Iversen. That was always the lie at the center of it. We told ourselves we were gardening the sky. We were gambling with it. And now the spring is wound past breaking, and when it goes—and it will go within two years, maybe three—it won't be forty-one thousand dead. It will be an order of magnitude worse, across four countries, and no one will ever be able to say the sky did it, because *we* did."

Brandt had pushed herself upright off Iversen's back. She stood swaying, one hand fisted in his sleeve for balance, and looked at Adeyemi the way you look at a man standing on a ledge.

"So report it," she said. "You're the authorizing officer. Report the failure."

"To whom?" Adeyemi spread his hands, the folder flapping in the wind. "The oversight committee that approved the program? The ministers whose signatures are on the classification order? The backers who funded it through three shell foundations—the same money, Ms. Brandt, that flows through men like Corven?" He shook his head. "I have written the report. Four times. It sits in a safe at the forward station because every chain of command it would travel up is owned by the thing it condemns. A report like mine doesn't get read. It gets *buried*, and then the man who wrote it gets buried with it, politely, in a posting to nowhere, and the program runs another season, and the spring winds tighter."

"Then why are you here?" Iversen asked. "Why let us pass? Why show us any of this?"

"Because a buried report is a dead letter," Adeyemi said, "but data in the open air is weather. It moves. It can't be recalled." He nodded at the drive, somewhere in Iversen's ruined pack. "Halvorsen's readings. Your testimony. My model runs—all of it, together, corroborated from three directions. No committee can bury that. No minister can classify a fact that ten thousand people have already heard." He met Iversen's eyes. "I spent eleven years believing I was preventing a famine. I was right to believe it—that's the trap, Mr. Iversen. Conviction feels exactly like virtue from the inside. But belief without correction isn't service. It's just a longer way of being wrong. And I am done being wrong in private."

* * *

He stepped aside then. Actually stepped aside, boots grinding on the frozen scree, opening the path down the eastern face toward Solheim, toward the town and its single radio mast and whatever waited there.

"The patrol rotation changes at 0400," he said, brisk now, all business, because feeling was a luxury the altitude didn't permit. "After that, I can't hold them off the col, and I won't try again—my officers will start asking questions I can't answer. You have perhaps five hours of darkness. The eastern face is loaded with wind-slab; stay on the rock ribs where you can, and when the slope steepens past the seracs, descend in the lee. Your instincts will serve you better than any route card." He paused. "Your instincts. I've read your file, Mr. Iversen. The Karva forecast. The one that was right, and ruined you."

Iversen's jaw tightened. "What about it?"

"It was right," Adeyemi said, "because you read the sky the way the old pilots did—by body, by smell, by thirty years of inherited attention. That's the one instrument CLEAR SKY never accounted for. Our models can't see what a man feels in his teeth." Something moved behind his eyes, brief and unreadable. "It seems fitting that it should be the thing that ends us."

He turned to go, then stopped, and took one last thing from his coat: a slim black case, which he held out.

"My authentication codes," he said. "Officer-level. If the mast at Solheim is locked down—and it will be—these will open the transmission queue. Use my name. Let them trace it to me." He almost smiled, and this time it got all the way there, tired and crooked and human. "If I'm going to confess, I'd rather do it loudly."

Iversen took the case slowly, as if it might detonate. "Why?" he asked. "After everything. Why any of this?"

Adeyemi looked past him, east, where the clouds over the lowlands were organizing themselves into the shape of the coming snow—the first true snowfall, natural and unmodified, falling on a world that had forgotten what unmodified meant.

"Because I carried bodies in the famine year," he said. "And I swore, carrying them, that I would never again stand by while a preventable death was arranged by paperwork. I broke that oath for eleven years, one signature at a time. I don't intend to break it a twelfth time." He pulled his collar up against the wind. "Go. Five hours. And Mr. Iversen—if you reach Solheim, and the mast is guarded, and it comes down to a choice between the broadcast and your life—"

"Don't say it," Iversen said.

"I was going to say," Adeyemi continued, mild as morning, "choose the broadcast. Dead men tell no tales, but dead men's data travels at the speed of light."

Then he turned into the wind and went down the western side of the col alone, toward the forward station, toward the safe with four reports in it, toward the phone call that would end his career and possibly his liberty and might, if the arithmetic finally came out honest, save forty-one thousand people who hadn't died yet.

Behind him, the seam in the storm began to close.

Ahead of them, five hours of darkness, and a town with one mast, and a broker named Corven who had already arrived.
