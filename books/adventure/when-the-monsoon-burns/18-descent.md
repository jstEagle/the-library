## 18. Descent

The pass let them go the way a fist lets go of something it has been crushing.

Noor felt it in his knees first—the change in pressure, the way the wind that had been trying to peel him off the mountain suddenly lost its argument. Behind them, Adeyemi stood small and dark against the white, a man who had chosen to become a witness instead of a wall. Noor did not look back twice. Looking back was for people who had time.

He had Ilse on his back, her arms looped over his collarbones, her weight distributed the way Maret had taught him to distribute a pack: high, close, breathing with him. Her left thigh was wrapped in two of his shirts and a strip of tent fabric, the graze beneath it angry but clean. She had stopped apologizing somewhere above the ice bridge. That was how he knew she was still herself.

"How far?" she said against his ear. Her voice was scraped thin by three days of storm.

"Down," he said. "That's the only honest answer."

The drive rode against his sternum in its padded sleeve, strapped where his father's compass used to sit. He could feel it there like a second heartbeat, faint and failing. Ilse had checked the battery at the col with numb fingers and read him the number without inflection, the way you'd read a depth sounding under a sinking boat. Hours. Maybe a day if he kept it warm. The data inside it—eleven months of atmospheric readings, the whole rotten architecture of CLEAR SKY laid out in numbers—was dissolving even as they climbed down through the clouds that had been built to kill them.

So they went down.

* * *

The slope below the pass was the kind of terrain that killed experienced climbers on good days. It was neither snow nor rock but an argument between them—meltwater running under a skin of refrozen crust, the whole face rotting from the inside out because Adeyemi's engineered storm had dumped heat and cold into the same air mass until the mountain didn't know what season it was in. Noor could hear it working beneath his boots. A wet, shifting sound, like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

In another life he would have refused to descend this. In another life he would have called it, waited out the thaw cycle, lost a day and maybe Ilse's leg to infection. But the drive was dying and Corven was behind them and the mountain's confusion was, for once, a door rather than a wall.

"Talk to me," Ilse said. "If you go quiet I'll think you've decided something."

"I'm listening."

"To what?"

"The slope." He picked his way left along a rib of exposed granite, testing each hold with the axe before trusting it. "Every storm has a grammar. Where the wind stutters, where the snow loads, where the melt goes. My father used to say the weather is just the sky doing arithmetic out loud." He paused at a crevasse lip, reading the blue shadows in it. "They fired me for doing the arithmetic too well."

"You told me. Karva. The fleet."

"The Halvard fleet." His mouth tasted like the old bitterness, familiar as an old scar. "I said don't put out. Forty boats went out anyway because the harbor master's model said clear windows. My forecast said otherwise. I was right, and being right cost me everything, because the wrong people had bet money on the right numbers." He stepped across the crevasse where it narrowed, feeling Ilse shift her grip. "Everyone always asks what it's like to be disgraced. Nobody asks what it's like to be correct and ruined. It's worse. Ruin with evidence attached."

"Then this," Ilse said, and patted the drive's strap against his chest with one weak hand, "is your evidence finally paying interest."

He almost laughed. The sound came out as breath, white and brief.

* * *

By midday the cloud closed around them and took away every landmark, and this was when the old knowledge stopped being a story and became a rope.

There were no landmarks. There was no sun. There was no route, not really—the maps Ilse carried showed a trail that the storm had buried or rerouted, and the trail itself had been drawn by surveyors who had never seen this face in thaw. What remained was Noor, standing on a melting slope inside a moving mass of gray air, with a wounded woman on his back and a dying drive against his heart, navigating by feel.

He closed his eyes. Not long—a few seconds at a time—but enough.

Wind on the back of his neck: southwest, warm, carrying the smell of low valleys and wet earth, which meant the thaw front was below them and rising. Pressure in his ears: falling in slow pulses, which meant the afternoon would bring instability, squalls, short violent cells building off the warm ridges to the south. The quality of the light through his eyelids: flat, sourceless, the light of a cloud deck with no breaks—which meant no sun-softened slopes, no glare traps, the crust would hold longer than it should and then fail all at once.

His father had taught him this on the fells above their town, hands on Noor's shoulders, turning him slowly like dialing a radio. *Don't look at the storm, boy. Stand inside it. You're not a spectator, you're an instrument.* Everyone else in the forecasting academy had learned weather as data—as grids, as models, as numbers other people collected. Noor had learned it as a body of knowledge he happened to inhabit. And when his forecasts diverged from the models—and they always eventually diverged, because models are averages and storms are exceptions—it was Noor who was called arrogant, Noor whose judgment was overruled, Noor who watched the Halvard fleet sail into a cell he had named and described and dated.

Now the same exile-skill walked him down a killing slope one step at a time.

"Left here," he murmured, and turned along a contour he couldn't see but could feel—a compression in the air against his right cheek, a thinning of the wind's voice, the signature of a gully cutting down through the face. Gullies meant water. Water meant danger. But this gully ran east-west, sheltered, its floor packed hard by drainage, and it would take them four hundred meters down without exposing them to the open slope.

"You can't possibly know that," Ilse said.

"No. But the mountain does, and it's telling me."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only kind I have left." He ducked under an overhang of crusted snow, feeling it shed grit down his collar. "Ilse. When your team cut the pages out of the logbook—who made that call?"

She was quiet for a while. Long enough that he thought she'd faded.

"Henrik. Our lead. He got a transmission ordering us to purge the field records and await extraction. Henrik said the order came from someone who wasn't authorized to give it. He kept the originals, cut the copies so any inspection would find nothing." A breath. "Eleven days alone up here, I've had a lot of time to decide whether that was brave or stupid."

"What did you decide?"

"That it was both, and that both are required." Her arms tightened slightly around his shoulders. "Keep walking, Noor."

* * *

The first squall hit them at midafternoon, exactly when his ears had said it would.

It came out of the south like something poured—a gray wall of rain and graupel, warm enough at its leading edge that the snow around them began to slough and groan. Noor had perhaps ninety seconds of warning in the pressure drop and spent them badly-needed-well: he got them off the open slope and into the lee of a serac, wedged into a hollow where the wind went over the top of them, and got Ilse's bad leg elevated on his pack, and got the drive inside his jacket, against skin.

Then the world became noise.

Rain hammered the crust. Graupel rattled off the serac like gravel thrown at a window. Somewhere above them, the slope began to move—not a slide, not yet, but a slow wet creep, tons of saturated snow adjusting itself, finding new angles. Noor pressed his back to the ice and held Ilse and listened the way other men prayed.

*Listen. Don't look. You're an instrument.*

Under the roar he found it: a deep, rhythmic knocking, water moving through a channel somewhere beneath them, flowing fast and directional. Southwest to northeast. Downhill, toward the valley, toward Solheim. Every channel on this face would be running the same direction. Which meant—

"Meltwater routes," he shouted over the storm. "Everything drains northeast. If we follow the water we follow the fall line to the valley floor."

"You want to navigate by plumbing!"

"I once navigated a fishing fleet by the smell of the wind! Plumbing is a promotion!"

She laughed then—a cracked, disbelieving sound, half pain, and he felt it more than heard it, her chest shaking against his back, and something in him that had been clenched since Karva loosened one notch. This was the thing nobody had ever understood about him, not the academies, not the clients, not the Keslers with their ledgers: he loved this. Not the danger. The conversation. The mountain saying things and him understanding, after years of everyone insisting the conversation was nonsense, that he'd been fluent all along.

The squall passed in forty minutes, the way squalls do, and left the face transformed. What had been crust was now porridge. What had been route was now river. They descended through it in the long gold light of late afternoon, following channels of rushing meltwater down through terraces of rotten snow, Noor reading each terrace before committing to it—the lean of the slope, the pitch of the water's sound, the smell of the air coming up from below.

Twice the crust failed under him. Both times the axe went in and held, and both times he hung there with Ilse's weight swinging him toward the void and talked himself back onto solid ground in a voice he kept deliberately bored, because panic is contagious and boredom, apparently, also was.

The second time, Ilse said, "You're enjoying this."

"I'm tolerating it loudly."

"Liar."

* * *

Dusk came down purple and cold, and with it the freeze—the day's melt locking back into ice, the whole mountain creaking as it set like concrete. They needed shelter before full dark or the freeze would take them too, and there was nothing on the maps. So Noor did the last thing the academies had trained out of him and the mountains had trained back in: he asked the air.

Warm air rises. Cold air sinks. At dusk on a mountain face, the drainage winds begin to flow downhill like slow rivers of cold, and they pool in hollows, and where they pool, the snow stays softest longest and the wind-scour leaves the lee sides of ridges carved into caves and coffers. He stood still in the blue gloom and let the drainage wind touch his face, and followed it downhill with his eyes shut, hand on Ilse's arm, until the wind bent around an obstacle—and there it was. An overhang of glacier ice, undercut by summer melt, floored with old firn, dry as a cellar.

"In," he said. "Before it freezes shut."

They got the emergency bivvy up—half a tent, a silver shell, barely a coffin liner. Noor got Ilse out of the wind and got sugar and fat into her, the last of the pemmican, hot water from the stove in sips. Her wound had held. Her lips had color again. She looked at him across the tiny orange space with an expression he couldn't parse and didn't try to.

"The drive," she said.

He took it out. In the stove's light he could see the indicator through the casing: a thin red pulse, slower than it had been that morning. Dying, but not dead. He zipped it into the innermost pocket of his base layer, against his chest, where his heartbeat might argue with the cold.

"It'll make it," he said.

"You don't know that."

"I know the storm will break by midnight. High pressure behind the front—I can taste it. Clear, cold night, hard freeze, and tomorrow we get a full day of firm going. We'll reach the treeline by noon and Solheim by dark." He fed the stove another tablet and watched the flame stand perfectly straight in the dead-still air, confirmation of everything his body already knew. "And when we get there, we put this thing on the strongest transmitter in the valley and we tell everyone what CLEAR SKY did, and what it cost, and what it's still costing."

Ilse was quiet. Then: "Henrik would have liked you."

"Henrik is alive?"

Silence. The flame flickered once.

"No," she said finally. "But he would have liked you. That's not the same thing, and I'm done pretending it isn't."

Noor reached back through the dark of the bivvy and found her hand and held it, frostbitten fingers around frostbitten fingers, two broken instruments that still worked.

* * *

He woke at midnight because the silence changed.

Not woke—surfaced, the way a swimmer surfaces, into a blackness so complete and so quiet that his ears rang with it. The storm was gone. Above the overhang, through the ice, the sky was doing what he'd promised it would: clearing. He could feel the stars the way you feel heat from a stove, a fine pressure of cold light. The temperature was plummeting. By dawn the slope would be iron.

He lay still and let himself feel it—all of it, the whole instrument of himself. The high pressure settling over the range like a hand smoothing a sheet. The drainage winds flowing past the overhang, steady, unhurried, carrying the smell of pine and woodsmoke from far below. Woodsmoke. Human smoke. Solheim's valley, breathing up at them.

And under the woodsmoke, fainter, threaded through the clean cold air like a hair in milk: something burning that wasn't wood. Diesel. Ozone. The sharp chemical tang of generators running hard.

His eyes opened in the dark.

Generators meant equipment. Equipment meant either the relay station on the valley road—or a camp. A camp at the treeline, between them and Solheim, positioned exactly where anyone descending the pass would come down.

Corven hadn't sent mercenaries ahead. Corven had gone to the door and sat down beside it.

Noor lay in the freezing dark with the drive pulsing red against his heart and did the arithmetic one more time, the arithmetic that had ruined him and was now the only thing keeping them alive. One day of travel. A wounded woman. A dying battery. And at the bottom of the mountain, waiting in the woodsmoke, the man who had bought his ruin and was coming to collect the interest.

He touched Ilse's shoulder. Her eyes opened instantly—eleven days alone will do that.

"Trouble?" she whispered.

"Below us," he said. "Get dressed. We're racing the sunrise."
