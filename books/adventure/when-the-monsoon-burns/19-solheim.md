## 19. Solheim

The snow came down like an answer.

Ilse had spent eleven days in the Vantaa Range learning the language of weather the hard way—by hiding from it, starving under it, watching it erase the people she'd loved. She knew the engineered storms now, could taste their wrongness: a flat metallic edge to the air, a pressure that climbed when it should have fallen, clouds that moved like they were being steered. But this snow was different. This snow fell straight and slow and honest, fat flakes that tumbled without purpose, without design, the way snow had fallen before anyone thought to own the sky.

She stopped on the last shoulder of the descent and let it land on her face.

Below, cradled in the throat of the valley where the river finally remembered which way it was supposed to run, Solheim glowed. A scatter of lamplit windows, woodsmoke standing up straight from a dozen chimneys, and above it all—the mast. A lattice of steel rising from the roof of what looked like a telegraph office, guyed with wire that caught the light of the town beneath it and held it like a net full of stars.

"There," Noor said behind her. His voice was wrecked—three days of cold air and shouting over wind had stripped it down to gravel—but there was something underneath the ruin of it, something almost like hope.

Ilse didn't answer. She was looking at the trucks.

Two of them, parked at the near end of the town's single street, engines idling, exhaust pluming white into the falling snow. And beside them, figures. Four, maybe five. One of them wore a coat too good for this altitude, cut close at the waist, hands in its pockets, face tilted up toward the pass they'd just come down as though he'd been waiting for exactly this view.

Corven.

Her stomach did something complicated and cold. "Noor."

"I see them." He came up beside her, breathing hard, one hand pressed to his ribs where Dov's warning still lived in the form of bruised cartilage. The drive rode against his chest inside his jacket, strapped there with cord, warm from his body—and dying. They both knew it. Every hour since the ice bridge, the little indicator light on its casing had been dimming by degrees, amber going toward red, a candle burning down in a room with no more matches.

"He can't know we're here yet," Ilse said. "Not in this snow. Not unless he has thermal."

"He'll have thermal." Noor said it without bitterness, the way you'd say the sun will rise. He'd spent enough time around Corven to know the man bought certainty the way other men bought bread.

* * *

They came down through the treeline instead of the road, moving the way Ilse had learned to move during those eleven days alone—weight on the outside of the foot, pauses between steps, using the wind's noise the way a thief uses a crowd. Her body had become a stranger to her in the range: twenty pounds lighter, hands scarred, a graze along her upper arm wrapped in strips of Maret's blanket that had stiffened into a kind of armor. But the stranger her body had become was useful. It knew how to be small. It knew how to wait.

From the woodshed at the edge of town—they reached it as true dark fell, and the snow thickened until the lamplight of Solheim became soft yellow smears—they watched.

Corven stood in the middle of the street like a man at his own wedding. He wasn't pacing. That was the thing about Corven that Ilse found most obscene: he never paced, never fidgeted, never betrayed a single gram of doubt. He simply stood where he had decided to stand, and the world arranged itself around him. Two of the Keslers' men flanked the trucks. A third leaned in the doorway of the telegraph office, talking to someone inside, and every few minutes he'd glance back out at the street with the loose vigilance of a man being paid by the hour.

And there were others. Two more figures Ilse hadn't expected, hunched under the awning of the building across from the mast—one heavy, one thin, their silhouettes as familiar to her now as her own heartbeat.

"The Kesslers," she breathed. "Both of them. They came themselves."

"They'd want to." Noor crouched beside her, peering through the gap in the shed boards. "A payday like this? You don't send a courier." He was quiet a moment, and then, in a voice stripped of everything except arithmetic: "Corven sold my route to them twice. Once to collect me, once to sell me. Now he's selling them the ending."

"Then we go around. There must be another transmitter—"

"There isn't." He said it gently, which made it worse. "I asked in the last village before the storm closed the passes. Solheim's mast is the only one in forty kilometers that reaches past the range. Everything else is shortwave and prayer." He turned to look at her, and even in the dark she could see how hollowed his face had become, the frostbite damage on his fingers making them look like something carved from old wood. "One mast. One chance. And he's standing under it."

* * *

Ilse pressed her forehead against the cold board and let herself feel, for exactly ten seconds, the full weight of what eleven days and one mountain had cost.

She thought of Tomas, who had kept the survey instruments running right up until the end because clean data mattered more than his own frostbitten feet. Of Anselm, who'd cut the pages out of the logbook—not to hide anything, but so that whoever came after would find only the data, nothing personal, nothing that could be used to find families, to leverage grief. He'd understood, even then, that they were going to be erased, and he'd spent his last hours making sure the erasure would be incomplete. She thought of the gorge, the water running backward, Noor's arm locked around her ribs while the river tried to unmake her, and how afterward neither of them had said anything because there was nothing words could do that the water hadn't already done.

CLEAR SKY. Three failed harvests in the valleys below. Maret's face when she'd said *remember us*, and the terrible modesty of it—no plea for rescue, no request for justice, just *remember*. As if remembering were a thing a person could be asked to do, the way you'd ask someone to carry a stone.

The drive against Noor's chest held all of it. Every reading. Eleven months of the survey team's work proving that the monsoon wasn't failing—it was being spent, drawn down like a bank account by men who believed the sky belonged to them. And the drive was dying. Amber to red. Maybe six hours of battery left, maybe four. Enough to transmit once. Not enough to transmit twice.

That was the part no one said aloud, but it sat in the shed with them like a third person: whatever they did tonight, they would do once. If the transmission failed halfway—if the signal dropped, if the mast went dead, if Corven's men cut power to the telegraph office—there would be no second attempt. No retry. The truth would die in the wires, incomplete, and the world would go on believing the Vantaa survey team had simply vanished, victims of bad weather and worse judgment.

"One frequency," Noor murmured. He'd been saying it for two days, half to himself, working the problem the way he worked storms—out loud, hands moving, tracing invisible systems in the air. "Every open band, all at once, so it can't be jammed or intercepted cleanly. But the mast needs to hold long enough. Full burst, maybe ninety seconds of transmission. And I need—" He stopped.

"You need what?"

"A distraction," he said. "And a key."

* * *

The telegraph office door opened at half past eight, spilling lamplight onto the snow, and a woman stepped out—a local, gray-haired, coat buttoned to the chin, keys jangling at her hip. The operator. She exchanged a few words with the man leaning in the doorway, pointed up the street toward what might have been her home, and walked away through the falling snow, leaving the office lit but empty except for the guard.

Ilse watched the keys swing at the woman's hip until the dark took her.

"Noor," she said slowly. "The mast. Who runs it?"

He followed her gaze. She saw him arrive at the same place she had, saw it move across his exhausted face like weather. "Someone has to switch it on. Someone local. Corven doesn't have a key—he has money. There's a difference, and it's the only difference we've got."

"She won't just hand it over."

"No. But she might listen." He was quiet, and then he laughed—a single dry note with no humor in it, the sound of a man who has run out of everything except audacity. "I spent three years being the most disgraced forecaster on this side of the range. Everyone in these valleys knows the name. Noor Iversen, the man who cried flood and got laughed off every stage from here to Karva." He flexed his ruined fingers, wincing. "Maybe it's finally worth something. Being famous for being right."

"It's not enough," Ilse said. "Even if she believes you—even if she opens the office—you still have to get past five men to reach the transmitter, and the moment you touch that dial, they'll know. You have maybe ninety seconds of broadcast and zero seconds of escape." She turned to face him fully, and the snow came between them, settling on both their shoulders like a shared weight. "So don't plan an escape."

Noor went very still.

"You told me once, in the hut, why your father gave you the compass," she said. "You said he told you: *a direction is only worth what you're willing to spend to hold it.*" Her voice cracked on the last word and she let it. "You spent the compass on the bridge. Don't pretend you were planning to come back from that glacier either, Noor Iversen. I watched you do the math."

"That was different. The bridge was—"

"The bridge was the same as this." She took his wrist—the uninjured one—and felt his pulse hammering there, fast and alive and terrified, and she held it the way you hold something you intend to keep. "Listen to me. I pulled that drive out of Tomas's instruments with my own hands. I carried it through eleven days of hell so it would outlive all of us. It is not going to die ninety seconds into the only transmission that matters because you wanted to be noble." She was shaking now, and she couldn't tell if it was cold or fury, and it didn't matter. "We trade. We bargain. We use the one thing Corven wants more than the data."

Noor frowned. "Which is?"

"Me." She said it flatly, without drama, because drama was a luxury the mountain had burned out of her. "Corven doesn't want the drive, not really. He wants confirmation there are no witnesses. He told you that himself, in the contract you didn't read. A witness who walks into his hands voluntarily, with the drive, is worth more than a dead man and a scavenged chip. He'll trade the mast for that. He'll believe he's won." She smiled, and she knew from the way Noor's face changed that it was not a comforting smile. "Let him believe it. For ninety seconds."

* * *

Later—much later, when the snow had deepened and the lamplighters of Solheim had gone to bed and only the mast's aviation beacon pulsed red above the white street—Ilse stood alone at the gap in the shed boards and studied the man who had orchestrated all of it.

Corven hadn't moved. Hours now, and he hadn't moved, except once to check his watch. Snow gathered on the shoulders of his beautiful coat and he didn't brush it off, as if he'd decided that discomfort was a price he'd already accounted for, a line item in the ledger of the evening. Once, the thinner Kessler crossed the street and spoke to him, and Ilse watched Corven shake his head—just once, small, final—and the older man retreat. Even his employers obeyed him. Or perhaps, she thought, they simply recognized the same thing she was recognizing now: that Corven was not waiting for them.

He was waiting for the mountain to finish its work. He'd sent Noor up with a salvage contract and a hidden clause, and he'd assumed the range itself would do the killing for free, and when the range had failed—if it had failed—he'd come to collect the remainder in person. Noor Iversen was a line item. She was a line item. The whole valley, with its borrowed weather and its failed harvests and its dead survey team, was a column of numbers, and Corven was here to balance the books.

But he'd made one error, and it was the same error the program had made, the same error Adeyemi had made with his famine projections and his engineered storms: he had counted everything except what people were willing to lose.

Ilse touched her bandaged arm, where the bullet graze had scabbed into a ridge like a contour line on a map. She thought of the drive's dying battery, amber fading toward red, and of the ninety seconds that would decide whether eleven months of truth lived or died in the wires.

Then she watched the guard at the telegraph office stamp his feet against the cold, turn up his collar, and take three steps under the awning—out of the snow, out of the sightline of the street—and she turned to Noor in the dark.

"Now," she whispered. "While he's warm. Before I lose my nerve."

Noor looked at her, and in the red pulse of the beacon above the mast she saw him understand exactly what she intended to do, and exactly what it would cost, and exactly why she was going first.

The snow fell on, straight and slow and honest, covering their tracks behind them as they slipped out of the woodshed toward the last door in the valley—and the men guarding it.
