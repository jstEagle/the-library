## 20. One Frequency

The snow came down the way it always came down in the Vantaa Range at the end of the season — not falling so much as arriving, a slow white settling of the whole sky, as if the mountain had decided the year was over and was putting itself to bed. Noor stood at the edge of Solheim's main street with Ilse leaning against his shoulder, her blood seeping through the field dressing he'd tied at the col, and looked at the radio mast.

It rose from the roof of the telephone exchange like a needle through cloth. Forty meters of galvanized steel, guyed with wire that sang faintly in the wind, its red aviation lamp blinking with a patience that seemed almost mocking. In a valley of sodden timber houses and shuttered fishing sheds, it was the only thing pointing at the sky. The only thing that could point at the sky.

And at its base, hands in his pockets, breath steaming, stood Corven.

He had two of the Keslers' men flanking him — Noor recognized the bulk of them from Karva, from the way debt moved through a room before the debt collectors did — and he was not holding a weapon, which was worse. Corven never held the weapon. He held the thing you needed, and he waited.

"I wondered how long the mountain would take to deliver you," Corven called, when they were close enough. His voice carried the pleasant, unhurried tone of a man discussing freight rates. "You're late, Noor. You were always going to be late. That's why I chose you."

"Chose him for what?" Ilse said. Her voice was rough, scraped thin by the graze and the cold, but her eyes were steady on Corven with the flat attention of someone who had spent eleven days learning to read threats in a landscape.

"For the same reason you choose a plumb line," Corven said. "Because it falls straight. Give a ruined man a rope and he'll climb it every time. It's not cynicism, it's engineering." He turned his gaze to Noor, and for one unguarded moment there was something almost like respect in it. "You brought it down. I didn't think you would. I had money on the pass."

"The drive stays out of your hands," Noor said. "That's not a negotiation."

"Everything's a negotiation. That's the first thing the program's backers taught me, back when I still thought I was the clever one in the room." Corven lifted his chin toward the exchange. "You want the mast. I have the key — and the operator's log-in, and the goodwill of the town council, who I've been drinking with since Tuesday. You have a dying drive, a bleeding friend, and frostbite in both hands. So here is my offer, and I'll only make it once."

He reached into his coat and produced the key. Small. Brass. It turned slowly in his fingers, catching the red blink of the mast light.

"You," Corven said. "Unarmed, cooperative, and delivered to the Keslers' advance man at the bridge at dawn. In exchange — the key, the log-in, and my personal guarantee that Ms. Brandt walks out of this valley untouched. The data, you can broadcast. I don't care about the data anymore. The data's worth something only while it's exclusive, and Adeyemi's confession will gut that value within the week. What the Keslers want is the man who embarrassed them. What the program wants is a body to hang its failure on. You can be both, Noor. It's the most useful thing you'll ever be."

The snow ticked against Noor's hood. Somewhere down the street a door slammed, a dog barked twice and thought better of it. Ilse's weight against his shoulder had grown heavier; she was spending blood she didn't have to stay upright.

"And if I say no?" Noor asked.

"Then we take the key back, the drive dies in your pocket tonight, and in the spring two more names go on the survey team's memorial." Corven smiled, small and genuine and terrible. "I'm offering you a better deal than the mountain did."

Noor looked at the key. He looked at the mast, and the storm behind it — because there was a storm behind it, there had been one building all day, and his hands had been reading it since before his mind caught up. The pressure had been falling all afternoon in that particular stuttering way, not the smooth slide of a natural system but the sawtooth shudder of something being pushed, something being *held* and *released* in increments. He had spent his whole life learning the grammar of storms, and this one was speaking in a broken accent, and it was speaking about lightning.

The engineered weather had a schedule. Engineered weather always had a schedule — that was its weakness, the flaw he'd been exiled for pointing out. You could not steer a monsoon without modeling it, and you could not model it without rhythm, and rhythm could be read.

The discharge would come off the ridge to the northeast. It would come soon — within the hour, maybe within the half — and when it came it would come down the mast like a spear down a throat, because the mast was the tallest thing in the valley and the valley was a bowl of charged air.

"Deal," Noor said.

* * *

They took his axe, his knife, the spare batteries, everything with an edge or a charge. Ilse argued with her eyes and then, when that failed, with her voice, low and furious against his ear while the Kessler men patted him down. "You're not a line item. Don't let him make you a line item."

"I'm not," Noor said. "I'm a fuse."

She didn't understand yet. He pressed the drive into her hands — the drive, the whole reason for the mountain, eleven days of her life and three of his, Dov dead in the whiteout, Maret's valley behind them, the compass case empty on his belt — and he said, "When I signal, you climb. The operator's shack is at the mast base. Corven has the log-in, but the old man who runs the exchange — Voss, the one with the stove — Corven's been drinking with him, which means Corven trusts him, which means Corven told him things. Get Voss. Tell him the harvests were borrowed. Tell him Maret said to remember."

Ilse stared at him. "And what will you be doing?"

"Watching the sky," Noor said. "It's the one thing I was ever good at."

Corven walked him down the street to the bridge at the valley's mouth, where the Keslers' advance man waited with a truck and a tarp and the bored efficiency of men who moved problems for a living. The snow was thickening. The red lamp on the mast blinked and blinked. And Noor, hands bound loosely in front of him, stood in the falling white and did the one thing no one thought to stop him from doing.

He read the storm.

It was all in the snow, if you knew how to listen. The flakes weren't falling — they were drifting in long lateral sweeps, northeast to southwest, which meant the wind at cloud height was running opposite to the wind at ground level, a shear, a separation of charge. The air tasted metallic at the back of the throat, the way it did when the potential gradient went steep. The hair on his forearms, beneath the frostbitten skin, had begun to lift. On the ridge to the northeast, the cloud base was lowering in a slow, muscular descent, and beneath it the snow had stopped falling entirely — a clear slot, a hole punched in the weather, and through the hole he could see the flicker. Not lightning yet. The storm clearing its throat.

Engineered weather had a schedule. And this pulse — he counted the flickers, timed the intervals — this pulse would discharge within minutes, and it would discharge along the path of least resistance, and in the valley of Solheim there was exactly one path of least resistance standing forty meters into the sky with its feet in a telephone exchange.

Corven had bought a man who could read storms. Corven had never once asked what a man like that would do with a storm.

* * *

The signal was a shout — Noor's voice, cracked and carrying, the only weapon left on him: "Ilse! *Now!*"

He saw her break from the exchange doorway, saw Voss the operator hurrying beside her with a ring of keys and the bewildered urgency of a man whose stove-side confidences had just become testimony. Corven spun, and for the first time since Karva, the smoothness fell off him entirely — Noor watched it go, watched the broker's face become just a man's face, cold and afraid, and thought: *there you are.*

"What did you do?" Corven shouted at him across the snow.

"Nothing," Noor said. "Yet. I'm just the plumb line, remember. I fall straight."

The flicker on the ridge came faster. The clear slot under the cloud was glowing now, a bruised violet light that had nothing to do with sunset. The Kessler men were looking up. Everyone was looking up. The whole valley had gone quiet in that way valleys go quiet before a discharge, the pressure pressing down on the eardrums, the snow hanging almost motionless in the charged air, every living thing waiting for the sky to make up its mind.

"Get him in the truck," Corven said, and his voice had lost its music.

But the truck wouldn't start. Noor had counted on that too — had counted on the electromagnetic stink in the air, the way engines stuttered when the gradient went steep; he'd watched it kill a whole chase convoy once, on a day that had ended his career. The Kessler man cranked the ignition and the engine turned over and died, turned over and died, and Corven stood in the snow with a brass key in his hand and nothing in the world it would open.

On the mast, the red lamp went dark.

Not failed — *charged*. Noor could feel it from here, the whole structure humming, singing, the guy-wires whining a note that climbed and climbed. Ilse was inside the shack now, he could see the light, see her silhouette bent over the console with Voss, see the old operator's hands — steady, unhurried, the hands of a man who had spent forty years talking to the sky — moving across the frequency dials.

Every open frequency. Voss knew how. It was in the old treaties, the old emergency protocols, the ones nobody used anymore: one transmitter, every band, the truth shouted on all of them at once so that no one could claim they hadn't heard.

The first strike hit the ridge behind them and the world went white.

Noor didn't flinch. He stood in the truck's headlights with his hands bound and watched the light crawl down the mountainside, and in the afterimage he saw the second flicker building, the main discharge, the one the whole engineered pulse had been climbing toward. Minutes. Maybe less.

Inside the shack, a light came on — a small green lamp above the console, and beneath it, Voss's voice, amplified, rolling out over the valley in the flat cadence of a man reading numbers that would end a government: *"This is Solheim relay, broadcasting on all open frequencies. Transmitting survey data, Vantaa Range expedition, with corroborating testimony. Program designation: CLEAR SKY—"*

The words went out into the storm. Noor couldn't hear the data itself, but he could hear the *fact* of it, the carrier wave, the hum of a voice speaking into every ear within a hundred kilometers — the fishing boats in the sound, the waystations on the lowland roads, the patrol radios on the passes, the garrison at Karva, all of it, all of them, at once. There was no taking it back. There was no exclusive left to sell. Corven's payday was evaporating with every syllable, and Corven knew it, and Corven turned toward Noor with the expression of a man watching his own ledger catch fire.

"You," Corven said. "You did this. You planned this since the bridge—"

"I planned it since the tent," Noor said. "You should have read the sky. It's the first thing they teach you."

The main discharge came off the ridge like the mountain splitting open.

Noor had seen a thousand strikes. He had chased them across three seasons of flat green plains, measured them, loved them, been ruined by his knowledge of them. He had never stood this close to one that was aimed. The bolt came down the clear slot and found the mast the way water finds the drain, and for one full second the valley had no shadows, only light — the mast a column of white fire, the snow on the street blazing violet, every window in Solheim lit from the outside — and then the thunder arrived and took the sound of the world away.

When Noor could hear again, the mast was smoking. The red lamp was gone, the guy-wires were singing a broken note, and the antenna at the top — the antenna that had carried the truth out on every open frequency — was a curl of black slag dripping off the side of the tower.

The drive had transmitted. And the mast would never transmit again. One frequency, one chance, spent in a single stroke — and no evidence left for Corven to seize, no broadcast left for the program to jam, nothing but the truth, already loose in the world, moving at the speed of radio into a hundred kitchens and garrisons and fishing boats where it could not be called back.

Corven stood in the snow with the brass key in his open hand. He looked at it for a long moment, as if seeing it for the first time — a small thing, a thing that had opened nothing — and then he looked at Noor, and there was nothing left in his face to sell.

"The Keslers will kill you for this," Corven said. "The program will kill you for this."

"No," Noor said. "The program is going to have to explain itself. That's worse."

The Kessler men had gone — he realized it slowly, the way you realize a storm has passed — their truck cranking to life now that the charge had drained from the air, their judgment already made: the debt was worthless, the collateral had burned, and men like that did not stay for the aftermath. Corven looked after the truck's taillights with something almost like envy.

Then he looked up the street, at the small figure limping out of the exchange doorway with a bloodstained bandage and a dying drive and eleven days of survival in her eyes, and Corven — broker, buyer of ruined men, engineer of plumb lines — did the only thing left in his inventory.

He ran.

He ran into the snow, toward the dark end of the valley, toward the pass that Adeyemi had sealed and the storm that was still unspooling over the ridge, and Noor did not move to stop him. The mountain would do what the mountain did. It had been collecting its toll all season, and it was very good at its work.

Ilse reached him, breathless, gray-lipped, alive. Behind her, Voss stood in the exchange doorway with his keys in his fist, looking up at the smoking mast with an expression Noor would remember for the rest of his life — not grief, not triumph, but the quiet satisfaction of an old man who had finally said the thing he'd been keeping behind his teeth for three failed harvests.

"Every band," Voss said. "It's out there. It's not coming back."

The snow kept falling. The first true snow of the season, clean and unengineered, drifting down over the slag and the brass key and the empty street — and far to the northeast, on the sealed pass, a single set of headlights appeared through the white, climbing slowly down toward Solheim, carrying someone who had heard the broadcast, or was running from it, or both.

Noor took Ilse's arm. "Whoever that is," he said, "they're coming to us. Let's go find out which."
