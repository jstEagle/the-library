## 1. The Weight of Water

The clerk who hired me wouldn't meet my eyes.

He'd chosen the back booth of a tea house in Ratnagiri that smelled of cardamom and damp plaster, and he kept his voice below the drumming on the tin roof, as if the rain itself might be reporting to someone. Deshmukh, he said his name was. A ministry clerk, third grade, twenty-two years of service, and hands that shook around his glass until he set it down.

"Six people," he said. "A survey team. They went into the hills above the Vaitarna headwaters three weeks ago to re-mark boundary stones for a land reassessment. They were due back on the ninth." He paused. "It is the twenty-sixth."

"And the ministry has done nothing for seventeen days?"

"The ministry has done exactly what it intended to do, which is nothing." He said it flatly, without self-pity, and I understood then that he was not a fool. Fools don't hire freelancers off the books. Fools don't pay half in advance in cash folded inside a newspaper. "Officially, the team's permits lapsed and they extended them informally. Unofficially—" He stopped. Looked at the rain.

"Unofficially, nobody wants them found," I finished.

"I want them found," he said. "I want to know if they're dead or if they're being made dead slowly. There's a difference, Ms. Thackeray. I've worked under this government long enough to know the difference matters."

He slid an envelope across the table. Inside: a topographic sheet of the Konkan hill country, hand-annotated, the contour lines dense as fingerprints around a valley the mapmakers had named with bureaucratic indifference—Survey Compartment 44-K—and a photograph of six people squinting into monsoon light on some office steps. The man in the center was fifty-ish, silver at the temples, with the particular stubborn set of the jaw that I'd learned to recognize in mirrors of my own father's old expedition photos.

"That's Iyer," Deshmukh said. "Team leader. Good man. The others are good people too. That's why they're in trouble."

I should have said no. I want to be honest about that, because everyone assumes the people who do this work are brave, and mostly we are just bad at refusing. But I looked at the map—at 44-K, at the way the hills folded around that valley like a closed fist—and something in my chest turned over, the way it always does when terrain looks like terrain my father taught me to read before I could read words.

"Half now," I said. "And you never contacted me."

* * *

The road ended where roads end on that coast: at a wall of green so wet and aggressive it seemed less like vegetation than like weather given roots. I hired a fisherman to run me upriver as far as the water allowed, which by the third day of rain wasn't far, and after that I walked.

The monsoon in the Konkan hills is not weather. It is a condition of existence. It falls in sheets that erase horizons, fills every hollow until the land becomes a series of lakes connected by streams that used to be paths, and it does all of this with a sound like applause—vast, indifferent, continuous. My father used to say you don't fight the monsoon, you negotiate with it. He said it the way he said everything: as though the landscape were a colleague and I were a promising student he hadn't yet decided to keep.

I was nine years old the first time he brought me into country like this. He crouched at the edge of a flooded gully and made me look at it for ten full minutes before he'd let me cross.

"What do you see?"

"Water."

"You see water. What else?"

I didn't know. He waited—he was very good at waiting, it was his best quality and the weapon he used against us all—and finally I saw it: the way the current ran clean on one side and silted on the other, the way a fallen bamboo had lodged upstream and was quietly building a dam, the way the far bank bulged slightly wrong, too green, too soft.

"It's lying to us," I said.

Something moved across his face—not pride exactly, more like recognition, the look of a man finding a tool he didn't know he'd ordered. "Everything out here is lying," he said. "The river lies about its depth. The forest lies about its distance. Your job is to catch the lie early enough that it doesn't cost you anything permanent." Then he crossed first, testing each step, and held out his hand for me, and for one season—one whole shining season—I believed I was the thing he'd been waiting for.

He chose the dig instead. Charnapura, the year after. Chose the cameras and the funding committee and the woman from the university who laughed at his jokes, and when my mother asked him when he was coming home he said *when the season ends*, and seasons, for a man like him, are geological.

I caught myself standing in the rain remembering this, and made myself walk.

* * *

I found the first sign on the fourth day, at the confluence where the map showed an old footpath climbing toward the compartment boundary.

It was gear. Not scattered—the way animals or accident scatter things—but arranged. A rucksack set upright on a stone shelf above the flood line, weighted with a rock. Inside: a theodolite wrapped in oilcloth, dry. A coil of rope, coiled properly. A mess tin containing matches sealed in wax, which is the kind of thing only a careful person does, and a folded paper gone soft with damp.

The paper was a permit. Ministry issue, laminated once, the kind that's supposed to survive fieldwork. Someone had torn it—deliberately, cleanly, down through the middle of the stamp—and left both halves in the tin. Not lost. Torn. Which meant someone wanted whoever came looking to understand that the permission had been revoked, or renounced, or had never really existed at all.

I stood there a long time with the two halves in my glove, rain hammering the hood of my jacket, doing the arithmetic of it. Six people go into these hills with government authority to mark boundaries. Three weeks later their equipment sits above the waterline, packed by someone with discipline, and their permit comes home torn in half.

You don't abandon a theodolite because you're fleeing. You abandon it because you no longer intend to be found by the people who issued it.

I climbed higher.

* * *

Above the confluence the valley narrowed and the forest changed—older trees, darker understory, the air going cool and mineral the way it does near stone. And here the trail did something trails almost never do in this kind of country: it stopped.

Not faded. Stopped. Six adults, three weeks of movement, and not a single broken fern past a certain line, not a boot print in the black loam, not a snapped twig. I worked the ground for two hours in failing light, moving in expanding arcs the way he'd taught me, reading the lie out of the landscape, and the landscape simply declined to have an opinion. It was as if six people had walked up that path and been taken up by the rain.

Except.

At the edge of my widest arc, where a granite shoulder of hill rose out of the trees, I found what the rain had been hiding. Water was pouring off the rock face in a dozen silver threads, and behind one of them—only visible because the wind gusted and thinned the curtain for half a second—there was darkness. A seam in the stone. A cave mouth, drowned to within a meter of its lip, running with runoff.

And on the wet rock beside it, low, where only someone kneeling would ever see it, someone had scratched a mark into the granite with a knife or a nail. Fresh. The edges still bright.

A surveyor's benchmark symbol. The old kind. The kind my father used, the kind that appears in his earliest papers, before the fame, before everything—a circle with a cross through it, quartered.

Underneath, four letters, scratched fast and deep:

**44-K**

I knelt there in the pouring dark with my heart going like a trapped bird, and I understood three things at once, the way understanding sometimes arrives all together or not at all.

The team was alive. They had gone to ground deliberately, and they knew someone might come, and they had left a sign only another surveyor would read.

They were not hiding from the flood.

They were hiding from the ministry that sent me.

And somewhere below me, down through the trees, faint under the rain but unmistakable—engines. More than one. Moving slow along the valley track toward the village I'd passed at dusk, the one with the single street and the shrine and the fishing boats hauled up beyond the high-water marks.

I put out my hand and touched the scratched stone, cold as a confession, and started down.
