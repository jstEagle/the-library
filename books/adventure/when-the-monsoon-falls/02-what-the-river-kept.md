## 2. What the River Kept

The river gave the team back in pieces.

Wren found the first piece at dawn on the third day: a brass theodolite leg, mud-sucked, standing upright in a paddy terrace as if someone had planted it. Surveyors didn't abandon tripods. They cursed them, carried them, repaired them with tape and prayer. Standing one upright in open ground was a message, and Wren read it the way their father had taught them to read terrain thirty years ago, crouched over contour maps in a tent that smelled of kerosene and pipe smoke.

*Look for what doesn't belong to the water.*

Wren had spent two days learning what belonged to it. The trail from the last known campsite — coordinates scribbled on the clerk's napkin back in Panaji, along with a phone number he'd made Wren memorize and then swallow the paper's evidence in his office sink — led up through terraces gone feral, past a shrine drowned to its eaves, into hills where the rain came down so hard it erased sound itself. No tracks. That had been the first wrongness, reported in the ministry's terse cable: *no tracks, no bodies, no contact.* Six people don't vanish off a ridge without leaving a print. Unless they wanted to be untracked, or unless someone else had come through after them and done the erasing.

The tripod leg said: neither. It said *we are here, and we are careful.*

Wren stood in the rain and let the grief do its usual arithmetic. Every ridge here was a lesson their father had given them and then walked away from. *Read the slope before you trust the path,* he'd say, hand heavy on Wren's shoulder, and then he'd chosen a path that didn't include them at all — the grants, the honors, the name carved into foundation stones across three countries while a child waited at boarding school gates that never opened early enough. Wren had built a career out of being good at the thing he taught, in places he'd never go, and told no one this was a wound. Freelance surveyors were supposed to be unattached. It was practically a job requirement.

The leg pointed, deliberately, downhill toward the river gorge.

* * *

They were under the water.

Not drowned — hidden. The gorge narrowed two kilometers below the shrine, and where it narrowed there was an old temple complex the census maps marked as ruins, three shrines cut into basalt above a bend where the current ran green and deep. The lowest shrine's entrance sat below the floodline. Wren found it by the same trick the team must have used: a seam of drier stone, a draft moving against the rain, the faint mineral smell of air that had been breathing through rock for centuries.

Wren left the pack, kept the knife, and swam the entrance on a breath.

Inside, the water shallowed to knee-depth over ancient paving, and the dark ahead held a light. Not torchlight — something steadier. A hurricane lamp, hooded, set behind a screen of woven palm. And a voice, low and level, saying in Marathi: "You've been walking our ridges for two days. Come slowly, and say who sent you."

"Nobody sent me," Wren said, hands out, water dripping. "That's the problem."

A shape resolved against the lamp glow: a woman in her sixties, gray braid plastered flat, holding a machete with the ease of someone who'd cut sugarcane all her life. Behind her, in the shrine's inner chamber, six figures. Five adults. One girl, maybe fourteen, watching Wren with the flat assessment of someone raised around strangers with guns.

"You're not ministry," the woman said.

"I'm not anything. Wren Thackeray." The name landed strangely in the wet dark; Wren saw the team leader — a compact man with a bandaged forearm and reading glasses fogged beyond use — go still. "I was hired to find you. Quietly. By a clerk named Deshpande. He's terrified, which I took as a good sign. Terrified men usually know exactly what they're afraid of."

"Iyer," the man said. "Ravi Iyer. Team lead." He didn't offer a hand. "Thackeray. As in—"

"Yes."

"As in the grant."

The word dropped into the chamber like a stone into the flooded courtyard outside. Wren felt it go down and down. "What grant?"

Iyer exchanged a look with the woman — Salunkhe, she was called, though whether wife or sister or commander of the others Wren couldn't tell — and then did something Wren would think about for years afterward. He reached into a waterproof satchel, drew out a journal swollen with damp but intact, and handed it across like a man surrendering a weapon he no longer trusted himself to hold.

"Read it," he said. "Then decide whose side you're on. We've been deciding for eleven days."

* * *

*11th June.* We broke camp because Meera's grandmother sent word downriver: men at the village asking for us by name, men who did not introduce themselves to the sarpanch. Government teams get asked for by number, not name. Names mean someone has read our interim report, and only four people should have had it.

*12th June.* Moved the collection at night. I will not call them artifacts anymore; that word is how museums make theft sound like scholarship. These are deeds. Copper-plate charters, twelfth century, sealed grants recording that this valley — every hectare inside the concession boundary — was gifted to a temple trust in perpetuity, with boundary stones we have now located and photographed in situ. Crown land, the 1911 grant says. Uncultivated waste, free for mining lease. Except the waste was not waste, and the crown's own surveyors recorded the temple's claim and then filed it somewhere it could be forgotten. Filed, I now believe, by a young assistant archaeologist whose signature appears on the certification page.

*13th June.* I looked up the signature today in the district archives copy Salunkhe brought me. E.M. Thackeray, Assistant Surveyor, certified the boundary reclassification. His field notes contradict his own certification — I have both documents. He knew. He documented the temple's charter accurately in his notes and signed the classification anyway. Either he was coerced or he chose. In 1912, either way, a family rose on the difference, and the family is the minister's, and the concession expires in nineteen days unless renewed, and renewal requires exactly the fraud my team has spent six years documenting.

I keep writing his name as if writing it will make it mean less. It does not. The man who falsified the map that starves this valley is the father of the person who just swam into our shelter asking nothing.

Or asking everything. I haven't decided yet either.

*15th June.* Enforcers again at the village. Three vehicles this time, licensed to a quarrying subsidiary. They took the sarpanch's brother to the quarry office for questioning. He came back silent and would not eat. Meera's grandmother says: let them come up. Let them see the water. The gorge does our guarding better than any gun.

*16th June.* The girl Meera knows passages under the ridge that appear on no survey — mine included, and mine are good. She calls them the temple's veins. If it comes to movement, we move by her, not by map. I am recording this so that if we fail, whoever reads it understands: the land was never empty, and the people were never lost. Only the paperwork lied.

*17th June.* Thackeray's child slept twelve hours in the inner shrine. Woke, ate, and asked to see the plates. Not photographs — the plates themselves. Held the charter like it might bruise. Then asked me one question: "Did he know?" I said yes. Wren nodded once and went and stood in the flooded doorway a long time, looking at the water coming down.

I have seen that look before, on soldiers reading casualty lists. It is the look of someone recalculating everything they inherited.

* * *

Wren finished the journal by lamplight while the rain worked the mountain overhead like a patient animal. Eleven days of entries. A man documenting his own disappearance in case there was no one left to tell it aloud.

"The certification," Wren said finally. "Show me."

Salunkhe produced a folder wrapped in oilcloth. Inside, photostats of the 1911 classification, and beneath them, the field notes in a hand Wren knew intimately — had grown up watching it fill ledger after ledger at a kitchen table, before the kitchen table became a desk in a ministry and the ledgers became other people's business. The handwriting hadn't changed in a hundred years of family habit. Same forward slant. Same way of pressing too hard on the downstrokes, as if arguing with the paper.

E.M. Thackeray. Great-grandfather. The name on the original grant, certifying temple land as crown waste, opening the valley to the concession that fed the minister's family for four generations.

"He documented the truth," Wren said. "And signed the lie."

"That's the vertigo," Iyer said quietly. "It isn't one villain. It's a hundred small honest men each signing one dishonest line, until the dishonesty becomes the law. Your family's name is the first signature. Ours is the latest. The question is where yours stops."

Outside, far below, engines. Not rain-sound — engine-sound, climbing the valley road, three at least, low gears, deliberate. Meera appeared at the flooded entrance like she'd been summoned by the noise, and her face said everything the journal hadn't: *they're not passing through.*

"They'll reach the village by dark," Salunkhe said. "Then they'll start up the gorge."

Iyer closed the oilcloth over the plates and looked at Wren with the calm of a man who had already run the numbers. "Permits expire in nine days," he said. "We can't stay and we can't be seen leaving. So, Thackeray — you found us. Now help us decide what your name is going to mean."
