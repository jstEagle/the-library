## 3. Permits and Floodlines

The rain stopped an hour before midnight, and somehow that was worse.

Wren stood at the mouth of the flooded tunnel behind the temple, watching the water breathe. It rose and fell in slow swells, like something sleeping with its back to the ceiling. Meera had said the passage ran four hundred meters under the hill and came out in a laterite cutting above the old ferry ghat. She'd said it the way she said everything — flat, certain, already bored with the question.

"It floods to the roof in heavy rain," she added now, because Wren had asked. "But the rain stopped."

"For how long?"

"Until it starts again." She shrugged. "The monsoon doesn't take appointments."

Behind them, in the cave, Iyer's team was wrapping the stone plates in oilcloth and fishing net floats — anything that would float, anything that would take a knock. Eleven artifacts. Four hundred years of land grants and temple records that said, in script older than the ministry's oldest filing cabinet, that the hill had never been anyone's to sell. Wren had read them by lamplight until the letters swam. Their father's name sat in the margin of the original survey like a signature on a confession.

*Thackeray, R. — verifying officer.*

Wren had grown up learning to read terrain from a man who'd spent his career misreading it on purpose.

"We move now," Iyer said, coming up out of the cave with a lantern and a limp he was pretending wasn't there. "Dawn is when the permits lapse. Dawn is also when Deshmukh's men stop pretending to be polite. I'd rather not be underground when both happen."

"You're limping."

"I'm walking," Iyer said. "The distinction matters to me. Keep it."

Meera went first. Of course she did. She carried no light — she said the tunnel had a current, and light made you watch the water instead of feel it — and within ten steps she was a rumor in the dark. Wren followed the sound of her breathing and the rope strung along the left wall, slick with moss, and tried not to think about the weight of the hill overhead. Four hundred meters. The whole monsoon-soaked mass of it, held up by laterite and faith.

The water came up past their knees, then their thighs. Cold in a way that wasn't temperature — a cold with intent. Somewhere behind them, in the black, the others moved in single file, the artifacts lashed to a salvaged door plank that two of Iyer's surveyors steered like a barge. Every few minutes someone's lantern guttered and the dark closed in like a hand.

"Here," Meera said, materializing beside Wren. "Feel the wall."

Wren's fingers found a groove in the stone. A channel, cut clean, man-made.

"Old sluice," Meera said. "The temple people cut it to feed the paddy fields before the British, before the ministry, before everyone who thinks they discovered this hill. My grandmother's grandmother carried water through here." A pause. "So when people ask why we know the tunnels, you can tell them we don't *know* them. We remember them."

"That's a distinction lawyers wouldn't honor."

"Lawyers," Meera said, "honor paper. Water doesn't."

* * *

Iyer's journal, that same night, written by lantern in the tunnel's first air pocket while the others rested:

*The boy — Thackeray's child, though I must stop thinking of them as a child — carries the old man's eyes and none of his certainty. I have watched them read the grant documents three times, and each time they touch the margin signature the way you'd touch a burn. Good. Grief that stays hot keeps you honest.*

*I have been a government servant for thirty-one years. I believed, until this month, that the law was a river and we were its surveyors — that our work was only to map where it ran. I now understand we were mapping a river that had been dammed upstream by men with ministries in their pockets, and calling the dry bed "the truth."*

*The permits expire at dawn. Deshmukh's people think that means the land reverts to them. Let them think it. Paper expires. Stone doesn't. That is the entire strategy, and I am entrusting it to a stranger and a girl who is fifteen and fearless, because the alternative is trusting no one, and no one has ever carried anything anywhere.*

*My leg is worse. I will not write that in the part they'll read.*

* * *

They came up out of the tunnel into a cutting above the ferry ghat at 3 a.m., and the sky was the color of a bruise going yellow at the edges. Dawn was close. The river had swallowed the ghat steps whole; only the temple flagpole showed, and the tops of the coconut palms along the bank, bending like men arguing with the wind.

And on the far side of the water: lights. Three, maybe four, moving along the opposite bank. Not searching. *Waiting.* Deshmukh's men had guessed the ghat, or Meera's cousin had talked after all, and either way the crossing was dead.

"There's another way," Meera said, and Wren heard it — the flatness cracking, just slightly. "The old footbridge. Upstream. It's rope and bamboo and it hasn't been tested since last monsoon."

"Tested by whom?"

"By nobody. That's the problem."

Iyer looked at the water, then at the door plank with its eleven stones, then at Wren. "We can't all cross before light. Someone has to hold the bank and make them think we're still here, still deciding. Buy the others the hour they need to reach the village council."

"I'll hold it," Wren said.

"You don't know this terrain."

"I know *terrain*," Wren said, and heard their father in it, and didn't flinch this time. "You know paperwork. Go be a document, Iyer. Get yourself and those stones somewhere they can't be amended."

Iyer almost smiled. Then the far bank flared — a flashlight beam sweeping the water, finding nothing, sweeping again — and the moment for smiling passed.

The crossing was exactly as bad as advertised. Wren went last after the plank, testing each rope with their full weight, the river pulling at their legs like a debt collector. Halfway across, a rope strand parted with a sound like a snapped tooth, and Meera — already on the far side — talked them across in a low, bored voice, the way you'd talk someone through a fever. *Left foot. No, your other left. Don't look down, the river's ugly from above anyway.*

Wren made the far bank. Turned back.

Iyer was still on the near side, lantern raised, moving deliberately along the waterline — visible, loud, a decoy made of flesh and thirty-one years of service. The flashlight beams on the far shore converged toward him. Wren saw him set the lantern down on a rock, saw him turn toward the lights with his arms slightly out, the posture of a man who had decided to be an obstacle.

Then a shot — flat, wet, swallowed by rain starting again — and Iyer's lantern went out.

"IYER—" Wren's voice tore across the water and the rain took it.

Meera's hand closed on their arm, iron under the boredom. "The stones go to the council or he bled for nothing. *Move.*"

Wren moved. They moved through the rain and the bamboo and the dark with the oilcloth bundle against their chest and Iyer's last journal entry in their head — *paper expires, stone doesn't* — and one thought arriving with every heartbeat, arriving like the water rising:

The permits weren't the trap. The permits were the *bait*. The whole law was a trap, built by men whose names were in margins, and dawn was coming to spring it.

Behind them, downstream, a second shot echoed off the hill — and then, closer, the sound Wren had been dreading all night: engines. On the village road. Coming toward the council house.

Where the stones were supposed to go.

Where Wren was already going.
