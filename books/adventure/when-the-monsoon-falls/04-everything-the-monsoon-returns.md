## 4. Everything the Monsoon Returns

The district office smelled of wet paper and old power, the way all government buildings in the monsoon belt did—like a filing cabinet left out in the rain. Wren stood in the corridor outside the Collector's chamber, survey case under one arm, Iyer's blood still stiff on their sleeve from two nights ago, and waited to be summoned like a supplicant.

They had dressed for this. Clean shirt borrowed from Meera's father, boots scrubbed of red mud. Respectability as camouflage.

The door opened. "Thackeray. Come in."

The minister's son was younger than Wren expected—younger than Wren, maybe thirty, with the soft hands and hard eyes of a man who had never once been told no by anything that mattered. Dev Rathore. He sat behind a desk too big for him, and behind him hung a framed photograph: his father shaking hands with a white-haired man at some ribbon-cutting decades ago.

Wren knew that white hair. Had grown up under it.

"Sit," Rathore said. "You've had an eventful week."

"I've had a wet week. There's a difference."

He smiled without warmth. "The survey team you were sent to find has been located. Alive, remarkably. The ministry is grateful. Your contract is fulfilled." He slid an envelope across the desk. Payment, plus something extra—Wren could see the thickness of it through the paper. "There's also a matter of a secondary report. Unofficial findings. Artifacts, inscriptions, documentation regarding the historical status of certain concession lands. That material never reached your hands, of course. You found nothing but flood damage and abandoned gear."

Wren set the survey case on the desk between them.

Rathore's eyes flicked to it, then back up. "And?"

"And I'm deciding what it's worth."

"Name a number. A real one. Land in the hills. A position. My father's reach extends further than you've probably imagined, and I promise you his gratitude lasts longer than his anger—but only if you give me a reason for gratitude." He leaned back. "Or you can walk out of here with nothing, and I'll have men in Ambegaon by Friday asking villagers where a stranger carried a metal case. Villages talk, Thackeray. Especially villages with daughters who guide strangers through tunnels."

The threat landed exactly where he'd aimed it. Wren felt it in the chest, cold and precise, and understood suddenly why Iyer had chosen caves over courts. The law wasn't a shield here. It was the weapon.

"You know what's funny," Wren said. "Your father's signature is on the original grant. 1954. Crown land transferred to private concession—except it was never crown land. It was temple trust property, held continuously since before the survey records begin. Your family has been mining stolen ground for seventy years, and the paperwork proving it has been sitting in a flooded cave the whole time, waiting for someone competent enough to drown looking for it."

"My father says documents underwater are documents that don't exist."

"Your father taught my father that," Wren said. "I read the correspondence. It's in the case."

Something moved behind Rathore's eyes—the first honest thing in the room. Fear, quickly caged.

"The permits expire at dawn," he said quietly. "After tomorrow, the concession reverts to open tender. Anyone could win it. Do you understand what happens to Ambegaon if a company with no history there takes the lease? At least we employ them. At least we leave the temple alone."

"You've left it alone because you didn't know it was there. That's not mercy. That's ignorance."

* * *

Later—how much later Wren couldn't say—they stood alone in the room, and the rain came down so hard against the tall windows that the world beyond the glass dissolved into grey static.

Rathore had made his final offer. Wren had made none.

Wren opened the case instead. Inside: the official survey report, stamped and signed, the one that would close the file and bury the team's findings forever. Beneath it, wrapped in oilcloth, the copies—photographs of the inscriptions, scans of the 1954 grant, Iyer's journal transcribed in Wren's own hand. And beneath those, the originals themselves. Copper plates. Palm-leaf bundles sealed in wax. Seventy years of proof, dry and patient.

"You're going to burn a government document in a government office," Rathore said. "That's a crime."

"So is fraud across three generations. We're even."

Wren took the brass lamp from the corner, tipped it, and let the flame find the survey report's edge. The paper caught with a soft rush, ink curling black, the minister's signatures blooming into ash. Wren held it until the fire licked their fingers, then dropped the last burning corner into the wastebasket and watched it die.

"There," they said. "Officially, the team was lost. Officially, nothing was found. Officially, the monsoon keeps its secrets." They closed the case on the originals. "Unofficially, these belong to the people whose grandparents buried them. I'm taking them to Ambegaon. To the village council. Not to a museum, not to a ministry, not to you. If the council wants to fight you in court someday, they'll have the weapons. If they'd rather keep the temple hidden and let your lease lapse into someone else's hands—that's their choice too. Not yours. Not mine."

"You're making an enemy you can't afford."

"I grew up poor and unloved," Wren said. "Enemies are the one luxury I've always been able to afford."

At the door, Rathore spoke again, quieter. "The man in the photograph. Your father. He'd have taken the money."

"That," Wren said, "is the entire problem with my family. Goodbye."

* * *

Ambegaon received them under a sky the color of wet slate. The village council met in the schoolhouse because the council hall was knee-deep in river, and Meera sat in the front row with her arm in a sling and her chin up, daring anyone to call her a child.

Wren laid the oilcloth bundle on the teacher's desk and unwrapped it piece by piece while the elders watched: the copper plates, the palm-leaf deeds, the photographs, the transcription of Iyer's journal. Sarpanch Bhosale, a woman with a voice like gravel and a face like a locked door, turned each item over with hands that shook only slightly.

"This says the hill is ours," she said finally. "It has said so for four hundred years."

"It has. Now it says so in writing the courts will accept."

Bhosale looked around the room, at the elders, at Meera, at the empty chairs where the mining company's men used to sit when they came collecting gratitude. "We could bury it again," she said. "Let the water keep it. No tenders, no lawyers, no ministers. Just the hill and us."

"It's yours to bury," Wren said. "It's yours to raise. Either way, it stops being mine today."

The old woman studied Wren for a long moment. "You are Thackeray's child."

Not a question. The name traveled; Wren should have known.

"I am."

"He signed away our hill. You signed it back. The ledger balances." She folded the oilcloth closed with terrible gentleness. "Go home, surveyor. Or go wherever it is you go. But know that when the monsoon falls on this village, there will be a light in the temple, and it is lit for you."

* * *

The letter took three days to write, mostly because Wren kept starting it in the wrong place—at the betrayal, at the grant, at the photograph in Rathore's office—and having to cross it out and begin again at the beginning, which was never the argument, only the weather.

*Father,*

*I found the team. All eleven alive, which surprised both of us, I think—Iyer least of all; he seems constitutionally incapable of dying. The evidence is with the village now, and the survey is ash, and I burned it in a government office like a character in one of your stories, except nobody applauded.*

*I spent twenty years measuring my life in miles from you. Konkan to Cairo, Cairo to wherever you weren't. This week I learned the distance was never geographic. You taught me to read terrain, and I used it to read you, and the terrain was worse than I thought—old frauds, worn smooth, easy walking if you don't look down.*

*But here's the thing I couldn't write until now: I don't want the miles back. I want something else. I want to know if the man who taught me to love maps ever loved anything the maps couldn't hold. Answer honestly or don't answer. I'm done grading.*

*The monsoon ends next week. I'm staying through it. There's a girl here who navigates flood tunnels like other people take stairs, and a village that lit a lamp for me, and a hill that has been stolen and returned within a single lifetime, which may be a record.*

*Come see it sometime. Bring nothing. Sign nothing.*

*—Wren*

Wren sealed the envelope, walked it to the post office in the last of the rain, and mailed it standing in the doorway, watching the water sheet off the tin roof in silver ropes. Down the valley, the temple hill rose green and unclaimed out of the mist, and somewhere beneath it the river ran dark and full, carrying whatever it carried toward the sea.

For the first time in twenty years, Wren Thackeray stood still, and the distance measured itself—and found it was no distance at all.
