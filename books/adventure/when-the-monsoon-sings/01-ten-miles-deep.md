## 1. Ten Miles Deep

The river at Mile Nine ran the color of gunmetal, and Isandro Coldharbour loved it for exactly that reason. Gunmetal kept no reflections it didn't want to keep. You could lean over the side of a boat at dawn and see nothing of yourself in it—no face, no history, no name—just the slow green-black turn of the current and, far below, the suggestion of something resting on the bottom where nothing had any business resting.

She checked her gauges a fourth time. Air: full. Depth line: marked and coiled. Knife on the left calf, backup light clipped inside the left cuff where her hand would find it blind. The rules were simple, and she had built them herself, plank by plank, over ten years, and they held her weight the way a dock holds—without comment, without complaint.

*Descent at four meters per minute. Bottom time forty minutes, no exceptions. One task. One crate. No curiosity.*

Curiosity was how people drowned. Not technically—technically people drowned because of nitrogen narcosis, or fouled lines, or silt-outs, or the simple arithmetic of running out of air with thirty meters of black water overhead—but underneath all the technical reasons, if you dug down through the sediment of every diving accident report she'd ever read, you always found the same thing: someone wanted to know what was around the next bend of the wreck, or under the next panel, one more compartment past the point their plan accounted for. Curiosity was just greed wearing a lamp.

Isandro did not dive out of greed. She dove because down there, at ten miles' worth of accumulated depth logged across her body like rings in a tree, the world reduced itself to things she could count. Breath. Meters. Minutes. A man who'd once tried to interview her about her salvage work had asked how old she was, and she'd told him the truth, which was that she didn't know anymore—not in years. She knew it in miles. Four hundred and eleven miles of bottom time, give or take, since the day she'd walked away from everything with a name attached to it. She was four hundred and eleven miles old, and she intended to keep adding mileage quietly until the water finally decided she'd paid enough rent.

"Bottom's at fifty-two," said the ferryman, a leathery woman named Odile who took payment in cash and questions never. "You sure this is the spot? There's nothing here. I've run this stretch twenty years. Nothing ever sank here."

"That's why it's worth looking at."

Odile grunted, which among ferrymen of her vintage passed for philosophy, and killed the engine. The silence that followed had texture—the river slapping the hull, the creak of the anchor line paying out, somewhere upstream the long complaint of a bird Isandro couldn't name and didn't want to. She liked not knowing the names of things. Names were handles, and handles got grabbed.

She spat in her mask, rinsed it, settled it against her face, and put the regulator in her mouth. The first breath underwater was always the truest moment of her day. Above the surface, breath was automatic, thoughtless, wasted. Down there it became a transaction, deliberate, each inhale purchased and spent. She had once believed—back when she'd still been the person whose name she refused to use—that meaning lived in big gestures, public ones, the kind other people could witness. Now she knew better. Meaning lived in a single clean breath at depth, in the small perfect economy of a body doing exactly what it needed to do and nothing more.

She rolled backward off the gunwale.

The water took her the way cold water does—not welcoming, not hostile, simply present, a vast indifferent fact. Fifty-two meters was deep enough that the light changed character halfway down. It went from gold to green to a blue so dark it wasn't a color anymore but a memory of color, and by the time her fins found the mud, her world was the size of her lamp beam and the rhythm of her own breathing. Bubbles rose past her faceplate like a string of silver beads being counted off by some patient clerk.

The wreck sat where the coordinates said it would sit, which was the first wrong thing.

Navy wrecks—real ones, the ones that happened honestly—did not sit neatly. They broke apart, scattered themselves across a hundred meters of bottom, told their disaster in debris. This thing lay intact. A river barge, maybe thirty meters, sitting upright on its keel as though it had been lowered into place by a crane rather than sunk by catastrophe. Her lamp swept the hull and found the name plate deliberately ground away, a smooth rectangle of bare metal where letters should have been. Around the grinding marks, faint and fresh, someone had welded new steel over the cargo hatch seams. New welds on an old wreck. Someone had sealed this barge *after* it sank.

Isandro hung in the water above it and let the rules do their work. *One task. One crate. No curiosity.* The client—a voice on a scrambled channel, payment routed through three shell accounts, instructions delivered in flat bureaucratic prose—had been specific. A sealed crate, forward hold, port side. Recover it. Do not open it. Do not examine the vessel. Do not surface anything else. Payment on delivery, half again if completed within seventy-two hours.

She had taken stranger jobs. She had taken jobs from voices with worse manners. What she had never done—and this was the load-bearing wall of her entire reconstructed life—was ask why. Why was a question with a long tail. You pulled on it and it dragged you up through the water column, past your depth line, past your safety stops, all the way to the surface where people used names and expected answers, and Isandro Coldharbour had spent a decade making herself a creature of the middle depths, where nobody asked anything and the only currency was competence.

She swam the length of the hull to the port-side hatch. The welds were ugly, hurried work, done by someone welding underwater with equipment that fought them—bubbles frozen in the beads, spatter like iron filings. But thorough. Whoever had sealed this barge had wanted it *unopenable*, which meant whatever was inside mattered enough to spend real money burying.

Her torch came off her belt. The rules said: recover the crate. The rules did not say the crate would be behind forty centimeters of fresh weld.

She cut.

It took eleven minutes, and she counted every second of them, because counting was prayer, counting was ballast. The torch hissed; molten steel dripped and hardened instantly in the cold water, little gray teardrops raining down onto the mud. Inside the hatch, darkness absolute. Her lamp pushed a cone into it and the cone came back with almost nothing—just the pale suggestion of stacked shapes, cargo netting, and there, lashed to a pallet two meters in, the crate. Gray composite, military-issue stenciling burned off or painted over, roughly the size of a coffin. She noted the comparison and declined to feel anything about it.

The crate weighed perhaps sixty kilos dry. Wet, at depth, with no lift bag rigged yet, it was a negotiation. She ran the sling lines through the pallet ties, checked each knot twice—knots were another form of rule, knots were promises the rope made to you and kept—and inflated the lift bag in short, disciplined bursts. The crate rose off the pallet with a groan of settling cargo, and the whole barge seemed to exhale around her, a belch of silt from somewhere in the dark interior that swallowed her lamp beam whole.

Silt-out. Zero visibility. The oldest killer in the book, and the moment it happened, Isandro felt—not fear, exactly, but its well-mannered cousin, respect. She froze. Hand on the guideline. Breathing slowed, stretched, made economical. In the total blindness she became aware of the wreck around her in a way sight had prevented: the pressure of it, the held-breath hush of a sealed space, and beneath her fins, through the mud, a vibration. Faint. Rhythmic. Like machinery. Like a pump, running, down here, fifty-two meters under a river everyone official insisted had nothing in it.

Machines needed power. Power needed maintenance. Maintenance meant people.

*No curiosity.*

She counted to sixty. The silt began to thin—not clear, never clear, but translucent, a brown fog instead of a brown wall—and she followed her guideline out through the hatch, along the hull, up into the water column with the crate swinging gently below her like a sleeping animal she'd stolen from its den.

At her safety stop, hanging at five meters while her blood quietly gave back the nitrogen it had borrowed, she looked down. The wreck had already vanished into the murk, but the vibration was still there, faintly, coming up through the water into her ribs. She realized she was humming along with it without meaning to, some low note matched to the machine's frequency, and she stopped herself with the particular irritation of a woman catching her own hand on a stove.

Above her, the surface rippled with early light. Below her, a dead barge hummed. Between them, Isandro Coldharbour hung in the water, four hundred and twelve miles old, holding a rope tied to a box she had been paid very well not to open.

Odile said nothing when the crate came aboard, which was why Isandro hired her. They wrapped it in a tarp, wedged it amidships under fishing nets that had never caught a fish, and ran downriver with the current doing most of the work. By the time they reached the boathouse—an honest-to-god boathouse, wood rotting at the waterline, smelling of diesel and creosote and the sweet rot of the flooded forest—Odile had spoken exactly nine words, six of them about fuel.

"You want help moving it?"

"No."

"Didn't think so." Odile counted her money twice, folded it into a rubber band, and pushed off from the dock without goodbye. Isandro watched the ferry shrink into the morning haze and felt the ordinary satisfaction of a transaction cleanly closed. No names exchanged beyond the ones printed on money. That was the whole architecture of her life now, and it stood, and it would go on standing.

The crate sat on the boathouse floor, dripping, ticking softly as the composite cooled.

She made coffee she didn't drink. She cleaned her gear with the ritual attention of a monk at ablutions—rinse, hang, inspect, coil—and all the while the crate sat in the corner of her eye like a guest who wouldn't leave. Sixty kilos of sealed mystery. The client's money was already in the third account, untraceable, converted into diesel and air fills and the quiet annuity of her anonymity. Job done. Rules observed. The correct move—the *only* move, the move the last ten years of her life had been engineered to make possible—was to leave the crate in the corner until the client's courier collected it, and then never think about it again except as one more mile added to the log.

She opened her logbook instead. Leather, salt-swollen, pages soft as cloth. Four hundred and twelve entries going back ten years, each one stripped of anything personal: date, site, depth, bottom time, conditions, salvage recovered. No names of clients. No locations more specific than river miles. If anyone ever seized the book, they would learn a great deal about currents and nothing at all about her. She wrote today's entry in her small square handwriting, and at the bottom, where she always did, she added the running total.

*Miles logged: 412.*

Then she sat with her coffee going cold and listened to the crate tick.

Here was the thing about rules, she admitted, staring at it: they worked only as long as they were stronger than the thing they were holding back. A dam is a rule the river agreed to, temporarily. And she had built hers in a decade of calm water, and she had never once tested them against anything that actually wanted out.

The stencil under the paint-over. She'd seen it on the way out, lamp beam grazing the crate's flank, and she'd told herself she hadn't seen it, filed it under *no curiosity*, locked it in the same drawer as the humming and the fresh welds. But drawers in her head had begun, lately, to stick. She could see it now with perfect clarity: partial letters, the ghost of stenciled text showing through cheap overpaint like bones through skin.

Not navy stenciling. Navy crates said things like ORDNANCE and DO NOT DROP in block capitals with serial numbers. This had been different. Smaller lettering. Administrative. The kind of stencil a laboratory uses.

And one word, legible before the paint covered it, in careful engineering print:

**HEADWATERS.**

The word sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. Headwaters were ninety miles upstream, past the gorge, past the terraced country, in the zone the charts marked RESTRICTED in red hatching and the government maps simply left blank, as if the river were born from nothing, as if water could begin anywhere it pleased. Nobody salvaged in the headwaters. Nobody went there at all. And yet here, fifty-two meters down, behind welds fresher than her decade of exile, someone had sealed a laboratory-stenciled crate with that word on it and paid a stranger to pretend it didn't exist.

The client's instructions surfaced in her memory with new weight: *Do not open. Do not examine the vessel.* Not *do not look*. *Do not examine.* The phrasing of someone who knew exactly what examination would find.

Isandro set down the coffee. She crossed the boathouse floor—four steps, she counted them, because counting was still ballast—and knelt beside the crate, and put her palm flat on the wet composite, the way you'd check a horse or a door or a wound.

Through the shell, faint as a pulse, she felt it again. The vibration from the wreck. Except the wreck was fifty-two meters down and nine miles away, and this crate was on her floor, and it was humming too.

Something inside it was breathing. Or sampling. Or waiting.

Ten years of rules said walk away. Ten years of rules had kept her alive, anonymous, intact—a woman measured in miles, owing nothing, answerable to no one, her past drowned so deep even she only visited it in nightmares. The rules had never lied to her. The rules deserved her loyalty.

But rules, she thought, kneeling there in the diesel-smelling dark with her hand on the humming crate, were written for the river you planned for. And somewhere upstream, in country no map admitted to, the river had already changed its mind.

She went to the tool bench and came back with a pry bar.
