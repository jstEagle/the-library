## 2. The Crate That Breathes

The crate sat on the workbench all night like a guest who wouldn't leave.

Isandro had carried it up from the dive locker at dusk, when the light through the boathouse windows went the color of weak tea, and she had told herself she would not open it. That was the whole arrangement. Recover, deliver, don't ask. The navy man—Commander Voss, a name like a door closing—had paid half up front and half on delivery, and the terms had been spoken plainly enough: *You bring it up, you bring it in, you don't look inside.* She had agreed the way she agreed to most things, with a nod that cost nothing and promised less.

But rules were only comfortable when they made sense, and this one didn't. Salvage contracts said *don't keep*. They said *don't sell*. They almost never said *don't look*, because looking was how divers died—curiosity was a hazard of the trade, priced into every job—and a client who feared her eyes more than her hands was a client who had something to hide that wasn't valuable. Valuable things got insured. Valuable things came with manifests, with paperwork, with lawyers. This crate had come up from forty feet of cold green water wrapped in a tarp and a lie, and the lie had a weight to it she could feel across the room.

She counted her life in miles logged, not years lived. Eleven thousand, four hundred and six miles of bottom time, if she was honest about the tally, and she was honest about very little else. Miles were clean. Miles added up. A mile was a thing you could survive and then never think about again. Years were different. Years accumulated. Years had names attached to them, and faces, and one particular year ten miles back that she kept sealed the way she kept everything sealed—tight, dark, pressurized, safe.

The crate breathed. That was the thing she couldn't get past. Not literally—though at two in the morning, alone in a boathouse with rain starting against the tin roof, she'd have sworn she heard it—a slow exhalation through the gasket seam, a sigh of trapped air finding its way out after three days underwater. Air from depth. Air that had been down there with whatever the wreck really was, because the wreck was not what the charts said it was either. She'd known that before her fins touched water. The sonar shadow had been wrong for a cargo hauler—too tall, too narrow, hull lines like something built to be fast rather than heavy—and the riverbed around it had been scoured in ways rivers don't scour, as if something had been dragged, or dropped, or dug.

She made herself tea she didn't drink. She checked the mooring lines twice. She sat in the canvas chair by the wood stove and read the same page of a tide almanac for an hour, and the crate sat there under the workbench lamp, and the rain thickened, and finally—somewhere past three, in the dead hour when even her discipline gets drowsy—she stood up, crossed the room, and put her palm flat on the lid.

Cold. Colder than the room, colder than it should have been, holding its deep-water chill like a grudge. The wood was good oak, marine-grade, brass corners gone green. No stencil. No serial. Just a single stamped mark near the latch, small enough that she'd missed it underwater: a circle with a vertical bar through it, and beneath the bar, in letters worn nearly smooth, a word she had to tilt the lamp to read.

*RESTRICTED.*

Her thumb moved over the letters once, twice. Then she went to the tool wall and took down the pry bar, and told herself it was only inspection. Only due diligence. A diver had a right to know what she'd been breathing next to, what she'd hauled through the water column strapped to her lift bag, what might be leaking or corroding or ticking. It wasn't curiosity. It was safety. She was very good at telling herself things; it was, after all, the primary skill of her last ten years.

The lid came free with a sound like a held breath released.

* * *

Inside: not weapons, not gold, not anything worth a salvage fee at all.

Styrofoam cradles, molded to their contents, still faintly damp. Glass vials nested in rows, twenty of them, each sealed with wax and labeled in a small precise hand—not typed, *handwritten*, which was wrong for any official operation, official operations didn't trust handwriting—each vial holding liquid that caught the lamplight strangely. Water, obviously. River water. But not river water the way she knew it. The samples from the lower reaches ran amber-brown with tannin, ordinary as tea. These ran clear, and in the clearest ones something shimmered at the meniscus, a faint oily iridescence, like the skin of a soap bubble, like the sheen on a puddle over gasoline. She lifted one vial to the lamp and turned it, and the light bent through it with a rainbow edge that made her teeth ache.

She set it down carefully, wiped her fingers on her trousers without deciding to.

Beneath the vials, flat and wrapped in oilcloth: charts. Real charts, linen-backed, the expensive kind survey offices used, and she unrolled the first one across the bench and weighted the corners with wrenches and stood over it for a long time without moving.

It was the river. Her river—the one she'd been diving in, living beside, hiding along—for six years now. But drawn upstream, past the limits of her own mental map, past the last marker buoy and the last fishing camp, into country she'd never had reason to enter and had quietly promised herself she never would. The headwaters. Forty miles of gorge and flooded forest where the maps in the chandlery simply stopped, printed with a blank space and a border line and the same word she'd just pried off a crate lid.

*RESTRICTED.*

But whoever had drawn this chart hadn't stopped at the border. The linework ran straight through the blank space, confident and dense, and it was full of things that shouldn't have been there. Contour lines describing terraces cut into the gorge walls—terraces too regular to be natural, too stepped, too squared. Shafts, marked with the little crossed-pick symbol miners use, a dozen of them, two dozen, clustered along the upper bends like burrows. A tailings pond drawn as a blue shape that no blue shape should be, sitting above the aquifer line with no liner indicated, no berm, no containment—just sitting there on the chart like a wound left open. And everywhere, in the margins, in the title block, in the fine print of the scale bar: stamps. Government stamps, the kind she recognized from harbor authority documents, circular and officious, each one dated.

She checked the dates three times, because the first two times she assumed she'd misread.

The oldest stamp predated the wreck. Predated the navy's "cargo loss." Predated, by weeks, the official story she'd been paid to help bury. Which meant the wreck wasn't the beginning of anything. The wreck was a *response*—a cover story laid down after the fact, a body dumped to explain a wound. Something had already happened upriver, something bad enough that someone had needed a sunken ship to blame it on, and the ship they'd chosen had conveniently sunk, and she had conveniently been available to make sure it stayed sunk and gave up nothing.

She thought about the water at the wreck site. How clear it had been. Too clear. She'd noted it at the time and filed it under *nothing*—visibility running thirty feet where the season allowed eight, the silt settled as though the river had been filtered. Rivers downstream of mines don't run clear. Rivers downstream of mines run the color of their wounds.

Unless the mine was upstream of the wreck, and the wreck sat below a weir of settled poison, and the clarity was just the calm above the fall.

She stood in her boathouse at four in the morning with rain hammering the roof and the lamp burning low, and she felt the thing she had spent ten years building her life to avoid feeling: the pull. Curiosity was a hazard of the trade, priced into every job, and she had always paid it gladly because paying it kept her sane—but this was past curiosity now. This was the specific vertigo of realizing the ground under your mooring is not the ground you thought it was. Six years she had lived on this river because it was nowhere, because it was forgettable, because nobody important looked at it. And somebody important had been looking at it the whole time—looking hard enough to stamp it, chart it, sink a story over it—and her anonymity, her one possession, had been sitting inside someone else's secret this entire time like a coin lost in a mattress.

The stove ticked. Rain ran off the eaves in ropes. One of the vials, sitting in its foam cradle, seemed to hold the lamplight a fraction longer than physics strictly allowed, and Isandro Coldharbour—who did not open crates, who did not ask questions, who logged miles instead of years—heard herself say aloud, to the empty boathouse, in a voice roughened by disuse:

"What are you?"

The river outside said nothing, but it said it loudly.

* * *

She slept badly and woke with salt-crusted eyes and the decision already made, which annoyed her, because she liked to at least witness her own decisions. By seven she had repacked the crate exactly as found—vials in their cradles, charts rolled and wrapped, lid seated, pry marks turned toward the wall—and by eight she was standing in the chandlery doorway waiting for the harbor office to open, because the arrangement required delivery today, and she wanted to watch Commander Voss's face when he took custody of it. Faces were data. Faces were the closest thing to honesty the navy sold.

Voss didn't come. A lieutenant came instead—young, rain-wet, too careful with his hands—and signed for the crate without opening it, without weighing it, without so much as glancing at the seal, and that told her more than any face would have. You don't sign blind for something worthless. You sign blind for something you've been ordered not to understand.

"Any complications?" the lieutenant asked, pen poised.

"Routine recovery," she said. "Hull's a cargo hauler, sits the way the charts say. Nothing down there but mud and paperwork."

He wrote it down. He believed it, or he'd been instructed to believe it, and either way she watched the crate go into a navy truck under a tarp and felt the strange double sensation of a job well done and a door closing behind it. Half her fee would arrive by evening. The other half on confirmation of receipt. Clean. Finished. Done.

She told herself that all the way back to the boathouse, and she almost managed it, right up until the radio crackled.

* * *

The set was old, salvaged off a trawler, mounted above the chart table where she took weather reports and the occasional contract call. It hissed the way it always hissed, static like sand poured on glass, and then a voice came through it that she did not know and could not place—male, middle-aged, clipped in the way of people who learned diction somewhere institutional and have been shedding it ever since.

"Salvage vessel *Kestrel*, this is a contracted inquiry. Do you copy?"

She let it hiss for three full seconds. Nobody had this frequency. Nobody had this *name*—she'd registered the boat under a name that led nowhere, bought through a lawyer who asked nothing, and the callsign was a private joke between her and her own paranoia. Whoever was calling had done homework.

"Copy," she said finally. "Identify."

"Consider me the second half of your last invoice." A pause, static filling it like water filling a boot. "You recovered a crate Tuesday from the site at Mile Nine. I'm authorized to offer you a follow-on job. Same terms."

"I don't take jobs from voices."

A dry sound that might have been a laugh. "Fair. I can't give you a face yet—that's part of the terms, and the terms are the only protection either of us has. But I'll give you this much: the crate you pulled up shouldn't exist. The survey that produced those samples was struck from the record eleven weeks ago. Every chart you saw under that lid is supposed to have been burned. Someone paid good money to make that work disappear, and someone else—" the voice paused, choosing words like footing on scree, "—someone else wants to know why badly enough to hire the only diver who's been on that wreck and walked away."

Her hand had gone still on the chart table. Outside, the rain had thinned to mist, and the river lay gray and patient, keeping everything it knew.

"The second site," the voice said. "Mile Fourteen, east bank, where the creek comes in. There's equipment down there that needs recovering. Instruments, records. Same rules as before: recover, deliver, don't ask."

"And if I ask anyway?"

Another pause, longer. When the voice came back it had dropped its institutional polish entirely, and underneath it she heard something she recognized, because she heard it in her own head some nights: fear, wearing competence as a coat.

"Then I'd say you're the first person in eleven weeks to ask the right question. Mile Fourteen, *Kestrel*. Tide window opens tomorrow at dawn. There's a bonus in it for speed, and a great deal of trouble in it for anyone else who gets there first."

The channel clicked dead. Static poured back in, anonymous as rain.

Isandro stood at the chart table a long while, the mist silvering the windows, the river breathing against the pilings below her floorboards. She should burn the memory of those charts out of her head. She should take the fee, log another mile, stay sealed. That was the rule. That had always been the rule, and the rule had kept her alive and invisible for ten years, and invisibility was the only currency she had ever trusted.

But the crate had breathed, and she had opened it, and somewhere between the iridescent vials and the stamped dates she had stopped being a woman who found wrecks and started being a woman who knew something. Knowledge doesn't reseal. She knew that better than anyone—she was, herself, a sealed thing that had failed to reseal, once, ten years ago, at terrible cost.

Mile Fourteen. East bank, where the creek comes in.

She pulled the tide almanac toward her, found tomorrow's window, and began, without quite admitting it, to plan a dive.
