## 3. Gear With No Ghosts

The river changed its mind three miles before I did.

I'd been reading water since before I could read words—my father's doing, back when there was a father and a river that hadn't learned to lie—and the current below the second site spoke in a dialect I didn't trust. It moved too evenly. Big rivers are supposed to stutter, to braid and argue with themselves around boulders and deadfall, but this stretch ran smooth as poured glass, as if something upstream had sanded off every irregularity. As if the water were trying very hard to behave.

Tamás would have had a word for it. I hadn't hired him yet; that came later, though at the time I didn't know it was later. That morning it was just me and the boat and the crate lashed under my tarp like a secret I'd stopped pretending I wasn't keeping.

The coordinates from the radio message put the second site in a gorge the maps called *unimproved terrain*, which is bureaucratic language for a place nobody wanted to spend money describing. Steep walls of black shale. A canopy so thick the river ran green even at noon, light coming down in coins that never quite reached the surface. I motored up against a current that should have fought me harder than it did, and the whole time the hair on my forearms stood at attention like a crew of tiny sailors who knew something I didn't.

I logged the miles out of habit. Eleven. Twelve. Thirteen. The numbers were how I kept time—not years, which accumulate whether you want them or not, but miles, which you have to earn. Ten years of salvage work had taught me that distance was the only honest ledger. You could fake a name. You could fake a smile for a harbormaster. You could not fake the space between where you started and where you stood.

Fourteen miles upriver, I found the camp.

* * *

I smelled woodsmoke first, thin and cold, the ghost of a fire rather than a living one. Then I saw the canvas: a dome tent half-hidden under fern cover, guy-lines still taut, pitched with the competence of people who expected to come back to it. There was a drying rack with no clothes on it. A mess kit hanging from a branch, upside down, dry. A latrine trench dug properly downwind and filled in.

Everything about it said *we'll be back by dark.*

Nobody had been back by dark. Nobody had been back by any dark, judging by the leaf litter drifted against the tent's north wall—a season's worth, maybe more, brown and matted, while the rest of the forest floor wore this year's bright fall. The camp existed in its own weather system of abandonment. Time had walked around it carefully, the way you step around a sleeping animal.

I beached the boat and stood on the bank for a long moment, doing what I always do before entering someone else's story: I counted exits. The gorge wall behind, climbable but loud. The river ahead, my way out. A game trail threading east through the ferns. Three exits. Good enough.

"Hello the camp," I said anyway, because protocol is protocol, and because part of me—the part I keep fed on rations of denial—still hoped for an answer.

The gorge answered instead: my own voice, doubled and flattened, coming back to me sounding like a stranger's.

The fire pit held ash and three stones cracked from heat. Beside it, arranged with a neatness that made my chest hurt, sat four pairs of boots. Not scattered, not kicked off in panic. Set side by side, toes pointing at the river, laces tucked in—the way you leave boots when you intend to step back into them in an hour. Two pairs were government issue, the kind with the survey bureau's stamp embossed at the heel. One pair was civilian hiking gear, expensive and worn soft. The fourth pair was small. Women's size, rubber-soled, the left one patched at the toe with neat stitches.

Boots by the fire. No bodies. No tracks leading away—I checked, walking a slow spiral through the mud and duff in my own dive boots, reading the ground the way I read water. There were tracks leading *in*: boot prints, weeks old, hardened into the clay like fossils. There were tracks around the camp, the ordinary circulation of four people living in a small space. And then, at a line maybe ten meters from the fire pit, the prints simply stopped being prints. Not washed out. Not overgrown. Just—ended, mid-stride, two of them, as if the walkers had stepped off the edge of the world.

Beyond that line the ground continued, ordinary and innocent, offering no opinion on where they'd gone.

I crouched at the boundary for a long time. My breath fogged despite the warmth. Because here was the other thing, the thing my skin had been trying to tell me since mile eleven: the air was warm. Not monsoon-warm, not valley-bowl warm. Warm in a way that rose from below, faintly, steadily, like standing near an oven door. And the nearer I got to the river, the warmer it got.

* * *

The instruments were still running.

That was the detail that undid me—not the boots, not the vanishing tracks, but a solar panel angled on a rock shelf above the waterline, its cable running down to a data logger bolted into the shale inside a waterproof case. The case's indicator light blinked green. Steady. Patient. Alive.

I knelt beside it and wiped the condensation off the little screen with my sleeve. The interface was standard survey bureau hardware—I'd salvaged enough drowned equipment to read these menus the way other people read menus in restaurants. Water temperature, logging at fifteen-minute intervals, going back ninety-one days. I scrolled the graph and felt the dread arrive the way I'd felt the warm air arrive: quietly, from below, without any single moment I could point to and say *there, that's when it started.*

Ninety-one days ago the river here ran nineteen degrees. Cold, high-altitude water, exactly right for the elevation. Over the following weeks the line climbed—slowly, then not slowly. Twenty-two degrees. Twenty-four. Twenty-six. In the last ten days of record it had plateaued at twenty-eight and a half degrees, holding there with the flat confidence of a number that has stopped apologizing for itself.

Twenty-eight and a half degrees. Nine degrees above what this river had any business being. Somewhere upstream, the water was being heated—or fed from something heated—and it had been happening for three months, and the survey team had watched it happen on screens exactly like this one, and then they had taken off their boots, set them neatly by the fire, and walked into the trees without finishing their work.

Except the logger said they *hadn't* finished. The last entries weren't just temperature. Someone had reconfigured the unit eighty-six days ago to add a second channel. Turbidity. Dissolved solids. And a third channel labeled only with a serial number I didn't recognize, spitting out readings in a range that made no sense for any natural parameter—until I noticed the values tracked the temperature almost perfectly, rising and falling together, two curves holding hands.

Whatever was warming the river was also carrying something in it. Something the team had thought worth building a whole new sensing channel to chase.

I sat back on my heels. Above me the canopy let down its coins of green light, and somewhere a bird made a sound like a hinge that needed oiling, and everything looked exactly like a wilderness is supposed to look, and nothing was fine.

* * *

I went into the water because it was the only thing left to interrogate.

This is the truth about me that the miles have carved: I trust water more than I trust ground. Ground can hide things indefinitely—bones, graves, the names of people who used to be somebody. Water at least shows you its face. So I kitted up on the bank, checking my gear twice because the camp had put a static charge of wrongness in my hands, and rolled backward off the rocks into the current.

The first thing was the temperature. I've dove cold rivers all my working life; I know how they hold their chill against your skin even in summer, how the thermocline sits a few meters down like a lid. This water was bathwater at the surface. At five meters it was *warmer*. At ten, warmer again. Every rule inverted. I was diving downward into heat, and every instinct I owned screamed that heat means vents, vents mean geology, geology means the survey team was here chasing something that comes out of the earth—and the earth around here, according to the charts in the crate I'd been paid not to open, was marked RESTRICTED in letters that took up half the page.

The second thing was the light.

At twelve meters, in water that should have been tea-dark with tannin, I could see clearly—too clearly. The river carried a faint luminosity, not bioluminescence, nothing alive in its flicker, but a diffuse pearly sheen, as if the water itself had been dusted with something that refused to settle. I cupped a hand and scooped some toward my mask. In the beam of my torch the water swirled with a suspension so fine it was almost color—except where the light caught it edge-on, and then it flashed iridescent, a slick of rainbow sheen rolling through my fingers like the skin on cooled milk.

Oil wouldn't do that, not dispersed this fine, not this deep. Mine tailings might. Certain chemical effluents might. I wasn't a chemist. I was a woman hovering in warm, glowing water, watching a river wear jewelry it should never have owned.

I followed the sheen. It moved with the current, and the current here moved *up*—not the whole river, but a column of it, a slow vertical drift along the gorge wall, rising from somewhere below the reach of my torch like smoke rising through still air. Warm water rising. Suspended load rising. Everything rising from a source I couldn't see, deeper than my light, deeper than my comfort, down where the gorge floor dropped away into a darkness that swallowed my beam whole.

I hung there at the edge of that darkness for a while, breathing hard through my regulator, listening to the only sound in the world: my own air, in and out, in and out.

Below me, the river went down forever, warm and glowing, like a throat.

I ascended slowly, doing my safety stops with a discipline that felt absurd—decompression stops, in nine degrees of excess warmth, as if the danger here were bubbles in my blood and not the fact that I now knew, in the wordless way divers know things, that whatever the survey team had found, they'd found it *down*, and the finding had not gone well for them.

* * *

Back on shore, I did the thing I'm best at, which is making lists.

The camp gave me plenty. Four people, based on the boots and the four sleeping rolls inside the tent—sleeping rolls still rolled, which meant they'd left expecting to sleep elsewhere, or never expecting to sleep anywhere again. Rations for six weeks, mostly untouched. A rifle rack with no rifles. A satellite phone charging dock with no phone. And pinned under a stone on the mess table, protected from the damp with the care people give to things they mean to return for, a field notebook.

I told myself I wouldn't take it. I told myself a lot of things that day.

The notebook was water-stamped paper, survey bureau stock, and the handwriting inside belonged to at least two different people—one cramped and precise, one looser, given to underlining. The precise hand had logged instrument calibrations, weather, mileage. The loose hand had logged something else entirely. Entries like:

*Day 12 — Third anomaly sample. Column at the wall face persists. Dr. V says don't spec the source until we have chain-of-custody sorted. She keeps looking downstream like she expects company.*

*Day 15 — Found the old adit. It's not on ANY map. Timbered, recent timber. Generator fuel smell inside. We are NOT the first ones up here and whoever was first did not file a report.*

*Day 17 — R. wants to pull out. V. says we finish the transect or the data dies with us. Note: the water at the intake sample reads 40x baseline for [illegible]. Sending priority burst tonight.*

And then, in the cramped hand, on the last written page, a single line:

*They know we know. Moving camp at dawn. If anyone reads this, the answer isn't up the river. It's under it.*

No date after that. No signature. The notebook ended the way the boot prints ended: mid-stride, at the edge of the explainable.

I sat on the mess table's bench with the notebook in my hands and the warm wind moving through the gorge, and I understood that I had crossed a line I couldn't uncross. A week ago I was a diver with a rule: recover, deliver, don't ask. The rules had kept me alive through the years I don't talk about, kept the name I buried safely buried. But rules are like riverbanks—they hold only until the water rises, and mine had risen past the top of everything, and now I was standing in the flood holding a dead-or-disappeared survey team's notebook, having breathed their warm strange river, having read the last sentence of a person who may well have written it knowing no one ever would.

*The answer isn't up the river. It's under it.*

I looked at the water. Green-gold in the afternoon light, smooth as glass, wearing its faint iridescence like a woman wearing another woman's coat. Beautiful. Wrong. Ninety-one days of data blinking patiently on a machine whose owners had stepped off the face of the earth.

Somewhere downstream, in a boathouse, sat a sealed crate full of geological charts with RESTRICTED stamped across the headwaters, and I had already broken the seal on that particular obedience. The navy had paid me not to open it. Someone else had paid me, in a voice I couldn't place, to salvage this site—and had not answered the radio since.

I should have packed the notebook, fired up the engine, and run fourteen miles downstream to the nearest town with a telephone, and handed the whole thing to someone whose job it was to carry such things. That was the smart play. That was the play of a woman who counts miles instead of years, who has built her whole life on the principle that other people's catastrophes are ballast you jettison at the first sign of weather.

Instead I sat in an abandoned camp as the light went bronze and the warm wind came down the gorge smelling faintly, unmistakably, of diesel and hot metal, and I listened to the river move past with its cargo of glow, and I heard—very distinctly, very quietly, from somewhere far upstream around the bend where the cliffs narrowed to a throat—the sound of machinery.

Not generators. Not boats. Something rhythmic, heavy, patient. Pumping.

The survey team had sent a priority burst eighty-six days ago and vanished. The river had been dying from underneath for three months. And the thing under the river was still running, still working, still feeding its warm bright poison into every mile downstream, toward villages and towns and mouths, toward everyone who had ever trusted this water, which was everyone.

I closed the notebook and put it inside my jacket, against my skin, where I keep the things that have decided my life for me.

Then, from the downstream side of the camp—from the direction of my boat, the direction I had come from, the direction from which no one should be coming—a voice said, "Don't shoot. Please. I need to know if you found the transect data."

A man stepped out of the fern cover with his hands raised and his eyes red-rimmed from more than the river wind, and he was looking at me the way drowning people look at anything that floats.

He was carrying a survey bureau case, and he was alone, and he said, "My name is Wren Pell. Everyone else is gone."
