## 4. A Surveyor Named Pell

Wren Pell had learned to measure her failures in ink.

Every map she'd ever drawn carried a little of it—the smudge of a thumb dragged across wet contour lines, the ghost of an eraser gone gray with second thoughts, the red circles her examiners at the Cartographic Institute had inked around her errors like wounds they wanted her to remember. *Disgraced* was a word that followed her now, printed small beneath her name on the professional registries, and she had come to think of it as a kind of altitude: a low place from which every direction was uphill.

She sat in the survey team's base camp at Mile Nine with that word wrapped around her like a wet coat, watching the river run past in the blue hour before dawn, and told herself—for the hundredth time, for the thousandth—that staying behind had been the responsible decision.

The fire had burned down to a red eye in its ring of stones. Around it, the camp held the particular silence of a place recently abandoned by people who expected to come back. Four tents still stood, guy-lines taut, rain flies buttoned down against weather no one had yet met. Mugs hung from their hooks beside the mess tarp. Okafor's field boots stood side by side outside his tent, laces tucked in, the way he always left them because he said bare feet in the morning grass were the only honest thing left in his life. Someone's laundry dried on a line between two alders—a shirt, two pairs of socks, a bandana—stiffening in the cold.

They had walked out three days ago to run the upper transects. Six of them, into the restricted headwaters, carrying instruments and rations for five days. They had promised to radio in at dusk each evening.

The radio had spoken twice. Then not at all.

Pell pulled her coat tighter and looked at the gear they'd left behind—the gear you leave behind only if you believe you're coming back. The backup theodolite in its padded case. The spare batteries, racked and labeled in Okafor's careful hand. The medical kit, unopened. And the instruments that were *not* spares, the ones that should have gone upstream with the team but hadn't, because Pell had argued—argued with her whole disgraced, defensive heart—that running the full array through the gorge was redundant, wasteful, a waste of good equipment on terrain the ministry had already declared closed.

She had won that argument. She had been so proud of winning it.

Now the spectrophotometer sat in its case beside her tent like a sleeping animal, and every time she looked at it she thought: *if I hadn't won, I would have gone with them. If I hadn't won, I would be missing too, instead of sitting here being the only person in the world who knows where they went.*

The river made its sound. It had made the same sound since she arrived, a broad hush broken by the tick and knock of debris against the pilings of the old sampling dock, but in the last day Pell had begun to hear something wrong inside it—a thinness, a note missing, the way a room sounds wrong after a piece of furniture is removed. She couldn't have said what the river usually sounded like; she'd been at this camp less than two weeks. But her body knew. Her body had grown up on rivers, on the brown patient water of the delta country where her mother fished, and it knew this one was speaking with something caught in its throat.

She rose, stiff-kneed, and walked down to the dock to do what she had done every morning since the silence began: check the sample bottles.

The team had moored a string of sampling buoys off the dock's end, each one feeding a glass bottle on a timed valve. Routine hydrology. Pell had volunteered for the collection runs partly to be useful and mostly to have somewhere to put her hands while the radio stayed quiet. She hauled the first bottle up now, uncapped it, held it to the graying light.

It should have been tea-colored. This stretch of the Coldharbour ran tannin-brown all summer, stained by the flooded forest upstream, opaque as steeped leaves.

This water was almost clear. And in the early light, tilted against the sky, it held a faint iridescence—an oily rainbow sheen that broke apart when she turned the bottle and knitted itself back together when she stilled it. Not oil. She knew oil; everyone who worked rivers knew oil. This was finer, more obedient, a shimmer that lived *in* the water rather than on it, as though something dissolved had decided to catch the light on its way past.

Pell stood very still, the bottle trembling slightly in her grip, and felt the shape of her disgrace rise up in her memory with terrible clarity.

Three years ago she had signed a map she knew was wrong.

That was the whole of it, the seed of everything. A mining concern in the northern ranges had retained her firm to chart their lease boundaries, and the survey data had shown—plainly, undeniably—that their planned tailings works sat over a seasonal floodplain that would carry contamination straight into three villages' water supply. Her supervisor had received a call. The call had changed the numbers. And Wren Pell, twenty-six years old and hungry for a permanent posting, had redrawn the contours to match, had inked the lie in her own precise hand, had even—this was the part that woke her at night—*beautified* it, smoothing the lines until the falsified map was lovelier than the true one would ever have been.

Two children in those villages had died of what the water carried that spring. The inquiry that followed had found the falsification, found her initials in the margin of the revision, and found her, when they called her to testify, unable to say anything except *I was told to.* The registry struck her name with a notation she could recite verbatim: *Professional judgment compromised under commercial pressure.*

She had told herself a story afterward, the way disgraced people do: that she'd been young, that she hadn't known the consequences, that she was a different person now. And mostly she believed it, in daylight, in company. But standing on the dock at Mile Nine holding a bottle of shining water, she understood suddenly and completely that she was standing at the beginning of the same map again. Same shimmer. Same corporate fingerprints waiting to be found upstream. Same choice, offered a second time—and this time six of her colleagues were already inside the question, already silent, already possibly dead because a woman with her history of obedient cartography had argued the expensive instruments out of the field pack.

"I'm not signing anything this time," she said aloud, to the river, to the bottles, to no one. Her voice came out cracked and small. "Whatever it is. Whatever they offer. Nothing gets signed."

The river hushed on, thin-voiced, keeping whatever it remembered.

* * *

She spent the morning doing the work of the desperate: making order.

If the team came back—or if anyone came looking for them—there should be records. She inventoried the camp, logged the buoy readings hour by hour in her field book, photographed the iridescent samples against a scale card, sealed duplicates in wax and hid them in three separate places: the false bottom of the instrument crate, a waterproof cache sunk under the dock pilings, and a hollow in the big alder that she'd noticed her first week and never mentioned to anyone, because even then some instinct had told her that a woman with her record should keep copies of things.

At noon she climbed the ridge behind camp with the signal antenna and tried the relay frequencies again. Static, and the far-off mutter of the government band, and once—she was nearly sure—a fragment of voice, compressed and garbled, gone before she could tune it. She logged the time anyway. She was, whatever else was true of her, still a meticulous woman. Shame had not taken that from her. Shame had, if anything, sharpened it: she recorded everything now, triple-checked everything, initialed nothing without reading it four times.

From the ridge she could see a long way upriver—the gorge narrowing, the terraced slopes rising beyond it into cloud, and somewhere up there, past the line where the official maps simply stopped, her six colleagues walking into whatever the ministry had closed. She raised the binoculars and swept the upper water out of habit and saw, for the third time that week, a boat.

It was a long way off, a dark sliver working upcurrent against the white noise of the rapids, moving with the kind of economy that meant skill. Not a patrol boat—patrols came down from the north checkpoint and wore their engines loudly, announcing themselves. This was a smaller craft, low in the water, and it moved the way her mother's fishing boat used to move, reading the current instead of fighting it.

Pell watched it until the haze took it, and made a decision she would later understand she had actually made years ago, on the day she first inked a lie into a map and swore, privately, never again.

* * *

The boat came down to Mile Nine near dusk.

Pell heard it before she saw it—the change in the river's voice as a hull displaced it—and she was already standing at the end of the dock when the craft slid into view around the willow bend, riding low, engine cut, drifting the last hundred meters on momentum. A woman stood at the stern with a push-pole, and even at distance there was something about her stance that Pell recognized from a lifetime of watching people work water: the balanced stillness, the weight settled into the hips, the total absence of wasted motion. This was not a tourist. This was not a bureaucrat. This was someone who lived half her life underwater or wished she did.

The boat bumped the dock tires. The woman looked up.

She had the kind of face that gave away nothing on purpose—weathered, watchful, eyes the flat gray-green of deep water—but Pell had spent three years being assessed by inquiry boards and had developed a connoisseur's ear for other people's silences. This woman's silence had a name buried in it. Pell knew the species. She owned one herself.

"Looking for someone?" the woman asked. Her voice was low, unhurried, faintly amused, as though the whole river were an inconvenience she'd agreed to tolerate.

"Are you offering?" Pell countered, and saw the flicker of surprise, quickly banked, and felt a small coal of courage light in her chest. Good. Conversation. Conversation could be steered.

"Isandro," the woman said, after a pause that measured Pell precisely. "Salvage. I've got a contract at a wreck downstream. You're a long way from any wreck."

"Wren Pell." She didn't offer a title. Titles were landmines. "Cartographer." She watched the word land and watched Isandro file it without comment. "Base camp for a ministry survey team. Six people went upriver four days ago. They've gone silent."

"And you want me to go find them." Isandro set the pole down and finally, deliberately, looked at the camp behind Pell—the neat tents, the drying laundry, the boots outside Okafor's tent. Pell watched her read it the way a diver reads a hull: systematically, looking for the breach. "You've got a radio. You've got a government behind you with helicopters and budget lines. Why bring a stranger into it?"

"Because the government isn't answering either." It came out rawer than Pell intended. She made herself continue, level. "I've filed. Three times. The relay station acknowledges receipt and takes no action. The checkpoint at Mile Twelve has doubled its guard. When I asked the district office why a hydrological survey needed a military cordon, the clerk looked at me the way you look at someone who's asked a question they were told you wouldn't ask." She pulled the sample bottle from her coat pocket and held it out. "And because of this. Off our own buoys, below your supposed wreck. That's not tannin. That's not silt. I've tested it with the kit we have—pH drifting, conductivity off the scale, and a spectrograph signature I can't match to anything natural. Something upstream is dissolving into this river, Ms. Coldharbour, and my colleagues are upstream of it, and every institution that should care has decided not to."

Isandro took the bottle. Held it to the last of the light. For a long moment the only sounds were the river and the creak of the dock, and Pell stood in that silence feeling like a specimen herself—aware of how she must look: rumpled, sleepless, disgraced, begging.

"You said cartographer," Isandro said at last, not looking up from the water. "Ministry survey teams don't usually hire private salvage. So either you're lying to me about something, or you're the one person on that team nobody important listens to." Her eyes came up, and they were not unkind, which was somehow worse. "Which is it?"

The old shame rose in Pell's throat like floodwater. She could feel the registry notation hovering in the air between them, the red-circled errors, the smoothed and beautified lie. Every instinct honed by three years of hiding said: deflect, minimize, give the stranger nothing.

But the bottle was shining in Isandro's hand, and six people were silent upstream, and Wren Pell had sworn, on a spring night three years ago with two children's names in the inquiry report, that the next time the water was wrong she would say so out loud, whatever it cost.

"Both," she said. "I'm the one nobody listens to. And I'm lying to you about one thing—I'm not here on the ministry's behalf anymore. I'm here despite them." She made herself hold the gray-green gaze. "My name is on a professional blacklist. I falsified a map once, years ago, because I was told to, and people were hurt because of what I helped hide. I've spent three years being small and careful and invisible." Her voice steadied as she went, the way a river steadies past a rock. "I am done being invisible. My team is up that gorge. The water is poisoned. Somebody is paying a great deal of money for both of those facts to stay quiet, and I think you already know it, because you're diving wrecks the navy swears don't exist."

Something moved behind Isandro's eyes—recognition, maybe, or the reflexive flinch of a woman hearing her own concealment named aloud. She looked away first, out at the darkening water, and for a moment her whole guarded posture seemed to shift, like ballast rearranging.

"The wreck's real enough," she said quietly. "What was in it isn't salvage." She turned the sample bottle slowly in her fingers, watching the sheen turn. "You said the checkpoint doubled its guard."

"This week."

"And your team went up before that."

"Four days ago. Before the guards, before the silence, before—" Pell gestured at the bottle. "Before all of it got loud."

Isandro was quiet for a long moment. Downstream, miles away, Pell knew, lay the navy's nonexistent wreck and whatever sealed crate had come out of it; she didn't know how she knew, only that the woman on the dock carried knowledge the way divers carried nitrogen—in solution, under pressure, waiting for the right depth to release it.

"If I go up there," Isandro said finally, "and we find what I think we're going to find, there's no coming back down to a quiet life. Not for you, not for me. Whoever owns that water will own the search for us too." She looked at Pell directly. "So ask me again. Knowing that."

Pell's hands were shaking. She let them. Some things deserved to be witnessed shaking.

"Please," she said. "Come upstream with me. Find my people. Find what's killing the river. I'll draw you every map you need—I'll get us through the gorge blind if I have to. I have spent three years being the person who kept quiet, and I am asking you, begging you, not to make me spend a fourth."

The last light went off the water. Somewhere up the gorge, in the country the maps refused to name, her colleagues were silent, and the river carried its secret shine past the dock and away toward ten thousand people who had no idea what they were drinking.

Isandro looked upriver for a long time. Then she stepped back off the dock onto her deck, and said, in a voice that had already decided:

"We leave at first light. And you'd better know the way, cartographer—because I've got a feeling nobody's mapped what's up there honestly in years."

Pell exhaled, and it felt like surfacing.

"One more thing," Isandro added, reaching for her pole, her eyes already on the current. "We'll need a guide. Someone who knows these waters above Mile Twelve." A pause, and something wry and wary crossed her face. "And I think I know exactly who to ask."
