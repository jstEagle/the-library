## 5. The Guide Who Lies Well

The river town of Sallow Bend smelled of diesel and wet rope and, underneath it all, something sweeter that I couldn't name until I'd been there three days—overripe fruit rotting in the flooded lower streets, sugar going to vinegar in the heat. The monsoon was still weeks out by the almanacs, but the water had already come up over the cobblestones of the market quarter, and the vendors had simply moved their stalls onto planks and barges and kept selling as if dry ground were a rumor from another life.

I liked a town that adapted without complaint. It reminded me of me.

I found Tamás Oyelaran on the third morning, though I didn't know yet that I was looking for him. I was at the fuel dock, paying too much for two-stroke, when a long wooden boat slid past me against the current with an ease that made my hands stop moving. The channel below the bend was a mess this time of year—braided shallows, deadheads rolling just under the surface like sleeping animals—but the man at the tiller read it the way Pell read maps, the way I once read tide tables: not as information but as language. He didn't fight the current. He negotiated with it. Three times he should have been pushed onto the wing dam, and three times he tilted his hull a few degrees and let the river decide to change its mind.

I watched him tie off, jump to the dock with a coil of line already in his hand, and start arguing cheerfully with the fuel merchant about the price of diesel as if the argument were a game they'd been playing for years and both of them intended to win forever.

"Oyelaran," the merchant said to me later, spitting toward the boat. "If you want to go upriver, he's the only one who'll take you past the narrows. And if you're smart, you won't ask him why."

I wasn't smart. Asking why was the only part of me that still worked properly.

* * *

He kept a room above the net-drying shed, and when I knocked he opened the door already holding two cups of coffee, which told me either that he'd seen me coming or that hospitality here was reflex rather than calculation. Both, probably. His face was broad and unhurried, mid-forties maybe, with a scar through one eyebrow that looked old enough to have stopped being interesting to him. His eyes did the thing I've learned to watch for in people who live on rivers: they went to my hands first, then my boots, then the set of my shoulders—the assessment of a working body before anything else.

"You're the diver," he said. "The one asking after the headwaters."

Word travels fast in a town where the streets are underwater and everyone's standing on the same planks.

"I'm looking for a guide," I said. "Upstream. Past the narrows, up into terrace country."

"Nobody goes into terrace country."

"People used to."

"People used to drink the water straight from the river, too." He said it lightly, but his eyes didn't. He stepped back and let me in anyway, which was its own kind of answer.

The room above the shed was spare and immaculate—charts rolled and tied with string, a wall of tools hung by silhouette outlines painted on the board so any missing piece announced itself. A man who noticed absence. That would matter later; I noted it then. On the table lay a child's drawing, crayon on butcher paper: a house, a river, three stick figures, and the river colored not blue but a strange pale green-gold, the way children color things they've actually seen and adults tell them they've seen wrong.

I didn't ask about the drawing. I was learning, slowly, that the questions you don't ask are sometimes the ones that get answered first.

"Two hundred a day," he said, "plus fuel, plus hazard pay for anything above the narrows. You provide your own insurance, because I don't have any, and if you die I'll bury you respectfully and keep your deposit."

"Generous."

"I'm told I have a warm manner." He sat, poured the second cup, and pushed it across the table toward me without asking whether I wanted it. "How far up?"

"The headwaters."

The word landed in the room and stayed there. Somewhere below us a dog barked at the water, or at something in it.

"The headwaters are restricted," he said. "You know that. There's a navy station at Mile Nine and signs every half mile after that, and the signs aren't decorative. Last season a patrol boat put a family's skiff ashore twelve miles down because their boy had a fishing line over the rail at Mile Seven. Twelve miles, to make a point about one fishing line."

"I know what the signs say."

"Do you know what they mean?" He leaned back, arms crossed, and studied me with the frankness of a man who has decided the polite version of this conversation is a waste of both our mornings. "Because I've had three clients this year ask about the upper river. Two were journalists, and I turned them away, because journalists get people shot. One was a company man with very clean boots, and I turned him away faster. Which are you?"

"None of those."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the truest one I have." I drank the coffee. It was terrible and strong and exactly right. "I'm a salvage diver. I was hired to recover equipment from a wreck. The wreck turned out to be a story someone told to keep divers' hands busy, and the equipment I recovered says the upper river is sick. I want to know how sick, and why, and who signed the paperwork. That's all. I'm not a journalist. I'm not a company man. I'm nobody, and I intend to stay that way."

Something moved behind his face when I said *nobody*—not disbelief, not pity. Recognition. He covered it fast, but I've spent ten years getting good at seeing things people cover fast.

"And you need a guide," he said, "because the channels above the narrows shift every flood, and the charts are thirty years old, and the navy doesn't update them because the navy doesn't want anyone going up there, charted or not."

"Yes."

"And you can't do it alone."

"No."

He was quiet for a moment. Outside, rain started—not the monsoon, just the ordinary afternoon drumming that came off the hills most days—and the sound of it on the tin roof filled the pause the way water fills a footprint.

"Three hundred a day," he said. "Above the narrows is four. And I'll need to tell my sister where I'm going, and she'll need to be lied to convincingly, so add a day at the start for lying well."

"When can you leave?"

"Tomorrow, if the fuel comes." He stood, and I understood the interview was over, and I also understood that somewhere in the last ten minutes he had decided to say yes, possibly before I ever knocked. "One condition. Not negotiable."

"Name it."

"If we find what you're looking for—and we will, because things that poison rivers leave fingerprints everywhere—you don't get to un-see it. Whatever you do afterward, whatever you choose, that's yours. But you don't get to look away halfway and pretend the river's fine. I've watched people do that. They come back down and buy houses on the bluff and the water keeps dying under them and they learn not to taste it." His voice stayed level, almost gentle, which made it worse. "I won't ferry a man who's planning to forget. So decide now. Are you the kind who looks, or the kind who looks away?"

I thought about the crate in my boathouse with its government stamps. About boots by a cold fire with no tracks leading out. About the ten years I had spent perfecting the art of looking away, and how well it had worked, and how much it had cost, measured in a currency I refused to count.

"I'm the kind who looks," I said. "It's my worst habit."

For the first time, Tamás Oyelaran smiled, and it changed his whole face into something younger and more dangerous. "Good," he said. "Me too."

* * *

We left at gray dawn, before the heat could build, and within an hour I understood that I had not hired a guide so much as acquired a translator for a country I'd only ever visited. The flooded forest began at Mile Four, where the levee gave up its argument with the river and the water spread out into the trees—black water, mirror-still between the trunks, so that we seemed to move through a forest reflected in a ceiling of sky. Orchids hung from branches that should have been twenty feet above the floodline. A heron stood in the shallows with the patience of furniture. The air was thick enough to chew, green-smelling, loud with insects and the constant small machinery of the water itself.

Tamás poled where the prop would foul and ran the engine where the channel deepened, and he narrated none of it, which I appreciated. Guides who talk constantly are selling something. Guides who are silent are reading.

But silence makes me itch, and itch makes me talk, and by Mile Six I'd learned a handful of things he offered freely: that he had two sisters and a brother-in-law who ran the net shed; that his mother lived downstream at a village called Ashfall Landing, past the delta, where the river finally got tired and laid down its sediment; that he'd guided geologists up this stretch twice before the restrictions tightened, and both times they'd paid him extra to forget the route, and both times he'd taken the money and memorized the route anyway.

"And what don't you offer freely?" I asked, because directness is a tool and I was curious whether he respected it.

He considered the question longer than most people would have found comfortable. A kingfisher detonated off a branch ahead of us, electric blue, gone.

"My reasons," he said finally. "You get my skill and my silence and my back, if it comes to that. My reasons stay ashore. Fair?"

Fair. It was more than I'd offered him, honestly—I'd given him a story about a wreck and a lie about being nobody, and he'd taken both without blinking, which meant he knew they were lies, which meant—

Which meant he knew they were lies, and hired me anyway.

I turned that over while the forest closed around us and the channel narrowed and the current, faint but insistent, began to pull us upstream's wrong direction. A man who agrees too easily isn't agreeing with you. He's agreeing with his own plan, and you've just become part of it. I'd been on the other side of that equation often enough to know the shape of it from inside. The question was what his plan was, and whether mine could survive the collision.

There was also the matter of the drawing on his table. The green-gold river. Children draw what they see. His family lived downstream. Downstream of what?

At Mile Eight the trees thinned and the first terrace ruins showed on the bluff above us—old stone steps swallowed by vine, a retaining wall older than the charts, and cut into it, incongruous as a tooth in a fist, a galvanized pipe stenciled with a faded logo I didn't recognize and a date stamp that was not old at all. Tamás saw me looking and didn't slow down, but his jaw tightened in a way I was beginning to catalog.

By late afternoon we made camp on a shelf of high ground at the edge of the narrows, and he built the fire small and screened, military-neat, and cooked rice and dried fish with a competence that suggested he'd fed crews in worse places than this. We ate without talking. The river talked enough for both of us—a low conversation over the rocks ahead, the narrows rehearsing their noise.

"Tomorrow we run them at first light," he said. "Water's lowest at dawn. After that you hold on and I work, and you don't touch anything, and if I say down, you get down."

"I've run rapids."

"In a salvage tender with forty horsepower and a coast guard escort?" He shook his head, almost fond. "The narrows don't care what you've run. They care whether you listen. Most people don't listen."

"And you do."

"I grew up on this water." He poked the fire down to embers with the economy of a man who wastes nothing, including motion. "My grandfather ran cargo through these narrows before the dam changed everything. He used to say the river remembers every boat that's ever disrespected it, and it keeps the list."

"Superstition."

"Hydrology," he said, "with better manners."

I laughed—actually laughed, short and surprised, the first one in longer than I could measure—and he glanced over at the sound with an expression I couldn't read and didn't try to. We settled into our bedrolls on opposite sides of the fire, and the forest performed its night shift around us, and I lay awake doing the arithmetic I always do: miles logged today, miles remaining, margin for error, exit options. Eleven miles behind us. Nineteen hundred to the sea, if a person were fool enough to want the sea.

Somewhere past midnight, half-asleep, I heard him moving—not much, just the soft scrape of him sitting up, and the faint click of something metal. When I cracked my eyes, he was at the edge of the firelight with a hand-line in the water, fishing, or pretending to fish, and looking upstream into the dark with his whole body angled toward the headwaters like a compass needle that had gotten tired of pointing north and chosen something else instead.

A man whose family lives downstream of a restricted zone. A man who agrees too easily. A man who fishes at midnight facing the thing he claims to fear.

In the morning, the narrows waited, white and loud, and Tamás stood at the tiller with the sun not yet up, and said, "Hold on to something that means it," and took us in.
