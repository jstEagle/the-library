## 6. Miles Eleven Through Nineteen

The river gave them the first three miles as a gift, and Tamás Oyelaran did not trust gifts.

He sat in the stern of the longboat with the tiller against his hip, reading the water the way his grandmother had read faces—looking for the small betrayals, the twitch at the corner of a current that meant something was being hidden underneath. The flooded forest closed around them in green corridors, kapok roots rising from brown water like the ribs of drowned cathedrals, orchids hanging overhead in ropes of white and violet. Somewhere a hornbill laughed its ugly laugh. The morning smelled of wet bark and crushed leaves and, faintly, of diesel from their own engine, which he kept low because sound carried strangely here, bounced off the water and came back from directions that made no sense.

Isandro Coldharbour sat amidships with her arms folded, watching the banks.

Not looking—watching. There was a difference, and Tamás had spent enough years ferrying strangers up this river to know it. Tourists looked. They pointed at birds, asked the names of trees, took photographs of light on water. Isandro watched the way a woman watches a room she might have to fight her way out of. Her eyes moved bank to bank in a slow, patient sweep, cataloguing. He'd seen soldiers do it. He'd seen smugglers do it. He'd done it himself, once, a long time ago, before he learned that the river forgives everything except pretending you weren't afraid.

"Eleven miles," she said, checking the battered depth sounder clipped to her belt. She said it the way other people said amen.

"You count miles," he said. It wasn't a question. He'd been listening to her do it since dawn—miles since the boathouse, miles to the first bend, miles logged like coins into a purse only she could see.

"Everyone counts something."

"Most people count years."

"Years lie." She said it flatly, without drama, the way you'd say *the sky is blue*, and then she turned back to the bank as if the conversation were over, which by her rules it probably was.

Tamás smiled at the back of her head and let the engine drop another notch. Two days he'd known this woman. Two days, and already he could feel the shape of her silences the way you feel a stone through the sole of your boot—smooth where she wanted you to step, sharp where she didn't. She had hired him with cash counted out on the dock of his cousin's fuel depot, no haggling, which told him she didn't care about money or didn't have time to pretend to. She'd asked about his routes upstream with a diver's precision: depths, portages, the location of every sandbar that shifted after rain. And when he'd named his price—a price high enough to insult a tourist—she hadn't blinked.

She hadn't asked him why he agreed so easily either. That was the part that kept him awake last night, lying under his mosquito net listening to the frogs argue about the rain. Most people asked. Most people needed a reason they could hold, turn over, file away. Isandro Coldharbour had simply looked at him for a long moment with those gray river-stone eyes, nodded once, and started loading gear.

Either she trusted him completely, which would make her a fool, or she understood him completely, which would make her dangerous.

He was betting on dangerous. Fools didn't dive navy wrecks for money they never counted.

* * *

The rapids came at mile thirteen, exactly where he'd promised they wouldn't be gentle.

"Engine off," he called, and killed the motor, and the world changed. The silence of an engine dying on a river is not silence at all—it's the river turning up its own voice, and here that voice was a long rolling thunder coming down the channel ahead, white water braiding between black rocks, spray hanging in the air like breath in winter. "Paddle when I say. Not before. The eddy on the left is a liar—it looks like help and it will wrap us around that rock and take the boat apart."

"And the right?"

"The right is honest but expensive. We'll pay it."

He worked them through by feel and memory, the tiller useless now, steering with weight and paddle-calls, and Isandro paddled the way she did everything else—economically, precisely, without one wasted stroke or one wasted word. When the bow slammed through the standing wave at the throat of the chute and cold water broke over them both, she didn't shout, didn't laugh, didn't swear. She just leaned into the next stroke. On the far side, in the long green pool below the falls, dripping and breathing hard, she checked her sounder again.

"Fifteen," she said.

"You're bleeding," Tamás said.

She looked down at her forearm, where a branch or a rock had opened a thin line during the chute. Blood welled and thinned in the rain-wet air. She considered it with mild interest, the way you'd consider a smudge on a window, and wiped it on her trousers.

"That's it?" he said. "No hissing? No cursing my ancestors?"

"I've bled worse in better company."

"Ah." He shipped his paddle and let the boat drift. "So there *is* worse company. I was starting to think you'd been grown in a tank somewhere, all business and gray eyes."

Something moved behind her face—quick, submerged, gone. A fish shadow crossing shallow water. "You talk a lot for a guide."

"I talk exactly as much as the river requires. Right now the river requires me to fill a silence, because you've been sitting in it for two hours like it's furniture." He grinned at her, deliberately, the grin he used on frightened clients and suspicious officials alike. "Besides, I'm curious. You don't ask questions, so someone has to."

"I ask plenty of questions."

"About depths. About currents. About where the navy doesn't go." He ticked them off on wet fingers. "You know what you haven't asked me? What's upstream. Why a salvage job needs a guide who knows the headwaters. You paid me like a man buying rope, not like a woman going somewhere new. Which means you already decided what's up there before we left." He tilted his head. "I'm just wondering what you decided."

The drift carried them past a stand of bamboo, and the light came down through it in green blades, striping her face. For a moment she looked younger. For a moment she looked like someone who had once been easy to read, before life taught her encryption.

"I decided," she said slowly, "that a wreck the navy denies exists isn't a wreck. And that whoever pays to keep it quiet will pay more to keep everything else quiet too. And that a guide who agrees too fast has his own reasons for wanting to go upstream." Her eyes came up and met his, level, unblinking. "I decided we're probably the same kind of stupid."

Tamás laughed—a real laugh, surprised out of him, echoing off the gorge walls. "Same kind of stupid," he repeated. "My grandmother would have liked you. She said the river sends you the companion you deserve, not the one you want."

"What does that make us?"

"Punished, probably."

* * *

They camped that night on a shelf of gravel beneath an overhang of fig trees, and the leeches found them within the hour, as leeches always did, silent and inevitable as tax collectors. Tamás showed her the trick with salt, and she learned it in one demonstration, and after that they sat by the fire picking the flat dark worms off their boots and ankles and flicking them into the flames, where they popped with tiny obscene sounds.

"Leeches are honest," Tamás said, flicking another one. "That's more than I can say for most things on this river."

"You include yourself in 'most things'?"

"Oh, especially myself." He fed a stick into the fire and watched the sparks climb. Above them the stars were coming out in their thousands, thick as spilled salt, and the river ran silver-black below the gravel bar, murmuring its one long sentence over and over. "I lied to you twice today already. Want to hear?"

That got her attention. She turned, firelight moving on her face, and waited. She was good at waiting. He suspected she'd had practice—the kind of practice nobody volunteers for.

"One: I told you the eddy on the left was a liar. It wasn't. It's a perfectly good eddy. I just didn't want you trusting anything on this river but my voice." He held up a second finger. "Two: I told you my family lives downstream of the restricted zone. That's true. But I told it to you like it was background. Like it was weather." His voice dropped. "It's not weather."

Isandro said nothing. The fire cracked. Somewhere across the river a night bird called, three notes descending, like a question being asked and abandoned.

"My sister's boy," Tamás said, "is seven years old. Last dry season he started getting sores on his arms that wouldn't close. Then his gums. The clinic boat comes every six weeks; the doctor took one look at the water jar and wrote something in her book and didn't meet anyone's eyes. Our wells run clear in the morning and by evening there's a shine on them, a rainbow skin, like oil on a puddle. Like the water itself is wearing makeup to hide a bruise." He flicked another leech into the fire, harder than he needed to. "You asked nothing when I agreed too fast. So now you know why. I need a diver. I need someone who goes down into places other people won't. That's the truth, and it cost me something to say it, so we'll call it even for the eddy."

For a long moment Isandro studied the fire. When she spoke, her voice was quieter than the river.

"How many children sick?"

"In my village? Eleven that I know of. Downstream of us, more. The fishing camps don't count their sick—they just stop fishing and move." He shrugged, and the shrug hurt. "The government says the water tests fine. The government also says there's no wreck at mile nine, and you dove it anyway."

"Yes," she said. "I did."

"So." He spread his hands. "Two liars in a boat. One lies because the truth got people killed once and she buried the truth with them—" he watched her flinch, a small thing, a muscle beside her eye, and knew he'd guessed right, guessed it the way you guess a channel in flood season, by reading the surface break— "and one lies because the truth hasn't killed anyone yet and he'd like to keep it that way. Same kind of stupid."

"Don't do that again," Isandro said. There was no heat in it. That was the frightening part. There was no heat in it at all, just a flat cold precision, like the bottom of a deep lake. "Guessing at me."

"Then tell me something true instead. One thing. You don't have to say what you did ten years ago—I saw the name on your credentials at the checkpoint paperwork, the old one, the one you keep folded under the new license like a knife under a shirt. I won't ask what it means." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, firelight between them. "But tell me one true thing, Isandro Coldharbour, and I'll consider us square."

The night held its breath. Even the leeches seemed to pause.

She looked at him for a long time, and he watched her decide—not whether to answer, he realized, but how much of herself the answer would cost. He recognized the arithmetic. He did it every day, every time someone downstream asked him why he guided boats upriver toward the place that was poisoning his family, and he smiled and talked about fees and seasons and never once said the word *mine*.

"Miles eleven through nineteen," she finally said. "That's what you titled tonight in your head, isn't it? The stretch where we stopped being employer and guide."

"It's what I'll log."

"Fine." She pulled her knees up, wrapped her arms around them, and looked into the fire rather than at him, which he understood was the closest she could come to taking off armor. "One true thing. When I was down in that wreck—at mile nine, inside the hull, in the dark, with the crate in my hands—" her jaw tightened— "it was the calmest I've felt in ten years. Forty meters down, breathing off a bottle, no name, no history, no face. Just work and water." She exhaled. "And when I surfaced and saw the sun, I was disappointed. That's my true thing. Make of it what you want."

Tamás sat with that. Around them the forest breathed and clicked and whispered, ten thousand small lives conducting their small business, indifferent to confessions. He thought about the first time he'd ever lied on this river—to a surveyor, years ago, telling him the headwater channels were impassable so the man would turn back—and how he'd felt the same disappointment surfacing afterward, back in the daylight where the lie lived.

"Make of it what I want," he echoed softly. "All right. Here's what I want to make of it." He poked the fire, sending up a fistful of sparks. "A person who feels calmest underwater isn't hiding from the water. She's hiding from the surface. And a person who's disappointed by sunlight—" he glanced at her profile, sharp and still against the firelight— "has been running from something that lives in daylight. Names. Faces. Whatever it is you buried."

"Careful."

"No. You said make of it what I want." He smiled, and this time it wasn't the client-smile; it was the real one, rare, crooked. "Here's what I want, Isandro. I want us to stop lying to each other by degrees. I'll trade you. Every mile upstream, one true thing each. Miles twenty through thirty, thirty through forty. By the time we reach the headwaters, maybe we'll both be able to walk around in the daylight without flinching." He shrugged. "Or maybe we'll drown in the rapids and it won't matter. Either way, the river wins."

Isandro was quiet for so long he thought she wouldn't answer. Then she reached over, took the salt bag, and poured a careful line of it around her bedroll—a leech barrier, practical, unsentimental.

"Miles twenty through thirty," she said. "One true thing each." A pause. "You first, tomorrow. You've had tonight."

And that was when Tamás heard it—faint, upstream, riding down the current the way sound rides a river at night when the wind dies: engines. More than one. Deep-throated, heavy-hulled, running without lights, the particular laboring note of patrol craft pushing against the current in the dark.

Isandro heard it too. He saw it land in her the way a stone lands in still water—one ring, then stillness, total stillness.

"Kill the fire," she whispered.

He was already reaching for the sand.
