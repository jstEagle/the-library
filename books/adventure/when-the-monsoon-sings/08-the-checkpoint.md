## 8. The Checkpoint

The rain had followed them for two days, patient as a debt collector.

It came down now in long gray sheets that turned the river into something between water and smoke, and Isandro stood at the tiller of Tamás's flat-bottomed skiff and let it run off her hood in ropes. Behind her, Pell huddled under a tarp with the survey team's recovered instruments strapped to her chest like a life vest, and Tamás crouched at the bow reading the current the way other men read scripture—lips moving, eyes narrowed, utterly certain of what the river was telling him.

They were eleven miles below the checkpoint.

Isandro knew because she'd been counting. She counted everything now: miles logged, breaths held, minutes since the last sound of an engine that wasn't theirs. It was a habit from the old years, from the name she'd buried, and it had kept her alive through three salvage seasons and one very bad winter on the estuary. Rules were a kind of armor. Numbers were a kind of prayer. You could survive almost anything if you reduced it to figures small enough to hold.

The problem was that some numbers didn't stay small.

"Smoke," Tamás said, without turning around. "Two miles. Maybe less."

Isandro lifted her chin and tasted the air beneath the rain. He was right. Beneath the wet-iron smell of the flooded forest there was diesel exhaust, faint but unmistakable, riding the wind downriver like a rumor.

"Navy?" Pell asked, pushing out from under the tarp. Her glasses were fogged; she wiped them on her shirt and put them back on crooked, which somehow made her look both more competent and more terrified.

"Patrol boat," Isandro said. "Riverine class, if they're running the standard rotation. Shallow draft, mounted gun, four crew minimum." She said it evenly, the way you'd recite tide tables, and only afterward realized how much the knowing itself revealed. Pell's eyes found her over the fogged lenses and stayed there.

"You've been stopped before."

"I've been stopped before."

"By them?"

"By everyone." Isandro adjusted the tiller a degree, easing the skiff toward the bank where the drowned trees thinned. "Which is why we're not going to run."

* * *

Tamás argued first, because he always argued first. He pulled the skiff into the shadow of a strangler fig whose roots had swallowed a dead man's fence posts whole, and he turned to her with his easy face already dismantled, the real face underneath—the one that was older than his voice, harder than his jokes.

"We cut upriver through the oxbow," he said. "There's a channel behind the sandbar, maybe two feet deep. We drag her over. They never see us."

"They have thermal imaging and a helicopter on standby at Mile Twelve," Isandro said. "I've seen the requisition logs. A skiff hiding under a fig tree reads like a bonfire."

"How do you know about their requisition logs?"

Because I used to write them, she thought. Because I sat in a room with gray carpet and no windows and signed my name—my true name—to documents that moved men and machines up this same river, and then I burned that name and every bridge attached to it and swam away downstream like something wounded and lucky.

"Because I've been in this business a long time," she said instead.

Tamás looked at her for a long moment. Rain ticked against the tarp. Somewhere upstream, faint as a heartbeat, the patrol engine idled.

"You're going to talk to them," he said finally. Not a question.

"I'm going to talk to them."

"With what? Your winning personality?"

Isandro reached into the waterproof bag lashed beneath her thwart seat, past the dive knife and the spare regulator, to the oilskin pouch she hadn't opened in ten years. Her hands did not shake. She'd trained them not to, back when shaking hands got people killed, and the training had outlasted everything else about her.

She drew out a document case, matte black, government-stamped, and set it on the thwart between them like a card laid face-up on a table.

Pell leaned in. Read the embossing. Went very still.

"That's a hydrographic commission credential," she whispered. "That's not a diver's license. That's—that's *federal*. Those haven't been issued since—" She stopped. Recalculated. Isandro watched her do the arithmetic, watched her arrive at the sum, watched the color leave her face by degrees, like a tide going out. "These were issued under the old registry. Before the purge. Whoever holds this is either dead or—"

"Or buried," Isandro said. "Ten years ago, at the bottom of a different river, in a manner of speaking."

Tamás picked up the case without asking permission. Turned it over. His thumb traced the stamped seal, and when he spoke, his voice had lost its river-guide warmth entirely.

"This isn't your name."

"No."

"But it's a name they'll answer to."

"It's a name they'll *salute*," Isandro said. "If I do this right."

"And if you don't?"

She looked upstream, toward the idle of the engine, toward the checkpoint she'd known about since Mile Three because Tamás had told her about it two days ago in a voice too casual to be honest—which was how he told most truths—and she understood, with the cold clarity that sometimes came to her underwater, that she had been carrying this moment since the day she cracked open the crate that breathed.

Every cover burns eventually. The only choice you get is whether you light the match yourself or stand in the fire someone else sets.

"If I don't," she said, "we all go into a detention facility, and the evidence dies with us in paperwork. So. I do it right."

* * *

They came at the checkpoint the way she'd planned it: openly, slowly, down the middle of the channel, visible from half a mile, a salvage skiff doing exactly what a salvage skiff should be doing—nothing clever, nothing hidden. The rain eased to a drizzle as they approached, and the world resolved out of the gray: the concrete abutment of the old ferry crossing, the navy patrol boat moored alongside it with its gun mount shrouded in canvas, four sailors in wet ponchos watching them come with the flat patience of men who had been standing in the rain for six hours.

A lieutenant stepped onto the abutment. Young. New insignia, rain-darkened. He held a megaphone he didn't use, which Isandro took as a good sign—he wanted to hear himself think.

"Skiff, heave to. Kill your engine."

Tamás killed the engine. The current took them the last twenty meters, gentle as a hand on the stern, and Isandro stepped to the bow and stood in full view, hands loose at her sides, and let the lieutenant look at her: a woman in a battered dive suit, salt-scoured, ordinary, forgettable.

"State your business on the upper reach."

"Salvage recovery," Isandro said. "Contracted sweep of the Mile Fourteen obstruction. There's a sunken barge fouling the channel; the shipping authority wants it mapped and marked." She let a beat pass, then added the detail that made lies breathe: "We've already lost two days to this weather. I'd take it kindly if you didn't add a third."

The lieutenant's eyes narrowed. "Upper reach is closed to civilian traffic. Restricted waters above Mile Ten. You're carrying transit papers?"

Isandro raised the black case.

"I'm carrying better than papers."

He came down the abutment steps to the waterline, boots ringing on wet concrete, and took the case from her hand. Opened it. And here was the moment she had rehearsed for two days and dreaded for ten years: the moment when the past, dredged up and dried off, would be asked to perform.

Inside the case, laminated against waterproof board, was a federal hydrographic commission card bearing a photograph of a woman who was recognizably her—younger, shorter-haired, eyes not yet practiced at being nobody—and a name that was not Coldharbour. Above the name, a clearance tier that made the lieutenant's eyebrows climb. Below it, an authorization code she still remembered writing, ten years ago, in a room with gray carpet, in ink she had later tried to drink away with everything cheap enough to buy.

The lieutenant read it twice. Looked at her. Looked at it again.

"Ma'am," he said, and the word changed shape in his mouth, "this credential tier requires countersignature verification. If you'll permit—"

"Permit," Isandro said, "and then explain to your sector command why a Tier Two hydrographic survey was delayed three days by weather and a fourth by a sub-lieutenant's radio etiquette. The monsoon window closes in nine days. Every hour we sit here, the obstruction shifts, and someone's barge pilot dies on it in October. Do you want that call on your record, Lieutenant—"

"Aldous," he supplied automatically, and then visibly wished he hadn't, because she smiled at him the way you smile at a man who has just handed you a knife handle-first.

"Lieutenant Aldous. Run your verification. I'll wait." She settled onto the thwart as if she owned the river and the rain and the concept of waiting itself. "But run it fast."

* * *

He ran it fast.

What Isandro hadn't told Pell and Tamás—what she hadn't fully admitted to herself until the radio crackled and the voice on the far end, a voice from the old registry, a voice that had once signed off on her orders, said *"Verification confirmed. Tier Two holder, status active, advise cooperation"*—was that the name she'd buried had never actually been declared dead. She had buried it herself, privately, ceremonially, with salt water and silence. But on some ledger in some ministry, in some drawer no one had opened since the purge, the name had simply gone dormant. Sleeping. Waiting.

Waiting, it turned out, for her to need it more than she needed to be free of it.

The lieutenant returned the case with both hands, the way you return something valuable. "Apologies for the delay, ma'am. Channel's clear above you to Mile Sixteen. There's a second patrol element at the gorge narrows—I can raise them, give them your—"

"Don't bother," Isandro said. "Tier Two doesn't announce itself. That's rather the point of the tier."

"Yes, ma'am." He hesitated. Rain dripped off his poncho hem. "Ma'am—if I may. The survey team that went missing up there, back in the spring. Official word was equipment failure and drowning."

Isandro kept her face still. Beside her, under the tarp, she felt Pell stop breathing.

"What official word usually is," Isandro said.

"Yes, ma'am." He glanced upstream, at the gray wall of the gorge, and something moved behind his young face—something that might, in a few years, become conscience. "Their last transmission came through our relay. I logged it myself. It wasn't distress. It was—" He stopped himself, jaw working. "Nothing, ma'am. Clear channel. Good luck with your obstruction."

He saluted. She did not return it—Tier Two holders didn't, another rule she remembered with a lurch—and the skiff slid past the patrol boat close enough that she could see the sailors' faces under their poncho hoods, young and bored and mercifully incurious, and then the abutment fell behind them and the river bent north into the narrowing gorge and the checkpoint was gone.

* * *

For a mile, nobody spoke.

The gorge rose around them in walls of wet black stone hung with vines, and the river quickened between them, and Tamás steered through the first rapids with a precision that required all of his attention and gave him, Isandro suspected, an excuse not to look at her.

It was Pell who broke first. She pulled the tarp off her shoulders and leaned forward between the thwarts, and her voice came out low and shaking, not with fear—with something hotter.

"Their last transmission wasn't distress," she repeated. "You heard him. He *logged* it. Which means it went through official channels, which means somebody upstairs read it, which means somebody upstairs has known since spring that my team didn't drown—they sent a *distress-classified relay* through a naval frequency and called it equipment failure—"

"Pell." Isandro kept her eyes on the water. "Breathe."

"Don't tell me to breathe. You—" Pell's hand closed on her shoulder, spun her half around, and Isandro let herself be turned, because the woman deserved the eye contact. "Who are you? What tier was that? What did you *do*, ten years ago, that a navy lieutenant salutes your ghost?"

The rain had stopped. The gorge dripped. Somewhere high above, a bird cried once and got no answer.

Isandro looked at her—at the fogged glasses and the instruments clutched to her chest and the grief worn raw as river stone—and felt the strange weightlessness she always felt after spending the past, like a diver who has just dropped ballast and hasn't yet learned to trust the ascent.

"Ten years ago," she said, "I had a different name, and that name had clearance, and the clearance had access, and I used the access to sign off on a hydrographic survey of this exact watershed. A survey that was ordered, funded, and then buried—by the same offices that buried your team's last transmission. I found out what the survey was really mapping. I refused to falsify the final report. And when I wouldn't play, they gave me a choice: lose everything quietly, or lose everything loudly and take a few people down with me." She turned back to the tiller. "I chose quiet. I chose the bottom of a river. I've been diving under a dead woman's calm ever since."

The skiff rocked through the riffles. Pell sat back slowly.

"So the crate," Pell said. "The charts. The restricted headwaters. You recognized the survey style."

"I recognized my own handwriting," Isandro said. "On the margin notes. Someone reprinted my buried survey and used it as the mining consortium's baseline data. They built their operation on top of the map I made and then erased." Her laugh came out with no humor in it at all. "They're poisoning the watershed with my homework, Pell. That's the joke. That's the whole rotten joke."

Tamás brought them through the last of the rapids into a long, deep pool where the water went green-black and still, and only then did he speak, without turning around, his voice carefully level.

"The lieutenant said their last transmission wasn't distress," he said. "You said the survey was buried. My family lives fourteen miles below where this water goes." He let the skiff drift. "Everything you two keep in your locked boxes—it's all the same box, isn't it? Same lock. Same people who threw away the key."

Isandro met his eyes in the reflection on the still water—his face floating there beside hers, upside down, doubled.

"Yes," she said. "Same box."

"Then we'd best find out," Tamás said, and pushed the throttle forward, and the pool broke apart around them into white water and noise, "what else is sleeping in it."

Above them, at the head of the gorge, barely visible through the drizzle, the terraced slopes began—too regular, too engineered, humming faintly with a sound none of them could yet name.

And on the bank at Mile Sixteen, half-hidden in the vine-hung stone, a new sign had been bolted up since the spring, fresh-painted, official:

**CONSORTIUM WATERS — NO PUBLIC ACCESS — BY ORDER OF THE MINISTRY**

Someone had shot it twice.
