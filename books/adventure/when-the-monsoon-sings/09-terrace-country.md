## 9. Terrace Country

The river changed its mind at the gorge.

For nineteen miles it had been a brown, muscular thing, shoving us through flooded forest, but here it narrowed and dropped its voice, sliding green-black between walls of stone so high that morning arrived an hour late and left an hour early. Tamás felt the change in his hands before he saw anything—the current going strange against the paddle strokes, pulling sideways, as if the water were being siphoned somewhere beneath them.

He had known this stretch since he was nine years old, when his father first brought him upriver in a dugout with a cracked gunwale and told him: *past the gorge, you don't drink.* He had never asked why. That was the Oyelaran way—accept the rules of the river without demanding their reasons, because the river did not explain itself to anyone.

Except now the river was explaining itself, in a language of warmth and faint rainbow film, and no one downstream was listening.

"Easy," Isandro said from the bow of the lead boat. She hadn't spoken much since the checkpoint two days back, since she'd handed over credentials bearing a name that made the patrol officer's face go gray and respectful. Whatever name that was, whatever life it belonged to, she'd folded it away again like a knife wiped clean and sheathed. Tamás watched her do it and thought: *that is a person who has practiced disappearing until it became a skill.*

Pell sat amidships in the second boat, hunched over the survey team's recovered logbook even though there was nothing new to read, holding it the way other people held prayer beads. Her wrists were thin as oar shafts. When the gorge walls closed overhead she looked up, and up, and Tamás saw her lips move—counting something, or maybe just saying names.

"Terraces ahead," Isandro said.

Tamás already knew. You could hear it before you could see it—a sound below hearing, more a pressure than a noise, the deep animal hum of generators running behind stone. His father had heard that hum once, thirty years ago, coming down from the headwaters with a load of fish gone bad and a story he never finished telling. *Machines in the mountain,* he'd said, and then he'd stopped talking about it for the rest of his life, which was eleven more years, and not one of them easy.

*Past the gorge, you don't drink.* And past the gorge, apparently, you don't talk either.

* * *

They pulled the boats into a side channel where willow roots knitted the bank into something like stairs, and climbed.

The terraces announced themselves gradually, the way a lie announces itself—one small wrongness at a time. First the slope underfoot changed: too even, too graded, cut in long switchback shelves that no flood had ever carved. Then the vegetation thinned to scrub and mine-tail grass, silver-stemmed and bitter, the kind of plant that grew only where the soil had been turned inside out. Then the stones began—dressed blocks, fitted without mortar, their faces furred with moss so old it hung like pelt.

"These are old," Pell said, crouching to touch one. "Really old. Pre-colonial, maybe. Look at the tooling." Her fingers traced grooves in the block, and for a moment the guilt fell off her face and something brighter took its place—the pure animal joy of a cartographer touching ground no map had ever named. "This whole hillside was worked before anyone wrote it down."

"And then worked again," Isandro said.

She was standing ten yards upslope, at the seam where the ancient terracing ended and the modern began. Tamás joined her and saw it plainly: the old stonework giving way to poured concrete, rust-freckled rebar jutting from a retaining wall like the ribs of something buried alive. The concrete was newer than it should have been. Newer than the survey, certainly. Newer than anything officially permitted within fifty miles of these headwaters.

Someone had come up here with heavy equipment and government-grade indifference and built on top of a ruin, the way a squatter builds on top of a grave.

"Feel that?" Isandro said.

Tamás had been feeling it for an hour. The hum. Up close it resolved into layers—a bass note from somewhere deep in the hillside, a higher whine from over the next terrace, and threaded through both, a rhythmic clanking, mechanical and patient, like a heart that had learned to pump ore instead of blood.

"Generators," he said. "More than one. Big ones."

"Mining works this size don't run themselves." Isandro's voice was flat, but Tamás had learned to read the flatness—it was what she used when the alternative was feeling something out loud. "And they don't run abandoned. Abandoned machinery goes quiet or it goes to rust. This—" she tilted her head at the humming hillside "—this is being *operated.*"

Pell came up beside them, breathing hard from the climb, the logbook clutched to her chest. "The survey team's last coordinates were three terraces above us," she said. "Their transmission stopped right about—" she turned a slow circle, arm outstretched, finger tracking the ridgeline "—there. Behind that ridge. There's a saddle in the rock; I can see it from the contour data. Something's in that saddle."

"Something with electricity," Tamás said.

"Something with guards," Isandro said, because she had seen it first, because of course she had. She didn't point. She just shifted her weight slightly, angling her body so that following her eyeline looked casual, and Tamás let his gaze drift along it and found them: two figures on the terrace above, small against the sky, moving with the unhurried patrol rhythm of men who were bored rather than vigilant. Rifles slung. Not searching. *Stationed.*

His mouth went dry. Not with fear—or not only fear. With recognition. Because his father's unfinished story had ended, all those years ago, with a sentence the old man never said aloud but which Tamás had reconstructed a hundred times since, from fragments, from the way his father flinched at generator noise, from the way he'd burned a canvas pack in the yard one night and told the children it was moldy:

*They pay you to forget, boy. And if forgetting isn't enough, they have other ways.*

Tamás had always assumed his father meant the river. That the danger was the water itself, the drowned workings, the cold. Standing on this warm, humming hillside, watching bored men carry rifles across ruins older than any nation, he understood for the first time that his father had never once been talking about the river.

* * *

They moved the boats first—that was Isandro's insistence, delivered in a whisper that brooked no argument. Back down the side channel, under the willow curtain, hulls brushed with mud to kill the shine, gear redistributed into waterproof bags they could carry on their backs. It cost them an hour of light, and Pell spent the whole hour vibrating with impatience, checking and rechecking the logbook's final coordinates as though the numbers might rot if she looked away.

Then they climbed again, taking the willow-root stairs and then leaving them, cutting up through scrub where the tail grass grew tall enough to hide a crouched man. Tamás led—not because he knew this country, but because he was the best at walking quietly in it, a skill learned young, hunting duiker with his uncle in the flooded forest, learning how to place a foot so the ground forgot you'd stepped.

The terraces rose around them like the rings of a felled tree. Each shelf told its own decade. On the fourth level they passed a collapsed adit, its timber lintel black with age, spiderwebs strung across the dark mouth like curtains drawn by someone who'd left in a hurry a century ago. On the fifth, the masonry shifted to brick stamped with a company mark Pell photographed with shaking hands. On the sixth—

On the sixth, the past simply stopped pretending.

A road. An actual road, graded and graveled, switchbacking up the hillside, wide enough for trucks. Culverts of corrugated steel gleamed dully under the moss. Power lines ran along poles that had been planted, not grown, their insulators still white. And alongside the road, at intervals, markers: small metal posts painted regulation yellow, each stamped with a serial number and a crest Tamás didn't recognize but Isandro clearly did, because when she saw the first one she stopped walking entirely, and the look on her face was the look of a woman reading her own death warrant in a language she'd hoped to forget.

"You know that crest," Tamás said quietly.

"I know the format." Her jaw worked. "Government asset tags. I've seen ten thousand of them in another life. These are current-issue." She crouched, didn't touch the post, just studied it. "Current issue means current funding. Current funding means a ministry signed off on a mining operation inside a restricted watershed, and then denied the wreck, and then denied the survey, and then lost a survey team, and every single denial was written by someone who knew exactly what they were denying."

Pell made a sound low in her throat—not quite a word. Grief, maybe. Or vindication arriving too late to feel like anything but grief.

"Their instruments are still running," she said. "Back at the camp. Whoever took them didn't even shut down the data loggers. That's not a rescue, that's a *harvest.* They wanted the readings." She hugged the logbook tighter. "My team wasn't lost. They were *collected.*"

Above them, the hum deepened. The clanking resolved into something with a rhythm Tamás recognized from the dredges that worked the lower river in legal seasons—pump-and-hammer, pump-and-hammer—but scaled up, multiplied, a dozen dredge-hearts beating inside one mountain.

And then the trees broke, and they saw it.

* * *

The saddle held a bowl of worked earth perhaps four hundred meters across, and it was lit.

That was the thing Tamás would remember afterward, the detail that would surface unbidden for the rest of his life: it was mid-afternoon, the monsoon light thick and pewter-colored, and the bowl was lit anyway—floodlamps on scaffold towers, work lights strung along catwalks, the windows of prefabricated buildings glowing a flat institutional white. Against the grey sky it looked less like a camp than like a wound in the world, a place where the day had been surgically removed and replaced with something that never ended.

Terraced pits descended in steps toward the center, each step darker and wetter than the last. Pumps the size of houses sucked water up through pipes fat as temple drums, and where the water discharged into settling ponds, the ponds glowed. Not reflected light—*inherent* light, a pale opalescence, the same iridescence Tamás had been tracking upstream for six days, concentrated here into something almost beautiful, which was the most horrifying thing about it. It was beautiful. His village's poisoned wells, rendered at industrial scale, and gorgeous.

"There," Isandro breathed.

At the far rim of the bowl, half-shrouded in scaffold, stood the headframe of a mine shaft—new steel, freshly welded, a hoist wheel turning slowly at its crown. And beside it, a structure Tamás's eye kept sliding off and returning to: a low concrete building built into the hillside, windowless, its roof crowded with antennas. A relay station. A link in some chain of communication that officially did not exist, feeding signals to and from a hole in the mountain that officially did not exist, guarded by men who were officially on leave.

Movement everywhere. Figures in company coveralls on the catwalks. A truck grinding up the access road, headlights on despite the hour. And near the nearest pit, a cluster of figures in different clothing—civilian clothing, field clothing—and Tamás felt Pell go rigid beside him before he understood why.

"Pell—"

"That jacket." Her voice came out shredded. "The blue jacket. That's Okonjo's jacket. That's—I bought him that jacket when he made senior hydrologist, I was there, I—" She was already moving, scrambling up the last lip of scrub toward the saddle's edge, and Isandro caught her by the pack straps and hauled her down into cover with a force that knocked the breath out of her.

"Are you insane?"

"That's my *team,*" Pell gasped. "That's Okonjo, that's—there's Voss, the one limping, Voss broke his ankle in the rapids, I set it myself—he's *limping*, he's alive, they're all—" She twisted against Isandro's grip, and Tamás saw her face properly for the first time since the camp: not relief. Something far worse than relief. Because the surveyors weren't wandering free. They moved in a line, spaced evenly, and at either end of the line walked men with rifles carried not slung but *ready*, and behind the line rolled a utility vehicle with a mounted light, sweeping the pit floor in slow arcs.

Not colleagues. Inventory.

"They're working the pumps," Tamás said quietly. He could see it now—the surveyors' line terminating at a pump station, figures bending to gauges and valves under guard supervision. Skilled labor, conscripted. "They're keeping the operation running."

"Why?" Pell whispered. "Why keep them? Why not just—" She couldn't finish it.

"Because dead scientists can't calibrate extraction rates," Isandro said. Her voice was ice over deep water. "Because the consortium needs people who understand the aquifer to tell them how much poison they can pull before the watershed collapses. Your team isn't hostages, Pell. They're *consultants.*"

The word landed in the silence like a stone in a well. Tamás watched it sink through Pell, watched her arrive at the bottom of it: her colleagues, alive, kept breathing precisely because their expertise was worth more than their freedom. And somewhere in that arithmetic was the answer to the question that had been eating her since the empty camp—*why me, why spare the cartographer who stayed behind*—and the answer was: they hadn't decided yet. Or worse: they had, and the decision was still in transit.

"We have to get them out," Pell said. "Tonight. Now. We have to—"

"With what?" Isandro's whisper was razor-flat. "Three of us, no weapons, daylight, and a garrison we haven't counted yet. You run down that slope and you die, and Okonjo watches it happen, and then the guards know someone's in the hills and they sweep every terrace until they find our boats." She put a hand flat on Pell's sternum—not rough, just final. "We watch. We count. We learn the shift changes. We get one chance at this, and we take it *once.*"

Pell stared at her. And then, slowly, the fight drained out of her shoulders and something else replaced it—the terrible patience of a person who has just realized that loving people means sometimes doing nothing while they suffer, on the calculation that doing something would make everything permanently worse.

"How long?" she asked.

"Until dark. Then we count sentries by lamplight." Isandro settled into the scrub, making herself part of the hillside with the ease of long practice. "Get comfortable. We're going to be here a while."

Tamás settled too, but his eyes weren't on the guards or the pits or the surveyors' blue-jacketed line. They were on the windowless concrete building with the antennas, and on the yellow-tagged marker posts marching up the access road, and on the glowing ponds, and underneath all of it, on the memory of his father burning a canvas pack in the yard while the children watched and the smoke went straight up in the windless evening air.

*They pay you to forget, boy.*

He had come up this river telling himself he was guiding for money. He had let Isandro believe it. He had let himself believe it, mostly, on the good days.

But sitting here now, close enough to the source to feel its heat through the ground, watching the machinery of his father's unfinished story turn in the open air, Tamás Oyelaran finally admitted the truth he'd been carrying since the first iridescent film appeared on his village well: he hadn't hired a salvage diver. He'd hired a witness. Everything else—the rapids, the lies, the miles—they were just the price of getting her here.

Below, the hoist wheel turned. The cage descended into the mountain, carrying a load of something that glittered even in the floodlight, and the pumps drank, and the ponds glowed, and a guard on the nearest tower lifted a pair of binoculars and swept them slowly, very slowly, along the ridge line—

—and stopped.

Right where they lay.

Tamás went absolutely still, breath locked in his chest, and felt Isandro's hand find his wrist in the dark of the scrub and squeeze once:

*Don't move.*

The binoculars held. Five seconds. Ten. Somewhere below, a generator coughed and steadied. And then the guard lowered the glasses, said something to the man beside him, and turned away—and Tamás let his breath out in a thread, because the guard had turned away, yes, but the man he'd spoken to had not.

The second man was already reaching for a radio.
