## 10. The Living Camp

The generators told us before we saw anything.

We had been climbing out of the gorge for two hours, the terraces rising above us in broken steps of cut stone, and the sound had grown from a rumor to a fact: a low, patient thrumming that lived in the rock itself, in the soles of my boots, in the fillings of my teeth. Rivers make a hundred sounds—chatter, roar, the hollow gulp of water going under stone—but they do not hum. Machines hum. And machines, this far above the last legal mile of the river, meant one thing only: someone had paid an enormous amount of money for the world not to notice them.

Tamás stopped at the lip of the ninth terrace and crouched in the ferns, and I crouched beside him, and for a long moment none of us breathed. Pell knelt behind us with the survey instruments cradled against her chest like a sleeping child she was afraid to wake.

"There," Tamás said, barely a sound.

Below us, folded into a bowl of terraced ground where the old mining works had been carved into the hillside a century ago, lay a camp. But it was not the camp I had expected. I had expected tents, mud, the temporary geometry of a survey operation—tripods and transit cases and the sad little flags of a government presence. What I saw instead was permanence. Prefabricated structures bolted to concrete pads. Floodlights on steel masts, dark now in the afternoon but waiting. A crane. Stacked drums with stenciled markings I could read even at this distance, because I had spent ten years learning to read the language of things people dump into water. Tailings. Reagent. Processed concentrate. The drums were new. The concrete beneath them was newer.

And there were guards. Four that I could count from our angle, maybe more inside the light towers' blind spots, moving between the buildings with the unhurried economy of men who were bored rather than vigilant. Bored guards worried me more than alert ones. Alert guards expect trouble. Bored guards have stopped expecting anything at all, which means nothing has ever come for them—which means whatever they are guarding has never been seriously contested.

"That's not a survey camp," Pell whispered.

"No," I said.

"Surveys don't have cranes."

"No."

She was quiet a moment. Then, in a voice smaller than the whisper: "My team came up here eight weeks ago with permits for a hydrological assessment. Three weeks of sampling, then downriver." Her hands tightened on the instrument case. "There's no assessment happening here. There's *industry* here."

I looked at the camp again, properly this time, the way I look at a wreck before descending—reading structure, reading current, reading where the dead things are likely to be. The generators sat in a lined pit near the river's edge, and a pipe ran from the pit into the water, thick as a man's torso, and where it entered the river there was a faint stain on the surface that I might have called shadow if I hadn't spent my life learning the difference between shadow and chemistry. The water below the pipe moved wrong. It moved like water carrying something it didn't want.

Eight weeks ago, a hydrological team had come up this river with official permits. Eight weeks ago, the drums had already been stacked. Which meant the permits were either forged or purchased, and either way, the navy patrol boats we'd slipped past at the checkpoint had not been guarding a restricted zone.

They had been guarding a supply line.

* * *

We went down into the camp the way water goes down—finding every crack, committing to none of them until the last moment. Tamás led, because he knew this country the way I knew wrecks: by feel, by the memory of how stone breaks. He took us along a drainage channel between two terraces, through a stand of bamboo that smelled crushed and green, across a stretch of open ground during the exact ninety seconds when the guards changed position and their shadows all fell the same way. Pell shook the whole crossing. I put my hand between her shoulder blades and kept it there, and she steadied against my palm like a hull against a fender.

The building we chose was the farthest from the generator pit—a prefabricated bunkhouse with its door standing open to the heat, unguarded, because no one guards a room full of prisoners who have nowhere to run to.

I looked inside and understood, all at once, why the river upstream had felt wrong to me since the checkpoint. Not because of the chemistry. Because of the silence. This whole valley had the acoustics of a place where shouting had been tried and discontinued.

Six people sat at a long table under a hanging lamp, working. That was the word for it—working. Charts spread before them. Sample vials in racks. One woman was writing figures into a ledger with the mechanical diligence of someone who had learned that diligence was safer than thought. They wore civilian clothes gone gray with wear, and they wore lanyards, and the lanyards held identification cards with a logo I recognized from the crate in my boathouse: a stylized watershed, blue lines converging on a point. The consortium's mark. The mark that had been stamped, I now understood, on equipment the navy officially denied existed.

They looked up when we came in—six faces lifting in the lamplight—and what crossed those faces was not fear. It was something worse. It was the flat, practiced blankness of people who had learned that surprise gets punished.

Except one of them.

A young man at the end of the table, thin, sunburned across the cheekbones, with the calloused hands of someone who had handled rope and rock before handling paper. He looked at us—at me, specifically—and his face did something the other five faces did not do. It broke open.

"You're real," he said. His voice cracked on the second word. "You're actually—" He stood up so fast his chair went over. "People came. Someone actually came."

"Sit down," said the woman with the ledger, without looking up. "For God's sake, sit down, Dario."

"They came, Marta." He was laughing now, or crying; from a distance the two sound identical. "Eight weeks. Eight weeks and I told you someone would—"

"I said *sit down*."

But he didn't sit. He came around the table toward us, and Tamás shifted to block him, gentle as a gate swinging shut, and the man stopped and pressed both hands over his mouth and stood there shaking, and I watched the other five go back to their work with the terrible obedience of the long-broken, and I thought: *this is what the navy's silence looks like from the inside.* Not a wall. A table. A lamp. Six people doing arithmetic for the thing that owns them.

"We're looking for the survey team," Pell said. Her voice came out steady, which cost her something; I could see it costing her. "Cartographic unit nine. They transmitted a chemical signature from this grid six weeks ago and then went silent."

The woman—Marta—finally looked up. She was perhaps fifty, with the deep tan and permanent squint of fieldwork, and her eyes went over Pell's face, then mine, then Tamás's, cataloguing us the way I catalogue a dive site. When she spoke, her voice was dry as ledger paper.

"Unit nine is sitting at this table, cartographer. Minus two. Okafor tried the fence in week three. Vasquez tried the radio in week four." She turned a page. "The rest of us do our work, and we eat, and we live. You'd be surprised how reasonable captivity becomes after the first month."

"The transmission," Pell said. "The industrial signature. You found it before any of this—"

"We found everything." For the first time, something moved under Marta's flatness—not anger exactly, but its sediment. "We found the tailings breach, the aquifer contamination, the valve schedules, the shipping manifests. We documented all of it, beautifully, with chain of custody and duplicate records, because we are professionals." She closed the ledger at last. "And then a patrol boat arrived with a letter from the ministry confirming our permits had been revoked retroactively, and a man very politely explained that our findings would be reviewed in-house, and our review would take approximately as long as we remained useful. So now we review ourselves. Daily. Into their ledgers. With their stamps."

Dario was still standing there, staring at us, drinking us in like a man who'd been underwater too long. And I recognized the look, because I had worn it once myself, years ago, in another life, in a hearing room with fluorescent lights—the look of someone watching rescue arrive and knowing, with sick certainty, that rescue changes nothing unless the rescued can still be saved.

Then his gaze sharpened. Focused. Tilted, slightly, the way a man's gaze tilts when recognition moves through him sideways, arriving before he understands what it is.

He said, "Do I know you?"

"No," I said.

It came out fast. Too fast. Fast the way a hull pops when it's been stressed past tolerance—one clean sound and then the water comes in.

"You do," he said slowly. "I know your face. Not from—" He frowned, reaching. "Not from here. From before. From the hearings. You were—" His eyes went wide. "*You were on the commission.*"

The room changed. Five heads lifted from five sets of charts. Even Marta went still, her pen hovering above the page, and in the lamplight I watched six strangers arrive at the same conclusion at six different speeds, and I felt the old name rise in me like silt disturbed off a wreck's deck, clouding everything, refusing to settle.

I had buried that name ten years ago. I had buried it under a diver's registration, a salvage license, a boathouse with a good lock, a discipline of miles logged instead of years lived. I had built a life the way you build a wreck penetration: one fixed line at a time, never turning around, never stirring the bottom. And now a half-starved cartographer in a stolen lanyard had put his hand on the line and pulled.

"My name is Isandro Coldharbour," I said. "I'm a salvage diver. I was hired to recover equipment from a wreck that doesn't exist."

"That's not—" Dario took a step toward me. "That's not what I'm asking. You were Isandro Voss. You chaired the inquiry. You wrote the report that got buried—you stood up in front of the ministry and read the contamination figures out loud and they *buried* it, they buried you, they—" His voice climbed. "Half of us joined the service because of that report. Because someone finally said it out loud. And then you vanished, and everyone said you'd taken their money, and I *hated* you for six years—"

"Dario." Marta's voice cracked across the room like a winch cable snapping taut. "Outside. Now."

"He *is* the report," Dario said. "She's the reason any of us—"

"*Now.*"

One of the guards laughed somewhere outside, distant, easy. The sound came through the prefab wall thin as paper, and everyone in the room flinched except Marta, and I understood that the flinch was the true census of this place—who had been here long enough to learn it.

Pell was staring at me. Tamás was staring at me. Two people I had led up a forbidden river for eight days, two people whose lives I had held in my hands at the rapids and in the black water under the terraces, both looking at me as if I were a chart they had been navigating by and had just discovered was drawn in the wrong decade.

"Voss," Pell said quietly. "The Meridian Inquiry. Ten years ago. The dam company, the seepage cover-up, the village that—" She stopped. Recalculated. "You're supposed to be dead. Professionally dead. Everyone in the service knows the name Voss ended."

"It ended," I said.

"It's standing right in front of me."

"Yes."

Tamás said nothing at all. That was the worst of it. He leaned back against the doorframe with his arms crossed and watched me with an expression I couldn't read, and I realized I had been reading him wrong for days—I had filed his silences under *guide*, under *local knowledge*, under *man with his own reasons*—and now every silence re-sorted itself into something else, something I had no category for.

Marta came back in alone. She looked at me for a long moment, and then she did something I did not expect. She extended her hand.

"Marta Ilves. Unit nine. Formerly." Her grip was dry and strong. "I read your report. All four hundred pages, before they shredded it. It was the best hydrological document I've ever seen, and I've spent thirty years in this work." She let go. "I also read the addendum they made you sign. The recantation. I always wondered what they'd offered you to sign it."

"They offered me a choice," I said. "Between my name and my life. I took the trade. I've been diving under a different flag ever since."

"And now?"

"Now I'm here."

"Yes," she said. "Here." She glanced toward the window, toward the light towers and the bored guards and the pipe bleeding its stain into the river. "You should understand what 'here' means before you decide what it costs. There's a shipment going downriver Thursday. Concentrate, sealed drums, escorted. The man who runs this operation has been trying to find a licensed pilot-diver for three weeks, because the intake channel silted after the last storm and his own people won't dive it. He needs someone with salvage certification and no history." Her eyes came back to mine, and they were not unkind, which somehow made it worse. "And yesterday his security officer pulled the crew manifest from the checkpoint patrol. Your name came through their scanner, cartographer. Yours too. And yours, guide—they logged all three of you at Mile Forty-One."

The room went very quiet. Outside, the generators hummed their patient note, and the river carried its invisible cargo past the camp, down through the gorge, down past the flooded forest, down toward a village where children drank from wells that ran faintly iridescent in the evening light.

"So," Marta said. "They know you're coming. The question that matters now—the only question—is whether they know *why*. And whether, when they offer you whatever they offered you ten years ago, you'll find the price has gone up."

Dario was still looking at me across the ruined table, and his expression had settled into something that was neither hatred nor forgiveness but the raw material of both.

"You wrote the truth once," he said. "And then you buried it. So tell me, Voss—Coldharbour—whatever your name is." He leaned forward. "What happens the second time?"
