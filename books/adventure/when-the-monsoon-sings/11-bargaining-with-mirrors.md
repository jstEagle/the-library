## 11. Bargaining With Mirrors

Wren Pell had spent eleven years learning to read terrain from a distance—contour lines that whispered of cliffs, the pale seam of a dry riverbed hiding under green, the way a forest canopy changed its texture a half-mile before the ground fell away into gorge. Distance was mercy. Distance was the cartographer's oldest trick: you could make anything survivable if you shrank it enough.

There was no distance here.

She stood at the edge of the consortium's terrace camp with her hands bound loosely in front of her—not tied, *arranged*, the way a host arranges flowers—and watched two men in gray uniforms walk Isandro Coldharbour toward the long tent at the camp's center, and there was nothing between Wren and what was about to happen except forty feet of stamped earth and her own useless voice.

They had been brought in at dawn. Not captured—*received*. That was the word the man named Voss had used, smiling, when the patrol found them on the switchback trail below the terraces: *received*, as though they were parcels, as though the whole mountain had been waiting for delivery. Tamás had fought first, of course; she'd heard the scuffle before she saw it, heard Isandro's low voice cutting through it like a blade through reeds—*not here, not now, don't give them a reason*—and then they'd all been walking, escorted, up through the humming terraces where the generators breathed their diesel hymn into the thin morning air.

Now the others were held in a barracks building near the eastern works, and Isandro was being taken alone to the long tent, and Voss had stopped beside Wren on his way past, the way a man stops to admire a view he owns.

"You'll want to see this part," he said. "Most people do."

"I don't want anything from you."

"No." He considered her with something almost like kindness, which was worse than cruelty; cruelty she could have mapped, could have named and contoured and understood. "But you'll watch anyway. People always watch. It's why we don't bother locking doors."

He wasn't wrong. That was the humiliation of it. The door to the long tent stood open, and no one stopped Wren when she drifted toward it after them, close enough to see inside, close enough to be seen and dismissed—a disgraced surveyor, a woman whose name had been struck from three professional registries, someone whose opinion had been formally declared worthless by institutions far larger than this one. They let her stand at the threshold because letting her stand there was itself a message: *we are not afraid of what you know.*

Inside, the tent was absurdly civilized. A folding table with a white cloth. A carafe of water catching the lamplight. Two chairs. And on the table, arranged with the care of a museum exhibit, a folder, a brass key on a chain, and a single sheet of paper covered in dense official typesetting.

Voss entered behind her and took the seat opposite Isandro without invitation. He was perhaps fifty, silver at the temples, with the soft hands of a man who had never hauled anything heavier than a pen, and he wore his authority the way some men wore cologne—generously, so it arrived before he did.

"Ms. Coldharbour," he said. "Or—" he paused, delicately, a jeweler selecting a stone, "—shall we use the other name? I find people prefer their real names, eventually. It's exhausting, answering to an alias. Like wearing shoes that don't fit."

Wren saw it land. She saw it the way she saw everything—the flinch measured in millimeters, the stillness that was too deliberate to be calm. Isandro's face did not change, but her shoulders drew in by a degree, a coastline eroding by inches, and Wren thought: *she's done this before. Someone has held her name over her before, and she survived it, and she is deciding right now whether to survive it again.*

"Say whatever you came to say," Isandro said.

"What I came to say." Voss unfolded the typed sheet but didn't read from it; he knew it by heart, which told Wren this speech had been given before, probably more than once, probably to other divers, other witnesses, other problems that had walked up the mountain under their own power. "Is that you have found something. And having found it, you have exactly two options, and I am going to describe both of them honestly, because I believe honesty is cheaper than violence in the long run."

He poured water. Three glasses. He pushed one toward Isandro, one toward Wren at the threshold—remembered her, or pretended to—and kept one for himself, though he didn't drink.

"Option one," he said. "You take the shipment down."

He turned the folder around and opened it. Even from the threshold Wren could see the photographs inside: glossy, professional, the kind of documentation that cost money. A boat. A route map down the gorge with mile markers inked in a careful hand. A schedule.

"There's a cargo run leaving in six days," Voss continued. "Pilot's berth, paid at four times whatever your last contract was, plus a completion bonus that would buy you a new life in any port on the coast. You run the boat downriver, quiet, routine, the way dozens of runs go down this river every season. Nobody asks you what's in the hold. Nobody has ever asked. In exchange—" he tapped the typed sheet, "—full reinstatement. Your certifications restored. Your records sealed. Not destroyed—we're not barbarians—but sealed, which means the incident ten years ago becomes what it should have been all along: a filing error. A bad day that never happened. You walk out of here with a future that has your actual name on it. Not Coldharbour. The name you buried. The one that used to mean something."

The lamp hummed. Somewhere outside, a generator changed pitch, and beneath it Wren could hear the river, far down in its gorge, running wrong.

"And option two," Isandro said. Her voice was flat as slate.

"Option two," Voss said, and for the first time his warmth dropped away entirely, like a mask lifted rather than a mask put on, and Wren understood that the warmth had been the performance and this—this patient, administrative emptiness—was the man underneath. "You refuse. And then we proceed with the arrangements already in place. The survey team you've been looking for will finish their contract—they signed extensions, all of them, quite willingly once they understood the terms—and they will be relocated somewhere comfortable. Your guide will return to his village, which will continue to receive its medical outreach program, which is generous, considering. And you and Ms. Pell will be escorted back downriver to the nearest garrison town, where your names will enter a different kind of file. One that follows people. One that finds work before work finds them." He folded his hands. "No one dies, Ms. Coldharbour. I want to be clear about that. We are not murderers. We are simply the reason, and you would be the silence, and the river does the rest. Rivers are very good at doing the rest. They've been doing it since before either of us was born."

Wren's mouth was dry. Her glass of water sat untouched in her bound hands, sweating.

Because here was the thing—the thing she would carry afterward, the thing that made her complicit in a way she hadn't yet learned how to spell out: she had decoded the transmission. She knew what was in the water. She knew the chemical signature predated every official permit, every navy denial, every salvage contract built on lies. She knew, and she was standing at a threshold watching a bargain being made with her friend, and some cold, small, survival-shaped part of her brain was already calculating: *if Isandro takes the offer, I'm escorted out too. If Isandro takes the offer, I live.*

Temptation, she was learning, did not arrive wearing a stranger's face. It arrived wearing your own. It arrived sounding like your own voice saying *be reasonable, be practical, you've lost everything once already, you know what losing costs.* Ten years ago Wren Pell had signed off on a flood-control survey she knew was falsified, because a superior told her the correction would come later, in the next revision, and she had wanted to keep her position, and nineteen people downstream had drowned in a valley she had personally certified safe. She had not been evil. She had been *reasonable*. Reasonableness was the whole trick.

"Six days," Isandro said.

"The shipment leaves in six days. Your answer can wait until then. We're not savages." Voss smiled again, the warmth returning like weather. "Take the evening. Look at the folder. Look at the reinstatement order—it's genuine, countersigned, you can verify the seals. Everything you lost, returned. All you have to do is pilot a boat."

*And say nothing,* Wren thought. *All you have to do is be the silence.*

Isandro looked down at the folder. And Wren watched her—watched with the terrible clarity of someone who had spent her life reading surfaces for what lay beneath—and saw something she recognized with a sick lurch of kinship. It wasn't greed. It wasn't fear. It was longing. Pure, naked, unguarded longing, the look of a woman being shown a photograph of a house she'd once lived in and burned down herself. The sealed records. The name restored. The possibility of walking into a room someday and not feeling the temperature drop, not seeing people decide what they'd heard about her before she'd said a word.

Ten years of miles logged instead of years lived. Ten years of rules, of aliases, of a self packed away in crates. And here it all was, offered back, priced at one boat ride and one silence.

*Take it,* whispered the reasonable voice in Wren's own head, wearing her face. *Take it and we both walk out of here.*

Isandro reached out and touched the brass key on its chain. Just touched it. Didn't pick it up.

"What's this?" she asked.

"The pilot's berth key," Voss said. "Yours, whenever you want it."

"It opens the boat?"

"It opens everything you'd need. The boat, the fuel store, the crew quarters." He leaned back. "Consider it a promise. We keep our promises, Ms. Coldharbour. Ask anyone downstream who's ever taken our money."

*That's exactly what frightens me,* Wren thought. *Everyone downstream has taken your money. Everyone downstream is bought, or sick, or both.*

Isandro withdrew her hand from the key slowly, the way you withdraw a hand from water you've decided is too cold—or not cold enough. She looked at the folder a moment longer. Then she looked up, and her eyes found Wren's across the threshold, across the forty feet of stamped earth and everything unsaid between them, and held there for one second, two, and Wren could not read them, could not contour them, and hated herself for needing to.

"I said my answer can wait," Voss prompted gently.

"It can," Isandro said, and stood, and pushed back from the table, and the chair legs scraped the tent floor with a sound like something tearing. "I'll take the evening."

Voss inclined his head, satisfied, a man who had made this sale many times and recognized the closing signs—or believed he did. "Someone will show you to quarters. Dinner is at seven. The food is better than you'd expect."

Outside, the light had gone long and amber, and the terraces above the camp caught it and threw it back in dull metallic bands, tier upon tier of cut stone descending into the gorge like the steps of a drowned temple. Generators droned. Men in gray moved cargo with unhurried efficiency. Everything about the place was orderly, industrious, almost beautiful, in the way that wounds are beautiful on the X-ray, clean and legible and full of damage.

Wren fell into step beside Isandro as an escort led them toward the barracks, and neither of them spoke until the escort peeled off at a doorway and left them standing in the corridor with a barred window pouring gold light across the floorboards.

"You're going to take it," Wren said. She hadn't meant to say it aloud. It came out anyway, cracked and small.

Isandro didn't answer immediately. She was looking at her own hands, turning them over, as though checking them for something—damage, or evidence, or the ghost of a rope.

"Ten years ago," she said finally, quietly, "someone offered me almost exactly this. Records sealed. Name restored. Future included." A breath. "I took it. That's what the alias is. That's what Coldharbour is. It's what taking it cost."

Wren waited. The generator hummed. Down in the gorge the river ran on, warm and faintly iridescent, carrying its poison toward villages that had no idea, toward wells that children drank from, toward the sea.

"So you know what the offer is worth," Wren said. "You know exactly what it buys."

"I know precisely." Isandro closed her hands. When she looked up, her eyes were steady and unreadable as deep water, and Wren realized she still couldn't tell—which might have been the most frightening thing of all, that after everything, after the wreck and the crate and the empty boots by the dead fire and nine days of river, she still couldn't read this woman's depths. "Which is why I need to see something before I answer. Tonight. There's an intake structure on the lower works—I saw it on the charts in the crate, marked with government stamps. I want to look at it underwater, where their paperwork can't reach."

"They'll never allow—"

"They don't have to allow it." Something moved behind Isandro's eyes then, brief and cold and certain, and for the first time Wren thought she glimpsed the bottom. "I just need to know one thing first."

"What?"

"Whether the man who guided us up this mountain brought me here to find the truth," Isandro said, "or to deliver me to it."

Down the corridor, a door opened, and Tamás Oyelaran stepped out of the barracks room where the guards had put him, and stopped when he saw them standing together in the gold light, and his face did something complicated and quick—guilt and hope and fear braided into a single expression—before he smoothed it over into the easy guide's smile that Wren had trusted for nine days and was only now beginning to understand had been lying to her the entire time.

"Good," Isandro said softly, not to either of them, or maybe to both. "Then we can all stop pretending at dinner."

* * *

Dinner was served at seven in the mess tent, under lanterns, at a table set for four: Voss, Isandro, Wren, and Tamás, with guards at the canvas walls and the folder from the long tent lying closed beside Voss's plate like a guest of honor. The food was, infuriatingly, excellent—river fish, rice, greens, bread still warm—and Wren ate mechanically, tasting nothing, watching the candles gutter each time the flap of the tent lifted.

Voss talked pleasantly throughout, about the mine's history, about the region's poverty, about how much good the consortium's payroll did in towns that had none. Tamás answered questions about the river's moods with the fluent ease of a man reciting scripture, and laughed at the right moments, and Wren sat beside him cataloguing every laugh like a surveyor logging faults in a bridge.

It was Isandro who ended it. She set down her fork, folded her hands, and said, in the clear flat voice she used on the water:

"Show me the intake valve tomorrow. Before I give you my answer."

The tent went very quiet. Voss's smile stayed exactly where it was, but Wren saw his fingers still against the stem of his glass, and saw, too, the flicker of his gaze toward Tamás—one glance, quick as a card sharp checking his partner—and understood, with a lurch, that the offer had never been meant for Isandro alone.

"Of course," Voss said warmly. "Tomorrow, then. After breakfast."

Across the table, Tamás was staring at his plate, and his knuckles were white around his spoon, and he did not look up at anyone at all.
