## 12. Tamás's River

The fire had burned down to a red heart by the time Tamás decided he could not carry it another mile.

They had made camp on a gravel shelf where the river widened and went quiet, the kind of water his grandmother had called *the river thinking*—slow enough to hold the sky, deep enough to hide what it didn't want found. Across the channel, the flooded forest stood knee-deep in black water, the drowned trees wearing their pale bark like shirtsleeves rolled up for work. Somewhere above them, past the gorge walls that leaned closer with every mile upstream, was Terrace Country. Past that, the headwaters. Past that, everything.

Isandro sat with her back against a pack, cleaning her dive knife with the same unhurried strokes she used for everything—like time was something she rationed out of a tin. Pell was asleep already, or pretending to be, curled under a tarp with her survey instruments cradled against her chest like a sleeping child she couldn't put down. The firelight moved on Isandro's face and Tamás watched it move, and thought: *she will not forgive this.* And then he thought: *she has to hear it anyway.*

Because tomorrow they would reach the terraces. And after the terraces came the intake.

He fed a stick into the coals and watched it catch, and the smell of woodsmoke rose up and did what woodsmoke always did—it carried him home. Downstream. Nineteen miles downriver and then off the main channel and up a slough so narrow the trees closed overhead like hands folding, to a village of forty-one roofs that the maps called nothing at all because the maps had never bothered. Oyelaran country. His mother's dock. His sister's garden going gray.

"Your turn for watch," Isandro said, without looking up from the knife.

"I know." He turned the stick. "I want to tell you something first."

She looked up then. She had a way of looking—level, unblinking, like a woman reading water before a dive, checking for current under the calm. He'd been on the receiving end of it since the day he'd agreed too easily to guide her, and he knew what it meant: *I see you lying, and I'm waiting to see how long you'll keep doing it.*

"You've been building to something since Mile Fourteen," she said.

"I have."

"Then say it."

*Say it.* As if it were one thing, one stone he could lift from his chest and set down between them. But it wasn't a stone. It was a whole riverbed, and every rock he shifted would show the silt underneath.

* * *

He started, as he always started, with the wells.

"My village is nineteen miles downstream," he said. "You know that. I told you when you hired me—I told you my family lives there, told you I know every bend of this river because I grew up swimming it. All true. What I didn't tell you is why I agreed to take you upstream. Not the money. You paid well, but I'd have done it for nothing, and I think maybe you suspected that."

Isandro said nothing. The knife lay still in her hands.

"The first well went wrong in the dry season, two years ago. My aunt's well, the old one behind the church. The water came up sweet as always, and then by evening it had a shine to it. A rainbow skin, like oil, but nobody had spilled anything. We thought a dead animal had gotten into the casing. We pulled the pump, scrubbed the pipe, let it run for three days. The shine came back." He watched the fire eat the stick down to his fingers. "Then the second well. Then the third. Now eleven of our fourteen wells run iridescent if they sit overnight. The water tastes fine. That's the worst part. It tastes fine, and it shines, and we drink it anyway because there's nowhere else to drink from."

"And the children," Isandro said quietly.

It wasn't a question. He looked at her sharply, but her face gave him nothing except attention—the total, undivided attention of someone who had spent years listening to rivers and knew how to listen to people the same way.

"How do you know about the children?"

"Because I've seen this before," she said. "Not here. Somewhere else. Water that looks wrong and tastes fine is never just water." She paused. "Tell me about the children, Tamás."

So he did.

"There are nine children under ten in the village. Six of them have had fevers that come and go like weather. Two have sores on their gums that won't close. My niece, Adaeze—my sister's girl, seven years old—she stopped growing last year. Not stopped eating; she eats. She just... stopped. Like the river took whatever was supposed to make her taller and kept it for itself." His voice caught on the last word and he let it, because hiding it now would be one lie too many. "The clinic boat comes twice a season. They test the water and write numbers in a book and say the levels are 'within tolerance.' Tolerance for what? For whom? Not for Adaeze. Her hair falls out in handfuls when my sister brushes it."

The fire popped. Across the channel, something small moved through the drowned forest—a bird, or a fish rising—and the sound of it was enormous in the silence.

"So two months ago," Tamás said, "when a stranger came downriver asking after guides who knew the upper reaches, I said yes before she finished asking. Because I needed something. Not money." He met Isandro's eyes. "I needed a diver."

* * *

The silence stretched long enough that he heard the river again—not the slow thinking-water beside them but the memory of it, all of it, the whole length of the river talking at once. The rapids at Mile Eleven where he'd nearly swamped the boat and covered it with a joke. Mile Sixteen, where he'd steered them wide around a side channel and lied that it dead-ended in shoals, when really it led toward an old service road that climbed toward the terraces. Every deflection. Every question he'd answered with a question. Every time Isandro had asked why a man whose family lived downstream wanted so badly to go upstream, and he'd given her some smooth answer about family land, about ancestral rights, about knowing what was in his own watershed.

She'd known. He'd watched her know, the way you watch a fisherman watch your line, and he'd kept reeling anyway.

"The intake valve," Isandro said at last. "You said that like it means something specific."

"It does." He reached into his shirt and drew out the thing he'd been carrying against his skin since the day he left home, wrapped in oilcloth, warm now from his body. He unwrapped it and held it out across the fire.

A valve wheel. Small, brass, green with age—but not river-green. It was corroded in a way he'd learned to read the way Isandro read water: evenly, chemically, eaten from the inside by something that had flowed over it for years. Stamped into the metal, half-legible under the crust, was a serial number and a government foundry mark.

"My grandfather helped build the old works at the terraces," Tamás said. "Before the war, before it was abandoned. When I was a boy we used to find pieces of it washed down after floods—bolts, plates, once a whole gauge-glass. Souvenirs. Toys. My grandmother made me stop keeping them when one cut my hand and the cut wouldn't heal." He turned the wheel so the firelight caught the stamp. "This came up in my aunt's well. Two years ago. Pulled out of the casing sixty feet down, below the aquifer line, where nothing from the surface should ever reach."

Isandro took it from him. She handled it the way she handled everything recovered from deep water—with respect, with a kind of grief, as if the object were a body. Her thumb traced the foundry mark. Then the serial number. Then she went very still, in the particular way he had learned meant her mind had gone somewhere fast and far.

"This isn't salvage corrosion," she said. "This is process-side. This wheel sat on a line carrying tailings effluent, not clean water." She looked up. "There's a working intake under the terraces. Submerged. Drawing straight from the aquifer—or discharging into it."

"That's what I believe. That's what I need you to confirm." He heard his own voice go flat and careful, the voice he used for the hardest rapids. "My cousin dove the slough near the terraces last spring, before the patrols got thick. He found a concrete collar under the bank, a pipe big enough to swim into, running back under the hill. He got thirty meters in before his light failed and his nerve with it. But he felt the flow, Isandro. Moving water, underground, where no moving water should be. Inflow or outflow—he couldn't tell. And at the collar there was a valve assembly, brass, with a wheel on it just like this one."

"You want me to dive it."

"I want you to dive it. Find the valve. Tell me which way the water moves. If it's drawing—if they're pumping *from* the aquifer—then the shine in our wells is coming from somewhere else and I can stop looking. But if it's discharging—" His throat closed. He made himself finish. "If it's discharging, then everything my family has drunk for two years came out of that hillside, and somebody built the pipe, and stamped the parts, and locked the gate, and went home to clean water."

* * *

Isandro set the valve wheel down on the gravel between them, gently, the way you set down something that might still detonate.

"When," she said. "When were you going to tell me?"

"Honestly? I planned to tell you at the terraces. Tomorrow night. After I'd seen the works myself and knew whether the story even held together." He laughed, short and without humor. "But plans are for people who aren't scared. I've been scared since Mile Five. Every mile upstream I kept thinking, *if I wait until she's invested, she can't leave. If I wait until she's seen it, she can't refuse.* I was managing you. The way you manage a client. The way—" he hesitated— "the way I manage everyone, if you want the truth, and I think you do want the truth, because you've been watching me do it since the day we met."

"I have," Isandro said. No softness in it, but no cruelty either. Just the level water of her. "You agreed too easily. You steered us wide at the side channel and lied about the shoals. You asked about my past more than any guide I've ever hired and answered nothing about your own. I had you figured for consortium within the first week. Paid to get a diver up here and lose her."

The words landed harder than he expected—harder than anger would have. He flinched, and saw her register the flinch.

"I'm not consortium," he said.

"I know that now. The valve wheel bought you more credibility than any oath could have." She picked it up again, weighed it in her palm. "Nobody carries evidence like this for two years unless it's their own blood in the story."

"It is my own blood in the story." He pulled the collar of his shirt aside, showing her the ridge of scar tissue along his shoulder where the gauge-glass had cut him at nine years old, the cut that wouldn't heal for a month. "Maybe literally. Maybe that's what the tests would say if anyone ever ran them on us instead of writing *within tolerance* and rowing away." He fed the fire another stick, needing his hands busy. "You want to know the worst part? The part I couldn't say out loud until right now? I didn't hire you to expose anything. I'm not Pell, with her charts and her transmissions. I hired you because my niece is seven years old and stopped growing, and I am not a diver, and the answer to why is sitting at the bottom of a flooded hillside where only someone like you can go and look at it and come back and *tell me*. That's all. That's the whole ugly selfish truth of it. I used you. I picked you up at the dock like a tool."

The fire cracked. Isandro was quiet for a long moment, turning the wheel over in her fingers.

"Do you want to know what I was doing when you hired me?" she said finally.

"Running from something. Everyone who counts miles instead of years is running from something."

That surprised her—he saw it land, saw the flicker behind her eyes—and he realized he'd just told her something true about herself she hadn't handed him, and that maybe the ledger between them wasn't as lopsided as he'd feared.

"I was going to say," she continued slowly, "that using someone is only unforgivable if you were planning to throw them away. Were you?"

"No."

"Did you lie to me about anything that changed where we went, or what we faced?"

"No. The route was always honest. Only my reasons weren't."

"Then here's the difference between you and the people who built that pipe." She nodded at the valve wheel. "They stamped their names on the parts and hid the parts underwater. You hid your reasons, and tonight you hauled them up yourself, from sixty feet down, and put them in my hands." She set the wheel on the gravel between them again, deliberately, a marker driven into shared ground. "I've spent ten years burying my name so deep that sometimes I forget it myself. I don't get to judge a man for keeping one secret about a well."

Tamás breathed out. He hadn't known he'd been holding it since Mile Five.

* * *

Pell's voice came out of the dark, muffled by the tarp. "I've been awake for the last ten minutes, and I want it noted that I guessed most of this at Mile Twelve."

"Pell," Isandro said.

"The side-channel detour. The questions about the headwaters. The way he flinched every time I mentioned water testing." The tarp rustled and the cartographer sat up, hair flattened on one side, eyes already bright with the particular hunger she got around data. "Also, if that's a foundry stamp I'm seeing, give it here, because I have the consortium procurement codes memorized and I can date that casting to within a year."

Tamás passed the wheel over and watched Pell hold it to the firelight, lips moving, fingers counting stamp characters—and felt the strange lightness that follows a confession, the way the river feels after a flood passes and the water drops and shows you the shape of its own bed. Exposed. Ugly in places. But *knowable*, finally, all the way down.

"One more thing," he said. "And then I'll take first watch, and I'll never keep anything from either of you again, on my mother's dock I swear it."

Pell looked up from the stamp. Isandro waited.

"The patrol boats," Tamás said. "The ones that stopped us at the checkpoint. They're not navy. I've run this river twenty years—I know the hulls, the engine notes, the patrol schedules. Those boats fuel at the consortium depot at Mile Twenty-Two. Which means the checkpoint wasn't the government stopping strangers." He looked into the fire. "It was the mine making sure no one reached the terraces. And tomorrow, when we get there, we won't be sneaking past the government. We'll be sneaking past the people who poisoned my niece."

Silence. Then Pell, softly, holding the wheel up so the firelight poured through the corroded letters:

"Isandro. This serial number. It matches the intake hardware spec from the survey team's stolen manifest—the page they marked RESTRICTED." She swallowed. "Which means the valve Tamás's cousin found isn't old mining infrastructure at all. It's new. It's *current*. Whatever that pipe is doing under the terraces—"

"—it's doing it right now," Isandro finished.

She was already reaching for her dive bag.

* * *

Tamás took the first watch as promised, though no one slept much after that. He sat facing downstream, the way he always did at night on this river—facing home, facing the nineteen miles of water between him and forty-one roofs and a girl who had stopped growing—and listened to the current move against the hull of the world.

Tomorrow: the terraces. The generators humming in the dark. And somewhere beneath the hill, a brass wheel turning or not turning, an answer flowing one direction or the other, waiting at the end of a pipe only Isandro Coldharbour could follow.

He found, to his surprise, that he was no longer afraid of what she'd find.

He was afraid of what she'd do about it—and grateful, already, to be standing beside her when she did it.
