## 13. The Source

The river stopped being a river two miles back.

Isandro felt it before she saw it—the current losing its argument with itself, the water going glassy and obedient in a way no honest river ever was. Upstream of the terraces, the Monsoon's headwaters spread into a chain of still pools, each one feeding the next through channels too straight to be natural. Dug channels. Engineered water. Somewhere above them, behind the shoulder of the gorge, the true source waited, and it was not a spring.

"It's called the Throat," Tamás said, easing the boat through the last narrows. His voice had changed since the terraces—flatter, careful, the voice of a man walking on his own grave. "My grandfather's word for it. He said the mountain swallowed a lake once, and everything since has been the mountain learning to spit."

"Charming folklore," Pell murmured from the bow, her instruments cradled like an infant.

"It isn't folklore." Tamás cut the engine and let the boat drift. The silence that followed had weight. "The wells started changing eleven years ago. My grandfather said the Throat had opened again. Everyone laughed at him." He looked at Isandro, and there was nothing guarded left in his face; whatever lies he'd been carrying upriver, he had set them down somewhere in the last mile. "Nobody's laughing now."

They rounded the final bend and Isandro understood, all at once, why the navy denied this place existed.

The headwaters were not headwaters at all. They were outflow.

A concrete face rose out of the gorge wall ahead of them, forty meters of poured dam disguised—badly, or perhaps never meant to be seen—with shotcrete and netting meant to look like living rock. Water sheeted from three great sluice gates near its base, and where it fell into the pool below, the surface bloomed with that faint, wrong iridescence she'd first seen at the survey camp: a rainbow skin thin as breath, oil-slick colors that weren't oil. Above the dam, hidden by the ridge, something hummed. Generators. She could feel them in her teeth.

"That's not a mine entrance," Pell said quietly. "That's a *lid*."

"Everything I was told," Isandro said, "was that we were recovering equipment from a wreck." She heard how her own voice sounded—level, professional, the voice she used when the bottom dropped out of things. Ten years of practice. "There is no wreck. There never was a wreck."

"No," Tamás said. "There's a door."

* * *

They moored in the lee of a rock shelf where the patrol boats' sweep pattern couldn't reach—a gap in the coverage Tamás knew without being told, which told Isandro he had watched this place longer than he'd admitted. Longer than anyone sane would watch it.

Pell unpacked the survey team's remaining instruments with hands that shook only slightly. "The intake readings cluster here," she said, marking the pool on her chart. "Whatever's bleeding into the aquifer enters below the waterline. The chemical signature gets stronger the deeper you go. My colleagues logged it for six days before—" She stopped. Before they were taken. Before boots appeared beside a fire with no tracks leading out.

"So I go down," Isandro said.

"You don't know what's down there."

"I never do. That's the job." She checked her gauge bottle, her line, her knife. Rituals. Rules. The comfort of rules. "I go down, I find the source, I document it. That's all. No heroics. If the shaft narrows past my shoulders, I turn around and we find another way."

"And if it doesn't narrow?" Tamás asked. "If it opens?"

She looked at the black water sliding over the lowest sluice gate, water that had traveled through the dark heart of a mountain and come out wearing colors no water should wear.

"Then I'll know exactly how big the lie is," she said. "And I've spent ten years wanting to measure one of those."

* * *

The first thing the water took was sound.

At three meters the iridescent film gave way to a strange, clean clarity—the pool was filtered somehow, or the particulates settled fast—and Isandro descended through light that went from green to gray to nothing, following her guide line down along the dam's submerged face. Her lamp carved a cone out of the dark. The concrete under her hand was older than it looked from above, crazed with hairline fractures, and warm. Warm concrete. The whole structure was warm, like a body.

Seven meters. Nine. Her depth gauge ticked and her breathing echoed in her own ears, loud as surf. She counted her kicks the way she counted everything—miles, not years, meters, not memories. Eleven meters. Twelve.

At fourteen meters the dam ended and the mountain began.

The sluice gates were not discharges. They were mouths. Three tunnels bored straight back into the rock, each wide enough to drive a truck through, and the water flowing out of them came from somewhere far inside—she could tell by its temperature, by its taste against her lips when her regulator's exhaust bubbled past her chin. Mine water. Drainage adits. The dam wasn't holding a reservoir back; it was dressing up a wound.

She chose the center tunnel and swam in.

The ceiling rose. That was the first shock—that it kept rising, five meters, then eight, the tunnel opening into a gallery so vast her lamp couldn't find its walls. She hung in the beam's small circle and let the numbers assemble themselves: this was a shaft, or had been once, a working stope from some earlier mining era, and someone had recently widened it, shored it, fitted it with steel walkways that climbed the far wall into darkness above the waterline. Flooded mine. Deliberately flooded. And deliberately re-opened.

Her line paid out. Twenty meters. Twenty-five. The floor of the gallery came up to meet her lamp: silt, rubble, and then—

Pipes.

Dozens of them, thick as her torso, running along the gallery floor and vanishing into side adits, every joint wrapped in fresh gasket material, every valve wheel painted regulation government gray. She recognized the paint. She had spent a decade making sure she'd forgotten it, but recognition doesn't ask permission: the particular flat blue-gray, the stencil font on the identification plates. Ministry of Watershed Infrastructure. She had held a clearance card with that seal on it once, in another life, under another name.

She pulled herself along the pipe run, fins stirring ghost-silt, and found the first stamp intact beneath a skin of pale bacterial growth. She scraped it clean with her knife and pressed her slate against it to copy the plate: **MWI-DR-0417 — TAILINGS RETURN LINE — PROPERTY OF THE CROWN COMMISSION ON STRATEGIC RESOURCES.** A date. Fourteen months old.

Tailings return line. The words sat in her chest like cold iron.

Tailings were what mining left behind—the crushed, poisoned remainder, the slurry of heavy metals and processing chemicals that no one wanted and everyone downstream inherited. You contained tailings. You built ponds behind earthen dams and you prayed. You did not build *return lines*. Return lines meant recirculation, meant an active operation, meant—

The second adit breathed.

She felt it before she saw it: a pressure change against her ears, a slow pulse in the water, rhythmic as a sleeping animal. Not current. Current didn't pulse. She turned her lamp toward the side tunnel and the beam found the source of the breathing—a massive valve assembly, half a meter across, its wheel locked open with a chain and padlock, and beyond it a bore hole angling down into the mountain's root.

Something was pumping. Deep below her, in chambers she couldn't see, machinery was moving water and waste in a slow tide, and the pulse of it reached even here, even through forty meters of rock.

Isandro had dived wrecks for eleven years. She had swum through ships that had killed their crews, through planes that had fallen burning out of the sky, through the drowned bones of other people's catastrophes. She had a diver's faith in the dead: the dead stayed put. Wrecks grieved and then were still.

This place was not still. This place was alive, and it was sick, and she was swimming in its bloodstream.

* * *

The breach opened up at thirty-one meters, and it took her breath away in both senses.

The side gallery narrowed, then shouldered out into a cavern where her lamp simply failed—not malfunctioned, but failed *mattering*, swallowed by a space too large for light. She clipped her line and kicked forward until the beam caught the far wall, and what it showed her made her hang motionless in the water, regulator humming, heart slamming against the cold.

The tailings pond had been above them once. She understood it in a single terrible glance: a containment basin built into a natural hollow high on the mountainside, its earthen dam keyed into rock that the miners' own blasting had fractured. And the rock had done what fractured rock does. It had opened. Somewhere overhead, behind tons of stone, the pond's floor had split along a fault line, and the entire poisoned mass of it had begun to drain—not into a collection system, not into treatment, but *down*, through the broken mountain, into the same aquifer that fed the springs that fed the headwaters that fed the river that fed everything.

The cavern floor was the wound. A fissure ten meters wide ran its length, and from it rose a slow, continuous plume of gray-gold cloud—tailings fines suspended in groundwater, billowing upward like smoke from a fire buried in the earth. Her lamp lit it and the particles turned the beam solid, a pillar of dirty light. Around the fissure's mouth, the rock was stained in spreading aprons of ochre and rust, iron oxide blooms, the mineral signature of water that had passed through crushed sulfide ore. She knew the chemistry without wanting to. Acid mine drainage. Every liter rising through that fissure was carrying dissolved metals—arsenic, cadmium, lead, the alphabet of poison—into the watershed, and it had been doing it, by the size of those stains, for years.

Not months. Years.

The survey data had shown contamination dating back further than any official operation. Pell had decoded the transmission and found the signature dated weeks before the consortium's paperwork began. Now Isandro saw why. The paperwork was theater. The mine had been bleeding since long before anyone filed a permit, and the permits—the restricted zone, the vanished survey team, the navy's polite fiction of a wreck—were all designed to do one thing: keep anyone from looking at the wound while it grew.

She copied everything. Her slate filled, and she flipped it, and filled it again. Stamps on valves: MWI-DR-0417, 0418, 0419. Serial plates on pump housings bolted to the gallery walls, still wet with condensation, still *running*—she could feel the vibration in her palm through her glove. Government stamps on every valve, every pipe, every pump. The Crown Commission on Strategic Resources had signed its name to this chamber the way a man signs a confession, except the signature was meant to be a shield, proof that this was sanctioned, official, *nobody's crime because everybody's business*.

She photographed what she could with the sealed camera rigged to her harness, though she knew photographs taken in thirty meters of poisoned dark might be grainy ghosts. The slate was better. Slate and her own eyes. Evidence written in her own hand.

Near the largest valve assembly she found something else, and her blood went colder than the water.

A maintenance log, lashed to the pipe in a waterproof case, updated in a neat bureaucratic hand. She flipped pages with numb fingers. Flow rates. Pressure readings. Chemical assays. And in the margin of the most recent entry, dated nine days ago, a note in different handwriting—hastier, human:

*Fissure widening. Est. discharge up 40% this quarter. Recommend immediate remediation or controlled shutdown. Requesting escalation.*

Below it, in a third hand, stamped in red:

**ESCALATION DENIED — CONTINUE OPERATIONS PER SCHEDULE.**

Denied. They knew. Someone had stood in this chamber, or read these numbers in a warm office somewhere, had seen the wound growing, and had ordered it kept open. Continue operations per schedule. The schedule mattered more than the aquifer. More than the wells. More than the children Tamás hadn't yet told her about, though she was beginning to understand that he was going to.

Isandro hung in the plume of dirty light and felt awe and horror fuse into a single element, heavier than lead, filling her chest where her breath should be. She had come looking for the scale of the lie, and the lie was geological. It had strata. It had its own weather. Men had built machines inside a dying mountain to keep the dying efficient, and they had stamped their names on the machines so no one could ever say they hadn't known.

Her gauge read forty minutes consumed. Protocol said turn now. She touched the log case one more time—then stopped.

Because the log had a companion. Bolted beside it, newer, was a radio repeater unit, its antenna cable running up the gallery wall toward the surface, and clipped beneath it, a duty roster. Names. Shift rotations. Twelve names, twelve men keeping the pumps running in shifts, and the rotation began again tomorrow at dawn.

Which meant that at dawn, someone would descend these tunnels, read her slate marks if she left anything, notice the disturbed silt, and know the wound had a witness.

Protocol said turn around. Isandro copied the roster anyway, every name, every shift, and then she unclipped the spare evidence capsule from her harness, sealed the slate copies and a corner torn from the red-stamped order inside it, and stowed it deep in her buoyancy pocket where no cave-in or current could take it without taking her too.

Then she turned for home, kicking up through the plume, and the last thing her lamp showed her as the fissure fell away below was the gray-gold cloud rising after her, patient as time, already on its way to the sea.

* * *

She broke the surface in the pool's gray dawn light with Tamás's hands on her harness before she'd finished clearing her regulator, hauling her onto the rocks as if she weighed nothing, as if she were the precious cargo and not the woman who'd gone down to fetch the truth.

"The color of your eyes," he was saying. "You've been deep. How deep? What did you—"

"Thirty-eight meters." She stripped her mask, coughed, and held up the evidence capsule. Her arms were shaking now that the work was done, the fine tremor of cold and adrenaline, and she let them shake. There was no one here to perform steadiness for. "It's not a mine, Tamás. It hasn't been a mine for years. It's a machine for moving poison downhill, and it's wearing the government's name on every bolt."

Pell took the capsule with both hands, reverent, terrified. "The stamps? You're certain—"

"I copied them. I have the duty roster. I have an order, in writing, refusing remediation." Isandro looked up at the concrete face of the dam, at the shotcrete disguise, at the morning mist beginning to burn off the pools upstream. "They know it's getting worse. They're choosing this."

Tamás had gone very still. When she looked at him, she saw a man doing arithmetic with his village in his eyes—wells, children, years of sickness suddenly given a shape and a culprit and a date of origin. Eleven years ago, his grandfather had said the Throat had opened. Eleven years of iridescent water.

"We have what we need," Pell whispered. "We could go back downriver right now. Transmit from the relay at the gorge—"

"And be intercepted before we're halfway there," Isandro said. "Patrols run that stretch twice daily. We'd be handing them the capsule ourselves." She flexed her fingers, willing warmth back into them, and found her mind already moving the way it moved underwater—cold, methodical, counting. "No. We take it to the strongest point on the line. Mile Zero. The relay tower at the headwaters. Highest elevation, direct line to the regional transmitters, and close enough that if they come for us, the whole world will be watching the place they tried to pretend didn't exist."

"That tower is guarded," Tamás said. "That's where they took the survey team."

"I know." Isandro reached down and picked up her knife, folded it, stowed it. Small rituals. Rules. But the rules had changed now; she felt it in her bones, the way a diver feels a storm swell arriving through the hull of a boat before the sky shows a single cloud. "That's why we go tonight. Before the shift change. Before whoever reads that maintenance log comes down into the dark and finds my slate marks in the silt."

Above the dam, half-hidden by the ridge, the generators changed pitch—spinning up, as if the mountain itself had heard her plan and was warming its throat to answer.
