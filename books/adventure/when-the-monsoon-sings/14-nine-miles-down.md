## 14. Nine Miles Down

The shaft swallowed light the way the river swallowed everything else—without comment, without mercy.

Isandro hung at the edge of the flooded adit, checking her gauges a fourth time even though she'd already checked them three times. Two hundred feet of line on the reel. Backup torch clipped to her harness where her left hand could find it blind. Knife on the right calf, because fouled line was not a possibility down here; it was an appointment.

"You don't have to go all the way," Tamás said behind her. He stood on the collapsed timber staging that had once carried ore carts, and his voice had a strange flatness she'd learned to read as fear wearing work clothes. "The valve manifold is at ninety meters. You said it yourself. Ninety meters in a flooded mine with no visibility and rock that's been eating itself for thirty years."

"I said I'd reach it."

"You said you'd *try* to jam it." He crouched at the water's edge, and the torchlight caught the planes of his face—the stubborn jaw, the eyes that had lied to her beautifully for nineteen miles of river and then stopped lying all at once, two nights ago, when he'd shown her the photographs of his village's wells. The iridescent film on the water. The children with the gray gums. "There's a difference between brave and stupid, and I've been watching you long enough to know you can't always tell them apart."

"Neither can you," she said, and spat over the side, into the black. "That's why you hired me."

He didn't laugh. She hadn't expected him to.

The water below was not river water. It was mine water—heavy with tailings, thick as cold tea, holding no light at all past six inches from her mask. She'd dived black water before. Quarries. Wrecks in silted harbors. But this was different. This water had a texture, a weight against the skin like something alive and sick, and when she dipped a glove in it, the surface tension clung, faintly iridescent, the same wrong rainbow she'd seen on the survey team's abandoned instruments.

The breached tailings pond above the aquifer. Government stamps on every valve. She'd seen it yesterday from the ridge, Pell beside her with the decoded transmission printed out and trembling in her hands, and Isandro had understood then that the scale of it was the whole point. No one poisoned a watershed this thoroughly by accident. This was infrastructure. This was policy.

And somewhere down there, at ninety meters, was the intake manifold feeding the processing plant—a valve system she could foul, jam, wedge shut with the steel shims Pell had machined from salvaged rail spikes. It wouldn't stop the consortium. It would slow them. It would buy the village downstream weeks instead of days.

Weeks were what they had.

"Line signals," she said, running through it one last time because ritual was ballast and she needed all the ballast she could get. "One tug, I'm fine. Two tugs, coming up. Three tugs, I'm fouled and cutting. Four—" she looked at him, "—four means don't come after me."

"Four means come after you," Tamás said.

"Four means *don't*."

"Isandro." He said her name—not the buried one, the bought one, but he said it like it was hers anyway—and stepped closer. "I spent nineteen miles lying to you so you'd bring exactly this skill to exactly this hole. If you go down there and don't come back, every lie I told becomes just a thing I did to kill a woman. So either you take my four-tug promise, or you leave me on this platform and I follow you anyway without a line, which we both know is suicide, and then your death and mine are both on your conscience, and you'll hate that worse than drowning."

She stared at him. The torch guttered in the damp air. Somewhere far back in the tunnel behind them, water dripped with the patience of geology.

"You're insufferable," she said.

"I'm Oyelaran. We're famous for it."

She clipped the reel to her harness, put the regulator in her mouth, and let herself fall backward into the dark.

* * *

The first twenty meters were almost easy.

Almost. Black-water diving was never easy—it was a discipline of trusting instruments over instinct, of keeping the reel taut and counting kicks, of building the dive in her head like a chart she could navigate by feel. Down and along. The adit sloped at fifteen degrees, following the old haulage tunnel toward the lower workings, and the walls on either side of her torch beam were soft with decades of rust-orange sludge, iron oxide bleeding out of the rock like the mountain itself was wounded.

She counted kicks. Forty. Eighty. The line paid out smooth and silver in her beam, her lifeline, her umbilical, the one rule she never broke: *never lose the line.* Ten years ago someone had taught her that rule, and ten years ago she'd broken it, and the miles she'd logged since then were penance as much as profession. Miles, not years. Every mile a small act of staying alive when staying alive was the only thing left to be good at.

At sixty meters the tunnel opened into a chamber, and the chamber was breathing.

She felt it before she saw it—a pressure shift, a slow pulse in the water, like the mine inhaling and exhaling through some fissure she couldn't see. Her depth gauge read sixty-two. Above her, the ceiling arched away into darkness supported by timber sets that had been installed, according to Pell's charts, sometime in the last five years. New wood. New bolts. The consortium hadn't just been mining here; they'd been *investing*, propping open the throat of the thing so it could keep swallowing.

The manifold was ahead, per the charts. Thirty more meters of tunnel, then the pump gallery.

She swam on. The sludge thickened. Her torch beam shortened, the water itself turning opaque, and she switched to dead reckoning—compass bearing, kick count, the reel whispering out line behind her like a thread out of a spool. Seventy meters. Seventy-five. Her ears needed equalizing every few kicks now, the pressure stacking up, and she checked her gas: fine, plenty, turn the dive at a third.

Eighty meters. The compass needle shivered.

That was the first wrong thing. Compasses didn't shiver. Not unless there was iron moving nearby—or unless the mountain's magnetic guts were as scrambled as its hydrology. She steadied the bezel, kept her heading, told herself it was the machinery ahead, the pumps, the great iron heart of the operation she'd come to stop.

Eighty-five meters, the second wrong thing arrived as sound.

A groan. Deep, structural, rising from everywhere at once—the sound a ship makes in heavy seas, the sound a building makes in an earthquake, the sound rock makes when it has decided, on its own ancient schedule, that it is done holding. Isandro froze in the black water, torch beam shrinking around her, and felt the vibration come up through the water into her chest, into her teeth.

Then the ceiling came down.

Not all of it. Not even much of it, in the scheme of things—a section of the old stope maybe forty meters back, where new workings had undercut old ones, where the consortium's greed had hollowed what should have stayed solid. She heard it rather than saw it: a roar transmitted through water, a concussion that shoved her sideways and filled the world with silt and debris, and then the line went slack in her hand.

Slack.

Her mind did the arithmetic with terrible calm, the way it always did, the diver's cold ledger: slack line meant the reel end was gone. Buried under rock, or torn free, or severed. She was at eighty-eight meters in a flooded mine, in zero visibility, and her way home had just ceased to exist.

She held position. Breathe. Breathe. The silt was total now—not brown, not gray, just absence, her own torch beam bouncing back at her off the suspended earth like light trapped in milk. Somewhere above her, tons of mountain were settling into their new arrangement, and smaller collapses ticked and cracked through the dark like rain on a tin roof.

Four tugs. That was the signal. Four tugs meant *don't come after me.*

But there was no line to tug.

She found the wall with her gloved hand—left side, per the chart, keep the left wall and you'll find the chamber—and began to move. Slowly. Deliberately. Kick, feel, kick. The rulebook for lost-line procedures lived in her spine, installed there by a decade of dives: stop, secure, search in expanding circles, and if the circle finds nothing, ascend on backup and pray for open water. But there was no open water here. There was only tunnel, and the tunnel behind her was now a wall of rubble, and the air in her tanks was burning down at eighty-eight meters where every breath cost double.

Her torch found the manifold.

It loomed out of the murk like a cathedral organ—pipes and valves and bolted flanges, the intake throat of the whole poisoning apparatus, government-stamped steel gleaming dull orange in her beam. Right there. Twenty minutes of work with the shims and the wrench. Twenty minutes she might not have.

Isandro Coldharbour had hidden for ten years behind rules, and the rules said: abort. Surface—no, there was no surface—abort, find alternate egress, live to dive again. The rules had kept her alive through wrecks and currents and the one catastrophe whose name she still refused to say. The rules were all she had.

She looked at the manifold. She looked at her gauge. She thought about gray-gummed children drinking iridescent water, and about Tamás standing on the staging above, waiting on a line that would never signal again.

She swam to the manifold and started working.

* * *

The first shim went in clean. The second fought her. The third—

The third was half-seated when the secondary collapse came, closer this time, close enough that the shockwave picked her up and threw her against the valve assembly, and her primary torch spun away into the dark, and when she got her bearings her left glove was bleeding and the backup torch showed her a sight that stopped her heart colder than the water: silt, pouring down from overhead like smoke, filling the tunnel ahead of her, behind her, erasing the world in both directions.

Fouled. The word arrived with total clarity. Not the line—her. A length of cable, old hoist wire, had come down with the collapse and wrapped her harness, snagged across her tank valve, and every movement cinched it tighter. She could feel it now, a bar across her chest that wasn't the water's pressure. Something else. Something patient.

She drew the knife from her right calf. Worked it blind, by feel, the blade whispering against wire. Her gauge said she had perhaps twelve minutes of bottom time left if she burned it all, none for safety stops, and the ascent—there was no ascent. The tunnel behind was sealed. Ahead, past the manifold, the charts showed the pump gallery connected to a ventilation raise, vertical, maybe fifty meters up to a grate, maybe daylight, maybe another collapse, maybe nothing.

Wire parted. One strand, then another. Her breathing roared in her own ears, too fast, too deep, CO2 stacking, and she made herself slow down, made herself count, because panic was just another kind of drowning and she had promised herself, ten years ago, never again.

Then, through the water, faint and impossible:

Tugs. On something. Rhythmic. Four pulls, pause. Four pulls, pause.

She went still. Listened. Followed the vibration to its source—a thin yellow poly line, a *guide line*, the kind used for marking portages, tied off to a boulder near the manifold and running away into the silt-choked dark toward the chamber. Toward the collapse. Toward the way she'd come.

Tamás.

No. *No.* He was supposed to stay topside. He was a river guide, not a diver; he'd logged maybe forty recreational dives in his life, warm tropical water, none of this. The protocol had been explicit. The protocol existed precisely so that this would not happen.

Four pulls. Pause. Four pulls.

Coming *down.*

She grabbed the guide line and pulled back—one, two, three, four—*stay there, do not come down*—and the answer came back through the line like a heartbeat: four pulls, steady, unhurried, and then the unmistakable rhythmic drag of someone paying out line and descending, kicking down into the black toward her, and Isandro Coldharbour, who had not prayed since she was a girl, discovered that her mouth was forming words anyway.

* * *

He arrived out of the murk like something imagined—a shape, then a torch glow, then Tamás, in gear that fit him badly, her spare rig from the boat kit, fins churning, eyes enormous behind the mask. He'd tied himself to the guide line with a carabiner, a boy scout's solution, a river man's solution, and when his light found her face he didn't waste breath on words. He came straight to the hoist wire and put his hands on it beside hers, and together—her knife, his grip—they cut her free while the mountain groaned around them like something waking.

When the last strand parted, he took her wrist and pointed up: the ventilation raise. She shook her head—*gas, check gas*—and pressed her gauge to his mask. His eyes did the math faster than she expected. Hers said she had eight minutes. His, on the shared rig plan they'd improvised, said eleven.

He pointed at the manifold. At the third shim, still half-seated, hanging on by its threads.

She understood. He wasn't leaving. He was telling her: *finish it.*

They worked together in the dying light—she seated the shim while he braced her against the current of the intake flow, his hands solid on her harness, and when the valve ground halfway closed and the great pipes shuddered and the flow stuttered, thinned, choked, the whole gallery seemed to exhale. The pressure in the water changed. The poison's throat, narrowed to a slit.

Then they swam. Up the ventilation raise, hand over hand on the guide line, through a grate Tamás had pried loose on his way down—God knew how, God knew what it had cost him—into a vertical shaft where the water finally, blessedly, began to taste of iron instead of tailings, and then, at the top, a circle of gray-green light that was the sky.

They broke the surface in a flooded ventilation chamber open to the monsoon clouds, both of them coughing, both of them laughing or crying or some animal third thing, and Tamás pulled his mask off and shouted at her, actually shouted, his voice cracking off the stone:

"Four tugs means come after you! I *told* you!"

"It means *don't*—"

"It means whatever I say it means! You're in my country now, diver!" And then he laughed, huge and ragged, echoing up the shaft, and hauled himself onto the concrete lip of the chamber, and reached down, and pulled her out of the black water into the gray light, and held on to her arm longer than he needed to, neither of them saying anything, because some things forged at eighty-eight meters don't need language.

Below them, muffled by a hundred meters of rock and floodwater, the mine settled and groaned and went quiet, its throat half-closed, its schedule broken.

Above them, through the grate at the top of the raise, Isandro could hear engines. More than engines—voices, radio static, the organized noise of men who had just felt their intake pressure drop and were coming to see why.

Tamás heard it too. His laughter died. He looked at her, water streaming off his chin, and said quietly:

"So. Do we run, or do we meet them?"

Down in the dark below, the shim would hold for days, maybe a week. Up here, boots were already ringing on steel stairs.

Isandro checked her knife was still on her calf, and smiled with all her teeth.

"Neither," she said. "We take their boats."
