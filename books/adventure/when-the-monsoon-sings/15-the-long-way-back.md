## 15. The Long Way Back

Wren Pell had spent her whole career believing that maps told the truth. That was the whole point of her—of the profession, of the discipline, of the eleven years she'd given to contour lines and datum points and the patient, unglamorous work of measuring a world that did not care to be measured. A map was a promise: *here is what is here.* She had broken that promise once, ten years ago in a government office with bad lighting, when she'd signed off on a flood survey she hadn't verified because a director with a soft voice and hard eyes had asked her to. One line on one chart. It had cost her a career and bought her exile to the survey fringes, where the work was honest and the pay was thin and nobody remembered her name.

Now she stood at the edge of the consortium's living camp with a satchel full of decoded transmissions against her hip, watching the truth walk away from her on its own two legs.

* * *

The amnesty offer had come at dawn, delivered by a woman named Corvane who wore the consortium's gray like it had been tailored for her and spoke to the detained surveyors the way a veterinarian speaks to frightened animals—gently, reasonably, with an undertone that said *I have done this before.*

"Full reinstatement," Corvane said, standing on the low wooden platform outside the mess shelter while the nine surviving members of the Meridian Survey Team sat in a loose half-circle in the mud. "Salaries backdated to your original contracts. Records sealed. Transport downriver tonight, ahead of the monsoon surge. You'll be home within the week." She let that word sit in the wet air. *Home.* "All we ask is what you've already agreed to by contract: discretion regarding proprietary operations."

Pell watched their faces. She knew every one of them—not well, not the way you know people you eat with and argue with, but the way you know people whose instruments you calibrated, whose field notes you filed, whose handwriting you could identify from thirty feet. Okafor, the hydrologist, who kept a photo of his daughter taped inside his sample case. The Brandt twins, geologists, who finished each other's sentences and each other's rations. Vashti, the team lead, who had held them together through four months of detention with a spine made of routine. And little Ferro, the youngest, who still flinched at generator noise.

Four months. That was how long they'd been here, since the navy patrol boat had intercepted their survey run and delivered them not to a brig but to this camp in the terraces above the gorge—a camp that officially did not exist, staffed by guards who officially were not armed, running an operation that officially belonged to no one. Four months of being told their families had been notified they were safe. Four months of Corvane's voice, patient as water, explaining that the mining works above the gorge were a matter of national development, that the chemical signatures in their samples were within tolerance, that the headwaters were restricted for reasons they didn't need to understand.

And four months of Okafor quietly re-running his assays anyway, because Okafor was the kind of man who could not leave a number alone until it confessed.

"The signature isn't within tolerance," Okafor said now, in the flat tone he used for data. He was holding his sample case against his chest like a shield. "You know it isn't. We ran it forty times. The aquifer contamination predates your operation by six weeks, which means there's an earlier source, which means either you're lying about your timeline or someone upstream is lying about theirs. Either way—" his voice cracked, just slightly, "—my daughter drinks from a well two hundred miles downstream of this river."

Corvane's expression did not change. That was the thing about her that frightened Pell most—not cruelty, but the total absence of any need for it. "Dr. Okafor. Your daughter is exactly why the amnesty exists. Come home. Be a father. Let people whose job this is handle the chemistry."

"And if their job is lying?"

"Then they'll lie competently," Corvane said, "which is more than I can say for a hydrologist with a radio."

The silence after that had weight. Pell felt it press on her chest like deep water. Beside her, Isandro Coldharbour stood with her arms crossed and her face doing nothing at all—the diver's face, Pell had learned to call it, the one that came from spending hours where panic meant death—and Tamás Oyelaran leaned against a support post with his eyes moving constantly, cataloging exits, counting guards, doing whatever quiet arithmetic guides do.

Corvane turned to the half-circle. "You have until the boats are fueled. Noon." She stepped off the platform and walked away through the drizzle without looking back, because she already knew, Pell realized, exactly how many of them would come. She'd done the math before she ever opened her mouth. Somewhere in the consortium's ledgers there was probably a column for defections, a percentage, a cost per head.

Noon. The boats. The long way back.

* * *

They argued in the equipment shed, among the confiscated instruments stacked in crates marked HOLD, and it was the worst hour of Wren Pell's life, worse than the office ten years ago, worse than the dive, worse than the cave-in, because this time there was no enemy to blame. There was only choice, wearing everyone's face.

Vashti went first. She stood with her back against the crate rack and said, "I'm taking the amnesty," and the words fell into the shed like stones into still water. "Don't look at me like that, Okafor. I'm fifty-three years old. I have a pension. I have a mother in a care facility whose bills I pay. If I go upstream with you—if I put my name on whatever broadcast you're planning—they will bury me so thoroughly my own mother won't be able to find me, and then who pays for her oxygen?" Her jaw worked. "I held this team together for four months by telling them the truth would keep. I'm done pretending otherwise. The truth doesn't keep. It waits, and it costs, and somebody always pays, and I am telling you it will not be my mother."

"You'd let them win," Ferro whispered.

"I'd let them think they won," Vashti said, "while I go home and live, which is also a kind of sabotage, though a slower one. Don't romanticize dying for a cause, child. Dying is easy to romanticize from the side of the living."

The Brandt twins took opposite sides, which surprised no one who'd ever watched them argue over a stratigraphy column. Adaeze Brandt chose the amnesty—"I'll testify later, legally, through channels, the boring way that actually works"—and her brother Emeka laughed, a short ugly sound, and said, "Channels. Ten years of channels got us this camp," and the two of them stood forehead to forehead in the middle of the shed, twins who had shared a womb and a tent and twenty-nine years of argument, and neither one blinked, and Pell understood suddenly that this was not the first time they had chosen differently, only the first time the stakes had been a river.

Okafor never wavered. He repacked his sample case with the sealed vials—the ones Corvane's people had missed, hidden inside a hollowed-out barometer housing—and said only, "My daughter," as if that settled everything, and perhaps it did.

Ferro wavered over everything. Ferro cried, quietly, furiously, wiping his face with his sleeve and hating himself for it, and finally said, "I can't. I'm sorry. I'm sorry, I can't, they showed me photos of my sister's street, they know her address, they—" and stopped, because Emeka put a hand on his shoulder, and Ferro shook it off, and walked out of the shed into the rain to stand alone by the fuel drums, small and hunched and already gone.

That left five. Pell counted them the way she'd count stations on a traverse: herself, Isandro, Tamás, Okafor, Emeka. Five to carry the evidence downriver—or upriver, first, to the relay tower at mile zero, because Isandro had laid it out the night before with the cold clarity of someone reading a depth gauge: the tower was the only transmitter in the gorge strong enough to punch through monsoon interference to the regional net, and the intake valves below the tailings pond needed a diver's hands, and both things had to happen during the surge, when the patrols pulled back to high water. Everything converged on the storm. Everything depended on people choosing to stay.

"We need the boats fueled anyway," Tamás said into the silence. "However many of us are in them."

It was almost noon when they heard the shot.

* * *

Not a gunshot—a flare pistol, fired twice, the signal Corvane's people used for emergencies, and then shouting, and then the smell, which reached the shed before anyone understood it: diesel, sharp and spreading, pooling somewhere downhill toward the landing.

Emeka got there first. They all got there fast, but Emeka arrived with the particular velocity of a man who has already guessed, and Pell saw him stop dead at the tree line above the landing and make a sound she had never heard a grown man make.

Both boats. Their boats—the two long shallow-draft craft Tamás had scouted, the ones meant to carry five people and a satchel of evidence downriver through the gorge ahead of the surge. Someone had opened the bilge drains and left them open. Someone had poured the reserve fuel cans' contents into the hulls of both boats and ignited a slow leak-line of diesel across the dock planking, so that the fire wasn't dramatic—it was thorough. It moved along the boards with the patience of something following instructions. By the time the camp's guards came pounding down the slope, both hulls were burning from the keel up, and the reserve cans were melted slag, and the rain, which had been falling all morning, might as well have been falling on another river entirely.

"No," Emeka said. "No, no, no—" and then he saw the footprints in the mud at the edge of the dock, and Pell saw them too, and saw him see them, and wished with everything in her that she could unsee his face.

The prints were Ferro's. His boots had a split seam on the left toe; Pell had noticed it weeks ago filing his gear, had thought idly that someone should tell him. The prints walked from the fuel store to the dock and back, twice, and then away up the slope toward the mess shelter, where Corvane's people were quartered, where a young man with a frightened sister and a flinch for generator noise had gone to trade five strangers' escape route for his own.

"He sold us," Emeka said. His voice was very quiet. "He stood in that shed and cried and then he sold us."

"He saved himself," Vashti said, coming up behind them, breathing hard. Her face was gray. "There's a difference. There has to be a difference, or none of us survive anything." But her eyes stayed on the burning boats, and Pell thought: *she's recalculating. She's wondering what Ferro knows about her now, and whether sealed records stay sealed.*

Tamás crouched at the dock's edge, studying the burn pattern with the same attention he gave rapids. "Hull wood's green-treated," he said. "They'll char slow. Frames might hold." He looked up at Isandro, and something passed between them that Pell couldn't read—some guide-and-diver shorthand, some agreement made in black water. "We don't need both boats. We need one hull and nerve."

"We need a miracle," Emeka said.

"We need tools," Tamás said, "and about ninety minutes, and everyone who's staying to stop standing here and start carrying things."

Isandro was already moving. Of course she was. Pell watched her stride down onto the smoking dock—into the heat, into the stink, past the guards who had stopped at the tree line because the fire was inconvenient rather than urgent, because the boats burning served the consortium fine—and begin hauling the least-damaged hull to the waterline, alone, muscles corded, rain steaming off her shoulders. The woman who counted her life in miles logged, who had built a fortress out of rules and solitude, was salvaging a burned boat with her bare hands because five people she'd known for three weeks needed to reach a tower in a storm.

*Why?* Pell wondered, not for the first time. *What did you lose that made you willing to lose the rest?*

She knew part of the answer. She'd read the decoded transmissions; she'd seen the clearance codes referenced in the consortium's own logistics traffic, codes belonging to a name that matched no one on this river except the woman hauling the boat. Isandro Coldharbour had been someone before. Someone with access. Someone the consortium feared enough to offer everything back. And tonight or tomorrow, on that tower, Pell would learn the rest—and Isandro would have to give it, because the broadcast would need those codes, and codes required confession.

But that was later. That was miles downstream and hours dark.

* * *

Ninety minutes, Tamás had said. It took two hours and eleven, working in shifts while the guards watched from the slope with the studied indifference of men paid not to intervene, and when they finally pushed the half-charred hull off the mudbank, it floated—low, listing slightly to port, taking water at a rate Tamás pronounced "manageable, which is a guide's word for terrifying"—but it floated.

Five seats. Pell sat in the bow with the satchel strapped across her chest, feeling the evidence press against her ribs like a second heartbeat: the transmission logs, the chemical assay dated six weeks before any official operation, the geological charts with RESTRICTED stamped across the headwaters, and beneath all of it, the thing Okafor had carried four months against his heart—the original samples, iridescent, wrong, undeniable.

Behind her: Okafor, silent, holding his barometer. Emeka, staring back up the slope at the camp where his twin sister was even now boarding a gray transport boat with the amnesty in her pocket, neither of them waving. Tamás at the stern, hands on the tiller, reading the water the way other men read scripture. And Isandro amidships, soaked, soot-streaked, checking the fuel line with fingers that did not shake.

"One boat," Emeka said bitterly, as the current took them. "One burned boat and five of us. Against patrol craft and a monsoon."

"One boat," Isandro said, "is one less thing to hide."

Pell almost smiled. Then she looked back one last time—at the terraces, at the smoke, at the small figure standing alone on the dock where Ferro had stood, watching them go, too far away to read his face—and she thought about Vashti's words in the shed. *Somebody always pays.*

The river bent north, toward the gorge, toward mile zero, toward the storm that was coming early and angry off the mountains. The first fat drops of the true monsoon hit the water around them, dimpling its surface, and beneath the surface—Pell knew now, would always know—the aquifer bled its slow poison toward two hundred miles of wells and a girl named Okafor who drank without knowing.

"Hold together," Tamás murmured to the hull, to the river, to all of them. "Hold together."

The rain came down harder, and the long way back began.
