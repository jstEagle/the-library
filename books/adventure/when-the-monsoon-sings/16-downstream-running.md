## 16. Downstream, Running

The monsoon came early that year, and it came angry.

Tamás felt it before he heard it—a pressure change in his back teeth, a smell like wet iron rolling down the gorge. The river had been rising all night, brown water climbing the terraced banks in hungry increments, and by dawn the channel below Mile Zero had become something else entirely: a single muscle of current, flexing from wall to wall, carrying whole trees like splinters.

He stood at the bow of the lead boat with one hand on the painter line and let the spray hit him in the face. Behind him, Isandro crouched over the engine housing, sleeves rolled, hair plastered flat, her hands moving with the economy of someone who had decided not to be afraid and was making it stick through sheer repetition. Pell sat wedged against the fuel drum, cradling the transmitter case against her chest like a child she'd stolen.

Three boats. Eleven people. One chance to run the gorge before the patrol craft finished their sweep of the upper narrows and came looking for what had escaped them.

"Engine's good," Isandro called over the roar. "As long as it stays good."

"As long as anything stays anything," Tamás said, and pushed off from the bank.

* * *

The first three miles were almost kind.

Tamás knew this stretch the way he knew the scar on his own palm—every rock, every eddy, every place where the gorge pinched and the water stood up on its hind legs. He'd run it at high water twice before, both times alone, both times with nothing to lose but himself. Now he had ten lives riding behind him and a radio case full of evidence that would break the consortium open if it ever reached a tower strong enough to carry its voice.

"Left channel," he shouted, and the boats swung left, threading between two boulders that wore the flood like crowns, white water exploding off their shoulders. "Stay in my wake. Don't fight the current—argue with it."

Pell laughed at that, a short startled sound, and then stopped laughing when the gorge narrowed and the river dropped.

It wasn't a waterfall, exactly. It was more honest than that—a staircase of ledges, each one taller than a man, each one buried under foam so thick it looked solid. Tamás had named these ledges as a boy, sitting on his grandmother's hip while she pointed out where the old people said the river kept its teeth. *There*, she'd say, *that's where the water remembers who it has swallowed.*

"Line us up," he called back to Isandro. "Far right of the first ledge, then hard left. The flood's made a tongue down the middle but it ends in a hole you could drown a cathedral in."

Isandro didn't answer with words. She answered with the throttle, easing the boat into position with a precision that made something loosen in Tamás's chest. He'd watched her dive a flooded mine shaft two days ago. He should have known she'd treat whitewater the same way—not as an enemy, but as a problem with a solution, read patiently, entered cleanly.

They went down the first ledge like a stone going into a well. The world became noise. Water came over the bow in a green wall and left again, taking a coil of rope and one of Pell's shoes with it. The second ledge tried to swing their stern toward the hole; Tamás threw his weight against the tiller and felt the boat hesitate, consider, obey.

Then they were through, spinning in the caldron below, and he turned to check the other boats—

—and saw the third one sideways.

* * *

It happened the way disasters happen: slowly enough to watch, too fast to stop.

The third boat carried four of the freed surveyors, men and women who had spent weeks hauling ore samples under guard and had not steered a river since before their arrest. They hit the second ledge broadside. The flood took them like a hand takes a toy, rolled them once, twice, and set them down hard against the left wall, where the current pinned them and began, methodically, to fold the hull around the rocks.

"Beach!" Tamás roared, pointing at the gravel bar downstream. "Everyone out! Isandro—"

"I see it." She was already turning, already reading the eddy lines, finding the seam of slack water that ran along the bank like a private road. "Get them out. I'll hold here."

He leapt onto the gravel bar before the boat fully touched, boots skidding, and waded chest-deep toward the wreck. Two of the surveyors had made it to the rocks on their own, clinging like drowned cats. The other two were still aboard, and the boat was groaning now, the hull buckling inward with a sound like a giant biting tin.

"Give me your hand!" He reached the wreck, braced his feet against a boulder, and caught the wrist of a thin woman whose name he'd never learned. She came off the boat in a rush of water, and then the man behind her, and then the hull gave up entirely, folding around the rocks with a crack that echoed off the gorge walls.

The river ate the wreckage in seconds. That was the thing about flood water that no one who hadn't grown up on it could understand—it wasn't violent the way a storm is violent, all noise and theater. It was patient. It took things apart the way time takes things apart, and it did not care what they were.

He got all four onto the bar. One of the women was coughing up half the gorge; another had a gash along her forearm that would need stitching. And downstream, Isandro brought the remaining boat in against the bank with a touch so gentle it looked apologetic.

That was when Pell pointed upstream and said, very quietly, "Engines."

Tamás turned. Two black shapes were rounding the bend above the ledges—patrol craft, low-slung, armored bows, running the same flood that had just eaten a surveyor's boat because armor does not care about water the way aluminum cares about water.

"They'll run the ledges," Isandro said, coming up beside him, breathing hard. "They're built for it."

"Then we stop being slow." Tamás counted heads—eleven, all present, all upright—and made the decision he'd been dreading since Mile Zero. "We can't outrun them loaded. Not through the Narrows. Not with wounded."

"So we split," Isandro said. It wasn't a question. She'd already seen it, the way she saw seams in water. "Two boats. One runs heavy and quiet, hugging the bank through the fisherman's channels. One runs light and loud and gives them something to chase."

"No," Pell said.

"Pell—"

"You don't get to volunteer for the bait boat. You're the diver. You're—" She stopped, jaw tight, and Tamás watched her decide to be brave instead of reasonable. "I'll take the light boat. I know the Narrows' map better than anyone alive. I drew half of it."

"You drew it in low water," Tamás said. "In flood, half those channels are closed."

"Then I'll draw them again." She was already moving, slinging the transmitter case across to Isandro's boat, pressing it into Isandro's arms with both hands. "This goes downstream no matter what happens to me. That's the whole point of me now. Everything else is negotiable."

For a moment nobody moved except the river, which moved constantly, enormously, indifferent to their arithmetic.

Then Isandro said, "Mile fourteen, there's a side channel behind the Split Rock. It looks like a dead end. It isn't. It comes out below the Narrows." She gripped Pell's shoulder. "You come out the other side. That's not negotiable either."

Pell smiled—the first real smile Tamás had seen from her since the camp, crooked and terrified and alive. "Yes, ma'am."

* * *

They ran the Narrows like a rumor.

Tamás took the heavy boat into the fisherman's channels—slots of water barely wider than the hull, threaded between rock gardens that the flood had turned into fangs. There was no speed here. There was only patience at a pace that felt like screaming, poling and feathering the throttle, reading the water line by line. Behind him the surveyors bailed with cooking pots. Ahead, muffled by the canyon walls, he could hear the other chase: the snarl of the light boat's engine, deliberately loud, deliberately reckless, drawing the patrol craft after it like wolves after a bleeding deer.

Twice the engines came close—close enough that he cut his own motor and let them drift in silence behind a shelf of rock while searchlights swept the water overhead, close enough to hear the patrol crews shouting coordinates. Both times the sound swung away, chasing Pell's ghost down the main channel.

Both times, Tamás found himself praying to a river he'd stopped believing in years ago. *Let her be fast. Let her be lucky. Let her know about the strainer at Mile Sixteen.*

Because there was a strainer at Mile Sixteen—a fallen fig tree, roots and all, lodged in the channel since the last big flood, sieving everything the river brought it. In low water you portaged around it. In flood, it was a trap that ate boats and kept them.

He couldn't warn her. There was no radio on the light boat; radios could be tracked, and Pell had refused to take one anyway. *If they can hear me, they can find me,* she'd said. So Tamás ran his own channel and held in his throat the warning that might have saved her, and hated the geometry of it.

They came out below the Narrows at midday, into a stretch where the gorge opened and the rain finally arrived in earnest—not the earlier spitting threat but the true monsoon, a gray curtain falling so thick it erased the far bank. The river rose even as he watched it, climbing the banks in visible surges, and somewhere upstream the headwaters were receiving the storm's full attention, which meant that everything they'd survived so far was only the overture.

They waited at the confluence behind the Split Rock, engines idling, bailers bailing, watching the main channel for a boat that grew later with every minute.

An hour. Ninety minutes.

"She's smart," Isandro said beside him. Her voice was steady. Her knuckles, wrapped around the transmitter case, were not. "She'll have read the water. She'll have—"

"There's a strainer at Sixteen," Tamás said, because lying to her had become impossible somewhere in the last hundred miles, and because saying it aloud was the only prayer he had left.

Isandro went still. Then she started forward, toward the bow, toward the main channel, and Tamás grabbed her arm.

"If you go up there in this, you die. Both of you die. She told you what matters—the case goes downstream. That's the mission. That's the *only* mission now."

"I don't care about the mission."

"Yes, you do." He held her eyes. "You care about it exactly as much as she does. That's why she gave it to you."

And that was when the sound came down the gorge—not an engine, but a voice, thin and human, carried on the flood. A whistle. Three notes, repeated. His grandmother's signal, the one every river kid in the valley learned before they learned to swim. *Come. Come careful.*

They found Pell a quarter mile downstream, clinging to the Split Rock itself—the far side of it, the side that shouldn't have been reachable, in an eddy no bigger than a dining table. The light boat was gone. She was soaked to the bone, one arm hanging wrong, blood diluting pink down the rocks, and she was grinning like a woman who had cheated death and intended to brag about it for the rest of a life she'd very nearly not had.

"Took the tree at Sixteen at an angle," she shouted over the water, as Tamás eased the boat in and Isandro pulled her aboard. "Went over the top of it. Mostly. Mostly over the top." Her shoulder was dislocated; her collarbone beneath it was worse than dislocated. She shivered violently and kept talking, fast, fever-bright. "Patrols think I'm wrecked at the strainer. They're dragging the pool above it. We have maybe an hour before they figure out I'm not in it."

"Your arm," Isandro said.

"My arm is fine. My arm is a detail." Pell sagged against the gunwale, gray-faced, and pressed the transmitter case—she'd kept it, of course she'd kept it, strapped to her body through a rapids and a wreck—with her good hand. "Broadcast it. Broadcast it tonight, before they regroup. The tower at Mile Zero is behind us. But there's another relay—" she fumbled inside her jacket, produced a folded chart, wet but legible, "—the old weather tower, Mile Nine. Still powered. Still connected to the regional grid. If we can reach it before the patrols sweep downstream..."

She trailed off, shivering harder, and Isandro wrapped her in the boat's one dry tarp and looked at Tamás over Pell's bent head.

"Mile Nine," Tamás said. "Below my village."

"Your village," Isandro said quietly, "is directly downstream of everything we just escaped. When the broadcast lands, the consortium's first move will be to punish whoever helped us. Your people are standing in the path of that."

Tamás looked downstream, where the rain erased the world, where the gray wells waited, where his mother was probably standing at her door watching the river rise and wondering why her son had not come home.

"They've been standing in the path for three years," he said. "They just didn't know it. Now they will—and knowing is the only weapon anyone ever gave them." He turned the bow downstream and opened the throttle. "Run for the tower. And pray the monsoon sings louder than their guns."
