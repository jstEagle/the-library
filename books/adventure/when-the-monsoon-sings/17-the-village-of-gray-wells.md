## 17. The Village of Gray Wells

The river told us before Tamás did.

We'd been running downstream for the better part of a day—Pell strapped half-conscious in the bow of the lead boat, her leg bound in a splint Tamás had lashed from a paddle shaft and strips of his own shirt—and somewhere past the last of the gorge rapids, the water changed its mind about being beautiful. It had been green-gold all morning, monsoon-heavy, alive. Then it went gray. Not muddy-gray, not silt-gray. A flat, mineral gray, like the color of old solder, and where the current folded over itself I could see a faint sheen on the surface, an oil-slick rainbow that wasn't oil at all.

Tamás saw me looking and didn't pretend otherwise.

"Past Mile Four," he said quietly. "It's been like this since spring."

I said nothing. There was nothing to say that the river wasn't already saying better.

The village appeared around a bend in the late afternoon, when the light had gone long and copper and everything looked more forgiving than it was. Stilt houses along the bank, a dozen of them, maybe fifteen, their roofs patched with corrugated tin and palm thatch. A dock—half-collapsed, one section sagging into the water like a broken rib. Fishing nets hung drying between poles, and even from two hundred meters out I could see they were stiff with something that wasn't fish slime. Children ran down to the bank to meet us, shouting Tamás's name, and he stood up in the boat with his arms open like a man walking into weather he'd been dreading and craving in equal measure.

They got Pell out first, four hands under her, careful of the leg. She bit down on a scream and didn't let it out, which told me something about her too. Then the villagers turned to me, and I felt their eyes do what eyes always did—take my measure, find me wanting or not, decide.

A woman came through the crowd the way water comes through a crack: no hurry, no stopping. She was small and broad-shouldered, gray threading her hair at the temples, and she wore the particular authority of someone who has buried people and intends not to bury any more. She took Tamás's face in both hands, looked at him for a long moment, then embraced him hard enough to make him stagger.

"Your mother is at the temple," she said against his ear. "Praying you were dead so she wouldn't have to be angry."

"Then we shouldn't keep her waiting," Tamás said, and his voice cracked on it, just once.

The woman turned to me. Up close her eyes were a pale amber, almost gold, and they held the same iridescence I'd seen on the water. No—that was my imagination. That was the dread talking, painting everything in the colors it wanted.

"You brought him back," she said. Her English carried the rounded vowels of someone who'd learned it from radio and stubbornness. "I am Efua Oyelaran. His aunt. You are the diver."

"How did you—"

"He wrote us a letter three weeks ago." She smiled without warmth. "He said a stranger was coming upriver who would want to see our wells. He said you would pay him. He said—" she paused, choosing the words like stones for a wall, "—he said you would understand what you saw here, because you had already seen worse and done nothing about it. Was he right?"

* * *

They put Pell in the house nearest the temple, on a cot by the window, and the village's only nurse—a wiry man named Dossou who'd trained in the provincial capital and come home to find his skills mostly useless and then, lately, horribly useful—set the leg properly while Pell sweated through her shirt and refused, with tremendous dignity, to cry out again. I held her hand. She didn't ask me to. She also didn't pull away.

"It's a clean break," Dossou announced, wrapping. "She'll walk in six weeks if she stays off it, which she won't."

"I heard that," Pell said through her teeth.

"You were meant to."

I stepped outside into the blue hour. The village smelled of woodsmoke and river and, underneath it, faintly, of something chemical and sweet—the smell I'd first caught at the abandoned survey camp, the smell I now understood followed the water like a shadow follows a body.

Efua found me at the well.

There were three wells in the village, she explained, walking me between them as the first stars came out. The old well behind the temple, dug by her grandfather's generation, still sweet—or it had been until March. The new well by the schoolhouse, drilled with government money eight years ago, which had never run true but had run safe. And this one, the middle well, the one the whole east side of the village drew from, because it was closest and deepest and had never once failed them.

She lowered the bucket herself. The rope groaned; the pulley needed oil. When the bucket came up I expected gray water, and it was almost worse than that: the water looked clear. Clean. Ordinary. It was only when she poured some into a clay bowl and held it to the lantern that I saw it—the faintest opalescence, a ghost of rainbow swirling in the depths like the memory of a color.

"The children call it star-water," Efua said. "They think it's pretty."

"And the adults?"

"The adults stopped drinking it in April." She set the bowl down on the well's stone lip. "But you cannot stop a well from being a well. The water table is poisoned. It moves. In June the temple well showed the same shine. Last week, the schoolhouse well." She looked at me, and her voice stayed level the way a bridge stays level—with enormous hidden effort. "Do you know what is upstream of us, diver?"

"I've seen it."

"Then you know it has a name written on it. Government stamps. We found a valve cap in the flood debris after the spring rains, washed down from the gorge. My brother-in-law keeps it. Come."

* * *

Her brother-in-law's house was the largest on the row, and inside, amid fishing gear and drying maize and the ordinary clutter of a full life, he kept a shrine of evidence: a shelf above the doorway holding a rusted valve cap stamped with a consortium mark I recognized from the terrace works, a length of pipe with serial numbers, a child's drawing of the gray river with a black snake coiled in it, and photographs—dozens of them, dated in careful handwriting. Water samples in sealed jars, lined up chronologically like a slow-motion disaster. The earliest jars held clear water. By jar eleven, the shimmer. By jar twenty, the water had gone the color of tarnished silver.

"We are not educated people," Efua said, watching me examine them. "But we can read a calendar. The sickness started in the children first. Fevers that would not break. Sores in the mouth. Little Kofi—" she touched the child's drawing, the black snake, "—he is seven. He spent the wet season in the provincial hospital. They told his mother it was typhoid. Typhoid does not come from a well that runs silver."

"How many children?"

"Nine sick that we know of. One died in May." She said it the way you'd report the weather, because grief that size cannot afford inflection. "A girl. Six years old. Her mother still draws water from the temple well every morning and pours it on the girl's grave, because she says the dead should have clean water too, whatever the living must drink."

I put the valve cap down. My hands were steady—I had trained them to be steady in black water, forty meters down, when steadiness was the difference between living and dying—but something behind them was not.

"Why show me this?" I asked. "You don't know me."

"No," Efua agreed. "But Tamás risked his life bringing you here, and Tamás lies beautifully, but he does not lie about people. He told us you dove into a flooded mine to see the source with your own eyes. He said you nearly drowned doing it." She studied my face. "Is that true?"

"It's true."

"Then you have seen what we can only taste." She picked up the oldest jar, the clear one, and held it beside the newest, the silvered one, and the space between them was nine months and one dead child and a watershed dying from the head down. "The consortium sent men in June. Very polite men. They offered money for the whole east bank—all the houses, the wells, the nets. Relocation compensation, they called it. Enough to move everyone to the resettlement camp near the highway." Her mouth thinned. "My husband's grave is behind this village. My mother's grave. My grandfather fished this bend of the river before there was a province with this name. They offered us money to disappear, diver, and half the village wanted to take it, because the children are sick and the water is dying and hope is heavy to carry uphill."

"And the other half?"

"The other half remembered that rivers do not stop at property lines." She set the jars down. "If we take their money and move, the mine keeps bleeding. Other villages downstream. The delta fisheries. The city reservoirs, eventually. We would be paid to be the first ones silenced." She turned to face me fully, and in the lamplight her amber eyes were fierce and exhausted and absolutely unbroken. "So I will ask you plainly, because you strike me as a woman who respects plainness. Whatever you found up there—whatever names and stamps and signatures—you will carry it out of these hills. Not for us. For the river. Because the river cannot speak, and we have tried, and no one listens to poor villages."

* * *

I couldn't sleep. I sat on the dock—the intact half of it, legs dangling over water that glimmered wrong beneath the moon—and counted the things I knew.

I knew the tailings pond was breached. I knew the valves bore government stamps. I knew the survey team's instruments had logged the contamination weeks before any official operation began, which meant the operation was never an accident; it was a schedule. I knew the consortium owned the navy's silence, and probably a great deal else. I had samples in a waterproof case stowed under my boat's thwart—water from the source itself, drawn at depth, labeled in my own hand. Geological charts. Photographs. The transmission log Pell had decoded, with its industrial chemical signature dated into impossibility.

Everything. I had everything.

And it was worth nothing unless it left my hands.

That was the arithmetic that had kept me awake since the mine, since the tower plan had first taken shape in Tamás's furious whispering and Pell's fever-bright scheming. Evidence in a waterproof case is just ballast. It sinks at the same rate as guilt. To matter, it had to go out—to a news service, a regulator, a court, the world—and going out required the relay tower at Mile Zero, the consortium's own uplink, the one piece of infrastructure in these hills strong enough to push a signal through monsoon cloud cover.

And the relay tower required codes. Administrative override codes, clearance-tier authentication, the kind of credentials that couldn't be guessed or forged in a night.

The kind of credentials that belonged to a woman who had buried her name ten years ago in a different country, under different water, after a different failure that had taught her the price of standing in the light.

I heard footsteps on the dock boards and didn't turn. Only two people in this village walked that softly.

Tamás settled beside me, close enough that our shoulders touched. He handed me a mug of something hot and bitter—herbal, medicinal, the taste of a place that treated its sicknesses with what it had. Below us, the gray river slid past, carrying its invisible poison toward a thousand downstream lives with the serene indifference of all water everywhere.

"My aunt talked to you," he said.

"She talked at me. There's a difference."

"She likes you, then." He drank. For a while neither of us spoke, and the monsoon clouds moved in from the west like a slow armada, swallowing the stars one by one. "Tomorrow we plan the tower," he said finally. "Pell has the transmission schedule memorized. There's a gap during the storm peak—four hours, maybe five, when the patrols shelter at the lower station. It's enough time. If we're fast. If we're lucky."

"Luck isn't a plan."

"No," he agreed. "But it's the only crew member who works for free." He turned to look at me, and in the dark his face was mostly eyes. "There's something else. Pell figured it out tonight, working the numbers on the relay specs. The tower's authentication protocol—it's not consortium-native. It's federal infrastructure, requisitioned under emergency authority. Which means the override codes aren't consortium codes at all." He hesitated, and I watched him decide to say it anyway, because that was who Tamás was underneath all his beautiful lying: a man who, at the hinge moments, chose truth. "They're government codes. High-tier. Salvage-division tier, actually—the tower was built to serve naval hydrographic operations. Which means whoever transmits needs credentials from a salvage officer with active clearance." His voice gentled. "Isandro. Whoever transmits needs to be you."

The river made its sound beneath us, that endless patient whisper, and I thought about a name I hadn't spoken aloud in ten years. I thought about a courtroom, a verdict, a career ending in a single afternoon like a mast going over. I thought about invisibility as a country I had emigrated to, with its own language and laws, and how I had believed I would die there, peacefully, unnamed.

Instead a six-year-old girl was dead under a mound behind a temple, and her mother poured clean water on her grave because the living had none.

"Tamás," I said. "When we get to the tower—if we get to the tower—there's something you and Pell need to know first. About who I was. About what I did." I drained the bitter tea and set the mug down. "And you're both going to want to hear it before you stake your lives on my name. Because tomorrow, using it might be the only thing that saves this river—and it will cost me everything I have left."

Lightning flickered far to the west, inside the arriving monsoon, like something vast turning over in its sleep.

Tamás looked at me for a long moment. Then he nodded slowly, and said, "Then tell us tonight. The storm won't wait, and neither will the children."

Behind us, up the bank, a door opened—Pell, propped on crutches in the doorway, her splinted leg silhouetted against lamplight, having heard every word.

"Well," she said. "Might as well start now."
