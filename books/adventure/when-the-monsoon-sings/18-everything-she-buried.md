## 18. Everything She Buried

The rain came to the village two days before they were ready for it.

Wren Pell heard it first as a sound she couldn't name—a hush, then a hiss, like the river drawing a long breath through its teeth—and then it arrived in earnest, drumming on the corrugated roof of Tamás's cousin's house where the three of them had spread out everything they'd carried down from the terraces: the charts, the water samples, the survey team's field logs, the photographs Isandro had taken at the flooded shaft with her waterproof camera held steady in black water while the world above her tried to kill her.

Pell sat cross-legged on the concrete floor with a grease pencil behind her ear and the last transmission printout flattened under a jar of river stones, and she tried to make the data into a map. That was what she knew how to do. Give her coordinates and she would give you back a country. But this was not a country. This was a wound, and wounds did not render well at any scale she'd been trained in.

"Say it again," Tamás said from the doorway. He'd come in soaked to the waist, carrying a crate of the village's water tests—little glass vials racked in straw like eggs, each one holding a color that had no business existing in anything a child might drink. He set them down beside the samples from upstream. The colors matched. Of course they matched. "Say the part about the relay."

"The tower at Mile Zero," Pell said. "It's a government relay—hydrological telemetry, weather, emergency broadcast. It was built to serve the whole watershed. Which means it still has the transmit power to reach every settlement downstream, every patrol post, every news office that picks up river-band traffic." She tapped the printout. "And it's keyed. Old system, but keyed. You can't just walk in and press send. You need operator clearance codes—level four or higher—to unlock the transmitter for an unscheduled broadcast. Anything less, the tower logs your attempt and pings the nearest garrison. We'd be announcing ourselves before we ever said a word."

"So we need codes," Tamás said.

"We need *her* codes." Pell kept her eyes on the map. It was easier than watching his face do what it was going to do. "Isandro's. The ones tied to the name she buried."

The rain filled the silence that followed, patient as water finding a crack.

"She won't give them," Tamás said finally. "You've seen how she is. The woman counts miles so she doesn't have to count years. You think she's going to hand us the key to all of it?"

"I think," Pell said, "that she's already decided. I've watched her decide things for three weeks now. She decides the way a river decides—slowly, underground, and then all at once." She rolled the grease pencil between her fingers. "But deciding isn't the same as telling. And telling—" She stopped. Started again. "Telling is the hard part. Believe me. I'm the one who stayed at base camp while my team walked into that valley. I know exactly how much distance there is between knowing a thing and saying it out loud."

Tamás looked at her for a long moment, rain running off his jaw onto the floorboards, and whatever he saw in her face made him nod once and go back out into the storm without another word. That was the thing about Tamás Oyelaran that Pell had come to trust more than his navigation: he knew when a person needed the room to be empty.

She sat alone with the maps and the rain and the little vials of poisoned color, and she thought about names. Her own had never cost her anything—that was the difference between them, she understood now. Wren Pell had lost a career, a license, a place in the survey corps, but she had done it loudly, in daylight, with witnesses. Disgrace was a public wound; it healed in public too, scar tissue and all. Isandro Coldharbour had chosen the opposite surgery. She had cut her old self out whole and buried it somewhere deep, and she had lived ten years in the quiet country of a new name, counting miles, keeping rules, touching nothing that could dig the grave back up.

And now Pell was going to ask her to exhume herself in front of two people she'd known for less than a month.

*You owe her this much,* Pell told herself. *You owe her the asking gently.*

But she suspected gentleness wasn't what Isandro would want. Isandro struck Pell as a woman who had been handled gently her whole hidden life—gently avoided, gently not-asked—and who might, at the bottom of everything, be starving for someone to simply look straight at her.

* * *

They gathered that night in the boathouse, because Isandro asked for the boathouse.

Pell understood why the moment she stepped inside. It smelled of tar and rope and cold water, the way Isandro's boathouse upriver must have smelled—the one room in any structure that belonged to her trade and no one else's. A lantern hung from a beam. The dive gear hung drying along one wall, Isandro's suit ghost-limp, arms empty. Rain hammered the tin roof so hard the conversation would be private even with the door open, which it was, because Isandro didn't close doors if she could help it. Pell had noticed that early on. Doors closing were a sound she didn't tolerate.

Isandro sat on an overturned crate with her forearms on her knees, and she looked, Pell thought, like a woman at the edge of a dive: checked, calm, already halfway down.

"You'll want to sit," Isandro said.

They sat. Tamás took the workbench. Pell took an oil drum near the lantern, close enough to see Isandro's hands, because she had learned in the survey corps that people's hands told the truth their mouths were editing.

"My name isn't Coldharbour," Isandro began, without preamble, the way she entered water. "It hasn't been for eleven years. Before that I was—" and here her voice did something small and terrible, a hitch no deeper than a heartbeat, "—I was Lieutenant Isandro Vael, Corps of Hydrographic Engineers. Clearance level five. Salvage and demolition specialty."

Pell felt Tamás go very still beside her.

"I built the intake systems," Isandro said. "That's the joke nobody downstream will laugh at. Not the navy's salvage cover story—the real work, before that. River intakes, flood gates, tailings infrastructure. I designed half the valve assemblies you'll find stamped with government seals in that mine shaft, because I designed the *standard*. My standard. There are drawings with my signature on them sitting in a flooded tunnel nine miles upriver, poisoning every well in this valley, and I signed them when I was twenty-six years old and certain that engineering was a neutral country."

"It isn't," Tamás said quietly. It wasn't an accusation. It sounded almost like grief.

"No," Isandro said. "It isn't. I learned that the year the Karst Division project went wrong." She looked at the wall of hanging gear, not at them. Pell realized she was giving them the mercy of not being watched while they heard it—or perhaps taking the mercy for herself. "There was a dam. Upcountry, different watershed, you'll find nothing in the record now because I made sure of that later, which is its own crime. There was a fault line the surveys flagged and the consortium paid to have unflagged. I knew. I had the original readings. I was young and I was ambitious and a director I admired told me the numbers were within tolerance, and I wanted to believe him more than I wanted to check, so I signed the certification. Level-five clearance means your signature is law. My signature said the dam was safe."

The rain came down. Somewhere outside, a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

"Eleven years ago this month, it failed," Isandro said. "At night, during the spring melt. Forty-one people died in the valley below. Two of them were children who would have been—" Her hands, resting on her knees, closed slowly into fists and then, deliberately, opened again, finger by finger, the way a diver works through panic. "I wasn't there. I was at a conference receiving a commendation. I found out by telegram. And I did what I'd been trained to do by people better at it than me: I managed the narrative. I had the clearance to access the investigation files, so I accessed them, and I altered the ones that pointed at my certification, and I buried the original fault surveys under a classification seal that only a level-four code or higher can open. Then I resigned before the inquiry convened, changed my name, and went to work in salvage, where nobody asks a diver where she came from. They only ask how deep."

"Why tell us the number?" Tamás asked. His voice was rough. "Forty-one. Why that detail?"

"Because I've made myself remember it every day for eleven years," Isandro said. "Not the file numbers, not the hearing dates. The people. I don't know their names—I made sure I never learned their names, because learning them felt like something I hadn't earned—but I know the count, and I keep it the way other people keep prayers." She turned her head at last and looked at them both, and her eyes in the lantern light were dry and absolutely steady, which was somehow worse than tears. "So now you know why I count miles instead of years. Miles end. You log them and they're done. Years don't end. Years just accumulate everything you've done and sit there being true at you."

Nobody spoke. The lantern hissed. Water dripped from the hull of some old boat in the dark corner, a slow metronome.

It was Tamás who moved first. He crossed the boathouse—not quickly, the way he did everything on the river, reading the current before committing—and he crouched in front of Isandro so that he was looking up at her, which Pell suspected no one had done in a decade.

"My village has thirty-one wells," he said. "We tested all of them this week. Twenty-six run iridescent. Nine of our children are sick, and my sister's boy is the sickest, and I have lain awake doing arithmetic I'm ashamed of—counting whether forty-one dead eleven years ago buys more outrage than twenty-six poisoned wells does now, wondering if I'd trade your secret for my nephew's lungs." He held her gaze. "I never decided. I want you to know that. I never decided. But I thought about it, and you deserve to know that the man guiding you thought about it."

"Yes," Isandro said. "I know. I watched you think about it. It's why I trusted you."

Tamás laughed—one short, broken note—and put his hand briefly over hers, and Pell saw Isandro let it stay there for exactly three seconds before she withdrew, gentle as tide.

Then they both looked at Pell, and Pell understood that her turn had come, and that Isandro had arranged it that way on purpose. Confession for confession. Fair trade. The woman kept rules even in the wreckage of her own name.

"I stayed at base camp," Pell said. "When the team went up the gorge. I want to lie and say I was ordered to, but the order was optional and I chose the tent and the dry socks, because I'd already seen the chemical signatures in the early data and I knew what following them upstream might cost, and I calculated"—she heard her own voice flatten into the surveyor's cadence, the coward's native tongue—"I calculated that a disgraced cartographer was worth more alive to the truth than dead in a ravine. And then the transmission stopped, and I sat in that tent for six days with instruments running and no one coming back, and I learned that alive-and-useless and dead weigh exactly the same at three in the morning." She pulled the transmission printout from her jacket and laid it on the crate between them. "So. Codes."

Isandro looked at the printout for a long time. Then she reached into her collar and drew out a cord, and on the cord hung a flat brass tag, river-worn, the kind of thing a diver might wear as a keepsake. She'd worn it since the day Pell met her. Pell had assumed it was a memento mori, a dead brother, a lost ship.

It was a cipher tag. Stamped into the soft metal, nearly effaced by years of salt water and skin, was a sequence of characters.

"Level five," Isandro said. "Vael, I. Hydrographic Corps. The tower will accept it—if the registry hasn't been purged. If it has, we're pressing send on a dead man's key and the garrison comes anyway." She turned the tag over in her fingers, and Pell watched her hold eleven years of exile in the palm of her hand and consider setting it down. "Once I use this, the name comes back. Not quietly. Hearings. Headlines. Every reporter in the watershed will dig up the Karst failure inside a week, and they will print *Lieutenant Isandro Vael, whose certified dam killed forty-one people, exposed the consortium*—and they'll be right to print it, all of it, the exposure and the dam together. I don't get to be the hero of this. I get to be the whole truth, which is worse."

"Better," Tamás said.

"Worse," Isandro agreed, and almost smiled. "But yes. Better." She looked around the boathouse—at the drying suit, the tar smell, the door standing open on the rain—and Pell saw her take inventory the way she took inventory before a deep dive, checking each piece of equipment she was about to leave behind. "Ten years I built a life that fit through a hatch. One name, one boat, one rule: touch nothing that remembers you." She closed her fist around the tag. "Tomorrow we take the tower. And when Pell opens that transmitter, she opens it with my real name in the header. No anonymity. If the watershed is going to hear the truth, it's going to hear exactly who it's coming from—all of who."

Pell's throat tightened. "Isandro—"

"That's the deal," Isandro said. "Take it or leave it. I've spent eleven years believing the worst thing I could do was be known." She stood, and the lantern threw her shadow tall against the hanging gear, diver-shaped, weightless. "Turns out the worst thing was what I did to stay unknown."

* * *

Later, when Tamás had gone to wake his cousins and the village was nothing but rain and a few banked fires, Pell found Isandro out on the dock, sitting at the end with her boots off and her feet in the black water, which was such an Isandro gesture that Pell almost laughed—sitting in the very thing that had ruined her, feet-first, unafraid.

Pell sat down beside her. For a while neither of them spoke. The river moved beneath them, carrying its invisible poison toward the sea, and overhead the monsoon worked through the dark like a hand sorting papers.

"When I alter those files," Isandro said at last, to the water, "it took me four hours. Four hours, and I was good enough at it that no auditor ever caught it. Do you know what I've thought about, every day since? Not the dam. The four hours. How easy it was. How calm I was. I made tea halfway through." She flexed her toes under the surface. "People imagine monsters feel monstrous. They don't. They feel efficient."

"I know," Pell said. "I recalculated a floodplain once because a minister's brother owned land inside it. Took me an afternoon. Three hundred people flood every spring now, and I got a promotion." She hugged her knees. "We're not monsters, Isandro. We're just people who were excellent at the wrong thing, in rooms where excellence was the only virtue anyone checked."

Isandro was quiet a long moment. Then: "Why did you come after me? At the second site. You didn't know me. You begged a stranger with a boat to take you upstream into a restricted zone. That's not calculation. That's the opposite."

Pell watched the rain stipple the river. "Because I'd spent six days in that tent learning what staying costs," she said. "And because when you surfaced with that crate and wouldn't open it, I saw someone carrying the same weight I was. Different cargo, same weight." She shrugged. "Misery recognizes its accent."

Isandro huffed something that was almost a laugh. Then she lifted the cord over her head and held the brass tag out to Pell, flat on her palm, rain beginning to spot the metal.

"Hold this tonight," Isandro said. "If I change my mind by morning—which I won't, but if—I want it to be harder than reaching into my own pocket."

Pell took it. It was warm from Isandro's skin, lighter than it had any right to be, this small brass coin of a dead woman's name that could bring down a consortium and resurrect a disgrace in the same breath.

"First light," Pell said. "Tamás says the tower's generators run on a maintenance cycle at dawn—thirty minutes when the security grid resets. After that we're climbing a mountain in full monsoon with a garrison forty minutes behind us."

"Then we climb fast," Isandro said, and pulled her feet from the water, and for the first time since Pell had known her, she didn't check the sky, or the current, or the miles. She just stood, in the rain, in her own name, and started walking.

Pell followed, the tag clenched in her fist like a coal, and behind them the river ran on through the dark—sick, patient, and, if the dawn went right, not for much longer.
