## 19. The Tower at Mile Zero

The monsoon arrived the way grief arrives—not all at once, but in a pressure you feel in your teeth before the sky ever splits.

Isandro felt it first as a change in the river's voice. For nineteen miles it had spoken to her in rapids and riffles, in the low gossip of water over stone, but now, as they climbed the last switchback trail above the gorge with the rain beginning to fall in fat, warm drops that struck the leaves like knuckles on a door, the whole watershed seemed to hold its breath. The air went thick. The birds went quiet. Even Tamás stopped talking, which she had not once seen him do in all their days together.

"There," he said instead, and pointed.

The tower stood on a basalt shoulder of the headwaters where the river was born—where a hundred seeps and springs braided together out of the moss-dark folds of the mountain and became, within a mile, the broad brown artery that fed everything downstream: the gorge, the flooded forest, the terraces, the village of gray wells. The relay tower itself was older than the consortium's lies—a government survey beacon from some earlier century's ambition, sixty feet of rusted lattice crowned with a dish that had been repainted so recently its white still gleamed through the rain. A cable ran from its base down into the earth, and beside it, half-swallowed by fern, stood the intake works: a concrete apron, a steel hatch, a ladder bolted to rock, and beneath all of it—the thing Isandro had come nine miles down a river and ten years down her own life to reach—the submerged valve gallery where the tailings bled into the aquifer.

Pell crouched at the treeline, rain running off the brim of her ruined hat, and unfolded the survey map one last time. Her hands were steady. Isandro had watched them shake for two days after the escape from the living camp, after Marta Osei chose the consortium's amnesty and walked away downstream with the patrol boats, after Sabik cut their mooring lines in the dark and paid for it with a broken wrist and a silence no one could fault him for. But now the shaking had burned off, the way fog burns off before a storm, and what was left underneath was something harder.

"Generator shed here," Pell said, tapping. "Two guards on rotation—we watched them for an hour. They change at the top of every even hour, and they spend the changeover under the awning smoking, backs to the door. The relay room is unlocked because nobody expects anyone to be stupid enough to climb here in this." She looked up. Rain caught in her eyelashes. "Which we are."

"We're exactly stupid enough," Isandro said. "That's why it works."

Tamás laughed softly, once, without humor. He had his machete out and was testing the edge against his thumbnail, though there was nothing between here and the tower that needed cutting except the rain. His village lay thirty miles downstream, its wells running iridescent, its children coughing in the night, and Isandro had learned to read the particular stillness he carried—that coiled patience of a man who has been walking toward something for years and is finally close enough to hear it breathing.

"The intake hatch is your door," he said to her. "Mine is theirs." He nodded toward the generator shed. "Pell's is the sky."

"Then we go on my mark," Isandro said. "Not before. If I'm not back up in forty minutes—"

"You'll be back," Tamás said.

"If I'm not," she said again, and let the word hang there, heavy as the rain, "Pell transmits anyway. Everything we have. The charts, the samples, the transmission log, the photographs of the valves with the government stamps. You don't wait for me. You don't come looking. You send it." She looked at each of them in turn. "Promise me. Both of you. Because if this goes wrong and the evidence goes down with me, then everything—all of it, the camp, the gorge, Sabik's wrist, the miles—it was for nothing."

Pell met her eyes. In the gray light her face looked carved, older than her years, the way stone gets old. "I promise," she said. And then, quieter, with the ghost of the woman who had decoded a chemical signature alone in a base camp tent while her colleagues vanished upstream: "But you should know I've gotten very good at not keeping promises to people who deserve better ones. So come back, and make me a liar."

Isandro almost smiled. Almost. Instead she pulled on her hood and checked her gear one final time by touch, the way she did everything that mattered—blind, counting, certain. Tank. Regulator. Line reel. The cutting tool Tamás had ground sharp for her on a whetstone by firelight while pretending not to hum. The jamming wedges, cut from the hardwood of a wrecked patrol skiff, ironwood dense enough to stop a valve seat cold. Forty minutes of air. She had dived wrecks with less.

She counted life in miles logged, not years lived. She had said that once to herself like a comfort, like a rule. Somewhere between Mile Eleven and this moment, the rule had quietly died, and what remained was not a diver with a ledger but a woman with people—and people, she understood now, were the one cargo you could not seal in a crate and refuse to open.

"Mark," she said.

* * *

They came down through the ferns as the rain found its rhythm.

Tamás moved first, a shadow among shadows, flowing down the slope with the machete flat against his forearm, and Isandro lost sight of him almost immediately—which was the point of him. Pell followed the cable trench, bent double, carrying the transmitter case against her chest like a child. Isandro went last and lowest, along the streambed where the water covered the sound of her fins and tank, until the intake apron rose out of the murk ahead of her, gray concrete streaked black with mineral stain, the steel hatch set into it like a mouth.

Above her, muffled by rain, she heard the generator shed door bang open. Voices. A laugh. The even-hour changeover, right on schedule, right where Tamás wanted them—two men under an awning with cigarettes, while a third shadow they never saw slipped around behind the shed with a machete and twenty years of reasons.

Isandro worked the hatch wheel. It fought her, rusted, then gave with a shriek she felt in her spine more than heard, swallowed instantly by the drumming rain. Below, black water breathed up at her out of the shaft, smelling of iron and something sweeter, something wrong—the faint chemical sweetness she had first noticed at the abandoned survey camp, the smell of the river running iridescent. The aquifer's wound, exhaling.

She spat her regulator in, checked her gauge one last time, and went down feetfirst into the dark.

* * *

The valve gallery was a cathedral built by men who thought no one would ever pray here.

Her dive light swept across it in slow arcs: a flooded chamber the size of a chapel, cut into the mountain's root, its walls raw basalt glistening wet. Along the far wall ran the gallery itself—six great valves in a row, each as tall as she was, each connected by pipe runs thick as her waist to the breached tailings pond somewhere above and beyond the rock. Government stamps on every valve, just as she'd seen in the photographs: stamped plates, inspection tags, serial numbers that traced back through three ministries and two administrations and one navy that officially denied any of this existed. The consortium hadn't hidden the evidence. They had embossed it, certified it, filed it—and drowned it where no one would ever dive.

Except one.

Isandro swam the length of the gallery, laying her line as she went, clipping it to the anchor points Tamás had described from his father's stories of the old mine works. The water here was strange—too warm by degrees, faintly luminous at the edges of her beam where the tailings plume caught the light and scattered it into a ghostly, oily shimmer. It was beautiful. That was the horror of it. It was beautiful the way a lie is beautiful when it's told well, and she had been telling herself one for ten years: that a person could seal herself off, log her miles, keep her rules, and call the silence safety.

Valve one. She braced her boots against the pipe saddle, set the first ironwood wedge into the relief port, and drove it home with the mallet strapped to her thigh. Three strikes. The wedge seated with a sound she felt through her bones rather than heard—a deep, structural groan, the mountain noticing. When the consortium tried to reopen this valve, the wedge would bind the seat. Not forever. Long enough.

Valve two. Three strikes. The groove of it steadied her: strike, check, strike, check, strike. Rules. She had always been best inside rules. But these weren't her old rules, the ones that had kept her small and invisible and safe. These were new ones, written in a language she was only now fluent in: *come back up. Pell transmits. Tamás holds the door. The children downstream sleep clean.*

Valve three.

That was when the mountain spoke back.

It began as a vibration in the water, a pressure wave she registered through her chest before her ears—a deep, rhythmic thrumming, mechanical, getting louder. Her light snapped toward the sound and found it: at the gallery's far end, half-hidden behind a bulkhead, a pump. Running. Not idle—running hard, its impeller churning the poisoned water faster, feeding the bleed. The consortium wasn't just letting the tailings leak. They were pushing it.

And bolted to the pump housing, blinking green in the dark, a sensor node. A monitor. Which meant telemetry. Which meant somewhere above her, someone might be watching a screen flicker as a wedge seated in valve three.

Isandro hung in the warm, glowing water and made the calculation divers make: air, distance, time. Thirty-one minutes left. Four valves remaining, plus the pump, plus the climb. The math was thin. The math had been thin since the day she cracked open a crate she was paid not to open, and every mile since had been spent from an account she couldn't refill.

So she stopped doing math and started doing work.

Valve four. Three strikes, harder now, her arms burning, the mallet blows ringing through the flooded rock like a bell tolling underwater. Valve five. The sensor node's green blink had gone amber. Someone knew. Somewhere above, boots would be moving, radios crackling, and Tamás would be standing between those boots and the tower door with a machete and no intention whatsoever of losing.

Valve six—the master, the biggest, the heart of the bleed. She set the last wedge, raised the mallet, and the lights went out.

Not hers. The gallery's. Work floods high on the chamber wall, installed for the maintenance crews who never came, guttered and died, and the darkness that replaced them was total, ancient, the kind of dark that has been waiting under a mountain since before there were names for it. Her dive light suddenly looked small as a match. The pump thundered somewhere behind her, blind and huge.

Isandro Coldharbour hung in the black water at the bottom of the world and did the only thing left that mattered. She closed her eyes—there was nothing to see—and she dove the way she had learned to dive ten years ago, in another dark, in another life: by count, by touch, by line. Hand over hand along her own rope. Find the saddle. Set the wedge. One. Two. Three.

The master valve groaned. Bound. Held.

And then, through the rock, faint and impossible and unmistakable, she heard it: a sound rising from far above, filtering down through sixty feet of basalt and rain and sky. Not thunder. Not the pump.

Singing. High and wild and many-voiced—the monsong wind finding the tower's lattice and the gorge's throat and playing them both, the whole headwaters ringing like a struck instrument, and underneath it, thinner, human, carried somehow through stone and water alike: Pell's voice, or the memory of it, reading coordinates into a microphone, sending the truth downstream to every antenna on the continent.

The monsoon had arrived at Mile Zero. And it was singing.

Isandro turned for the surface with eleven minutes of air and a smile she couldn't explain, reeling in her line hand over hand through the luminous dark—too slow, too slow, the pump's wash tugging at her fins—and did not see, until her light swept back for the last check, the fresh movement at the gallery's far end: the bulkhead door, unsealed, swinging open, and the pale bloom of another dive light entering her cathedral from below.

Someone was coming up the flooded shaft from the mine side.

Someone who knew these waters.

Her gauge needle trembled at nine minutes. Above her, the hatch was a coin of gray light, impossibly far. Behind her, the second light grew brighter, sweeping, searching, closing.

Isandro killed her own lamp and vanished into the dark she had finally stopped being afraid of.
