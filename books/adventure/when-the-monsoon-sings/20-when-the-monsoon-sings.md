## 20. When the Monsoon Sings

**Pell**

The tower smelled of ozone and old rain, and Wren Pell loved it the way you love a thing that is about to kill you.

She had the relay's guts spread across a folding table like a surgery in progress—circuit boards, a jury-rigged transmitter, Isandro's clearance codes written on her forearm in grease pencil because paper could be lost and skin, apparently, could not. Outside, the monsoon had arrived in earnest, three weeks early and furious about it, hammering the corrugated roof with a sound like applause from an enormous, indifferent audience.

The codes had cost Isandro everything. Pell kept thinking about that—the way she'd said them aloud last night, flat as a shipping manifest, *my name was Anselm Vireo, I held Level Four hydrological clearance, and ten years ago I signed off on a diversion project that drowned a valley*, and how Tamás had simply nodded, as if shame were a river he'd swum before. Grace, it turned out, was mostly just refusing to flinch.

Now she keyed the sequence. The console woke with a stutter, coughed static, then found its voice.

"Broadcasting," she said into the handheld. "All bands. It's live."

Isandro's voice came back through the rain-static, thin and steady: "Copy. Give me twenty minutes."

Pell looked at the screen where the evidence scrolled in endless repetition—the chemical signatures, the government stamps, the photographs of the breached tailings pond, the survey team's final transmission decoded at last—and felt something loosen in her chest that had been clenched since the day her colleagues vanished. She had spent two years being the cartographer who mapped a lie. Tonight she was mapping the truth instead, and the truth, it turned out, transmitted beautifully.

Then the radio crackled with a second voice, cold and official: *"Unidentified transmission source, this is Consortium Security Patrol Six. Cease broadcast immediately."*

Pell smiled at the rain.

"No," she said, and keyed the repeat cycle.

* * *

**Tamás**

The road to the tower was a single track of red mud, and Tamás Oyelaran intended to keep it empty.

He'd taken the door position without being asked, the way he took most things—quietly, with a machete he'd carried since he was fifteen and a knowledge of this gorge so complete it was almost a form of prayer. He knew which footfalls the mud swallowed and which it announced. He knew that the patrol vehicle would have to slow at the washout at kilometer four, and that the driver would curse in exactly the way drivers cursed when they thought no one downstream could hear them.

The rain came down in ropes. Through it he watched headlights swing around the bend below, two vehicles, maybe eight men. Behind him, up the tower stairs, Pell's signal was already flying over the watershed like a bird released—reaching relay stations, reaching the provincial capital, reaching, if the fates were kind, every radio in every kitchen in every village along two hundred miles of dying water.

His village was forty miles downstream. His niece had a fever that wouldn't break. He thought of her small hands and put the thought away like a stone in a pocket, to be carried, not looked at.

The first vehicle stopped fifty meters out. Doors opened. Flashlights cut the rain into white blades.

"Tower's closed!" someone shouted. "Come down or we come up!"

Tamás stepped into the light, machete loose at his side, rain streaming off his jaw, and gave them his best lie—which these days were all truths wearing work clothes.

"You're welcome to come up," he called back. "But the broadcast's already gone out on six relays. You'd be climbing stairs to arrest a memory."

There was a pause. Rain filled it.

Then the flashlights started moving anyway, and Tamás set his feet in the mud, and began the arithmetic of holding a door.

* * *

**Isandro**

Nine miles down, the water did not sing. It screamed.

Isandro Coldharbour moved through the flooded mine shaft with her dive light carving a cone through silt-brown blackness, following the guide line toward the tailings valve while above her, somewhere beyond a hundred feet of rising floodwater, Pell's broadcast went out and Tamás held the door and the monsoon sang its one long note against the mountain.

The shaft was older than the consortium's maps admitted. Timber shoring, rotted soft, leaned inward like tired men. Her light found the valve assembly at sixty feet—a great iron throat bolted into the rock, government stamps still legible beneath a decade of slime, discharging a slow gray plume that curled upward like smoke from a buried fire. The aquifer drank it all. Every well downstream. Every child.

She set the jamming charge Tamás had built—crude, honest, village-made—and checked the line to surface. One tug for okay. Two for trouble.

One tug.

She was seating the charge when the mountain spoke.

It wasn't a sound so much as a decision—the whole shaft deciding, all at once, that it had been open long enough. A section of shoring upstream let go with a groan like a ship dying, and the silt came down in a wall, and her guide line went slack in her hand.

Cut. Cut line. She knew the rule; she'd written half the rules herself, ten years and a buried name ago. *A fouled line is a dead line. Surface on backup or don't surface.*

But the backup line ran through the collapse. And the valve hadn't sealed yet. And somewhere above her, a feverish child was drinking this water.

Isandro hung in the black water, breathing her remaining air in careful silver coins, and made the choice that had been making itself inside her since a crate first breathed in a boathouse: she stayed, and finished the work, and only then reached for a line that wasn't there.

Two tugs. No answer. Of course no answer. The line was gone.

Her gauge read nine minutes. The exit was a memory. She began swimming anyway, because that was the whole of what she'd ever known how to do—log the miles, make the strokes, refuse the arithmetic of despair—and in the dark ahead, impossibly, a light bloomed.

* * *

**Tamás**

They came up the stairs four abreast, and Tamás met them at the landing where the staircase turned, where one man with a machete and bad intentions could become a crowd's worst geometry.

He didn't want to hurt them. That surprised him a little. He'd expected to want it.

"Last chance to walk down," he said.

The lead man raised a pistol instead, and Tamás learned in that instant what the stories never tell you—that courage is mostly deafness, that in the moment itself you don't hear the thunder, you only see the lightning's afterimage and understand that you are still standing, still holding, still between the door and the thing behind it.

He swung. Someone screamed. A flashlight spun away into the rain and died. Hands found him—many hands, too many—and he went down under their weight with his cheek in the mud and the tower stairs above him and the broadcast, Pell's beautiful stubborn broadcast, still repeating, still flying, still free.

*"This is Relay Seven,"* the tower said above them all, serene as scripture. *"Repeating for those who joined late."*

Tamás laughed into the mud, blood and rainwater mingling between his teeth, and held on—not to the ground, but to the knowledge that it was enough. It had already been enough. They couldn't climb fast enough to catch a signal.

* * *

**Isandro**

The light resolved into a diver, and the diver resolved into the last person in the world who should be there.

Tamás's cousin Devi—no. Not Devi. She blinked salt water and silt from her mask and understood, with the strange clarity of low oxygen, that it didn't matter. What mattered was the spare regulator offered to her mouth, the strong grip finding her harness, the shared language of line and light that needed no words at all.

Later she would learn the whole story—how Tamás had broken protocol once before, in chapter and verse, and taught his family to do the same; how the village had listened to Pell's broadcast on a battery radio and sent their best swimmer upriver into a flood to answer two tugs that never came. How the water that poisoned them had also, tonight, delivered their rescue.

For now there was only the ascent: two divers rising through the screaming dark, sharing air, the sealed valve glowing faintly green behind them—sealed, jammed, dead—the gray plume already thinning like a lie exposed to daylight.

Her gauge hit zero as they broke the surface in the flooded adit, gasping, alive, the rain roaring down through the mine's broken throat above them like applause.

Above them, faintly, through the stone, through the water, through everything: the tower, still singing.

* * *

**Pell**

They came for the tower at 3 a.m., and by then it was far too late.

Wren Pell heard the scuffle on the stairs, heard Tamás go down, heard boots on the landing—and did the only thing left in her repertoire. She grabbed the transmitter's master drive, the physical core of it, and held it up over the rail like a heart torn bleeding from a chest.

"Break that door," she shouted into the storm, "and I drop it—but the broadcast finished its first full cycle eleven minutes ago. Check your own patrol radios. Ask your capital office why they're not answering. Go on. *Ask.*"

The men hesitated. Rain drummed. Somewhere below, Tamás laughed wetly in the mud.

And then, from the valley dark, came a sound none of them had planned for: engines. Many engines. Not the consortium's—these were farm trucks, fishing boats' outboards carried on trailers, the whole ragged congregation of the downstream villages arriving at mile zero with machetes and lanterns and fury, summoned by a signal that had been singing into their kitchens for hours.

The patrolmen looked at each other. Looked at the stairs. Looked at the darkness filling up with the people they had been poisoning.

They ran.

Pell set the drive down gently, went to the window, and watched the lights of the convoy climb toward her through the rain—hundreds of them, thousands, a river of light flowing uphill against every law of nature except the one that mattered.

Below, they pulled Tamás out of the mud, and he was laughing, and bleeding, and alive.

And from the flooded mine below the mountain, slick with silt and sharing one regulator's worth of borrowed breath, Isandro Coldharbour surfaced into the storm—into the noise, the lanterns, the outstretched hands—as the tailings valve stood silent behind her, sealed at last, and the monsoon sang over all of them like something finally, finally forgiven.

Downstream, forty miles away, a fevered child slept in a house where the radio had been playing all night—and in the morning, when they drew the well water, it would be the last morning the water ran gray.

But that was tomorrow's miracle. Tonight there was only the tower, the rain, the crowd, and three people at mile zero who had run out of miles—and found, at the end of all of them, each other.
