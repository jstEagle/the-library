## 21. Miles, Not Years

The hearings were held in a government building with marble floors that echoed like a cave, and Isandro thought, more than once, that she would rather be in an actual cave. At least down there the echoes belonged to her.

They sat her at a long table beneath a bank of lights hot enough to make the water bottle beside her sweat. Behind the table, a panel of officials whose names she had already forgotten asked questions in the flat cadence of people who had rehearsed their outrage. She answered what she could. She declined what she wouldn't. Somewhere behind the cameras and the stenographers and the men in gray suits taking notes they would never read, Wren Pell sat with the decoded transmission spread out before her like a confession written in someone else's hand, and Tamás Oyelaran stood when they called his name and did not sit again until he had finished telling them about the wells.

*The wells,* she remembered thinking. *Start with the wells.* Because everyone in that room drank water too, and it was the one thing none of them could argue their way around.

The consortium's lawyers came in waves. They had documents stamped with government seals—she had seen those same stamps on the valves, nine miles down in black water, and hearing them recited aloud in a climate-controlled room made her skin prickle as though she were still submerged. They talked about operational necessity. About classified partnerships. About a regrettable misunderstanding of survey data. One of them used the phrase *isolated hydrological anomaly* four times, and each time Pell's pen stopped moving, and each time Isandro watched her jaw tighten like a cable coming under load.

But documents can be forged, and language can be laundered. What could not be argued away was a jar of iridescent water from a village well, set on the testimony table by a girl of maybe eleven who had walked to the capital with her grandmother because Tamás's sister had organized the buses. The girl set the jar down with both hands, careful as an egg, and looked up at the panel, and said nothing at all. She didn't have to. The light caught the faint oily shimmer turning in the water, rainbow-thin, wrong, beautiful the way a fever is beautiful, and Isandro heard the room change. A sound like breath being held by two hundred lungs at once.

That was the moment, if she had to name one. Not the broadcast from the tower at Mile Zero, not the sealed valves, not even the photographs of the breached tailings pond that ran on every front page for eleven days. It was a child setting down a jar.

* * *

Her old name was public within forty-eight hours of the first hearing.

She learned it the way most people learn things now—by overhearing it. Two junior clerks in the corridor outside the committee room, voices pitched low but not low enough: *that's her, that's the diver, you know she used to be—* and then the name, spoken carelessly, the way you'd say any word, and Isandro kept walking because stopping would have meant something and she had spent ten years teaching herself that stopping always means something.

The headlines did the rest. THE DIVER WHO WENT UNDER, one paper called her, which was almost clever. Another dug deeper, the way papers do, and found the wreck off the southern coast, the inquiry that had ended no inquiry at all, the name struck from the salvage registries and the crew lists redacted and the whole quiet machinery of a cover-up that had worked precisely because no one had ever bothered to look under it. They printed her photograph from a decade ago—younger, harder, hair cropped short for the helmet seal—and beside it a photograph taken last month outside the courthouse, and between the two images lay ten years of miles, and none of them visible.

People wanted things from her after that. Interview requests stacked up at the boathouse—her boathouse, still hers, though it felt smaller every time she returned to it, the way a coat fits differently once you've grown. Documentary producers. Publishers. A man from a maritime museum who wanted her old dive logs *for the archive*, and she burned those logs herself in a steel drum before she could think better of it, watching ten years of careful numbers curl and go gray, and then sat on the dock afterward feeling not lighter but strangely hollowed, like a shell the sea had finished with.

Because here was the thing she could not explain to the producers or the publishers or the clerks in the corridor: she had built that silence deliberately. Mile by mile, dive by dive, she had constructed a life with no surface, no trace, no name anyone could hold. It had cost her everything worth having, and she had paid gladly, because the alternative—the other life, the one with the wreck and the inquiry and the name—had cost her something worse. Or so she had believed. For ten years she had believed it the way you believe a load-bearing wall: not because you've tested it lately, but because the ceiling hasn't come down.

Then the ceiling came down, and she was still standing.

* * *

The letters started arriving in the third week.

Most of them she didn't answer. Some of them she couldn't read past the first line—a woman whose brother had fished the lower river for thirty years; a schoolteacher sending class photographs of children holding hand-lettered signs; a man who wrote only *I knew your father, he would have been proud*, which she closed immediately and did not open again for six days, and when she did, she read it three times and put it in the drawer with the others instead of the fire.

One letter was different. No return address, handwriting she almost recognized, and inside, a single sheet:

*You don't know me, but I served on the inquiry board. Ten years ago. I signed the finding that buried it. I want you to know that some of us knew, even then, and said nothing, and have spent ten years saying nothing, and I am sorry. I am sorry in the way that changes nothing. But I watched you testify, and I watched you refuse to flinch, and I thought you should hear from at least one of us: what you did was braver than anything we did.*

She read it standing at the window of the boathouse while rain came down the glass in ropes. Braver. She turned the word over and found it didn't fit anything she recognized. She hadn't felt brave at that table. She had felt exposed, skinned, every mile of her carefully logged anonymity stripped away like fouled line peeling off a tank. She had testified because Pell was testifying and Tamás was testifying and there was a girl with a jar of poisoned water, and walking away from them had become, somewhere upstream, a thing she could no longer do.

That was all bravery had ever been, she suspected. Running out of places to hide that still had people in them.

* * *

The consortium broke in the spring, the way ice breaks—not all at once, but in groaning stages, cracks propagating faster than anyone predicted. Licenses revoked. Executives subpoenaed. Two of them fled the country and were photographed getting off planes looking diminished, ordinary, like men discovering that money is not buoyancy. The remediation fund was enormous on paper and would take decades to matter in the water, and everyone understood both halves of that sentence, and somehow it was still news that felt like winning.

Isandro watched most of it on the battered television in the boathouse, or didn't watch it at all. Pell called sometimes from the capital, where she'd been seconded to the new watershed authority—*they gave me a title, Isandro, a title, I'm a cartographer with a title*—and her voice on the line carried a lightness Isandro had never heard in it at base camp, back when Pell spoke like a woman paying down a debt. The debt wasn't paid; debts like hers never are. But it had been acknowledged, entered into the ledger, and apparently that was enough to let a person stand upright.

*Come work with us,* Pell said, more than once. *We're remapping the whole headwaters. Openly. Every contour public, every sample published. You know that river better than anyone alive.*

*I'll think about it,* Isandro said, which was what she said to everything now, and both of them knew it was not a no, and neither of them pushed, because Pell understood better than most that a person who has just lost her invisibility needs time to learn the shape of her own outline.

Tamás didn't call. Tamás sent word through his sister, who sent word through the ferryman, who left a note pinned to the boathouse door in a plastic sleeve against the rain: *Dock's rebuilt. Come see it before the monsoon. Bring nothing. —T*

Bring nothing. She smiled at that. As if she owned anything anymore that couldn't fit in a duffel, and half of that was dive gear.

* * *

She went upstream at the end of the dry season, riding the supply boat as far as the gorge, then walking the last miles on the trail she had once run down in the dark with a wounded cartographer over her shoulder and patrol craft burning floodlights on the water below. The trail looked different in daylight. Everything did. The terraced ruins above the gorge stood silent now, generators gone, the mining works sealed behind concrete and chain-link and a government sign promising monitoring that Isandro suspected would be diligent for exactly as long as the cameras stayed interested. But the river itself—the river was doing what rivers do, which is insist. It ran high and brown-green and loud, carrying its silt and its slow chemistry downstream toward the sea, and where the intake valve had been, divers from the authority had installed a monitoring station, a small yellow instrument house bobbing on pontoons like a patient dog waiting at a dock.

At the village, the wells were capped. New boreholes had been drilled up-slope, past the contaminated aquifer's reach, and a solar pump hummed day and night, and children who had spent a year drinking bottled water trucked in at scandalous expense now drank from taps that ran clear. Clear. Isandro filled a glass at the village tap and held it up to the sun and looked for the shimmer and found only water, plain and honest and slightly warm from the pipe, and she drank it standing in the road like a woman testing whether a dream had ended.

The sickness hadn't vanished. Medicine doesn't work like that. Some of the children would carry what the water gave them for the rest of their lives, and the village had learned to live with that the way villages learn to live with everything—by refusing to let it be the whole story. There was a new schoolhouse going up near the old one, funded by the remediation money, and on its half-finished wall someone had painted a mural: a river running from the mountains to the sea, and along its length, small figures—divers, surveyors, a guide poling a boat, a girl holding a jar. Isandro stood in front of it for a long time. She did not ask who had painted the figures, and no one offered to tell her, and the figure with the dive helmet had her shoulders, or close enough.

* * *

The dock was rebuilt the way Tamás did everything—thoroughly, without flourish, from timber milled locally and fastened to outlast whoever fastened it. He was on it when she arrived, re-caulking a seam, sleeves rolled, and he looked up and said, "You're late," as if she had an appointment, as if either of them had ever once been punctual in their lives.

"The hearings ran long."

"The hearings always run long." He set down his caulking iron and studied her the way he studied water before running it—reading the surface for what moved underneath. "You look tired."

"I am tired."

"You look more than tired." He patted the planking beside him, and she sat, and for a while they said nothing, which was the conversation they were best at. The river went by below them, patient, enormous. Downstream, invisible from here, it passed the second site, the abandoned camp, the checkpoint, mile after mile of the route they had run together when running it might kill them, and Isandro realized she could map it with her eyes closed, every bend, every eddy, every place the current changed its mind.

"Everyone wants to know what happens to you now," Tamás said eventually. "Pell asks. My sister asks. The ferryman asks, and the ferryman doesn't ask anyone anything."

"And what do you tell them?"

"That you'll turn up when you turn up. That rivers don't run on schedules." He picked up the caulking iron, turned it over, set it down again. "What do you tell yourself?"

It was the question she had been avoiding since the last gavel fell, and he asked it the way he asked everything—sideways, while looking at the water, giving her room to lie if she needed to. She almost took the room. Old habit. Ten years of habit.

"I keep counting," she said.

"Counting what?"

"Miles. It's how I—" She stopped. Started again, slower, dredging it up from wherever she kept the true things. "After the inquiry, after I—went under. I stopped counting years. Years are how you measure a life, and I didn't want a life, I wanted distance. So I counted miles. Miles logged, miles traveled, miles between me and the name. Every job, every river, every dive—I logged it like a pilot logs hours. It was the only number that mattered. Get far enough from it and it can't reach you."

Tamás nodded slowly, the way he nodded at rapids he intended to run. "And now?"

"Now I tried to count them this morning. On the boat coming up. All of them, everything since the wreck, everything since the tower." She looked out at the river, at the light lying on it like hammered tin. "I couldn't do it. I don't know where I stopped. Somewhere upstream, somewhere in the black water or the flooded forest or the night we ran the gorge—I lost the count. And I've been sitting here for weeks trying to feel like that's a loss."

"Is it?"

She laughed—a short, surprised sound, the first one in longer than she could date. "No. God help me, no. It's like putting down a pack I forgot I was carrying. I keep reaching for the strap and there's nothing there."

Tamás said nothing for a moment. Then: "My grandfather had a saying. *A river doesn't remember the miles. Only the banks that held it.*" He shrugged, embarrassed by his own wisdom, which was also a family trait. "I used to think it was about the water. Now I think it's about the person floating on it."

Below them, the current slid past, going somewhere without hurrying, and Isandro Coldharbour—who had been a name once, and then a ghost, and then a headline, and was now, improbably, a woman sitting on a friend's dock with caulking on her hands—watched it go and felt the strange double weight settle into her chest at last: grief for the self she had buried so carefully, and gratitude that the grave hadn't held. Both true. Both hers. Belonging, it turned out, was not the opposite of loss. It was what grew in the ground the loss left behind.

"Pell wants me on the headwaters survey," she said. "Openly. Charts published. My name on the title block."

"Will you do it?"

"I told her I'd think about it."

Tamás snorted. "You've been thinking about it for a month."

"I'm a thorough thinker."

"You're a coward with good equipment," he said mildly, and she elbowed him, and he grinned, and the grin did something to the air between them that she decided not to examine too closely. Not yet. Maybe not ever. Maybe that was allowed too—leaving some currents unread.

He reached into the toolbox at his feet and came out with something wrapped in oilcloth, and handed it to her without ceremony. She unwrapped it. Inside was a brass fitting, machined clean, stamped with a serial number—and beneath the number, freshly cut, a small mark she recognized: the survey team's field symbol, the one Pell used on every chart, the one that meant *verified, measured, true*.

"What is it?"

"Intake valve fitting. Off the old station. Authority decommissioned it last week." He looked out at the river. "Thought you should have a piece of it. The part that used to poison the water, turned into a keepsake. Seems like the kind of thing you'd want."

She turned it over in her hands. Heavy. Cold. Getting warmer.

"There's one more thing," Tamás said, and now he wasn't looking at the river, he was looking at her, and his voice had shifted into the register he saved for rapids and confessions. "The authority's first full expedition goes up to Mile Zero next month. To survey the source properly. Set permanent gauges. Pell's leading it." A pause, deliberate as a paddle stroke. "There's a berth open. Dive lead. Pays badly, works in weather, and everyone downstream will know exactly who you are the whole time."

Isandro looked at the fitting in her palm, at the small true mark cut into the metal, and felt the last of the old count slip loose and go downstream, unnumbered, unmourned.

"When do we leave?" she said.
