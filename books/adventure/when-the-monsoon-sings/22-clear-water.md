## 22. Clear Water

The river told the truth first.

That was how Tamás Oyelaran had come to think of it, in the year since everything: rivers were honest in a way governments never learned to be. You could stamp a valve, seal a file, bury a survey under a navy denial—but water carried what it was given, and it gave it back. For eleven years his family's wells had run gray, then iridescent, a faint oily rainbow swirling on the surface of the morning bucket like a bruise that wouldn't heal. His mother had learned to skim it off with a flat wooden paddle and pretend it was nothing. His youngest sister had learned to drink around the taste.

Now he stood at the well at dawn, the same well where he'd first shown Isandro the shimmer, and dipped the test vial down into the dark.

The water came up clear.

Not almost-clear. Not clear-with-an-asterisk. Clear the way water is supposed to be before anyone teaches it otherwise—clear enough that he could see the bottom of the bucket, the pale stones, the small silver fish that had begun returning to the shallows of the river in spring, first one, then dozens, then schools that turned the current into moving light. He held the vial up against the rising sun and watched the light pass through it without bending into any color but its own.

"Still testing?" his mother called from the doorway, half-mocking, half-not. She had stopped skimming the buckets months ago, but she hadn't stopped watching him test. Neither of them had said why. Some vigilance you keep even after the wound closes, the way you keep touching a scar to confirm it doesn't hurt.

"One more year," Tamás said. "Then I'll only test on Sundays."

"You said that last month."

"I did."

She smiled and went inside, and he stayed a moment longer, turning the vial in his fingers. The village had built the testing station itself—three long tables under a corrugated roof by the dock, salvaged instruments from the consortium's abandoned camp, recalibrated by Wren Pell, who had arrived with two crates of equipment and an apology she didn't know how to stop making until the village taught her she didn't need to make it anymore. Every household took its turn. The children had turned it into a game, racing to log their readings in the big ledger Pell kept, their handwriting crowding the margins. *Well 4, clear. Well 9, clear. River at Mile Twelve, clear.* Page after page after page of it, a whole book of a single word repeated until it became a kind of prayer.

Tamás capped the vial, wrote his reading in the ledger—*Dawn, Oyelaran well, clear*—and looked downstream, toward the headwaters, where the mountains stood blue and patient in the early light.

A year ago he had led a stranger up that river for reasons he hadn't confessed until the black water forced them out of him. A year ago his daughter's cough had been the sound that governed his nights. The cough was gone now. The doctor from the relief commission had said, in her careful way, that the worst of it would take years to leave their bodies, that clean water was a beginning and not an ending. He knew that. He carried it every day, the way you carry the memory of a fever—the gratitude and the grief in the same arm.

But this morning the vial was clear, and somewhere upstream a woman who used to count her life in miles was counting something else entirely, and Tamás Oyelaran let himself stand in the sun and feel, for one whole unguarded minute, hopeful.

* * *

Pell found the old map on a Tuesday, which felt right; Tuesdays had always been her day for finding things she wasn't looking for.

It was in the back of the survey team's field cabinet, behind a decade of dust and someone's abandoned lunch tin—a hand-drawn chart of the headwaters, pre-consortium, pre-everything, made by some cartographer forty years dead whose name had been worn away by thumbprints. The lines were beautiful. That was the thing about the old maps, she thought, spreading it across the table in the tower room that had become her study: they were drawn by people who loved the terrain, not people who wanted something out of it. Every contour sang. Every bend of the river had been walked, probably, by boots and patience.

Her own maps hung beside it on the wall—the ones she'd redrawn after the hearings, after the consortium fell, after the government finally, grudgingly, struck the word RESTRICTED from the headwaters and replaced it with a conservation designation nobody quite trusted yet. Her new charts were accurate to within centimeters. Satellites had confirmed every line. And still, whenever she looked at them too long, she saw the other version: the transmission she'd decoded in a sleepless week while her colleagues were missing, the chemical signature dated weeks before any official operation began, the shape of the lie rendered in data. She had been the one who read it and said nothing for six days. Six days. She could recite what happened in those six days like a penance: the patrol boat that came, the guards at the camp, the choice she'd made to stay quiet and alive rather than loud and disappeared.

Wren Pell had spent a year learning that survival was not the same as cowardice, and that forgiveness was not a thing you granted yourself but a thing you built, slowly, out of useful work.

The useful work had turned out to be the best part. She ran the monitoring station at Mile Zero now—the relay tower they'd seized in the monsoon, its antenna still standing, its transmitter upgraded twice since. Every reading from every tributary came through her consoles. She published it all openly, weekly, signed with her full name, disgrace and all, because she had decided that if her name was going to be attached to anything it would be attached to numbers no one could argue with. The ministry hated her. Two universities had offered her chairs. She had declined both, politely, and stayed in the tower where the wind came off the gorge and the river spoke all night in its repaired voice.

There was a knock below—two strikes, then one, the signal they'd invented during the bad weeks—and she climbed the ladder to find Isandro Coldharbour standing in the doorway with rain in her hair and a rolled document tube under one arm.

"You're wet," Pell said.

"It's the monsoon season. Everything's wet." Isandro set the tube on the table. "I brought you something."

"What?"

"A commission." Isandro cracked the seal and unrolled it. "Officially sanctioned. Ministry letterhead and everything—they want the restored headwaters mapped. Properly. Bathymetry, tributaries, the flooded mine shafts, all of it. Open record. My name on the title block."

Pell stared at the document, then at her. "You said yes."

"I said yes." Isandro's mouth did the thing it did instead of smiling—not quite, but close, closer than it used to get. "They asked me to assemble my own team."

"They asked the right person." Pell touched the edge of the chart, careful, the way she touched all documents that mattered. "Isandro. Do you know what this means? Your name. On public record. On a government commission."

"I know exactly what it means." Isandro looked out the tower window, down at the river running bright between its banks. "I spent ten years making sure no one knew my name. Turns out that was just practice for deciding who gets to know it now."

Outside, the rain thickened, drumming the tower roof, and neither of them moved to escape it. They had both learned, separately and then together, that monsoon rain over clean country sounded different than monsoon rain over poisoned country. Lighter. Almost like singing, if you were willing to hear it that way. Pell had once thought that phrase sentimental—*when the monsoon sings*—until she'd stood on this balcony during the first storm after the sealing of the valves and listened to the water come down on a watershed that was finally, actually healing, and understood that some sentences are only sentimental until they're true.

"When do you start?" she asked.

"Next month. After the flood crest." Isandro rolled the commission closed. "I'll need a cartographer who knows every contour of that country better than the satellites do."

Pell felt something open in her chest, wide and warm. "You'll need a guide, too," she said. "Someone who grew up on that water."

"I've already asked him. He said yes before I finished the sentence." Isandro paused at the door, rain-light behind her. "He also said to tell you there's a fish in the river at Mile Nine the size of your ego, and he intends to prove it before we leave."

"That fish does not exist."

"That's what he said you'd say."

Pell laughed—really laughed, the sound surprising her own ears—and Isandro stepped out into the rain without hurrying, because there was nowhere left she needed to hide from, and walked down toward the dock where the whole village seemed to be waiting for her, and Pell stood in the tower doorway a long time, watching her go, holding the commission like a lantern.

* * *

Isandro had always counted her life in miles logged. Ten miles deep, nineteen miles upriver, nine miles down into the black—it had been a way of measuring a life she didn't want to claim, distance instead of duration, movement instead of belonging. Miles couldn't be taken from you. Miles didn't ask your name.

She stood on Tamás's repaired dock in the late afternoon, the commission tube dry under her arm despite the drizzle, and watched the village come down to the water the way it did every evening now: children swimming in the shallows where children hadn't swum in eleven years, women washing vegetables straight from the river without boiling them first, an old man casting a net with the unhurried grace of someone who expected the net to come back full. The dock itself was new timber over old pilings, rebuilt by forty pairs of hands in a single week the summer before, and when she'd helped drive the last plank someone had carved her initials into it without asking, and she had pretended not to see, and had come back to touch them so many times the wood had gone smooth.

A year ago, anonymity had been the only thing she owned. She had guarded it like ballast—jettison everything else, keep yourself weightless and unseen, and nothing can sink you. Then a crate that breathed, a camp with no ghosts, a guide who lied well, a dive into a flooded shaft where government stamps gleamed on every valve, and she had learned the lesson the hard way, the way all her real lessons came: invisibility was just another word for drowning, slower, in air.

Now everyone knew her name. It had been in the broadcasts, the hearings, the headlines—*Isandro Coldharbour, salvage diver, witness*—spoken aloud in rooms she'd once paid good money to stay out of. Her old life was gone, unrecoverable, the contacts burned, the safe harbors closed. Some nights that still woke her at three a.m., heart hammering, reaching for a cover story that no longer existed.

And every morning, this: the dock. The ledger of clear readings. Tamás's sister running past with a test vial held aloft like a trophy, shouting the number before she'd even reached the station. Pell's weekly bulletins pinned to the village board, next to the school schedule and a child's drawing of the relay tower with a sun over it drawn in yellow crayon. People who said her name like it belonged here, like it had always belonged here, like a name was not a debt but a door.

"Thinking again?" Tamás dropped onto the dock beside her, fishing line already coiled, the eternal optimist. "Dangerous habit. I'd stick to diving."

"I'm taking the commission," she said. "Upstream. All of it. Mile Zero to the source."

"I heard. Pell can't keep a secret worth anything." He baited his hook, unhurried. "You know what's funny? A year ago I hired you because I needed a diver. Now I can't imagine the river without you in it. Like the water got used to you."

"The water and I have an arrangement."

"Does the arrangement include dinner? My mother's cooking. She asked specifically."

Isandro looked out at the river one more time—the clear water, the returning fish, the far blue line of the headwaters where her next miles waited, mapped this time in daylight, on the record, witnessed. Somewhere in the last year she had stopped counting. She couldn't say when. Maybe in the black water, when Tamás broke protocol and followed her down. Maybe on this dock, the day the initials went smooth. Maybe it didn't matter when. What mattered was that the ledger of her life had quietly changed units, and no one had told her, and she hadn't noticed until just now, sitting in the rain-smell and the frying-fish smell and the sound of children who would never know what this water used to carry.

"Yeah," she said, standing, offering him a hand up. "The arrangement includes dinner."

They walked up from the dock together, and behind them the river went on singing, all the way to the sea.
