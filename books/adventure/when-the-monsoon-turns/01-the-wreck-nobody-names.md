## 1. The Wreck Nobody Names

The river did not want her there. Theo Oyelaran felt it in the way the current leaned against her shoulders like a hand pushing her back toward the sea, in the way the silt hung thick as breath, in the way the light died three meters down and left her working by torch in a brown so complete it seemed less like water than like memory. She had been diving this delta for eleven months now, and she had learned its moods the way a person learns a landlord's: carefully, without affection, always aware that the arrangement could end.

The wreck lay on her side in thirty feet of tea-colored water, a coastal freighter called nothing anymore because her name had been ground off the hull long before she went down—or perhaps she had never carried one at all. The navy charts marked the channel as restricted. The navy patrol boats ran it twice a day, their wakes slapping the mangroves like slow applause for an audience of roots. And the navy, as far as anyone official was concerned, had no interest in what rested beneath their own exclusion line.

That disinterest was worth money to certain people, and those people were worth money to Theo, or rather she owed them money, which amounted to the same thing in the economy of the delta. Her debt sat with a salvage broker named Corvus Dain, who kept his office on a barge moored behind the fish market and whose smile Theo had never once seen reach his eyes. Fourteen thousand, plus interest calculated in a currency Dain invented himself—days. Every day she didn't pay, the number grew. Every day she dove, she bought another day. It was not freedom. It was a leash measured out one link at a time.

She checked her gauge. Forty minutes of air. Enough.

The cargo hold opened like a mouth. Theo slipped through the torn plating where the explosion had peeled the steel back—the freighter hadn't sunk; she'd been *sunk*, that much was obvious even to a diver who asked no questions—and descended into a cathedral of stacked crates, their wood gone soft as bread after decades underwater. Her torch beam moved across them in slow arcs. Cataloging was the work. Not recovering, not yet. Dain wanted a full inventory first, because Dain never spent a coin before he knew exactly how many coins existed.

*Crates, forty-one visible, dimensions standard shipping.* She scratched notes onto the waterproof slate strapped to her wrist, the pencil stub worn to a nub. *Contents unknown. Wood degraded. Markings illegible except—*

Except one. Low in the stack, half-buried in silt that the flood season had laid down like fresh snow, a crate bore stenciled letters that the water had failed to eat. She brushed the silt away with the flat of her glove, gently, the way you'd clear dirt from a face.

*VESSANIA — PORT AUTHORITY — DO NOT BREAK SEAL.*

Theo went still in the water. Even her bubbles seemed to hesitate.

She knew the word from nowhere and everywhere—from the margins of old atlases sold in the market stalls, from a song the bar girls sang in Port Calloway about a country that used to be across the strait before the maps were redrawn and the history books rewritten and the whole thing declared *a regrettable administrative matter*. Thirty years gone. A nation erased so thoroughly that saying its name aloud in certain company was either a joke or a confession.

A crate stamped with a dead country's seal, sitting in the hold of a ship the navy pretended not to own, in waters the navy forbade everyone else to enter.

She wrote it down anyway. That was the job. Curiosity was a luxury item, and Theo's budget had no room for luxuries. But her hand hesitated over the slate, and she noticed herself memorizing the crate's position in the stack—third row, port side, wedged under a collapsed beam—as if some part of her already suspected she'd need to find it again in the dark.

Above her, faint as a heartbeat through thirty feet of brown water, came the sound she had trained herself to hear the way other divers trained themselves to hear their regulators fail: engines. Diesel, twin screws, moving fast against the current.

Patrol boats didn't run this stretch mid-morning. The schedule was morning and dusk; she'd charted it across four months with the patience of a woman mapping a predator's territory. This was neither. This was a boat coming here, now, directly, its engine note tightening as it slowed.

Someone had told them where to look.

Theo killed her torch. In the sudden blindness she felt the wreck close around her—the soft rot of the crates, the cold seeping through her wetsuit, the vast indifference of the river pressing down—and she made herself move by memory instead of sight, hand over hand along the collapsed beam, out through the torn plating, up the hull's flank toward the light that wasn't light, just a marginally brighter brown. Her slate she tucked against her chest. Her knife stayed where it was; knives solved nothing against a patrol boat.

She surfaced inside the wreck's shadowed lee, between hull and mangrove bank, where the tangle of roots threw broken cover across the water. Fifty meters out, the patrol boat idled, gray paint streaked with rust, a man at the bow sweeping binoculars along the tree line. Theo breathed shallow. Breath made ripples. Ripples made witnesses.

The binoculars passed over her once, twice, and moved on. The boat held position for six minutes—she counted, because counting was what she did instead of panicking—then turned and ground away upriver, its wake rolling into the roots and slapping against the hull like something knocking politely at a door.

Theo floated in the shadow and let her heart rate come down by tens. Ten beats. Twenty. The river resettled around her, silt drifting, a kingfisher somewhere upstream making its rattling complaint. To anything else alive here, she was weather. She was nothing. That was the entire architecture of her life, built plank by plank over five years since she'd come downriver owing money and running from something older than money: be nothing, take nothing, leave nothing, owe no one.

It was a good design. It had one flaw, which was that a person could live inside it for years and then notice, on an ordinary Tuesday, floating in brown water next to a dead ship, that the design had no room in it for being *seen*—and that some starved part of her had begun to stand at the window of her own invisibility, looking out.

She swam for her skiff, beached and hidden two hundred meters downstream under a net of cut palm fronds. The paddle back to the salvage camp took twenty minutes against the current, and she used every one of them to rehearse what she would say and, more importantly, what she would not say. She would report the crates. She would not mention Vessania. She would not mention the patrol boat. She would not mention that the patrol boat's timing meant someone else knew about this wreck—someone watching her, someone who had watched her long enough to learn her schedule better than she'd thought possible.

Invisibility, she understood now, was not the same as safety. It was just danger with the lights turned off.

* * *

Dain's barge smelled of diesel, dried fish, and other people's desperation. He received her in the aft cabin, behind a desk bolted to the deck, beneath a mounted marlin that had seen better decades. He listened to her inventory with his eyes half-closed, like a man savoring a meal he intended to eat slowly.

"Forty-one crates," he repeated. "Standard shipping. Contents unknown."

"Wood's too far gone to open cleanly down there. I'd need to bring them up."

"And I'd need to know what's in them before I spend the air and the rigging." He tapped the desk with one finger. "You said markings on one."

"I said the markings were illegible."

"You said *except*." His eyes opened all the way, and they were the color of river water, flat and patient and bottomless. "You're a careful woman, Oyelaran. Careful women don't say *except* unless the exception matters."

Theo held his gaze because looking away was a tax she couldn't afford. "It's a port authority stamp. Half-rotted. Could be any port."

"Could be." He smiled, and the smile had teeth but no warmth, like a warning painted on a wall. "Here's what's going to happen. You'll bring up three crates. I'll choose which three. You'll do it tomorrow, dawn, before the first patrol. And you'll do it clean, because—" he paused, and the pause had weight, the way a dropped anchor has weight, "—I'm told the navy ran an unscheduled pass today. Mid-morning. Over my wreck."

*My wreck,* he'd said, and Theo filed the ownership claim away with everything else she'd learned about Dain: that he lent money to divers because drowned things couldn't testify; that his information network was better than the harbor office's; that he had known about the patrol run before she had, which meant he had sources inside it, or above it, or both.

"Unscheduled runs happen," she said.

"They do," Dain agreed pleasantly. "And scheduled divers get found. You've been invisible for five years, Oyelaran. Impressive record. It would be a shame if the river stopped cooperating." He slid a chit across the desk—her debt figure, recalculated, larger by the interest of one more day. "Three crates. Dawn. Don't disappoint me, and don't make me curious about you. Men stop protecting what they're curious about."

Theo took the chit and left without answering, because there was no answer that improved her position. On the gangplank down to the mud flats, she paused and looked back at the delta spread out around her: the braided channels silvering in the late light, the fishing boats coming home with their lanterns lit, the mangroves standing in the shallows like a crowd that had gathered to watch something and refused to say what.

Eleven months she'd worked these waters, and she had never once thought of them as hers. The river was an employer. It paid in days and docked her wages in fear. You didn't love an employer. You didn't grieve one when the contract ended. You kept your head down, your ledger straight, and your name out of every mouth.

But tonight, for the first time, the arrangement felt less like employment than like surveillance. Someone was watching her work. Someone had sent a boat to check on her. Someone—Dain, the navy, whoever stood behind them both in whatever arrangement men like them sealed with handshakes and never wrote down—had decided that the woman diving the unnamed wreck was worth keeping an eye on.

And underneath the silt, third row, port side, wedged under a collapsed beam, a crate waited with a dead nation's seal on its face, holding whatever it held, patient as only sunken things are patient.

Tomorrow at dawn she would bring up three crates. Dain would choose which.

She already knew which one she'd choose.
