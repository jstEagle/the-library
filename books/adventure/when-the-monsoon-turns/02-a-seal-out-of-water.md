## 2. A Seal Out of Water

Dawn on the delta came the color of old tin, and Theo Oyelaran was in the water before it finished arriving.

She liked this hour best—the hour when the river belonged to no one. The patrol boats wouldn't run their first circuit until the mist burned off, and the fishing families were still shuttered behind their mosquito nets, and even the birds held their commentary. Just her, the brown water, and the wreck below, breathing its slow rust-rot breath through the hull she'd come to know like a face.

She spat into the river—apology and permission, an old diver's habit her father taught her before he taught her anything else—and went down.

* * *

The *Vessane Star* sat in forty feet of tea-colored water, listing hard to port, her superstructure collapsed into a tangle that from above looked like a drowned cathedral. Theo had been diving her for eleven days now. Eleven days of cataloging cargo for Dain Voss's ledger: bales of rotted cloth, drums of congealed palm oil, brass fittings worth stripping, copper wire worth more. Ordinary salvage. Illegal salvage, technically—the navy had restricted the site after the sinking, thirty years back—but the navy's restrictions had a way of dissolving when nobody important was looking, and Theo had made a career out of being nobody important.

That was the whole trick of her life, if she was honest about it. Invisibility as a trade skill. She moved through the world the way silt moved through water: present everywhere, noticed nowhere. No debts on paper except the one to Voss. No name on any registry except forged ones. No home port, because a home port could be found.

The river didn't care who she was. That was why she loved it—or whatever passed for love in her, some thin cold thing that lived where other people kept their hearts.

She worked the third row, port side, fins stirring up ghosts. Her lamp cut a cone through the murk, and inside the cone the wreck revealed itself in pieces, always in pieces: a shoe. A child's sandal, small enough to fit in her palm, wedged in a scupper. A lacquered box gone soft with decades. She catalogued what was valuable and left what wasn't, and she did not think about the sandal, because thinking about the sandal meant thinking about whose foot had worn it, and whose foot meant the passenger manifest, and the manifest meant the question everyone in the delta asked in whispers and then stopped asking: how does a ferry sink in calm water, in daylight, with a navy cutter anchored two miles off?

Theo had learned young that questions like that got divers hurt. She dove, she surfaced, she sold, she moved on. The river paid her; she didn't ask it about its past.

But today the crate was waiting for her, exactly where she'd left it yesterday, wedged under the collapsed beam, and today she had brought the pry bar.

* * *

It took her twenty minutes to work it free without bringing the beam down on herself. Twenty minutes of controlled breathing, of leverage and patience, of the beam groaning overhead like something waking. When the crate finally slid loose and rolled, it came up a bloom of silt that turned her lamp-beam to milk, and she hung there in the whiteout, weightless, blind, listening to her own heartbeat count seconds until the water cleared.

The crate was smaller than the others. Iron-banded, not nailed. And the seal—

She'd seen it yesterday at the edge of her light and told herself she'd imagined it. She hadn't. Stamped deep into the lid, under a skin of river muck she scrubbed away with her thumb, was a crest: a tower over crossed keys, ringed by a motto in a script she couldn't read but recognized anyway, the way you recognize a face from a photograph you saw once as a child.

Vessania.

There was no such place. There hadn't been for thirty years—not since the border treaties redrew the coast and the maps were reprinted and every atlas, every chart, every schoolbook quietly lost a nation between one edition and the next. Theo had grown up hearing about Vessania the way you hear about a ghost: the refugee camps that became permanent towns, the ferries that ran the delta carrying people with nowhere written on their papers, the night the last of them went into the water.

Her father used to say the maps lied by omission. He said it the way he said most things—drunk, bitter, staring at the horizon like it owed him money. Then he went out to sea one November and never came back, and Theo stopped saying anything at all.

She put her palm flat against the seal. Cold iron, cold water, and underneath both, something that felt almost like heat—the heat of a thing that mattered, which is the most dangerous cargo there is.

Bring it up, said the part of her that was always calculating. It's heavy metal at minimum. Iron bands, brass fittings. Voss will pay for the weight alone.

Leave it, said the older part, the part that had kept her alive. Some crates are ballast. Some things are sunk on purpose.

She was still deciding when she heard the engines.

* * *

Sound travels strangely underwater. By the time the engine noise reached her at depth it had lost its edges, become a vibration in her teeth, in the bones of her skull, in the water itself—a pressure that meant boat, and near, and coming closer.

Theo killed her lamp and went still.

Patrol boats ran a schedule. She knew the schedule the way she knew her own breathing: first circuit at 0740, second at 1100, nothing midday because the crews took their rice and their shade like anyone else. She checked her dive watch. 0612. An hour and a half early. Not a scheduled pass. Not weather—there was no wind yet, the monsoon still gathering itself somewhere out over the sea like a debt being counted.

The engine sound slowed. Throttled down. Held.

Directly above her.

In forty feet of opaque water, under a wreck the navy had officially closed, holding a crate stamped with the seal of a country that had been erased—Theo hung motionless and felt the particular cold that has nothing to do with temperature. The cold of being watched.

Because that was the thing her mind had already assembled while the rest of her stayed frozen: patrol boats don't find wrecks by accident. Patrol boats don't throttle down over open water unless they know there's something under them worth stopping for. Someone had marked this spot. Someone had been watching the *Vessane Star*, watching for divers, and this morning someone had watched *her* go down.

Eleven days. Eleven days of clean dives, clean exits, no wake, no witnesses. Had they watched all along? Or had yesterday been different—had the crate, the seal, been the line she crossed?

Above her, through the hull, she heard the boat's engine drop to idle, and then voices, muffled to nonsense by the water, and then a new sound that made her blood go thin: the clank and rattle of a chain paying out. An anchor. They weren't passing. They were staying.

Theo did the arithmetic fast, the way drowning teaches you to do arithmetic. Air check: a quarter tank, maybe less after the pry work. Surface interval: none. Options: swim up into the middle of a naval boarding party, or stay down and let the tank decide for her, or move.

She chose move.

She let the crate sink back into the silt—gently, gently, easing it into the murk with both hands like lowering a sleeping child, though her hands wanted to shake—and dragged the collapsed beam's shadow across the space where it had lain. Then she swam for the seaward side of the wreck, low along the hull, following the route she'd mapped in her head on day one for exactly this kind of morning. Through the gap in the plating where the torpedo—or whatever it had been; the navy said boiler explosion, the navy said a lot of things—had torn the *Star* open. Out over the sand shelf. Down-current, away from the anchored boat, letting the delta's slow pull carry her while she finned lazy and shallow, staying under as long as her air allowed, surfacing finally behind a screen of nipa palms four hundred meters off, where the channel narrowed and no boat bigger than a skiff could follow.

She broke the surface just enough to breathe, regulator out, mask pushed up, and looked back.

The patrol boat sat over the wreck like a gull over a dead fish. Grey hull, no name visible at this distance, a figure at the rail scanning the water with binoculars. Even from here Theo could read the posture: not routine. Searching. The binoculars swept the shoreline once, twice, and Theo sank to her chin among the palm roots and breathed through her mouth and thought about nothing at all, because thinking was a luxury for later.

After a long time—twenty minutes, thirty—the anchor came up. The engine woke. The boat swung in a slow circle, one last look, and headed upriver toward the navy station at Calloway Reach.

Not toward the open sea. Toward the station. Toward telephones.

* * *

Theo paddled her skiff back to the stilt dock at Sengkar with her arms doing the work and her mind doing everything else. The monsoon light was full now, flat and grey, and the river had woken around her—bathers, laundry boats, a woman slapping wet cloth against a stone with a rhythm like a slow heart. Normal life. The life that pretended, every single day, that the water hadn't swallowed a nation.

She tied off, stripped her gear, and made herself go through the ritual: rinse, hang, log the dive in her head. Three crates planned. One recovered so far—cloth, worthless. Two more down there, plus the thing under the beam.

Plus the thing under the beam.

Here was what she knew, laid out clean as tools on a bench: the navy had come early, once, the morning after she'd touched the sealed crate. Coincidence was possible. Theo had built her whole survival on assuming coincidence was possible, because the alternative—that the world was paying attention to her specifically—was a kind of vanity she couldn't afford.

But her father had another saying, from his sober hours, which were rare and therefore true: *the river doesn't hide things, it holds them. Holding isn't hiding. Everything held comes up eventually.*

Someone had sent that boat. Someone knew a diver was working the *Star*. And whoever it was would come to the docks next, because that was the next logical place to look, and Sengkar had exactly one dock, and Theo had exactly one skiff tied to it, painted the same faded green as forty other skiffs but hers nonetheless, hers, traceable, *visible*—

For eleven years she had been silt. This morning, in forty feet of brown water, she had become a shape on a sonar screen.

She crouched at the edge of the dock, splashed water on her face, and let herself feel it fully for three seconds: the dread, cold and total, the certainty that the careful architecture of her invisible life had developed a crack she couldn't see the bottom of.

Then she stood up, because feeling it longer than three seconds was how people froze, and frozen people got caught.

Plan. Simple plan. Dive again tomorrow? No. Never again on that wreck—let the crate keep its secrets, let Voss scream, eat the penalty, disappear upriver tonight before anyone connected the green skiff to the diver on the sonar. She'd done it before. She could do it again. She always did it again.

She was reaching for the mooring line when she saw the woman.

Standing at the far end of the dock, out of the shade, where anyone standing would be either stupid or deliberate. Mid-thirties, travel-worn, a canvas map case slung across her body like a bandolier, and eyes that were doing what Theo's own eyes did—cataloguing, measuring, deciding. The woman looked at the skiff. At Theo. At the dive gear dripping off the rail.

Then she reached into her jacket, slowly, and Theo's hand found the knife strapped under her arm.

"You're the one diving the *Star*," the woman said. Not a question. "I need to talk to you about what's in that crate."

The river slapped against the pilings. Somewhere behind her, faint but unmistakable, an engine was coming down the channel toward Sengkar—too steady, too purposeful, and moving much too fast for any fishing boat.

Theo kept her hand on the knife and did not answer, and understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water closing over her head, that the neutral territory she had lived in for eleven years had just stopped existing.
