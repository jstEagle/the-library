## 3. The Cartographer's Debt

The rain had not yet come, but the air had already learned its weight. Amara Sen felt it pressing on her shoulders as she crossed the fish market toward the dock, her satchel heavy with maps she no longer trusted and a letter she had read so many times the creases had gone soft as cloth.

She had been awake since three, sitting at her drafting table with her brother's correspondence spread out like an autopsy. Dara's letters from the *Vessanian Star* — that was what he'd called the ship in his last one, though every official record named it something else, some bland coastal freighter with a registry number and no history. He had written about the crew, about the cook who sang Vessanian hymns off-key, about the captain who kept a crest painted inside his sea chest where no port inspector would see it. A seal, Dara wrote. A double tide and a broken crown. The mark of a country that used to be.

Amara had spent six years pretending those letters were grief talking, a drowning man's nostalgia for a homeland that existed only in their mother's stories. She had catalogued them, dated them, filed them in acid-free sleeves like a good archivist of things that didn't matter. And then yesterday, in the wet grey light of the fish market, she had watched a salvage diver haul a canvas bundle up from the restricted wreck, and she had seen — just for a moment, before the tarp closed over it — the edge of a brass fitting stamped with a double tide and a broken crown.

She had not slept since.

The dock smelled of diesel and brine and the sweet rot of fish heads thrown back to the gulls. The diver was there, coiling rope with the economy of someone who never wasted a motion, and Amara took the chance to study her properly. Theo Oyelaran. The name had cost her two days of asking around, and most people who knew it pretended they didn't. A woman built like a cable, dark-skinned, hair cropped close against the water's chill, moving through the dock's chaos as if she were the only solid thing in it. She did not greet anyone. No one greeted her. She worked alone, ate alone, and when a patrol boat slid past the harbor mouth she went very still, the way small animals go still.

Amara had rehearsed this conversation on the walk over. She had planned to be reasonable, professional, offering fair compensation for salvage rights. But watching the woman coil that rope — watching her check the horizon twice in ten seconds without seeming to look at all — the rehearsal fell apart, and what came out instead was raw and unarchived.

"You found a chest down there."

Theo didn't startle. That was the first thing Amara noticed. She simply finished the coil, set it down, and turned with the unhurried attention of someone who had survived by never being rushed into anything. Her eyes were the color of river water after a storm — brown shot through with green, unreadable.

"Careful," Theo said. Her voice was low, roughened by years of breathing compressed air. "People who talk about cargo on open docks tend to stop talking altogether."

"I'm not talking about cargo." Amara stepped closer, close enough to smell the salt dried on the woman's jacket. "I'm talking about a seal. Double tide, broken crown. Vessania."

Something moved behind the diver's eyes — not recognition exactly, but the flinch of a person who has heard a name they hoped was buried. "Never heard of it."

"It was erased from the maps thirty years ago," Amara said. "You'd have to be my age or older to remember it, or you'd have to have seen it recently. Stamped in brass. On a chest you pulled out of a wreck that the navy tells everyone sank in a storm."

The gulls screamed over the fish stalls. Somewhere behind them a generator coughed to life. Theo picked up her rope coil again, deliberately, and Amara understood it for what it was: a door closing.

"My brother crewed on that ship," she said, quieter now. "Dara Sen. Cartographer's apprentice, second contract, nineteen years old. His letters describe that crest. They describe the crew singing Vessanian songs below decks. They describe a passenger manifest that didn't match the cargo manifest, and a captain who was afraid of something more than weather." Her voice caught, and she hated it, and she pushed through anyway. "Six years ago the navy told me he drowned in a storm. There was no storm. I've checked the meteorological records. I've checked everything. The only thing I couldn't check was the wreck itself, because your navy forbids diving on it, which means the only person who knows what's down there is standing in front of me refusing to look me in the eye."

Theo looked her in the eye then. It was worse than being ignored.

"I pull cargo," she said. "I don't pull history. What's in the chest is worth money to certain buyers, and questions are worth nothing to anyone. You want my advice? Go home. Burn the letters if you have to. Some wrecks stay sunk for a reason."

"Some wrecks stay sunk because men put them there," Amara said. "My brother is one of the reasons."

For a long moment neither of them spoke. The tide sucked at the pilings beneath their feet. Then Theo sighed — a small, defeated sound, quickly mastered — and said, "How much?"

"What?"

"How much are you offering? You came here to buy it. Everyone comes to buy. Name a number and I'll tell you it isn't enough, and we can both stop pretending this is about anything else."

Amara reached into her satchel and drew out an envelope — her savings, three years of freelance survey work, the money she'd been keeping for a proper expedition north. She held it out. "Four thousand. That's everything I have that isn't nailed down."

Theo didn't take it. She looked at the envelope the way a starving man looks at food he's decided not to eat, and Amara realized, with a strange lurch, that she needed the money — badly, visibly, in the frayed cuffs and the mended dive bag and the boat behind her listing at its mooring with a patch on the hull that hadn't been done properly. Whatever debt drove this woman, it was not a small one.

"Keep it," Theo said finally. "Buy yourself a new map. Because if you give me four thousand for that chest, you'll spend the rest of your life wondering what else was in it, and wondering is how people like us end up at the bottom of rivers."

"People like us," Amara repeated. "And what are we?"

"Drowned," Theo said simply, "or waiting to be."

She shouldered her dive bag and turned away, and Amara stood there holding an envelope full of useless money, feeling the conversation slip through her fingers like the river itself. It was only as Theo reached the head of the dock that Amara called out — not the offer she'd rehearsed, but the truth underneath it:

"She had a hundred and forty passengers, Theo. Refugees. My brother's letters say so. Somebody sank a ship full of people and called it weather, and you're going to sell whatever proves it to the highest bidder and dive somewhere else tomorrow like the water doesn't remember."

Theo stopped. She did not turn around. For three long breaths she stood at the edge of the dock with the grey water lapping below her, and Amara thought — absurdly, desperately — that she might answer.

Then a voice came from behind the fuel shed, cheerful and oiled as bilge: "Well. This is a conversation I'm glad I didn't miss."

Amara knew that voice. Half the delta knew it. Odell Vassek — salvage broker, lender of last resort, collector of debts both financial and otherwise — emerged into the grey morning light wearing a linen suit too clean for the dock and a smile that had never once reached his eyes. Two of his men drifted behind him with the loose-limbed patience of people paid to wait.

"Theo Oyelaran," Vassek said warmly. "And a friend. How unusual. You don't have friends, Theo. It's one of the things I value about our arrangement." His gaze slid to Amara, to the envelope in her hand, and sharpened. "And you'd be the Sen girl. The cartographer. Asking questions about the *Star*, aren't you? Everyone always asks about the *Star* eventually. It's practically a tradition."

"We were just leaving," Theo said flatly.

"Oh, I don't think so." Vassek strolled closer, hands clasped behind his back, admiring the water as if he owned it — and perhaps, Amara thought, in every way that mattered, he did. "You see, I make it my business to know what my divers bring up. And when my diver brings up a Vessanian state chest off a navy-restricted wreck, and then meets with the sister of a crewman who's been writing letters to naval archives for six years — well. That's not salvage anymore. That's leverage." He smiled at Amara. "Yours is worth more than hers, actually. Grief makes people reckless. Reckless people pay anything."

"You sold me out," Theo said. There was no surprise in it. That was the worst part, Amara thought. The woman had expected this.

"I prefer 'monetized mutual exposure.'" Vassek's men had drifted, casually, to either end of the dock. "Here's the situation. Captain Reyes at the naval station has been asking after a chest with a certain crest. Certain other parties — less official, better armed — have also expressed interest. I intend to conduct a small auction. Unless—" he paused delicately— "the chest happens to disappear. Go downriver, say. Into the flood channels where no auction can reach it. In which case both of you become problems instead of assets, and I solve problems differently."

The rain chose that moment to arrive — not the monsoon yet, just a first thin drizzle, silvering the dock, beading on the envelope in Amara's hand. She watched Theo calculate. She could see it happening behind those storm-colored eyes: the exits, the odds, the price of each path. A woman who treated the river as an employer, weighing whether to quit.

Then Theo moved — fast, faster than Amara had guessed she could — grabbing Amara's arm and pulling her backward toward the listing boat at the mooring, the one with the bad patch on the hull.

"Get aboard," Theo said. "Both our problems just got on the same boat."

"That wasn't the deal," Amara managed, stumbling after her. "I offered you money. You refused."

"Money buys chests." Theo was already hauling up the mooring line, the drizzle running off her close-cropped hair, her jaw set like something poured rather than grown. "What I need now is a witness who can read documents and draw charts, and a reason bigger than a debt. Congratulations. You're both."

Behind them, Vassek's voice carried easily over the water, still pleasant, still smiling: "Downriver, then! Do give my regards to the flood channels!"

The engine coughed, caught, coughed again. Amara gripped the rail of a stranger's leaking boat and watched the dock — her dock, her town, the whole careful architecture of her six-year search — slide away into the rain, and understood that she had just made a choice she could never chart her way back from.

Ahead, the river widened toward the delta, grey and patient, already swelling with the coming monsoon.

It was waiting for them.
