## 4. Running Before the Rain

The dock was burning before Theo got the engine to turn over.

She heard it first as a sound she couldn't name—a wet, greedy crackling, like something enormous chewing—and then the light came, orange and wrong against the grey-green water, throwing the pilings into silhouette. Smoke rolled low along the boardwalk, thick with tar and burning rope and something sweeter underneath that she refused to identify. The fuel shed. Someone had put a torch to the fuel shed.

"Go," Amara said from the bow, both hands white-knuckled on the chest. "Theo. *Go.*"

"I'm going." The *Vireo*'s engine caught on the third try, coughed, caught again—an old two-stroke diesel that had been dying since before Theo bought her, and which had apparently decided tonight it wanted to live. The boat lurched away from the dock with all the grace of a drunk leaving a bar fight. Behind them, flames climbed the shed's corrugated walls and licked out across the planking in bright fingers.

Figures moved through the smoke. Three of them, maybe four, unhurried, the way men are unhurried when they know the job is done. One of them raised something long and dark, and Theo didn't wait to see whether it was a rifle or a flare gun. She hauled the tiller hard over and put the burning dock between them and whatever came next.

The first shot hit the water ten feet astern. The second she never heard—the squall arrived then, all at once, the way monsoon weather does, not arriving so much as *landing*, a wall of rain moving down the channel like a curtain being dragged across the world. It hit the *Vireo* broadside and the river went white with it, and the shot, if there was one, was swallowed whole.

Then there was only rain, and the engine's ragged heartbeat, and Amara's voice cutting through it: "Are we—did we—are they following?"

"Not yet." Theo peered aft until her eyes stung. The glow of the fire had already dissolved into a smudge, a bruise of orange behind the rain. "Give them time. They'll come."

"Why would they burn the dock? There were people living on that dock."

"There were people who saw us load this." She nodded at the chest without looking at it. It sat between them in the bilge water like a third passenger, dark wood gone soft with thirty years of river, the brass seal on its lid still bright where the silt hadn't reached. Vessania's crest: a river forked around a mountain, the motto worn nearly illegible. *Ex undis veritas.* From the waves, truth. Somebody had chosen that motto for a country that no longer existed, and Theo thought there was a bitter joke in it now that nobody would ever get to laugh at.

Amara had wrapped her arms around the chest when the shooting started, the way you'd shield a child. She hadn't let go since.

"The leak's worse," she said.

"I know."

"You knew before I said it?"

"I always know." Theo wiped rain from her face with the back of her wrist. It made no difference; the sky simply poured more. "Bilge pump's running. We'll make the old survey station before she gives out, if the engine holds and the channel hasn't shifted."

"And if it has?"

"Then we'll find out together how well you swim."

Amara didn't laugh. Theo hadn't expected her to. The woman had spent the last hour watching men she'd probably bought cigarettes from try to kill them, and grief does strange arithmetic—it subtracts humor first.

* * *

They ran south down the main channel for twenty minutes, visibility shrinking to nothing, the banks erased, the whole delta become a single grey room with no walls. Theo steered by memory and by the feel of the current against the hull, reading the water the way her father had taught her to read it—not looking *at* the surface but *through* it, feeling where the channel deepened, where a sandbar waited just under the skin of the river like a bone about to break through.

Rain hammered the deck. Rain found every gap in the cabin canvas and came through in cold threads down the back of her neck. Rain filled the bilge faster than the little pump could argue, and twice Theo had to ease off the throttle while Amara bailed with an empty rice tin, working in a rhythm that was almost, absurdly, domestic—scoop, throw, scoop, throw—while the world outside tried to drown them.

"He said you'd run," Amara shouted over the noise, somewhere past the half-hour mark.

"Who did?"

"The broker. Okafor. He told me you were reliable but that you'd run if it went bad. That everyone runs eventually. He said it like he was proud of knowing that about people."

Theo kept her eyes on the water. "Okafor knows a lot of things he shouldn't."

"Did you sell me out to him? When I came to your boat?"

The question landed clean, no anger in it, just the flat curiosity of someone who needed the answer more than she needed comfort. Theo respected that. She'd have respected it more yesterday.

"No. But I should have known he was watching. Nobody finds a wreck that fast unless they're being led to it. I led them to it. I just didn't know I was holding the leash."

"And now?"

"Now they want the chest, and they've stopped caring whether I'm attached to it."

Lightning cracked the sky open ahead of them, and in that single white instant Theo saw the channel bend, saw the drowned mangroves standing in rows like a congregation, saw—far off, upstream, a smudge of running lights where no lights should be. A boat. Big enough to be navy, fast enough not to be.

She killed the running lamps and turned downriver into the dark.

* * *

They sheltered in the lee of a collapsed fish weir two hours later, engine idling low, rain still coming down but gentler now, the squall spending itself against the land. The weir was an old thing, bamboo poles leaning out of the water at drunken angles, and it broke the wind enough that Theo dared to open the cabin and look at what they'd stolen—or rescued, depending on which story you believed, and Theo was beginning to suspect the stories mattered more than she'd ever let herself think.

Amara had a knife. Of course she did; cartographers traveled alone too often not to carry one. She worked it around the chest's seal with hands that shook only a little, and when the hasp finally gave—a sound like a knuckle cracking, loud in the quiet—the smell that rose out of it was river-mud and old paper and something chemical, oilcloth preservative, someone having packed these documents to survive exactly this kind of neglect.

Inside: oilcloth bundles. Dozens of them. And beneath them, wrapped separately, a ledger bound in green leather gone black with mold.

Amara lifted the ledger as if it might bite. Unwrapped the nearest bundle with the care of a woman handling a reliquary. Inside were papers—typed pages, mimeographed pages, a few handwritten ones in a careful bureaucratic hand—and a photograph, and Theo watched Amara's face as she read, watched the color leave it in stages, like a tide going out.

"What is it?" Theo asked, though some part of her already knew. You didn't seal things in a chest with a dead nation's crest unless the contents were worth killing a country over.

Amara didn't answer. She read. Her lips moved. Then she turned the top page toward Theo, and her voice, when it came, was very steady, which is its own kind of terrible.

"It's a manifest. Passenger manifest. The *Coromandel Star*, out of Vessania, eleven years before it sank here." Her finger traced a column of names. "Four hundred and six souls listed. Refugees. They were evacuating when the border closed—these were the ones who made it aboard." She turned the page. Turned another. "And this—" her breath caught, restarted, "—this is a naval signal log. Dated the night of the sinking. Orders."

"Orders to do what?"

Amara looked up at her, and the rain drummed on the cabin roof, and the river breathed against the hull, and Theo understood that she was about to hear something that would not fit back inside silence once it was spoken.

"*Scuttle,*" Amara said. "Not sink. Scuttle. Opened seacocks, timed charges, a coordinated abandonment ordered from above. These names—" she held up the page, and even in the dim lantern light Theo could see the typed ranks and signatures, three of them, four, officers' names in neat columns with a signature block at the bottom that included a name Theo had seen before, recently, on a patrol boat's transom as it swept past the wreck site with its searchlights off. "*Reyes.* Captain E. Reyes, executive authorization. He signed the order, Theo. He sank a ship full of refugees and then spent thirty years making sure nobody dredged it."

The river said nothing. Rivers are good at that. They take everything and testify to nothing.

Theo looked at the name until the letters blurred. She thought of the patrol boats, the restricted zone, the speed with which someone had known she'd found the chest. She thought of Okafor's smile, of the burning dock, of four hundred and six names in a column that ended mid-page because the typist had run out of ribbon or patience or heart.

Her father had drowned within sight of this delta. That was the whole of the story she'd ever allowed herself—that the sea took him, indifferent, the way the sea takes everyone. She had built her entire life on that indifference. It was easier than the alternative.

But the sea didn't scuttle ships. Men did.

"We can't stop," she heard herself say. Her voice sounded foreign, stripped of its usual flatness. "Whatever they send after us—we keep this moving. Downriver. To Calloway. There are presses in Calloway that don't ask permission."

Amara was already rewrapping the bundle, movements quickening, grief converting itself to velocity in real time. "There's more," she said. "Crew lists. My brother's name is on one of these, Theo—he wasn't cargo, he was *crew*, they hired him and then—" She stopped. Pressed her palm flat against the ledger as though checking for a pulse. "There's more. Enough to hang them all."

Ahead, downstream, thunder rolled along the horizon like a door being closed somewhere far away. And beneath it, faint but unmistakable, carried on water that carried everything: engines. More than one. Coming down the channel after them, patient as the rain.

Theo put the throttle forward.

"Hold on to that chest," she said. "It's the only thing on this boat worth more than we are."
