## 5. What the Flood Remembers

The village had drowned years ago and the river had simply never given it back.

Amara saw it first as a suggestion through the rain—a church spire leaning like a man listening at a door, the roofs of two houses breaking the current in black triangles. Theo brought the boat in slow, engine at a whisper, threading between what had once been a fence line and what had once been a street, and tied off to a doorframe that still stood, stubbornly, above the waterline. The door itself was gone. Everything else had been taken by the flood, or had surrendered to it.

"Kampong Selat," Theo said. "Nobody's lived here since the levee broke in '09. The government moved them inland and forgot the coordinates."

"Then nobody will look for us here."

"Nobody looks anywhere anymore. That's the whole trick of it." Theo said it lightly, but Amara was learning the weight under her lightness—the way she said *nobody* and meant *me*.

They carried the chest into the tallest house, the one whose second floor had stayed dry, more or less, its walls furred green with mold, a wardrobe standing in the corner like a mourner. Rain came through the roof in threads, tapping a tin basin someone had left behind a lifetime ago. Amara hung the tarp over the window gap and by the time she turned back, Theo had the chest open and was sitting very still in front of it, the way a person sits before a thing they are afraid to touch.

* * *

The documents were wrapped in oilcloth, three layers of it, and inside that they were dry as bone. Amara lifted them out with both hands, careful as if lifting a sleeping child. Ship's manifests. A cargo log. A crew roster with names struck through in pencil. And beneath all of it, in a folder stamped with a crest she had only ever seen in her brother's letters—a crowned wave, drawn in a hand too hurried to be official—typed pages, carbon copies, the kind that were made to be destroyed and had somehow, miraculously, not been.

She read the first line and her mouth went dry.

"What is it?" Theo asked.

Amara didn't answer. She couldn't. The rain filled the silence for her, drumming on the tin basin, running down the walls in bright veins.

It was a report. Dated nineteen years ago, three months before the *Vessania Star* went down in the delta with all hands—or so the official record said. It listed the passengers. Not cargo. *Passengers.* Two hundred and eleven names, most of them Vessian, a nation that had already been erased from every map by then, its people scattered, its ships unwelcome in every port. The *Star* had been their last chartered passage out, a refugee run dressed as a freight manifest, and the report said—Amara read it twice, three times, her lips moving—the report said the navy had known. Had boarded her at the mouth of the delta. Had ordered her scuttled with the passengers below, because letting two hundred Vessian refugees land would have been, in the language of the report, *a politically unmanageable precedent.*

The signatures were at the bottom. Four of them. Officers' names, ranks, a date, and a final line in a different hand: *Executed under sealed orders of Capt. E. Reyes.*

"Reyes," Theo said quietly. "That's a name I know."

Amara looked up. Theo's face had gone the color of the river.

"You know him?"

"Everyone in salvage knows Captain Reyes. He runs the blockade at Calloway. He's been hunting this chest since I pulled it up—I just didn't know why he'd care." Theo's jaw worked. "Now I know."

Amara turned back to the pages because looking at Theo's face had become unbearable, and because the pages were her brother. Dev Sen had crewed on the *Star* for one season, one run, the summer he turned twenty-two, writing home letters that Amara had kept in a biscuit tin for nineteen years. *The work is strange but the pay is honest,* he'd written. *The passengers travel below and we don't ask questions. Mara, they sing at night. Whole songs, in a language I don't know, and the hull carries it up like the ship is singing too.*

She found his name on the roster. *D. Sen, deckhand.* Someone had drawn a line through it in pencil, and beside it, in the same hurried hand that had drawn the crowned wave, a single word: *ashore.* He had missed the last voyage. He had been ashore when the navy came, and had lived exactly long enough to learn what happened to the ship and the two hundred and eleven people he'd sung with, and then he had died anyway—fever, the letters said, but Amara had never believed fever. Fever didn't explain why his last letter, the one that arrived after the news, had been written in a hand that shook, or why it said only: *They will tell you it was the weather. It was not the weather.*

"Read it out loud," Theo said.

Amara looked at her. "Why?"

"Because I've been carrying it in silence since the wreck, and silence is heavy. Because names that stay in the dark stay dead. Read them."

So Amara read. She read the manifest first, the false one, *machinery parts, agricultural equipment,* and then the true one, and she read all two hundred and eleven names into the drowned house, her voice steadying as it went, the rain keeping time. Some of the names she stumbled over. Some she had to spell out from the carbon smudge. Theo sat with her back against the wardrobe, eyes closed, listening the way sailors listen to weather—not to the words but to what was coming underneath them.

When Amara finished, the light through the tarp had gone from grey to the color of old pewter. Neither of them moved. The chest sat between them like an open grave that had finally, after nineteen years, been given a stone with the right names on it.

"He wrote to me every week," Amara said. It was the first time she had said any of it aloud in years. "Dev. My brother. He was going to send for me when he'd saved enough. He wanted us to buy a boat. A survey boat—he'd worked a season with the coastal mapping office and he loved it, loved the idea of drawing the water, making the dangerous places legible so people wouldn't drown in them." She laughed, and it came out wrong. "And then he drowned in the one place he'd made legible for other people. The navy sank his ship and called it weather, and I spent nineteen years mapping coastlines for the same government that killed him, drawing their charts, being *useful*, and I never once—" Her voice broke and she let it. "I never once looked at the water and asked it what it remembered."

"The water remembers everything," Theo said. "It's the only honest archive there is. It just doesn't testify."

Amara wiped her face with the heel of her hand. "Your turn."

Theo went still. It was a specific stillness, Amara realized—a diver's stillness, the stillness of someone conserving air.

"There's no turn," Theo said. "There's your brother and there's two hundred and eleven people, and there's a chest, and that's the whole story."

"Your father was a diver too."

It wasn't a question. It was in everything—how Theo checked her gear twice, how she never dove without a line, how she'd gone white at the name *Reyes* in a way that had nothing to do with navy captains.

"My father was a lot of things," Theo said. "None of them on paper."

"The broker said—"

"The broker says whatever gets him paid." Theo stood, abruptly, and went to the window gap, and stood with her back to Amara, watching the rain come down the drowned street like the river rehearsing for something. "We should sleep in shifts. Patrol boats run the main channel at night, even in weather."

"Theo."

"Shifts, Amara."

Amara looked at her—the set of her shoulders, the way her hand had gone, without her seeming to notice, to the compass hanging at her neck, thumb moving over its face the way other people touch a wound. Amara had watched her do it a dozen times since the dock and had never asked, because grief recognized grief and kept its distance. But the chest was open between them now. The dead had been named. It seemed suddenly obscene to leave one drowning unnamed.

"You touch that compass the way I touch Dev's letters," Amara said quietly. "I'm not asking for the story. I'm asking you to stop pretending there isn't one."

For a long moment Theo didn't move. Rain threaded through the gap and ran down her sleeve and she let it.

"He went down off Calloway Point," she said at last, not turning around. "Nineteen years ago. Same year as your brother's ship. Same season." Her thumb stopped moving on the compass. "Salvage job, deep water, bad conditions. His partner came up. He didn't. The official finding was equipment failure. The partner said the same. Everyone said the same." A pause, long enough that Amara thought she'd finished. "Reyes was the officer who signed off on the dive. And last year, drunk, in a bar in Calloway, he told me the air line hadn't failed at all. He told me it had been *managed.* My father had seen something on a wreck he wasn't supposed to see, and the navy managed it, and my father's partner was paid to say *equipment failure*, and I was eleven years old and I believed them for twenty years because believing them was easier than being an orphan twice."

The rain filled the silence. Amara found she couldn't speak. Two hundred and eleven names on a page, and one name that had never even made it onto a page, and the same captain's signature under both drownings like a watermark running through the whole delta.

"So no," Theo said, still facing the water. "There's no turn. There's just the same hand on both of us, and I've spent my whole life staying small enough that it couldn't find me again. And now—" she turned, finally, and her eyes were dry in the way that Amara recognized as the driest kind, "—now I'm sitting in a drowned village holding the one thing that can make that hand let go of everyone it's ever held under. And I don't know what to do with that. I know how to disappear. I don't know how to matter."

Amara crossed the room and sat down beside her, close enough that their shoulders touched. Neither of them said anything else for a while. Outside, the river moved through the streets of Kampong Selat, patient as a creditor, and the rain kept its rhythm on the tin basin, and somewhere far downriver, though neither of them could have known it yet, a navy cutter was turning into the delta with its running lights dark.

It was Theo who heard it first—she was always the one who heard the water first. She went to the window gap, head tilted, and when she came back her face had changed.

"Engine," she said. "Big one. Coming up the channel, and it's not running quiet."

Amara was already folding the documents back into their oilcloth. "How long?"

"Minutes. And it's not a smuggler. Smugglers run quiet." Theo was moving now, killing the lamp, gathering line. "It's the navy. And Amara—" she paused at the door, the compass glinting at her throat, "—if that's Reyes, then he knows exactly where we are. Which means somebody told him."
