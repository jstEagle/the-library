## 6. The Broker's Price

The broker found them at low tide, the way rot finds a hull.

Theo heard him before she saw him—the particular cough of an outboard running rich, throttled down to a polite idle as it came around the drowned pilings of what had once been a fish cannery. She was on deck with a rag and a tin of grease, pretending to work on the winch, and she let her hands keep moving while her eyes tracked the boat: a long, low river skiff painted the gray of nothing in particular, the color every smuggler in the delta wore like a uniform. Vessalio stood at the tiller with his hands folded, which was somehow worse than if he'd held a gun. He never needed one. That was the whole architecture of him.

"Madame Oyelaran." His voice carried across the water without effort, soft as silt settling. "You look well. The river agrees with you."

"The river doesn't know me," Theo said.

"No." He smiled, and gold flashed at his back molar, catching the flat morning light. "It only employs you. I remember."

He tied off at her stern without asking, and she didn't stop him, because stopping him would have been a statement, and statements were things she'd spent eleven years learning not to make. Amara was below, asleep in the narrow berth after a night spent reading documents by lamplight until the names blurred. Theo was glad of it. Some conversations you wanted to have standing up, alone, where your face could do its work unwatched.

Vessalio stepped aboard and looked around her boat with the fondness of a man appraising livestock. The leaking seam he'd have already known about. The patched sail. The water-stained charts curling in their tube by the wheel. He catalogued her poverty the way she catalogued wrecks—methodically, without cruelty, because cruelty was inefficient.

"You've made me come a long way," he said. "Through navy patrol lanes. Do you know what that costs?"

"I imagine you'll tell me."

"I imagine I will." He settled onto her fish box like it was a chair in his own parlor. "Here is what I know, Madame Oyelaran. I know that Captain Reyes has two cutters sweeping the eastern channels, working inward like a man closing a net. I know he has your description—your *real* description, not the one on whatever papers you're carrying this month. And I know that your debt to me stands at four thousand, two hundred marks, plus the interest that accrues while you hide from me in flooded mangrove country." He paused, delicately. "I also know that you are sitting on a chest full of paper worth considerably more than all of that. To certain buyers."

The tide was going out around them. Theo could hear it, the slow exhale of the delta draining through its channels, exposing mudflats where the egrets stood like sentries who'd forgotten what they were guarding. She kept her voice level. "You want the chest."

"I want to *buy* the chest. There is a difference, and the difference is that you get paid." He raised one finger. "Four thousand, two hundred marks, forgiven. Every mark of it. Eleven years of looking over your shoulder for my collectors—gone. And more than that." His eyes were patient, almost kind. "Reyes wants you too, Madame. A diver who can be identified, carrying contraband from a restricted wreck? He doesn't need evidence against you. He needs only to find you. But if the chest comes to me, I can give you something Reyes cannot buy at any price: my protection. My channels. There are ways out of this delta that no cutter will ever find, because I paid for them to stay off the maps. You and the cartographer could be in Calloway under new papers inside a week, or across the strait entirely, if you prefer. Nobody would ever say the name Oyelaran again."

*Nobody would ever say the name.* It landed in her like a hook set deep. Because that was the thing she'd wanted, wasn't it? For eleven years. Invisibility as a kind of salvation. Her father had been visible—visible on the water, visible in the union hall, visible enough that when the sea took him there were men who said they weren't surprised—and look what visibility had bought him. Theo had built her whole life on the opposite principle. Be small. Be gray. Be the color of nothing in particular, like Vessalio's skiff.

And here was the broker offering to make her invisible forever, professionally, guaranteed.

"What about the documents?" she asked. "What happens to them?"

Vessalio spread his hands. "They go to a buyer. That is all you need to know."

"It isn't, though. Is it." She wiped her hands slowly on the rag, buying time, watching his face. "Because there are exactly two kinds of people who pay for thirty-year-old naval records, Vessalio. Historians don't pay this much. So it's the other kind. The men whose signatures are on those pages."

The smile didn't move, but something behind it did—a flicker, a recalculation, the brief mechanical adjustment of a man whose pitch has met an obstacle he hadn't priced in. "Madame. What those papers say or don't say is not your concern. Your concern was cargo recovery. I hired you to strip a wreck quietly, and you have done so, and now the quiet part is over and the smart part begins. Take the debt relief. Take the protection. Let the past belong to the people who own it."

"The people who own it," Theo repeated. "You mean the men who sank it."

"I mean," he said, with the first edge entering his voice, "the men who can afford it."

Below decks, faintly, Theo heard Amara turn over in the berth. The boat rocked. Somewhere out in the channel a kingfisher hit the water and came up flashing.

She thought about her father's hands. That was the strange place her mind went—she hadn't let herself go there in years—but there it was: his hands on the wheel of their old boat, scarred from wire rope, turning the compass bezel with one thumb while he taught her to read a bearing. *A heading is a promise, little fish,* he used to say. *You pick it, and then everything you do either keeps it or breaks it.* She'd been nine. She'd thought he meant navigation.

She thought about the refugee families in the documents. Three hundred and eleven souls, manifest number 77-V, loaded onto a ship called the *Cormorant's Grace* and sent into a storm the navy itself had forecast, with the seacocks opened behind them, because three hundred and eleven refugees were inconvenient and a scuttled hull left no witnesses. Except one. Except a crewman named Sen who'd understood what he was being ordered to do and had hidden a copy of the orders in a sealed chest, and had died for it, and whose sister was sleeping ten feet below Theo's feet.

Eleven years ago, Theo had taken money from Vessalio because she had nothing and nowhere, and she'd told herself the debt was just arithmetic. Numbers owed, numbers paid. Neutral, like the river. But arithmetic had a way of compounding into ownership, and ownership into obedience, and she was standing on the deck of it right now—four thousand, two hundred marks of quiet, of grayness, of *nobody will ever say the name Oyelaran again*, offered to her like a lifeboat, if only she'd hand three hundred and eleven dead people back to the men who killed them.

"Tempting," she said.

"I'm told I'm persuasive."

"You are. That's the problem with you." She folded the rag, laid it on the winch, and made herself meet his eyes. "No."

Vessalio went very still. Stillness was his weapon the way other men used knives. "Think carefully, Madame. Reyes does not forgive. And I do not offer twice."

"You offered twice just now. Debt and protection in the same breath—that's twice." She heard her own voice come out flat and steady, and was distantly surprised by it. "Here's my counteroffer. You leave this boat. You tell your collectors the debt stays where it is, and if they come near us, I go to Captain Reyes myself and trade him something better than a chest. I'll trade him *you*. I know how much of his patrol schedule runs through your channels, Vessalio. I've dived under your boats. I know what you carry and where."

"That would destroy you too."

"Yes," Theo said. "It would. That's why it's credible."

For a long moment the only sound was the tide and the creak of the mooring line. Then Vessalio laughed—a short, genuine sound, which frightened her more than anything else he'd done. "Eleven years," he said, standing, smoothing his coat. "Eleven years you've been the most reliable diver I employ. Invisible. Careful. Cheap." He stepped to the gunwale. "I confess I'm disappointed. Sentiment is expensive, Madame Oyelaran. Someone always makes you pay retail."

He dropped into his skiff, untied the line with two economical motions, and let the current take him backward off her stern. At the tiller he paused, and the gold tooth caught the light one last time.

"For what it's worth," he said, "I liked you better when you were hiding."

Then he was gone around the pilings, the cough of his engine fading into the green distance, and Theo stood alone on her deck with her heart going like a hammer on sheet metal.

She'd done it. She'd chosen. And the choosing felt less like freedom than like stepping off a wreck into black water—no bottom, no line, nothing to hold but what she carried.

Amara's head appeared in the companionway. She'd heard. Of course she'd heard; the boat was twelve meters long. Her eyes went from Theo's face to the empty channel and back.

"He offered to erase it," Theo said. "All of it. The debt. Everything."

"And you said no."

Theo looked downriver, toward the east, where two navy cutters were even now closing the net, and toward the south, where Port Calloway waited behind a blockade, four days' fuel away through waters that were about to fill with rain.

"I said no," she said. "Which means we need to run. Now. Before he decides disappointment is cheaper than patience."

Amara was already reaching for the chart tube. "Then we thread the narrows," she said, unrolling her brother's last survey across the wheel. "There's a channel he marked that isn't on any naval map. If it still exists—we might be the first boat through since he died."

Theo took the wheel and turned her bow toward the mangroves, toward the dark water where the maps ran out.

Behind them, far downstream, the first thunder of the true monsoon rolled up the delta like a door closing.
