## 7. Narrow Waters

The navy found the first exit before we did.

Amara saw it from the bow of Theo's boat—a grey shape sliding across the mouth of the channel like a lid closing over a jar—and felt the geography rearrange itself in her chest. She had drawn these waters once. Every sandbar, every dead channel, every mangrove island that was really three islands at low tide and one at high. The survey had been her first commission out of apprenticeship, paid for by a ministry that no longer existed, for a coast that had since been redrawn by flood and forgetting.

She had not thought of those charts in years. Now they were the only thing between them and a cell.

"Port side," she said. "There's a cut behind the drowned chapel—maybe two meters wide."

Theo didn't argue. That was the thing Amara was still learning about her: she argued with everything except the water. When Amara spoke of channels and depths, Theo's hands simply moved, wheeling the boat hard enough that the wake slapped the mangrove roots like a reprimand. The engine note dropped as they slid into shadow, into the green cathedral dark where the canopy closed overhead and the air went thick with rot and salt and the sweetish stink of silt turned over by their hull.

Behind them, the patrol boat's searchlight swept the open water where they should have been.

"They'll circle back," Theo murmured. Her eyes never stopped moving—forward, aft, up to the canopy where the light came down in coins. "They know this delta better than we do."

"No." Amara unrolled her charts on the deck boards, weighting the corners with the bilge pump handle and a coil of rope. The paper was soft as cloth now, foxed at the edges, but her own hand was still legible—the careful italics of a twenty-three-year-old cartographer who had believed maps were neutral things. "They know what's on the official charts. This—" she tapped a thread of blue ink running inland from the estuary, unnamed, unlabeled except for a small annotation in someone else's handwriting, *D.'s shortcut, don't tell the ministry*— "this isn't on any of them."

Theo leaned over. Rain began then, the first fat drops of the afternoon squall stippling the paper, and Amara covered the chart with her body without thinking, the way you'd cover a child.

"What is it?" Theo asked.

"My brother drew it." The name came easier than it had a week ago; grief was learning to be a tool instead of a wound. "Devan. He crewed coastal runs after he left the survey office. He used to say the ministry's charts were written for people who wanted to be found." She traced the channel with one finger, following it as it threaded between islands that had no names because he'd never bothered to give them any. "He marked his own routes. Fuel stops, safe anchorages, places to hide if you needed to hide." She looked up. "I used to think it was just—adventure. A young man playing pirate. I never asked who might need hiding from."

Theo said nothing, but something in her stillness changed, the way water changes when a current shifts beneath it.

* * *

The squall arrived properly an hour later, arriving the way monsoon squalls do—not gradually but all at once, as if the sky had been holding its breath since morning and finally let it go. Rain came sideways. The world contracted to a grey-green tunnel of water and leaves, visibility measured in meters, sound erased except for the engine and the rain hammering the canopy overhead like applause from a vast indifferent audience.

Amara navigated by memory and arithmetic. Depth soundings called out against her brother's numbers, corrected for tide. Bearing held against a compass rose she herself had inked. Theo steered, and Amara fed her the river one sentence at a time:

"Hundred meters, then bear right where the dead kapok leans left."

"Sandbar's moved—shoaling on your port, give it a boat-length more room."

"Here. Slow. There's a submerged fence post from the old fish traps—Devan circled it twice, so it must have claimed at least two hulls."

Theo obeyed each instruction without question, without hesitation, and Amara understood, somewhere in the middle of the fourth hour, that this was the most intimate conversation she had ever had with another person. Not confession—navigation. Two women trusting each other completely in the narrowest possible space, where one wrong number meant mangrove roots peeling the hull open like fruit.

"You're good at this," Theo said at one point, and it sounded almost accusatory.

"I'm good at reading dead men's handwriting." Amara wiped rain from the chart, though the rain replaced it instantly. "My brother wrote me letters for six years. Coordinates disguised as descriptions. *The fishing was poor off the second island, but the third held plenty.* I thought he was being poetic." Her throat tightened. "He was teaching me his map in case he ever couldn't come home to explain it."

"And now you're using it to run from the people who killed him."

"Yes." She said it flatly, and found that saying it flatly was a kind of strength. "And I intend to use it very well."

* * *

The second exit closed at dusk.

They surfaced from the mangroves into a wide lagoon, rain thinned to mist, and there—two kilometers across the flat pewter water—sat a patrol boat at anchor, lights burning, exactly astride the channel that led to the sea. Beyond it, faint as a bruise on the horizon, the last light of the west was dying.

Theo cut the engine and let them drift among the mangroves at the lagoon's edge, hidden in the tangle of prop roots, and for a long time neither of them spoke. Amara could hear the other woman breathing, controlled and deliberate, the breathing of someone managing fear rather than lacking it.

"They're not searching," Amara whispered. "They're waiting. They know every way out of this lagoon, so why burn fuel?"

"Because someone told them our options." Theo's voice was low and bitter. "Reyes has the hydrographic office. He probably has my broker too, if he doesn't already have the broker's ledger memorized." She turned, and in the failing light her face was all angles and shadow. "How many ways out?"

Amara unrolled the chart again, though she barely needed it. Three exits on the official record: the main channel north, watched; the southern passage past Watchman Key, which Devan had crossed out himself with a single word—*mined?*—in 1947, unverified, unusable; and the eastern cut, which existed on no chart at all.

"There's a fourth," she said slowly. "But it isn't a route. It's a rumor."

"Theo Oyelaran does not run on rumors."

"Theo Oyelaran also doesn't have a choice." Amara put her finger on the blank space east of the lagoon, where her brother's ink simply stopped, as if the land beyond were too secret even for him. Except—it wasn't blank. Under the lamplight, tilted just so, she could see the ghost of pencil beneath the blankness: graphite erased but not fully, leaving a depression in the paper that her fingertip could read even where her eyes couldn't. Braille for sighted people. Devan's habit—he'd done it with coordinates he didn't want visible to customs inspectors.

She closed her eyes and read with her finger.

"Channel," she breathed. "Runs behind Serpent Island. Shallow—two point one meters at low water, four at flood. Narrower than anything we've been through. It comes out..." Her finger stopped. Her heart stopped with it. "It comes out at the old fueling station on Cane Row."

Theo went very still. "Cane Row is Reyes territory. It's the first place he'd look."

"It's the last place anyone would expect us to come *from*." Amara looked up. "Every exit they've closed assumes we're running seaward along the routes a sane person would take. Devan's channel doesn't go seaward. It goes *through*. Behind them. We surface inside their cordon instead of outside it, and we're past the net before they understand the net has a hole."

For a long moment Theo studied her, and Amara felt the weight of that look like a hand on her shoulder—assessment, doubt, and underneath both, reluctantly surfacing, respect.

"You've been cargo since the dock," Theo said. "Passenger. Woman I'm protecting." A crooked, humorless smile. "You just out-navigated a navy captain."

"I grew up with a boy who treated the delta as a puzzle box," Amara said. "Some of it soaked in."

"All right." Theo took the wheel again, and her voice shifted into the register Amara was beginning to recognize as her working voice—flat, fast, certain. "Flood tide turns in forty minutes. We ride it in at full throttle and pray your brother's soundings weren't optimistic. You call the depth, I steer, and neither of us stops talking until we're through. If we ground, we ground fighting."

"If we ground, we're dead," Amara said quietly. "Two-one meters. Your boat draws how much?"

"One-nine, light." Theo's jaw tightened. "Light means no cargo weight. Which means the chest rides in your lap, and if we take water, you hold it above the flooding and I hold the wheel, and we both learn to swim in mangrove soup."

Amara gathered the chest onto her knees. Through the wood she could feel the documents inside, dry still, wrapped in oilcloth—the names of the drowned, the orders that had drowned them, thirty years of silence sealed under a Vessanian crest. Her brother had carried this ship's sister cargo once. Perhaps he had known. Perhaps that was why he'd learned every secret channel in the delta: not adventure at all, but escape routes, mapped in advance, for a truth he'd never gotten the chance to carry out himself.

"Devan," she whispered, "if you're anywhere, be here."

The tide turned. Theo opened the throttle.

* * *

Serpent Channel was less a route than a wound in the world.

The mangroves closed overhead until the sky was a ragged green seam. Roots reached for the hull on both sides, close enough to touch, trailing beard-curtains of moss that dragged across the deck like fingers checking their passage. The water itself was the color of strong tea, opaque, so that Amara's soundings were the only eyes they had—lead line swinging, numbers called, Theo's hands answering each number before the echo of it faded.

"Three-two."

"Steady."

"Two-six. Bear left—the channel bends around a root mass, Devan wrote *hug the left root or die.*"

"I'm hugging."

"Two-four. Two-two—" Amara's voice caught. "Theo. Two-one-five. That's fifteen centimeters under your keel."

"Then we're floating on mud and prayer, and prayer is doing the work." But sweat stood on Theo's forehead despite the cool rain, and her hands on the wheel were white-knuckled, and she did not slow down, because slowing meant squatting deeper, settling into the mud, dying comfortable. The engine roared. The channel narrowed. Somewhere behind them, impossibly far away and impossibly close, a patrol boat's engine note changed pitch—searching, circling, blind.

"Flood tide's lifting us," Amara called. "Two-three. Two-five. Hold your line—"

A root scraped the hull with a sound like a fingernail down a chalkboard, long and shrieking, and then released. And then the channel widened, and deepened, and bent south, and the green ceiling tore open to show a sky going violet with evening, and ahead—low, ramshackle, lit by a single yellow lamp—lay the fueling station at Cane Row, sitting quiet and innocent inside the naval cordon like a smuggled letter inside an envelope.

Theo eased back on the throttle. The roar died to a mutter, then a whisper, then nothing but water against the hull and Amara's own heartbeat.

They drifted toward the dock. No one challenged them. No one even looked.

"We're inside," Amara whispered.

"We're inside," Theo agreed, and there was something wild and exhausted and almost laughing in it. Then her gaze fixed on the station, on the figures moving by the fuel pumps, and the laugh drained out of her face. "Amara. The man at the pump. Grey coat, hands behind his back."

Amara followed her stare. Even at this distance, even in the bad light, the posture was unmistakable: unhurried, proprietary, a man waiting because waiting always worked for him.

"He knows we're coming," she said.

"He doesn't know we're coming." Theo cut the engine entirely and let the current carry them the last hundred meters, silent as driftwood. "He *arranged* it. That's Captain Reyes. He closes every door except the one he wants us to walk through." Her hand found the tiller and gripped it. "He wants to talk to me."

"Then we go around—"

"There is no around. Not anymore." Theo looked at her then, and Amara saw that the fear was gone, burned away, replaced by something harder and colder and terribly calm. "Get the chest ready. Whatever happens at that dock, whatever he offers me—you keep rowing. Promise me."

"Promise me you won't trade it," Amara countered. "Not the chest. Not for amnesty, not for your debt, not for anything he says about—"

She stopped. Because Theo flinched. A small flinch, quickly mastered, but Amara had spent a week learning to read this woman the way she read charts, and she knew what it meant.

"He knew my father," Theo said quietly. "That's the offer he's carrying. And I intend to let him make it—right up until the moment I refuse."
