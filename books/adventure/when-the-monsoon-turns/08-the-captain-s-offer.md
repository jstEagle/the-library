## 8. The Captain's Offer

The fueling station at Kilometre Nine was a dying thing even before the monsoon got to it—a single rusted pump on a concrete apron that the river had been patiently reclaiming for a decade, its edges furred with green, its fuel shed roofed in corrugated tin that sang all night under the rain like a struck instrument. Theo had chosen it because it was the kind of place nobody chose. The kind of place you only came to when your tank was empty and your options were emptier.

Which, she thought, watching the rain come down in gray ropes off the edge of the awning, described her situation with uncomfortable precision.

Amara was inside the shed, counting out their last crumpled notes to the station keeper, an old man with cataract-pale eyes who asked no questions and gave no change. The *Vela* sat low against the dock, riding heavy with river water she'd been bailing for two days, her engine coughing like a smoker. They needed fuel. They needed it badly enough that Theo had let herself stop moving for twenty minutes, and twenty minutes, she knew, was exactly how long it took for the world to find you when you'd spent six years learning not to be found.

She heard the boat before she saw it—two engines, running quiet, which was worse than running loud. Loud meant smugglers or drunks. Quiet meant professionals.

The patrol launch slid out of the rain wall like something surfacing from underwater, gray hull, gray canopy, no running lights. It came alongside the dock without a bump, without a word, and four sailors in dark fatigues stepped onto the apron with the unhurried economy of men who had done this many times and never once been surprised by the outcome.

Theo stood very still beside the fuel pump, one hand resting on the hull of the *Vela*, and did the arithmetic she always did: distance to Amara, distance to the water, weight of the chest, speed of the current. The numbers came back bad. They always came back bad. That was what being cornered meant—the math stopped working.

"Ms. Oyelaran."

He stepped out from under the canopy last, and she knew him before he introduced himself, because he looked exactly like what he was: Captain Reyes, forty-some years old, silver at the temples, a face built for official photographs and arranged so that nothing on it ever moved first. His uniform was dry. That was the detail that snagged in her mind, absurdly, as the rain hammered down around them—he stood in the middle of a monsoon and his shoulders were dry, because a sailor held an umbrella over him, and some deep animal part of Theo registered this as the most obscene thing she had ever seen.

"You have me at a disadvantage," she said.

"I don't think I do." He came closer, boots ringing on wet concrete, and stopped just outside arm's reach, which told her he'd read her file too. "Captain Emilio Reyes. Coastal Command." He glanced at the *Vela*, at the waterline stain, at the patched exhaust. "You've been hard to find. Most people in your profession are easy to find. They want to be found. You've gone to real trouble."

"I'm a salvage diver," Theo said. "Trouble is the job description."

"It is." He smiled without any warmth reaching his eyes. "It's also, historically, a way of disappearing. You're good at it. Your father was too."

The name landed in her chest like a hook set behind the sternum. She kept her face still. Six years of practice; she could hold a face still through almost anything. "I wouldn't know. He left when I was nineteen."

"He left," Reyes agreed mildly, "in a boat called the *Cormorant*, out of Port Calloway, eleven years ago this October. Cargo run. He never arrived." He turned his head slightly, watching the rain sheet off the awning, and Theo understood suddenly that he wasn't performing this memory. He was consulting it. "I was the officer who cleared him through the checkpoint at Serrano Light. Last man to log his departure."

The river roared softly under the dock. Somewhere inside the shed, Amara's voice rose and fell, counting money, unaware.

"So you knew him," Theo said. Her voice came out level. She was proud of that, later, that it came out level.

"I signed his transit papers. I waved him through." Reyes looked back at her. "And then, three days later, I received a signal ordering me to stand down the search when the *Cormorant* failed to report. A routine vessel loss, the signal said. Weather event. No survivors expected, no investigation warranted." He paused. "I complied."

The rain filled the silence. Theo became aware of her own heartbeat, slow and enormous, like something turning over in deep water.

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because I want you to understand that I am a man who complies." He said it simply, without self-pity, and somehow that was worse than if he'd flinched. "I have complied for thirty years, Ms. Oyelaran. With orders, with signals, with the quiet understanding that certain wrecks stay unnamed and certain cargo stays buried. I sank ships for that understanding. I let men drown for it. Your father among them—not directly, not by my hand, but I knew the waters he was sailing into, and I said nothing, because saying something would have cost me my career, and I valued my career." He folded his hands behind his back, a parade-ground posture, immaculate. "I am telling you this because I am about to offer you a bargain, and I want you to weigh it knowing precisely what kind of man is offering it."

"And what kind of bargain does a complying man offer?"

"Amnesty." The word dropped between them like an anchor. "Full pardon. Your debt to Halloran's broker—yes, we know about Vasko, we know the figure, we know what happens to divers who default on Vasko—erased. Clean papers. A berth in any port in the delta, working openly, no more running. You walk away from the chest, you tell me where Sen's brother's letters are, and you spend the rest of your life visible." He tilted his head. "You've spent six years invisible, Ms. Oyelaran. I'm offering you the thing you've wanted most. All it costs is everything you found."

Inside the shed, the tin roof sang. Theo watched the rain hit the river and thought about visibility. About how she had taught herself to be weather, to be current, to be nothing the world could get a grip on—and how, somewhere downriver in a drowned village, she had sat across from Amara Sen while the documents dried by a kerosene lamp, and felt, for the first time since her father's boat went missing, that she was carrying something instead of hiding from it.

"The men in those documents," Theo said. "You were there. At the scuttling."

Reyes didn't answer, which was an answer.

"Were you the one who gave the order?"

"I was the one," Reyes said quietly, "who made sure the lifeboats were locked."

Something went white and silent in Theo, a pressure change, like the moment before a depth charge. She thought of the manifest pages, the names of the refugee families, the children listed by age. She thought of her father's compass in her pocket, its needle swinging toward a north that no longer existed on any map. She thought of him waving from the deck of the *Cormorant*, and of a signal officer somewhere standing down the search, and of thirty years of rain falling on a river that remembered everything and told no one.

"You're offering me amnesty," she said, "for staying quiet about a massacre. And you're telling me you ran the boats."

"I'm offering you a future," Reyes said. "I'm telling you the truth so you'll believe the offer is real. I don't bluff, Ms. Oyelaran. Ask anyone who's dealt with me." He reached into his coat and produced an envelope, heavy cream paper, sealed with wax—an actual seal, archaic and deliberate—and laid it on the fuel pump between them. "Terms inside. You have until the tide turns. After that, my launch escorts the *Vela* to port, with or without your cooperation, and Ms. Sen goes in for questioning. She strikes me as fragile. Grief does that."

That was the mistake. Not the offer—she'd expected the offer, had braced for it the way you brace for cold water. But he'd said *fragile* about Amara, in that voice, the voice of a man who had spent thirty years deciding which lives were worth the paperwork, and the whiteness in Theo's chest stopped being shock and started being fuel.

She thought: *he wants me to consider it.* That was the whole architecture of the trap. He wanted her standing here weighing amnesty against duty, wanting it, hating herself for wanting it, so that whatever she chose, he owned the choosing. So she considered it. Deliberately. Let her eyes go to the envelope, let her breath slow, let her shoulders drop half an inch—the performance of a tired woman doing arithmetic. She even picked up the envelope. Turned it over. Felt the wax seal under her thumb.

"You knew my father," she said, musing, as if the fact had just occurred to her. "Did he ever mention me?"

"I believe he had a daughter he was working for. Saving up to bring home."

"He was bringing home a compass," Theo said. "Bought it in Calloway. Brass case. He said a man who knows where north is can't be lost, no matter what the charts say." She slid the envelope into her pocket, and watched Reyes's composure settle by a single degree—the smallest loosening around his eyes, the look of a man who believed he had won. "I'll need until the tide turns to talk to Ms. Sen. She's fragile."

"Of course." He stepped back. Rain drummed on the umbrella above him. "You're a reasonable woman, Ms. Oyelaran. Reasonable women live long lives."

He walked back to his launch. The engines woke, twin throats clearing themselves, and the gray hull withdrew into the gray rain until there was nothing left of it but the wake slapping against the pilings.

Theo stood a long time with the envelope burning in her pocket. Then she walked into the shed, where Amara looked up from the fuel receipt with a question already forming, and Theo put the unopened envelope on the shelf beside her, and said:

"We leave when the tide turns. Both tides. Get the charts."

Amara stared at her. "What did he offer you?"

"Everything I ever wanted." Theo pulled her father's compass from her pocket and set it on the shelf next to the envelope, needle trembling, finding a north that existed on no map but hers. "Which is how I know exactly what it's worth. Nothing."

Outside, the rain was thickening toward something larger. Downriver, past the drowned villages and the narrow waters, Port Calloway waited behind its blockade—and Theo Oyelaran, visible now whether she liked it or not, began to plan how she was going to sail straight through the middle of it.
