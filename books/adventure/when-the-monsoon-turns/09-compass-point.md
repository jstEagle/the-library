## 9. Compass Point

The pilot's shack sat on stilts over the black water at the edge of Port Calloway, its walls patched with tin and tarps, a single lamp burning in the window like an eye that refused to blink. Theo had heard about Old Sefa from three different smugglers in three different channels, and all three had said the same thing: he could take you through the mined shallows where the navy wouldn't follow, but his price was never money.

She climbed the ladder with salt crusted in her hair and the compass heavy against her ribs. Behind her, moored in the shadow of a rusting freighter, the *Kestrel* sat low in the water, her seams weeping, her engine coughing like a man with water in his lungs. Amara waited aboard with the chest lashed to the deck and her brother's charts spread across her knees, watching the naval lights strung across the harbor mouth like a net of small cold stars.

The old man inside didn't look up when she entered. He was mending a net by lamplight, his hands moving with the patience of someone who had outlived every hurry he'd ever known.

"They say you don't take coin," Theo said.

"I take what people can't afford to lose." His voice was gravel poured slow. "That's the only payment worth anything. Coin comes back. What you give up stays given."

Theo set the compass on his table. The brass casing caught the lamplight, dented along one edge where it had struck something—her father's knee, the deck of the *Maren*, she'd never known—and the needle swung once, twice, and settled toward north as if greeting an old friend.

Old Sefa put down his net. He turned the compass over in his palms the way another man might hold a sleeping bird, and something moved behind his eyes—not greed, not even longing exactly, but recognition.

"Where did this come from?"

"My father. He went down off the Vessanian coast thirty years ago." She kept her voice flat. "Or so I was told."

"And you've carried it how long?"

"Since I was nine years old."

He nodded slowly, setting it back on the table between them, the needle trembling faintly with the vibration of the water below. "Then you know what it costs. Good. People who hand me things they don't understand—I don't want their business." He stood, joints cracking like rigging in a storm. "Your boat will never make the outer channel. Too slow, too loud, too much freeboard for the swell. But there's a gap in the mine line past the breakwater—a gap I helped survey before your navy decided maps were state secrets. I'll take you through in my own craft. Fast, low, dark. You'll be under their guns for four minutes, maybe five. After that, either you're at the press office steps or you're feeding the fish."

"The chest goes with us or there's no deal."

"The chest is your affair." He was already pulling a slicker from a hook, already moving toward the door with the economy of a man who had run this errand in his mind a thousand times. "Bring your friend. Bring your papers. Leave everything else."

Theo looked at the compass one last time. The needle pointed north, steady now, certain—the way her father must have been certain, the way every drowned navigator is certain until the water takes the certainty and leaves only the instrument. She thought about the weight of it against her ribs through twenty years of diving, of running, of being no one anywhere. It had pointed her home when she'd had one. It had pointed her away when home became a debt she couldn't pay.

Now it pointed at nothing she needed. That was the strange mercy of it.

She left it on the table.

* * *

They crossed the harbor in Old Sefa's boat—a narrow black-hulled thing with a muffled engine and a hull so low it seemed less to float than to slide, a rumor moving across the water. Amara crouched beside Theo in the bilge, the chest strapped between them, her breath coming fast and shallow.

"I counted eleven gun positions," she whispered. "On the charts. Eleven."

"You counted what they published. There'll be more."

Ahead, the blockade stretched across the harbor mouth: two patrol cutters riding their anchor lights, floodlights sweeping the water in slow white arcs, and beyond them the dark hump of the breakwater with its single gap, unmarked on any chart, known only to men who had learned the coast when the coast still belonged to itself.

The first flare went up when they were two hundred meters out.

It burst red above the water and for one terrible second the whole harbor turned the color of an open wound—every wave lit, every hull, every face—and Theo saw the patrol cutter swing its searchlight toward them with the ponderous grace of something large deciding to kill.

"Down!" Sefa barked, and killed the engine.

They drifted. The water slapped the hull. The searchlight swept past them once, hung on the far side of the channel, swept back. Theo pressed her palm flat against the chest and felt Amara's hand come to rest on top of hers, and neither of them spoke, because speaking was breathing and breathing was noise and noise was death.

The light passed over them a second time. Held. Moved on.

A voice crackled across the water, distorted by loudhailer: "Small craft, identify yourself or be fired upon."

Sefa restarted the engine without answering, and the little boat leapt forward into the dark.

What followed, Theo would remember afterward in fragments, the way divers remember bad bottoms: the muzzle flash strobing off the breakwater; the whistle of rounds going wide and then not wide; Amara shouting bearings—"left, LEFT, the chart says the sandbar shifts after the third piling"—while Sefa threaded the gap with the calm of a man driving to market; the chest jumping against their knees as rounds slapped the water around them like hail on a tin roof; the moment a bullet tore through the gunwale a hand's width above Theo's shoulder and she felt, absurdly, not fear but fury, clean and clarifying, the same fury that had carried her out of the broker's office two days ago when she'd chosen the documents over her own skin.

The mines were the worst part. They rode the shallows like sleeping animals, dark domes just breaking the surface, and Sefa took them at full speed with nothing but memory and nerve, calling depths from a childhood thirty years dead: "Six feet here—four—hard to starboard, NOW—" and the boat heeled, scraped, righted, and then they were through, past the last buoy, out into open water where the rain came down like a verdict and the guns fell silent behind them, cheated.

Amara was laughing. Or crying. Theo couldn't tell in the dark, and didn't ask, because she was doing both herself.

* * *

The press office of the *Calloway Courier* occupied a narrow building on Rope Walk, its windows fogged with steam from the printers working the night shift inside. They came ashore at the fish market stairs, wading the last few meters as Sefa's boat settled beneath them—he'd taken a round below the waterline in the final stretch, and the river was reclaiming him inch by inch, patient as ever.

"Go," he told them, waving them off with the same unhurried hands that had mended nets by lamplight. "I know this water better than my own name. It won't drown me tonight."

Theo hesitated—one last hesitation, she promised herself, the last of a lifetime of them—and then she and Amara were hauling the chest up the wet stairs, through the fish market's sleeping stalls, past a night watchman who took one look at their faces and found urgent business elsewhere.

The editor was a woman named Ilse Brandt, gray-haired, ink-stained, awake at three in the morning because the truth, she told them later, keeps worse hours than any journalist. She listened while Amara laid out the documents on her desk—the manifests, the orders signed in a captain's neat hand, the passenger lists of the *Vessa Star* with nine hundred names that had never appeared in any official record—and Theo watched the woman's face change as she read, watched thirty years of careful silence crack and fall away like ice off a hull in spring.

"Where did you get these?" Ilse asked quietly.

"A wreck nobody names," Theo said. "In a river that remembers everything."

Outside, the monsoon redoubled, hammering the roof, filling the streets, washing the gutters clean and dirty all at once. Somewhere out in the flooded harbor, Old Sefa's boat was going down or staying up; somewhere behind them, Captain Reyes was learning that his blockade had failed; somewhere south, the broker was counting losses that no amount of salvage could recover.

Amara touched Theo's arm. Her hand was shaking, her eyes bright and exhausted and alive. "It's done. Whatever happens now—it's done. They can't sink it twice."

"No," Theo agreed. "They can't."

And she realized, standing in the steam-warm print room with the presses beginning to turn and the rain drowning the world outside, that she felt lighter than she had since she was nine years old—that the absence against her ribs where the compass used to sit was not a loss but a space, opened up, waiting to be filled with something she hadn't chosen yet.

Ilse Brandt looked up from the documents, and her voice was steady as a keel. "You two are going to want to see tomorrow's front page. And you're going to want to be somewhere very public when it lands—because by sunrise, every man whose name is on these papers is going to know exactly where to find you."
