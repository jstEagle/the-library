## 10. After the Rain

**Theo**

The rain stopped on a Tuesday, and the whole delta seemed to exhale.

Theo stood in the doorway of the press office at Port Calloway and watched the water retreat from the streets like a tide going out of a wound. Mud lay everywhere in silver sheets. Children were already out in it, barefoot, chasing a ball that had floated in from somewhere upstream—maybe from one of the drowned villages, maybe from farther than that. The sky had that rinsed, bruised clarity it only got after weeks of monsoon, as if the whole world had been scrubbed and hadn't yet decided what to be.

Behind her, inside, the printing press clattered. Amara had been in there since dawn with the editor, a small fierce woman named Okonjo who read documents the way Theo read water—for what moved beneath the surface. Every few minutes Amara came to the window, pressed her palm flat against the glass, and looked at Theo until Theo raised a hand. Then she went back to work.

The chest sat on Okonjo's desk, empty now, its Vessanian seal photographed a hundred times over. The documents were being set in type. Names. Theo kept thinking about that. Not numbers, not *an incident in the eastern channels, thirty-one years past*—but names. *Ilse Maroon, age six. Dario Sen, age twenty-two.* She'd heard Amara say her brother's name aloud only once, standing over the typesetter's tray, and she'd said it the way you lower something heavy into water: carefully, all the way down, no splashing.

Theo had spent eleven years making herself unnameable. No lease signed under her own hand. No debts except the one—and even that she'd owed to a man who dealt in aliases. She had learned to walk through ports like current through mangroves, felt but never seen.

And now her name was going into print. *Theodore Oyelaran, salvage diver, recovered the evidence.* Okonjo had asked her twice if she wanted it that way. Twice Theo had said yes, and both times the word had tasted strange in her mouth, like drinking from a cup that wasn't hers.

She stepped off the porch into the mud. It sucked at her boots, greedy, familiar. Down at the harbor wall, the navy launch was tied where it had been since yesterday morning, when Captain Reyes had been walked down its gangplank in handcuffs, his whites gray with river silt, his face doing something complicated that Theo chose not to look at too long. Two of his officers had come quietly. One had wept. The third—the young one, Ferro, who had fired on them in the shallows and missed on purpose, she was almost sure of it now—had handed over his sidearm with a salute so crisp it looked like an apology.

*He knew your father,* Reyes had told her, back at the fueling station, when he thought he was buying her. *I let him die. That's the man you're protecting yourself for.*

She had carried those sentences for nine days like ballast in her chest. This morning, watching him marched past the fish market while women sold smoked mullet ten feet away and didn't even turn their heads, the ballast had shifted. Not gone. Just—settled. Ballast you could sail with.

A boy ran past her through the mud, chasing the ball, and splashed her to the knee. She laughed. It surprised her, the sound of it, rusty as an old winch.

* * *

**Amara**

Amara found the broker's room at the Salt Anchor Hotel empty except for a ring of keys and a smell of cheap tobacco already going stale.

The landlady, a narrow woman with ink-stained fingers, shrugged when Amara asked. "Paid up three days ago. Left before dawn, before the rain even quit. Didn't take the newspaper." She nodded at the table by the window, where yesterday's edition lay folded open to page one: *SCUTTLED: THE TRUTH OF THE *VESSEL OF GRACE*, THIRTY-ONE YEARS SILENT.*

So he'd seen it. Amara picked up the paper and imagined him reading it here, alone, before first light—reading the names, reading the inquiry board's announcement, reading the line about Captain Elias Reyes relieved of command and remanded to naval justice. Reading, perhaps, the small item at the bottom: *the whereabouts of the salvage broker known as "Marrow" remain unknown; authorities believe he left Port Calloway by water.*

Gone, then. Not caught, not punished. Amara had wanted, for one hot hour after they'd reached the press office with the river swallowing their boat behind them, to see him answer for selling them out at the dock—for the fire, for the smugglers, for every mile of running water between there and here. But the flood had taken what it took, and Marrow had simply poured himself into some other channel, the way water does. Maybe that was its own punishment, she thought, setting the paper down. To spend your whole life moving and belong nowhere. She knew enough about that kind of thirst to recognize it.

Outside, the town was drying itself in strips. Laundry appeared on lines like flags of surrender to the sun. On the harbor road, carpenters were already prying the storm shutters off Okonjo's office, and someone had chalked a crude drawing of a chest on the wall beside the door with the words *THE RIVER REMEMBERS* underneath. Someone else had added a spray of flowers in blue chalk. Amara stood looking at it for a long time.

Her brother had died because men decided thirty-one refugees were worth less than their careers. Now his name was on a hundred walls and a thousand lips, and inquiry commissioners were arriving on the morning packet with leather cases and grave faces, and none of it brought Dario back, and all of it mattered anyway. She had learned that distinction somewhere in the drowned village, reading his last letters aloud to a woman who flinched at the word *drowning* and wouldn't say why. All of it mattered anyway.

She bought two cups of ginger tea from the cart on the corner and went to find Theo.

* * *

**Theo**

Theo was on the dock, of course. Where else. She'd rented a slip for the night—a real rental, under her real name, the transaction clerk blinking at her like she'd spoken in tongues—and she stood on the planks looking down at the little boat she'd pulled off the bottom of the river two days ago.

It was a loss. Anyone could see that. The hull was stove in along the port side where the blockade's wake had driven them onto the mined shallows' edge; the engine had swallowed half the Calloway channel; the cabin smelled permanently of bilge and gun smoke. She'd bought her for almost nothing eight years ago, in a port whose name Theo had deliberately forgotten, and the boat had never had a name either. That had seemed right, once. Things with names could be traced. Things with names could be taken away.

Amara's footsteps came down the dock behind her, unhurried, and Theo didn't turn around. She knew the rhythm of them now the way she knew tide tables.

"Tea," Amara said, handing her a cup. "The landlady says Marrow skipped out before dawn."

"I figured." Theo wrapped her hands around the heat. "Men like that don't get arrested. They just relocate."

"You're not sorry?"

"Honestly?" Theo watched the light move on the water. "I don't have room to be. I used everything I had being angry at Reyes." She turned then, and found Amara watching her with that cartographer's patience, the look of someone measuring a coastline they intended to keep. "Debt's cleared, though. Okonjo paid it off this morning—said recovering the documents constituted services rendered. Marrow can bill a ghost."

"So you're free," Amara said. "Completely."

"Yeah." Theo laughed, short and without much humor in it. "Turns out free is terrifying. I had a plan, you know. Eleven years I've had the same plan. Get paid, get gone, don't leave a wake. There's a freighter out of Calloway Thursday. I checked the schedule this morning like it was a reflex." She looked down at the ruined boat. "I even packed."

Amara followed her gaze to the deck, where Theo's duffel sat by the wheel, zipped, ready. Next to it, impossible and deliberate, lay the compass.

She'd bought it back. Or rather, the pilot—old Sefu, who'd threaded them through the mines with his teeth gritted and his hands steady—had taken her father's compass as payment and then, three days later, pressed it back into her hands at the harbor gate and refused to discuss it. *"You pointed true,"* he'd said. *"Instrument like that deserves to keep working."* So it sat there on the deck, brass worn to gold at the edges, its needle settling toward north with the calm of something that had survived worse than she had.

"That's the plan?" Amara asked quietly. "Thursday's freighter."

"That's always the plan."

"And how does it feel?"

Theo opened her mouth to give the practiced answer—*fine, it always feels fine*—and found she couldn't. The rain had washed the practiced answers out of her along with everything else.

"It feels like drowning," she said finally. "But the good kind. The kind where you decide to come up."

Amara set her tea down on the piling. From inside her coat she drew a folded sheet of paper, soft at the creases, and held it out. Theo took it. A chart—hand-drawn, ink faded to brown, the paper warped where river water had touched it years ago. She recognized the style immediately. The same sure lines as the survey charts Amara had used to thread them through the mangroves, the same tiny precise lettering. But this channel wasn't on any of those. It ran northwest from the flooded villages, through country marked on official maps as *impassable marsh*, and along its length, in that careful hand, someone had written depth soundings, turning points, a note at the head of it:

*D. Sen's route. Nobody's supposed to know. If you're reading this, keep it that way—or finish it.*

"He drew it the year before he died," Amara said. "He was going to map it properly. Publish it. Open the villages to the trade routes so they'd never be invisible again—never be places where things could happen and nobody would ever hear." Her voice stayed level, but her thumb moved over the paper's edge, back and forth, back and forth. "He never got the chance. And I've been carrying this chart for eleven years like it was a grave marker. I'm tired of it being a grave marker, Theo. I want it to be a map."

The rain-washed air smelled of mud and diesel and ginger tea and, faintly, of green things growing back. Out past the breakwater, the sea lay flat and enormous, holding all its weather in reserve.

"I'm leaving Thursday," Theo said. It came out wrong. It came out like a question.

"The commission wants the channel surveyed before the next monsoon," Amara went on, as if Theo hadn't spoken, or rather as if she had, and the answer were already known. "Officially. With funding. They need divers who can work black water and a navigator nobody's bribed yet." She looked up, and her eyes were dry and bright and absolutely certain. "I need a partner. I'm asking you to stay. Not hide with me. Stay. Be visible. Be *named*. Help me finish my brother's map."

Theo looked at the compass. The needle trembled once, a tiny shiver, and settled.

For eleven years, every compass she'd owned had pointed her away—out of debt, out of sight, out of other people's histories. Her father's compass had pointed her into the worst water of her life, and somehow that had been the first time it had ever pointed anywhere true.

She thought about the freighter, Thursday, anonymous bunk, nameless wake. She thought about a channel full of drowned villages slowly giving their dead back to the light. She thought about a boat with no name, stove-in and sunk and hauled up again, which was, when she really looked at it, the story of her entire life—and which deserved, she realized suddenly, achingly, a name.

"Okay," Theo said.

Amara blinked. "Okay?"

"Okay. I'll stay." She crouched, laid her palm flat on the ruined gunwale, and felt the old wood take her weight without complaint. "But she needs a new hull, a new engine, and—" she glanced up, and the grin that broke across her face felt like a hatch opening after years underwater, letting in all that unbearable air and light— "a name. Boats that carry truth ought to have one. I'm thinking we christen her after a cartographer."

Amara laughed—a real laugh, startled out of her—and covered her brother's chart with both hands as the wind came off the sea, warm now, smelling of the dry season coming.

"*Dario,*" she said. "Yes. She'll be the *Dario*."

Down the dock, the harbor bell rang the change of tide. The needle swung, steadied, and held—north, and home, and forward, all at once.
