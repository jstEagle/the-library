## 1. Miles, Not Years

Seven years, four months, and eleven days since Kanchen Gorge. I don't count the years anymore. I count the miles.

Years are a liar's arithmetic. They make it sound like time is doing something for you — healing, softening, filing the edges down. Years promise that one morning you'll wake up and the thing will have become a story instead of a wound. But miles are honest. A mile is a mile whether you walk it in grief or in joy, and your body keeps the ledger regardless of what your mouth says about being fine.

So: seven years of miles. Enough to circle the earth twice if you walked them in a line, which I haven't, because lines imply destinations and I gave up on those around the same time I gave up on sleeping through the night. Instead I've walked loops. Treks out of Rungli and back to Rungli, low-season work for low-season wages, guiding tourists who want their photographs taken looking contemplative at four thousand meters and want their tea hot at every stop between.

The monsoon was three weeks out when the letter came, and until then the season had been going the way all my seasons go: competently, forgettably, at a pace designed not to disturb anything buried.

* * *

Rungli sits at the end of the road like an afterthought someone forgot to erase. One street, mostly, cobbled and crooked, running downhill from the bus depot to the river. A tea house with a roof that leaks in exactly the right place so the drips fall into a brass pot instead of on anyone's head — old Mrs. Sen arranged that years ago and considers it her life's masterpiece. A supply shop whose owner, Bikram, stocks climbing rope he hasn't sold a length of in seven years because the only person in town who buys rope stopped buying rope. Two guesthouses. A police post where the constable sleeps more than he patrols. And above it all, past the terraces and the prayer flags gone grey from weather, the Khumbal Range standing in its own weather system like a jury that never adjourns.

I'd been back in town two days, having delivered a party of six German birdwatchers safely down from the Dzongla ridge, where they had seen, by their delighted count, forty-one species and zero disasters. That's my specialty now, if you can call it a specialty: the guided absence of catastrophe. I am very good at nothing happening.

I was sitting in Mrs. Sen's tea house doing what I do on rest days, which is drinking tea I didn't taste and reading a paperback I wasn't reading, when Bikram came in holding an envelope the way you'd hold something that might be venomous.

"This came for you," he said. "From the district office."

I looked at the envelope. Official buff paper, stamped, addressed in typed letters — *Hazel Amberdale, care of Sen's Tea House, Rungli.* Nobody sends me official mail. Official mail goes to people with addresses, reputations, futures. Official mail goes to people whose names aren't shorthand for a cautionary tale told to new guides over stove fires: *don't end up like Amberdale.*

"Did they say what it's about?"

"They said deliver it." Bikram set it down next to my cup and retreated a respectful distance, the way you retreat from a dog of uncertain temperament. "You're not in trouble, are you?"

"I'm always in trouble," I said. "It's just usually theoretical."

He laughed, uncertainly, and went back to his shop. Mrs. Sen pretended to wipe down tables near mine, which is her way of eavesdropping without technically committing a crime. The whole street was pretending not to watch me through the window. In a town this size, an envelope from the district office is better entertainment than television.

I let it sit while I finished my tea. This is a trick I learned in the mountains: when something frightens you, make it wait. Fear feeds on urgency. Give it ten minutes and it shrinks to a manageable size, or at least to a size you can carry.

Then I picked up the envelope, turned it over, and broke the seal.

Inside: one page, typed, on ministry letterhead. The Ministry of Home Affairs, Border Districts Division. It said:

*Mrs. H. Amberdale — You are requested to present yourself at the Office of the District Commissioner, Rungli, at 10:00 hours on Thursday the 14th, regarding a matter of some urgency concerning your professional qualifications. Failure to appear may result in administrative action inconsistent with your interests.*

Signed, in ink that had bled slightly: **D. Rathore, Joint Secretary.**

There was no phone number. There was no "regrets." There was no explanation of what professional qualifications of mine could possibly interest a Joint Secretary, since as far as the government was concerned I had none — they'd seen to that personally, seven years ago, when they took my guide's license, my mountain permits, and my good name in one tidy administrative motion and distributed the pieces among the newspapers.

*Administrative action inconsistent with your interests.* I read that sentence four times. It has a certain poetry, if you enjoy the poetry of threat. It means: we know things about you. It means: come, and we'll tell you which lever we're holding.

Mrs. Sen drifted closer with her cloth. "Bad news?"

"Unknown news."

"That's worse." She refilled my cup unasked. "In my experience, known bad news you can fight. Unknown news fights you first."

"In your experience," I said, "what does the district office want with a trekking guide?"

She looked at me for a long moment over the rim of her own cup. Mrs. Sen is seventy-something and has survived three governments, two husbands, and one flood, and she has the eyes of a woman who has watched a lot of men come down off that mountain wrong.

"Nothing good," she said. "But then, you knew that before you opened it."

* * *

I spent the two days between the letter and the appointment doing what I always do when the past reaches for me: walking.

Not guiding — there was no group, no route, no purpose anyone could invoice. Just walking. Out past the terraces at dawn, up the old pilgrim track toward the ridge, far enough to see the whole valley laid out below me and the white wall of the Khumbal beyond it. I measured things as I walked, the way I always do. The distance from Rungli to the ridge: nine kilometers. From the ridge to the last village under the high country, Thangmo: another fourteen. From Thangmo to the glaciers where nobody who valued their license — and I no longer had one to lose — would go without reason: thirty-some, depending on the route, most of it ugly.

And behind all of it, always, the number underneath the numbers: the gorge. Eleven kilometers of river canyon where seven years ago I made a decision in ninety seconds of white water and screaming wind, and six people paid for it, and I've been paying installments ever since.

People think disgrace is a single event. It isn't. It's a climate. It rains on everything. When I walked into Bikram's shop these days, he still hesitated half a beat before speaking to me, some muscle memory of the year when my face was on the front pages under the word NEGLIGENT. When new guides passed me on the trail they looked away, the way you look away from an accident site. Even the mountain itself seemed to have opinions; permits were denied, routes were closed for maintenance whenever my name appeared on an application, small bureaucratic doors swinging shut with a sound too quiet for anyone else to hear.

Here is what actually happened in the gorge, for the record, though there is no record anymore except mine: the weather turned early. The forecast said two days. We got twenty minutes. A client named Aldous Wren — rich, impatient, insistent on crossing the high bridge before the storm rather than camping short — started across against my explicit instruction, and the anchor failed, and I went after him onto a bridge that was already dying, and I got him to the far side, and two others behind him did not make it, and the ones who did make it made it because I called the retreat that cost us the equipment and nearly cost us the season. Four dead. Or five, depending which newspaper you bought. The inquiry took eleven weeks and concluded that the guide had permitted the crossing. Which was true in the sense that I had planned the route. It was false in the sense that I had ordered everyone to wait, and one man's impatience had outweighed my authority, and dead men's families needed somewhere to put their grief, and a disgraced foreign-born guide with no family and no lawyer was a convenient shelf.

Aldous Wren himself wrote a letter in my defense. It arrived a year later, after the verdicts were settled and my name was already mud. By then the truth was an inconvenience, and inconvenient truths get filed.

So yes. I measure in miles. Because miles don't ask what you're atoning for. Miles only ask that you keep putting one foot past the other, and that much, even now, I can do.

* * *

Thursday came grey and low, cloud sitting on the ridgelines like a lid. I put on the clean shirt — the one without holes — and walked down the street at nine-thirty, taking my time, letting the town watch me go. Word travels fast in Rungli; by the time I reached the district office, I'd bet money that Bikram had already told Mrs. Sen, who had already told the constable, who was probably placing a small wager with himself on whether I'd come back out free.

The district office was a colonial leftover, whitewashed once and meaning to be again, with a fan turning slowly under the ceiling and a clerk behind a desk who looked at me the way clerks look at people who arrive in boots.

"Amberdale," I said. "Ten o'clock."

"He's expecting you." A pause, weighted. "Upstairs. Second door."

The stairs smelled of dust and old paper and the ghost of cigarettes. The second door was heavy wood, half-glazed, with a brass plate: JOINT SECRETARY, BORDER DISTRICTS. Through the glass, a silhouette stood at the window with its hands clasped behind its back, looking north toward the mountains, which struck me as either a coincidence or a rehearsal.

I knocked.

"Come in, Mrs. Amberdale." The voice carried easily through the door, pitched to be heard, warm in the way a snare is warm if you're the kind of animal who appreciates craftsmanship. "It's Ms., I should say. Forgive me. Do come in."

The office was neat in the way of rooms that are used for decisions rather than work. A desk with nothing on it but a folder, a telephone, and a brass lamp. Maps on the walls — survey maps, contour-shaded, the Khumbal Range rendered in blue ink like veins. And the man from the window, turning now to greet me: mid-forties, silver coming in at the temples precisely enough to suggest it was managed, a suit too good for the altitude, and eyes that did the thing I distrust most in a man's eyes, which is to say they smiled independently of the rest of his face.

"Dev Rathore," he said, extending a hand. "Joint Secretary. Thank you for coming. I confess I wasn't sure you would."

"You threatened me in writing. I find threats focus the mind."

"An invitation," he corrected gently, "with emphasis." He gestured to the chair across the desk and sat down himself, folding his hands on the blotter beside the folder. Up close he smelled of some cologne that had no business surviving the journey to Rungli, something expensive and urban, a smell of committee rooms and air conditioning. "May I say, you're shorter than your reputation."

"My reputation is mostly altitude."

That got a real smile, briefly, before the machinery resumed. He opened the folder — unhurried, deliberate, a man who had rehearsed this — and turned it around so I could see the photograph clipped inside.

I looked. And the bottom dropped out of my stomach in that slow, silent way it does, the way it did once before, over a gorge, in twenty minutes of weather that was supposed to take two days.

Because I knew that campsite. Anyone who'd worked the Khumbal would know it: the bench of flat ground below the seracs on the Meridian Glacier, sheltered from the north wind, water within fifty meters. I'd camped there myself, years ago, back when I still had a license and a future. Tents pitched neatly in a row. Stoves set out. Gear racked with the care of disciplined professionals.

And not a soul in any of the photographs. No tracks leaving. No disturbance. Boots, I could see even in the grainy print, lined up outside the tent doors — as if six experienced surveyors had stepped out of their footwear in the middle of the highest icefield in the range and walked away barefoot into the cold.

"The Meridian Survey," Rathore said quietly. "Six members. Government contracted. Last radio contact nineteen days ago. A patrol flew over yesterday." He tapped the photograph. "This is what they found. Everything intact. Everyone gone."

I kept my eyes on the picture, because looking at him would have meant showing him what my face was doing. "People don't vanish from a glacier."

"No," he agreed. "They don't. Which is why I need someone who knows that country intimately, doesn't trust easy answers, and has—" the smile again, brief and surgical, "—no reputation left to protect. Someone with nothing to lose investigates thoroughly. Someone with something to lose gets careless."

"And if I refuse?"

He leaned back, laced his fingers over his vest, and regarded me with what I imagine he considered kindness.

"Ms. Amberdale. There is an extradition request sitting in a drawer in the capital with your name on it. Old charges. The gorge. It has been dormant for seven years because it suited certain people to let it lie. Dormant things, however, can be woken." He spread his hands, apologetic. "So. You may walk out of here and take your chances with a courtroom in a jurisdiction that has never once been kind to you. Or you may accept a temporary field commission, lead a small recovery team into the upper Khumbal, and find out what happened to six of our best people. Do that, and the request stays asleep. Permanently."

Outside the window, the clouds had lifted just enough to show the high peaks, white and indifferent, keeping whatever counsel they keep.

I thought about miles. About how many I'd walked to stay clear of exactly this — government business, official attention, the machinery that had already chewed me up once and spat out the pieces labeled guilty. Every instinct I owned, every scar-hardened reflex, said stand up, walk out, keep counting.

But I looked at the photograph again. At the tents. At the boots, lined up so neatly outside the doors, pointing away from the glacier like six small compass needles all indicating the same direction.

Nobody walks uphill barefoot. Nobody leaves their boots on a glacier unless something made them.

And beneath the fear, in the place where I keep the thing that got me onto mountains in the first place — the thing no inquiry ever managed to confiscate — something stirred and said: *those are my people out there. Guides, climbers, surveyors. People who belong to the ice. If they're dead, they deserve to be found. If they're alive, they deserve better than a patrol flyover and a folder.*

Rathore watched me weigh it, patient as a fisherman.

"What team?" I asked finally. "How many? Who picks them?"

"I do, largely. You'll have final say on route and safety — within reason. You leave Monday." He slid a pen across the desk, then a single sheet: a contract, a field commission, a clause releasing the ministry from liability, another clause binding me to discretion. Of course there was a discretion clause. There is always a discretion clause.

"One condition of mine," I said.

His eyebrows invited it.

"When we find them — whatever state we find them in — their families hear it from the team, not from a press office. That's non-negotiable."

Something flickered behind the smiling eyes — annoyance, maybe, filed instantly away — and then he nodded, gracious as a host. "Done."

I signed. My hand was steady, which surprised me. Seven years of practice at keeping my hands steady while everything else shakes.

"Wonderful," Rathore said, reclaiming the paper, and for just a moment the warmth dropped out of his voice entirely, the way a mask drops, and what was underneath was neither kind nor cruel but simply calculating, a scale finding its balance. "Welcome back to government service, Ms. Amberdale. I have a feeling this is the beginning of a very productive relationship."

Later — much later, on a glacier, with blood on the ice and the truth in my hands — I would remember that sentence, and the way the light sat on the maps behind him, and understand that I hadn't signed a contract at all.

I'd signed an invitation.

And the mountain had already accepted it.
