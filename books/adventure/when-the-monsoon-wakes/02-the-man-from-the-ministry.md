## 2. The Man from the Ministry

The district office in Rungli smelled of wet paper and old ambition, which is to say it smelled exactly like every government building I'd ever been thrown out of.

I arrived at nine sharp because the letter said nine, and because I had learned the hard way that when a bureaucracy summons you, punctuality is the only dignity they'll let you keep. The letter itself had been a masterpiece of menace-by-politeness. Heavy cream stock, official seal, and language so carefully neutral it could have been an invitation to a wedding or a subpoena. *Your presence is requested at the District Office, Rungli, on the morning of the 14th, regarding matters pertaining to your professional history.*

My professional history. That's one way to describe the thing that ended my career, killed two people, and taught me that mountains keep receipts.

I sat on a wooden bench outside the Deputy Director's suite for forty minutes, watching a ceiling fan turn with the enthusiasm of a man being forced to attend his own retirement party. A clerk with ink-stained fingers glanced at me twice, decided I was nobody, and went back to stamping things. There is a particular kind of invisibility that comes with disgrace — people look straight through you, as if failure were a kind of camouflage. Seven years of practice had made me very good at it.

At nine-forty the inner door opened and a man stepped out who was not what I expected.

I don't know what I expected. Some bloated district officer, probably, all sweat rings and self-importance. Instead: Dev Rathore. Mid-forties, grey at the temples in a way that looked earned rather than inherited, dressed in civilian clothes cut well enough to suggest he considered uniforms beneath him but authority very much not. He had the lean, unhurried build of someone who'd once done fieldwork and now supervised people who did it for him. His eyes did a quick inventory of me — boots, hands, posture — the way mine would have assessed a client's fitness on day one. Professional courtesy between predators.

"Hazel Amberdale." He said it like he was confirming a specimen label. "Thank you for coming."

"You didn't leave me much choice."

"I always leave people choice." He smiled without any warmth reaching the upper floors. "It's just more efficient when they pick the right one. Come in."

* * *

His office was too tidy for a working man and too bare for an honest one. No photographs. No family. A map of the Khumbal Range hung behind the desk, and I noticed — because you can't spend your life reading terrain and then stop — that someone had marked a section of the high glacier with three small red pins, clustered close together like wounds.

He poured tea without asking if I wanted it. Good tea, which told me something. Men who offer bad tea want something small. Men who offer good tea are softening you up for something large.

"Do you know why you're here?"

"Your letter was admirably vague."

"The Meridian Survey." He set a folder on the desk but kept his hand flat on it, the way a card player rests on his ace. "A six-person scientific team, government-chartered. They went into the Khumbal high country eleven weeks ago to conduct glaciological work. They were due out five weeks ago." He paused. "They haven't come out."

"And that's my problem because—"

"Because we're assembling a recovery party. Small, fast, before the monsoon closes the passes. And because you know that mountain better than anyone alive, including," he added mildly, "the people who blacklisted you."

I laughed. It came out wrong — too short, too hard. "You've read my file, Mr. Rathore. Whatever it says, it doesn't say *hire her.*"

"It says you made a judgment call at nineteen thousand feet that cost two lives. It also says that in eleven years prior, you brought thirty-one clients home alive through weather that grounded army helicopters." He opened the folder at last and turned it toward me. "The ministry doesn't hire saints, Ms. Amberdale. It hires competence. Saints are expensive and they ask questions."

The photos were the first thing I saw, and they were wrong in a way that took my breath and held it.

Six dome tents, pitched in a neat crescent on a moraine shelf above the icefall. Guy lines taut. Snow stakes driven properly — properly, by someone who knew their trade. Cook shelter up, table rigged between two crates. A stove sat in the open, kettle beside it. Gear laid out on ground cloths with the neatness of people who expected to come back for it.

No snow inside the tents. No collapse. No scatter. No tracks leading away — none anywhere, and this was the part that made the back of my neck prickle, because that photo had been taken, according to the timestamp, nine days after the team's last radio contact. Nine days of wind and weather, and still the camp looked like everyone had just stepped away to look at something.

"No tracks," I said.

"No tracks."

"Nine days after last contact, there should be drift against the tent walls. Wind-scour on the lee side. Something."

"There isn't." He watched me over the rim of his cup. "That's why you're here. You read ground the way other people read print. Tell me what you see."

I told him. Tents abandoned deliberately, not lost. Boots — I could see them, lined under the tent flaps. People don't walk out of a high camp without their boots. Not alive, anyway. Not together. "Either they left on foot in conditions I can't explain," I said, "or something happened that didn't involve walking out at all."

"That's the phrase our own analysts used. 'Didn't involve walking out.' Very reassuring." He closed the folder. "Here is the arrangement. You guide a recovery party of four plus yourself into the high Khumbal. You locate the Meridian Survey or determine what became of them. You bring back anything recoverable — equipment, records, remains. In exchange, the ministry revisits your file. Licenses reinstated. The incident re-examined. Your name cleared."

The words landed in me like a rope tossed to a drowning woman, and I hated how fast my hands reached for it. Seven years. Seven years of low-season treks and tourist walks and measuring my life in distances walked because there was nothing else left to count. My name. My name was the only thing I'd ever actually owned, and they'd taken it, and here was a stranger offering to sell it back.

"What's the catch?" I asked, because there is always a catch, and a guide who doesn't look for it ends up as a cautionary tale.

"No catch. A condition." He folded his hands. "Discretion. What the survey found or failed to find is a matter of state interest. Your party will include a ministry liaison. Everything that comes down that mountain comes through official channels first. You are a guide, Ms. Amberdale, not an investigator. Find them. Bring back what you're told to bring back. Nothing more."

"And if I say no?"

Rathore leaned back, and for the first time he looked genuinely regretful, which frightened me more than anything else in the room.

"Then tomorrow morning a courier delivers documents to the police post here in Rungli. Extradition papers. The charges from the Kanchen incident were never formally resolved — your home country has been content to let them sleep as long as you stayed quiet and far away. But files wake up, Ms. Amberdale. All it takes is someone motivated to knock the dust off." He spread his hands, a gesture of terrible reasonableness. "You'd be on a plane within the month. There's a cell waiting where you used to live that has your name on it. Has had, apparently, for seven years."

The room got very quiet. The fan turned. Somewhere below us, Rungli went about its morning — porters loading, prayer flags snapping, the ordinary world carrying on without knowing that in a bare office above it, a woman's whole remaining life was being weighed on a scale she couldn't see.

I thought about running. Of course I did. I'd spent seven years learning the border towns, the back trails, the ways a person disappears. But Rathore watched me think it, and I saw in his face that he'd already priced that option in. He wasn't a man who offered deals he hadn't already won.

"You need me," I said. "Whatever's up there scared six trained people enough that they walked out without their boots. You don't send a liaison and three hires into that. You send me, because I'm the best, and because I'm expendable, and because I'm desperate enough to say yes." I met his eyes. "How am I doing?"

For a moment — just a moment — something flickered behind his expression. Not admiration. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man seeing a tool that understands it's a tool.

"Adequately," he said. "Sign here."

* * *

The pen was heavy and cold and it moved across the paper like it knew the way.

I signed. Of course I signed. Trapped women sign; it's what makes them useful. But as I wrote my name — my ruined, borrowed-back name — I made myself a promise in the silence underneath the scratch of the nib: *you get to use me once, Rathore. One trip. And whatever's on that mountain, whatever your red pins are hiding, I'm going to see it with my own eyes. Not through your channels. Mine.*

He shook my hand at the door. His grip was dry and precise, calibrated, like everything else about him.

"Your team assembles Thursday. Liaison named Priya Nair — she'll handle logistics. A surveyor, Tomas Brandt. A medic, Kwan. And a porter, Dorje, who knows the approaches better than the maps do." He almost smiled. "Try to keep them alive, Ms. Amberdale. It reflects poorly on everyone when guides lose people."

The door closed behind me and I stood in the corridor breathing paper-smelling air, and I realized my hands were shaking — not from fear, exactly. From the specific vertigo of a climber who has just checked her anchor, tested the rope, felt it hold —

and heard, deep in the hardware, a faint and unmistakable creak.

Outside, the sky over the Khumbal was clear and blue and beautiful, the way skies are right before they bury you.
