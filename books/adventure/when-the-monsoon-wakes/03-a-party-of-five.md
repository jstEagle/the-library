## 3. A Party of Five

The district office had a back room, and the back room had a table, and around the table they'd arranged my future like evidence at an inquest.

Rathore made the introductions himself, which told me something. Men who trust their people don't hover. He stood by the window with his hands folded behind his back while he gestured each of them forward like a man unveiling statues he wasn't entirely proud of.

"Hazel Amberdale," he said, "this is your team."

*My* team. As if I'd chosen them. As if any of this had been a choice.

The first one up was young — mid-twenties, maybe, with the kind of face that hadn't yet learned to keep secrets behind it. He shook my hand with both of his, which is either sincerity or training, and in his case I suspected both.

"Tomas Reyes," he said. "I'm — well, technically I'm with the Cartographic Division, but really I'm here for the survey reconstruction. The Meridian team's route data, their instruments, anything we can recover. It's a remarkable opportunity, actually. Their last triangulation figures were—"

"Tomas." Rathore said it quietly, and Tomas stopped as if someone had cut his string.

"Surveyor," I said. "Got it."

He was earnest the way a puppy is earnest, all feet and enthusiasm, and I filed him under *liability* with a small asterisk beside it — because underneath the babble there were good hands. I'd noticed his grip. Calloused in the right places. A man who'd actually handled rope recently, whatever his title said.

The second introduction was a woman who did not stand. She sat at the far end of the table with a leather folder squared to its edge and looked at me the way customs officers look at luggage.

"Priya Nair," she said. "Ministry liaison. I'll be coordinating communications and logistics."

"Liaison," I repeated. In seven years of walking away from things, I'd learned that *liaison* is what you call a handler when the paperwork needs to look polite. Her folder was too thick for logistics. Logistics fits on one page: food, fuel, dates. Hers had tabs.

"Ms. Amberdale." A nod, nothing wasted. "I've read your file."

"I'm sure you have."

"All of it."

"So have I," I said. "It makes better reading than most."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth — not quite a smile, more like the place where a smile had been demolished and the rubble left standing. She went back to her folder. I put her under *dangerous*, and then, because I try to be fair, under *dangerous to whom?*

The third man was introduced without ceremony because he clearly didn't believe in it. Big through the shoulders, weathered past guessing, with hands that looked like they'd been carved from something that grew on the north side of mountains. He nodded once when Rathore said his name — Dorje — and that was the whole of it.

"Dorje will handle carrying and high-altitude logistics," Rathore said. "He knows the Khumbal better than anyone living."

"Better than anyone living," I echoed. "That's a specific way to phrase it."

Dorje looked at me. His eyes were the color of old glacier ice, and about as forthcoming. But something passed across his face when Rathore said it — not fear exactly. Recognition. The look of a man hearing a truth spoken aloud that everyone had agreed to leave buried.

"He means it as a compliment," Priya said smoothly.

"I don't think he means anything as a compliment," I said, and Dorje's mouth did something that in a less carved face might have been amusement.

The fourth one came in late, which I liked immediately, because men who arrive on their own schedule are usually men who know why they're somewhere. He was compact, grey at the temples, moving with the particular economy of someone who'd spent years learning how little motion a body actually needs. He stuck out a hand.

"Kwan. Medic."

"Just Kwan?"

"Just Kwan."

"Ex-army?" I asked, because the way he scanned the room's exits while shaking my hand answered the question before I could.

"Ex-something," he said. "You'll be glad of it above the snowline."

I believed him. That was the trouble with this whole arrangement — I kept believing individual pieces of it, and the pieces refused to fit together.

* * *

Rathore dismissed the others — "logistics briefing, Ms. Nair will coordinate" — and kept me behind, which surprised no one, least of all me. When the door closed he didn't sit. He stood looking at the map on the wall, the Khumbal rendered in contour lines like fingerprints pressed into paper.

"You noticed," he said.

"That your surveyor talks too much, your liaison reads files for pleasure, your porter thinks your phrasing is funny, and your medic counts doors? Yes. I notice things. It was in my file."

"The team is balanced."

"The team is four people with four separate reasons for being there, none of which they'll say out loud." I leaned against the table. "Tomas wants the science. Fine. Kwan wants — what? Hazard pay? Something to do with his hands? And your liaison has a folder with tabs, Dev. Tabs are where honesty goes to die."

"And Dorje?"

"Dorje I haven't figured out. Which worries me more than the rest of it."

For a moment he almost smiled, and for a moment I almost remembered why I'd once thought men like him were worth trusting. Then it closed over, smooth as a frozen pool.

"You have eleven days before the monsoon fronts move in," he said. "After that, the range closes until September. If the Meridian team is alive up there, eleven days is the difference between a rescue and a recovery. You'll want cohesion, not suspicion."

"Cohesion comes from shared purpose. You've given me five purposes and one map."

"You have the map," he agreed. "That should be enough."

It was the closest thing to a confession he ever made, though I didn't understand it fully until much later. He handed me the route plan — the Meridian team's last known position marked with a red pin, neat as a funeral announcement — and I took it, and we both pretended this was a normal transaction between professionals.

"One more thing," he said, as I reached the door. "Whatever you find up there, you report to Ms. Nair first. Radio protocol. Standard chain of command."

"Of course," I said.

I want it noted, for whatever my word is worth these days, that I intended to keep it. Right up until keeping it became impossible.

* * *

We staged out of a gear shed behind the district office, and if you want to know a person, watch them pack.

Tomas packed like a student cramming for an exam — everything labeled, everything doubled, a first-aid kit he clearly hoped someone else would use. He also packed a camera with film, actual film, which made me like him despite myself. People who still shoot film believe evidence matters.

Priya packed like she was furnishing a small apartment: order, redundancy, a radio set wrapped in oilcloth that she handled the way other people handle infants. She caught me watching and said, "Communications are the mission, Ms. Amberdale. Without them, we're just tourists with opinions."

"We're already tourists with opinions," I said. "The radios just make it official."

Kwan packed light and mean. Medical kit, bivouac kit, a stove he'd modified so thoroughly it barely resembled a stove anymore. And one item I pretended not to see, wrapped in a sock at the bottom of his rucksack, because pretending not to see things is a professional skill of mine and I like to stay in practice.

Dorje arrived last, carrying everything he owned, which appeared to be very little, and began checking the party's loads without asking anyone's permission. He redistributed weight with the silent authority of a tide. Tomas started to protest about his tent placement, got halfway into the sentence, and then met Dorje's eyes and repacked his own bag.

"There," Dorje said, when it was done. It was the longest speech he gave all day.

By late afternoon we were loaded, and by early evening we were on the trail north out of Rungli, five people roped together by nothing but a shared direction and the government's money. The valley smelled of woodsmoke and wet stone. Somewhere above us, the Khumbal sat under its clouds, keeping whatever it keeps.

I walked point because nobody argued, which told me they'd all read my file too.

* * *

The first camp was unremarkable, which is why the evening around it stays with me. Unremarkable camps are where people show you who they are, because there's nothing else happening.

Tomas talked. That was his contribution — a running commentary on glacial moraine composition, on the Meridian Survey's published papers, on the geology of the upper valley, delivered with the breathless conviction of a man who loved his work and hadn't yet learned that love is a vulnerability. He'd studied the vanished team the way other people study saints.

"They were the best in the field," he said, hunched over the stove. "Dr. Anselm Voss ran the tightest triangulation program I've ever seen in print. And now they're just — gone. Tents up, everything intact. How does that even happen?"

"People leave camp for lots of reasons," Kwan said, not looking up from his stove.

"Together? Uphill? Leaving their boots?" Tomas shook his head. "There's a rational explanation. There always is."

"Sure," Kwan said. "Usually it's a bad one."

Priya sat slightly apart, outside the firelight's full reach, doing something quiet with her radio — checking frequencies, I assumed, or reporting in, or doing whatever liaisons do when the rest of us are being human at each other. Every so often her eyes came up and moved across the group, cataloguing. I knew the exercise. I'd done it myself in another life, counting clients' weaknesses the way a banker counts debts.

Dorje cooked. He cooked the way he did everything, without discussion, and the food was better than it had any right to be, and when Tomas thanked him he grunted in a tone that could have meant *you're welcome* or *stop talking.*

And Kwan — Kwan checked on people. Not obviously. He'd drift past each of us with some small errand, a spare peg, a question about blisters, and come away having looked at our hands, our gait, our breathing. By nightfall he'd assessed all four of us, and I'd watched him do it, and he'd watched me watching him, and neither of us mentioned it. There's a courtesy between professionals that consists entirely of mutual surveillance conducted politely.

Later, when the others had turned in, I found him cleaning his medical kit by headlamp, each instrument wiped and laid out with liturgical care.

"Ex-army," I said, sitting down across from him. "Which army?"

"The kind that doesn't get thanked." He didn't look up. "You were a guide."

"Was. Past tense is the operative part of my entire biography."

"Seven years ago." Now he looked up. "I read about it. The Ferro Col rescue."

"Then you know the summary version. Guide takes clients onto a ridge in deteriorating weather. Weather wins. One client dies, guide survives, guide gets blamed, guide loses everything. The end."

"That's not what I asked."

"No," I said. "But it's the only answer that travels well."

He considered me for a moment with those unhurried eyes. "In my experience," he said, "the people who give the traveling version are usually hiding the true one. And the true one is usually worse for them than the rumor."

"Goodnight, Kwan."

"Goodnight, Amberdale." He snapped the kit shut. "For what it's worth — the ridge story, the real one — I've carried worse down off mountains. So have you, probably. We'll see."

I lay in my bag that night listening to the wind test the tents, and I turned his words over like a stone found in a boot. Everyone on this expedition had orders they wouldn't share and a version of themselves they traveled with instead of the true one. Tomas wore his heart on the outside, which was its own kind of disguise. Priya wore a folder. Dorje wore silence. Kwan wore competence.

And I wore seven years and several thousand miles, and I was starting to suspect that Rathore had assembled this party not despite our secrets but because of them — that a team of people with something to hide is a team that can't compare notes.

Clever, if you're the sort of man who thinks in those terms.

The trouble with clever men is that they build for the weather they expect. And the Khumbal, I would learn, does not consult anyone's expectations.

* * *

We broke camp at first light under a sky the color of dirty pewter. The monsoon fronts were still days south of us, but you could feel them gathering like a debt coming due — pressure in the joints of the world, the air gone heavy and expectant.

Priya raised Rungli on the radio for the morning check-in, transmitted, and waited. Static answered. She tried again, adjusted the antenna, tried a third time, and got a whisper of carrier wave that dissolved before it resolved into words.

"Atmospherics," she said, when I raised an eyebrow. "Pre-frontal interference. It'll clear."

"It had better," I said. "Eleven days, remember. If we lose comms up there—"

"We won't lose comms, Ms. Amberdale." She wound the antenna cord with precise fingers. "The ministry builds redundancies into everything."

I believed her, then. I want that on the record too, because later, when I learned what redundancies the ministry built and into what, I needed to remember that there had been a day — one clean, cold morning on the trail, with the mountain ahead and the storm still far away — when I trusted the equipment, trusted the plan, trusted the five of us.

Trust is a rope. You don't inspect every strand when you're standing on solid ground.

We walked. The valley narrowed, the villages thinned, the trail climbed into country where the maps went shy about details. Behind us, Rungli shrank to a scatter of roofs. Ahead, the clouds around the high peaks turned slowly, like something stirring in its sleep.

Tomas caught up to me at the mid-morning halt, cheeks bright, eyes shining, and pointed at a notch in the ridgeline where the Meridian team's route crossed out of sight.

"Two more days," he said, "and we're in their footprints."

Nobody answered. Even Dorje, twenty paces back with half the world's weight on his back, had gone quiet in a way that wasn't his usual quiet — a listening quiet, a man walking toward a house where something happened once.

I checked my watch, did the arithmetic on weather and distance, and didn't like either number.

"Move," I said. "While the sky's still deciding."

And the sky, all that day and into the next, went on deciding — patient, ponderous, like a ministry official weighing what to tell you and what to bury.
