## 4. The Last Village

There are two kinds of silence in the high country, and I have spent forty years learning to tell them apart.

The first kind is the honest one — the silence of stone and wind and distance, the silence that simply means no one is there to make noise. It asks nothing of you. You can sleep inside it like a warm coat.

The second kind is the silence people make on purpose. The silence of a room that goes quiet when you walk in. The silence of a question nobody will answer. That kind has weight. That kind leans on your chest while you sleep.

Thakding was full of the second kind before we even reached the prayer flags at its edge.

I knew it from the dogs, if you want the truth. Dogs are better readers than any of us. In a village where strangers come and go with trekkers' money, the dogs trot out to inspect your boots and decide whether you're worth barking at. In Thakding, the dogs watched us from under porches with their ears flat, the way they watch when something has already gone wrong in the house behind them.

"Cheerful place," Kwan said beside me. He said it quietly, which for Kwan is the same as saying it loudly. He had been quiet since the helicopter dropped us at the roadhead, quieter than a man who carries morphine for a living should be, and I had decided not to ask him why yet. Everyone on this expedition was carrying something they hadn't unpacked. I included myself. Especially myself.

The others were strung out behind us on the trail: Tomas chattering at Priya about contour intervals or some other thing that made him happy, and Hazel Amberdale at the rear, the way she always walked, watching our backs instead of her feet. A guide who walks last is either careless or haunted. She was neither careless nor, I thought, stupid enough to be only one thing. Seven years she'd been carrying her disaster up and down these ranges, according to the talk in Rungli, and talk in Rungli is cheap but it isn't always wrong.

The village sat in its bowl of terraced barley the way villages do at the end of the trail — hunkered, practical, its roofs weighted with stones against wind that comes off the ice fields smelling of old cold. Smoke rose straight up, which meant the weather was holding, which meant we had maybe four good days and then the monsoon would arrive and close the mountains like a book.

Four days to find six missing men. The ministry's arithmetic, not mine. But then, nothing about this trip was mine except my legs and my name, and I was beginning to wonder how long I'd keep both.

* * *

We took shelter in the tea house because it was the only building with a roof beam that didn't look like an apology, and because Tsering's wife still kept it, and Tsering's wife owed me a debt older than most of the bridges on this trail.

She saw me come through the door and her face did three things very quickly: gladness, fear, and then a careful arrangement of the two into something neutral, like a woman folding a flag she isn't allowed to fly.

"Dorje-la." She poured butter tea without being asked, which is the Thakding way of saying *you are known here*, and set it in front of me without meeting my eyes, which is the Thakding way of saying *but not too much.*

"Auntie." I used the old word, the respectful one, though she was no blood kin of mine. "You look well."

"I look old," she said. "Which is well, at my age."

Tomas came in shaking rain off his jacket and asking questions the way he asked everything, all at once and slightly too loud — was there a room, could we charge batteries, had anyone come through recently, a survey team, six men, government papers? At the word *survey* the tea house went into the second kind of silence so completely that I could hear the fire ticking.

Tsering's wife looked at the floor. Her son, a boy of maybe fifteen who'd been mending a net by the window, put down the net, picked it up again, and stared at it as if it might explain itself.

"We see few government men," she said at last, to the fire rather than to Tomas.

"But someone came through," Tomas pressed. "Six of them. Weeks ago. There must have been—"

"Tomas." Hazel's voice from the doorway. Not loud. Just placed, the way she placed her feet on rotten ground. He shut his mouth.

I drank my tea and watched the room the way I watch a snow slope — not for what moves, but for what is holding itself very still. And what was holding itself still was everything. The grandmother in the corner who had stopped spinning. The boy with his net. Two men by the door who had been talking when we arrived and now had discovered a profound interest in their own boots.

In Rungli, the low town, people talked about the Khumbal freely enough — the passes, the yak routes, the year the bridge went out. But up here, closer to the mountain, the talk thinned. I had noticed it for years and filed it away the way you file away a creak in a rope bridge: noted, avoided, never examined. A man can walk around a thing for a long time. Sometimes his whole life.

Hazel sat down across from me and lowered her voice. "You felt that."

"I felt it."

"They're frightened. Of us?"

"Of the question," I said. "Maybe of who sent us to ask it."

She turned her cup slowly. She had a way of looking at things sideways that I was beginning to trust more than most people's looking-straight-at. "Rathore said the survey team radioed from somewhere above here. Last transmission. Somebody in this village heard that radio, or saw them pass, or buried their rubbish. Somebody knows."

"Somebody knows," I agreed. "And somebody has decided knowing is dangerous."

"You've been here before?"

"Many times. Long ago." I finished the tea. It was bad, scorchy, made with milk from a yak who had opinions. "My grandfather's country is higher than this. But Thakding was the last warm bed on his route too."

Something moved behind her eyes — the quick arithmetic she did with everyone's past, weighing it against whatever she carried of her own. She didn't ask. That, too, I was beginning to trust.

* * *

Evening came down blue and cold, and the village closed itself like a fist.

I went out to see to the loads, because loads don't secure themselves and because I wanted to walk through Thakding alone and listen to what it wasn't saying. The smoke smell. The barley, half-cut in the fields, which was wrong — harvest should have been finished weeks ago, unless hands were short, unless men had gone somewhere, unless people were keeping close to their houses after dark. A village leaves its fields half-cut the way a man leaves a sentence unfinished. Something interrupted him.

At the mani wall at the village's upper end, an old woman was adding a stone.

I knew her by sight if not by name — every valley has one like her, a woman so old she has outlived her own generation and become a kind of landmark, the way a lightning-split pine becomes a landmark. She wore the striped apron of a married woman decades past marriage, and her hands on the stone were steady in the way of hands that have done ten thousand thousand small work-days and no longer consult the mind about anything.

She watched me come up the path. She was not afraid of me. I had the sense she had finished being afraid of individual people sometime around the reign of a king whose name I'd only seen on old coins.

"You're taking them up," she said. Not a question. Old women don't waste breath on questions they already know the answers to.

"The foreigners, yes. Up-valley. Looking for lost men."

"Lost." She tested the word like a fruit. "The ones with the machines."

"You saw them?"

"I hear things." She placed another stone on the wall, patted it once, a small benediction. "A village hears. Six men came up in the spring, with instruments and a radio and a young officer who smiled too much. They camped above the moraine. Then the radio spoke one night, and after that—" She shrugged, a gesture that contained an entire philosophy of the universe, none of it comforting.

"After that what?"

"After that, men came from the government. Not smiling men. Men who asked what the villagers had seen, and wrote it down, and told us what we had seen, which was nothing." Her eyes came up to mine, black and bright and unhurried. "They were very clear about the nothing, Dorje, son of Yeshi. So clear that we have been practicing it ever since."

The cold went into me then, the specific cold that has nothing to do with altitude. "And what did the village see? Before it practiced?"

She didn't answer directly. Instead she reached into the folds of her dress and brought out something wrapped in a scrap of red cloth, worn soft as skin.

"My grandmother carried this over the pass the year the soldiers came," she said. "Copper. Old copper, older than the monastery, older than the names of things. It was for protection on the ice." She held it out. "Now I give it to you, because you're going where it's needed, and I'm not, and the dead don't care whose neck it hangs on."

"I can't take—"

"You can and you will, because I'm old and stubborn and you'll be here all night arguing otherwise." She pressed it into my palm and folded my fingers over it herself, her grip surprisingly strong, dry and hot as sun-baked rock. "Listen to me, son of Yeshi. Your family walked this mountain before there were maps. You know what the glacier holds."

"I know what glaciers hold," I said carefully. "Ice. Stone. Sometimes—"

"Bodies." She said it plainly, the way you say *rain*. "The mountain keeps what falls into it. And sometimes the mountain is given bodies. And sometimes those bodies are given by men who then write down that nothing happened." She leaned close. She smelled of juniper smoke and butter and the particular dust of very long life. "The ice keeps its debts, Dorje. Every one. It pays slow, but it pays exact. Those men with the machines found the ledger. Now the men who owe are coming to burn the books."

My mouth was dry. "You know this."

"I know what I heard on the night the radio spoke. I know what the soldiers dug for in the moraine with their long knives. And I know that when the ice gives back what it was given, it wants a witness." She stepped back and looked at me the way my mother used to look at me when she was deciding whether to tell me a hard thing. "Your grandfather was a witness. That's why I'm telling you and not the loud one with the questions."

"What happened to my grandfather?" I asked, and my voice came out strange, younger than I am.

"He went up with the porters in the soldier year," she said. "Some came down. He didn't. And the government paper said the storm took them, and the storm was blamed, and the storm has been blamed for many things on this mountain." She turned back to her mani wall. "Go and sleep, Dorje. Tomorrow take them up. And when the ice shows you what it shows you — remember that a witness is also a debt. The mountain collects both."

* * *

I did not sleep well, which will surprise no one who has met me.

I sat in the dark of the tea house with the amulet in my hand, turning it over. Copper, green with age, worked into the shape of a coiled serpent or a river — the old makers left such things ambiguous on purpose, I think, so that the symbol could be whatever the bearer needed. On the back, worn nearly smooth, a line of script in a hand older than any alphabet taught in schools now. My grandfather had carried one like it. I remembered that suddenly, the way memory arrives — not requested, uninvited, complete: a copper shape on a cord against his brown chest, and him telling me, boy that I was, *the mountain remembers, little one, so we must remember better.*

I had thought that was just an old man's poetry. Old men's poetry has a way of turning out to be inventory.

Across the dark room, Hazel Amberdale sat awake too, cleaning her crampons by feel, methodical as a monk counting beads. She didn't pretend to sleep, which I respected. After a while she spoke without looking up.

"You were gone a long time."

"An old woman gave me a blessing."

"That's not what your face says."

I almost laughed. "No. It isn't." I weighed whether to tell her. The amulet sat in my palm, warm from my hand, heavy beyond its grams. "Her grandmother crossed the pass the year soldiers came to this valley. Porters went up with them. Some didn't come down. The record says storm."

Hazel's hands went still on the crampon strap. In the dark I couldn't read her face, but I could read the quality of her stillness, which was the stillness of a woman fitting a new piece into a picture she'd already begun to distrust.

"The Meridian Survey found something," she said slowly. "That's why they stopped transmitting. That's why Rathore wants it 'recovered.' And that's why the villagers won't say the mountain's name."

"It seems so."

"And the survivor, if there is one — he isn't hiding from the mountain."

"No," I said. "He's hiding from the men who owe."

She was quiet a long moment. Then: "Seven years ago I broke the rule. No one left behind. One client, one storm, one decision, and I've paid for it every day since." She set the crampons down. "If there are men on this mountain who left people behind on purpose — and wrote it down as weather —"

She didn't finish. She didn't need to. Some sentences are load-bearing; you leave them standing.

Outside, the wind turned, and I felt it change against the walls — the shift from the dry katabatic breathing of evening to the wet south-wind breath of the monsoon feeling for its way into the valleys. Early. Everything this season was early. Four days, maybe less, and then the sky would close and whatever the ice was going to show us would have to be shown fast.

I tied the cord of the copper amulet around my neck and tucked it under my shirt, against my skin, where my grandfather's had lived.

Above the village, past the last prayer flags, the mountain stood against the stars, black on blacker, wearing its glacier like a held breath. Somewhere up there were six sets of gear with no ghosts in it, a boot print that ended in nothing, and — if the old woman's arithmetic was right — a ledger of debts written in bodies, waiting forty years and more for someone to read it aloud.

The ice keeps its debts. It pays slow, but it pays exact.

Tomorrow we would go up and find out what it intended to pay us.
