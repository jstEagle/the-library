## 5. Gear Without Ghosts

The camp announced itself before we saw it — a smell, of all things. Woodsmoke gone cold and sour, the ghost of a fire that had died weeks ago and never been relit. Then a corner of green nylon through the moraine, and my stomach did something complicated that I chose not to examine.

"Camp's ahead," I said, because someone had to say it out loud. "Single pitch. Six tents by the aerials I'm seeing."

"You can count tents from here?" Tomas asked, and there was that eager note in his voice again, the one that made him sound like a labrador who'd read about mountains in a book.

"I can count *tarps* from here," I said. "Tents are a guess until you're standing on them."

Dorje said nothing, which was how Dorje agreed with things.

We came up the last rise single file, the way you do when you don't know what's waiting, and the Meridian Survey camp unfolded below us in its shallow bowl of gravel and old ice-melt. Six dome tents, pitched with the neatness of people who expected to come back to them. A cook shelter strung between two boulders, taut as a drumhead. The solar panel array angled correctly at the sun. A prayer-flag line someone had hung without understanding it, the colors already bleached to ghosts.

Everything intact. Everything *waiting*.

"Nobody touch anything yet," I said, and heard Priya make a small note-taking sound behind me.

I stood at the edge of the bowl for a full minute before I went down into it, because this was the part of the job nobody puts in the brochures: reading a place before it reads you. Seven years ago I'd walked into a disaster site and missed what it was trying to tell me, or so the inquiry board had decided, and whatever else they'd taken from me they'd left me with this — a habit of looking at ground like an autopsy.

The first thing wrong was the quiet orderliness of it. Abandoned camps are never tidy. People leave in a hurry or they leave in stages, and either way they leave a sediment: dropped gloves, half-buried wrappers, the archaeology of hurry. This camp had no sediment. It looked less abandoned than *paused*, like the whole survey had stepped behind a rock to relieve themselves and would be back any minute to complain about the coffee.

The second thing wrong was the boots.

They were outside the tents. All of them. Twelve pairs lined up along the guy-lines in two neat rows, laces tucked inside, the way you store them overnight so the morning frost doesn't stiffen the leather. Not scattered. Not lost in a scramble. *Stored.*

Kwan crouched beside them without touching, hands clasped behind his back like a man at a museum. "Twelve pairs," he said. "Survey team was six."

"Spare boots," Tomas offered.

"Six spare pairs," Kwan said, "matching, all worn in, all different sizes." He looked up at me. "You pack spares that match your daily pair? Same model, same wear pattern?"

I hadn't thought about it yet and he'd already done the thinking for me. That was why I'd wanted him on this circus even if Rathore had picked him, not me. "No," I said. "You don't."

"So they took nothing," Kwan said, "and left everything. Including the boots."

"Maybe they hiked out in expedition sandals," Tomas said, and then heard himself, and went pink. "Sorry. Bad joke."

"It's not a joke yet," I said. "Give it an hour."

* * *

I worked the site the way I'd been taught to work an avalanche debris field: grid, then detail, then gut feeling, in that order. Grid first.

The cook shelter told me the most. Stove cold, fuel cans full — three of them, unopened, seals intact. Food stores stacked in crates against the rock: rice, dahl, oats, chocolate, a truly criminal quantity of instant noodles. Enough for six people for five more weeks, easy. A pot sat on the cold stove with cooked rice dried to a crust in the bottom, which meant somebody had eaten a meal and then not washed the pot, which meant somebody had expected to wash the pot later, which is the single most human thing about the whole site. You can fake a tidy evacuation. Nobody fakes unwashed dishes.

There was a radio in the shelter, a proper base set, antenna still up. I checked it the way you check a dead man's pulse, knowing the answer. Dead. Not broken — dead. Battery terminals clean, fuses whole, handset coiled where it lived. It simply wasn't talking, and neither, apparently, had anyone tried to make it.

"No distress log?" I asked the air generally.

Priya appeared at my shoulder with her notebook. She'd been doing that all day — materializing quietly whenever I found something, writing it down, saying almost nothing. Ministry liaison as human carbon paper. "The official record shows no transmission after June ninth," she said. "Weather window closed for four days, then reopened. No contact resumed."

"And nobody thought that was strange?"

"The ministry thought it was strange enough to hire you," she said, and for the first time something moved behind her eyes that wasn't bureaucratic. Something that might have been warning, if I'd been in a mood to read warnings.

Detail pass next. I went tent to tent while the others watched, and each one was a small museum of interrupted life. A journal open to a page of barometric readings, pen resting in the gutter. A paperback with a wrapper of noodles marking the page. Someone's medication blister pack, half used, sitting square on a sleeping bag rolled — *rolled*, not stuffed — with a compression strap cinched over it. Sleeping bags don't roll themselves. You roll yours in the morning, when you get up. All six bags were rolled.

"They got up," I said aloud, mostly to myself. "Morning routine. Boots off at night means boots on in the morning — except they didn't put them on. They rolled their bags, ate breakfast — there's the pot — and then walked out of their own boots."

"Into what?" Tomas asked. His voice had lost the labrador brightness. "Socks? Barefoot?"

"That would be the question," I said, "if the question weren't insane."

* * *

The gut-feeling pass came last, and it's the pass I trust most, though I'd have denied that under oath. Gut feeling is just pattern recognition too fast for language, and the pattern here was screaming something I couldn't translate yet.

I walked the perimeter. And there — on the gravel apron below the camp, where the melt-stream fan spread out fine and printable — the tracks started.

Or rather, they didn't. That was the thing. There were boot prints going *in*: a week and a half old, weathered but legible, six sets climbing up from the valley route, the ordinary signature of an ordinary arrival. I knelt over them and read them like a letter from home. Heavy tread, one man favoring his right ankle, someone walking with a surveyor's measured pace, someone else drifting off-line the way tired people do near the end of a day. A normal team ending a normal day.

And leaving the camp — heading uphill, toward the seracs, away from the valley, away from every sensible direction on God's green earth — there were footprints in socks.

Bare feet, some of them. I could see toe impressions, individual toes, pressed into the fine volcanic grit with a clarity that made my skin crawl, because toes mean weight, and weight means walking, and walking barefoot uphill on glacier-adjacent gravel at four thousand nine hundred meters in the morning cold is not something human beings decide to do. It's something human beings are photographed doing afterward, in reports.

Six lines of prints. Side by side. Not staggered like walkers, not strung out like a rope team. *Abreast.* Like people walking shoulder to shoulder who had no further need of personal space.

They went up the moraine ridge, all six lines together, and the gravel thinned, and the prints faded, and above a certain altitude there was only ice, and ice keeps nothing unless it wants to.

I must have been crouched there a long time, because eventually Dorje's shadow fell across the prints beside mine. He looked at them for a while. He didn't crouch. He stood the way the old woman in the last village had stood, when she wouldn't come out to look at us but watched us from her doorway the whole time we watered the mules.

"Barefoot," I said. "Uphill. Together." I heard my own voice and it sounded reasonable, which was the worst part. Reasonableness was all I had left and I was spending it fast. "People don't do this, Dorje."

He was quiet long enough that I counted his breaths. Four of them. "My grandfather climbed in leather shoes with straw wrapped round them," he said finally. "Above the snowline. In winter. Because the mountain asked."

"The mountain *asked*."

"When it asks," Dorje said, "you go." He turned and looked up-valley, at the white wall of the seracs catching the afternoon light, beautiful and blank as a held breath. "These men were not taken," he said. "Taken men leave signs. Struggle. Things dropped." He nodded at the tidy camp, the rolled sleeping bags, the rows of patient boots. "This is not taken. This is *answered*."

I wanted to laugh. I opened my mouth to do it, and found I couldn't, because my gut-pattern engine — the same one that had kept me alive through eleven seasons and killed my career in one bad night — was running hot and coming back with the same answer Dorje had just spoken out loud. The camp wasn't a crime scene. Crime scenes have violence in them. This was something else. This was six people who had received an invitation.

"Here's what I know," I said, standing, dusting off my knees, retreating hard into procedure because procedure was load-bearing. "One: nobody left this camp in distress. Two: nobody left this camp equipped. Three: they left together, uphill, on foot, without boots, without food, without the radio, without telling anyone. Four—" I stopped.

"Four?" Tomas prompted.

"Four, the ministry sent five of us to find six people who walked into the ice on purpose, and paid our expenses in advance." I looked at Priya, who was writing, always writing. "Your bosses have a theory, Priya? About what happened here?"

She capped her pen with great care. "The ministry has a question," she said. "That's why you're here."

"Wrong," I said, pleasantly, because I'd learned that tone from a lawyer once and it works. "Ministries don't have questions. Ministries have *answers* they're missing evidence for. Somebody upstairs already knows what these footprints mean, and we're not the search party. We're the footnote."

Nobody answered that. Kwan looked at the sock-prints going uphill. Tomas looked at his own boots as if suspecting them of conspiracy. Dorje looked at the mountain, and touched something small at his throat — the copper amulet, I realized, the old woman's gift — and let it go.

* * *

We pitched our own camp fifty meters downwind of theirs, because I am a professional and professionals sleep in haunted campsites all the time, and also because I did not want to sleep in those tents and I would have resigned before admitting it.

By stove-light I ran the numbers one more time, because numbers are a rosary and I say mine nightly. Six people. Morning routine completed. Meal eaten. Bags rolled. Boots stored. Then six lines of sock-and-barefoot prints, abreast, uphill, into the ice, fading at the gravel line two hundred meters short of the seracs. No tracks beyond. No body. No gear. No note.

In eleven seasons I had found the drowned, the fallen, the frozen, the buried, the lost, and the merely stupid. Every one of them had left a trail, because everything leaves a trail; that was the first law I'd ever learned and the last one I still believed in. Trails end in one of three ways: rescue, recovery, or time. These trails ended in none of the three. They ended the way a sentence ends when someone else takes the pen.

"Hazel." Kwan, at the edge of the firelight, low. He nodded up-valley.

I followed his finger. The light was going blue and gold behind the seracs, and in that light — I told myself it was refraction, wind-loading, a rib of ice catching the last sun the way ice does — but in that light there was a shape high on the glacier tongue, dark against the blue shadow, upright, still, at exactly the place where six lines of footprints stopped being six lines of footprints.

It didn't move. It didn't need to. It was positioned the way a door is positioned.

"Tell me that's a serac," I said.

"A serac," Kwan agreed, without conviction.

Behind us, in the abandoned camp, something shifted — canvas breathing in the evening wind, or twelve empty pairs of boots settling on their guy-lines, or nothing at all. Dorje murmured something under his breath in his grandmother's language, and touched the copper at his throat, and the mountain, patient as debt, waited for morning.
