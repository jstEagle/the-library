## 6. Tracks That End

I've been shot at twice in my life, and both times I remember thinking the same absurd thought: *so this is what the paperwork is for.* The army trains you for chaos the way a mother trains a child for vegetables — relentlessly, and with no guarantee any of it takes. What it doesn't train you for is quiet. Quiet on a glacier at four in the morning, when you're the one on watch and everyone else is breathing in that deep, trusting rhythm of people who believe someone competent is awake.

That morning, I was the someone. Debatable.

The light came up grey and sideways off the ice, the kind of light that makes everything look like evidence of something. I'd been up since three with a thermos of tea gone bitter and a headache I attributed to altitude and refused to attribute to Priya's snoring, which carried through nylon like a diesel generator. Below me, camp: four tents staked into the moraine, our little colony of suspicion. Hazel Amberdale slept the way I imagined she did everything — efficiently, arms crossed, as if even unconscious she was keeping score. Tomas slept like a golden retriever who'd read about sleep in a book. Dorje didn't seem to sleep at all so much as pause.

And above us, maybe six hundred meters up the glacier's shoulder, the Meridian Survey's abandoned camp sat in the morning shadow like a sentence nobody had finished.

We'd reached it the day before. I'll be honest — I'd expected wreckage. That's what missing expeditions look like in my experience: torn fabric, scattered kit, the archaeology of panic. Instead we'd found order. Tents pitched square. Stoves packed and cold. Ration boxes stacked by weight, by date, by some system that said *we intended to come back.* And boots. Every pair of boots left behind, lined up outside the tents like patients waiting for a doctor who never made rounds.

Hazel had walked the site for an hour without saying more than four words, and then she'd said them: "They walked out barefoot. Together. Uphill." She'd looked at the arrangement of things the way I'd once seen her look at a crevasse bridge — reading weight, reading lie. "Nobody walks uphill barefoot on a glacier because they want to."

So naturally, at dawn, I volunteered to go look further. Not because I'm brave. Because I'm the medic, and medics develop a professional relationship with dread. We ignore it until it presents symptoms, and then we treat what we find. Something about that camp had presented symptoms. I wanted to catch it early.

* * *

I told Hazel I was scouting the route. She was already awake — of course she was; I suspect she slept with one ear cocked like a gun dog — and she studied me for a long moment over the rim of her mug.

"Alone?"

"Someone should stay with the tents."

"That's not an answer, Kwan."

"It's the answer that gets breakfast made sooner." I shouldered my pack. "Two hours out, two hours back. Radio check every thirty minutes. If I miss one, you come looking, and you come looking roped, because I'm not writing the incident report on this trip twice."

Something moved behind her eyes at that — the ghost of whatever lived in her file, the thing Rathore had used to leash her. Seven years ago, they say, Hazel Amberdale left a client on a mountain. I don't know the details. Nobody does, officially. But I know what it costs a person to become a rule-keeper after something like that. It costs everything, and they pay it daily, in small coin, hoping no one notices the account is empty.

"Thirty minutes," she said finally. "And Kwan — if you find anything that looks like a person, you do not approach it. You describe it to me and wait."

"Aye, Major," I said, which was a joke, mostly.

She didn't smile. That should have been my second warning. The first was the weather, which had gone still in that particular way mountains go still before they decide something.

* * *

The traces started two hundred meters above their camp, and if you can call them traces, which is generous. A scuff in the moraine gravel. A thread of blue synthetic fiber snagged on rock, bright as a vein against all that grey. Then another scuff. Then, where the moraine gave way to clean glacier ice, the print.

I crouched over it for a long time before I touched it.

One boot print, pressed into glacial ice — pressed *into* it, not onto it, the way a boot prints into snow. Except this wasn't snow. This was old ice, the kind that's been compressed for decades, hard enough to ring under a boot heel, slick enough to make Dorje move like a man walking on his own reputation. Ice doesn't take footprints. Ice shrugs them off. You could stand here all day stamping like a frustrated toddler and leave nothing but bruises.

But there it was. A single boot print, crisp as a plaster cast, tread pattern intact down to the little star-shaped wear mark at the ball of the foot. Barefoot, my arse — somebody had put their boots on. One boot. One step. And around it, nothing. No trail leading in, no trail leading out. Just the one statement, pressed into ten thousand years of frozen water, like a signature on a document.

I did what any sensible ex-army medic does when confronted with something that violates physics: I took a photograph, radioed in on schedule, and reported a boot print in the ice.

Hazel's voice came back flat and careful. "In the ice."

"In the ice. Depth of maybe eight millimeters. Single impression. Tread's a Vibram-style lug, heavy boot, size — I'd guess forty-four, forty-five."

A pause long enough to have weather in it. "Kwan. Is there a second print?"

"I'm looking at two hundred meters of glacier, Hazel, and the only thing on it is one footprint and my own growing sense of regret."

Another pause. When she spoke again, her voice had changed register — dropped into something quieter, the voice of a person talking themselves down a ladder. "Describe exactly what's around it."

"Meltwater channel running east-northeast, maybe a meter across. Some surface ablation, a centimeter or two of roughening. Serac field above, half a klick. Nothing else." I hesitated, then gave her the part I'd been sitting on. "Except it's fresh. Edges are sharp. There's no refreeze film in the depression. Whatever made this, made it recently. Days, not weeks."

Silence on the line. Somewhere below, a raven said something rude and flapped off toward the valley, having better sense than the rest of us.

"Come back," Hazel said. "Now."

"Hazel—"

"Now, Kwan. That's not the ministry talking. That's me."

There it was again, that cost-of-rule-keeping thing. She wasn't ordering me because she liked ordering. She was ordering me because the last time she'd let someone wander, it had ended the way it ends in her file. So I went back. Mostly. I went back via a small detour, because I'm a hypocrite with a compass, and the detour is where I found the names.

* * *

The rockface stood off the glacier's east flank where the ice had retreated from it — a rib of black gneiss thrust up through the white like a bone through skin. You'd never notice it from below. From the ice, though, at the right angle, in the low morning light, the shadows in it caught the eye. Somebody had known that. Somebody had chosen that angle deliberately, the way you choose a wall for a memorial, or a billboard.

Six names, carved into the stone.

Not scratched — carved. Cut deep and clean with real tools, each letter maybe ten centimeters tall, the work of hours, done by someone with time and intent and no fear of being interrupted. I ran my glove over them and felt the edges, sharp still, unweathered. Recent. Weeks at most.

*ARJUN MEHTA.*
*SOFIA LINDQVIST.*
*DANIEL OKAFOR.*
*PRIYA NAIR.*
*MARCUS WEBB.*
*ELIAS VANN.*

I knew the names. We all did. They'd been read to us in Rathore's district office off a laminated sheet, six lines of government type, along with photographs that managed to make every one of them look guilty of something. Six surveyors of the Meridian Expedition, missing eleven months, presumed dead, recovery authorized, discretion emphasized. Discretion had been the word Rathore leaned on, the way a man leans on a bar. *Discretion, Ms. Amberdale. The ministry values discretion.*

I stood in front of those names for a long time, and I'll tell you what went through my head, because it wasn't grief, not yet. Grief needs a body, or at least a story. What went through my head was triage logic, the cold arithmetic you learn in field hospitals: *six names, carved by living hands, means at least one of the six was alive to carve them. Alive, and with tools, and with time. Missing eleven months. Eleven months of food is a lot of food. Eleven months of a man alone on a glacier is a lot of man.*

And underneath the arithmetic, quieter, the thing I didn't want to look at directly: *why carve your friends' names?* You carve names for the dead. You carve a memorial. Which meant whoever held the chisel believed five of the six were dead — or wanted whoever found this to believe it. Either way, they'd picked a spot only visible from the ice, aimed at future eyes, and left a message in the one language mountains understand: permanence.

Unless it wasn't a message for us at all. Unless it was a message for whoever came next, and next, and next — a warning written the way warnings get written when the writer doesn't expect rescue.

My radio crackled. Thirty-minute check. I looked at it for a moment, weighing honesty against reassurance, and chose a third option, which was cowardice with good diction.

"Kwan to base. Nothing further. Heading back."

"Copy," Hazel said, and I heard her not believing me, which is its own skill, and one she has mastered.

* * *

I told them at lunch. All of it — the print, the measurements, the names. I laid it out the way I'd lay out a casualty report, clinical, complete, because I've learned that fear spreads fastest through gaps. Give people the whole wound and they can bandage it. Leave them imagining it and they'll imagine it worse.

Tomas went pale in stages, like a developing photograph. "Carved," he kept saying. "With what? There was no — their gear was all accounted for, we inventoried it—" He stopped. We all watched him arrive at it. "Their ice axes. The piton hammers. Those weren't in the inventory."

"No," Hazel said quietly. "They weren't."

Dorje said nothing, which from Dorje was a paragraph. He sat turning something over in his fingers — that copper amulet the old woman at the last village had pressed on him, the one he'd shown us with the embarrassed reluctance of a man displaying a superstition he didn't quite own. *The ice keeps its debts,* she'd told him. He'd translated it for us with a shrug, making it small. Watching him now, I understood he hadn't made it small for us. He'd made it small for himself.

Priya wrote in her notebook. Of course she did. Everything about Priya was notebook-shaped — precise, closed, angled slightly away from the rest of us. But I caught her pen stop when I said the name *Vann*, and I caught her looking at Hazel afterward with an expression I couldn't file anywhere. Not pity. Not calculation. Something between inventory and apology.

"So we have a survivor," Tomas said. "Or we have — I mean, someone's alive up there. That's good. That's the mission. That's why we're here." His voice climbed hopefully toward the end of the sentence and found no ledge to land on.

"Is it?" Priya asked, mild as milk, and then pretended she hadn't said it.

Hazel stood. She did that thing she does where she plants herself and becomes load-bearing. "It changes nothing about today," she said. "We move up to the serac line tomorrow as planned. We search systematically. If Dr. Vann — if anyone — is alive up there, they've survived eleven months alone on this ice, which means they're better at this than all of us, and they will find *us* long before we find them." She paused. "What it changes is that we stop calling this a recovery."

"What do we call it?" Tomas asked.

Hazel looked up the glacier, toward the white silence where a single bootprint sat in ten thousand years of stone-hard water, and I watched her choose her words the way you choose footholds.

"A contact mission," she said. "From here on, nobody goes anywhere alone. Nobody. Rope teams of two minimum, radio checks hourly, and anyone who sees anything — anything — reports it before approaching it." Her eyes came around to me, and there was no anger in them, which was worse. "That includes detours, Kwan."

"Understood," I said, and meant it, and knew I'd break it again inside a day, because that's the other thing the army teaches you: rules exist for the version of you that isn't scared yet.

* * *

That night I couldn't sleep, so I sat up with the stove and listened to the glacier.

People think glaciers are silent. They're wrong. Glaciers talk all night — groans, cracks, that deep hydraulic sighing of meltwater finding its way through the dark interior, like the mountain clearing its throat before it says something. Most nights it's just noise. You learn to sleep inside it.

But around two in the morning, the talking changed. All of it stopped at once — total silence, the whole valley holding its breath — and then, from somewhere up under the seracs, came a sound I can only describe as a knock. Three impacts, deliberate, spaced evenly. Stone on ice, or ice on ice. Then silence again, and the ordinary groaning resumed, casual, as if embarrassed.

I sat very still with my hand on the stove, telling myself: rockfall. Thermal contraction. Ice calving somewhere internal. All the reasonable diagnoses. I am a medical professional; I know how to give bad news a benign name.

Then, faintly, from high up in the dark, I heard it again.

Three knocks. Pause. Three knocks.

And I thought about a man alone for eleven months on a glacier, with tools and time and no one to talk to, listening every night for footsteps that never came — and what a man like that would teach himself to do, night after night, to test whether the mountain had finally sent him company, or sent him something else.

I woke Hazel. She was awake before my hand touched the tent, sitting up in the dark with her eyes already open, and when I told her, she didn't ask if I was sure. She just said, "Get Dorje," and began pulling on her boots, and outside, far above us in the black, the knocking came a third time —

— closer.
