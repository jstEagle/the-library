## 7. What Priya Carries

There is a particular kind of loneliness that comes with being the only person in a tent who knows she's lying.

I want to be clear about this: I didn't start out a liar. Nobody does. You begin as a junior analyst in a ministry office in the capital, filing reports about water tables and road grants, and you tell yourself that the work matters, that someone has to keep the machinery honest from inside. Then one day you're good at keeping secrets — not because you enjoy it, but because you're discreet, and competent, and you don't flinch when men like Dev Rathore say things in low voices across polished tables — and competence, in my experience, is the most dangerous thing a person can be noticed for.

So here I was, night four above the last village, lying in a nylon coffin at four thousand meters while the wind tried to peel the mountain down around us, holding a document I was never supposed to open until I was alone and certain no one would ever know.

The others were asleep. Or pretending to be. Tomas snored the way only twenty-four-year-olds can snore — with total commitment, an entire personality expressed through the sinuses. Kwan slept like a soldier, which is to say silently and not at all; twice tonight I'd heard him turn over, quick and controlled, the way a man checks his surroundings even in his dreams. Dorje had taken first watch and then simply kept it, sitting outside under the tarp with his back to the wind as if weather were a rumor he'd declined to believe.

And Hazel Amberdale slept in the tent next to mine like a woman who had made her peace with being hunted.

I'd watched her settle in tonight, after the day's march, and there was something in it that unsettled me more than the altitude or the orders folded against my ribs. She checked her boots before she put them away. She checked them again. She laid out her rope in a coil so precise it looked ceremonial, and then she lay down on her side facing the door, and within ninety seconds her breathing went long and slow — but I'd have bet my pension she wasn't asleep. That's not sleep. That's a woman listening to the mountain the way other people listen for footsteps.

Seven years ago, they say, she walked down off a mountain with three clients dead and her name in every paper from here to the capital. They say she left a man behind. They say a lot of things about Hazel Amberdale, and most of what they say was written by people who needed a villain more than they needed the truth. Rathore had shown me the file. All of it, including the parts that never made the papers. And I will admit — privately, in the dark, where admissions cost nothing — that reading it had changed how I looked at her. Not with pity. With something closer to professional alarm. Because the file told me two things: that Hazel Amberdale was exactly the kind of person who would refuse to leave someone behind, and that the ministry had once destroyed her for it.

Which raised an interesting question about what the ministry intended to do with her now.

* * *

The second set of orders had been given to me at the district office, in a brown envelope sealed with wax, which should have been my first warning. Wax seals are theater. Rathore loved theater the way small men love large desks. "Your operational instructions," he'd said, sliding it across the glass like a man dealing cards he'd already counted. "Open them only if circumstances require clarification."

"Circumstances such as?"

"Such as finding something."

I'd carried the envelope for eleven days. Through Rungli, up the valley, past the village where the old woman had pressed a copper amulet into Dorje's hand and said words I pretended not to hear — *the ice keeps its debts* — through every camp and every silence, feeling it against my ribs like a second heartbeat. And every night I told myself the same thing: tomorrow. Tomorrow I'd decide whether to open it. Tonight I just wanted to sleep.

Tonight, finally, the lie ran out of road.

It wasn't conscience that did it. Let me be honest about that too, since honesty is apparently the theme of my evening. It was the radio.

We had two radios. The primary set had died on day two — a power fault, the technician in Rungli had said, shrugging in the way of technicians everywhere, a shrug that means *don't ask me to be accountable*. The backup had worked fine until yesterday afternoon, when it developed a sudden, total, and frankly suspicious deafness. Kwan opened the casing tonight after dinner, by lamplight, with the delicacy of a surgeon and the expression of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't like.

"Corroded contact," he said. "On both sets."

"Both?" Tomas asked.

"Both." Kwan looked at me when he said it. Just for a moment. Just long enough.

Now, I am many things, but I am not stupid, and neither is Kwan. Corrosion doesn't happen symmetrically on two radios packed separately, unless the corrosion was applied. And the only person who'd handled both sets before departure was the ministry logistics officer in Rungli. The same office that issued my envelope.

So: we were cut off, on a mountain, looking for six missing government surveyors, and our ability to call for help had been arranged in advance to fail. There are only two reasons to sabotage your own rescue team's communications. Either you expect them to find something you don't want reported.

Or you expect them not to come back at all.

I waited until Kwan's lamp went out. I waited another hour, counting Tomas's snores the way sailors count waves. Then I took out the envelope, cracked the wax — it split cleanly, professionally, the way everything Rathore touched split cleanly — and read.

* * *

The document was one page. Bureaucracy is efficient that way; it takes real skill to compress an atrocity into four hundred words of numbered clauses.

*Operational Directive 9-K (Supplementary). In the event the Meridian Survey party or its effects are located:*

*1. All materials of scientific, cartographic, or documentary nature recovered by MS-6 prior to cessation of contact are classified State Priority. Recover intact.*

*2. If recovery intact is not feasible, ensure non-recovery. Non-recovery includes destruction, burial, or removal beyond reach of unauthorized parties.*

*3. Personnel of the recovery team are cleared for Objective 1 only. Do not disclose Objective 2. If any member of the recovery team demonstrates awareness of Objective 2, reassess their status.*

*4. Reassessment authority: D. Rathore.*

That was all. No signature beyond the initials. No definition of "non-recovery" that acknowledged it might apply to anything with a pulse. But clause three sat there on the page like a splinter working toward the heart: *reassess their status.* I knew what reassessment meant. I'd drafted language like it myself, years ago, in the water-resources division, back when I still believed the worst thing my prose could do was misallocate an aquifer.

If the survey's findings could be brought home, bring them home. If they couldn't, make sure no one else ever saw them. And if anyone on your own team figures out what you're carrying —

Well. Priya, old girl. Which category do you fall into?

I lay there with the page in my gloved hands and let the wind have its way with the tent, and I thought about the shape of the trap, because the shape was beautiful, really, in the way a well-made leg-hold trap is beautiful. Rathore hadn't sent soldiers. Soldiers ask questions, and worse, they answer to other soldiers. He'd sent five people who couldn't afford to distrust each other: a blacklisted guide with no options, a boy surveyor hungry for a career, a porter whose family lived in villages the ministry could ruin with a phone call, a medic whose discharge papers I'd glimpsed and whose loyalty to institutions had clearly died somewhere he didn't discuss. And me. The liaison. The eyes.

Five people, each holding a piece of the truth, each assuming the others were clean. That's not a team. That's a fuse, and Rathore was holding the match.

Because here was the arithmetic I couldn't escape: six surveyors had gone up that mountain with functioning radios and government sanction, and they had stopped existing. No tracks. No distress call. Tents pitched, stoves cold, boots left behind — Hazel had stood over that campsite reading it like scripture, and I'd watched her face go carefully blank, and blankness in a woman like Hazel is a scream. Whatever ended Meridian Six hadn't been weather. Weather leaves messes. This had been tidy.

Tidy like a directive. Tidy like clause two.

*Ensure non-recovery.*

I thought about the phrase *cessation of contact*, the bureaucratic euphemism doing its quiet work. Somewhere above us, in the ice, were either six bodies or six witnesses, and my instructions treated those as the same category: material. Recoverable or not. And now, thanks to clause three, there were potentially eleven of us on this glacier, and the document in my hands drew no line between the surveyors and the people sent to look for them.

Hazel. Would Hazel be recoverable? I turned onto my back and stared at the tent roof glowing faintly with starlight and asked the question properly, because it deserved proper asking. If Hazel found whatever the survey found — and she would; that woman could read a mountainside like a ledger — if she understood what it meant, what would she do?

She'd tell everyone. Immediately, loudly, at altitude, in a storm, it wouldn't matter. Hazel Amberdale was the woman who got destroyed for refusing to leave a man behind. Clause two would mean nothing to her. Clause one would mean less. She was, by every account in her file and every hour I'd spent watching her walk, categorically unrecoverable.

And Kwan? Kwan held his silences like ammunition, but I'd seen him check the radio casings twice, unsatisfied, a man who trusted nothing he hadn't disassembled himself. He'd already smelled it. He was waiting for confirmation, and confirmation was currently crumpling in my fist.

Tomas would follow whoever spoke last with the most confidence. Dorje followed the mountain, and the mountain, according to a very old woman, kept accounts.

And me?

I'm the one who carries things. That's always been my role — liaison, courier, the person between. I carry messages, supplies, secrets, blame. I have carried things for Rathore for three years without opening them, because unopened things can't implicate you. An envelope stays innocent as long as it stays sealed. That was the bargain I'd made with myself, the little civil-service covenant: I move the machinery, I don't ask what it grinds.

But I'd opened it. That was done. There is a moment — everyone who has ever crossed a line knows it — when the act itself is smaller than you feared and larger than you imagined, simultaneously, and afterward you are simply a person who has done a thing, and all your careful architecture of deniability comes down like wet snow.

I had read clause two. I now knew what I was carrying. And knowing, I discovered, is not survivable in the same body as obeying. Something has to give.

* * *

Here is what I did instead of sleeping, in the order I did it:

I copied the directive. By penlight, into the back pages of my field notebook, in compressed shorthand of my own devising, the same shorthand I used for drafting minutes nobody was meant to parse closely. The original went back into the envelope, the envelope back against my ribs. A courier keeps the cargo; that much of my training deserved to survive.

Then I crawled out into the cold and stood under the tarp beside Dorje, who acknowledged my existence with a glance that somehow contained an entire conversation's worth of assessment.

"You should sleep," he said.

"So should you."

"The mountain talks at night. Someone should listen." He said it without mysticism, the way you'd say *the kettle needs watching*. Then, after a silence long enough to hold a decision: "You heard the old woman."

"I heard her."

"She gave me this." He showed me the copper amulet, dull in the dark, worn smooth by generations of thumbs. "My grandmother's mother wore one like it. Before the government came up here the first time and the government's records say no one came up here at all." He wrapped it away again, carefully. "You ministry people write down what you want true. The ice writes down the rest."

I had no answer for that. I want it noted that I, a career liar, was rendered speechless by a porter quoting his grandmother, and that it felt like being audited by the universe.

Last, I did the thing I'll defend to no one. I moved to the gap between the tents and crouched there, looking at the dark seam of Hazel's doorway, listening to her measured, deliberate, almost-certainly-fake breathing, and I asked myself the question clause three had planted: *if she demonstrates awareness, reassess her status.* Reassess. And beneath it, quieter, the real question, the one I'd been carrying longer than the envelope:

When the moment came — and on this mountain, with these orders, the moment was coming the way the monsoon was coming, inevitably and on schedule — which side of the directive would I stand on? The side of the seal and the initials and the polished desk? Or the side of the woman in the next tent who had already lost everything once for the crime of counting every human life as recoverable?

I didn't know. That was the truth of it, the whole ugly truth: I didn't know yet. But I knew I was running out of nights in which not-knowing was a safe place to live.

I went back into my tent. I zipped the door. And as I lay down, I heard it — faint, from Hazel's tent, unmistakable to anyone who'd spent the last hour cataloguing her fake sleep: the soft, deliberate sound of a zipper easing open, and then silence, and then nothing at all.

Either she was checking on the storm.

Or she'd heard my penlight, and my pen scratching, and she was out there in the dark right now, demonstrating awareness.

Reassessing me.
