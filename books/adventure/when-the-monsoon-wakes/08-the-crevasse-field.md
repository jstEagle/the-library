## 8. The Crevasse Field

The glacier above the abandoned camp didn't look like a killer. It looked like a road — a broad white avenue running up the valley between walls of blue ice, smooth as poured milk in the early light. That's the thing about glaciers that people who've never roped up on one never understand: they don't look dangerous. They look like the safest ground you've ever seen. All their violence is hidden underneath, like a polite family with something terrible in the cellar.

I'd been reading the surface all morning, and I didn't like what I read. Sagging lines in the snow where the ice below had thinned. Little depressions that caught shadow differently than they should. A glacier is a river, and rivers have channels, and somewhere under our boots there was water doing what water does — carving rooms into a hundred meters of ice and leaving nothing but a skin of snow to walk on.

"Rope up," I said, for the third time since camp.

"We're on a *road*," Tomas said, not unkindly. He was twenty-six and had the particular confidence of someone whose instruments had never lied to him. "The survey team came this way."

"The survey team left their boots behind," I said. "Their judgment isn't my benchmark."

Behind him, Kwan snorted — approval or amusement, hard to tell with him — and started coiling rope without being asked twice. Dorje was already tied in; he'd been tied in since we broke camp, before I said anything, which told me he'd read the same signs I had. Priya hesitated, then clipped on, her face carefully neutral, like roping up was a formality she was humoring me with.

Five people, one rope team split into two groups, thirty meters apart. Standard practice. The kind of thing that feels absurd right up until the moment it's the only reason anyone goes home.

We walked. The glacier rose in long gentle ramps, and the sun climbed, and by midday the snow went soft in that treacherous way it does — firm enough to hold your weight at seven in the morning, rotten as cake by noon. I called the change, moved us to tighter spacing, put Dorje on point because he read snow better than any of us and I'd stopped pretending otherwise.

He stopped so suddenly I nearly walked into his pack.

"Here," he said.

"Here what?"

He crouched and brushed snow away with two fingers, delicate, the way you'd brush dust off a sleeping child. Underneath: a faint blue tint in the white, a lens of clear ice over darkness. A snow bridge. And not an old one — the edges were crisp, recent, the kind of bridge that forms overnight and fails by afternoon.

"Bridge," Dorje said. "Thin."

"How thin?"

He looked up at me. He has one of those faces that doesn't waste expression, so when he gave me a small shrug, it landed like a verdict.

"Probing poles," I said. "Single file. Step where I step."

We spent forty minutes crossing maybe eighty meters of ground, each of us probing every step, the poles punching through into black air more times than I wanted to count. The bridges held. We marked the route with wands and reached solid ice on the far side, and I let myself breathe, and that — I want to be honest about this — that was the mistake. Relief is how mountains get you. Not when you're scared. When you've just stopped being scared.

Tomas was ten meters ahead of me, off the marked line, angling toward a rock outcrop he wanted to sample. He wasn't roped to me anymore — we'd gone to moving unroped on the "safe" ice, because the safe ice had lulled us, because I'd let it.

The sound the mountain made wasn't loud. That's what I'll remember. Not a crack, not a boom — just a soft *whump*, like a heavy book closing somewhere far below, and then Tomas wasn't standing anymore. He was down in a hole, and the hole was swallowing him, arms pinwheeling, snow collapsing around him in slabs, and he made a sound I'd heard once before, seven years ago, from another mouth — a short, high note of pure animal surprise, the sound a person makes when the world simply stops holding them.

Everything I know happened in the next four seconds, and most of it happened without me deciding it.

I dropped to self-arrest, ice axe buried, boots dug in. "ROPE!" — screaming it though nobody was attached to him, screaming it at the universe. Kwan hit the snow beside me a half-second later, axe in, and Dorje was already moving, low and fast, flanking wide because he knew — everyone who's done this knows — that you never approach a crevasse lip straight on, that the edge itself is a lie waiting to give way.

Tomas fell maybe four meters before the funnel narrowed and jammed him. When I crawled to the lip and looked down, he was wedged in the throat of the crack, chest pinched between two walls of blue ice, backpack crushed against the ceiling of the void beneath him. His face was white as the snow around it. His crampons dangled over open dark.

"I can't—" His voice came out tiny. "Hazel. I can't feel my—"

"You can." I made my voice flat and boring on purpose. Boring saves lives; panic is contagious and I was the only one close enough to catch it first. "You're stuck, not falling. Breathe. Look at me. What color is my jacket?"

"What?"

"*What color.*"

"Red. It's red."

"It's red. Good. Keep looking at it." I turned. "Kwan — anchor. Two screws, back from the lip. Dorje — equalize a second anchor, we'll need a redirect. Priya, rope bag, now."

And here is the thing I will say about Priya, because this chapter of the story belongs to it: she didn't hesitate. Whatever orders she carried, whatever second set of instructions lived folded in her kit like a knife in a mattress — for four minutes on that glacier she was just hands and rope, flawless hands, feeding me line exactly when I needed it, no slack, no surge. Whatever else she was, she was good.

We built the haul system in the time it takes to make tea. C-pulley, redirected through the second anchor, Kwan's shoulders and mine on the hauling line while Dorje coached Tomas from the lip — *move your feet, kick in, help us, don't go deadweight* — and Tomas, bless him, did not go deadweight. He climbed the wall of the crevasse on our tension, crampon points chattering against blue ice, and when his head came over the lip he was crying without any noise at all, tears freezing on his cheeks, and he grabbed my wrist hard enough to bruise and said, "Thank you, thank you, thank you," like a man saying grace.

We got him onto solid ice and stripped his harness and checked him over — Kwan's job, and Kwan did it silently and thoroughly, pressing along Tomas's ribs, checking his pupils, flexing his fingers one by one. Bruised ribs. Wrenched shoulder. Nothing broken. A man pulled out of the earth's throat with nothing but bruises.

"Camp," I said. "Now. Before the afternoon rot opens anything else."

Nobody argued. Even Priya.

* * *

We got the tents up in the lee of a serac wall and lit the stove inside the big one, all five of us crammed in because nobody suggested splitting up and nobody had to. There are rooms you share after a thing like that. It's not a decision. It's gravity.

Kwan melted snow and made tea that was mostly sugar, which is medicine, whatever the textbooks say. Tomas sat propped against his pack with his ribs wrapped, holding his mug in both hands like it might escape. For a long time nobody talked. The stove hissed. Outside, the wind had come up, worrying the tent fabric in long slow breaths.

It was Tomas who broke first, because of course it was. He was the one who'd fallen; near-death makes talkers of quiet men.

"I keep hearing it," he said. "That sound. It wasn't even loud. That's the worst part. You'd think—" He shook his head. "Four meters. If that bridge had been two days older, I'd have gone through into God knows what. There could be a hundred meters of nothing under there."

"There was," Dorje said quietly. Everyone looked at him. "When I probed near the lip. The pole went down past its length. No bottom."

Tomas closed his eyes.

"You saved my life," he said to me. "Both of you. All of you." He opened them again, and there was something new in his face — not gratitude exactly, something rawer. "Can I ask you something? And you can tell me it's none of my business."

"People always ask anyway," I said. "Might as well skip the apology."

"Why do you do this? After—" He stopped himself, but too late; we all heard the word land. *After.* Everyone on this expedition knew why Hazel Amberdale was available to guide a ministry recovery mission in the dead season. Everyone had been politely not saying it for six days. "I'm sorry. That was—"

"No, you asked." I turned my mug in my hands. The tea had gone lukewarm. "You want the story? Fine. It's short and it's ugly."

They waited. Even Dorje, who I would have sworn didn't care, angled slightly toward me in the way that was, for him, the equivalent of leaning forward.

"Seven years ago I was guiding a client up the east ridge of Dzara Kang — commercial route, well within my competence, I want that understood. Storm came in early. Forecast said three days out; it arrived in nine hours. My fault for trusting the forecast, if you want to assign blame, and plenty of people did." I kept my voice level. It had taken years of practice to keep it level. "My client, Gerald Whitcombe, banker, fifty-one, ambitious beyond his lungs. On the descent he lagged. Weather closing, light going. I had a choice: stay with him and risk both of us, or take the rest of the team down and come back for him."

"And?" Kwan said. Not cruelly. Just wanting the shape of it.

"He begged me not to leave him. So I didn't. I stayed. We bivouacked at seven thousand meters with no shelter and half a stove. I did everything the book says. Everything *right*." I laughed, and it came out wrong, and I let it. "He died at 3 a.m. Hypoxia, exposure. And here's the part that ended me: the inquiry found that if I'd left him when I first considered it — descended with the others, come back with support — he'd probably have died anyway, alone, and I'd probably have lived, and my record would be clean. Staying was the wrong call. Staying killed him and nearly killed me and got me blacklisted, and the inquiry wrote it all up in beautiful bureaucratic prose about *risk assessment failures* and *guide responsibility thresholds*, and the family sued, and the association folded like paper, and that was that."

The stove hissed. Somewhere above us, the serac wall ticked and settled, ice talking to itself in the cold.

"So no," I said, "I don't do this despite what happened. I do it because of it. Because the only thing I've ever been any good at is the thing that ruined me, and a person can't stop being what they are just because being it hurt somebody." I looked at Tomas. "You fell today and five people worked like one animal to pull you out. Nobody checked orders. Nobody hesitated. That's the job. That's the whole job. Everything else — the permits, the politics, the men in offices — that's just weather of a different kind."

Priya was watching me with an expression I couldn't read. Something had shifted behind her eyes while I talked, some ledger being re-balanced, some category being reconsidered. I noticed it and filed it away, because I notice things — it's how I've stayed alive this long — but I didn't understand it yet. I would. Oh, I would.

"My grandfather crossed this glacier," Dorje said, into the silence. "Carrying loads for expeditions. Before the roads, before any of it." He said it to the stove, not to us. "He never spoke of the high valley. When other old men told stories, he went outside to smoke. My grandmother said the mountain took something from him he never named." He paused. "Today, when Tomas fell — I thought of him. How it must feel, to hear a man fall into the ice and know the ice keeps what it takes."

"That's grim," Tomas said weakly.

"That's true," Dorje said. "On this mountain, best to prefer true."

Kwan topped up mugs all around, and for a while the talk went small and human — Tomas's sister getting married in the spring, whether he'd make it home for it; Kwan's habit of sleeping with his boots on, army-bred, twelve years out and still counting exits; even Priya offered a scrap, a story about her first posting, mispronouncing a village elder's name so badly he adopted her on the spot to save her further embarrassment. Small stories. The currency of trust. You spend them in the low places and hope they hold.

I sat there listening and felt something I hadn't felt in seven years, and it frightened me more than the crevasse had: I was part of a team. Not leading one — part of one. The distinction mattered. Leading was a role you performed. Part-of was a thing you were, and it could be taken from you, and the last time it was taken it had taken everything with it.

I should have remembered that fear. Held onto it. Let it make me careful.

Instead I lay in my bag that night listening to the wind and thinking: *maybe this time, maybe these people, maybe the mountain owes me one.*

The mountain doesn't owe anybody anything. I knew that. I'd known it since I was nineteen years old and watched it swallow my first winter. But knowing a thing and believing it are different countries, and I was a long way from home.

Sleep took me eventually, shallow and full of falling. And in the grey hour before dawn, when the wind dropped and the glacier went silent as held breath, I heard Dorje outside the tent, speaking softly in his own language to someone who wasn't there — and when I unzipped the flap, careful and quiet, he was standing at the edge of camp facing up-valley, toward the high country we were climbing into, holding something small and copper in his open palm, turning it over and over in the starlight.

He felt me watching. He didn't turn around.

"Go back to sleep, Hazel," he said. "Tomorrow we find what the ice kept."
