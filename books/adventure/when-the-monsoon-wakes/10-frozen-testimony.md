## 10. Frozen Testimony

The cave had a sound to it. That was the first thing I understood, standing at its mouth with my headlamp beam swallowed by blue. Not silence — silence is just an absence, and this was a presence. A low hum, like the mountain breathing through its teeth. Meltwater moving somewhere deep below us, patient as a debt collector.

Dorje stood beside me and would not go in.

I want to be fair to him here, because I've had seven years to think about what fairness costs, and I know now that refusing to walk into that cave might have been the sanest act performed on that mountain by anyone, including me. But at the time I only saw a porter who'd carried sixty kilos over passes that would kill most men suddenly unable to take one more step onto flat ice.

"Dorje." I kept my voice level. "Talk to me."

He looked at me, and his face in the lamplight was older than it had been that morning. "My grandmother said the ice keeps its debts," he said. "She didn't say what happens when you come to collect."

Then he picked up his load anyway, because that's who Dorje was, and followed me in.

* * *

The instruments were the first wrong thing.

You have to understand how gear behaves when people abandon a camp in a hurry or in terror. It scatters. It tangles. Panic leaves fingerprints all over a site — dropped poles, slashed tent lines, a stove kicked over mid-boil. I'd read the Meridian camp two days earlier like a crime scene precisely because of what *wasn't* there: no panic, no scatter. Boots left behind in neat rows.

This was worse. This was *order*.

The survey team's instruments were arranged in a careful arc around the far wall of the cave — theodolite on its tripod, centered like an altar piece. Range finders. The seismic unit with its cables coiled in perfect loops. Six headlamps, six sets of them, laid out side by side on a flat shelf of ice, all switched off, all pointing toward the wall.

Tomas came up beside me, breathing hard from the climb through the seracs, and made a small sound in his throat.

"They set up a survey," he whispered. "In here. Why would they—" He stopped. His headlamp found the wall, and he stopped talking altogether.

The ice in that section of the glacier was old. Old ice has a color you don't forget once you've seen it — not white, not blue exactly, but a deep glassy teal, dense as cathedral stone, compressed by centuries until the air bubbles trapped inside it are so small they scatter light like something alive. The wall of it rose maybe four meters from the cave floor, curved, polished smooth by meltwater in places and rough as scar tissue in others.

And it held shapes.

At first, in the flat sweep of my lamp, I took them for rock inclusions — stones the glacier had swallowed on its long crawl down from the col. Then my beam caught the curve of a boot sole. Then the spread of fingers, pressed flat against the ice from the inside, the way a man presses his hand to a window.

"Hazel." Kwan's voice came from behind me, and I had never heard her sound like that. Kwan, who had stitched a man's scalp in a moving truck and complained only about the lighting. "Hazel, there are people in there."

* * *

We did not speak for a while after that. There is a kind of cold that goes past shivering into stillness, and I think we all entered it together, six lamps aimed at the wall, our breath hanging in the air like the ghosts we were looking at.

It was Priya who moved first. She walked forward slowly, the way you approach a wounded animal, and put her lamp close against the ice.

"Uniforms," she said. Her voice had gone thin and official, the ministry voice, and then she heard herself using it and let it drop. "Colonial-era. Look at the buttons. The puttees."

She was right. Even distorted by the ice — bodies compress strangely under pressure, stretched and flattened by decades of the glacier's slow grip — I could make out the high collars, the leather belts with brass fittings gone green, the wrapped leggings of men who had marched for an empire that no longer existed. Three of them, close together near the center of the wall. Their arms were bound behind their backs. I could see the rope. The glacier had preserved even that, dark lines crossing wrists that had been pulled tight enough, once, to grind bone.

And each of them had a wound at the base of the skull, small and neat, the kind of wound that takes no skill and no courage to inflict on a bound man.

"There are more," Dorje said quietly. He had come forward after all, drawn despite himself, and his lamp swept along the wall to the left where the ice grew rougher. Shapes there too. Smaller frames. Wrapped in cloth rather than uniforms — porters' dress, the kind still worn in villages on the far side of the range. He counted under his breath in his own language, and I watched his lips move, and I watched him stop counting.

"How many?" Tomas asked.

"Eleven," Dorje said. "That I can see. There may be more deeper in." He turned to look at us, and his voice was steady in the way that a wire is steady right before it parts. "Soldiers and porters. Bound and shot. Together."

Nobody said the obvious thing, which was: *together*. Soldiers of the old regime executed alongside the local men who carried for them, dumped into a crevasse or a melt shaft together, sealed in the same grave. Whatever purge had happened here, whoever had ordered it, they hadn't even bothered to sort the bodies first. That detail hit me harder than the wounds did. Contempt doesn't discriminate.

Kwan crouched at the base of the wall, examining the ice with her lamp angled low. She's the one who taught me, later, to read a glacier like a ledger. "The crevasse closed over them," she said. "Meltwater refroze season after season. They're encased, not buried. The ice moved them down-slope maybe a kilometer in however many years, and the cave opened around them recently — see the fresh fracture line?" She pointed to a seam running diagonally overhead. "This exposure is new. Weeks old, maybe less. Before that, nobody could have seen them."

"And the Meridian Survey did," Priya said.

"The Meridian Survey was mapping exactly this section," Tomas said. His voice had recovered some of its professional spine, and he clung to it visibly, the way a man clings to a rail in heavy seas. "Seismic work, ice-depth profiling. If anyone was going to find a void in the glacier, a cavity, an inclusion field—"

"They weren't surveying ice depth," Priya said.

We all looked at her.

She had gone pale, and she was staring at the wall of the dead with her arms wrapped around herself, and when she spoke again her voice was very quiet. "Their instruments are arranged facing the wall. The theodolite is sighted on the bodies. They documented this. They photographed it, measured it, recorded positions — that's why everything is placed like this. They turned the cave into a survey station. Into evidence." She swallowed. "They knew what they'd found. And they reported it."

"To whom?" I asked.

Priya didn't answer. She didn't have to. We all knew who sent us. We all knew whose signature sat on the orders in her locked case, whose name was on the letter that had dragged me out of Rungli with a threat of extradition folded inside it like a knife in an envelope.

Dev Rathore had sent five people up this mountain to find six surveyors who had found eleven corpses in colonial uniforms, bound and shot, in a massacre the state had never acknowledged — and if the state had never acknowledged it, then the state had a reason, and the reason had a name, and the name was probably still spoken in rooms with good ventilation and bad consciences.

* * *

Here is what I thought about, standing in front of that wall of ice, and I'll tell you plainly because it shames me and shame shared is at least weight distributed:

I thought about my own file.

Seven years ago I came down off a mountain with one client alive out of four, and the inquiry asked me questions shaped like traps, and I answered them honestly, and honesty turned out to be the wrong tool for the job. The report said *guide error*. The word *blacklisted* went into a database somewhere, and I learned to measure my life in distances walked instead of years lived, because distances don't ask you to defend yourself. I told myself for seven years that what happened was weather and bad luck and one impossible decision made in ninety seconds of screaming wind. I mostly believed it.

But I knew — I always knew, down in the place you don't visit — that the report wasn't written to find truth. It was written to end the inquiry. Someone needed a name to hang the disaster on, and mine was the cheapest one available. I was scapegoated. I have never once said that sentence out loud before, and I'm saying it now only because that cave pried it out of me, because standing in front of eleven murdered men preserved in state ice, I finally recognized the machinery. The tidy report. The convenient conclusion. The inquiry that ends exactly where someone powerful needs it to end.

The Meridian Survey had found the original document of that machinery. The template itself. Somewhere in some ministry archive there was a purge that got filed under *resolved*, and these eleven were the resolved part, and six surveyors had stumbled onto the physical proof and done the professional thing, the honest thing — they had surveyed it, documented it, radioed it in.

And then they had taken off their boots.

I looked at those neat rows of instruments again, and at the six headlamps laid out side by side, switched off, pointing at the dead like candles at a vigil, and a new cold went through me that had nothing to do with altitude. Boots left behind. No tracks leaving. A team that had been told to wait — I remembered the shape of that thought from the camp above, the stoves cold but the food untouched, the tents pitched and orderly — a team that had been told *wait, help is coming* would wait. Would settle in. Would keep working, because professionals work. Would arrange their evidence carefully, because that's what you do when you believe the truth is finally going to be heard.

And then, one night, without their boots—

No. I stopped myself there. Because the alternative reading was worse and more strange: that they had walked out barefoot, together, uphill, into the ice. That something had made six trained surveyors abandon their boots and their camp and walk into the glacier's mouth. Either way, the story ended the same way: no survivors, no tracks, and a ministry that wanted the findings *recovered* — or, failing that, ensured unrecoverable.

"We should document everything," Tomas was saying. "Photograph the wall, log positions, the same protocol they used. If anything happens to us—"

"Nothing is going to happen to us," Priya said, too fast, too flat.

Kwan looked at her. I looked at her. Priya met our eyes with the particular blankness of a person holding a door shut from the inside, and I made a private note, the way you note a hairline crack in a carabiner: not broken yet, but no longer to be trusted with a life.

Dorje had barely moved through any of this. He stood closest to the wall, at the edge of his lamp's reach, and after a long time he reached out and touched the ice — just one gloved fingertip, resting on the surface above one of the smaller shapes, one of the porters.

"My grandfather carried loads on this range," he said, to the ice as much as to us. "For the survey houses. For the army columns. He went up one season and the family was told the route took him." His finger stayed where it was. "There was no route. There was no body. There was nothing. My grandmother waited nine years and then married a man she did not love, and she never once said my grandfather's name aloud again, because saying it meant admitting he was gone."

The hum of the meltwater filled the pause.

"He's not in there," Tomas said gently. "It's been too long. These are older—"

"I know he is not in there." Dorje lowered his hand. "But whoever put them in there is the same whoever. That does not change. That *whoever* is still employed. That whoever probably has a pension." He turned around and looked at each of us in turn, and I will carry his face from that moment for the rest of my life. "So now you understand why the village would not speak of the mountain."

* * *

We worked for two hours. It was Kwan who insisted, and she was right — grief is a luxury you schedule, and evidence is perishable. We photographed the wall from every angle. Tomas logged positions with the theodolite, hands shaking, voice reciting figures like a prayer. Priya recorded everything twice, on two devices, and I noticed she kept one device in an inner pocket against her skin, and I noted that too.

It was during this work that I saw the thing I should have seen immediately, and I want to record my failure honestly because it nearly cost us everything later.

The ice wall was not uniform. Near its left edge, higher up, close under the fresh fracture seam, there was a section where the surface had been disturbed — scratched, scored, marked with tool lines that were recent. Recent meaning days. Meaning weeks at the outside. Meaning since the Meridian Survey arrived.

Someone had been digging at the ice. Chipping at it, near the top, where the fracture had opened a hollow — a shelf, a pocket in the glacier's body, big enough for a man to shelter in if the man were desperate and patient and willing to live inside a tomb to stay hidden.

I raised my lamp toward it and my heart did something complicated.

"Kwan," I said quietly. "Above the third body. The fracture line. Bring your light."

Two lamps converged on the shelf. The scoring marks caught the glow. And in the shadowed pocket behind them, half-hidden by a lip of ice, something glinted — metal, small, cylindrical.

A film canister. Just one, wedged deliberately in view, placed there the way you place a signpost. Placed there for someone to find.

Or placed there as bait.

Kwan started forward. I caught her arm. Above us the fracture seam ticked softly, settling, the glacier shifting its weight like something turning over in sleep, and every instinct I owned — instincts that had kept four other teams alive and gotten one of them killed — stood up and screamed that we were being watched.

The cave breathed its low water-hum. Eleven dead men waited in their blue glass. And somewhere in the dark above the dead, something moved — a scrape, a shift, deliberate enough that all five of us heard it, and all five of us froze.

From the shadow of the ice shelf, a voice cracked and rusted, speaking English with a scholar's precision:

"If you've come for the film, you're forty days too late. And if you've come from the ministry—" a wet, ragged breath, "—then you'd better pray the ice takes you before I do."
