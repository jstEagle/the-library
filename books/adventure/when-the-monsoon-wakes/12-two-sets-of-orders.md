## 12. Two Sets of Orders

I have always believed that guilt is a luxury of people with clean hands, and I have kept my hands very busy indeed.

The night after the crevasse, I sat in my tent with a headlamp turned low and a document folder balanced on my knees, and I told myself what I have told myself for eleven years in government service: that orders are not choices, they are weather. You don't argue with weather. You dress for it, you move through it, and if it kills you, well — no one blames the sky.

The folder was waterproofed, sealed, and stamped with a ministry cipher I had spent three years earning the clearance to read. Inside were two pages and one envelope of photographs. The photographs I did not look at again. I had looked at them once, in Rungli, before we ever set foot on the glacier, and they had shown me six surveyors' tents pitched in perfect order, stoves cold, boots arranged outside the doors like men expecting to be called back for them. The images had been taken from altitude, by drone, by someone who knew exactly where to look. Which meant someone in Rungli had known where to look before Meridian ever went missing. Which meant the survey hadn't been lost. It had been *expected* to stop reporting.

I read the two pages instead. I had them memorized by then, but reading them was a kind of penance, the way Hazel Amberdale reads terrain — not for beauty but for threat.

*In the event the Meridian findings are recoverable, recover them. In the event they are not recoverable, ensure they are not recoverable.*

That was the whole of it. One sentence, twice, with different verbs. Somewhere in the ministry there was a man — I could picture his office, the good tea, the framed photograph of himself shaking hands with someone more important — who had written that sentence and considered it complete. No list of what the findings might be. No definition of *recoverable*. No instruction about the six missing surveyors, whose names appeared nowhere in either page. They were not variables. They were rounding errors.

And beneath that sentence, in smaller type, the part I had recited to myself most often: *The liaison will exercise judgment regarding personnel whose objectives diverge from those of the recovery operation.*

Personnel. Plural. It did not say *the guide*, though we both knew whom it meant. It left me room, you see. That's the cruelty of contingency language — it never orders you to do a thing. It arranges the furniture so that when you do the thing, you can tell yourself you chose it.

I looked at the radio then. It sat in the corner of my tent where it had sat since Rungli, a ministry-issue HF set in a green case, and I thought about the evening at base camp below the last village when Tomas had fiddled with it for an hour trying to raise the district station and gotten nothing but a hiss like sand pouring through the world. Bad ionosphere, I'd said. Storm interference. I'd said it easily, because I am very good at saying things easily, and Tomas had believed me, because he is twenty-six and believes people, which is either his finest quality or the one that will get him killed.

I unscrewed the back panel with the small flat blade of my pocketknife. I had never actually opened it before. In Rungli, a technician from Rathore's own section had "calibrated" it while I signed manifests ten feet away, and I had watched him work and felt nothing I could name — a faint chill, maybe, filed under *not my department*. Now I saw what he'd done. A component removed from the transmitter stage, its leads neatly capped so the board would look whole. A receiver deliberately detuned off our assigned frequencies. And tucked into the housing, a small module I didn't recognize until I turned it over: a beacon. Transmitting our position, on a schedule, to whoever held the matching receiver.

Rathore had not sent us to find the Meridian Survey. He had sent us to find the *findings*, and he had made sure we could never call home, and he had made sure he could always find us. We were not a rescue party. We were a retrieval mechanism with legs, and every one of us — Dorje with his silence, Kwan with his flares, Tomas with his earnest notebook, Hazel with her seven years of exile — had been packed into this valley like instruments in a case, to be used and closed up again.

I sat with that for a long time. Outside, the wind worked at the guy-lines with patient fingers, and somewhere above us the glacier groaned through its long cold argument with gravity, and I thought about how easy it would be to screw the panel back on, zip the folder shut, and go to sleep as Priya Nair, liaison, eleven years of service, pension accruing.

Then I heard the tent flap, and Hazel Amberdale said, "Don't get up," in the voice of a woman who has already decided you're going to anyway.

* * *

She came in without being invited, which surprised me less than it should have. She stood dripping slightly, snowmelt on her gaiters, and she looked at the open radio case, and then at the folder on my knees, and then at me, and she did all three things slowly, the way she does everything, as if the world were a route she was reading for avalanches.

"Well," she said. "So it wasn't just me."

"Just you what?"

"Who couldn't sleep." She crouched by the stove, fed it a tablet of fuel, and watched the blue flame catch. "You've been watching me sleep, Nair. Don't look shocked — you think I got blacklisted for being unobservant? Third night running your lamp's been on past two. And you check the perimeter before you sleep and again when you wake, and you check it like a soldier checks it, not like a desk officer. Somebody taught you that." She glanced at the radio. "Somebody also taught you to lie about a dead radio with a straight face, which takes practice."

I should deny it. That was the protocol; some deep bureaucratic ganglion fired the word *deny* down my spine even now. But I was tired, and the storm was coming — I could feel it in the way the flame leaned, in the pressure behind my ears, the same barometric dread Hazel had named at dinner with a shrug that fooled nobody — and there is only so long a person can stand in rising water pretending it's dry.

"The radio was sabotaged before we left Rungli," I said.

"I know."

"You know?"

"I've known since day three." She said it mildly, horribly mildly. "Tomas couldn't get a carrier wave. Not weak signal — *no* signal, on any band, and Tomas can raise a station from inside a tin box. So either the atmosphere developed a personal grudge against us, or the set's gutted. Then I noticed the case seal had been re-crimped. Factory crimps are uniform. These weren't." She looked up at me. "What I don't know is whether you sabotaged it or whether you knew and let it happen. Those are different crimes, and I've been lying here nights deciding which one makes me angrier."

"And which one won?"

"Neither. What won was watching you give Kwan your spare heat pack yesterday when he thought no one saw." She rubbed her face with both hands, and for a moment she looked not like the disgraced guide from the briefing file but like a woman carrying something heavy up a hill she hadn't chosen. "Talk to me, Priya. Because tomorrow the weather closes and whatever's up in that ice — whatever Rathore actually sent us for — we walk into it blind, with no radio, and I need to know if you're the knife in the party or just another fool holding one."

There it was. The question, laid out flat between us like a crevasse lid about to give.

I want to say I hesitated out of loyalty. I want to say the eleven years weighed on me, the clearances earned, the promotions, the small apartment in the capital with the ministry calendar on the wall. Some of that is true. But the larger truth is uglier and simpler: I hesitated because confessing is a cliff-edge, and I have spent my whole career walking three meters back from every edge, and here was Hazel Amberdale asking me to step off with her, onto ground with no rope above it.

"Read this," I said, and handed her the folder.

* * *

She read fast. Faster than I expected — she read the way she moves, no wasted motion — and when she reached the second sentence, the one about ensuring non-recovery, her face did something I'll remember as long as I live. It didn't harden. It *emptied*. Like watching snow slide off a rockface and leave the bare dark stone underneath.

"Ensure they are not recoverable," she read aloud. Her voice was level. Level the way ice is level right before it isn't. "Six men walked uphill out of their own camp without boots, and the ministry's concern is filing." She looked at the photographs, at the neat tents, the waiting stoves. "They knew. Before we ever flew in, they knew the team wasn't coming back, and they dressed it up as a search and hired the one guide in the country desperate enough to say yes."

"They offered you extradition," I said quietly. "It's in your file."

"My file." She laughed, one dry note with no humor in it anywhere. "Of course it is. He built the trap with my own record, Priya. Seven years ago I called a turn wrong in a whiteout and a client died, and every inquiry since has found the same thing — my error, my responsibility, my name on the report. Do you know what I've learned about scapegoats? The state doesn't hate us. It *keeps* us. A scapegoat in storage is an asset. You can spend her whenever you need the books to balance." She tapped the folder. "And now he needs the books balanced again, and he's spending me. And you."

"Yes," I said. "Me too."

"Are you?" She looked at me straight on, and her eyes in the lamplight were the color of wet slate. "Because there's a third page to this, Nair. The one about personnel whose objectives diverge. I've been around soldiers and I've been around bureaucrats, and that sentence isn't soldier language. Soldier language says *eliminate*. Bureaucrat language says *exercise judgment*. Judgment means deniability. It means if it goes wrong, Rathore has never met you, and if it goes right—" she paused, working it through, and I watched her arrive at it the way you watch someone reach a summit ridge, "—if it goes right, you come home with the findings and a promotion, and the six surveyors stay missing, and I stay ruined, and everyone who knows what's in that ice stays quiet forever. That's the deal you were carrying. Isn't it."

I didn't answer. My silence answered.

Hazel nodded slowly, and what frightened me was that she didn't look angry. She looked *tired*, the specific tiredness of a woman recognizing a shape she'd seen before. "Seven years ago," she said, "there was a man on my rope team — a porter, Tenzin, nineteen years old — and I left him anchored at a belay because the client was dying and I judged — *judged*, Priya, there's that word — that going back for him would kill us all. The mountain disagreed. The inquiry disagreed differently. And every night since, I've lived in the space between what I judged and what was true." She folded the folder closed. "So I'm going to ask you one thing, and I want the truth, because I've had enough of other people's managed truths to last the rest of my life. Right now, tonight, sitting here — what does your judgment say?"

And that was the moment. Not the burning. The question.

Because I went to look for the answer in the place I'd kept it for eleven years — the cool administrative room in my head where facts are weighed and careers are protected — and I found the room empty. Or not empty. *Flooded.* All the tidy furniture floating. Six names carved into a rockface by men who had walked uphill barefoot into the ice rather than wait for whatever came in the night. An old woman in the last village pressing a copper amulet into Dorje's hand and saying the ice keeps its debts. Tomas laughing over the stove last night, telling us about his sister's wedding, alive and warm and utterly unaware that a man in the capital had written a sentence with him on the wrong side of it. And Hazel, asleep across the tent wall, breathing slow, guarded even in sleep, the most competent and most discarded woman I had ever met.

My judgment said: *you are the cleanup crew, Priya. There is no version of these orders where you are anything else.*

"There's a beacon in the radio," I said. "Someone's been tracking us the whole way."

"I assumed as much. Can you kill it?"

"I can do better than kill it." I picked up the pocketknife. "I can make it lie."

* * *

Here is what I did, and I record it plainly because plainness is the only penance available to people like me.

I did not destroy the beacon. Destroying it would tell Rathore's watchers, whoever held the matching receiver, that the instrument had died — and instruments die naturally in storms, true, but a careful man plans for that too. Instead I opened the module and found what I hoped I'd find: a fixed transmission schedule, dumb as a clock. I couldn't reprogram it. But I could starve it. I clipped its antenna lead and spliced in a length of wire from the radio's broken internals, coiled and shielded, enough attenuation to cut its signal to a whisper — strong enough to reach a receiver on a nearby ridgeline, too weak to reach anything beyond. Then I moved the set to the far corner of the camp, behind the rock wall Dorje had built for the kitchen, pointed away from the glacier, and let it keep whispering our position to the stone.

As far as any listener could tell, we were still here. Sleeping in our tents. Waiting out the weather. Exactly where a cleanup crew would want us to be.

Hazel watched me work without comment, and when I finished she said only, "How long have you known what he was?"

"Since Rungli," I said. "Since the technician calibrated the radio and I signed the manifest and told myself it wasn't my department." I looked at my hands. They were steady. That seemed obscene. "Eleven years, Hazel. Eleven years of *not my department*. You know what I've finally understood? There is no department. There's just the mountain, and what's buried in it, and which side of the rope you're standing on."

She was quiet a moment. Then she took the folder from where it lay between us — my orders, my career, my eleven years compressed into two pages and a cipher stamp — and held it out toward the stove.

"No," I said.

Her eyebrows rose.

"Not like that." I took it from her hand. "If you burn it, it's your fire. Your choice, your temper, your recklessness — and everything I did afterward gets explained by the company I kept. The disgraced guide corrupted the poor liaison. Rathore will write that report himself; he's probably drafted it." I fed the pages into the flame one at a time, watching the cipher stamp curl and blacken, watching the sentence about *non-recovery* bloom orange and collapse into ash. "This is mine. I read them. I obeyed them as far as I'm going to. And I burned them myself, awake, knowing exactly what it costs." The last corner caught, and for a heartbeat I saw the word *personnel* glowing like an ember before it went. "Let him explain *that*."

Hazel said nothing for a long moment. The stove ticked. Outside, the wind had begun to rise in earnest, a low freight-train sound building in the high passes, and the first flakes ticked against the flysheet like fingers testing a lock.

"Kwan and Dorje," she said finally. "Do they know?"

"Dorje suspects everything. It's his profession. Kwan follows the mission he's given until someone gives him a better one." I looked at her. "Give him a better one."

"And Tomas?"

"Tomas trusts us." I heard how it sounded and didn't soften it. "That's the problem. Tomorrow he learns what we're actually carrying down this mountain, and either he becomes the strongest man in this party or he shatters, and I genuinely don't know which."

"He'll hold." Hazel stood, stretching, and at the tent flap she paused. Snow swirled past her shoulder into the lamplight, melting on the fabric of her sleeve. "Why?" she asked. Not accusing. Genuinely asking, the way she asks about routes. "Eleven years. Why throw it into a stove for six dead men you never met?"

I thought about the old woman's amulet. *The ice keeps its debts.* I thought about a sentence written in a warm office, reducing human beings to recoverable and non-recoverable. I thought about my own reflection in the polished table of every briefing room I'd ever sat in, nodding along, exercising judgment.

"Because somebody has to pay them," I said. "And I'd rather it cost me a career than cost us the truth."

Hazel looked at me for a long moment, and then she did something I did not expect. She smiled — small, crooked, the first one I'd seen from her, gone almost before it arrived. "Welcome to the rope team, Nair," she said, and ducked out into the storm.

I sat alone with the ashes and the ticking stove and the muffled beacon whispering our false position to the rocks, and I felt lighter than I had in eleven years, which is a terrible thing to feel on the night before everything breaks.

Because the storm was here now — truly here, the monsoon's advance guard arriving early and angry, the kind of front that erases routes and buries camps and gives cover to men with rifles. And far below us, I knew, a helicopter pilot somewhere was looking at a blinking point on a screen, confirming that five fools were still huddled exactly where they were supposed to be.

By morning, that point would be a lie. And when the armed men came up the glacier expecting sleeping targets, they would find cold tents, a cold stove, and a trail leading up — toward the cave under the ice, toward whatever Vann had found, toward the truth that Rathore had sent six of us to bury and that I had just decided, alone, by stove-light, to dig up instead.

The wind screamed down the col. Somewhere above us, in the dark, the ice groaned like something waking.

We would need to move within the hour.
