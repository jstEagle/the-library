## 13. Vann's Journal

Vann slept like a man who had forgotten how and was relearning it badly — in snatches, with his hands curled around the film canisters even unconscious, as if some part of him expected them to be pried away at any moment. Kwan had sedated him lightly, over his furious objections, because the man's frostbite needed tending he wouldn't hold still for. Now he lay in the lee of the ice wall behind our snow cave, breathing in shallow, suspicious increments, and I sat three feet away with his journal in my hands and felt like a thief.

"He'd hate that," Priya said quietly. She was watching me watch him.

"Yes," I said. "Which is why you're going to tell me why I should read it anyway."

"Because tomorrow one of us might be dead, and then it would just be paper." She said it without drama, the way she'd started saying everything since she burned her orders. Grief had flattened her into something very direct. "And because you're the one who's going to decide what happens to him. Not Rathore. You. You should know what you're deciding about."

I wanted to argue. Instead I thumbed on my headlamp, angled it low against the blue of the ice, and opened the journal.

The first thing I noticed was the handwriting: a surveyor's hand, precise, small, ruled by habit — and then, page by page, coming apart at the seams. The letters stayed neat. The sentences stopped being.

* * *

The early entries were almost boring, which is its own kind of heartbreak. Elevations. Barometric readings. Notes on serac movement along the eastern flank, melt rates, a running complaint about the instant coffee. Vann wrote like a man who loved his work the way some people love a language — fluently, constantly, for the pleasure of it. There were sketches: stratigraphic columns, a lenticular cloud, Dorje's profile done in three quick lines with the caption *"our porter D. has seen everything twice and been impressed by none of it."*

Dorje, when I showed him that line later, grunted. It was the closest thing to laughter I'd heard from him above the last village.

Then, on the ninth day of the survey, the tone shifted. Not dramatically. A surveyor doesn't do dramatic. But the coffee complaints stopped, and the entries got longer, and the margins filled up with questions instead of numbers.

*Found an anomaly in the moraine exposure below the eastern seracs. Fabric embedded in old ice — textile, possibly leather. Initial assumption: modern climbing debris. It is not modern.*

The next entry:

*We cut back the face today. I will not describe what we found in this entry. I need to be more systematic. Meridian is a scientific organization. We will document this properly.*

I have read a lot of bad prose written by frightened people — my own included, seven years ago, in the incident report I wrote at 3 a.m. from a tent in the rain while a ministry lawyer waited at base camp to help me find the right words. Frightened people reach for procedure the way drowning men reach for anything. *We will document this properly.* I could hear him saying it out loud, to himself, in the dark of a tent much like mine, telling himself that if the measurements were careful enough, the thing would stay a measurement and not become what it actually was.

It didn't stay a measurement.

* * *

By the twelfth day the entries were transcripts. Vann had photographed everything — hence the canisters welded to his fists — and catalogued the site with a rigor that made my teeth ache, because rigor was all he had. Bodies in the deep ice. Colonial-era uniforms, the kind the old Survey Corps wore before anyone thought to ask what the people who lived here thought about being surveyed. Bound wrists. Bullet wounds. Porters' clothing among them — homespun, local — mixed with the khaki like the mountain itself had refused to keep the categories separate.

*Minimum twelve individuals,* he wrote. *Likely more; the ice has taken some beyond recovery. Wounds consistent with execution, not combat. This is not a battle site. This is where someone brought people to end them, and the glacier kept the receipt.*

*The village accounts are consistent: porters conscripted for a government expedition in the year the records call 'the quiet season.' They did not come back. The families were told they died in a storm. There was no storm. There is always a record, and there is always a story, and they are never the same story.*

I sat with my headlamp humming and my breath fogging the lamplight and understood that I was reading the account of a man walking toward the exact cliff I'd gone over, seven years ahead of schedule.

Because here is what happens next, and it happened to me too, and I would bet my last rupee it has happened to someone else on some other mountain while I was sleeping off a trek in Rungli: he reported it. That was the procedure. That was the *proper documentation*. He radioed the ministry, and he used the correct channels, and he trusted that the truth, once delivered to the people whose job it was to receive truths, would be handled like something valuable.

*Radioed District Office, priority channel, per protocol. Acknowledgment received. Instructed to remain on station and secure the site. Reinforcement team dispatched.*

He underlined *reinforcement team* twice. I could see the exact moment the underline changed meaning — the first pass, hope; the second, four days later, dread. Because the reinforcement team took nine days to arrive, and when it came, it came at night, and it did not come to secure a massacre site.

*They came at 0300. Six men, armed, no insignia. I knew two of the faces from ministry functions — the kind of men who carry other men's authority the way porters carry loads, except what they were carrying was orders to make us the eleventh through twenty-second bodies in the ice.*

*Okafor tried to talk to them. He led with the data. He honestly believed the data would protect us — that they had come because the findings mattered, that knowledge was the whole point of the exercise. I watched him understand, in real time, standing in the snow in his long underwear, that we were not the point. We had never been the point. We were a complication.*

*I ran. I want to write that I fought, or that I carried someone, or that I did anything but run. I ran. I know this terrain better than they ever would — that is the only reason I am alive and they are not looking for a sixteenth body. I went up. Into the caves under the seracs, where the meltwater goes. I took the film because Okafor shoved the canisters into my chest and told me to go, and the last thing I heard was him saying my name, not screaming, just saying it, like a man handing off a baton.*

*That was nineteen days ago.*

I looked up. The cave breathed around us — that slow glacial respiration, air moving through passages older than every government that had ever claimed this range. Kwan sat awake across the chamber, watching the entrance, her flare gun across her knees. Tomas slept the boneless sleep of the young. Dorje was outside somewhere, doing whatever Dorje did in the dark, which I suspected was listening.

Nineteen days. The man had lived alone in the guts of a glacier for nineteen days on meltwater and whatever rations he could salvage, keeping the film warm inside his jacket against his skin, coming down at night to check whether anyone had come for him, and each time learning that no one had — or worse, that the ones who came were the wrong ones.

*Day 4:* *No aircraft today. Or none that stopped.*

*Day 7:* *Saw lights below camp. Did not go down. Watched until dawn. They burned the tents. All of it. Our gear, our records, the copies of the site documentation. Very thorough. Whoever sent them wants this mountain to have no memory except the one it keeps itself.*

*Day 11:* *I have begun talking to the dead. Not literally. Mostly not literally. I tell them what I'm doing. I think Okafor would approve of the methodology. I think Okafor would tell me to stop apologizing and start planning.*

*Day 14:* *If anyone reads this: I am not crazy. Check the ice below the eastern seracs. Count the uniforms. Count the homespun. Ask the villages what happened in the quiet season. The state will tell you I am mad, or a traitor, or a ghost. I am none of those things. I am a geologist. I know how to read strata, and this country's history is written in layers, and somebody has been falsifying the record for seventy years.*

*Day 17:* *I keep thinking about the report I'll write. Isn't that absurd. Nineteen days in the ice and I am composing the report. As if the report is the thing that saves anyone. As if paper stops bullets. Okafor believed in paper. I watched paper fail him at three in the morning.*

*Day 19 (morning):* *People on the glacier. Five of them. One moves like a guide.*

I closed the journal on that line and found my eyes hot and my jaw aching from clenching.

One moves like a guide. He'd watched us come up the icefall and the only thing he could classify us as, after everything, was a threat with good footwork. And who could blame him? Every person who had ever arrived on this mountain carrying official paper had arrived to erase something. I had arrived carrying Rathore's paper. I had signed his deal in a district office because the alternative was extradition, and I had told myself it was survival, and maybe it was, but Vann had spent nineteen days learning exactly what that signature purchased.

Here is the thing nobody tells you about disgrace: it makes you fluent in other people's. Seven years ago a storm killed a client on my rope and the ministry needed a name to hang it on, and mine fit, and so I became the woman who broke the rule — *no one left behind* — except the full report said the weather window collapsed in forty minutes and the decision point was already past, and none of that mattered, because a scapegoat isn't a conclusion, it's a convenience. I knew what it was to have your truest account rewritten by people with better stationery. Reading Vann's journal was like reading my own incident report written by a braver man.

The difference between us — and I turned it over all night, because it mattered — was that Vann had run *toward* the evidence. He'd crawled into the ice with the film against his heart and stayed there, starving, half-mad, rather than come down empty-handed. I had come down from my disaster with nothing but a clean jacket and a ruined name and let both define me. He'd had nineteen days to become bitter and instead he'd become a witness. There's a difference, and the difference is everything, and I intended to get him down this mountain alive if it cost me the last shreds of whatever Rathore still held over me.

Because that was the other thing the journal settled. Rathore hadn't sent a recovery team. He'd sent a search party — searching for the film, for the journal, for the loose end named Elias Vann. The gear photos, the extradition threat, the short weather window: all of it calibrated to put expendable people on the ice to flush out a survivor, and then the cleanup crew would tidy the whole equation. Priya had figured it out first. I'd signed it anyway. The journal didn't change what I'd done. It changed what I'd do.

* * *

Near dawn — that grey non-time when the storm outside paused to reload — Vann woke, and his eyes found the journal in my lap before they found my face.

"You read it." His voice was gravel and rust. Not a question.

"Some of it."

"Then you know I watched you for two days before I let you find me." He pushed himself up against the ice wall, wincing where Kwan's dressings pulled. "Two days deciding whether you were the second team or the third kind."

"What's the third kind?"

"The kind that comes to help." He laughed, or produced the sound where laughter used to live. "I'd forgotten that kind existed. That's what nineteen days does. It edits your categories." His gaze went to the canisters, still strapped to his chest, then to me. "You move like a guide. I wrote that. I've been reading the way you handle the rope, the way you read the ice. So I'll ask the only question that matters, and I'd like the truth, because I have had enough of officials and their versions."

"Ask."

"Why did they send you? The truth. Not the cover memo."

I gave it to him straight: the letter, the extradition threat, the deal, the photos of the abandoned camp laid out on Rathore's desk like bait. I watched him take it in without flinching, the way you'd watch a man confirm a diagnosis he'd already suspected.

"So I'm the job," he said. "And the film is the deliverable."

"The film is the deliverable," I agreed. "You were supposed to be recovered. What happens after recovery was never specified. I've learned to notice what isn't specified."

"And now?"

"Now I've read your journal. Now Priya's burned her orders. Now there are six armed men somewhere on this mountain with a shorter supply line than ours and no interest in taking prisoners." I fed the stove a pinch of fuel and watched the flame stand up straight in the still air. "So here's my version, Dr. Vann, and you can take it or leave it: nobody delivers you anywhere. Not to Rathore, not to a cell, not to a quiet disappearance. We walk you out ourselves, all the way down, and the film goes to someone who'll print it, not file it. That's the plan. It's a bad plan. It's the only one I've got."

He studied me for a long moment, this man who had spent nineteen days learning to distrust the human face, and I watched him decide — not that I was trustworthy, he wasn't fool enough for that, but that I was the best available version of the truth.

"My grandmother," he said finally, "kept a phrase on her kitchen wall. *The record is the resistance.* She survived something you'd file under the same heading as what's in my ice. She said the people who win aren't the ones with the guns. They're the ones who write it down before the guns arrive." He tapped the canisters with one frost-nipped finger. "Everything's in there. Site coordinates, photographs, the radio logs, the names of the men who came at night — I memorized the faces, I can draw them. Seventy years of it. Enough to hang a ministry."

"Enough to get us all killed getting it down," Kwan said from the entrance, without turning around. "Storm's shifting. Wind's backing to the southeast, which means it'll funnel up the valley we need to descend."

"How long?" I asked.

Kwan checked the sky the way other people check a watch. "Hours. Maybe six of them where the mountain lets us move. After that, monsoon proper, and nobody moves for days — including, if we're lucky, the men hunting us."

"Six hours," I said. "Down through the icefall, past the seracs, to the traverse. With a half-frozen witness, a wounded—" I stopped. Priya's wound was bound and stable, but *stable* is a word that means *not yet worse*, and mountains eat *not yet worse* for breakfast. "With what we've got."

"That's the plan?" Vann asked. "Six hours?"

"No," I said. "The plan is what Dorje's doing right now."

We all looked at the entrance. Dorje stood there, backlit by the grey, holding the copper amulet in his open palm, and the look on his face was one I hadn't seen on him in nine days on the ice — not taciturn, not unreadable, but *certain*, the way a man looks when the debt he's been carrying has finally come due and he knows the road home.

"I know a way down," Dorje said, "that is not on any map the ministry owns. My grandfather walked it. Before they killed him."

Outside, the wind changed its mind, and the monsoon cleared its throat, and we started packing.
