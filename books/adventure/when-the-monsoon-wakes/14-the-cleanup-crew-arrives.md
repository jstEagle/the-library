## 14. The Cleanup Crew Arrives

I want it on the record — my record, the one nobody will ever read, because I am a junior surveyor whose most significant professional accomplishment to date is not dying in a crevasse — that I heard the helicopters first.

Not Kwan, with his army ears and his habit of tilting his head like a heron before speaking. Not Dorje, who reads weather in the behavior of ravens. Me. Tomas Okafor-Reyes, twenty-six years old, the youngest person on this mountain by a decade, and for once in this expedition's miserable history, the canary in the coal mine was me.

We were back at the cave. That's the thing you need to understand about the timeline: we had gone down to the icefall camp to rest and argue and pretend we weren't carrying the moral equivalent of a live grenade, and then Vann had refused to stay at the camp — *they'll come for the film where they expect it to be*, he kept saying, in the voice of a man who had spent weeks talking to ice — so we'd hauled ourselves back up through the moraine to the cave under the seracs, all of us, because Hazel had made the call that we don't split up. The rope-team rule. She said it like a woman reciting a penance, and none of us understood why until later, and by later everything was white.

So: the cave. Mid-morning, if you could call it morning, the light coming through the glacier's throat the color of old bottle glass. Priya was rewrapping Vann's frostbitten hands. Dorje sat at the cave mouth with the copper amulet in his palm, turning it over the way other men turn coins when they're nervous. Hazel was studying the journal by headlamp even though there was daylight, because she said she wanted every word of it in her head before anyone could take the paper away from her. And I was doing what I do best, which is fussing with instruments — Vann's instruments, actually, the Meridian Survey's theodolite and barometers arranged around that wall of black ice like an altar, and I'd been straightening them, cataloguing them, giving myself something small and knowable to do while the big unknowable thing sat frozen ten feet away wearing colonial-era uniforms.

That's when I heard it. A sound under the sound of the glacier. A thumping, arrhythmic, wrong — the mountain doesn't thump. The mountain creaks and groans and cracks like an old ship, but it does not *thump*.

"Is that—" I started, and Kwan's hand closed on my shoulder hard enough to leave a bruise, and he tilted his head, heron-style, and went very still.

"Rotors," he said. "Two. Maybe three. Coming up the valley from the south."

Nobody moved for maybe three seconds. Three seconds is a long time when your whole future rearranges itself. I remember thinking, absurdly, about the permit paperwork Rathore's office had filed for us — I'd seen copies, I'd *organized* the copies, because that is who I am as a person — and nowhere in those documents was there any mention of helicopter support, because helicopters couldn't fly in this weather window, that was the entire point of the window, that was why we were racing the monsoon on foot like it was 1953.

Which meant these weren't our helicopters. Which meant—

"Priya," Hazel said quietly. "You want to tell me what's coming up that valley?"

Priya's face did something complicated. She'd burned her orders two nights ago — I watched her feed them into the stove piece by piece like she was feeding a fire a confession — but apparently some orders don't burn all the way through. "Rathore said nothing about a second team," she said. "He told me the radio failure was equipment." Her voice went flat and careful. "He lied about the radio. He would lie about this."

"How long?" Hazel asked Kwan.

Kwan cocked his head again, listening. "Ten minutes. Less if they've got a landing spot scouted. There's flat ice on the shelf above the seracs — good enough for a light utility bird in a pinch."

"The cave," Vann said, and his voice cracked like thin ice. He'd been half-frozen for weeks; his voice did everything like thin ice now. "They'll find the cave. They always knew about the cave. They told us to wait here — *wait here, Dr. Vann, assistance is en route* — and then the men came at night with rifles and I ran into the ice like a rat into a wall, and you're standing in the place they came to empty out." He was pressing himself backward against the black ice wall as though it could swallow him, which, given what was inside it, felt like asking the wolf for shelter from the dog.

Hazel stood up. I've watched Hazel Amberdale make maybe forty decisions since Rungli, and I have a theory that she makes them all in the same half-second and spends the rest of the time deciding whether to say so out loud. This one took less than half a second.

"We're leaving. Now. Everything goes with us — film, journal, Vann, nothing stays in this cave for them to photograph and burn." She was already kicking snow over the stove's remains. "Dorje — how do we get off this shelf without going past the landing zone?"

Dorje rose without hurrying, which is how he does everything, including, I suspect, outrunning avalanches. He looked at the amulet, then at the white glare at the cave mouth, then at the mountain itself, the way you'd look at a relative you were about to ask a dangerous favor of.

"There is the meltwater channel," he said. "West side. It drops through the seracs to the lower glacier. Bad ground. Ice the consistency of bad bread."

"Bad bread will do," Hazel said. "Kwan, point. Dorje, sweep. Priya, you've got Vann. Tomas—"

"I'll carry the instruments," I said, because someone had to say something useful and also because those instruments were the only physical proof besides the bodies themselves that the Meridian Survey had been here doing actual science, and I had already decided, somewhere in the last two days, that I was willing to be shot over a theodolite. My mother would be furious. My mother has been furious about my career choices since I chose them.

"No," Hazel said. "Leave the instruments."

"They're evidence—"

"The bodies are evidence. The journal is evidence. The film is evidence. Brass and glass are weight, and weight is time, and we just ran out of time." She looked at me, and there was something in her face that wasn't unkind but was adjacent to it — the look of someone who has buried enough weight on mountains to know exactly what it costs. "I'm sorry. Go."

I left the instruments. I want you to understand that it physically hurt, that some part of me is still up there in that cave straightening a theodolite no one will ever read the measurements from. But I left them, and that decision bought us maybe ninety seconds, and ninety seconds turned out to matter.

* * *

The storm arrived on schedule.

I mean that literally — Dorje had been calling it for two days, watching the cloud stack up over the western peaks like a debt collector, and he'd said, in his unhurried way, that it would break "when the mountain decides." Well. The mountain decided at approximately the same moment the first helicopter cleared the southern ridge, and I have since had occasion to wonder whether the old woman's amulet works both ways — whether the ice keeps its debts, plural, whether it collected ours and theirs in the same afternoon.

The wind hit us as we came out of the cave mouth, a horizontal shove of spindrift that erased the world beyond twenty meters. Above us, through the tearing white, I heard the rotors change pitch — the pilot fighting the same air we were drowning in — and then a shape resolved overhead, huge and gray and briefly, horribly clear: a utility helicopter, unmarked, skids hung with gear, and a man leaning out of the open door with binoculars.

He saw us. I'm sure of it. Twenty meters of visibility works both directions when the other party is looking down from fifty feet. For one suspended moment the man in the door and I made eye contact across the chaos, and I saw him lift a hand — not waving. Signaling. Pointing down at us, then sweeping toward the shelf, directing.

Then the cloud took the helicopter back, and Kwan grabbed my pack strap and hauled me westward, and the chase began in earnest.

Here is what a chase looks like on a glacier in a building whiteout, in case you were imagining something with music: it looks like nothing. It sounds like rotor noise swelling and fading behind you like a heartbeat you can't trust. It feels like running blind over ground that wants to kill you anyway, following the heels of the person ahead, except the person ahead is a silhouette and then a suggestion and then a memory, and the rope at your waist goes taut and slack and taut, and every taut moment is a small prayer that the person on the other end hasn't stepped into a crevasse that the storm is hiding specifically for you.

The six men came down the shelf behind us. I never got a clean look at more than two of them at once — whiteout fragments, dark shapes in unmarked gear moving with the economy of people who train for this — but I counted muzzle flashes once, two sharp orange stutters in the white, firing at shapes or at nothing, warning shots or bad marksmanship or just the storm being generous. Kwan didn't return fire. "Waste of flares," he shouted at me at one point, which I chose to interpret as gallows humor rather than an accounting error.

The man leading them — I saw him once, close, for three seconds, and three seconds was plenty — wasn't dressed like soldiers dress. No insignia, no flags, gear with the tags cut out, the sartorial signature of deniability. But he carried himself like authority, the way Rathore had carried himself in that district office, and when he shouted an order his voice cut through the wind with the confidence of a man who has never once been told no by anything softer than gravity. I heard him call out, once, over the storm:

"Dr. Vann! You're ill! You need medical assistance!"

And Vann, roped between Priya and me, laughing — a horrible sound, breathless and cracked — called back: "You offered assistance last time too!"

After that they stopped pretending and started shooting in earnest.

* * *

The meltwater channel nearly killed us four separate times, and I will describe only the worst, because a man has limits.

It was a slot where the summer melt had carved a tunnel under the seracs and then collapsed its roof, leaving a trench of blue ice polished slick as glass, dropping in steps. Going down it in clear weather would be a technical descent requiring anchors and patience. We went down it in a whiteout with armed men above us, on a running belay, which is mountaineering language for *we did something stupid fast instead of something smart slow*.

Hazel went first. Of course Hazel went first. She moved down that trench like water finding its level, placing her axe picks by feel, calling holds back up to us in short flat words — *left foot, ledge, step wide, don't trust the middle* — and I followed her voice more than her shape, because the shape kept disappearing. Behind me, Priya fed Vann down hand over hand, and Vann, who hadn't walked more than a kilometer in six weeks, descended on adrenaline and fury alone, making sounds I will hear in dreams for years.

Halfway down, the ice gave.

Not under me — under Dorje, sweeping at the rear, and the crack came up through the rope like a rifle shot of its own, and then the rope came tight around my waist with a force that lifted me off my feet and slammed me against the trench wall, and for one full second I was a link in a chain holding a falling man over a drop I couldn't see the bottom of.

I have thought about that second a lot since. In that second I learned what Hazel must have known for seven years — that the rope doesn't care about your guilt or your redemption, it only cares whether you hold, and holding is not a moral act, it's a physical one, boots braced, axe planted, body screaming, hold, hold, *hold* — and then Kwan was beside me with his boot over mine and his weight added to the line, and below us Hazel had anchored somehow, impossibly, in the dark, and the rope stopped paying out, and Dorje's voice floated up out of the white, unhurried as ever:

"I am fine. The ice is not."

We got him up. Of course we did. Nobody left behind. I heard Hazel say it under her breath as we hauled — not to us, to herself, like a rosary — and I finally understood that for her this whole catastrophe was happening twice: once on this mountain, and once seven years ago, in a place she never talks about, where the rope came tight and she let go. I didn't ask. Some ledgers aren't mine to read.

* * *

We lost them at the bottom of the channel.

Not cleanly — I could still hear the rotors quartering the upper glacier for another hour, hunting, the sound bouncing off the seracs so it seemed to come from everywhere — but the storm closed the door behind us, and the men in the unmarked gear were not mountain men. That much was obvious from their tracks, when we crossed them once in a lull: they moved in a straight line because their training told them to, and glaciers punish straight lines. They'd already postholed to the thigh twice. One of them had dropped a glove. Kwan picked it up, examined it, and dropped it in a crevasse with an expression I'd describe as professional courtesy.

By late afternoon we were on the lower glacier, moving south through weather that had graduated from hostile to apocalyptic, and the conversation happened that I suppose I always knew was coming.

Hazel called a halt in the lee of an ice block, and we huddled — six people and a film canister, which was effectively a seventh member of the party, the heaviest one — and she laid it out with the terrible clarity of a woman who has already done the arithmetic.

"They'll sweep the lower glacier by morning. They have thermal imaging and we have a hypothermic geologist. We can't outrun them and we can't hide from them, not all of us together." She looked at each of us in turn. "So we split the cargo. Someone takes Vann and the film down a route they won't expect, and someone else makes noise on the main route and draws the sweep."

Silence. Wind. Somewhere above, thunder — real thunder now, the monsoon arriving in earnest, rain beginning to fall on snow at this altitude, which meant everything below us was about to turn to soup.

"No," said Priya.

"It's the only play—"

"You said no one left behind." Priya's voice was shaking, and not from cold. "On the glacier. You said it like a vow. Now you want to split us in a whiteout with shooters on the ice?"

Hazel closed her eyes. When she opened them, she looked older, and I realized I was watching a woman stand at the exact intersection of her worst memory and her best instinct, and neither one would yield the crossing.

"I know what I said," she said quietly. "And I know what breaking that rule cost me. But there's a version of *no one left behind* that gets all six of us captured and the film burned and Vann dead, and that version helps no one. The rule isn't *stay together*. The rule is *nobody carries the weight alone*." She held out her hand, palm up, to the center of our huddle. "So we decide together. All of us. Right now. Who goes, who stays, who carries what. Nobody gets assigned. Everybody volunteers."

And that — I swear this is true — that was the moment I stopped being afraid. Not rationally; the fear stayed, it lives in my chest now like a tenant who pays no rent. But I put my hand on hers, and then Kwan did, and Dorje placed his copper-amulet hand on top of ours like a benediction, and Priya last of all, and Vann reached out from the middle of us and laid his bandaged fingers on the pile, and we made the plan in the roaring dark.

I won't tell you the plan. Not because it's secret — the storm will keep it better than I could — but because plans made in whiteouts have a life expectancy measured in minutes, and ours died within the hour, gloriously, at a place called the icefall, in a way I still can't talk about without my hands shaking.

What I can tell you is what I heard, as we moved south into the rain and the rotors hunted the glacier behind us: Hazel, at the front, saying it again, to herself, to the mountain, to whoever keeps the ledger of miles and debts.

"No one left behind. Not this time. Not ever again."

The monsoon broke fully over the Khumbal Range, and the mountain began, at last, to wash itself clean — and somewhere above us in the white, six armed men learned what Dorje's grandmother's generation always knew: that the ice keeps its debts, and it collects from whoever is standing on it when the bill comes due.
