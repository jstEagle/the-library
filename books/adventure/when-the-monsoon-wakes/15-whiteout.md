## 15. Whiteout

The storm found us an hour after we lost the helicopters, and it did not come gently. It came the way debts come — first a whisper, then a demand, then the whole edifice of your life collapsing around your ears.

We were still above the icefall when the sky went from grey to nothing. One moment I could see the seracs rearing up like broken teeth against the cloud; the next, the world simply ended at arm's length. Wind screamed down off the col with a sound like a freight train falling downstairs, and the snow stopped being weather and became a substance, a white solid you had to push through rather than walk in.

"Snow cave," I shouted, and my voice came back to me flattened, as if the mountain had already decided what it thought of my opinions.

Dorje heard me. Dorje always heard me, even when the wind was trying to peel the sound out of the air. He put two fingers to his lips and whistled — a thin, piercing note that cut through the gale like wire through cheese — and we turned into the wind together and stumbled downslope until he stopped and pointed his ice axe at a drift banked against the lee side of a ridge shoulder.

It took us forty minutes to dig. Forty minutes of Kwan and Tomas taking turns on the shovel while Priya lay propped against her pack, grey-faced under her parka hood, one hand pressed to the dressing on her shoulder where the bullet had gone through flesh and not bone. She hadn't made a sound since the icefall. That frightened me more than the storm did. People who are hurt and silent are either very brave or very much worse than they're letting on.

The cave, when it was done, was barely big enough for five. A low tunnel burrowed into the drift, a sleeping platform raised at the back, walls packed smooth by the flat of the shovel and then by our hands. We crawled in out of the white roar and the silence that followed was so total it rang. The kind of silence that has a heartbeat in it. Ours.

Kwan got Priya's boot off and checked the circulation in her toes, then her fingers, then made her drink water with salt dissolved in it while she watched him with the flat, patient eyes of someone who has decided not to complain about anything ever again. Tomas melted snow on the little stove, feeding it fuel tablets with the careful economy of a man who knows exactly how many are left and doesn't want to say the number out loud. Vann sat in the deepest corner with the film canisters strapped inside his jacket against his chest, arms wrapped around himself, shivering in that deep rhythmic way that isn't performance — it's the body doing arithmetic it doesn't like.

I sat nearest the entrance, because that's where I always sit. Old habit. The sentry spot. The place where whatever comes for you comes for you first.

For a long time nobody talked. There's a particular quality to exhaustion that makes conversation feel like spending money you don't have. We'd run through whiteout on rotten glacier with six armed men somewhere behind us and a wounded woman between us, and every one of us had burned through something today that wouldn't be refilled by sleep.

It was Tomas who finally broke it, because Tomas was young enough to still believe that problems yield to being discussed.

"We can't all make it down." He said it quietly, staring at the stove flame as if it might contradict him. "Not in this. Not with—" He nodded at Priya without looking at her, which was its own kind of mercy. "The monsoon's closing. Rathore's people will sweep the lower routes when it lifts. If we're all together, we're all slow, and if we're all slow—"

"Tomas." Kwan's voice, low.

"No. Let me say it. Someone has to say it." He looked around the cave, and in the stove light his face was hollowed out, older than his years. "Vann carries the film. Vann is the evidence. If one person gets out with him — one strong person, moving fast, light — the rest of us split their attention. Draw them off. Buy time."

"You mean some of us stay behind," Dorje said. Not asking. Stating, the way he stated most things, like reading coordinates off a map.

"I mean the mission survives," Tomas said. "That's what missions are. You don't get to keep everyone."

* * *

I let the silence sit there for a moment, because I wanted to be sure I answered the boy and not my own fear.

Seven years ago, in another range, under another sky, I had stood in a tent almost exactly this size and listened to almost exactly this argument. Different voices. Same arithmetic. Three climbers stranded high, storm closing, one rope team fit enough to move and one too hurt to try. And a younger Hazel Amberdale — licensed, respected, certain of her own competence the way only the untested are — had listened to the logic and found it airtight. Go down fast. Get help. Come back for them.

The logic had been airtight. The avalanche had come in the eleven hours it took to walk the rescue up. And when they pulled what was left out of the snow, the inquiry had needed someone to blame, and the guide who left them was so much more convenient than the mountain that had killed them. Blacklisted. Erased. Seven years of measuring my life in distances walked because distances didn't ask questions.

Here is what I learned that I did not learn in any inquiry report: the rule exists for the moment when breaking it feels reasonable. That's the whole point of it. *No one left behind* isn't a sentiment. It's a load-bearing wall. You take it out and everything else comes down eventually — the trust, the team, the person you have to look at in the mirror at three in the morning when the stove won't light.

"No," I said.

Tomas flinched. "Hazel—"

"No. Listen to me, because I'm only going to say this once and then I'm going to sleep and you're going to think about it while I do." I leaned forward into the stove light. "Seven years ago I split a team. Sent the strong ones down, stayed logic on my side of the ledger. Two men died waiting for a rescue that arrived eleven hours late. Eleven hours, Tomas. Do you know how long eleven hours is in a storm? It's forever. It's the rest of your life, compressed."

Nobody moved. Even the wind outside seemed to lean in.

"The rule isn't there because it always works. Sometimes staying together kills everyone. I know that. You know that. But here's what splitting does even when it works: it makes the leaving permanent. It teaches everyone watching that they're cargo — that when the weight gets heavy, somebody does the math and puts them down. Priya took a bullet today standing over a man she met nine days ago. What do you imagine she thinks we owe her?"

Priya's voice came out of her hood, thin but steady. "She thinks we owe her the truth. All of it. That's why she's still carrying it." Her mouth twitched — almost a smile. "Also she thinks Tomas is an idiot, but a well-meaning one."

"Thank you," said Tomas, with dignity.

"But she's right about the math being real," Kwan said. He'd been quiet so long I'd nearly forgotten he was there, which with Kwan was a tactical error. He unfolded from the wall, joints cracking, and held up one hand, ticking points off. "One: we have maybe two days of fuel. Two: Priya can't move fast, maybe not at all tomorrow. Three: Rathore's crew knows these slopes now; they'll sweep the standard lines. Four—" he paused, and his eyes went to Vann, huddled in the corner with his arms around the film like a man holding a child, "—four, our passenger is half-dead already and getting deader."

"So the honest answer," Tomas said, "is that both options are terrible."

"The honest answer," I said, "is that terrible options are the only ones worth having rules for. When the choices are good, anyone can choose. The rule matters when it costs." I looked around the cave, at each of them. "So here's mine. We go down together. Slow. Dorje picks the line — not the standard routes, the old porter trails, the ones that don't appear on Rathore's maps. We move when the visibility allows, hole up when it doesn't, and we spend Priya's strength like it's the last of the fuel, because it is. Nobody walks ahead. Nobody stays behind. If the mountain takes one of us, it takes all of us, and the story dies with us — fine. At least it dies whole."

"And if that means none of us makes it?" Tomas asked. Quietly. Not challenging anymore. Asking, the way you ask a question you already fear the answer to.

"Then we'll have kept the only thing we actually control," I said. "Who we were while we failed."

* * *

The stove died an hour later and the cold came in like a tenant who'd been waiting for the room. We arranged ourselves the way you do in a snow cave — bodies against bodies, warmest in the middle, wounded at the center of the warmth. Priya in the middle, then Vann, then Kwan, then me, then Dorje at the entrance because he insisted and because arguing with Dorje about sentry duty is like arguing with a glacier about where it wants to be.

Sleep came in fragments. Somewhere in the small hours I surfaced to hear Vann talking — not to me, not really to anyone, the low muttering of a man whose defenses have been eroded past the point of maintenance. He was telling Dorje about the night the armed men came. About crawling into the ice caves while his colleagues' torches went out one by one behind him. About forty days in the dark, eating survey rations and meltwater, listening to the glacier settle around him like a thing digesting.

"They waited," he whispered. "They radioed and the ministry told them to wait, and they waited, and then the waiting killed them. Don't wait. Whatever happens — don't be the ones who wait."

"We're not waiting," Dorje said. His voice in the dark was gravel and calm. "We're resting. There's a difference. Waiting is what you do when you've given your route away. Resting is what you do when you know exactly where you're going tomorrow."

"Do you?" Vann asked. "Know where you're going?"

There was a pause long enough that I thought Dorje had gone back to sleep. Then, softly, so quietly I almost missed it:

"My grandfather walked these trails before there were maps. The ice keeps its debts. Tomorrow we collect."

* * *

Morning, if you could call it that, was a grey suggestion through the snow-blocked entrance. The storm had dropped to a snarl rather than a scream — still whiteout above, but down in the drifts and hollows the air moved instead of merely existing. Kwan redressed Priya's shoulder and pronounced her, in his laconic field-medic way, "alive, furious, and mobile, in descending order of certainty."

We ate the last proper meal — oatmeal and sugar, hot, eaten slowly and without conversation, the way you eat a meal you suspect is also a farewell to something. Then I crawled to the entrance and dug out the snow plug and looked at the world.

White. Endless, featureless, hostile white. Somewhere below us was the last village, four days' travel on a good day. Somewhere behind us were six men with rifles and a helicopter. Somewhere inside my jacket, in a waterproof bag, was a copy of the journal pages I'd transcribed by headlamp — insurance, though against what, I couldn't have said.

"Hazel." Dorje crouched beside me at the entrance, the copper amulet glinting dully at his throat. He was looking down-slope into the murk with an expression I'd learned to read as close to troubled. "Storm's turning. Coming round to the southwest by afternoon. If we take the old trail along the moraine, we'll have the wind at our backs and their eyes full of weather."

"And if we're wrong?"

He smiled — the first time I'd seen him smile since the amulet, and it wasn't entirely comforting. "Then we'll be wrong together. All five of us. Equally."

I looked back into the cave — at Priya flexing her bad arm, jaw set; at Tomas checking and rechecking straps he'd already checked; at Kwan packing the med kit with surgical finality; at Vann, watching me with fever-bright eyes, one hand pressed flat against the film canisters over his heart.

Five of us. One truth. One way down.

"All right," I said, and the word came out steadier than I felt, which is the most a guide can ever promise. "Rope up. No one left behind."

And that was when Tomas, squinting down-slope into the thinning murk, went rigid and grabbed my sleeve.

"Hazel. Down there. On the moraine — movement. Two of them. Maybe three. They've found the trail."
