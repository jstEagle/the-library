## 17. Ambush at the Icefall

I have been shot at twice in my life before this mountain. The first time was in Kunming, by a man whose wife I had — this is a long story and not a flattering one — and the second time was during my last year of service, in a place I'm still not cleared to name. Both times, the thing I remember most clearly is how ordinary the sound is. Not like the movies. Like a door slamming in another room. Your body understands it before your mind does: *that sound means someone is trying to end you*, and then everything goes very simple and very fast.

The third time was at the icefall, and it sounded exactly the same.

But let me back up, because the icefall deserves its introduction.

* * *

We had come down off the glacier's shoulder in the grey hour before dawn, moving on Dorje's instinct and Hazel's arithmetic. The monsoon was close now — you could smell it, that green wet weight riding in from the south like an animal smelling blood — and every hour we delayed was an hour the storm would spend on top of us instead of behind us. Vann could barely walk. Priya had taken his arm over her shoulder without being asked, and something in the way she did it told me she'd already made her decision about which side of this story she wanted to die on. People make those decisions quietly, days before anyone notices. I've watched soldiers do it. The ones who survive are usually the ones who decided early.

Dorje stopped us below a wall of blue shadow and said one word: "Icefall."

He said it the way you'd say *landslide* or *widow*. The Khumbal's icefall wasn't a route so much as a wound — a place where the glacier poured over a rock step and shattered itself into towers the size of buildings, leaning against each other in attitudes of slow collapse. Somewhere in there, Dorje said, was a bridge. His grandfather's generation had used it. His grandfather's generation had also, he did not say but I understood, died in numbers on this same ground, and the amulet at his throat caught the pre-dawn light like a small stubborn coal.

"Through it or around it?" Hazel asked.

"Around takes two days. Through takes four hours." He looked up at the seracs, reading them the way Hazel read campsites — for what was wrong with them. "Four hours if the mountain lets us."

"The mountain," Tomas said, "has not been letting us do anything all week."

"The mountain," Hazel said, "is the least of our problems right now." She turned and looked down-valley, the way she'd been turning and looking down-valley every twenty minutes since the helicopters came. "It's him I don't trust to give us four hours."

She meant Rathore's men. Six of them, unmarked gear, dropped onto the glacier below us the previous afternoon like seeds out of a pod. We'd watched them through Tomas's binoculars, fanning out across the ice with the unhurried competence of people who had done this kind of work before and expected to do it again. They weren't searching for us. They were herding us. Every route we'd considered since had somehow been the route they were positioned to watch, until the only direction left was through the icefall — which, I realized later, was probably the point. A collapsing maze is a wonderful place to corner someone. You only have to guard the exits.

Kwan, I told myself, you are a medic, not a tactician. But I had carried a rifle for eleven years, and some arithmetic doesn't wash out. Six hunters, five of us, one of us half-dead already. The math was ugly no matter how you arranged it.

So we went into the ice.

* * *

What I remember of the crossing itself is mostly sound. The icefall talks constantly — groans, ticks, deep subterranean booms like a giant clearing its throat under the floor of the world — and every sound makes you flinch because every sound might be the one that precedes several thousand tons of ice deciding to lie down. We moved rope-length apart, fast and quiet, following Dorje's memory and the copper amulet, which he touched before every turn as though asking permission. Once, crossing a slope of old avalanche debris, Vann stumbled and went down hard, and Priya caught him, and for a moment they were both just kneeling in the blue light among the frozen rubble, and I thought: *this is what we look like from above. Small. Slow. Easy.*

Then the light changed ahead of us, and Dorje raised his fist, and we stopped.

The bridge was real. That was the miracle of it — a shelf of ice maybe three meters wide spanning a crevasse you could have swallowed a bus, worn smooth and glassy, older than any of us, holding. On the far side the route climbed toward the moraine and the village beyond and everything that resembled safety. It was, objectively, beautiful. It was also, objectively, rotten; I could see the stress fractures feathering out from its edges like frost on a windowpane, and when Dorje tested it with an axe-tap, the note it rang was flat and dead.

"One at a time," Hazel said. "Light steps. No talking on the span." She looked at each of us in turn, and her eyes lingered on me a half-second longer than the others, and I knew why. Because I was the biggest. Because I was the rearguard. Because she had already, silently, given me the job I would have given myself.

"I'll hold here," I said, before she could ask. "Cover the crossing."

"You don't have a gun, Kwan."

"No," I said. "I have these." And I pulled the flare case out of my pack — six marine flares I'd been hauling since Rungli because I am constitutionally incapable of leaving useful fire behind. "Six of them. Enough light and enough noise to make six armed men think twice about stepping onto bad ice in a hurry."

Hazel looked at the flares, then at me, and something passed between us that I don't have clean words for. Recognition, maybe. She had spent seven years believing she was the kind of person who leaves people behind. I had spent eleven years knowing I was the kind who stays. Between the two of us we almost made one whole guide.

"Two minutes per crossing," she said. "If it starts going, you drop everything and run. Promise me."

"Medics don't promise things like that," I said. "Go."

* * *

Tomas crossed first, because he was lightest and because Hazel wanted the route proven. He came off the far side shaking, grinning, alive. Then Vann, roped to Priya, who took most of his weight and none of his fear. Then Dorje, who moved across the rotten span like a man walking through his own childhood home in the dark — sure-footed, unsurprised, touching the ice once in the middle as though greeting a relative. Then Hazel, last of the rope team, pausing halfway to look back at me across the gap.

That was when the first bullet hit the serac above my head.

The sound, as I said, is ordinary. A door slamming in another room. But the ice sprayed white an arm's length from my face, and the crack of the rifle arrived a beat later, flattened and stretched by the walls of the icefall, and my body did what bodies do: it went simple and fast.

"GO!" I screamed, and lit the first flare.

The flare went up red and screaming, and for a few seconds the whole icefall was bathed in that harsh theatrical crimson, shadows leaping, the serac walls bleeding light, and I turned and held it high over my head like the world's most suicidal lighthouse keeper. Below and behind me, down the glacier's shoulder, I saw them: four figures in the open, spread wide, rifles up. The other two would be flanking. Of course they would be. Rathore didn't hire improvisers.

They hesitated. Good. Flares don't stop bullets, but they do something else — they announce that the people you're shooting at can see *you*, clearly, in color, and that changes the psychology of ambush. Ambushers want to be weather. I made us both visible, and for three full seconds nobody fired.

Three seconds is a long time on rotten ice.

Hazel came off the bridge at a run. Priya and Vann were scrambling up the far slope. Dorje had hold of Tomas's collar, hauling him behind a boulder. And I stood on my side of the crevasse with a burning flare in my fist and did the only thing left to do, which was to be loud.

"THAT'S FAR ENOUGH!" I bellowed, in English and then, because I'd spent years posted near their border, in rough but serviceable Nepali-accented Mandarin, then Hindi, throwing my voice around the icefall so it seemed to come from everywhere. "SIX OF US! FLARES AND DETONATORS IN THE SERACS! YOU WANT TO BRING THIS WHOLE WALL DOWN ON YOUR OWN POSITIONS, KEEP SHOOTING!"

I had no detonators. I had five flares and a bad attitude. But the second flare went up orange, and the third went up white, and the shooting stopped entirely, and I heard — faintly, across the ice — a voice giving orders in a tone I recognized from my army years. The tone of a man who has been told to accomplish something and is realizing the cost is climbing.

They started moving anyway. Spread out, low, professional. Toward the bridge.

And here is the thing about standing alone on the wrong side of a crevasse with three flares left and four armed men coming: some part of you, the part that has done the arithmetic since Kunming, understands exactly how this ends. I understood it. I stood there anyway, because Tomas was on the far side, and Priya, and Vann, and Hazel, and Dorje, and because there is a rule — *no one left behind* — that Hazel had taught us in a snow cave two nights ago with tears frozen in her eyelashes, and rules only mean anything if somebody pays to keep them.

"Haz!" I shouted across the gap. "The bridge won't take them in a group — make them cross one at a time and keep moving! Don't wait for me!"

"We're not leaving you!"

"You ARE, actually, it's the whole plan, GO!"

The fourth flare went up. And then the mountain, who had been listening to all of this with the patience of the very old, decided to contribute.

I felt it before I heard it — a shudder through my boots, deep and structural, the icefall shifting its weight. Above the route, a serac the size of a chapel leaned, groaned, and began, with terrible slowness, to fall. Not toward me. Toward the bridge.

"THERE IT GOES! CROSS NOW OR NOT AT ALL!"

What happened next happened in pieces, and I've assembled them since from what others saw, because I was busy. Hazel shoved Vann and Priya onto the span. Dorje anchored the rope and paid it out fast. Tomas screamed something. The serac came down in a white thunder that erased the world, and through the rolling smoke of ice-dust I saw the bridge flex, crack along its feathered fractures, and hold — barely, sagging, dying — while Priya half-carried Vann across its buckling spine.

And then the shooting started again, wild now, panicked, men firing through falling ice at shapes in the dust, and one of the rounds found flesh.

I saw Priya stagger. Saw her twist — not away from the bullets but *into* them, her body curling around Vann's like a shell around its animal. Saw them both go down on the far lip of the crevasse, her on top, her arms still locked around him, doing the job to the end.

The fifth flare burned in my hand, forgotten, dripping.

"PRIYA!" Hazel's voice, raw, from somewhere in the whiteout.

The bridge groaned. The far edge of it crumbled into the dark and the whole span tilted, and I understood with perfect clarity that whatever window had existed was closing, and that I was on the wrong side of it, and that the men coming for me were thirty seconds out.

I threw the last flare not at them but at the base of the nearest serac on *their* side, where the fresh-fallen debris lay loosest, and shouted every detonator threat I had left, and in the confusion of flame and settling dust I backed to the broken bridge, looked down into the blue dark once — hello, old friend — and jumped the widening gap as it cracked beneath me, hands catching ice, boots scrabbling, Dorje's rope snapping taut around my chest and hauling me up the far side like a fish out of a dream.

Behind me, the bridge went down entire, folding into the crevasse with a sound like the mountain sighing, taking the route and half my dignity with it. The shooters' voices rose in frustration on the far side — cut off, walled out, furious.

I lay on my back in the snow, lungs full of knives, and laughed until Hazel's face appeared above mine, furious and relieved and crying without seeming to know it.

"Kwan—"

"She's hit," I gasped. "Priya. Check her. I'm fine. Go."

* * *

Here is what I know about wounds, professionally: the body lies about them at first. Adrenaline is a magnificent anesthetic and a vicious liar. When I reached Priya, kneeling in the trampled snow with Vann cradled against her chest and her own blood spreading beneath her in a bloom too large and too fast, she was trying to apologize.

"I moved wrong," she kept saying. "I moved wrong, I'm sorry, I was supposed to—"

"Shut up," I said gently, the way you say it to people who are allowed to ignore you. "Save the air."

Two rounds. One through the meat of her shoulder — survivable, ugly, manageable. One lower, in the left side of her abdomen, and that one I did not like at all, because it had gone in and not come out, and because she was pale in a way that had nothing to do with altitude, and because her hands were cold when everything about exertion should have made them hot.

I worked on her with the kit and the calm I keep folded away for exactly this, packing, binding, pushing fluids, and all the while the monsoon wind arrived over the moraine like a tide coming in, warm and wet and enormous, the first true breath of the storm season, right on schedule. The mountain washing itself. The clock running out.

"She needs a hospital," Hazel said. Not a question.

"She needs a helicopter," I said, "and we both know who owns the helicopters."

Above us, on the far side of the fallen bridge, a flare of their own went up — white, clinical, patient. They had time. They had radios. They would find another way around, or they would simply wait for the storm to do what bullets hadn't.

Vann knelt in the snow beside Priya, holding her good hand, and the film canisters were strapped to his chest under his jacket, and his eyes when he looked up at Hazel were the eyes of a man doing arithmetic of his own.

"Leave me," Priya whispered. Her voice was steady, which frightened me more than screaming would have. "You have what they want. I'm the thing slowing you down. Leave me and run."

Hazel crouched, took Priya's face in both gloved hands, and said, in a voice I will hear for the rest of my life:

"No one left behind. That's the rule. That's the *whole* rule. Everything else is weather."

Then she stood, and looked up at the storm swallowing the peaks, and looked down the long white miles to the village, and I watched her make the decision that would cost her everything she'd just refused to sell — because carrying a wounded woman down a mountain in the breaking monsoon, with killers behind and no route but the worst one, was not survival. It was testimony. It was choosing, with her legs, what she believed.

"Dorje," she said. "How far to the old shepherd track?"

"Close," Dorje said, touching the amulet. "If the debt holds."

"It holds," Hazel said. "Get her up. We move now."

And the monsoon woke above us, and the rain began.
