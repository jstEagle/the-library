## 18. Rathore's Bargain

The helicopter came up the valley like a wasp that had learned ambition.

I heard it before I saw it — a flat, mechanical buzzing that didn't belong at four and a half thousand meters, where the only things with any business flying are choughs and avalanches. Kwan spotted it first, a black speck crawling up the glacier's throat below the icefall, and for one stupid second my heart did something hopeful, because a helicopter means rescue, means stretchers and hot tea and someone else's problem.

Then I remembered who sends helicopters to mountains.

"Down," I said. "Behind the serac. Now."

We dragged ourselves and Priya into the blue shadow of an ice block the size of a chapel. She was conscious, which was more than she'd earned an hour ago when the bullet went through her shoulder on the icefall bridge. Kwan had done what Kwan does — pressure, morphine, the flat calm of a man who has stitched people in worse places than this, though not many. Her lips were gray. Her eyes were furious. Of the two, I'd have bet on the fury keeping her alive.

The helicopter didn't land. It couldn't — no flat ice down there, nothing but the broken jumble below the icefall where we'd crossed like sinners on a tightrope. It hovered instead, close enough to the glacier that its rotor wash sent spindrift smoking off the surface, and set down in my mind with far more precision: *Dev Rathore came himself.*

"He wouldn't," Tomas whispered. "Ministry officials don't—"

"You've met him?" I asked.

"No. But—"

"He came." I'd known it since the sound reached us. Men like Rathore delegate everything except the moment their whole architecture of lies is about to walk off a mountain on the back of a disgraced guide. Some things you attend personally.

* * *

We watched from cover while a figure climbed out onto the ice — alone, which was either arrogance or confidence, and with Rathore it was always both wearing each other's coats. He stood there in a parka too clean for the altitude, hands in pockets, looking up at the icefall the way a man looks at a staircase he intends to buy.

He couldn't see us. I was almost sure he couldn't see us. But then he raised one hand, unhurried, and beckoned — the small, impatient gesture of a man summoning a waiter — directly toward our serac.

"He knows," Dorje said quietly. It was the longest sentence he'd produced in two days, and it landed like a dropped ice axe.

"He's guessing. Or he's got thermal optics, which I sincerely hope the ministry doesn't issue with the stationery." I looked at Priya, gray and burning on the ice, at Vann pressed against the wall of the cave mouth behind us with his film canisters clutched to his chest like organs he'd grown back after losing them once already. At Kwan, whose face said *whatever you're about to do, do it fast.* At Tomas, who was nineteen going on forty and looking at me the way people had stopped looking at me seven years ago — like I might actually know what to do.

"I'm going down there," I said.

Absolute silence. The good kind of silence, the kind where everyone is too appalled to argue.

"Hazel—" Kwan started.

"If he's here to negotiate, someone has to hear the offer. If he's here to kill us, better it starts with me and ends with all of you running the other way with Vann." I checked the rope at my waist out of habit, though there was no one to anchor it to. "Besides. I want to look him in the face when I say whatever I'm going to say."

"And what are you going to say?" Tomas asked.

"I genuinely don't know," I admitted. "That's the fun part."

* * *

The descent to the helicopter took twenty minutes of careful, hateful footwork through the debris below the icefall — the same ground we'd crossed under fire, now littered with flare casings and the shattered remains of the serac bridge, blocks of ice the size of buses lying where they'd fallen like the mountain's own opinion of our crossing. Rathore waited at the bottom, standing on the glacier with his hands still in his pockets, watching me come with the expression of a man who has never once been kept waiting by weather.

Up close he looked wrong for the place. Too rested. His breath wasn't even ragged, though we were high enough that mine came in rags. He'd flown up from Rungli in a heated cabin and stepped onto the roof of the world as if it were a hotel lobby, and some petty animal part of me hated him for how easily he stood there.

"Ms. Amberdale." He said my name the way you'd read a figure off a ledger. "You look terrible."

"It's the altitude. And the gunfire. And the betrayal." I stopped six feet from him, close enough to talk, far enough that if either of us swung it would be honest. "Your crew shot one of my team. Your liaison, incidentally. The woman carrying your orders."

"Priya Nair is alive?"

"Barely. Thanks for asking. Really, the concern is touching."

Something moved behind his eyes — not guilt; I want to be clear that it was not guilt. Recalculation. A man discovering his spreadsheet has an error in it. "That was not sanctioned. Colonel Sengupta was instructed to contain the situation without casualties."

"Contain the situation." I laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, jagged, echoing off the icefall. "Is that what we're calling six dead surveyors? Containment? Is that what you called it thirty years ago, when your predecessors marched those porters up here and put bullets in them and left them for the glacier to file away?"

Rathore's face did not change. That was the frightening thing. Not a flicker. Somewhere under that calm was a man who had heard this accusation before, or expected it, or simply had no room in his architecture for anything so inefficient as being shocked.

"You've seen the site," he said.

"We've seen the bodies. Bound and shot, in uniforms older than my father. We've seen what your Meridian Survey found before you sent men up to finish the job. We've got Dr. Elias Vann, who spent three weeks in an ice cave eating snow and watching his colleagues' boots walk away downhill without him." I stepped closer. "So yes. We've seen the site."

For the first time, Dev Rathore took his hands out of his pockets.

* * *

Here is the thing I have never told anyone, the thing I will carry out of this chapter of my life the way the glacier carries its dead: in that moment, standing on the ice with the man who owned my ruin, I was not thinking about Vann, or the massacre, or Priya bleeding in the shadows above. I was thinking about a letter.

Seven years ago, after Kanchen Gorge, after the inquiry, after they took my license and my name and handed them both to the newspapers, I received a letter from the Amberdale family of the deceased — the client I'd failed to bring home. It was three sentences long. *We do not blame you. We blame the mountain. Please stop writing to us.* I have read that letter perhaps four hundred times. I know it the way monks know scripture. And every time, the first sentence lifts me an inch off the ground and the third sentence puts me back down through the floor.

*Please stop writing to us.* Because my name was attached to their grief. Because every time I wrote — apologies, explanations, the full forensic truth of what happened in the gorge that day — I made them relive it. My redemption required their suffering, and so I stopped. I walked. I measured my life in miles. I let the record stand because clearing it would have cost other people more than it cost me to stay dirty.

That is what I thought about while Rathore spoke. Not justice. Not Vann. A letter.

"Let me tell you where you stand, Ms. Amberdale," Rathore said, and his voice had changed — dropped its bureaucratic shell and revealed the negotiator underneath, the reasonable man, the man who understood people. "Below you: me, a helicopter, and six men with rifles who are currently climbing the moraine on foot because their colonel misjudged your route. Above you: a storm system the meteorological office tells me will close this valley within eighteen hours. You cannot go up. You cannot stay. Down-valley is me. So let us discuss the only variable in this equation that remains open, which is you."

"I'm listening," I said, hating myself for how much I meant it.

"The inquiry file on Kanchen Gorge is not closed. It is *held*. There is a difference, and you of all people know it. Held files can be reopened. Reopened files can be corrected." He let that sit in the thin air between us. "Corrected files produce restored licenses. Restored licenses produce cleared records. Cleared records, Ms. Amberdale, produce a woman who can stand in a village square and say her name out loud without waiting for someone to flinch."

The glacier creaked somewhere beneath us, settling its ancient weight, and I swear the sound sounded like agreement.

"And in exchange," I said. My voice came out steady. I don't know how. "In exchange for Dr. Vann and the film."

"In exchange for Dr. Vann and the film, yes. He comes down with me. He'll be treated well — I give you my word. There will be a debriefing, a period of convalescence, and in time he'll return to his work. The material will be assessed by qualified historians and handled with appropriate discretion." Every word was smooth as rope worn soft by use. "And you walk away with everything they took from you. Everything. Seven years returned in a single signature."

* * *

I want it more than air.

There. I've written around it for pages and there it is, plain as a crevasse underfoot. For one long moment — and I mean a real moment, ten seconds, fifteen, an eternity measured in heartbeats — I wanted it more than air. More than Vann's film. More than the dead porters in the ice. More than Priya's blood drying on Kwan's bandages. I stood on that glacier and felt seven years of walking rise up in me like floodwater, all those miles, all those cheap treks and silent lodges and nights measuring my life in distances because distances were the only unit left that didn't shame me — and I saw it end. I saw the last mile. I saw my name spoken in a tea house and nobody flinching. I saw the gorge file stamped *corrected*, and I swear to God I could smell the ink.

Rathore watched me want it. That was his craft, I understand now. He didn't push. He stood in the wind with his ruined composure and his clean parka and he simply held the offer out the way you hold out water to a dying man, and he let the mountain do the persuading, and he watched me want it.

"You hesitated," he said finally, softly. Almost kindly. "That's all right. Honest people hesitate. It's the liars who answer instantly." He reached into his coat and produced a folded paper — actual paper, at altitude, because Rathore believed in the theater of documents — and a pen. "One signature. That's the entire price. One signature, and you stop counting miles."

And that — that was the mistake. Not the offer. The phrase.

Because he'd read my file, obviously. He knew everything, the ministry always knows everything, and somewhere in the surveillance reports and the trekking-permit logs there was a line about the disgraced guide who kept walking, who measured her life in kilometers, who had once been overheard in a lodge in Rungli telling a drunk German tourist that she counted miles the way other people count sins. Rathore had done his homework. He'd picked the phrase precisely because it would cut.

But he'd picked it from the outside. He'd picked it the way you pick a lock with a diagram — accurately, mechanically, without ever having lived inside the thing he was opening. He thought the miles were my prison.

They were my penance. And there is a difference, and I of all people know it.

* * *

The pen was in his hand. The paper was in his hand. The wind came down off the icefall carrying the smell of snow that hadn't melted in ten thousand years, and somewhere above us Priya was breathing shallowly against the cold, and somewhere behind me a man who had crawled into a glacier to survive his own government was trusting a stranger with the only proof that his colleagues had existed at all.

"No," I said.

Rathore didn't move. "Ms. Amberdale—"

"You hesitated," I said. "Honest people hesitate. I hesitated. I want you to know that. I stood here and I wanted it so badly I could taste it, and if you'd offered me anything smaller — my license alone, just the license — I might have signed. But you offered me everything, and everything is the one thing I finally understand I can't have, because everything includes the part where it stops costing anyone anything, and it should cost. It has to cost. That's the whole point of a debt." I looked up at the icefall, at the blue walls holding their dead. "The ice keeps its debts. Someone told me that once. I thought it was superstition. It's bookkeeping."

Rathore folded the paper closed, slowly, along its original crease. When he looked up at me again the negotiator was gone and what remained was worse — the administrator, the man for whom I was no longer even a variable, merely a rounding error scheduled for deletion.

"That's unfortunate," he said.

"Yeah," I said. "It really is."

He turned and walked toward the helicopter, and I turned and started climbing, and neither of us looked back, because we were both professionals, and because we both already knew how the next few hours were going to go. Behind me the rotors spun up, flattening the spindrift, and ahead of me the icefall rose white and broken into the bruising sky, and the monsoon stood on the southern horizon like a debt collector who had all the time in the world.

I had maybe an hour before his men finished climbing the moraine. Less, probably. I had a wounded woman, a paranoid survivor, a boy, a soldier, a porter, and a secret worth killing for, and a storm coming in that would swallow the valley whole.

I had refused the deal. Now I had to survive refusing it.

I put my head down and started running up the mountain.
