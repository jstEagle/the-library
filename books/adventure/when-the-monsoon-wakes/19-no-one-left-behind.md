## 19. No One Left Behind

Rathore's offer hung in the air between us, and for one long moment I let myself live inside it.

Licenses restored. Record cleared. Seven years of exile undone with a signature. I could see it all laid out like a route on a map: the guide's badge back in my hand, the clients who'd stopped returning my calls, the name Hazel Amberdale scrubbed clean of the word *negligent*. All I had to do was hand over a half-dead man and a few canisters of film. Men had sold their souls for less. Most men would have sold them for exactly that.

"I want it," I said.

Rathore smiled. He was standing below the icefall in the lee of the helicopter, which ticked and cooled behind him like some enormous metal insect, and he looked freshly pressed, absurdly out of place, a man who had never once been cold. "Of course you do. You're not a fool, Amberdale. You know what your life is worth now versus what it was worth an hour ago."

"That's the thing about wanting things." My voice came out steadier than I felt. "You can want something with your whole chest and still know it's poison."

"Then think practically. Priya is bleeding out on that moraine. Kwan has maybe forty minutes before the cold takes his hands. The storm—" he gestured at the wall of cloud swallowing the upper mountain, the monsoon arriving right on schedule, right on cue, as if it too were on the ministry payroll— "is going to close every route down within hours. I am the only way off this glacier alive. For all of you."

He wasn't wrong. That was the worst part. He wasn't wrong about any of it.

I thought about Priya lying in the snow with a bullet through her shoulder, her lips gray, her breath coming in shallow little pulls. I thought about Kwan wrapping her wound with hands that didn't shake because Kwan's hands never shook, but whose eyes had gone somewhere flat and far away, the place soldiers go. I thought about Vann clutching his film canisters to his chest like a man holding his own heart outside his body.

And underneath all of it, seven years deep, I thought about Suresh Tamang, face-down in the snow at nineteen thousand feet, because I had turned back for the summit and told myself — told everyone — that the weather window mattered more than the man lagging behind our rope team. *No one left behind.* It was the first rule they teach you. I broke it once, and a man died, and the world decided I was the kind of person who breaks rules, and maybe the world was right.

"No," I said.

Rathore's smile didn't move, but something behind it did. "Amberdale—"

"You're offering me my old life back so I'll help you bury a massacre. Those two things cancel each other out. If I take your deal, there's no old life worth having." I shouldered my pack. "And you're wrong about the only way off this mountain. You're thinking like a man who reads maps. I'm thinking like someone who's walked this ice for twenty years."

"There is no other way off."

"There's always another way. It's just usually worse."

Behind me, Dorje made a sound that might have been a laugh. Rathore's men — six of them, armed, moving up from the helicopter pad onto the lower icefall in loose formation — had begun to advance.

* * *

Here is what I knew about the glacier below the icefall, knowledge bought with twenty seasons and one catastrophic mistake:

It was rotten.

The monsoon had been chewing at it for weeks from underneath, meltwater running through it like blood through a body, hollowing the interior into caverns and bridges and traps. From above it looked like any other stretch of white. From above it looked walkable. But I had crossed it twice this season, and both times the ice had groaned under me like an old floor, and both times I had seen the telltales: the sag in the surface where the snow bridge over the main crevasse had thinned, the dark stain of meltwater weeping through a seam, the way the seracs upstream leaned like drunks against each other.

Six armed men in heavy gear, carrying rifles and radios and the weight of their own certainty, could cross it fast and hard, the way soldiers cross things — because soldiers are trained that hesitation kills.

On this glacier, hesitation was the only thing that kept you alive.

"We go down," I told the others, low and fast, crouched behind the rock rib where we'd taken shelter when the helicopter came in. "Not around. Down. Straight across the rotten section."

Kwan stared at me. "That's suicide."

"That's arithmetic. They weigh more than us, they move faster than us, and they don't know what's under them. We do. We go slow, spread out, single file, exactly where I say. They come after us the way soldiers come after people, and the mountain does the rest."

"And if it does it to us instead?" Tomas asked. His voice cracked on the last word. He was twenty-six and three days ago he'd nearly gone into a crevasse, and he was still here anyway, which told me more about him than any credential ever could.

"Then it does it to us," I said. "But I've read this ice. I know where its bones are." I looked at each of them. Vann, shivering, feverish, refusing to let go of the canisters. Dorje, calm as stone, the copper amulet glinting at his throat. Priya, conscious now, gray-faced, her left arm bound tight against her chest. Kwan, already repacking the med kit with the economy of a man who'd done it under fire before.

"The rope-team rule," I said. "No one left behind. Not this time. Not ever again. If anyone goes down, we all stop. Understood?"

Nobody argued. Maybe they saw what it cost me to say it. Maybe they were just too cold to argue. Either way, we roped up.

* * *

The crossing took everything I had.

I led, reading the surface the way you read a page of bad handwriting — every shadow a warning, every hollow a question. Thirty meters behind me, Dorje brought Vann. Then Kwan with Priya slung between him and Tomas, her boots dragging, her teeth chattering through clenched jaw. The storm came down around us in ragged sheets, visibility dropping to fifty meters, then thirty, then less, and I navigated by feel and memory and the small stubborn compass in my head.

Behind us, faint through the wind, I heard them coming. Boots on ice. Voices clipped and military. A flashlight beam swinging wild in the whiteout.

"They're on the ice," Dorje said quietly.

"Good," I said, and meant it, and hated that I meant it.

We reached the first marker — a bent wand I'd planted on the way up, half-swallowed by new snow — and I turned the party hard left, onto the section I'd named privately, in my own head, *the bad table*. A broad flat pan of glacier that looked like the safest ground for a mile. Wasn't. Beneath it, meltwater had eaten out a chamber the size of a chapel roofed with ice perhaps a meter thick in places, thinner where the water had worked hardest. I'd probed it with my axe on the ascent and decided then that no client of mine would ever set foot on it.

Today I set five clients on it, because the alternative was bullets, and because I knew — I *knew*, the way you know a ladder will hold you — exactly how much weight it could bear if that weight moved slowly and was spread wide.

"Slow steps," I called back. "Wide apart. Don't bunch."

The armed men did not take slow steps. Of course they didn't. Their leader — the big man with Rathore's authority folded in his breast pocket — wanted his quarry, and whiteout or no whiteout, quarry was visible ahead as a ragged line of figures, and soldiers are trained to close distance, and closing distance means speed.

I heard them hit the bad table at a jog.

The glacier spoke.

It wasn't a crack, exactly. It was deeper than a crack — a sound like a cathedral clearing its throat, a vast subterranean groan that came up through my boot soles and into my spine. I'd heard it once before, on a different mountain, four seconds before a snow bridge the size of a tennis court dropped a client into blue darkness. Four seconds is enough time to fall flat and anchor. It is not enough time to run.

"DOWN!" I screamed. "FLAT! NOW!"

My team dropped. All of them — even Priya, even Vann, bodies hitting snow because they trusted me, God help them, they trusted me. Behind us the running footsteps did not drop. There was a shouted order, cut short. Then the world opened.

The sound of the ice failing was nothing like the groan. It was a sharp report, like a rifle, then a second, then a dozen, a machine-gun rattle of fractures racing across the bad table, and then the whole roof went. I rolled in time to see it happen: six men, flashlights, rifles, the whole armed machinery of the state, dropping into a hole that hadn't existed ten seconds earlier, swallowed by the glacier in a bloom of powder snow and blue light. One of them — the leader — caught the lip, arms hooked over the edge, screaming, and for one horrible clear moment his eyes found mine across thirty meters of broken ice.

Then the lip crumbled, and he was gone, and the glacier sealed itself over the sound of him like water closing over a stone.

Silence, except the wind.

Nobody spoke. Tomas was shaking. Vann had his eyes shut, lips moving — prayer or arithmetic, I couldn't tell. Kwan looked at me with an expression I couldn't read and didn't try to.

"They chose to run," I said, and my voice sounded strange to me, thin and old. "I gave them the same warning I'd give anyone. They chose to run."

Dorje put a hand briefly on my shoulder. "The ice keeps its debts," he said. "Not yours. Its own."

I didn't know if that was comfort or condemnation. I took it anyway, because it was all that was on offer, and we moved.

* * *

Below the bad table the glacier gentled, and the storm became the whole world.

The monsoon arrived properly then — not the ragged leading edge we'd been dodging for days but the true thing, a warm wet hammering deluge that turned snow to slush and visibility to nothing and every step into a negotiation. We descended through it by dead reckoning, by wands, by Dorje's memory and the copper amulet he held sometimes in his fist like a talisman doing actual work. Priya faded in and out. Once she surfaced enough to grip my sleeve with her good hand.

"Amberdale. The radio. I told you it was sabotaged—" 

"You told me. Save your breath."

"Rathore never—" She coughed. "Never intended—"

"I know what he intended. Sleep."

She slept, or went under, and Kwan monitored her with the grim focus of a man rationing his own fear. Her pulse stayed thready but present. Thready but present. I made it a mantra. Thready but present. Every hundred meters of descent I checked it myself, because checking it gave my hands something to do besides shake.

Somewhere in the worst of it, in the white roaring blindness, Tomas stumbled alongside me and shouted, close to my ear: "How do you know where we're going? You can't see anything!"

"You learn the mountain with your feet!" I shouted back. "Eyes are optional!"

It wasn't entirely a lie. Mostly it was faith — the accumulated faith of twenty years, thousands of kilometers, the belief that my body remembered routes my mind had forgotten. And partly it was the truth I'd learned seven years ago and spent seven years denying: that the mountain doesn't care about your plans, your orders, your redemption arc. It cares about weight and angle and water and time. Everything else is negotiable.

We descended through the night. Or what passed for night, which was nothing, which was gray on gray on gray, the storm erasing the difference between day and dark until time itself went soft. I counted steps instead of hours. Four thousand steps. Six thousand. At eight thousand, the grade eased, and I smelled woodsmoke under the rain, and knew we'd made the moraine above the last village.

* * *

They came out to meet us.

I don't know how they knew. Perhaps Dorje had sent word somehow on the ascent; perhaps the mountain itself had told them, the way mountains tell villages things. But lanterns appeared through the rain — a dozen, two dozen — and figures in oilskins, and hands reaching for Priya, for Vann, for all of us, pulling us down off the moraine and into the village like survivors of a shipwreck being hauled aboard.

The old woman was waiting at the first house. She looked at Dorje, and at the amulet at his throat, and something passed across her face that I will spend the rest of my life trying to name. Grief and vindication and release, all at once, the look of a debt acknowledged after decades.

"He carried it home," she said, in her own tongue, and Dorje translated, voice rough: "She says the ice has paid what it owed. Now we owe it silence no longer."

Priya was laid by the fire, and Kwan knelt over her, and for a long terrible hour none of us breathed easily. Then she opened her eyes, found my face among the ring of strangers, and said, hoarsely: "Still here. Told you I was better than my orders."

Vann sat wrapped in blankets with the film canisters in his lap, and when I crouched beside him he looked at me with eyes that had finally stopped being paranoid and started being simply exhausted.

"They'll come for this," he said. "Whatever happens now, they'll come."

"Let them come," I said. "There's a village full of people between them and you, and a monsoon behind it, and I don't think either of them is taking messages."

Outside, the rain hammered the roofs like applause, or like judgment, or like a mountain washing itself clean of everything that had been buried in it. Inside, the fire crackled, and Priya breathed, and Vann slept, and I sat by the door with my axe across my knees and watched the dark and understood, finally, with a clarity that felt almost like peace:

I had broken the rule once, and it had cost a man's life and my own name. Today I had kept it, and it had cost six armed men their footing and me whatever remained of my future. Both debts were paid now, or as paid as debts ever get. What remained was the truth, sitting in a geologist's lap in a firelit room in a village the government's maps barely acknowledged.

Tomorrow we'd carry it down to Rungli. Tomorrow I'd have to decide what to do with it — and with myself.

Through the doorway, down-valley, I could see headlights. Far off, crawling along the valley road through the rain. Too many of them, moving too slowly, in convoy.

Someone had told Rungli we were coming.

Whether they'd told it as news or as warning, I wouldn't know until morning.
