## 20. Descent

I want to say something noble about the last three kilometers, but the truth is I remember almost nothing of them. There is a kind of exhaustion that doesn't erase memory so much as file it somewhere you can't reach — and I had been filing for two days straight. What remains are fragments: the sound of Priya's breathing through the improvised stretcher, wet and ragged like a bellows with a hole in it; Kwan's hand on my shoulder blade steering me around a rock I hadn't seen; the rain arriving not as weather but as a verdict.

Because that's what it was. We'd been racing the monsoon for nine days, and somewhere above the icefall the mountain decided to stop toying with us. The first drops came down fat and warm and wrong-feeling after weeks of cold, and then the sky simply opened, as if someone upstream had pulled a plug out of the world. Within an hour the trail was a river. Within two, the river was a torrent. Dorje led us down switchbacks that existed mostly in his memory, one hand on the amulet at his chest, shouting instructions over the water in a voice I'd never heard him use above a murmur.

"Left! Stay left — right side goes!" The right side went, demonstrably, a slab of hillside peeling away into the gorge below us with a sound like a giant clearing his throat. Nobody screamed. We were past screaming. We just moved left.

Hazel carried Vann's pack on top of her own, because of course she did. Kwan had the stretcher's front handles and I had the back, and between us we carried sixty kilograms of unconscious liaison who had put her body between a bullet and a man she'd been ordered to bury. Her blood had soaked through the dressing twice before we'd gotten the pressure right, and every time I checked, Kwan would look at me over her body and give this small, infuriatingly calm nod, as if to say: *still alive, keep walking.* He'd been giving that nod for thirty hours. I have decided it is the bravest thing I saw on that mountain, braver than the icefall, braver than the serac bridge, braver than any of it. A nod, repeated, in the dark, in the rain.

Vann walked. That was the miracle none of us could explain. The man had spent — by his own count — eleven months in the ice caves, eating glacier melt and whatever the survey caches still held, and he should have been a skeleton wrapped in frostbite. Instead he shuffled along behind Hazel on legs like bamboo poles, feverish, half-blind, muttering coordinates to himself, refusing all offers of help except once, when the trail crossed a log bridge over white water, when he gripped Hazel's sleeve and did not let go until solid ground came back under his boots.

And then, around a shoulder of the mountain, through the grey curtain of rain, we saw smoke.

Not cloud-smoke. Cooking smoke. Woodsmoke, rising from stone chimneys, from the village of Thangma — the last settlement, the place where doors had closed and voices had dropped and an old woman had pressed a copper amulet into Dorje's hand and told him the ice keeps its debts.

The debts, apparently, were about to be settled.

* * *

They heard us before they saw us. Of course they did; the whole valley heard us, or rather heard the water we were bringing down with us, the flood-chatter of the streams swollen past their banks. By the time we staggered into the first terrace of barley fields, half the village was standing under eaves watching us come, and nobody moved to help, and I felt my heart do something complicated and painful, because I understood. We were strangers. We were government-adjacent strangers, which was worse. We were six mud-caked ghosts dragging a stretcher and a madman out of a mountain that had already taken enough from these people.

Dorje walked ahead of us into the square.

He stopped in the middle of it, rain streaming off him, and he did something I will think about for the rest of my life. He took the amulet from around his neck, held it up in both hands — the way you'd hold something that isn't yours, something entrusted — and he said, in a voice that cracked exactly once:

"I'm coming home."

An old woman came out of the third house on the left. She was small and bent and she moved with the unhurried certainty of someone who has outlived everyone who ever gave her orders. She looked at Dorje for a long moment. Then she looked at the amulet. Then she said something in her own language, short and fierce, and crossed the square and took Dorje's face in both hands like he was a boy come in late for supper, and pressed her forehead against his.

I don't speak the language. I didn't need to. Some sentences translate themselves.

After that, everything happened at once, the way things do when a whole community decides something without holding a meeting. Doors opened. Firewood appeared. Two men took the stretcher handles from us with the gentle firmness of people relieving you of something heavy you've been carrying too long, and carried Priya into a house that smelled of juniper smoke and dried meat, where a woman with capable hands — somebody's grandmother, somebody's authority — was already boiling water and laying out clean cloth. Another woman sat Vann down by a fire and began, without asking permission, to cut the rags of his clothing off him, clucking at the state of him in a tone that transcended language entirely. Even half-delirious, Vann flinched from her hands until she said something sharp, and he went still, and I swear the man nearly wept at being handled like a patient instead of a problem.

Kwan and I stood in the doorway dripping, superfluous, watching our patients be taken over by better medics than either of us. It was Kwan who finally laughed — a short, cracked sound, the first time I'd heard him laugh since the crevasse field.

"We're fired," he said.

"Good," I said. "I quit."

We stayed anyway. Old habits.

* * *

By full dark the monsoon had committed itself completely, rain hammering the slate roofs like applause that wouldn't stop, and the village had organized itself around us the way water organizes around a stone. Word had gone out — I don't know how, runners, I suppose, or some older network of signal fires and cousinage — and by midnight there were men on the trail below the village with lamps and poles, and by morning, when I stumbled out to check, there was a barricade across the road. Not subtle. Felled trees, stacked stones, a couple of very old men sitting on top of it drinking tea with the serene immovability of furniture.

Nobody said the word *blockade*. Nobody had to. When I asked one of the men — via Dorje translating — what they'd tell the soldiers if soldiers came, he shrugged and answered through Dorje:

"Tell them the road washed out." A pause. "It did wash out. Mostly true. Best kind of lie."

"The army has helicopters," I pointed out.

The old man considered this, sipping his tea. Then he gestured at the sky, at the low grey ceiling pressing down on the ridgelines, at the rain falling with the patience of something that intended to fall for four months.

"Helicopters," he said, "do not fly in monsoon. Soldiers do not walk in monsoon. Monsoon," and here he smiled for the first time, showing fewer teeth than the average smile requires, "is ours."

I have never been so glad to be trapped anywhere in my life.

Priya survived the night. I want to write that sentence larger than the page allows. The bullet had gone through her left shoulder, missing everything that mattered by margins Kwan later described as "insulting," and the village medic — Ama Lhamu, Dorje called her, though whether she was his actual grandmother or simply the grandmother of the entire valley remained unclear — cleaned and bound the wound with a competence that made Kwan go quiet with professional respect. By morning Priya was awake, grey-faced, furious about being horizontal, and asking in a voice like gravel whether anyone had counted the film canisters.

"Twice," Hazel told her. "They're fine. You're fine. Lie down."

"I am lying down," Priya said, with the dignity of an empress, "under protest."

Vann's fever broke near dawn. I was there when it happened — I'd appointed myself his watch, partly because I couldn't sleep and partly because I wanted to hear more of the story while he was too sick to ration it. He surfaced from the delirium all at once, the way deep divers surface, and found my face above him and grabbed my wrist with a strength that surprised both of us.

"The journal," he rasped. "Is the journal—"

"Dry. Safe. Hazel has it."

"And the film?"

"With the film. Everything's together. Everything's safe."

He stared at me for a long time, and I watched him do the arithmetic — eleven months alone in the dark doing sums with dead friends' names — and then he lay back and closed his eyes and said, almost conversationally, "Then I can sleep," and slept for fourteen hours, and snored, and it was the best sound I heard all week.

* * *

It was evening of the second day when Vann came to find Hazel.

I was there — I seemed to be there for all the important moments now, self-appointed witness, the surveyor's instinct to record running even here — sitting on the step of the house where they'd lodged us, watching the rain come down through the last light. Hazel was outside too, because Hazel didn't do well indoors anymore; seven years of blacklisting had taught her that rooms have walls and walls have ears. She was cleaning the rope. Of course she was. The rope that had held Tomas on the crevasse lip, that had held all of us on the serac bridge, coiled and inspected and dried section by section with the tenderness other people reserve for children.

Vann crossed the yard slowly, moving like a convalescent, which he was, wrapped in a borrowed coat three sizes too big. He stood in front of her until she looked up.

"You should have it," he said.

Hazel's hands went still on the rope. "Should have what?"

"All of it." He crouched down — it took him a while; his knees had opinions now — so that their eyes were level. "The film. The journal. All of it. Not because you asked for it. Because I watched you for nine days." His voice was rough, unused to long sentences, but steady. "Rathore sent you. Every reason to believe you were his. And you—" he stopped, started again. "On the icefall, when they were shooting, you came *back*. For Priya. For me. People don't come back. In eleven months, I learned what people do. They run. You came back."

"That was just—"

"Don't," Vann said, gently. "Don't do the thing where you explain it away. I've had eleven months to get good at recognizing the truth when it walks past me. It's rare. Take the film."

Hazel looked at him for a long moment. Rain drummed on the eaves. Somewhere behind us a door banged, and voices argued cheerfully about firewood, and the ordinary machinery of a village evening ground on, indifferent to history being passed hand to hand in its yard.

"I don't know what I'm going to do with it yet," Hazel said finally. It wasn't refusal. It was honesty, and both of them knew it.

"Yes, you do," Vann said. "You've known since the cave. You're just scared of it, because you know what it costs." He pressed the bundle into her hands — oilcloth, corded, heavier than its size, the way truth always turns out to be. "I paid eleven months. Priya paid a shoulder. Dorje's family paid fifty years. Don't make their payments worthless because you're afraid of the bill."

Hazel held the bundle like it might detonate. Maybe it would. Maybe that was the point.

"Why me?" she said quietly. "Why not take it out yourself? Find your own journalist, your own—"

"Because I'm a geologist," Vann said, and for the first time since the cave, he smiled. It transformed his face, made him briefly the man in the survey photographs, young and sunburned and certain. "I read ice. I don't read people. You read people, Ms. Amberdale. You read Rathore the moment you met him. You read Priya. You read all of us. You knew what we were before we knew it ourselves." He stood, slowly, joints cracking. "So read this. Read what it wants. And then decide. But decide soon."

"Why soon?"

Vann looked up at the sky, at the rain, at the wall of weather sitting on the passes like a lid on a pot.

"Because the monsoon buys us weeks, not months," he said. "And men like Rathore don't stop. They wait. There's a difference." He turned toward the firelight, then paused. "There's a journalist in Rungli. Meridian used their office for mail drops — I trusted them before, in another life. If you go down, go to Rungli. Ask for Suresh Iyer. Tell him the glacier kept its receipts."

He said it like a password, because it was one. Then he went inside, out of the rain, leaving Hazel standing in the yard with the evidence of a massacre in her arms and her face doing something I'd never seen it do in nine days of hell on the mountain.

She looked afraid.

Not of the bundle. Of the choice inside it. And I understood, sitting there getting soaked on the step, that the descent wasn't finished — that the mountain part was over, but the real descent, the one that went down through Rungli and the district office and whatever Rathore had waiting at the bottom, was only beginning. And that Hazel Amberdale, who had led five broken people across a glacier and out from under gunfire, was more frightened of this next part than anything the Khumbal had thrown at her.

She caught me watching her. Wiped her face — rain, obviously, just rain — and managed something that was almost a smirk.

"Get inside, Tomas. You'll catch your death."

"Too late," I said. "Caught it weeks ago. It's chronic."

She almost laughed. Then she looked down at the bundle in her hands, and the almost-laugh died, and she stood there a long moment in the rain, deciding.

I went inside. Through the window, later, I saw her still out there, motionless, the oilcloth bundle held against her chest like something rescued from a fire — and the rain coming down on her, and on the village, and on the whole drowned valley, washing and washing at everything, as if the sky itself were trying to decide what could be forgiven.

In the morning, she told us, she'd start making plans.

Down. To Rungli. To the journalist, to the truth, to whatever bill came due.

None of us slept much that night. Not because of fear, exactly. Because we could all feel it — the way you feel a change in pressure before a storm breaks — that something was ending, and something else, something with teeth, was about to begin.
