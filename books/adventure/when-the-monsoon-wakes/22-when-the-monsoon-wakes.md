## 22. When the Monsoon Wakes

The cell in Rungli District Jail has a window the size of a loaf of bread, and for four months it has been my only weather report. I have learned to read it like a guide reads sky: the grey before dawn means rain by noon; a hard blue sliver means the passes are drying out and somewhere someone is walking on ground I used to know by the sound of it underfoot.

I stopped counting miles in the Khumbal. Up there, distance stopped being something you conquered and became something you survived, one rope-length at a time. Down here, in a room I could cross in three strides, I find I've stopped counting days too. The trial is coming. Or it isn't. Rathore's people keep promising it will be quick, which is what they said about the rescue seven years ago, and about the recovery mission, and I have developed a professional distrust of promises made by men who never lace their own boots.

They let me have paper. Not much — six sheets a week, rationed like oxygen above eight thousand meters — but enough to write letters that mostly go unanswered. Tomas writes back. Dorje doesn't write, but every few weeks a package arrives: tea, dried meat, and once, wrapped in cloth gone soft with handling, a second copper amulet. I wear it now against my sternum. It's warmer than it has any right to be.

Kwan sends postcards from a hospital in the lowlands where she is learning to be a civilian again, or pretending to. Her last one said only: *The mountain called. I didn't answer. You'd have been proud.* I laughed until the guard on duty knocked to check I wasn't losing my mind, and then I laughed a little more, quietly, because maybe I was.

Priya is not in a jail. Priya is in hearings — parliamentary ones, the kind with microphones and men in good suits discovering, live on camera, that the ministry they funded has been feeding them stories for forty years. She burned her orders on the glacier, but she kept the ashes, and apparently ash makes excellent evidence when you're testifying about a sabotage order signed nine days before a "rescue" team ever left Rungli. I think of her sometimes the way I think of fixed lines on a hard pitch: the thing everyone said would fail, holding.

* * *

Vann comes on a Tuesday, which is visitation day, which is the day the corridor smells most strongly of disinfectant and boiled cabbage. I hear him before I see him — the uneven step, the cane he refuses to use properly, tapping the wall more than the floor. Seven months since the icefall, and he still walks like a man negotiating with the ground.

He looks better than the last time I saw him upright. In the cave he'd been a grey scrap of a man, all knuckle and stare, clutching film canisters like a mother clutching children out of a fire. Now he has flesh on his face again, and glasses, and a cardigan that some aunt or committee has knitted him into respectability. He sits down across the table from me and puts both hands flat on the metal, the way you do when you want the other person to see they're steady.

"Hazel Amberdale," he says. "I've been wanting to meet you properly for months."

"You met me in a cave."

"I met a hallucination with excellent rope skills in a cave." His mouth twitches. "For a while I thought I'd died and the afterlife had sent a rescue party as a joke. Then you got me down the mountain, and I thought — no, this is real, because nothing in the afterlife would smell like that tent."

"The tent smelled like Kwan's antiseptic and Tomas's instant noodles."

"It smelled like being alive," Vann says, "which is worse, if you've spent eleven weeks thinking you were dead." He takes off his glasses, polishes them on the cardigan, puts them back. Underneath the small talk his hands are not steady at all. "I'm here because I wanted to tell you before you read it in the papers. I testified yesterday. Four hours. They had to stop twice because I couldn't breathe."

"What did you tell them?"

"Everything. The survey. The radio call. The instruction to wait for a retrieval team that never came." He swallows. "The men who came at night instead. I told them about the ice, Ms. Amberdale. About what's in it. Names, ranks, dates. There are porters in that glacier whose families were told they drowned in a ferry accident in 1961. My instruments dated the ice over them. The state shot them and the glacier kept the receipts, and last week a parliamentary subcommittee finally opened them."

I look at my hands on the table. They're guide's hands, scarred across the knuckles, nails cut short for glove work. For seven years those hands have been the hands that lost three clients on the Khumbal's south face. That's what the file says. That's what Rathore read aloud to me in his office with its potted palm and its photographs of abandoned tents: *negligence, abandonment, falsified route reports.* What the file does not say — what I only learned up there, in the whiteout, from Dorje, in a voice like stone rolling — is that the storm I turned back into was a storm the ministry's own weather station logged as unsurvivable, and that the route report I supposedly falsified was altered after the fact, upstairs, by people who needed a dead guide more than they needed a dead truth.

Three clients. I broke the rule — no one left behind — because the mountain gave me no choice I could live with, and the mountain took them anyway, and then the ministry took my name as change.

"I also told them about you," Vann says quietly.

"Don't."

"They should hear it. A woman with everything to lose walked back into the range that ruined her, found the thing they killed six men to bury—" He stops himself. "Five men. Six including me, technically, though I was more buried than dead."

"Tomas counted you among the living from day one," I say. "He carried your share of the weight the whole way up. Ask him sometime why. He'll tell you it was ballast."

Vann laughs — a rusty sound, like a door opening in a house long shut. Then he leans forward, and the humor goes out of him like heat out of a broken tent.

"They're dropping the charges," he says. "Not all of them. Not yet. But the extradition warrant is void — Rathore signed it under a ministry authority that no longer exists, and the new inquiry has flagged it. The negligence finding is under formal review. Your original route logs surfaced last month. Unaltered copies. Someone in the district office kept them in a drawer for seven years out of, I suspect, cowardice, which turns out to be a slow-acting form of conscience."

I don't say anything. Outside the bread-slice window, the light has gone the flat pewter grey that means heavy weather. First monsoon of the season, maybe. The guards hate monsoon; the corridors flood; the prisoners love it, because rain on the roof is the closest thing to music this place owns.

"You don't seem surprised," Vann says.

"I stopped being surprised by paperwork a long time ago. Paper can do anything. That's the whole point of it." I turn the amulet over under my shirt. "What happens to you?"

"A lectureship. A book contract. Therapy." He says the last word carefully, like a man testing thin ice and finding it holds. "And a promise. When the glacier gives them up — and it will, faster now, the melt is accelerating — I want the families there. I want the porters' grandchildren standing on that ice when their grandfathers come home. And I want you there too, if you'll come. As the guide. Because nobody else alive knows that route, and because—" he hesitates— "because it should be someone the mountain owes an apology to, standing on it when it pays."

"That's not how mountains work," I say. "They don't apologize. They just are. We're the ones who owe."

"Then consider it a debt collection," he says, and we leave it there, because a guard is hovering, and because some things are better carried than discussed.

* * *

The scandal widens the way water finds cracks. That's the phrase the journalist uses — Meera Chettri, the woman I handed the film to in a Rungli teahouse while police sirens started up two streets over. She visits now too, officially as press, unofficially as something closer to family. She brings newspapers I'm technically not allowed and biscuits I definitely am not, and she narrates the collapse of Rathore's world with the relish of a woman who waited years for a story and got a continent of one.

Rathore resigned the week the sabotage order went public. Resigned, and then was arrested trying to board a flight under a name so lazy it was practically a confession — *Dev Ram*, honestly, the man guided covert operations and couldn't invent a surname. The cleanup crew's commander talked. Of course he talked; men like that always talk once they understand no one is coming to buy their silence. He described the orders in detail: recover the findings, silence the survivor, leave no witnesses. He described being given my name in advance. *Amberdale will lead them in,* the briefing notes said. *She has no credibility. Whatever she reports will be discounted.*

That sentence did more damage to me than any charge. I read it four times in Meera's clipped voice before I asked her to stop. Not because it hurt — well. Because it hurt in the old place, the place where the blacklisting lived, and I have spent seven months learning that the old place heals slower than frostbite but it does heal, provided you stop picking at it.

But here is the thing I keep turning over at night, the way you turn a worry-bead: Rathore chose me because he thought I was disposable. And he was right about the disposal part and wrong about everything else, because disposable people are the ones with nothing left to protect, and a person with nothing left to protect will walk onto rotten glacier ice carrying a wounded stranger while armed men sink behind her, and will hand a dying man's testimony to a journalist instead of trading it for her own clean record, and will sit in a cell eating boiled cabbage rather than run, because running is just another way of leaving someone behind.

He thought he was hiring a scapegoat. He hired a rope leader. The difference is everything.

* * *

Tomas visits on Thursdays. He has a government job now — actual survey work, honest coordinates, maps of places that only need mapping. He brings printouts sometimes, topographic sheets of the Khumbal, and we spread them on the visiting table and I trace routes with my finger while he watches like a student watching a master, which embarrasses me and steadies me in equal measure.

"The melt's bad on the lower seracs," he says today, tapping the icefall. "Kwan's old position — the flanking ridge — it's gone. Slid in June."

"Good," I say, and mean it. Let the mountain take back its battlegrounds. Let the icefall forget the blood, the way it forgot everything else for sixty years, and remember it differently now — as testimony instead of grave.

"Do you know what you'll do?" he asks. "After. If they clear you."

I look at the map. The Khumbal Range in contour lines, brown and blue, patient as scripture. Somewhere on that sheet is a camp where six men took off their boots and walked barefoot uphill into the ice, together, toward whatever they'd found — and I have stopped needing that image to be rational. Some things walk toward the truth even when the truth kills them. I used to think that was madness. I'm starting to think it might be the only sane thing anyone did on that mountain, including me.

"I don't know," I say, and it comes out easier than I expected. "Seven years ago I would have lied. Said yes immediately, licenses back, boots on, first client by autumn. Now—" I smooth the crease out of the map. "Now I think I'd like to train guides. Young ones. Teach them the rule properly. No one left behind — not the rule as a slogan, the rule as arithmetic. Rope length, weather margin, turnaround times. All the boring ways of keeping the promise so you never have to break it spectacularly."

Tomas grins. "You'd be terrible at bureaucracy."

"I'd be magnificent at bureaucracy. I'd fail every candidate who reminded me of Rathore and pass every one who reminded me of Dorje, and the ministry could file complaints about my bias directly into the nearest crevasse."

He laughs, folds the map away, and then, awkwardly, the way young men do when feeling ambushes them, he says: "We wouldn't have made it without you. Any of us. I did the math on the descent, after. Carrying Priya, in that rain, on that terrain. The math doesn't work, Hazel. We shouldn't be alive."

"The math never works," I say. "That's what the mountain teaches. You do it anyway, and then you do the next bit, and eventually you're at the bottom and everyone's breathing. Miles aren't measured in kilometers, Tomas. They're measured in breaths. Everyone still breathing at the end — that's the distance you covered."

He leaves, and I sit with that, surprised to find I believe it.

* * *

Dorje comes the day before the hearing. He doesn't sign the visitor book so much as annotate it, and the guards have learned not to object. He sits across from me, sets down a thermos of butter tea, pours two cups without asking, and we drink in the silence of people who shared a storm and don't need words for weather anymore.

"My grandmother's village is sending a delegation to the hearing," he says at last. "Eleven families. The ones the ice kept." He looks at the table. "The old woman who gave me the amulet — she died in the spring. Peacefully. Before the news broke. My mother says she knew anyway. She said the debt was moving, you could feel it in the ground."

"I'm sorry."

"She waited sixty years. She got to wait knowing it was almost over." He drinks. "Hazel. My grandfather's name is on that rockface with the others. Kwan found it. Carved with the rest." He slides something across the table: a photograph, taken by Tomas on the descent, of the carved names. Six Meridian names. And beneath them, older, half-effaced, a line of porter names in Devanagari script, and one of them, third from the left, is his grandfather's.

"I didn't know it was there," I say. "On the climb. I looked right at it and saw only rock."

"So did the men who put the bodies in the ice," Dorje says. "That was always their mistake. They thought the mountain keeps secrets." He stands, drains his cup, and pauses at the door. "It keeps debts. There's a difference. Secrets rot. Debts get paid."

Then he's gone, and I hold the photograph a long time, and outside the bread-slice window the light goes dark as felt, and the first real rain of the monsoon arrives like something released from custody.

* * *

The hearing itself is almost anticlimactic — a room, a table, a fan turning overhead with the rhythm of a tired heart. The charges unravel in front of me the way Priya said they would: not dramatically, but thread by thread, an altered log here, a voided warrant there, Vann's four hours of testimony entered into the record in his careful geologist's voice, each date pinned like a specimen. The presiding officer asks me to give my account of the recovery mission, and I do — plainly, without ornament, because I am done with ornament. When she asks why I released the evidence to the press instead of returning it to the ministry as instructed, I say:

"Because the ministry sent men with guns to retrieve it, ma'am. You don't return evidence to the people shooting at it."

Someone in the gallery laughs, and is shushed, and laughs again anyway.

It takes three hours. At the end, the negligence finding from seven years ago is referred for full review with a recommendation I won't see for months. The extradition matter is closed entirely. I walk out of the hearing not free, exactly — there will be more rooms, more tables, more fans — but lighter, the way you feel at a high camp when the storm breaks and you realize the load you've been carrying has gotten wet and frozen and then, somehow, dried again overnight, and your shoulders have forgotten its true weight.

Meera is waiting on the steps with a photographer she pretends she didn't bring. "Headline tomorrow," she says. "'Guide Cleared of Extradition; Massacre Inquiry Widens.' Or I could go with 'The Woman Who Wouldn't Leave Anyone Behind.' More sales."

"Use the first one," I say. "The second one wasn't my idea. It was Dorje's grandmother's. Give her the credit. She earned it sixty years early."

* * *

That night, back in the cell — because cleared of extradition is not the same as cleared, and the review will take the winter — I stand at the bread-slice window and watch the monsoon do its work.

Rain runs down the glass in ropes. Beyond the wall, beyond the town, the Khumbal is getting washed. Every summer it's the same: the storms scour the dust off the rock, swell the meltwater, shift the ice, and in the autumn the mountain appears scrubbed and new, as if the climbing season's griefs had never happened. As if it could forget. But I know better now. It doesn't forget. It keeps. And what it keeps, it eventually gives back — bodies, names, truths, debts — on its own schedule, in its own language, to whoever is patient enough and stubborn enough to listen.

Seven years I measured my life in miles walked away from that mountain. Then one letter dragged me back, and I learned that the miles were never the point. The point was the rope. Who's on it, who's holding it, whether you turn around when you promised to, whether you go back when you swore you wouldn't. I broke the rule once, and it cost three lives and my name, and I kept it once, at the edge of everything, and it cost nearly everything else and bought back something better than a name.

I don't know yet if I'll guide again. The review might restore my license; it might not. Clients might come, or they might read the old headlines and choose safer company. I've made peace with not knowing. Vann wants me on the ice when the porters come home, and I said yes, and that's a kind of guiding already — guiding the dead down, gently, at last.

The rain thickens. On the glass, drops gather and merge and run, and through them the lights of Rungli blur into soft constellations, and somewhere past them the mountain stands in the storm taking its bath, washed clean the way only monsoon can wash a thing — not erasing it, but rinsing it bright.

I touch the copper amulet at my throat. It's warm.

Outside, the rain wakes the whole valley, and for the first time in seven years, I am not counting anything at all.
