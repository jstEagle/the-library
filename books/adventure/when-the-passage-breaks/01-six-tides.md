## 1. Six Tides

The wreck was forbidden, which was why Casper Sterling dove it at dawn, alone, with no flag flying and his transponder switched off like a guilty conscience.

He went down the anchor line into water the color of old bottle glass, green going to black, and the cold closed over him in layers—the surface warm as bathwater from yesterday's sun, then a band of chill, then the deep cold that lived down at forty feet and never left. His lamp cut a cone through the murk and lit up the *Merrow's Pride* where she lay on her side in the silt, masts snapped, hull furred with weed so thick she looked less like a ship than a reef that had learned to dream.

She'd been down eleven years. Nobody was supposed to touch her—harbor authority had posted the notice himself, back when Casper was still a boy running lines for his father—and nobody did, because the fine for touching her was more than most salvagers earned in a season. But fines were levied by men, and debts were collected by Marek Voss, and between those two facts Casper had done his arithmetic in the dark hours before first light and come out on the wrong side of the law.

Fourteen days. That's what Voss had given him. Fourteen days to find nine hundred crowns or lose the *Kestrel*, and with her every tool of the trade he owned, and after that there wasn't much left to be but a shorehand on someone else's deck, hauling other men's money up out of the cold.

So: the *Merrow's Pride*. Copper fittings alone would clear half the debt. The brass bell, if it hadn't rotted loose from its bracket, might clear the rest. He worked by feel mostly, prying at the galley bulkhead where the fittings ran thickest, his knife blade whispering against wood gone soft as bread, and the silt rose around him in slow brown ghosts whenever he moved too fast. A school of silver perch poured past his lamp like spilled coins. Somewhere below him, deeper in the wreck's drowned throat, something shifted—a beam settling, maybe, or just the river breathing through her ribs—and he told himself it was the current and kept working.

His father had taught him to dive this stretch of river, back when the compass on Casper's wrist still pointed true north and the man wearing it could still stand up straight. *The river gives to them that ask polite,* his father used to say, tapping the brass case with one cracked fingernail. *It takes double from them that grab.* Casper grabbed now. There wasn't time to be polite.

He filled the mesh bag until its weight dragged at his shoulder strap—copper pipe fittings, a porthole with glass miraculously whole, a ship's lantern crusted green as seafoam. Enough. Maybe enough. He checked his gauge, saw the needle lying toward empty the way his accounts always did, and started up the line.

The light changed as he rose, black to green to hammered silver, and he broke the surface into a morning so clean it felt like an accusation. Mist stood off the water in long white banners. The far bank was a dark smudge of pine. And tied alongside the *Kestrel*, riding her wake with the easy insolence of a vessel that had never once been boarded, was a navy launch, gray and sharp-nosed, with a woman in a lieutenant's coat sitting on Casper's own stern bench as though she'd bought it.

Casper hung on the ladder rungs, treading water, and considered his options. They were few and all of them bad. Swim for the far bank? She'd watched him surface; she'd know these waters better than he wanted to think about. Go back down? The wreck would keep, but his air wouldn't, and drowning to avoid a conversation struck him as excessive even for his week.

So he climbed aboard, dripping, and set the mesh bag down between them like a feeble shield.

"Lieutenant," he said, because her coat said lieutenant and the rest of her said trouble.

"Commander Ashen, actually." She didn't rise. She was perhaps forty, gray at the temples, with a face like a chart—everything marked, nothing wasted. Her eyes went to the bag, to the porthole's pale circle of glass, to the green-crusted lantern. "You're diving a restricted wreck."

"I'm diving my own boat's anchorage. The wreck happens to be under it."

"The wreck is under your anchorage because you anchored over it at dawn with your transponder dark." She said it without heat, the way a man reads coordinates. "I've been sitting here twenty minutes watching you strip copper off a grave. I could write you a citation that would end your career on this river."

"You'd have to find a career to end." Casper wrung out his hood, not looking at her. "What do you want, Commander?"

Something flickered across her face—not quite approval, but the shape of it. "Directness. Good. I don't have time for the usual dance." She reached into her coat and produced a folded paper, laid it flat on the bench between them, and weighted it with a small brass object that Casper's eyes went to before he could stop himself.

A compass. Old pattern, hinged lid, the kind they hadn't made in thirty years. It sat on the paper with the stillness of a held breath.

"There's a wreck," she said. "Not this one. Upriver, past the second narrows, in water deep enough that the fleet's charts show nothing but blue. Six days ago a survey team went out to look at it. They haven't reported since."

"Then report them missing."

"They're not missing," Commander Ashen said quietly. "They were never sent. There is no survey team. There is no wreck. If you ask anyone at the naval office, they will tell you, with great sincerity, that no such expedition exists."

The mist moved off the water behind her in slow procession. Casper found he hadn't breathed in a while and corrected that.

"And you want me to believe you," he said, "over yourself."

"I want you to stop believing anything the fleet tells you, starting with me." She turned the compass over with one finger, an idle, almost tender motion. "On that wreck is a strongbox. Sealed, navy-stamped, lead-lined. I need it brought up and brought to me, unopened. In exchange—" she nodded at the paper under the compass "—your debt to Marek Voss ceases to exist. Not paid. Erased. There's a difference, and you'll understand it eventually."

Casper looked at the paper. Even folded, even upside down, he knew the shape of Voss's letterhead—he'd seen it often enough, usually through a haze of humiliation. "That's not yours to erase."

"No. But the man who holds Voss's paper owes me a favor older than your debt, and favors are the only currency on this river that doesn't devalue." She met his eyes at last, and hers were tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep. "Sterling. I know who your father was. I know what he pulled out of the water at Corren Shoal, and whose life it was. Questions unasked, either direction. That's the offer."

The river lapped against the hull. Somewhere upstream a heron lifted off with a sound like a sail filling. Casper heard all of it and none of it, because underneath the morning sounds his own blood was loud, doing arithmetic again: nine hundred crowns, fourteen days, the *Kestrel*, the ledger, the long slow grind of being owned by a man who smiled while he counted.

Offers like this always cost more than they paid. His father had taught him that too, though not in words—in the way he'd gone quiet for a year after the war, in the way he'd never once said the name of the officer whose life he'd saved, in the way he'd died owing nothing to anybody and owning almost as little. Wary temptation. That was the honest name for what sat in Casper's chest now, heavy as the mesh bag, gleaming as the porthole glass.

"What's in the box?" he asked.

"Questions unasked."

"That's an answer."

"It's the only one I'm authorized to give." She stood, finally, and the launch rocked gently against the *Kestrel*'s hull. "Six tides, Sterling. The river turns six more times before the window closes—currents, moon, the narrows running safe. After the sixth tide, whatever happens upriver happens without us. You can bring me the box, or you can keep diving graves for copper." Her gaze dropped to the mesh bag, then back up. "Though we both know which one pays."

She stepped down into the launch. The engine caught on the second try, a low mutter, and the gray boat began to swing its nose toward the channel.

"Commander." Casper's voice came out rougher than he intended. "If there's no expedition, who am I bringing the box to? You? Or whoever's waiting when you go quiet too?"

Ashen paused with one hand on the gunwale. For a moment the chart of her face showed him something unmarked—a raw edge, quickly covered.

"Bring it to me alive," she said, "and you'll never have to find out."

The launch pulled away into the mist, gray into white into nothing, and Casper stood alone on his deck with a bag of stolen copper, a folded promise, and fourteen days that had just become six tides.

He unfolded the paper. Voss's letterhead, sure enough—but the figures beneath it weren't crossed out. They were simply gone, the ledger lines blank, as if the debt had been unwritten rather than forgiven. Somebody with real reach had done this. Somebody who could make nine hundred crowns un-happen.

He should burn it. He knew that the way he knew weather coming—offers like this always cost more than they paid, and the interest was never printed on the page. He should finish the dive, sell the copper, take Voss's beating like a man, stay small and solvent and free of whatever the fleet was burying upriver.

Instead he folded the paper along its creases and put it inside his coat, over his heart, where the important and the terrible both tended to live.

Then he noticed the compass.

The commander had taken hers with her—he'd watched her pocket it. But his father's compass was on his wrist, where it had lived for six years, dead as a stone, needle frozen at some forgotten bearing since the week of the funeral. He'd kept it anyway. Divers are sentimental about instruments; an instrument that has lied to you once teaches you more than one that never has.

The needle was moving.

Slowly, with the deliberation of a thing long asleep turning over, it swung off its frozen bearing and settled—steady, certain, ignoring the iron of his own boat, ignoring every law of brass and magnetism he'd ever been taught—pointing upriver.

Toward the second narrows. Toward the wreck that didn't exist.

Casper stood very still in the last of the mist, and the tide began to turn beneath his keel, and somewhere above the far bank the sun cleared the pines and laid a bright road across the water, all the way upriver, as if the river itself were showing him the way and daring him to walk it.

* * *

He gave himself until noon to talk himself out of it.

He failed by eleven-thirty.

The *Kestrel* sat low in the water with her tanks full and her engine warmed, and Casper stood at the harbor master's window in the customs house, watching the salvage fleet's boats rafted together along the north quay like sleeping animals, and did the only arithmetic that mattered. Six tides. Voss's men drank at the Anchorline every evening and talked too loudly, and by tomorrow night the rumor of a navy job upriver would be worth more than the rumor was true, and boats would move regardless. If he was going to run the narrows ahead of a crowd, he needed to leave before the crowd knew there was a race.

Which meant he needed to see Voss first. Not to beg—never that again—but to buy time with the only coin he had left, which was the appearance of compliance. Show the broker his face. Let Voss smell fear and count it. Keep the ledger open just long enough to close it from the other end.

Outside the customs house, the harbor smelled of tar and brine and the particular sweetness of rope rotting in the sun. Gulls argued over the fish market. Everything ordinary. Everything exactly as it had been yesterday, before a gray launch had slid out of the mist and offered to erase him.

His father's compass ticked softly against his wrist as he walked, needle steady, pointing upriver, patient as a debt.
