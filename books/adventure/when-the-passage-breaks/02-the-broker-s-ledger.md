## 2. The Broker's Ledger

The harbor office of Marek Voss smelled of tallow, tar, and old paper—paper that had been counted too many times by too many hands. Casper Sterling stood on the worn floorboards and felt the building settle around him like a jaw closing.

He had dressed in his cleanest shirt for this. It hadn't helped. Voss's clerk had looked him up and down the way a man appraises a boat with a soft hull, and now Casper waited beneath a lamp that buzzed like something trapped, watching dust turn in the light while the tide clock on the wall ticked toward low water.

*Low water.* Everything in this town ran on tides, including debts.

The inner door opened without a knock from within. "Mr. Sterling." The voice was dry as a mooring line left in the sun. "You're punctual. That's new."

Marek Voss was smaller than men remembered him being. That was the trick of him. People expected bulk to match the weight of what he held—the paper on half the working hulls in the harbor—and were always briefly wrong-footed when they found instead a narrow man in a gray coat, precise as a surveyor's rule, with eyes the color of water over slate. He did not offer a hand. He never offered hands; he took them, when he took anything at all.

"Sit," Voss said, and sat himself behind a desk so bare it looked swept for inspection. A single ledger lay closed upon it, bound in green leather gone black at the corners. Casper knew that book. Every man in the harbor knew that book, the way sailors know a reef they've struck.

He sat. The chair was lower than Voss's. He was almost certain it was meant to be.

"You're wondering why I asked you here instead of sending the usual arrangement." Voss opened the ledger without looking down at it, the way a man opens a door he has walked through ten thousand times. "The usual arrangement is for men who might run. You don't run, Sterling. You sink slowly and pay what floats up. It's one of your few virtues."

"I came because you asked," Casper said. His voice came out steadier than he'd expected. Somewhere below them, through the floorboards, he could hear the harbor working—hulls knocking against pilings, gulls arguing over offal, the long groan of a crane swinging cargo. Sounds of a world where money moved honestly, or at least loudly. Up here it moved quietly, and quiet money was the kind that drew blood.

"Your account." Voss turned the ledger toward him, and there it was, in ink gone brown with age: *C. STERLING — vessel *Saltmark*, gear, advances, interest compounded quarterly.* The number beneath it had grown again since winter. It grew the way barnacles grew: steadily, invisibly, until one day the hull wouldn't answer the helm. "Four hundred and eleven marks, twelve shillings. Do you know what that buys me, Sterling? Not much. Do you know what it buys *you*? Nothing. That's the arithmetic of debt. You own a boat worth perhaps two hundred, and I own the paper on her, and between those two facts lies a gap that only work can fill."

"I've been diving," Casper said. "Wrecks off the point. There's brass down there. Copper sheathing. I've been bringing up—"

"Copper." Voss said the word as if tasting it. "Yes. And yet your deposits have been thin. Which brings me to why we're speaking face to face today, man to man, ledger to debtor." He folded his hands. They were small hands, clean, with a clerk's calluses rather than a sailor's. "I've heard a rumor."

The lamp buzzed. Out on the water, someone shouted an order, and the sound came through the window glass flattened and far away, like a voice heard underwater.

"Rumors are cheap in this town," Casper said.

"This one cost me three marks and a favor, which is more than most rumors are worth, so I expect it to be true." Voss leaned back. "A naval officer. Young. Lieutenant's boards. Came to this harbor on a fleet tender three days ago, asked no questions at the customs house, and paid a visit—to a salvage diver. Not to the guild. Not to the brokers. To a diver with debts." He smiled, and it was a small, tidy thing, like a knot pulled tight. "A diver named Sterling."

Casper kept his face still. It was a skill learned young, at a table like this one, across from a man like this one, while his father's debts were read aloud like charges against a criminal. *Don't let them see the water rise,* his father used to say. *A flooded hold sinks quiet or loud, but either way it sinks.*

"I dive wrecks," Casper said. "Officers sometimes need things dived. That's not a rumor, it's a trade."

"Of course." Voss nodded pleasantly. "And I'm sure this officer asked nothing strange. Nothing about sealed boxes. Nothing about ships the fleet doesn't keep in its registers." He watched Casper with those slate eyes, and Casper understood, with a cold settling in his chest, that the rumor had teeth—that somewhere in this office, in some drawer, was paper connecting a lieutenant's name to a tender's manifest to a day and hour, and that Voss had bought his way one link down the chain before running out of chain.

Or before choosing to stop. With Voss you could never tell whether you'd reached the end of what he knew or the edge of what he wanted you to think he knew.

"I don't discuss clients," Casper said.

"No. Clients are sacred. I respect that—it's why I do business with divers and not with officers. Officers talk. Divers hold their breath." Voss turned the ledger back around and drew a pen from a drawer, uncapped it, and made a small mark in the margin beside Casper's name. The scratch of the nib was the loudest thing in the room. "Here is what respect is worth, though. Your quarter's interest comes due in nineteen days. After that, the note on the *Saltmark* calls. I exercise it, I sell her at auction, and the auction price of a working hull in a harbor full of working hulls is—" he made a small gesture, a shrug of the pen hand, "—whatever the lowest desperate man will pay. You've met yourself. You know the figure."

Nineteen days. Six tides of good diving weather, if the weather cooperated, and the river mouth had been sulking all week behind a wall of fog.

"I'll pay," Casper said.

"I believe you intend to. Intention is common as gull feathers." Voss capped the pen. "But intentions don't clear ledgers, and I'll be honest with you, Sterling, because honesty between us has always been our currency: I would rather have my four hundred eleven marks than your boat. Boats depreciate. Debts compound. So consider this meeting a courtesy—a rare one. I am telling you, face to face, that I know something is moving in your life. Something with navy ink on it. And I am telling you that whatever it pays, I will want to know how it was earned, because if the fleet is hiring divers for unregistered work, then the fleet owes duties and fees to this harbor, and this harbor's books are kept—" he touched the green ledger once, lightly, the way a priest touches an altar, "—by me."

"And if I say no?"

Voss looked genuinely puzzled, as if Casper had asked what would happen if he declined to breathe. "To what? I haven't offered anything. I've only observed the weather." He rose, and the meeting was somehow already over, the way his meetings always ended—on his feet, door open, the other man still seated and lower than before. "Nineteen days, Sterling. Bring me marks, not mysteries. If the mystery pays better than the marks, well." The small smile again. "We both know which one you'll bring me."

* * *

Outside, the fog had burned off and the harbor lay bright and merciless under a hard noon sun. Casper walked the length of the quay with his collar up even though the day was warm, past the fish stalls and the rope walks and the chandlers' windows full of brass and glass, and mapped his escape routes the way he mapped a wreck: methodically, knowing every route had its obstruction.

Run south, work the coastal wrecks under another port's flag: the *Saltmark* carried Voss's lien papers in her locker, and any harbor master with a ledger of his own would seize her inside a week. Run north, sell the boat outright and vanish inland: two hundred marks against four hundred eleven owed, and Voss's reach extended further inland than most men believed—he owned the ferry concessions, the grain factors, half the pawnshops in the river towns. Stay and dive honest: copper and brass at current prices, six tides' worth, maybe forty marks if the sea gave generously, and the sea had stopped giving generously years ago.

Every road ended at the same place: this desk, this ledger, this narrow gray man with clean hands.

Which left the lieutenant. Which left the box. Which left a job the fleet officially didn't have, paying—by the lieutenant's careful phrasing—*enough to change the shape of a man's accounts.*

Casper stopped at the rail where the quay overlooked the channel. Below him the water swung green and brown with the ebb, carrying scum lines and a dead gull out toward the bar. Somewhere upriver, past the checkpoints and the fog banks, lay a wreck the charts didn't name, holding something the fleet denied owning. And somewhere behind him, in a tall narrow office smelling of tallow, a man had just priced that secret into the harbor's books.

Two forces now knew about the job: the one who wanted it done, and the one who wanted it taxed. Casper had the distinct feeling of a diver hanging in mid-water between surface and bottom, pulled by both, owned by neither.

He turned his collar down. Nineteen days. Six tides.

He went to find his boat, and to check, first, the one instrument aboard older than his debts—his father's compass, dead these five years, riding in its box beneath the wheel.

It was, he told himself, just a habit. Just something to check.

Like a man checking a wound to see if it's stopped bleeding.
