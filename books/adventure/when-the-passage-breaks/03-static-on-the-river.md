## 3. Static on the River

The fog came off the water in slow gray tongues, and Casper Sterling let it.

He sat in the wheelhouse of the *Maren Kay* with a mug of coffee gone cold between his hands, watching the harbor dissolve until the cranes were suggestions and the breakwater was rumor. Three days since Lieutenant Ashford had stood on his deck with rain beading on his officer's cap and made an offer that smelled like a trap dressed in navy wool. Three days of saying nothing to anyone, which was its own kind of noise.

The strongbox. The wreck the fleet denied. Debts forgiven, questions unasked.

Casper had spent those three days doing what he always did when a decision wouldn't come: he worked. He'd re-caulked the port seam, replaced the fouled zinc anode, sharpened every blade on the boat whether it needed it or not. Now there was nothing left to sharpen, and the decision was still sitting in his chest like a swallowed hook.

So he did what he'd done every morning for eleven years, without thinking about why: he turned on the radio.

It was habit older than the debt, older than Voss's ledger, older than the *Maren Kay* herself. His father had kept the set warm at all hours—"the river talks, Casper, if you're patient enough to listen"—and Casper had inherited the habit along with the compass and the hollow feeling where a father should have been. He dialed through the channels the way other men said their prayers. Fishing fleet chatter. Weather. The coast guard repeating itself in its flat bureaucratic voice.

And then channel nine, the one he'd found by accident two nights ago—a low band frequency, half-buried in static, carrying voices that didn't belong to fishermen or brokers or anyone who said *copy* instead of *aye*. Military cadence. Clipped call signs. An expedition that officially did not exist, talking to itself upriver like ghosts who hadn't yet learned they were dead.

"—say again, base, your last is broken—"

Casper leaned forward. The voice was thin, stretched over distance and interference, but he knew the shape of it now. Three mornings he'd listened. Three mornings of routine reports delivered in that flat naval monotone: depth soundings, current speeds, supply counts. A survey expedition, Ashford had called it. Hydrographic work. Nothing worth remembering.

But nobody ran hydrographic surveys with that much tension in their voices.

"Base copies," the radio crackled. "Advise you hold position until—"

Static. A long wash of it, white and empty as the fog outside.

Then, faintly, a different voice. Younger. Tighter. "—not natural, this isn't—the instruments are all reading wrong, everything's pointing—" And then something else, words drowned under a surge of interference, and beneath the static, so faint Casper might have invented it, a sound he'd heard only once before in his life, the night his father's boat went down: a man screaming into a radio that no longer carried.

Then nothing. Not silence exactly. Silence has edges. This was the absence of them—a carrier wave still open, transmitting the river's own cold breath to anyone listening.

Casper sat very still.

The coffee had gone stone cold in his hands. Outside, the fog pressed against the glass like something waiting to be let in. He told himself what any sensible salvager would tell himself: equipment fails. Weather happens. Upriver in the gorge country the storms came down off the ridgeline without warning and ate signals whole. Eleven years on this water had taught him that the river took radios the way it took anchors and pride and occasionally people.

But the instruments are all reading wrong, everything's pointing—

His father's compass sat on the chart table where it always sat, needle dead and motionless under its cracked crystal, pointing wherever gravity and habit left it. It had been dead for six years, ever since the night of the storm, and Casper kept it aboard anyway because some things you keep even when they've stopped working. Especially when they've stopped working.

He looked at it now without knowing why.

The needle lay still. Whatever the young voice upriver had been describing, it wasn't happening here. Not yet.

Casper exhaled and reached for the transmitter.

Here was the problem, laid out plain as a chart: he reported this, and he became a man who had heard something he shouldn't have. He didn't report it, and he became a man who let thirty voices drown while he weighed his own skin against theirs. Either way, the river collected. The river always collected; his father's ghost had taught him that much before the water finished the lesson.

He keyed the mic. "Coast guard station Gray Harbor, this is civilian vessel *Maren Kay*, do you copy?"

Static, then the flat voice he knew from a hundred weather broadcasts. "*Maren Kay*, Gray Harbor. Go ahead."

"I'm picking up an open carrier on the low band, channel nine. Naval traffic. I've been monitoring it three mornings—it went down mid-transmission about ten minutes ago. Voices reporting instrument failures, then nothing. I think they're in trouble."

A pause long enough to have weight. When the voice came back, it had changed—not alarmed, but careful, the way a man gets careful when he steps onto ice. "*Maren Kay*, confirm your monitoring of—that frequency is not an assigned civilian channel."

"It's not assigned to anybody. That's my point. There was an expedition on it."

Another pause. Longer. In the background Casper heard a second voice, low, asking a question he couldn't make out, and the first voice answering with a single word: *Sterling.*

His own name, spoken like a file being opened.

"*Maren Kay*." The voice was smooth now, too smooth, a surface over moving water. "Be advised there are no naval expeditions currently operating in this sector. Your report will be logged. Maintain radio discipline on unassigned frequencies. Gray Harbor out."

The channel clicked dead.

Casper lowered the handset slowly, the way you lower something you suspect might be loaded.

No expeditions currently operating. Except he'd heard them. Three mornings of depth soundings and supply counts and men calling each other by numbers instead of names. And now an open carrier wave singing the river's static out into the gray, attached to thirty men the navy had just erased with four sentences and a click.

They weren't missing. Missing required having existed first.

He sat back and looked at the compass again. Dead needle. Dead crystal. Six years of pointing nowhere, faithful to nothing.

*Everything's pointing—*

Pointing where?

The question slid into him cold as river water in February, and he couldn't have said why, except that the young voice on the radio had sounded less afraid of drowning than of whatever the instruments were telling them. Casper had grown up on stories of the gorge country, where the old charts showed blank spaces and the pilots refused to run at night, where his father had gone looking for something and the river had kept him. Men didn't scream like that over broken equipment. Men screamed like that when the world stopped agreeing with itself.

Outside, the fog was lifting, peeling back from the water in rags. And there, riding at anchor fifty yards off his stern where no boat had been five minutes ago, sat a gray launch with no markings and a figure at its rail watching him through binoculars.

Casper didn't move. Didn't reach for the radio again. He sat in his wheelhouse with his cold coffee and his dead compass and let himself be watched, and understood, with the particular clarity that comes just before a fall, that the choice Ashford had offered him three days ago was never really a choice at all. The navy hadn't asked him to find a box. They'd asked him to be the one man foolish enough to go looking where everyone official had agreed not to look—and now that the expedition had stopped transmitting, he wasn't the salvage diver anymore.

He was the search party.

The figure at the rail lowered the binoculars. The launch's engine turned over, once, and held—an idling threat, a held breath.

Casper rose, crossed to the transom, and lifted the canvas off his dive gear. If they wanted to watch him prepare, let them watch. Let them log it. Let whoever owned that smooth coast guard voice read in his movements exactly what Casper already knew in his bones:

Thirty men were silent on a river that officially held no one, and the only instrument left in the world that might know where they'd gone was hanging dead on his chart table, waiting for a reason to wake.

As if it heard him thinking, the needle twitched.

Just once. Just enough.

Casper stared at it, and the fog closed back over the gray launch, and somewhere upriver, under the static, the open channel went on broadcasting to no one at all.

* * *

He gave the launch until noon. It stayed. So did he—but he stayed busy, running tanks, checking regulators, coiling line with hands that needed the work, and all the while the needle lay quiet again, and all the while the open carrier hissed on channel nine like a wound that wouldn't close.

At 12:40, a dinghy putted over from the launch. The man who climbed aboard the *Maren Kay* wore civilian oilskins over a posture that no oilskin could disguise, and he smiled the way government property smiles: efficiently.

"Mr. Sterling. Commander Hale." No hand offered. "You made a transmission this morning."

"I report what I hear. That used to be encouraged."

"What you heard was atmospheric interference on an unassigned frequency." Hale's eyes went past Casper's shoulder, cataloguing the wheelhouse—chart table, compass, dive gear, the *Maren Kay*'s tired honest clutter. "There is no expedition. There was no expedition. I'd take it as a personal favor if your radio developed narrower tastes."

"And the carrier wave that's still open right now? You can hear it from your launch. Interference doesn't hold a channel for three hours."

Hale's smile thinned by a degree. "Rivers do strange things to signals, Mr. Sterling. Especially upriver. Especially this week." He paused, and let the pause do its work. "Lieutenant Ashford spoke with you. Whatever arrangement exists between you and this service, it does not include commentary on frequencies. Do we understand each other?"

Casper held his gaze. "Your men stopped talking mid-sentence, Commander. Somebody's mother raised one of them."

For the first time, something moved behind Hale's eyes—there and gone, quick as a fish turning. "Somebody's mother," he repeated softly. "Yes. Well." He stepped back to the rail. "Dive safe, Mr. Sterling. The river's been hungry lately."

The dinghy carried him back through the rags of fog. Casper stood at the transom watching the gray launch hold its station, and felt the last comfortable lie drain out of the morning: they knew. Hale knew the channel was live, knew the expedition had gone quiet, and had come not to ask what Casper heard but to tell him what he was permitted to have heard.

Which meant the denial wasn't bureaucracy. It was policy.

Which meant whatever waited upriver was something the navy would sink its own reputation to bury—and had maybe already sunk more than that.

Casper went inside, took his father's compass off the chart table, and held it in both hands like a question he was afraid to ask aloud. The needle hung dead and indifferent, faithful to nothing, the way it had been for six years.

On channel nine, the static breathed.

"Talk to me," Casper whispered—to the river, to the dead, to the small brass instrument in his palms. "Somebody up there, talk to me."

The needle shivered.

Swung left. Held—steady, deliberate, unmistakable—pointing not north but upriver, toward the gorge country, toward the blank space on the charts where his father had drowned and thirty men had just stopped existing.

Toward the mouth of the river, where the water ran black and the old pilots refused to go.

Casper set the compass down with hands that were not quite steady, and began, quietly, to load the boat.
