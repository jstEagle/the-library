## 4. Compass North

The compass had been dead for eleven years.

Casper knew the exact count because he'd stopped counting at ten and then felt ashamed of himself for keeping a ledger of grief. His father's brass compass—*the* compass, the one that had ridden in Aldous Sterling's coat pocket through thirty years of salvage work, through the war's last convoy runs, through every dive Casper had ever watched the old man walk into and one he hadn't walked out of—had died the winter after the funeral. The needle had simply stopped agreeing with the world. It would swing lazily to some private north of its own invention, settle nowhere, drift like a drunk leaving a tavern. Casper had oiled it, demagnetized it, held it over every lodestone and engine block on the *Maren*, and finally wrapped it in flannel and put it in the drawer beneath his berth where dead things went to be kept rather than buried.

He was mending net in the wheelhouse when he heard it.

That was the absurd part—the sound. A small, dry ticking, like a beetle turning over in a wooden box. He sat still with the twine in his fingers and listened, telling himself it was the hull settling, the tide shifting under the pilings, the ordinary percussion of a boat at rest. The *Maren* made a hundred sounds at anchor and he had names for all of them. This wasn't one of them.

The drawer was open six inches. He never left it open.

Inside, the flannel bundle lay as he'd left it, but the ticking came from within it, patient and small, and when Casper unwrapped the cloth the compass sat in his palm warm—warm, in a cold wheelhouse, in October—and its needle was moving. Not swinging. Not drunk. It swept in a slow arc, hesitated, and settled toward the southwest with the calm certainty of a thing that had been asleep a long time and now intended to stay awake.

Southwest. Toward the river mouth.

"Old man," Casper said quietly, "you're broken."

The needle didn't so much as tremble.

* * *

He spent the rest of the morning proving it wrong, because that was what Sterling men did with impossible things: they tested them until the impossible either apologized or doubled down.

First the ship's compass, mounted in its binnacle above the wheel—a proper gimballed instrument, serviced last spring. North, steady as scripture. He carried the old brass compass to the bow, to the stern, down into the engine room past the battery bank, up the mast steps with the wind pulling at his collar. Everywhere the binnacle said north, the dead man's compass said southwest, unwavering, its needle riding on its jewel bearing with a smoothness it hadn't shown since before the funeral. Not jittering. Not hunting. Pointed.

Then he got serious about it.

He laid out everything on the wheelhouse chart table like a surgeon laying out instruments: the hand-bearing compass he used for running lines, two spare needles from the spares tin, a cheap hiking compass he'd won in a card game and never trusted, and the *Maren*'s backup azimuth mirror. Five instruments. He set them in a row and stood over them, and four of them agreed with each other to within a degree, and the fifth pointed southwest across the harbor water toward the river mouth as if there were a second pole hidden out there somewhere beyond the breakwater, magnetic and personal, waiting for him specifically.

He tried the classic tests. Iron: he swept a wrench around the housing, and the needle gave a single polite wobble and returned to southwest. Electricity: he ran it along the radio's antenna lead while the set crackled, nothing. He even held it against the hull plating near the engine, where thirty years of magnetized steel should have sent any needle into hysterics, and the needle only firmed up, as if it were glad of the company.

By noon he was sitting on the wheelhouse bench with the compass flat on his knee, watching it point at the river mouth, and the eerie part wasn't that it moved. Instruments failed all the time; saltwater saw to that. The eerie part was that it moved *with intention*. A broken needle wanders. This one had an address.

His father had bought the compass in Port Callow the year Casper was born, off a deaf old chandler who claimed it had come off a survey vessel that mapped the upper river before the war. Aldous had never fully trusted it—"temperamental," he'd called it, "like everyone worth knowing"—but he'd carried it anyway, and once, when Casper was nine and full of questions, the old man had turned it over in his scarred hands and said something Casper hadn't thought about in decades:

"It doesn't always point north, boy. Sometimes it points at what's owed."

At nine, that had sounded like poetry. At thirty-four, sitting in a wheelhouse with a broker's deadline three tides away and a naval lieutenant's sealed orders in his coat, it sounded like a sentence being read aloud over his head.

Because southwest, past the breakwater, past the last channel buoy, was the river mouth. And up the river, forty miles through country the fleet pretended not to care about, lay the drowned hulk the lieutenant had paid him to forget the name of. And somewhere between here and there, an expedition that officially did not exist had gone quiet on a frequency that officially did not exist, and Casper was the only man on the coast who knew they'd stopped talking.

He hadn't decided yet. That was the truth of it, and he held onto the truth of it the way a drowning man holds a spar. He had not decided. There was still time to be sensible. Take the strongbox job if the box was reachable, sell whatever was inside, pay Voss, keep breathing. Or refuse the whole rotten arrangement, sell the *Maren*'s spare anchor and the dinghy and his father's diving watch, scrape together enough to buy another month of Voss's patience, and keep his name off whatever list the navy kept of people who knew too much. Both roads were bad. Both roads were *roads*, which meant they went somewhere, which was more than could be said for standing still.

But the needle pointed southwest, and it did not waver, and it was warm in his hand like a living thing.

* * *

In the afternoon he rowed out to the mooring buoy to check the pennant line, mostly to have something to do with his hands, and the compass rode in his coat pocket the way it had ridden in his father's for thirty years, and he caught himself talking to it.

"Don't get ideas," he told it, sculling with the oars shipped. "You're a tool. Tools don't want things."

The needle held its line over his knee, southwest, southwest, and the current under the hull was running the same direction, out toward the mouth, and the tide tables pinned inside the wheelhouse said the next ebb began at dusk. Six tides, the lieutenant had said. He'd already burned two deciding.

It was coming back in that he heard the gulls change their minds all at once—the way they did when something big moved below them—and he looked up and saw the salvage fleet.

Three boats, maybe four, standing out past the breakwater in loose formation, working engines, deck cranes lashed down for open water. Not fishing boats. Salvage. He knew the silhouettes the way other men knew faces: the *Corvane* with her patched funnel, the pair of converted trawlers Voss kept on retainer, and behind them a low gray shape with too-clean lines that no harbor registry would admit to. They were heading not out to sea but along the coast, northeast first, the long way—the way you'd go if you meant to enter the river from the seaward side without passing the harbor pilot station and its inconvenient questions.

Casper stood up in the rocking dinghy so fast the compass nearly went overboard.

They knew. Someone had talked—Voss, or someone who sold to Voss, or the navy itself changing its mind about deniability—and the race he'd been telling himself he hadn't entered had already started without him, and the starting gun had fired two tides ago.

He sat back down slowly. His heart was going hard against his ribs, but underneath the fear there was something else, colder and stranger, and it took him a moment to recognize it because he'd never felt it about a piece of brass before.

Relief. The decision had been taken out of his hands—or rather, both bad roads had collapsed into one, and the one road left happened to be the one his father's compass had been pointing at all morning, steady as a heartbeat, warm as a held hand.

He pulled for the *Maren* with the current helping him, and the needle swung gently with each stroke but always came home to southwest, patient, certain, like a dog that knows the way and is willing to wait for the fool holding the leash.

Aboard, he didn't wrap the compass in flannel. He set it on the chart table, open, facing the river mouth, and weighted it with a shell casing so it couldn't wander, though it showed no inclination to. Then he pulled the tide tables down and read them properly for the first time in days.

Ebb at dusk. Ebb again before dawn. Two windows to run forty miles of river in the dark, past checkpoints, past snags, past whatever had made an expedition of grown men stop speaking mid-sentence.

He was coiling rope when the radio, tuned out of habit to the dead expedition channel, coughed.

Not static this time. Static had a texture, a wash, a white nothing. This was different—a carrier wave, faint, rhythmic, deliberate. Three pulses. Pause. Three pulses. Pause. Someone out there in the dark upriver was keying a microphone they weren't speaking into, tapping the only signal left to them, over and over, into a silence nobody was supposed to be listening to.

SOS, in the oldest shorthand there was. Not a call for help. A call for *witness*.

Casper stood in the wheelhouse with his hand flat on the chart table, beside the compass, and outside the light was going amber and long over the water, and the tide was nearly turned.

"All right," he said—to the compass, to the river, to the dead man whose hands had worn the brass smooth. "All right. I'm coming."

He started the engine.
