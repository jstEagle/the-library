## 5. The Fleet Weighs Anchor

Casper heard the fleet before he saw it—the deep-throated cough of a dozen diesels turning over in the dark before dawn, a sound like the harbor clearing its throat to swallow something whole.

He stood on the deck of the *Maren*, coffee going cold in his hand, and watched the lights come alive along the salvage quay. Cranes swung awake. Floodlamps bloomed white against the river fog. Men moved along the docks in that particular hurry that meant money was close and someone else might get there first.

He counted hulls. Nine. Ten. The *Corvus* at the end of the line, Voss's own tender, riding low with fuel.

They were going upriver.

His hands went still around the mug. Three days since the static had eaten the expedition's last transmission. Two days since his father's compass had begun its slow, patient insistence on a heading no chart agreed with. One day since he'd loaded tanks and line and told himself—lying, the way he always lied to himself about the water—that he was only running tests.

Now the fleet knew too. Which meant Voss knew. Which meant the anonymous tip had gone out from somewhere warm and dry, somewhere with a ledger open on the desk and a pen held like a knife.

"Damn you," Casper said quietly, to no one, to everyone.

The *Maren* rocked under him as a barge wake rolled past. He looked at the compass lashed by the wheel, its needle trembling toward the river mouth like a dog straining at a leash, and understood that whatever window he'd imagined he had, it had just closed. The fleet would run the checkpoints in daylight, loud and legal, throwing their weight at every navy patrol between here and the narrows. They'd draw the eyes. They'd draw the guns.

Which meant the dark was his.

He dumped the coffee over the rail, where it vanished into black water without a sound, and went forward to cast off.

* * *

Ilsa Reyes felt the fleet move through her feet before her ears caught it—a tremor in the pilings of the *Salt Grace*, a shiver running up through old wood and older iron, the harbor itself announcing what was coming.

She'd been awake for hours anyway. She was always awake before dawn; it was a habit she'd learned in the years when waking meant Casper Sterling's boat sliding out past the breakwater without her, and she'd never managed to unlearn it, though God knew she'd tried.

Now she stood at her wheelhouse window with a mug she wasn't drinking and watched the salvage quay light up like a carnival, and she did the arithmetic she'd been doing since midnight.

An anonymous tip had come through the brokers' wire at 23:40. She'd seen it because she paid a dockhand to read her everything that crossed the wire, and because after eleven years in this trade she trusted no message that arrived unsigned. *Wreck located above the narrows. High-value cargo. First flag takes all.* No coordinates. No name. Just enough bait to fill the river with hungry boats.

And there was exactly one man who wanted the river filled with hungry boats right now, one man whose whole business was other people's debts, and his name was Marek Voss.

"He's flushing him," Ilsa said to the empty wheelhouse. "Like a covey of birds."

Because Casper would be upriver already or near enough. Because a tip like that could only be aimed at one diver stubborn enough to run alone, and the point wasn't the wreck—the point was making sure that when Casper surfaced with whatever the navy man had sent him down for, there'd be ten witnesses and a bounty on his mast. Voss didn't need to catch him. He just needed the fleet to do it for free.

She set the mug down harder than she meant to.

Eleven years ago she'd have been on that quay with the rest of them, elbowing for position, laughing at the frenzy. Eleven years ago she and Casper had raced each other up and down this coast for pocket change and the pleasure of beating each other, and losing to him had never once felt like losing, because afterward there'd be rum on his deck and her head on his shoulder and the whole gray sea doing nothing but breathing.

Then his father drowned, and the debt came, and Casper changed into a man who took jobs he wouldn't talk about, and she had watched him drown slowly in air instead of water, and she had not reached for him, because pride is just fear wearing good boots.

She wasn't proud anymore. She was tired, and angry, and standing in front of a choice with two doors and a wall behind them.

Door one: race them. Take the *Salt Grace* upriver fast and clean—her boat was the fastest thing on this water that wasn't military—and beat the mob to the wreck, take the cargo, pay her own quiet debts, and let Casper be somebody else's problem. That was the salvager's answer. That was the answer she'd have given at twenty-six.

Door two: warn him. Raise him on the channel they'd never formally retired, the one they both kept quiet out of some superstition neither admitted to, and tell him the trap was sprung, tell him to run, spend her own lead in the race buying his escape.

Her hand hovered over the radio. It hovered a long time.

If she warned him, he'd run blind, and blind men on that river died. If she raced him, she'd win or lose, and either way she'd be one more set of jaws in Voss's mouth, doing the broker's work for him, tearing at the one man she—

She stopped herself. Named it plainly, the way you name a snag before you steer for it: she loved him. Had never stopped. Had built an entire career on the far side of the harbor so she wouldn't have to watch his boat come and go.

So she did neither.

Ilsa Reyes turned from the radio, went down into the engine room, and began pulling the *Salt Grace*'s maintenance covers off with her own hands, in full view of anyone walking the dock, looking for all the world like a woman whose boat was broken.

Let the fleet run loud and daylight and stupid. Let Voss think his trap was closing on schedule. She'd wait for dark, slip out past the breakwater with her running lights cold, and take the middle passage—the one only three captains alive knew, because two of them were dead—and she'd find Casper on that river not as his rival and not as his rescuer but as the third thing, the thing nobody had planned for.

Not racing. Not warning. Running her own race, on her own water, for reasons she'd examine later, if she lived.

Outside, the first of the fleet's boats backed off the quay, its searchlight sweeping the fog like a lighthouse gone mad.

* * *

By midmorning Casper was thirty miles upriver and the world had narrowed to brown water, green walls, and the low mutter of his engine. He ran without charts open, which was madness, except that he had a better instrument than any chart ever printed lashed to his wheel, and it had not wavered once in two days.

The compass needle lay steady on a bearing that cut across every bend of the river like it knew a straight line the water didn't. His father's compass. Dead for six years, salted and silent, and now alive and pointing somewhere impossible with the calm certainty of a man giving directions to his own grave.

At the second checkpoint he hunched under a tarp among fish crates while a naval launch idled alongside, and he listened to the boarding officer complain to his own crew about the traffic—"whole damn fleet went upriver this morning, like a fire drill"—and heard, beneath the complaint, the thing underneath: confusion. The patrols hadn't ordered the fleet through. The fleet was simply coming, the way floodwater comes.

After the launch fell away, Casper ran on, and the river changed around him. The banks drew closer. The current turned cold. Somewhere in the last hour the birds had stopped, all of them, and the silence they left behind was worse than any noise, a silence with weight, a silence like held breath.

Near dusk he found the camp.

He smelled it before he saw it—woodsmoke gone sour, and under that, faintly, the sweet-rot smell he knew from bodies the river gave back. The tents stood on the east bank in a stand of dead cedars, and they were wrong. Torn open. Poles snapped. Gear scattered in a radius no storm makes, because storms throw things in one direction and this wreckage had been thrown in every direction at once.

He beached the *Maren* and went in with a flare pistol and no good sense.

Inside the largest tent, papers everywhere. Most were ruined, pulp and ink. But one page had dried flat against a supply crate, and he picked it up with fingers that had gone strangely numb, and read it twice because the first time refused to mean anything.

It was journal paper. Expedition stock, the navy's own watermark. And in a careful, failing hand:

*—the box is the reason. Whatever they told us about survey work, the box is the reason we're here, and I don't think we were meant to reach it. R. says the orders contradict themselves. R. says if we surface with it, we don't surface at all—*

The rest was torn away.

Casper stood in the ruined tent with the page shaking in his hand, and the dread that had been following him since the static finally caught up and put its arms around him. This wasn't salvage. It had stopped being salvage pages ago, maybe miles ago, maybe the moment a lieutenant sat on his gunwale and offered forgiveness priced in silence.

Drag marks scored the mud from the tents down to the waterline. Wide ones. Many. Leading in.

The river slid past, black now, patient, keeping whatever it kept.

Casper walked back to his boat, stowed the page inside his jacket against his heart like a letter he intended to deliver, and cast off into the dark. Behind him, far downstream, the searchlights of the fleet came flickering up through the trees like lightning that couldn't decide.

And ahead, unseen, in the cold middle channel, another set of engines ran with their lights doused, closing on his wake.
