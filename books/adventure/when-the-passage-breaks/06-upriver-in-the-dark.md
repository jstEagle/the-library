## 6. Upriver in the Dark

The river at night was a different animal entirely.

By day it was work—brown water, barge traffic, the honest noise of engines and gulls. But after dark it became something older, something that moved with a patience Casper didn't trust. He ran the *Maren* with her running lights doused, navigating by memory and moonlight and the pale ribbon of foam where the current broke over the shallows. The channel markers had gone dark two miles back. Officially they were under maintenance. Unofficially, someone wanted this stretch of river blind.

He'd passed the first checkpoint an hour ago—a navy pinnace riding at anchor off Gullard Point, its searchlight sweeping the water in slow, bored arcs. He'd killed his engine a quarter mile out and drifted past on the tide, sitting in the dark of his own wheelhouse while the light crawled across the water toward him, touched the hull's shadow, and slid away like a hand patting down a coat pocket. His heart hadn't slowed since.

The compass sat on the chart table beside him, needle steady as a plumb line, pointing not north but upriver—upriver and slightly east, toward the drowned fingers of the old delta where the charts went vague and the soundings stopped being anyone's business. His father's compass. Dead for six years, resurrected three days ago into something that no longer cared what north thought of it.

"Whatever you are," Casper murmured to it, "you'd better be worth dying for."

The compass did not answer. It never did. But it held its bearing the way a dog holds a point, quivering faintly against the glass, and Casper had learned in thirty years on the water that when something insisted this hard, you either followed it or spent the rest of your life wondering.

The radio crackled. He flinched, then reached for it out of habit—the expedition's channel, 16.8, which he'd been monitoring since the static took them. Nothing but the river's own voice now: wind in the reeds, the far hoot of a train crossing the estuary bridge. Four days of silence. Four days since Lieutenant Havel's voice had dissolved mid-word—*"...moving it to the secondary site, we'll need the—"*—and become a wall of white noise that the navy swore was sunspot activity and Casper knew was a door closing.

He throttled up and ran on.

* * *

The snags came at him out of nowhere, as snags always did. The old delta was a graveyard of timber—pilings from wharves that had rotted before his grandfather was born, whole trees carried down in some forgotten flood and driven into the mud butt-first, their branches waiting just under the surface like hands. The charts called it the Comb. Salvage men called it other things.

Casper ran it slow, standing at the bow with a boathook despite the cold, feeling the current try to shove the *Maren* sideways onto every one of those waiting arms. Twice he felt the hull kiss wood and held his breath until she slid free. The third time he wasn't so lucky—a submerged spar raked her keel with a sound like a fingernail down slate, and he spent ten miserable minutes in the shallows with a lantern, confirming she'd only scarred her paint.

It was while he stood there, ankle-deep in freezing water, that he smelled the smoke.

Not woodsmoke. Something chemical underneath it—paraffin, maybe, or lamp oil—and beneath that, faintly, the sweet-rot smell of burned cloth. It came threading downriver on the land breeze, thin as a rumor.

His lantern found the bank: reeds, black mud, the shoulder of a hill rising into pine. And cut into the hillside, half-hidden by the trees, a track—the kind of track men make when they drag heavy things repeatedly along the same path.

The compass needle swung, for the first time in three days, and pointed directly at it.

* * *

He beached the *Maren* in a cut of shingle below the track and went up alone, lantern hooded to a slit, the boathook in one hand because he owned no gun and never had. The smoke smell thickened as he climbed. Somewhere above, water dripped off leaves with obscene regularity, counting out seconds he didn't want counted.

The camp sat in a clearing where the track leveled off—a shelf of ground overlooking the river through a screen of pines.

He knew before his lantern found the first tent that no one was living here anymore. You learn to read camps the way you read weather. This one had the stillness of a place already abandoned by everything except its own bad history.

There had been four tents. Two were collapsed, their poles snapped—not fallen, *snapped*, the aluminum bent outward as if something inside had pushed its way through. A third had burned. Its frame stood like a ribcage over a crust of ash, and the paraffin stink rose from it in waves. The fourth still stood, mostly intact, its flap hanging open on the darkness within.

Casper stood at the edge of the clearing for a long moment, listening. Wind. Dripping water. Far off, the river working its stones. No voices. No engine. Whatever had happened here had finished happening—or had moved somewhere else, somewhere the compass wanted him to follow.

He made himself walk the clearing first, methodical, the way his father had taught him to approach any wreck: look before you touch, touch before you take.

There were crates stacked near the cold fire ring, most of them empty, lids pried and tossed aside. One remained sealed, stenciled with a naval ordnance mark he pretended not to recognize. Cooking gear lay scattered as if dropped mid-use—a pot still sitting on the fire ring, rainwater pooled in it, a spoon standing upright in the water like a tiny flag planted by whoever had left in too much of a hurry to pull it out. Boots. One boot, anyway, half-sunk in the mud by the burned tent, and Casper looked away from it quickly because looking at it too long invited questions about the foot.

And paper. Paper everywhere, weighted under stones, soaked and drying and soaked again. Pages torn from journals, scattered by wind into the reeds. He gathered what he could find, seven pages total, and crouched by the lantern to read them while his hands shook in a way he told himself was the cold.

Most were logistics—stores tallies, depth readings, dive schedules in a cramped engineer's hand. The expedition had been diving. Of course they had been diving; Havel had hired Casper to do the same thing, badly, from the other end of the river. But the depth figures were wrong. They dove deeper than the wreck should sit, deeper than the charts allowed for, day after day, and the notes grew shorter and stranger as the numbers grew larger.

Then a page in a different hand—looser, hurried, the ink smeared where water had hit it:

*"Day 9. Marta insists the box is intact but the seal has been broken from the inside. I told her that's impossible. She showed me the scratches. Inside of the lid. Made from within."*

Casper set the page down. Picked it up again. Read it twice more.

The next page was worse:

*"Day 11. Command ordered us to raise it regardless. I want it on record—I am recording it, this is my record—that we were not sent here to salvage. We were sent here to confirm it stayed sunk. The manifest Havel carries lists the strongbox as recovered property. It is not property. It is evidence. Everything the fleet did in the winter of the blockade is in that box, and someone above Havel wants it either in a vault or at the bottom of the world."*

The last page was barely legible, written fast, the pen pressed hard enough to tear through:

*"They came at night. Not the fleet. Men without lights, without markings. Voss's people, if I had to guess—he's been buying information up and down this river for months. They took the box and they took Aldous and they dragged—"*

The sentence ended there. The rest of the page was a smear, and then nothing.

Casper sat back on his heels in the mud of a dead man's camp and let the horror arrive all at once, the way cold water arrives when a suit fails—not gradually, but as a single crushing fact.

Voss. Marek Voss, who owned Casper's debt the way a spider owns a web, who had smiled across his ledger desk and said *I hear the navy's been shopping, Sterling. Interesting friends you keep.* Voss had known. Voss had always known. And Voss's men had come up this river ahead of everyone, taken the strongbox, taken at least one man alive—

The drag marks.

He made himself stand. Made himself follow the track to where it narrowed between the pines and descended toward the water, and there his lantern found what the journal page had promised: gouges in the soft earth, parallel lines of them, deep-set, the kind left by heels, not wheels. Two sets. One large, one small. They ran forty yards down to the shingle and ended at the waterline in a churned confusion of prints and disturbed silt that the river had already begun erasing.

Someone had been dragged into the river. Two someones. And the river had kept them.

Casper turned back toward the standing tent, because there was one thing left he couldn't leave unread. Inside, the gear was military-neat even in abandonment—bedroll rolled, kit stowed—except for the chart table, which had been searched thoroughly and recently. Drawers pulled fully out and set on the floor. Cushions slashed. And pinned under the edge of the table itself, as if someone had hidden it in the last ten seconds they were given, a single sheet of waterproof paper with a naval stamp and four lines of type:

**STRONGBOX — RECOVERY PRIORITY ABSOLUTE. CONTENTS NOT TO BE VIEWED BY CONTRACT PERSONNEL. DELIVER TO HAVEL, LT. CMDR., INTACT AND UNOPENED. FAILURE TO COMPLY CONSTITUTES TREASON UNDER THE ACT.**

Havel's name. Havel's signature. The same lieutenant who had sat on Casper's boat at dawn and offered to buy his silence with his freedom.

Casper folded the order into his jacket, next to the journal pages, and walked back down the drag-marked track to the water. The compass waited on the *Maren*'s chart table where he'd left it, needle steady, patient, pointing east-northeast—out past the last of the delta islands, toward the deep hole where the old charts simply said *unsounded.*

The journal had named the secondary site. The compass had been leading him there since the day it woke. And now, somewhere between the dead camp behind him and the black water ahead, Casper understood that the choice he'd been telling himself he was still making—debt or truth, Voss's ledger or Havel's secret—had been made for him days ago, the moment a dead man's handwriting had crossed his palm.

This stopped being a job at that burned tent. Maybe it had stopped being a job the instant the compass first swung.

He cast off from the shingle into the dark, ran the *Maren* out past the last markerless mile, and watched the compass needle lean forward like a hound coming to the end of its leash—because ahead of him, faint through the pre-dawn fog, he could hear it: the rhythmic clank of a winch, and voices, many voices, working something heavy at the water's edge.

And under the voices, unmistakable even at this distance, the flat commercial accent of Marek Voss, giving orders.
