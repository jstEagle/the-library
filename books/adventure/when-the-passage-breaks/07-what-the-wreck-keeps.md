## 7. What the Wreck Keeps

The river turned to ink at forty feet.

Casper had dived black water before—harbor channels at night, the drowned quarry outside Marrow's Bend—but this was different. This was cold that came up through the suit like a hand closing around his spine, and dark so complete it felt less like an absence of light than a presence of its own, something with weight and patience, waiting for him to make a mistake. His lamp carved a cone maybe six feet deep into it and gave up. Beyond that cone, the river simply continued without him.

He checked his gauge. Sixty feet. The wreck should be below him somewhere in the next twenty, if the compass could be believed—and the compass was the only thing he had left to believe. It sat strapped to his wrist now, his father's old brass thing, its needle steady as a sermon, pointing not north but down and slightly east, toward a place no chart admitted to. He'd stopped fighting it two tides ago. You didn't argue with your dead father's instruments. You followed them, and you hoped they were taking you somewhere you could survive.

Seventy feet. His ears had long since stopped complaining; now they just ached dully, like old bruises. The cold sharpened. Somewhere above him, faint as memory, he could hear the *Marrow's Grace* ticking against her mooring line, Ilsa's anchor lights bleeding down through the water in three soft orange smears. She hadn't spoken to him since she'd caught up with him at the abandoned camp. She'd just stood on her deck with her arms crossed and said, "You're going down there anyway," and when he'd nodded, she'd said, "Then I'll hold your line," and that was all. Some things didn't need more words than that.

Eighty feet. His lamp swept across something pale and vertical—a snag, a dead tree standing upright in the silt like a drowned sentry—and then past it, and then the bottom rose up to meet him, gray mud and scattered stone, and out of the mud, a shape.

The wreck was smaller than he'd expected. That was the first wrongness. The fleet's records—if the fleet kept records of ships it swore never existed—described a coastal survey vessel, ninety feet of riveted steel. What lay before him was barely sixty, hull crusted white with mineral bloom, sitting canted on its port side like a drunk asleep against a wall. And the second wrongness came fast behind the first: the hole in her starboard flank wasn't storm damage. It wasn't reef damage. It was a breach torn outward from inside, metal peeled back in petals, jagged edges bent away from the hull.

Casper hung in the water beside it for a long moment, his breath loud and ragged in his own ears, and understood what he was looking at the way you understand a lie you've been telling yourself: slowly, and then all at once.

Ships sank inward. Pressure crushed them, rocks gored them, the sea pushed and the steel gave. Steel did not burst open from within unless something inside it wanted out—or unless someone outside it wanted in, badly enough to use explosive.

*They scuttled her,* he thought. *They blew her open and let the river take her.* And then, colder: *Or they blew her open to make sure nothing inside ever came back.*

His father's compass pulled at his wrist. Down the hull, along the starboard rail, toward the superstructure where the wheelhouse had collapsed into a tangle of frame and glass gone opaque with age. He swam.

* * *

Inside, the wreck kept its own weather.

Silt hung in the flooded corridors like fog that had forgotten how to move, and Casper's lamp turned it to gold smoke. He moved slow, hands ahead of him, feeling for obstructions the way his father had taught him—*the water will tell you where it doesn't want you to go, boy, if you listen with your fingers*—and the water told him plenty. A door hanging by one hinge. A chair drifting at eye level, turning gently, endlessly, like something thinking. A boot. Just a boot, upright on the deck plating, laced still, empty.

He found the first body in what had been the mess.

It had been down here long enough that the river had done its patient work, and Casper made himself look anyway, because looking was the job now, looking was the whole job. The man wore no uniform. That mattered. The expedition was supposed to be naval, Lieutenant Ashcombe's people, and this man wore civilian wool, rotted to lace, a surveyor's instrument case still slung across what remained of his chest. In one skeletal hand, clutched so tight the finger bones had locked around it, was a pencil stub. In front of him, weighted under a stone, a notebook lay open to a page the water hadn't reached, the ink ghosted but legible:

*Day 9 — V. insists the manifest is false. Says the boxes were loaded at night, off the books. Says whatever's in them, Command wanted it at the bottom before anyone counted it. We are not a survey crew. We are witnesses being disposed of.*

Casper read it twice. The second time, his hands were shaking hard enough that his lamp beam jittered across the page like something alive.

*Witnesses being disposed of.*

He thought of Ashcombe's face in the dawn light aboard the *Grace*, that careful officer's blankness, the way he'd said *questions unasked* like a man reciting terms he'd memorized and hated. He thought of the radio channel dissolving into static mid-sentence, mid-word, and the navy clerk's flat voice: *There is no expedition. There has never been an expedition.* He thought of Marek Voss's ledger, and realized, with a clarity that felt like cold water entering his lungs, that Voss and the fleet and whoever had signed the order to sink this ship were all the same animal wearing different collars. They bought men. They erased men. They kept ledgers, and every man was a line in one.

The compass tugged. Deeper. Toward the engine room, toward the lowest point of the hull, where the breach petals opened onto the black.

* * *

The strongbox was exactly where the journal page said it would be—bolted to the deck of the small compartment aft of the engines, a room barely bigger than a closet, the kind of space you'd put something in if you never intended to take it out again.

The box was open.

That stopped him the way the breach hadn't. He'd built the whole job around that box: pry it free, bring it up, trade it to Ashcombe or sell it past him, pay Voss, walk away whole. He'd rehearsed the cutting torch angle, the lift bag math, the lies he'd tell at each checkpoint. And here it sat, lid thrown back like a mouth mid-shout, interior bare, the mounting bolts sheared clean—not rusted, not corroded, *sheared*, cut from outside with tools and intent.

Beside it, half-settled into the silt where he'd slid down against the bulkhead, was the expedition leader.

Casper knew it was him because of the insignia case still hooked to his belt, waterproof, and because of the photograph tucked inside it: a woman and two children squinting into harbor sunlight, and on the back, in careful pencil, *Tell them I held the line.* The man's other hand rested on the open box's rim, palm up, fingers curled—the posture of a man who had died reaching for something that was already gone. Or guarding something that had already been taken. There was a wound high in his chest that the river had cleaned to white bone, and it was a wound Casper recognized from the fish markets of his childhood: made by something with a blade, by a human hand, at close quarters.

Not drowning. Not the sinking. One of their own, or one of the ones who came after, down here in the dark, cutting the box free of the last man who tried to keep it.

Casper hung there, breathing his own noise, and felt the terrible arithmetic settle over him. Whatever had been in the box was gone—taken upriver or taken ashore, in someone else's hands now, unsellable, unreachable. The job was dead. The debt was alive. Voss would take the boat, then the rest, and there was nothing at the bottom of the river to stop him.

He was turning for the ascent line when the light caught it: a shimmer, off to his right, where the compartment wall met the overhead. An air pocket. Trapped gas in the inverted corner of the hull, silvered and trembling, the kind of lens divers know the way sailors know weather.

And in front of it, pressed into the corner like a thing trying to become part of the ship, a man.

He was alive. That was the horror of it—alive, after however many days, skin gray as the silt, lips split, eyes enormous in a face gone hollow, clutching a canvas document case to his chest with both arms the way a drowning man clutches driftwood. When Casper's lamp hit him, the man flinched so violently he struck his head on the hull, and then he began to laugh—a thin, cracked, bubbling sound, half sob, the laughter of someone who has been alone with the dead too long and has started keeping their hours.

"You're not one of them," the man rasped. His voice was ruined, barely a thread. "You're not. They wear lamps too. They came back with lamps." His grip tightened on the case until his knuckles stood out white through the grime. "I heard them cutting. I heard them take it. I stayed quiet. I stayed *quiet*, and they left, and I've been—" He broke off, shuddering, and looked at Casper with eyes that had seen the bottom of everything. "Ashcombe sent you? Did Ashcombe send you?"

Casper pulled his regulator free, letting his voice come low and strange in the trapped air. "Who's asking?"

"Corporal Denn. R. Denn." The man fumbled the case open with shaking hands and thrust it toward the lamplight, and Casper saw paper inside—dozens of pages, orders, manifests, a signal log, sealed with stamps Casper recognized and wished he didn't. "It wasn't cargo," Denn whispered. "You understand? It was never cargo. The boxes held the proof. Proof of what Command did at Halvern Reach last spring—the convoy, the convoy they wrote off as lost to weather. They sank *this* ship to sink the proof, and us with it, and the box they sent you for—" His laugh came again, worse than before. "The box is the decoy. There were two. The real one's been in my hands for eleven days."

Above Casper, far away, faint through eighty feet of black water, he heard it: an engine. Then another. Boats moving on the river above, running heavy, running fast, coming downriver toward the wreck.

Denn heard it too. He went rigid, and his whisper came out steady for the first time, steady as a man who has finally run out of anything left to lose.

"That'll be them. So dive me up, diver. Dive me up fast, or we both learn how long a man can stay quiet."
