## 8. Ilsa's Wake

The river gave nothing back.

Ilsa Reyes had learned that about water long before she learned it about men: you could pour yourself into it for years and it would keep your effort the way a miser keeps coins—counted, hoarded, never returned. She stood at the wheel of the *Marrow*, running her own race upriver while the salvage fleet's lights strung out behind her like a rosary of lies, and she thought about all the years she'd poured into Casper Sterling and how he'd never once noticed the tide of it.

Three days since the anonymous tip had lit the harbor on fire. Three days since Voss's men had come through the fleet like cholera through a crowded ship, waving contracts and doubling bounties, and every captain with a hull and a hunger had signed on to hunt one man and one box. Ilsa had signed too. Her name was on the bounty ledger in ink she hadn't let dry yet in her mind, because signing was the only way to be allowed upriver at all—and being upriver meant being close enough to choose.

She hadn't chosen yet. That was the honest shape of it. Every hour she ran the *Marrow* harder than the fleet expected, burning fuel she couldn't afford, telling herself she was racing them when what she was really doing was outrunning her own decision.

The river narrowed here. The banks leaned in, black walls of cypress hung with mist that moved against the wind, which was wrong—mist should follow wind, the way debt follows a diver, the way trouble follows Sterling—but everything past the second checkpoint had been wrong. The checkpoints were empty. Not abandoned; emptied. Coffee still warm in a tin cup on the last one, a radio hissing on its bracket, a chair turned as if someone had stood up mid-sentence and simply walked into the trees. She'd put a boarding party's worth of dread into that image and carried it forty miles without setting it down.

Behind her, the fleet's leading boats were an hour back, maybe less. Ahead, the channel bent around a drowned timber camp, and there—

She saw it before her mind agreed to see it.

A boat adrift. Small, patched, riding low and crooked in the current, turning slowly with no hand on her helm. Ilsa knew that boat the way you know a scar. The *Pelican*. Casper's father had built her from river oak and stubbornness, and Casper had kept her alive with wire and prayer ever since, and Ilsa had spent one whole summer aboard her learning what it felt like to be happy in a way that frightened her.

"Easy," she said—to the wheel, to herself, to the girl she'd been. "Easy now."

She brought the *Marrow* alongside slow, fenders out, and looked down onto the *Pelican*'s deck. Empty. A coil of line dragged overboard and trailing. A dive tank lying on its side. And on the thwart, placed deliberately, square in the middle where no wave would take it: a compass.

His father's compass. Dead for years—Ilsa had held it once, laughed gently at him for keeping a broken thing, and watched him take it back with a look that told her she'd misunderstood something fundamental about him. It lay face-up now, needle swinging, and even from the rail of the *Marrow* she could see it wasn't pointing north. It was pointing downstream. Toward the wreck. Toward whatever waited below cold black water.

He'd left it for her. Or left it for anyone. But she knew, with the certainty that had ruined her and remade her twice, that it was for her. Casper Sterling didn't leave messages. He left objects, and trusted you to read them.

Ilsa stood at the rail a long moment, the mist beading on her neck, the fleet's engines a distant growl growing less distant by the minute.

Then she went below, took her own contract off the chart table—the paper with her signature, the paper worth more money than she'd seen in five years—and fed it into the stove.

It burned fast. Bounty papers always did. They were printed on hope, and hope has a low flashpoint.

* * *

She found the inlet half a mile on, where the compass needle pointed hardest—a slot in the cypress wall barely wide enough for the *Marrow*, choked with duckweed gone gray and thick as felt. She went in on the tender alone, oars muffled with rags, because whatever had emptied those checkpoints might still be hungry, and she wasn't going to feed it a crew.

The drag marks started at the waterline.

She saw them in the gray light and made herself look properly, the way her mother had taught her to gut fish—quickly, completely, without flinching. Marks in the mud, parallel, deep at the heel and shallow at the toe. Two sets. One set led down into the water and did not come out. The other set came out of the water, staggered, uneven, the prints of someone who had crawled.

Crawled toward the camp.

The expedition's camp sat on the hummock above the bank, and it was worse than the checkpoints. Torn tents. Equipment scattered with a violence that had no theft in it—they'd taken nothing, which meant they hadn't been robbed, which meant they'd been *taken*. A field desk overturned, papers soaked to pulp. And pinned under a stone, one page that the rain hadn't reached, written in a tight naval hand:

*Strongbox recovered. Contents confirm. If we do not surface, the truth is in the box and the box is in the wreck and God forgive us for what we were ordered to find.*

Ilsa read it three times. On the third reading her hands stopped shaking, because shaking was useless, and usefulness was the only religion she'd ever kept.

She followed the crawl marks to their end: a hollow beneath a fallen trunk, screened with cut boughs laid by a careful hand. Someone had hidden here. Someone had been hiding here recently enough that the cut stems still wept sap.

"Casper," she said quietly, to the dark under the boughs. "It's Ilsa."

Nothing. Then, so faint she almost missed it, a sound like breath trying to decide whether to be words.

"I burned my contract," she said. "There's a fleet an hour behind me and I just made myself worthless to them. So either you come out, or you let me stand here looking stupid, and I have never once in my life forgiven anyone who made me look stupid."

The boughs shifted.

He came out sideways, low, one arm braced, and he was carrying another man—half-carrying, half-dragging, a skeletal figure wrapped in expedition canvas whose eyes were open and fixed on nothing. Casper looked like he'd been dredged up himself: gray-lipped, hands torn, a cut over his brow crusted black. He stared at her as if she were a hallucination he intended to be polite to until she faded.

"You're supposed to be hunting me," he rasped.

"I am." She stepped forward and took the survivor's weight off him without asking. "Badly."

For a moment neither of them moved, and the mist moved for them, sliding between their shoulders like something old and patient finally satisfied. Then Casper made a sound—not quite a laugh, more the ghost a laugh leaves behind—and his knees decided the conversation was over. She caught him with her free shoulder and lowered them both down among the roots.

"The box," he managed. "It was open. Empty. He—" a nod at the man in her arms— "Vasquez. He had the documents. Navy scuttled their own ship, Ils. Sank it with their own people aboard to bury what was in the hold. He's the only one left. He's been living in an air pocket in the wreck for six days."

Six days. In black water, in the dark, with the dead. Ilsa looked down at Vasquez's ruined face and understood why his eyes had stopped reporting to the world.

"He won't let go of the papers," Casper said. "Won't eat unless they're touching him. I got him out because—" His voice caught, and she heard what was underneath the catch: not fear. Grief. "Because his lieutenant died getting him to the pocket. Because somebody has to carry this the rest of the way, and it isn't going to be me deciding alone anymore."

"It was always going to be you deciding alone," Ilsa said. "That's your whole tragedy, Sterling. You think love is a thing you do in private."

He looked at her then—really looked, the first time in eleven years—and the river seemed to go quiet around them, holding its breath along with everything else.

"You burned it," he said. "Your contract."

"Every word of it." She shifted Vasquez's weight. "Which means the fleet will figure out where I went, and when they do, Voss's men will come down this inlet with guns and a grudge, and I'll have exactly one boat, one ex-lover, one madman, and a set of papers worth killing for. So." She stood, and offered him her hand, palm up, the same offer she'd made on the *Pelican*'s deck a decade ago when he'd chosen the sea instead of her. "Are you coming, or are you going to make me look stupid twice in one day?"

Casper took her hand. His grip was cold and cracked and it closed around hers like an answer to a question he'd been asking himself for years.

They loaded Vasquez into the tender as the light failed. Behind them, far down the channel, the fleet's searchlights began to sweep the bends—one, two, three beams walking the water like fingers feeling for a pulse.

Ilsa rowed. Casper sat with the survivor's head in his lap and the documents pressed flat against the man's chest, keeping them warm, keeping them real.

"There's one honest officer left," Casper said quietly, as the inlet mouth opened onto the main channel and the last of the day drained west. "Lieutenant Hale. He hired me. He doesn't know what happened up here. If the truth surfaces any other way, the navy buries it again—deeper this time, with us inside."

"So we get it to Hale."

"At the river mouth. At the sixth tide." He looked down at Vasquez, then at her, and there was something in his face she'd only seen once before—on the deck of the *Pelican*, the summer the world was still possible. "Ilsa. When we give these away—and we're going to give them away—I'll have nothing. No box, no bounty, no debt paid. Nothing."

Ilsa shipped the oars for one stroke, letting the current take them, and smiled at him across the dark water.

"Casper," she said. "You've got a compass that points at true things and a woman who burns contracts. You've never had more."

Ahead, at the edge of the dark, the river mouth waited—and riding at anchor across it, lanterns lit in a pattern she knew too well, the fleet had arrived first.
