## 9. The Sixth Tide

The river mouth at slack water was a held breath.

Casper had known it all his life—the way the current paused there between tides, neither salt nor fresh, a gray-green stillness where the water seemed to listen. His father used to say the mouth was where the sea took its toll and the river paid its debts, and you never wanted to be caught in the exchange. Now he sat in the wheelhouse of the *Magpie* with the engine ticking as it cooled, watching three boats close the gap behind him like fingers folding into a fist, and understood that he had run out of river.

Voss's men came first. Two boats, low and black, running without lights, their wakes long white scars on the darkening water. Behind them, farther out where the harbor channel widened toward the fleet anchorage, the gray shapes of naval pickets had appeared in the last hour—two of them, then three, their searchlights sweeping the shallows in slow, patient arcs. The fleet had stopped pretending. Whatever orders had once said *no expedition exists* had been replaced by something older and simpler: nothing leaves this river.

Ilsa's boat was not among them. He had looked for her wake all the way down from the wreck, every mile of black water, and found only his own reflection and the compass on the dash, needle steady now, pointing not north but seaward, pointing at the one honest thing left in this estuary: Lieutenant Aldous Wren, standing alone on the stone jetty below the signal tower, holding a lantern he did not raise.

*Come in alone,* the radio had said, an hour ago, a voice Casper hadn't heard since the night the expedition went silent. *Come in alone or don't come in.*

Casper had come alone. He'd left the survivor—Maren Oduya, half-starved, half-mad, clutching her documents like a mother clutching a child—in the chart locker of a derelict shrimper upriver, wedged under nets no salvage crew would bother cutting. He'd given her the flare gun and taught her the signal: two red stars if anyone came for her, one green star if she saw him coming back. She had watched him go with eyes that had seen the navy sink its own ship and could not yet stop seeing it.

Now the tide was turning. He could feel it under the hull, the faint lean of the water beginning to run seaward, the sixth tide since Voss's deadline, the last one before the fleet's cordon tightened into something no boat could slip. On the jetty, Wren's lantern stayed down. Behind Casper, the first of Voss's boats put its bow wave up and began to eat the distance.

He stepped out onto the deck.

The wind off the sea carried salt and diesel and the cold iron smell of rain that hadn't fallen yet. Gulls wheeled over the breakwater, screaming their idiot joy, indifferent to ledgers and courts-martial and men about to lose everything they'd carried across six tides. Casper stood at the rail with the strongbox lid—he'd kept it, why he couldn't say, maybe because an empty box is still a box—and watched Wren come down the jetty steps to the waterline, boots ringing on wet stone.

"Casper Sterling." Wren's voice carried flat across the still water. "You were told to come in alone."

"I am alone."

"Your friends aren't." Wren nodded past him, at the black boats. "Voss's people. And behind them—" he didn't finish it. He didn't have to. Everyone in this estuary knew what waited behind everything now.

"They can hear me fine," Casper called back. "Good. Let them."

The lead boat was fifty yards out and closing. Casper raised his voice, pitched it the way his father had taught him to pitch it across a storm deck, so it reached the ears it needed to reach and no others were fooled.

"Lieutenant! You want what's in my hold?"

Wren went very still. "I want the truth, Sterling. That's all I've wanted since the *Providence* went down with forty souls aboard and my government wrote them off the books."

"The *Providence*," Casper repeated, loud enough for the boats, for the pickets, for the listening radios he knew were tuned to this channel. "That's the name. That's the ship your fleet says doesn't exist. I've seen her keel, Lieutenant. Forty fathoms down in cold black water, and I've seen what your expedition found beside her—orders, signed and sealed, proving the navy scuttled her own vessel to bury what she carried. And I've got those papers in my hands right now."

The gulls screamed. The tide leaned harder, dragging at the *Magpie*'s hull, pulling her seaward against her anchor line. In the wheelhouse behind him, the compass needle trembled and held.

"You're bluffing," said a new voice, and Marek Voss stepped out of the wheelhouse of the lead boat, and even at fifty yards Casper could see the ledger-man's smile, thin as a knife laid flat. "Sterling. You magnificent fool. You think I came all this way for papers? Papers are worth what someone will pay. I came for *you*. Your debt doesn't care what you found down there. Your debt comes due tonight, with interest, and interest means the boat, the gear, the compass your dead father left you—everything. Hand it all over and maybe I let you swim."

"There's just one problem with that, Marek." Casper kept his eyes on Wren. "The papers aren't in my hold. They're not on this boat. They're somewhere you'll never find them, and they stay there unless one thing happens."

"And what's that?" Voss called, amused, closing.

"A public accounting." Casper's voice cracked out over the water like a whip. "Not a sealed file. Not a quiet court-martial where the record gets burned and the families get a form letter. A public one. Names read aloud. The *Providence* named. The forty souls named. Every newspaper on the coast, every harbor office, every tavern wall. You do that, Lieutenant—*you*, personally, on the record, tonight—and by morning the papers are in your hands and Marek Voss's leverage dies with the story, because you can't blackmail a man whose debt has been forgiven by daylight."

Silence, except the gulls and the turning tide.

"You're giving it away," Voss said at last, and for the first time the amusement was gone. "Forty years of my life building what I built, and you're going to hand it to a junior officer with a lantern."

"I'm not handing it to him," Casper said. "I'm handing it to the truth. There's a difference. You wouldn't know it—you've spent your whole life confusing the two."

Wren had not moved. The lantern light caught his face, young and exhausted and carrying something heavier than rank. When he spoke, his voice did not carry theatrically; it carried honestly, which was worse and better.

"Sterling. If I do this—if I stand on that jetty and read those names into the log with witnesses, knowing what it costs me, knowing they'll strip my commission before the ink dries—how do I know you won't simply sell the papers to Voss tomorrow anyway? How do I know this isn't a second auction?"

Casper looked at him for a long moment. Then he reached into his coat and drew out the one page he had kept back from Maren's bundle—not the orders, not the signatures, but the expedition leader's final journal leaf, the one naming the strongbox, the one written in a hand that had known it was writing from inside a grave. He held it up.

"Because I'm going to give you this now," he said, "as surety. And because six tides ago I'd have sold my father's compass to pay a debt, and I've learned the difference between what a man owes and what a man owes *for*." He hurled the page in a flat arc across the water. It landed on the jetty stones at Wren's feet, paper pale as a moth. "Read it. Read it aloud. Right now, with Voss's men listening and your pickets' radios open. Make them all witnesses whether they want to be or not."

Voss's boat was twenty yards out now. Casper saw the man's hand move, saw the signal passed, saw the second boat swing wide to cut off the *Magpie*'s seaward escape—and understood, with terrible calm, that it didn't matter anymore. There was no escape to cut off. Escape had never been the plan. The plan was the opposite of escape: it was standing still while the truth crossed the water faster than any boat.

Wren picked up the page. He read it once, silently, and Casper watched the man's face change the way a face changes when it stops being possible to pretend. Then Lieutenant Aldous Wren straightened, turned to face the jetty, the boats, the pickets with their searchlights frozen mid-sweep, and began to read aloud.

"*Expedition log, final entry. We have located the Providence. She was sunk by our own side. The orders are in the strongbox. If we do not surface, let the record show—*"

He read the names. All of them. Commander Hale. Engineer Sotiris. Maren Oduya, listed among the lost, and Casper closed his eyes briefly at that, promising her he'd correct it. Forty names into the wind, into the radios, into the open ears of every man on every boat in the river mouth, while the tide ran seaward and the gulls went mad overhead and Marek Voss stood on his own deck discovering, visibly, inch by inch, that his ledger had just been balanced by someone else's hand.

When it was done, Wren folded the page and put it inside his coat, over his heart, and looked at Casper across the water.

"The location," he said quietly. "As agreed."

"Upriver," Casper said. "Derelict shrimper, third bend above the old cannery. Chart locker. There's a woman with it who deserves to be carried home, not arrested. See to both, Lieutenant, or the surety you hold is worthless."

Behind him, Voss's boat slowed, drifted, turned. There was nothing left to take. A man cannot repossess what has been given to the light. Casper watched the black boats fall away upriver, chasing a story that no longer belonged to them, and felt something in his chest unclench that had been clenched since the day he'd signed Voss's ledger—a debt not forgiven but dissolved, the way salt dissolves, the way names dissolve when they are finally spoken.

The tide was running full now. The *Magpie* pulled at her line, wanting the sea.

On the jetty, Wren raised his lantern at last—one slow arc, acknowledgment, farewell, warning, all of it—and Casper weighed anchor and let the sixth tide take him out through the river mouth, past the pickets that did not stop him, past the breakwater where the gulls wheeled, out into the open dark where the compass on the dash swung gently, gently, and settled pointing home.

It was only when the harbor lights sank below the horizon that he remembered the green flare he owed a woman in a chart locker—and turned the wheel.

* * *

The derelict shrimper rose out of the fog on the third bend, nets hanging like drowned curtains, and from her deck a single red star climbed into the sky, bloomed, and fell.

Someone else had found her first.
