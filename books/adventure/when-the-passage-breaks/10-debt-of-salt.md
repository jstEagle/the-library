## 10. Debt of Salt

The tide came in soft that morning, the way it did in the weeks after everything, as if the river itself had decided to be gentle with him for a while.

Casper knelt on the deck of the *Maren's Wake* with a scraper in one hand and a bucket of salt-crusted paint chips at his knee, working his way along the gunwale plank by plank. The morning sun came off the water in long copper sheets. Gulls argued over something dead upcurrent. His hands—split at three knuckles, stained black in the creases where no soap would ever reach—moved with the slow patience of a man who had finally run out of deadlines.

There was no deadline now. That was the strange part. He kept reaching for it the way a tongue reaches for a missing tooth: the sixth tide, Voss's ledger, the fleet's guns bearing down the channel. All of it gone, dissolved like sugar in warm water, and what remained was just this—a boat, a morning, a job with no one holding a knife to the back of it.

He'd sold nothing. That was the arithmetic he turned over in his head while he worked, the sum he could not stop checking even though it never changed. The strongbox's contents—the documents, the photographs, the expedition leader's own hand naming the order that sent the *Vigilant* to the bottom with her own crew's knowledge—had gone to Lieutenant Ashford, or rather through him, into the hands of men who wore their honesty like ill-fitting coats but wore it nonetheless. Casper had walked out of that meeting at the river mouth with an empty strongbox under one arm and a debt that no longer existed and not one coin to show for either.

Voss's leverage had collapsed the same week. When the truth surfaced—and it surfaced everywhere at once, ink and radio and the shouted questions of men who'd lost brothers on the *Vigilant*—the harbor broker's careful web of paper became worthless overnight. A debt held over a man is only as good as the man's fear, and fear needs darkness. The inquiry burned the darkness off the harbor like fog off morning water. Voss himself had left town on a coastal steamer before the subpoenas finished arriving, and Casper had stood on the quay watching the smoke fade and felt—not triumph. Just the absence of weight. Like setting down a load so heavy you forget your own spine has a curve to it.

He set down the scraper and flexed his fingers, looking down the length of his boat.

She was ugly with repair. New planking pale as bone against the old wood weathered gray, caulking lines still weeping tar, the transom rebuilt where the fleet's wake had nearly torn it loose in the last mad run downriver. He'd traded his diving gear for the lumber. Every tool worth pawning was pawned. He owned, at current count, a boat, a change of clothes, a dented kettle, and his father's compass—which lay on the thwart beside him now, brass case scuffed to bare metal, glass clouded at the edges.

It pointed north. It had pointed north for eleven days.

* * *

Ilsa arrived at midday, as she always did, announced by the slap of her skiff's hull against the pilings and the particular rhythm of her boots on the dock—one heavy, one lighter, from the old break in the *Corvus*'s deck she'd never gotten around to fixing because fixing it would mean admitting the *Corvus* was getting old too.

"You missed the harbormaster," she said, dropping onto the thwart across from him without asking, which was how she'd always sat on his boats, going back to a time when there'd been no need to ask about anything. "And the harbormaster missed me, which is rarer."

"Did you get it?"

"I got it." She let the silence sit a beat, because Ilsa Reyes had never in her life given good news without making you wait for it. "Coastal charter. Legitimate. Survey work for the lighthouse authority, mail runs when they're shorthanded, salvage only by contract and only aboveboard." She pulled the folded papers from inside her jacket and slapped them on the thwart between them. "They wanted a diver with a clean record. I told them yours was clean as of six weeks ago and dirty in all the interesting ways before that, and that the interesting ways were public knowledge now and therefore practically references."

Casper picked up the papers. His name was printed there—his actual name, in official type, next to hers. *Sterling & Reyes, Coastal Charter.* He read it twice. Something in his chest went tight and warm and untrustworthy.

"They'll pay us," he said.

"They'll pay us money. Actual coin. You remember coin."

"I've heard of it."

She laughed, and the sound carried out over the water, and a gull somewhere took offense and moved off down the channel. She looked older than she had in the spring—or no, he thought, she looked the same and he was finally seeing her straight, without the war on, without the fleet between them, without the bounty notice he knew she'd been offered and knew she'd refused. She'd burned her own contract to keep him out of the fleet's hands. He'd learned that from Ashford afterward, secondhand, the way you learn the things people do for you when they don't want thanks.

"The survivor signed on yet?" Casper asked.

"Tomas? He's coming Thursday. Says he wants water under him that isn't black." Ilsa's voice gentled on the name, the way everyone's did now. The man they'd pulled from the air pocket beneath the river had spent two weeks in the naval hospital learning to sleep with the lights on, and then another two learning to sleep at all. He'd asked Casper once, in a voice like paper, why Casper hadn't sold the documents—what they'd have fetched, what it would have meant. Casper hadn't had an answer that wasn't embarrassing. Something about his father. Something about the compass. Something about how some things you carry and some things carry you, and you don't get to choose which until the water's already over your head.

"He'll do," Casper said. "Better than do."

"He asks about you. Did you know that?"

"I figured."

"He doesn't ask about anyone else." She said it lightly, but her eyes stayed on him, and Casper found the horizon suddenly very interesting.

* * *

That evening, with the light going amber and long across the harbor, Casper took the compass out of its oilcloth and set it on the thwart and watched it.

His father's compass. Dead for years—needle stuck, sluggish, pointing wherever the last knock had left it, a relic Casper had kept out of stubbornness rather than sentiment. Then, in the weeks before everything, it had begun to move. Swinging toward the river mouth. Steady, impossible, patient as a summons. He'd tested it against every instrument he owned and it disagreed with all of them, faithfully, and he had followed it upriver into the dark because there was nothing else in his life that seemed to know where it was going.

Now it pointed north. True north, checked twice against the harbor beacon and once against the stars coming out over the headland. It simply pointed, the way a compass is supposed to point, dull and honest and done.

He understood it the way you understand a dream after waking—not logically, but completely. The compass had been his father's, and his father had died owing money to men like Voss, and the needle had dragged Casper toward the thing that would either ruin him or free him, and it had not cared which. It had only cared that he go. And now the going was over. The debt was paid—not in coin, in salt, in the cold black water of the true wreck, in the look on Tomas's face when Casper put the documents in his ruined hands and said, *these are yours, they were always yours.* Survival had cost him everything he carried. The gear, the coin, the secrecy, the shame, the whole rigged ledger of his life. And it had left him this: a boat with new planking, a charter with his own name on it, a woman on his dock who had chosen him over a bounty without hesitating long enough to make it dramatic, and a small brass compass that finally, finally rested.

"You're talking to that thing again," Ilsa said. She'd come back aboard without him hearing—she still did that, still moved over water and wood like a rumor—and she stood at the rail looking down at the compass with an expression caught between affection and unease.

"It stopped."

"Stopped what?"

"Pulling." He turned it so she could see the needle, steady as a nail. "It used to point at the river mouth. Toward the wreck. Toward—" He shrugged. "Toward whatever my father wanted me to find, maybe. Or whatever I needed to. Now it points north and stays there."

"And that's good?"

"That's good." He closed the lid. The click of the brass clasp sounded, absurdly, like a door shutting gently on a room he'd lived in a long time. "A compass that points at trouble is useful exactly once. After that you want one that just tells you where you are."

Ilsa sat down beside him, close enough that her shoulder touched his, and neither of them moved away from it, and the harbor lights came on one by one down the quay like a slow decision being made.

"First charter's in ten days," she said. "Lighthouse survey, south coast. Boring as church."

"Good. I've had enough interesting."

"We'll need a third pair of hands eventually. Someone who can read charts."

"Hire Tomas."

"I meant someone who can also cook."

Casper laughed—really laughed, the first time since the river mouth, and it startled a gull off the pilings and surprised even himself, the sound coming up rough from somewhere long unused. Below them the tide turned, unhurried, faithful, going out and knowing it would come back. That was the whole secret of it, he thought. The water didn't hold grudges. It took what the river brought down and it carried it to the sea, and then it came back clean.

He opened the compass one more time before stowing it. North. Steady. Staying.

Out past the breakwater, far down the darkening channel, a ship's lantern swung into view—running lights he didn't recognize, a hull riding low and heavy, moving against the traffic pattern toward the harbor mouth. Ilsa saw it too. Her shoulder stiffened almost imperceptibly against his.

"Charter boat?" she asked.

"No charter rides that deep," Casper said quietly, watching the lantern come on. "Nobody's supposed to be running cargo into this harbor after dark. Not anymore."

The light grew closer, steady and silent, and the compass in his hand—closed, stowed, done—seemed for one strange moment to grow warm against his palm.
