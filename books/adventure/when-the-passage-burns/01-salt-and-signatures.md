## 1. Salt and Signatures

The saltflats at dawn looked like the sky had fallen and forgotten how to rise.

Odile Ishikawa crouched at the edge of the white and read the ground the way other men read letters—slowly, with his lips moving. The crust out here was thin as communion wafer, and everything that crossed it signed its name. A lizard left a calligraphic squiggle. A wind-scoured tumbleweed wrote a long confession ending in a ditch. And somewhere ahead, maybe two hours gone now, maybe three, a thief had written her name in bootprints small enough to belong to a woman or a light man running heavy.

She'd been clever about it. He gave her that much. She had walked the last mile of hardpan in the shadow of a rock ridge where the salt held no print, and she had dragged a thornbush behind her for fifty yards to feather her trail, and she had stepped in the tracks of a caravan of salt-gatherers heading south before cutting west where no one but a madman would go, because west was nothing. West was the drowned country's doorstep, the flats that stretched toward the old Vessian basin like a tongue of bone.

But cleverness leaves signatures too. The thornbush she'd dragged had left a groove in the crust, faint as a scar on a knuckle, and it pointed west. The salt-gatherers' caravan had turned south at the marker cairn; her prints had turned south with them and then simply stopped, which meant she hadn't stopped at all—she'd stepped off the line of march onto bare stone where the salt couldn't hold her, and stone remembers nothing except heat.

Odile straightened, worked the stiffness out of his knees, and looked west into the glare.

He'd been tracking her since the river town of Callow's Ferry, three days back, when she'd lifted a chest off a Combine barge moored under lantern-light and walked away with forty pounds of somebody else's secrets balanced on one hip like it was a sleeping child. He hadn't seen the theft itself. He'd seen the aftermath: the barge master screaming, the Combine guards fumbling their lanterns, and a figure in a dust-colored poncho going over the levee rail with the unhurried ease of someone crossing a street. By the time Odile reached the rail, there was only the river below, black and indifferent, carrying its cargo of moonlight toward towns that never asked where the water came from.

Everyone else had run downstream along the bank, shouting. Odile had stood still and thought: *if I were stealing from the Combine, would I run toward their checkpoints, or away from every road they own?*

West, then. Into the salt.

* * *

He moved through the morning the way he did everything—alone, economically, without ceremony. His kit weighed what it weighed because he had learned, over eleven years of not staying anywhere, exactly how little a man needs to keep moving: water, a strip of dried goat, a fire-bow, a knife his mother had given him when he still answered to another name, and a coil of rope he'd used twice and carried anyway, the way you carry a promise you're not sure you can keep.

The debt was why he moved. That was the honest version, and he told himself the honest version on mornings like this one, when the loneliness got loud enough to hear over the wind. Eleven years ago he had been someone else—a boy with a different name in a downriver town called Serrit's Landing, a town that had trusted him, and he had done a thing with a ledger and a bribe and a locked granary door that he did not let himself remember in full sentences. People had gone hungry because of him. One man had died, an old ferryman who'd stood up to the grain-hoarders and gotten a knife for his trouble, and everyone in Serrit's Landing knew whose unlocked door had let the hoarders' men in.

So he'd left in the night, and he'd left his name behind like a coat that no longer fit, and he'd picked up a new trade on the way out: finding things. Runaways. Stolen goods. Sometimes people who didn't want to be found, who paid him to stay found-lost. It was work that suited a man with no fixed address and no face anyone remembered twice. He was good at it. He was, if he was honest, better than good—he could read a trail the way priests read scripture, and out here on the flats, where the whole world was a page, he felt the competence settle over him like a second skin.

And underneath the skin, always, the same quiet arithmetic: *one more job, one more payment, and the debt gets smaller.* Except the debt wasn't money. You couldn't pay a dead ferryman's family in coin, because they didn't want coin—they wanted the man they'd trusted back, and that man had drowned himself in a new name somewhere between Serrit's Landing and here, and all that was left was Odile Ishikawa, who kept moving because stopping meant standing still long enough to be recognized, and being recognized meant being known, and being known meant the ledger of Serrit's Landing opening again in front of witnesses.

Moving was easier. Moving was a kind of penance with a rhythm to it. Left foot, right foot, horizon, horizon.

The sun climbed and the salt threw its light up at him as well as down, so that he walked inside a brightness with no edges. Sweat traced lines through the dust on his face. He passed the carcass of a burro half-sunk in the crust, mummified to leather, its ribs arched like the frame of a boat that had tried to sail the sky. He passed a cairn some traveler had raised generations ago, its stones fused by salt into a single pale fist. He passed nothing else, and the nothing went on for miles, humming.

That was the thing about the flats nobody warned you about: the silence wasn't empty. It was full. Full of every sound you'd ever failed to say out loud. Out here, with no wind and no birds, Odile could hear his own heartbeat and, beneath it, older and quieter, the voice of his mother telling stories she shouldn't have told—the ones about Vessia, the drowned nation, the country that had owned the river's headwaters until the maps were redrawn and the name erased. *The water remembers*, she used to say, in the low voice she saved for forbidden things, *even when the ink forgets.* He'd been six. He'd thought she meant it the way grandmothers mean weather talk.

He was thirty-one now, and he still didn't know what she'd meant, but the words came back to him out here, on the doorstep of the drowned country, and he wondered—not for the first time—whether he was tracking a thief or following a story home.

* * *

Near midday he found where she'd slept.

A shallow scrape beneath the overhang of a salt-cored bluff, sheltered from the wind, and in it the cold ash of a very small fire—smaller than caution required, which told him she'd been short on fuel, or short on confidence, or both. Beside the ash, pressed flat into the crust where she'd sat: the outline of a chest. Forty pounds of wood and iron banding, resting in one place long enough to leave its signature in the salt.

He crouched over the impression and felt something turn over in his chest, slow and cold, like a fish in deep water.

Because the chest had been heavy enough to leave a print, and she had carried it alone across three days of saltflat, and she had not abandoned it even when abandoning it would have doubled her speed. Whatever was inside mattered more than her life. And people who stole things that mattered more than their lives were either desperate or dangerous, and in Odile's experience the desperate ones were worse, because desperation didn't negotiate.

There was something else in the ash. He almost missed it—a scrap of paper, singed at one corner, dropped or discarded. He smoothed it against his knee. It was a bill of lading, Combine-issue, the kind stamped on every barge manifest between Callow's Ferry and the sea. Most of it was burned away, but one line survived, printed in the Combine's tidy bureaucratic hand:

*CONCESSION PERMITS — RIVER SOURCE — VALID THRU SPRING EQUINOX —*

Below it, a fragment of a seal: a circle broken by a wave.

Odile sat back on his heels and stared at it for a long time.

He knew that seal. Not from manifests—from his mother's stories, drawn in charcoal on the underside of a flour sack while the wind screamed outside and the lamps burned low. A circle broken by a wave. The mark of Vessia, the nation that wasn't, the country the Combine had swallowed and then scrubbed from every map, every ledger, every gravestone. His mother had said the seal was outlawed on pain of the lash, and she'd drawn it anyway, because she said forgetting was a slower kind of dying and she refused to help it along.

And now here was the seal's ghost, half-burned, in the ashes of a thief's fire, attached to words that made no sense and all the sense in the world: *river source*. The source was Combine-locked, Combine-diverted, Combine-myth. Every child in the desert learned early that you didn't ask where the water came from, the way you didn't ask a rich man where his teeth came from. But a permit—a *concession permit*—was a legal instrument. A claim. Paper that said, in the language the Combine itself had written, *this water may belong to whoever holds this.*

His mouth had gone dry. He drank, deliberately, making himself move through the motions, and folded the scrap into his breast pocket next to his heart, which was beating faster than the heat alone explained.

*Run, little thief,* he thought, looking west. *I'm coming.*

* * *

The afternoon taught him more about her than three days of prints had.

She was careful in a way that spoke of training, not temperament. At the dry wash where the flats finally broke into cracked clay and stone, she had paused—there was a sitting-print, brief, facing back the way she'd come—and then she'd entered the wash at a bend, walking on the stone lip rather than the sand floor, leaving the floor untouched. A soldier's habit. Or a spy's. Combine-trained, then, or trained by whatever the Combine trained against.

The wash ran west-northwest, a vein of shade winding down toward the old basin, its walls rising from knee-height to shoulder-height as the land fell. Odile followed it with his weight on the stones, his breathing timed to his steps, his eyes moving in the pattern he'd worn smooth over eleven years: ground, walls, skyline, ground. The light went long and gold and then copper, and the shadows in the wash pooled like spilled ink, and the temperature began its evening slide from unbearable toward merely cruel.

Twice he found fresh sign that stopped him cold.

The first was a bootprint—not hers—larger, deeper, pressed into the wash's sandy floor within the last day. Someone else had come through here recently, moving fast, and had not bothered to hide it, which meant they either didn't know they were followed or didn't care. Riders, probably. The Combine didn't send lone walkers after stolen property worth killing for.

The second was stranger: a cairn of three stones, freshly stacked, in the middle of nowhere, beside a scratch in the rock shaped like a bird's footprint. A signal. A mark on a route only certain eyes would read. The thief wasn't running blind. She was running toward something—or someone—and the something had left her breadcrumbs.

Odile stood in the failing light with his hand on the cairn's top stone and felt the shape of the job change under him, the way a trail changes when prey becomes predator. Three days ago this had been simple: recover the chest, collect the fee, move on. Now there were Combine riders in the wash behind him, a signal network ahead of him, and a stolen chest full of permits that could legally open the one place in the world the Combine had sealed shut—and his mother's voice saying *the water remembers* in his ear like a hand on his shoulder.

He should turn around. That was the rational move. Take the evidence to the barge master, take the fee, take the road east, find the next job, keep the ledger shrinking, keep the name buried. The smart play. The survival play. He'd made it a hundred times. It was practically his signature too.

Instead he took the top stone off the cairn, memorized the bird-track scratch, and put the stone back exactly as it had been, so the signal would still read true to whoever came next—and so they wouldn't know a fourth set of eyes had read it.

Then he went on, into the deepening blue of the wash, alone, the way he was always alone, the competence wrapped tight around the loneliness like wire around a core of glass.

* * *

Dusk came up out of the ground as much as out of the sky, the flats glowing a last bruised violet before the dark took them. Odile topped a low rise where the wash bent north, and there, a quarter mile on, he saw it: the ruins of an old cistern squatting in the wash's elbow, a dome of cracked Vessian-era masonry, older than any map that admitted it existed. Water-stains still wept down its flanks, fossil tears from a century of wetter years. A structure like that didn't get built by accident. It got built where water had been, or where water was meant to be kept.

And against the cistern's eastern wall, small in the last light, a figure was crouched over something rectangular, working at it with a pry bar.

Forty pounds of wood and iron. Three days of salt. And she'd stopped, in the open, at dusk, to fight the lock—because she couldn't open it, and whatever was inside mattered more than the risk of stopping.

Odile eased back below the lip of the rise. His heart was doing something complicated, part hunter's calm and part something he refused to name, because naming it would make it hope, and hope was a luxury for men with addresses.

He checked the knife at his belt, the rope on his shoulder, the water at his hip. He thought, briefly, absurdly, of his mother, and of a boy with another name who had once believed that lost things wanted to be found.

Then he went down into the wash, silent as salt, toward the cistern and the woman and the chest that had no business existing—and behind him, far back along the trail, faint on the evening wind, he heard the thing he'd been dreading since noon: hoofbeats. Many. Coming fast.

The race had just become three-sided, and Odile Ishikawa, who had spent eleven years perfecting the art of arriving first and leaving alone, was about to learn what happened when a man finally caught the thing he'd been chasing.

He quickened his step.
