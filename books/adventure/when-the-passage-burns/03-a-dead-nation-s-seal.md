## 3. A Dead Nation's Seal

The chest sat between them on the cistern's broken rim like something dredged from a grave, which, in a way, it was.

Odile had not touched it since Mara set it down. He'd learned long ago that objects with weight to them—real weight, the kind you felt in the wrists before you ever felt it in the mind—deserved a moment of stillness first. A moment of respect, or of warning. His mother used to say the desert was full of things that waited patiently to be picked up by the wrong hands. She'd said it while grinding saltbush for tea, her back to him, her voice pitched low even inside their own tent, because in the settlements there was always someone listening, and some words were worth more than a man's life if they were overheard.

He circled the chest now the way he'd circle a sleeping adder. Iron-banded, cedar beneath the iron gone silver-gray with age, the wood checked and split at the corners but holding. Salt crust along the lower seams told him it had been buried, or sunk, or both. The lock had been forced—not by Mara; her work was cleaner than that—but by someone in a hurry, sometime before her. The hasp hung open like a slack jaw.

And on the lid, centered where a maker's mark might go, pressed into a disc of green bronze gone black at the edges: a seal.

A river, rendered as three wavy lines. Above them, a tower. Around the whole of it, a ring of lettering worn nearly smooth, though Odile could feel the ghost of the grooves under his thumb when he dared to touch it—the raised relief of a script he didn't read but recognized anyway, the way you recognize a face across twenty years and a crowded market.

His mouth went dry. Not saltflat-dry, not trail-dry. Another kind entirely.

"That's—" he started, and stopped.

"I know what it is," Mara said. She sat on the far side of the cistern with her knees drawn up and her knife across her thighs, not threatening exactly, just present, the way a wall is present. "I couldn't open it. I told you that. I stole it because my employer wanted it stolen, and I delivered everything else, and this I kept because—" She paused, and Odile watched her decide how much truth to spend. "Because the seal frightened me. And things that frighten the Combine are usually worth being frightened of."

*The Combine.* He filed the word away. She'd said it once already, down in the wash, when she'd thought he wasn't listening—said it the way other people said *the government*, or *the weather*, or *God*: an enormous, impersonal thing that did what it liked.

"You know whose seal this is," he said. It wasn't quite a question.

"Everyone knows whose seal it is. That's rather the point of erasing a country—you don't get to forget it, you only get to pretend." She turned her knife over. "Vessia."

The name landed in his chest like a stone dropped down a well. He heard it fall a long way.

Because his mother had said that name too. Whispered it, actually—never spoken it full-voiced, never above the sound of the tea grinding, never where the walls were anything thinner than canvas and distance. *Vessia*. She'd told him stories on the nights the wind came off the flats hard enough to cover voices, stories she claimed were just stories, old tales, nothing, *Odile, eat your supper*—but she'd told them with her eyes on the tent flap, and she'd told them wrong on purpose sometimes, mixing details, so that if he ever repeated one to a schoolmaster or a neighbor it would be a broken tale, a useless tale, a tale that led nowhere. He hadn't understood that until he was grown. She'd been encrypting her own son's inheritance against confiscation.

In her stories, Vessia was a nation of water. Not a desert nation pretending at water, hoarding it in cisterns and rationing it drop by drop the way every settlement he'd ever lived in did—a *true* water nation, where the river ran through the middle of everything and nobody owned it because nobody needed to. Where the granaries stood on stilts above the floodplain. Where the tower in the seal—that tower—had been a countinghouse, and the counting had been done in water itself, measured and shared, and the three wavy lines meant not a decoration but a law: *the river runs for all who live beside it.*

Then the Combine had come. In his mother's telling it happened in a single season, though he knew now—everyone knew, in the vague bloodless way everyone knew—that it had taken longer, that there had been treaties and concessions and a war too short to be called one, and then maps redrawn, and then no maps at all. The drowned nation. The erased one. Children born after learned the word only as a warning: *don't ask about Vessia.*

"Open it," Mara said.

"You open it."

"I carried it two hundred miles. I've earned the right to make someone else do the frightening part."

Despite everything—the ache in his legs from four days of tracking, the knife across her knees, the dry wash wind coming off the flats with its smell of dust and old salt—he almost smiled. There was a particular companionship in shared cowardice. It was, he suspected, going to be the foundation of whatever this was going to be between them, and he found he didn't entirely hate that.

He knelt. The cistern's shadow lay cool across his shoulders, the first true shade he'd stood in since dawn, and he let himself have one breath of it before he lifted the lid.

The hinges shrieked. Of course they did. Somewhere in the ruins a bird exploded upward in alarm, wings clattering, and Mara's knife was suddenly standing point-down in the dirt beside her, her whole body coiled—but nothing came. Just the echo, rolling out across the flats and dying.

Inside, the chest was lined in oilcloth gone stiff as bark. Beneath it, documents. Dozens of them. Bundles tied with cord rotted to threads, folios wrapped in waxed leather, and on top, alone, a single folded sheet sealed with a stamp of red wax that had survived the years better than anything else in the box—still deep, still vivid, still wet-looking, as if the blood in it had refused to dry.

Odile unfolded it with hands that were steadier than he expected them to be.

It was a permit. He understood that much immediately, the way you understand the shape of a thing before its meaning. An official document, dense with that same worn-ring script, columns of figures, blocks of formal text—and stamped across the lower third, in ink faded to the brown of dried reeds, a date.

He couldn't read the script. But dates, in any civilized hand, use numerals, and numerals cross borders like smugglers.

He did the arithmetic twice. Then a third time, because the first two answers were impossible.

"The spring equinox," he said. His voice came out strange, flattened, like sound over the saltflats at noon. "These permits expire at the spring equinox."

"What permits?" Mara was on her feet now, crossing to him despite herself, despite the caution that was clearly woven into her the way rope is woven into a boat. "What are—"

He handed her the top sheet and pulled the next. Concession permits. Grant-of-right documents. He knew the form even in a dead language, because he'd seen its living cousins: water rights, grazing rights, passage rights, the paper architecture every power built on top of the desert to decide who got to drink. But these were different in one fundamental way, and the difference hit him like cold water, like falling:

They weren't issued *by* anyone. They were issued *to* Vessia. Granted, sealed, countersigned—concessions held by the drowned nation itself. Rights of access. Rights of drawing. And the source named in the header block, repeated in every document like a refrain—

He didn't know the word. But he knew the shape of it, the way the letters clustered, the way his mother's voice had shaped a similar-sounding word in a story once, a story about a place where the river began, a place the Combine had closed within a year of taking Vessia, a place that appeared on no map drawn since, a place his mother had called—

"*The source,*" he whispered.

Mara had gone very still beside him. She was reading—or trying to; her lips moved slightly, and he realized with a small cold shock that she could read more of the script than he could, that somewhere in her gilded, poisoned upbringing someone had taught her the language of the nation her people had erased. "These are original grants," she said slowly. "Pre-Conquest. Executed, witnessed, sealed. If these are genuine, they constitute prior claim. Prior claim supersedes—" She stopped. He watched her stop, watched her arrive at the same cliff edge he was already standing on. "Prior claim supersedes everything. Every license the Combine has ever issued. Every diversion charter. Everything."

The wind moved through the ruined cistern, sighing over the broken rim, stirring the oilcloth lining with a dry whisper like pages turning.

Odile looked down at the chest, at the bundles, at the red-wax seal on the topmost fold, and felt the wonder curdle in his chest the way milk curdles in heat—slowly, then all at once.

Because here was the thing his mother's stories had never said outright but had built, brick by careful brick, in the foundations of him: the desert didn't die on its own. The settlements shrinking, the wells failing, the towns downriver going begging while Combine barges ran fat and armed up a river that officially *ended* forty miles short of where it actually ended—none of that was weather. None of that was God. Someone had put their hand on the river's throat, and the hand belonged to the Combine, and the reason no one spoke of it was that the proof had been drowned along with the nation that held it.

Until now. Until a thief who was afraid of a seal had carried the proof two hundred miles through the salt and couldn't bring herself to sell it.

"These expire at the equinox," he said again, and now he understood why the date mattered, why whoever had packed this chest had left the freshest seal on top like a hand pointing. "Which means they're valid until the equinox. Which means—"

"Whoever holds them can file them," Mara finished. Her voice had gone quiet, stripped of its thief's irony. "Can claim the source. Can claim the river's headwaters under pre-Concession law, and the Combine's entire charter becomes—" She laughed, one short breath with no humor in it at all. "Do you understand what you're holding? This isn't treasure. This is a loaded weapon pointed at the heart of the Combine."

"And the Combine knows it's missing."

"Yes."

"And they'll be looking for it."

"They're already looking." She met his eyes, and for the first time since the dry wash, since the chase, since the knife, he saw fear in her face—clean, unarmored, the fear of someone who had finally counted the cost of the thing in her hands. "Not for me. For *this*. And Odile—" she said his name for the first time, deliberately, giving it back to him like something borrowed, "—whoever they find holding it won't be arrested. Arrest leaves witnesses. Arrest leaves paperwork. They will simply cease, the way Vessia ceased, and no map will show where we went."

The sun had cleared the cistern's eastern wall and laid a bar of light across the open chest, across the bundles, across the red seal, and the wax seemed almost to glow, alive, arterial.

Odile thought of his mother grinding saltbush with her back turned, encrypting a nation into bedtime stories. Thought of every dying well he'd ever drunk from. Thought of the river that officially ended forty miles short of the truth.

He should walk away. Put the chest down, walk north, lose himself in the flats the way he'd lost himself for fifteen years, moving, always moving, owing a debt under a name he no longer used. That was the sane arithmetic. That was survival.

But his hands were already closing the lid, already lifting, already feeling the true weight of the thing settle into his shoulders like a yoke.

"We need a forger," he heard himself say. "Someone who can authenticate the grants, prove provenance, make them un-deniable. Do you know anyone?"

Mara stared at him. Then, slowly, she smiled—not the sharp market-stall smile from the wash, but something underneath it, younger, startled into the open.

"I know a forger," she said. "Old man at Fenwick Bend. Half-blind, half-mad, and completely dishonest—which means he's exactly honest enough for this." She paused, and the smile faded into something graver. "You understand there's no walking away from this now. Once Brannoc touches these papers, we're all three of them in the story."

"Good," Odile said, and was surprised to find it was true. "Three's a crew."

He settled the chest into his arms and started toward the light, toward the flats, toward Fenwick Bend and whatever came next—and behind him, in the dark of the cistern, the dead nation's seal gleamed once on the closed lid, patient as water, waiting to be spent.
