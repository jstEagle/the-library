## 5. The Tollhouse at Fenwick Bend

The river found us before we found it.

We'd been walking two days out of the wash country, following the dry channels that the old-timers swore still ran underground, when the air changed. It was a smell first—mud and green things and something almost sweet, like rotting melons—and then a sound beneath it, low and constant, the way a sleeping animal breathes. Mara stopped on the lip of a bluff and pointed down without a word.

Fenwick Bend spread below us like a rumor of another world. The river came through there wide and slow, braided around gravel bars, and along its near bank stood a scatter of buildings leaning together for company: a ferry landing gone soft with moss, a rope bridge sagging like an old man's shoulders, and at the center of it all, a long low structure with a stone chimney and a roof patched in three different colors of tin—the tollhouse, where once upon a time the Combine had collected passage fees from every barge and skiff moving upriver toward the source they didn't want anyone to reach.

Nobody collected tolls anymore. The Combine had closed the upper river a generation back, and Fenwick Bend had been left to rot like a tooth in the desert's jaw. But people remained. People always remained, the ones too stubborn or too broken to leave.

"Brannoc keeps to the tollhouse," Mara said. She hadn't looked at me directly since the cistern. "He'll want payment."

"He'll get it." I touched the chest strap across my shoulder where the Vessian permits rode against my ribs. Two days I'd carried them, and two days I'd felt them the way you feel a coal wrapped in cloth—warm, patient, waiting to burn through. "What's he like?"

Mara considered the question with the seriousness she gave everything, as if each answer might be a trap she was walking into. "Old. Careful. He forged my father's travel papers for six years before anyone caught him at it." A pause. "They never did catch him. He retired."

"You knew him inside the Combine?"

"I knew *of* him. Everyone did. He was a story we told children who lied well—*the Brannoc will find your false words and eat them.*" Something that might have been a smile crossed her face and was gone. "Then he disappeared, and the story became that he'd died. Stories about the dead are safer than stories about the living."

We went down.

* * *

The tollhouse smelled of ink and lamp oil and old paper, a smell so dense it had weight, pressing against the skin like humidity. Shelves lined every wall, sagging under ledgers and folios and bundles of documents tied with string gone gray as cobweb. A single window let in a blade of afternoon light, and in that light sat an old man at a desk built for a much larger room, bent over his work like a question mark asking itself a question.

He didn't look up when we entered. His pen kept moving—a fine steel nib whispering across vellum, laying down letters so even they might have been printed.

"Toll's abolished," he said. His voice was dry as the shelves. "You're forty years late."

"We're not here to pay," I said. "We're here to buy."

The pen stopped. He raised his head slowly, and I saw a face like a dried riverbed—all cracks and channels, with eyes the pale gray of water-starved stone set deep in it. Those eyes went to me first, weighed me, dismissed me, and then found Mara standing half in shadow by the door, and stayed.

"Well," Brannoc said softly. "The story walked in wearing boots."

"You know me?"

"I know your face. You have your mother's jaw and your father's way of standing like the floor owes him money." He set down his pen with the care of a man handling something explosive. "I also know what it means when a Combine daughter comes to a dead man's door with a ranger at her back. So before either of you says another word—" he turned in his chair, unhurried, and lifted a small brass bell from the desk, "—understand that I have prepared for this day for thirty years, and the preparation involves a shotgun."

"I'm not Combine," Mara said. "Not anymore. They cut me loose."

"They always say that first." But he didn't ring the bell. His eyes moved between us, reading us the way he read documents—not the words but the watermarks, the pressure of the nib, the truth underneath. "Sit down anyway. Standing visitors make me nervous, and nervous men sign sloppy work."

There were exactly two stools in the tollhouse, which told you how many guests Brannoc expected. We sat. I unslung the chest and set it on the desk between us, and the sound it made—wood on wood, solid, final—seemed to change the light in the room.

Brannoc looked at the chest for a long moment. Then he reached out one spotted hand and laid his palm flat against the lid, over the seal, the way a doctor lays a hand on a patient's chest.

"Vessia," he said. Just the word. But saying it, his whole face changed—the cracks deepening, the gray eyes going bright and wet at once, a man looking at the grave of someone he'd loved. "Where did you get this?"

"A drowned courier," Mara said. "Upstream of the second bend. The river gave him back."

"The river gives back what it can't keep." Brannoc withdrew his hand as if the wood had burned him. "Open it."

I worked the lock—it wasn't locked, not really; the mechanism had rusted to suggestion—and lifted the lid. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, lay the permits: eleven sheets of heavy rag paper, each stamped with the seal of Vessia, each bearing dates and signatures and the particular dense beauty of official writing done by hands that believed in what they wrote.

Brannoc took the top sheet and held it to the window light. For a while the only sound in the tollhouse was the river breathing outside and the old man's breathing inside, and they were nearly the same rhythm.

"These are real," he said finally. "Third-issue concession grants, pre-Diversion. Signed by the Chancery of Waters before the Combine flooded the capital and redrew every map in the world." His thumb moved across the seal. "Do you understand what you're holding, boy? These aren't papers. These are *titles*. Whoever presents these at the source before the spring equinox holds legal claim to the headwaters. Legal claim, recognized under the old accords that the Combine never dared formally repeal because repealing them would mean admitting Vessia existed."

"And if nobody presents them?" I asked.

"Then the claim dies unfiled, and the source belongs to whoever's standing on it when the equinox passes." He set the permit down with exaggerated gentleness, the way you put down a sleeping animal. "Which is why the Combine wants these more than they've ever wanted anything. And why they'll burn the world to get them."

The lamp oil smell seemed suddenly flammable. Mara shifted on her stool.

"That's why I need you," she said. "If we're going to move upstream—if we're going to survive checkpoints—we need these authenticated. Certified genuine, under your hand, with whatever marks the Combine's inspectors trust. Can you do it?"

Brannoc laughed. It was a terrible sound, dry and short, like a branch breaking. "Child, I could authenticate a forgery of these. Authenticating the real thing is just good manners." He gathered the sheets, squared their edges against the desk with a tap tap tap, and then paused, holding them, not looking at either of us.

"But I'll tell you my price now, so you can refuse it and save us both the trouble of haggling."

"Name it," I said.

"Passage." He looked up, and the wet brightness in his eyes had hardened into something older and more immovable than any stamp ever pressed into wax. "I was born in Vessia. I was four years old when the water came over the walls. I have spent seventy years forging other people's names onto other people's lies, and I have one journey left in these legs, and I will spend it seeing the source of the river that killed my nation—or I will spend nothing at all. Take me upstream, or take your permits and go, and may the Combine catch you by the second bend."

The river breathed. Mara stared at him. I found I couldn't look away from his hands—those precise, criminal, beautiful hands resting on the papers like a father's hands on a child's shoulders.

"You're old," I said, because somebody had to say it.

"I'm *expiring*," Brannoc said. "It's different. Old men wait. Expiring men move." He pushed the permits back into the chest and closed the lid. "Well? The light's leaving, and I don't work after dark anymore. My eyes are honest that way."

* * *

He worked through the last hour of daylight like a man possessed, and maybe he was—possessed by seventy years of patience finally given permission to end. I watched him prepare the authentication seals: mixing the ink from three separate compounds so no single sample could be matched, heating the brass matrix over the lamp until the air above it shimmered, testing the impression on scrap until the Vessian crest came out perfect enough to make my chest ache. Mara watched too, from the doorway, arms crossed, and I noticed she'd positioned herself where she could see both the door and the window, and I noticed I'd done the same without thinking about it. Two prey animals in the same burrow, keeping opposite watches.

"There," Brannoc said at last, pressing the final seal into wax the color of dried blood. He blew on it gently, once, like blessing it. "Certified. Witnessed. Under my hand and mark." He showed us the reverse of each sheet, where he'd added a tiny cipher in the margin—invisible unless you knew to look. "Any Combine inspector who sees this mark will believe the documents passed through the Office of Reclamation years ago. Standard archival transfer. Boring. Nobody shoots a man carrying boring paperwork."

"And if they look closer?" Mara asked.

"Then they were going to shoot you anyway." He began wrapping the permits in fresh oilcloth with the tenderness he'd shown the originals. "Papers buy you the first lie. After that you're on your own."

That was when I heard the horses.

Not one horse—many, and coming fast along the near bank, hooves striking the packed mud of the old towpath with a rhythm I felt in my teeth before my ears sorted it out. I was at the window in three steps, low, below the sill, and Mara was beside me just as fast, and neither of us had agreed to do it. That's the thing about prey animals. They move together without deciding to.

Six riders. Combine gray, dust-plumed, fanning out across the landing with the drilled ease of men who'd swept a hundred river towns before this one. The lead rider dismounted at the ferry slip and said something to his men, and two of them peeled off toward the rope bridge and two toward the cluster of houses, moving door to door with the unhurried confidence of people who already knew the answer.

"They're not searching," Mara breathed. "They're *collecting*. Someone told them exactly where to come."

"Someone sold us," I said. "The cistern buyer. Or the courier's body drew attention. Doesn't matter." I turned. "Back door?"

"There is no back door," Brannoc said behind us, and his voice was very calm, the calm of a man who has rehearsed his own death for decades and finds the performance going differently than scripted. "There's the front, and there's the river."

"The river," I said, and looked at him, and he looked back, and I watched him understand what I was asking and watch him decide—there, in the space of a breath—to stop being a man who waited.

"The barge shed," he said. "Under the tollhouse. There's a raft. My raft. Built it myself over nine years, plank by plank, when the patrols weren't watching." He was already moving, snuffing the lamp, pulling a canvas sack from beneath the floorboards—ink bottles and seal matrices vanishing into it with the speed of long practice. "Nine years I told myself I built it for escape. Lies are my trade; I should have known better. I built it for *this*."

Boots on the tollhouse porch. A fist against the front door, once, twice—polite, terrible. "Tollhouse! Open in the name of the Combine Office of Reclamation!"

Brannoc pried up a section of floor that I would have sworn was wall, revealing a dark slot and a ladder down. Mara went first, chest and all—I thrust it into her arms and she took it without argument, which told me something about how far she'd already come from the woman who'd run from me across the saltflats. Then Brannoc, moving faster than his age had any right to allow. I went last, dragging the floor panel back into place as the front door splintered inward above us, and dropped into darkness that smelled of tar and river water.

The barge shed was a low stone vault open to the river on its downstream side, and there was the raft: twenty feet of lashed timber with a steering oar, a shallow cargo box, and a lean-to canopy of stitched hide. It was ugly and it was beautiful and it was ours.

"Cut the line," Brannoc said, untying the mooring cleat with fingers that shook only slightly. "Push on my word. The current does the rest—Fenwick Bend runs fast this season, we'll be past the point before they're off the porch."

Above us, footsteps crossed the floorboards. A voice, muffled: "Empty. Lamp's still warm."

"*Now.*"

We shoved. The raft ground against stone, hung, and then the river took hold of it the way the river takes hold of everything—absolutely, without negotiation—and slid us out into the brown evening water. Behind us, shouting erupted from the tollhouse; a rifle cracked and a ball slapped the water a boat-length astern, throwing up a white feather of spray. Mara crouched low with the chest hugged to her body. Brannoc took the steering oar with both hands and wore the expression of a man greeting an old enemy by its first name.

"Hold her mid-channel!" he called over the water-noise. "The point shoals on the left—left! *Left*, boy, are you deaf—"

I threw my weight on the second oar and we swung wide of the gravel bar by less than a raft's width, the shallows hissing past our flank, and then the bend opened ahead of us and the riders shrank to specks on the shore, and the specks shrank to nothing, and it was just us and the current and the first stars coming out over the canyon rim.

For a long while nobody spoke. The river talked enough for all of us.

It was Mara who broke it, finally, not looking up from the chest in her lap. "They'll follow along the bank. There'll be boats at the next settlement. This isn't over."

"No," I agreed. "It's not."

"And you—" She turned to Brannoc, and something in her voice had cracked open, some seam she'd kept sealed since the cistern. "You gave up everything. Your house. Your work. Thirty years of hiding, gone in ten minutes. Why? You didn't owe us anything."

The old man steered with his knees for a moment and dug in the canvas sack, producing a flask, which he offered around. We drank in turn—something fierce and homemade that stripped a layer off the throat. When the flask came back to him, he capped it and gazed upstream, where the canyon walls were closing in around the last of the light.

"I spent my life making true things false," he said. "Names, seals, signatures. Beautiful lies, and I was the best there ever was at them." His hand settled on the chest, on the seal, the same gesture as before—doctor, father, mourner. "And then tonight I sealed a document that was *true*. First time in seventy years. Do you understand? Everything I forged was a door painted on a wall. This—" he tapped the wood, "—this is a door that opens. I wasn't going to die on the wrong side of it."

The current carried us on. I took my turn at the oar and watched the canyon swallow the sky, and thought about doors, and debts, and the strange arithmetic by which a crew becomes a crew—not chosen, not trusted, not yet, but bound anyway, three strangers on a raft made of nine years of somebody else's hope, running upstream toward the one place in the world the Combine would kill to keep empty.

Behind us, far back along the darkening bank, a lantern kindled. Then another. Then a third, spaced evenly, like beads on a string.

They were following the river.

So were we.
