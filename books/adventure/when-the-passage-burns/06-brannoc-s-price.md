## 6. Brannoc's Price

The forge had gone cold three years before anyone noticed, and that suited Brannoc fine. A cold forge meant no customers, and no customers meant no questions, and no questions meant he could sit in the doorway of his ruined tollhouse at Fenwick Bend with a cup of barley tea going bitter in his hands and watch the river do the only honest work left in the world.

The river didn't lie. That was the first thing they taught you in Vessia, back when Vessia was a thing that could teach you anything. Water finds its level. Water remembers its way home. Men lie about water all the time—men had lied about it so thoroughly and so profitably that an entire nation had been scraped off the maps like a stain from a table—but the river itself kept no ledgers. It simply went where it was going, and it carried everything away except what it chose to keep.

Brannoc had spent sixty-one years learning to be one of the things the river chose to keep.

He heard them coming long before he saw them, because his ears were still good even if his hands had begun their slow betrayal. The creak of a raft hull drying wrong in the sun. Boots on the old towpath stones, placed by someone who knew how to place them. And underneath it, faint as a coin dropped in another room, the particular silence of two people trying very hard not to sound like they were following each other.

He smiled into his tea. He hadn't had visitors worth smiling at since the Combine's surveyors came through eight years ago, measuring his bridge pilings for a tax he couldn't pay on a structure that technically belonged to a government that technically existed.

"Door's open," he called, without turning around. "It's always open. That's not hospitality, it's rot."

A pause. Then footsteps, more certain now, and a man came around the corner of the tollhouse with his hand resting easy near his hip—not on a weapon, exactly, but in conversation with one. Tall, sun-cured, salt still crusted white in the seams of his coat. A desert ranger's walk: weight forward, eyes doing the talking. Behind him came a woman who moved differently, all economy and suspicion, like a blade that had learned to sheathe itself.

"You're Brannoc," the man said.

"I'm the ruin," Brannoc said. "Brannoc is what's left inside it. Sit down or don't. The stools are honest even if the floor isn't."

The ranger took him at his word and sat, which Brannoc appreciated. The woman stayed standing, which he appreciated more. You could tell a great deal about people by how they treated a stranger's furniture. This one treated it like evidence.

"I'm told you authenticate documents," the ranger said.

"I'm told a lot of things." Brannoc sipped his tea. It had gone past bitter into something almost philosophical. "Who told you?"

"A lockmaker in Corren's Ferry. She said if the paper matters, see the man at Fenwick Bend who makes dead seals live again."

"Mm. Sefla." He set down the cup. His hands were bad today—the tremor came up from the wrists on damp mornings, and the whole river valley had been holding its breath under a low gray sky for three days, promising rain it never delivered. "Sefla owes me a debt I'll never collect, so she spends it sending me customers instead. Very tidy. Very her." He looked at the ranger properly then, and let himself do the thing he did with every new face: read it like a document. Provenance, condition, signs of forgery. "You have paper that matters. You wouldn't have walked it this far upriver otherwise. Paper that matters gets people killed, and you don't strike me as a man who enjoys getting other people killed, whatever your trade says about you."

"And her?" The ranger tipped his head toward the woman.

"She strikes me as someone who'd enjoy it fine, but only if it was deserved." He turned his gaze on her. "Am I close?"

Something flickered across her face—not quite a smile, but the ghost of where one would go if it ever decided to risk the trip. "Close enough," she said.

"Well then." Brannoc pushed himself up out of the chair, joints announcing each stage of the journey like a town crier. "Show me the paper. And understand before you do: once I've seen it, I'm part of whatever it is. That's not a threat and it's not a boast. It's just how paper works. It implicates everyone who touches it. Ask any forger."

The ranger hesitated. The woman didn't. She stepped forward, dug into the satchel at her hip, and set a chest on the table between them with a click that sounded, to Brannoc's ear, like a sentence being handed down.

He knew the seal before he touched it.

Sixty years, and his body remembered before his mind could catch up—the shape of it rising under his palm like a face under a veil. The interlocked channels of the Vessian watermark, three rivers braided into a single current, struck deep into lead the way only a nation's press could strike it. Not a copy. Not a casting. The original die-work, done in the old mint at Serel Vess by craftsmen who had been drowned along with everything else, or scattered so thin that Brannoc had spent decades believing he might be the last pair of hands alive that knew what this metal was supposed to feel like.

His hands were shaking now, and for once it wasn't age.

"Where," he said, and his voice came out lower than he intended, rougher, "did you get this."

"Off a thief," the ranger said carefully. "Who got it off a courier. Who—" He glanced at the woman.

"Who the river gave back," she finished. "Downstream of the unmapped canyons. What was left of him."

Brannoc closed his eyes. He could see it without trying: a courier in Vessian gray, satchel strapped high and tight, running the last legal mile of a country that was about to stop being legal anywhere at all. Some things you carried because you were paid to. Some things you carried because if you didn't, no one else would, and the difference between those two kinds of carrying was the difference between a job and a covenant.

"Open it," he said.

The ranger worked the locks—good locks, old ones, picked rather than forced—and lifted the lid, and Brannoc looked down at the permits of Vessia.

Concession grants. Source rights. Stamped, countersigned, dated in the old calendar that the Combine had abolished along with everything else, and every one of them bearing the same fatal line in formal script: *valid until the spring equinox, after which all claims revert to the commons.*

He read that line four times. His eyes were still good enough for that, at least. *Revert to the commons.* The men who wrote these permits had built their expiry into the law itself—a failsafe, a door left open behind them. Whoever held valid permits when the equinox came could stand at the source and make a claim; whoever held nothing could not; and if no claim stood, the water belonged to everyone or no one, which in Vessian law had always amounted to the same word.

*Free.*

"They're real," he said quietly. "Every stamp. Every signature. I know the hands—I trained under two of them." He laughed, and it came out cracked down the middle. "I forged these men for thirty years. Counterfeited their flourishes for smugglers and land-grabbers and once, may the drowned forgive me, a Combine tax collector. And here they are, the actual articles, sitting on my rotten table in a chest that smells like a drowned man's satchel."

"So you can authenticate them," the ranger said.

"I can do better than authenticate them." Brannoc laid his trembling hand flat on the permits, gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping child. "I can make them unimpeachable. I can add the countersignatures that are missing—the courier died before the final registration, didn't he? Of course he did. Half-finished paper is worthless paper. But in the right hands, half-finished becomes finished, and finished becomes *lawful*, and lawful becomes a claim the Combine cannot laugh out of any court in the territory." He looked up. "Yes. I can do what you need. I'm the only man on this river who can, and we all know it, which is why you came to me instead of shooting me and taking my tea."

The woman shifted. "What's your price?"

And there it was. The question he'd been turning over since he'd seen the seal, the one that had been waiting in him for sixty years like water waiting behind a dam.

Because he could ask for gold. There was gold in this, clearly—two armed travelers, a chest of claims on the most valuable water in the world, a ranger with a soldier's posture and a thief with a killer's patience. He could ask for enough to rebuild the tollhouse, buy the bend, grow old stupid and comfortable. He could ask for protection, passage, a new name in a new town.

Or he could ask for the only thing he had ever actually wanted, which was not a thing at all but a direction. Upstream.

"You want to run the river to the source," he said. It wasn't a question. "That's what these are for. That's why you're here, and not selling them in some port town to the highest bidder—which, incidentally, would be the smarter play, and I say that as a man who has made his living being smarter than honest."

"We're deciding," the ranger said slowly, "what we're doing."

"Then let me decide it for you, at a price." Brannoc rose, crossed the ruined room to the cold hearth, and knelt—slower than he used to, much slower—with his back to them. From the cavity behind the fallen brick he drew a bundle wrapped in oilcloth, and unwrapped it on the hearthstone with the care of a priest handling relics.

A map. Hand-drawn, faded, the ink gone the brown of old blood. The river system of Vessia, every channel and weir and spring, drawn by his father's father, who had drawn it from his own father's memory, because the Combine burned the official surveys and the maps survived only the way love survives: illegally, secretly, in the hands of people who refused to forget.

"My name isn't Brannoc," he said, still facing the fire. "That's the name I bought when the maps burned. My mother named me Bren of the Vess, third son of a weir-keeper's line, born forty miles below the source in a town whose name you will not find on any chart printed in the last thirty years, because the town doesn't exist anymore either. They didn't just erase the nation, boy. They erased the *water*. Diverted it, renamed it, redrew every channel so the rivers that fed us would feed them instead. Twelve towns went dry so their reservoirs could fill. Mine was the fourth to die." He turned around then, holding the map, and let them see his face while he said the rest. "I was nine years old. I remember the year the river stopped reaching us. I remember the wells going brackish, and the orchards, and the sound my mother made at night when she thought we were asleep. I remember standing in the empty riverbed thinking the world had ended, and being told, gently, by men in Combine gray, that the world had merely been *reorganized*."

The ranger had gone very still. The woman's hand had drifted, unconsciously, to the chest.

"I became a forger because forgery is grief with a steady hand," Brannoc said. "Every fake seal I cut was a small revenge. Every document I authenticated honestly was a small penance. Sixty years I've waited for this paper to come up my river, and I'd begun to think I'd die waiting, and that my last act would be teaching some grandniece of Sefla's how to counterfeit a grain tariff." He set the map on the table beside the chest, so the old ink and the old lead lay together, two halves of a dead country reunited. "So here is my price, and it isn't gold. Take me with you. All the way up. To the source itself."

Silence, except the river.

"I'm sixty-one years old," he went on, quieter now. "My hands shake. My lungs fill with water if I sleep lying flat. By every reasonable measure I should be spending my last seasons within reach of a decent doctor and a soft bed. But I have spent my whole life making false copies of true things, and I am asking—no, I'm telling you—that before I go, I want to stand in front of one true thing. One. The water where it begins. The spring my grandfather tended, that my father was born beside, that I was exiled from before I could spell its name. They took the maps. They took the names. They took the *towns*. But they never took the source, because they couldn't—they could only cage it, and hide it, and pretend it was theirs." His voice caught, and he let it. He was too old for pride and too tired for performance. "I want to see it free. Even once. Even if the seeing kills me—and it may, I'm not a fool, I know what the unmapped canyons do to young boats and younger men. Let me witness it. Because a thing unwitnessed can be denied forever, and a thing witnessed by even one dying old forger becomes a fact that has to be lived with."

The ranger looked at the woman. The woman looked at the map. For a long moment neither spoke, and Brannoc recognized the look passing between them, because he'd forged documents for married couples and business partners and conspirators for half a century: it was the look of two people discovering, mid-argument, that they agreed.

"The canyons will try to kill us," the ranger said finally. "The Combine definitely will. And you're telling us you're sixty-one."

"I'm telling you I'm the best forger on this river, the only living soul who knows the Vessian countersigns by heart, and the only man in the territory who can read that map without squinting." Brannoc tapped the old parchment. "Besides which—you'll want a witness who can't be bought. I've been poor my entire life. I'm very good at it."

The woman snorted. It was, he suspected, the closest thing to laughter she permitted herself in company.

"The raft holds three," the ranger said. "Barely."

"The raft held three the day you stole it, and it'll hold three the day you lose it. Boats are honest that way." Brannoc began rolling the map with hands that shook and did not hesitate. "We leave tomorrow at first light, before the rain decides to keep its promise. I'll need tonight to finish the countersignatures—real ones, in period inks, undetectable by anyone short of the Combine's chief examiner, who is dead, because I checked."

"You checked," the woman repeated flatly.

"I keep abreast of my competition." He tucked the permits back into the chest and closed the lid, and his palm lingered on the seal a moment longer, saying goodbye to sixty years of waiting and hello to whatever came next. "Get some sleep, both of you. The loft's dry-ish and the ghosts are polite. Tomorrow we go upstream."

They climbed to the loft. The ranger paused at the ladder's foot. "Why 'the weight of witness'?" he asked—no, wait, that wasn't right, that was Brannoc's own thought, circling. What the ranger actually said was simpler. "You could have asked for anything."

"Yes," Brannoc agreed. "That's rather the point. When you spend your life forging other people's names, you learn exactly what yours is worth." He banked the last coals in the cold hearth, more ritual than necessity, and settled into his chair with the map across his knees like a sleeping animal. "Goodnight, Odile Ishikawa. Sleep while the river's quiet."

Through the cracks in the roof, the first rain finally came—thin, hesitant, tasting of the far-off mountains where the water was born and caged. Brannoc listened to it a long time, an old man keeping vigil over a dead nation's paperwork, and somewhere past midnight he took out his pens and began the countersignatures, each stroke a vow, each flourish a small resurrection.

By dawn the permits would be flawless. By dawn they would be lawful. And by dawn, whether the living liked it or not, Vessia would have three witnesses on the water, heading home.

* * *

He woke before light to the smell of woodsmoke that wasn't his, and lay still in the dark, cataloguing. Not the hearth—he'd let it die properly. Downstream side. Wet wool. Horse, or horses, moving slow on the towpath with the particular restraint of men who didn't want to be heard.

Combine riders moved exactly that way.

Brannoc rose without a sound, sixty years of forgery having taught him that the most valuable skill in the trade was knowing when to stop signing. He woke the others with a hand, not a voice, and saw the ranger come fully awake between one breath and the next, and the woman's knife already clear of its sheath like it had been waiting up for her.

"How many?" the ranger breathed.

"At least four. Maybe six. They're being polite about it, which means they're being thorough." Brannoc was already moving, pulling the oilcloth-wrapped map from its hiding place, pressing the chest into the woman's arms. "Back door, down to the boathouse. My raft's there—yes, I have a raft, I've had a raft for thirty years, what kind of forger would I be without an exit strategy?"

"And you?" the woman said.

"I'm right behind you. I just need a moment to leave a message." He was already at his workbench, sweeping his finest tools, his inks, his reference plates into a satchel with hands that had finally, blessedly, stopped shaking. Then he took one last piece of paper, blank and good, and wrote on it in a fair imitation of a Combine requisition clerk's hand:

*Nothing of value here. Property already seized. Proceed downstream.*

He pinned it to the door with a knife.

"There," he said softly, to the ruin that had kept him, to the bend that had hidden him, to the river that had carried everything he loved away and was now, at the end of his life, carrying him back. "Let's see them argue with my handwriting."

Outside, the rain thickened. On the dark water, the raft waited, and upstream, past the riders and the checkpoints and the canyons full of bones, the source held its breath—sixty years patient, one night longer.

Brannoc stepped aboard, and the river took him home.
