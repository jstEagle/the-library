## 7. Rapids of the Unmapped

The river stopped being a river on the third morning and became something else entirely—a rumor with teeth.

Odile felt the change before he saw it, the way a man feels a storm in the ache of an old break. The water beneath the raft went from brown-green murk to a hard, glassy green, cold enough that when it splashed over his boots it bit like ice. The banks narrowed. The cottonwoods thinned, then gave up altogether, replaced by walls of red and black stone rising on either side until the sky was a bright ribbon overhead and the crew were ants crawling down the seam of the world.

"Nobody's charted this," Brannoc said. He sat amidships with the permits wrapped in oilcloth against his chest, as if they might bolt from him like startled birds. His old hands were steady on the sweep oar, but his voice had gone quiet in the way it did when he was remembering things he'd rather not. "I told you. Past Fenwick Bend, the maps go blank. The Combine never surveyed here because the Combine never wanted anyone coming this way."

"They didn't want anyone *looking*," Mara corrected from the bow. She'd taken to riding point without being asked, her body reading the water ahead of them, calling out rocks in a low flat voice. "There's a difference."

"There is," Odile agreed, and poled them through a gap between two boulders that left paint scraped off the raft's starboard rail. "And we're about to learn what it costs."

He'd run rivers his whole life—the Vessa before the Combine dammed its middle reaches, the Sable, the nameless seasonal floods that roared out of the high country every spring and vanished into the sand. But those rivers had been *known*. They had names for their rapids, graveyards for their dead, ferryman's marks cut into stone by generations of men who'd learned the hard way where the water lied. This river had none of that. It was running wild and unrecorded through country no honest mapmaker had ever seen, and every bend was a question the water answered only once.

The canyon deepened. The light came down in shafts, gold and dusty, and the roar ahead of them grew from a mutter to a conversation to a sermon.

* * *

They lined the first rapid rather than run it—hauling the raft along a shelf of rock while the river thundered past below, white and furious, throwing spray forty feet up the canyon wall. It took four hours. By the end of it Odile's shoulders burned, his palms were raw, and Mara had slipped once on the wet stone and been caught by Brannoc's grip on her collar, and none of them spoke about it, because that was how trust worked: not in speeches but in the catching.

They ate cold beans on a gravel bar as the light failed, the canyon walls going purple, then black, the stars cutting down at them like nailheads driven through leather.

"My mother sang about this water," Brannoc said suddenly. He was looking up at the ribbon of sky. "Vessian lullabies. *Down from the snows, down through the stone, the river remembers everyone it has carried.* I thought it was just a song." He shook his head slowly. "I think it was directions."

Odile said nothing. He was listening to the river instead, and what he heard in the dark made the hair rise on his arms: not one voice but many, braided and layered, the sound of water moving through places it should not have been able to reach. Springs feeding in from the canyon walls, unseen. Side channels. Caverns. The river wasn't just running through this stone—it was living inside it.

"Sleep in shifts," he said. "Tomorrow we run blind."

* * *

They ran blind for two days.

The second morning brought the first real rapid—a quarter mile of chaos that no lining path existed for, sheer walls on both sides, nothing to do but point the raft down the throat and pray to whatever god kept fools afloat. Odile took the steering oar himself. Mara knelt at the bow calling the water the way she'd learned to call it somewhere Odile didn't ask about, her voice clipped and certain: *hole left, thread right, rock under the surface at two o'clock, commit.* Brannoc bailed. That was all any of them could ask of him—that and the permits, which he held against his ribs like a second heartbeat.

They hit the hole anyway. Of course they hit the hole; the water always wins the arguments it cares about. The raft stood on its nose, hung there for a heartbeat that lasted a year, and then slammed down into the trough so hard Odile felt his back teeth click together. A wall of green water came over the side and took everything loose—two bailing buckets, half their dried fish, the tin cup Mara drank from—and for thirty seconds Odile steered by faith alone, the oar chattering in his grip, the canyon spinning, until the river spat them out into a long calm pool below and let them live.

Mara laughed. Actually laughed—a short, startled bark of a sound, as if it had escaped her without permission. She caught Odile watching her and strangled it, but not before Brannoc grinned at her from the bilge, soaked to the skin, holding up the tin cup, which had somehow survived.

"Lucky cup," the old man said.

"Lucky crew," Odile said, and meant it more than he wanted to admit.

That night they camped on a bench of sand above the waterline, and Odile found himself doing something he hadn't done in years: talking. Not much. Just enough. Mara asked him why a ranger with no warrant and no payroll was hauling a dead nation's paperwork up a river nobody owned, and he gave her the truest answer he had.

"I owed a man my life once. He collected the debt by dying before I could pay it. Everything I've done since is interest." He turned the spit over the fire. "A name I don't use anymore. A town I can't go back to. You know how it is."

"I know how it is," Mara said, and for once she didn't sound like she was performing the knowing.

Brannoc snored between them, and the river talked all night in its many voices, and Odile slept better than he had in a decade.

* * *

On the fourth day they found the barge.

Mara saw it first—a pale shape wedged in the rocks at the mouth of a side canyon, and even from a hundred yards Odile knew what it was, because he knew the lines of Combine hullwork the way he knew the lines of a rattlesnake. They eddied out below it and climbed.

She had been a supply barge, forty feet if she was an inch, steel-ribbed with a shallow draft for freight work. Something had opened her portside hull like a knife opening a belly—rock, most likely, or the river itself finding a flaw and exploiting it with the patience only water has. She lay canted against the stones with her stern submerged, her deck plates furred with mineral stain, years of flood lines written across her in white and rust.

"She's been here a long time," Mara said quietly. "Five years. Maybe more."

"Before your time," Odile said, and watched her flinch, and didn't take it back.

They found the men—or what the desert had left of them. Three, maybe four, mummified in the dry heat of a stacked-rock shelter someone had built against the canyon wall, laid out with a care that said at least one of them had survived long enough to bury the others. Their uniforms had faded to the color of bone. One still wore a Combine surveyor's badge on a chain around his neck, and beside him, wrapped in the same oilcloth Odile now carried his own cargo in, lay a brass instrument case, empty, and a ledger swollen fat with river-damp, its pages fused into a single brick of pulp.

"He got them this far," Brannoc murmured. He stood over the graves with his hat in his hands, an old-country gesture Odile had seen him make exactly twice before. "Ran the unmapped water, wrecked, lost his instruments, buried his people—and kept going. On foot. Upstream." The old man's voice cracked. "He was trying to reach it. All these years ago, and he was already trying."

Odile crouched by the surveyor and looked at the ruined ledger, and thought about what it meant. The Combine had come up this river once, secretly, years ago—with instruments, with a crew, with orders. And they had died in the dark, and the Combine had never sent another expedition, never marked the charts, never told a soul. Because the source was a secret worth more than the lives spent chasing it. Because men who drowned in an uncharted canyon couldn't testify.

"They knew," he said aloud. "They've known where it was, or close to it, all along. They just couldn't get to it."

"And now we're getting there for them," Mara said. Her face was unreadable, but her hands weren't; they'd closed into fists at her sides. "We're running their errand on their river, carrying their paper, toward their machine. Does anyone else want to say that out loud, or is it just me?"

"It's not just you," Odile said. "But whoever holds the permits decides who drinks. That hasn't changed since Vessia stamped them. The only question is whether we're the kind of people who sell that, or spend it."

Mara looked at him a long moment. Whatever she saw in his face, she turned away from it, and started back down toward the raft.

* * *

They stripped the wreck before they left. It was Odile's idea and he didn't pretend otherwise: a steel-ribbed barge meant salvage, and salvage meant rope, plate, tools, and above all the barge's iron anchor chain, fifty feet of it, which would hold them against currents that their single grapnel never could. The work took the whole afternoon. It was brutal, shared labor—Mara and Odile wrestling deck plate free while Brannoc rigged a tackle from the barge's broken mast, the three of them shouting and swearing and hauling in rhythm until the rhythm became its own language.

Somewhere in the middle of it, hauling a crate of rivets up the bank hand over hand, Odile realized he was grinning, and that Mara was grinning back, and that neither of them had noticed when it started.

They built a proper towline off the salvaged chain, redistributed the weight, patched the raft's worst seams with tarred canvas from the barge's stores. By the time the sun dropped behind the western rim they had a boat again—not the same boat, a better one, a boat that three strangers had argued and bled and sweated into being. Odile stood at the steering oar and looked at his crew—his crew, the word arriving in his head uninvited and staying—and felt the loneliness that had ridden him for fifteen years shift its weight, just slightly, like a passenger deciding to stay.

"We'll run the next set at first light," he said. "Sound carries wrong in this canyon. If we hear anything tonight that isn't the river—"

"It won't be the river," Mara finished.

They anchored mid-channel behind a gravel bar, chain taut, and took watches. Odile took midnight. He sat with the permits in his lap and the stars wheeling down the slot of sky and listened to the water move in the dark, patient and enormous, carrying them upstream toward whatever waited at the top of the world.

It was near dawn when he heard it. Not the river. Under the river—a different sound, rhythmic, mechanical, faint as a heartbeat through stone. A pump, or a generator, or something turning, far upstream, hidden in the heart of the canyon.

He woke the others without a word. Mara listened, eyes closing, head tilted, and when she opened them again there was something in her face he hadn't seen before—something that looked almost like grief.

"That's the intake," she said. "I grew up hearing that sound through the floor of my father's study. We're close enough to hear the Combine breathing."

The river carried the sound to them all night, and none of them slept, and at first light they pointed the raft upstream into the noise.
