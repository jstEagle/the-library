## 8. The Girl Who Ran the Water

The fire had burned down to a red eye in its ring of stones, and Odile had gone to check the raft lines, and Brannoc slept with his back against a boulder, his mouth open, snoring like a man who had made his peace with the world and expected it to make its peace with him. Mara sat alone with her boots off, her feet blistered from three days of portage and current, and she watched the river slide past in the dark—black water carrying white noise, the sound of something enormous moving patiently toward a place she had been taught to call *ours*.

She should sleep. Tomorrow they would run the unmapped water. But sleep was a country she'd been exiled from years ago, and so instead she did what she always did when she couldn't sleep: she took out the permits.

Not the chest—the chest was heavy, lacquered wood and iron bands, and it sat between them all now like a fourth member of the crew who never spoke. She carried one permit only, folded into a square soft as cloth from handling. She unfolded it by firelight. The Vessian seal gleamed up at her, that drowned nation's double wave beneath a crown of reeds, stamped deep enough to feel with a fingertip. Below it, in faded violet ink: *Concession granted for the diversion and use of the headwaters, source to third cataract, in perpetuity until the spring equinox of the forty-first year.*

In perpetuity until. The phrase was a contradiction, and contradictions were how lawyers wrote mercy into contracts. Her father had explained it to her once, when she was eleven, standing before a map table taller than she was. He had put his hand flat on the paper, on the blue thread that marked the river, and said, *Everything is negotiable, little fish. Even forever. Especially forever.*

She had believed him then. She had believed him for twenty years after that, which was longer, and worse.

* * *

The Combine raised its children the way it built its weirs: deliberately, in straight lines, with no gap left unplanned.

Mara grew up in the Spirehold, the administrative citadel above Corren's Landing, in a wing of the building where the windows were tall and narrow and framed nothing but sky, because the Combine understood that a view of the world gave children ideas. Her father, Aldous Vane, was the Engineer of Diversion—not a title anyone outside the inner halls ever heard spoken, because officially there was no diversion. Officially the river simply ran thin some years, the way rivers did, and the downriver towns' wells going brackish was an act of the desert, not an act of men.

Her father came home smelling of chalk and machine oil. That was her first memory of him, really—not his face, which changed with the light, but the smell: chalk dust on his sleeves from the survey tables, oil under his nails from the great brass models he built in miniature before the Combine built anything true. He kept a model of the whole watershed in his study, a landscape the size of a dining table, carved plaster mountains and a river of actual water pumped through glass channels, and when Mara was small he would let her float paper boats on it. He would open a sluice gate with a tiny brass key and her boat would be swept sideways into a channel that led nowhere, circling a reservoir that fed nothing, and he would watch her face carefully as she realized the boat could not get home.

*Do you see?* he would say. *The water doesn't care where it goes. Only we decide what it means.*

He meant it as a lesson in power. She heard it, even at six, as a confession.

The other children of the Spirehold were taught letters and ledgers and the Catechism of Order—*the Combine provides, the Combine plans, the Combine endures*. Mara was taught those too, but she was also taught the real curriculum, the one served after dinner in her father's study: hydrology, surveying, the reading of gauges, the language of gates and heads and flows. While other girls learned to dance, she learned to calculate the drawdown of a reservoir. While they learned which families to marry into, she learned which towns could be allowed to fail.

Because that was the word they used. *Allowed.* As though drought were a permission the Combine granted or withheld, and the twelve downriver towns—Fenwick, Sallowmere, the salt-poor villages along the old delta—were students whose rations depended on their conduct. When a town protested its dry wells, her father would adjust a gauge, and the adjustment was called *discipline*, and the town would quiet, and everyone at dinner would speak of the harvest as if the rain had simply been kind this year.

Mara was nineteen the first time she saw the source itself.

It was presented as an honor. She was flown up the escarpment in a Combine lift-car, one of the few passengers ever permitted, and walked the last mile through a tunnel her father's crews had bored through living rock, and came out into a cavern where the spring rose out of the earth like a held breath finally released—a pool the size of a village square, impossibly blue, impossibly still, feeding a river that eleven million people downstream called God's mercy or luck or the desert's one kindness, depending on their theology.

And around the pool, caging it, stood the machinery.

Pumps the size of houses. Intake channels barred with iron. A diversion gate through which most of the spring's flow vanished sideways into a covered aqueduct running east, toward the Combine's private territories, the green enclaves where the Directors kept gardens that mocked the desert. What remained—what was *allowed* to remain—trickled down the old riverbed, enough to keep the towns alive but never enough to let them grow, never enough to make them anything but grateful and weak.

Her father stood beside her at the rail, and she watched his face as he looked at the water, and what she saw there was not greed. That was the horror of it, the thing she had spent ten years trying to unlearn. It was pride. He looked at the caged spring the way a father looks at a daughter on her wedding day. *I built this,* his face said. *Out of chaos, order. Out of waste, purpose. No one else could have done it.*

*Mara,* he said, without turning, because he knew she was crying and preferred not to witness it, *one day this will be yours to manage. I have arranged it.*

That was the moment she began planning her escape. Not that night—she was too well-made for that, too much her father's daughter; she planned the way he built weirs, over years, in stages, leaving nothing to chance. But that night was the cornerstone.

* * *

The theft itself had taken four years to arrange and eleven minutes to execute.

By thirty she was Deputy Registrar of Concessions, a position her father had engineered for her with the same care he gave his sluice gates, and the Registrar's vault held the Combine's oldest instruments of claim—and among them, sealed in a lacquered chest older than the Combine itself, the Vessian permits. The Combine had won them in the war nobody named, taken from the drowning archives of a nation erased from every map, and had never used them. Why would they? They already had the water. The permits were a relic, a trophy, a legal ghost.

But ghosts, Mara had learned in the Registrar's office, have legal weight. A concession validly issued by the sovereign authority of Vessia, never revoked, expiring at a fixed date—that was a claim. Whoever held those permits at the spring equinox could file them, and the filing would supersede every arrangement the Combine had built since. The lawyers had argued about it for a decade and concluded it was impossible: the chest was sealed, the archive guarded, the permits worthless without authentication by a Vessian-born hand—which was why, when she found Brannoc's name in an old registry of displaced persons, she had begun, quietly, to build her plan around a forger she'd never met.

She hadn't planned on Odile Ishikawa.

She hadn't planned on any of it, honestly. The break-in had gone clean; the exit had gone wrong; a night-watchman she'd bribed turned honest at the worst hour, and alarms sang through the Spirehold, and she had run across the rooftops of Corren's Landing with a chest strapped to her back while her father's name was shouted through courtyards below. She had sold everything portable within two days—documents, seals, the registrar's codes—to buyers in the salt towns, converting her future into coin and passage. Everything except the chest, which she could not open and could not abandon, because it was the only thing in the world that proved she had ever wanted anything besides what her father wanted for her.

And then a ranger had come out of the saltflats like a story her nursemaid once told, patient as weather, and cornered her at a ruined cistern, and instead of turning her in had opened the chest with her and gone pale at what was inside.

She had watched him go pale and thought: *he understands it faster than I did, and he has no father teaching him, and he is afraid of it anyway.* And she had felt, for the first time since the rooftop, something she refused to name because naming it would make it a debt.

* * *

Now she sat by the dying fire with the permit in her hands, and Odile came back up the bank from checking the lines, moving quiet as he always moved, and she knew he had seen her sitting with it and chose to sit down beside her anyway.

"You should sleep," he said.

"So should you."

"I sleep in shifts." He nodded at the paper. "You've read that one a hundred times. It doesn't change."

"It changes every time." She folded it, unfolded it. "That's the problem with documents. They're mirrors."

Odile said nothing. That was his gift, she was learning—he let silence do the work that other men did with questions. She hated him a little for it. She told him anyway, because the fire was low and the river was loud and lying had gotten very heavy to carry.

"I wasn't stealing those permits to free the water," she said. "You should know that before tomorrow. Before the rapids. If I die in the white water and you're standing on the bank wondering who I was—I want you to know who I was."

"Tell me," he said.

"I was stealing them to buy my way out." The words came easier than she expected, the way a tooth comes out easier than it should. "My father is Aldous Vane. The Engineer of Diversion. There is no diversion, officially—but I grew up watching him close gates on towns like Fenwick until they knelt. I grew up floating paper boats on a model of the river and learning exactly which channel led nowhere. The permits were my escape plan, Odile. Authenticate them, sell them to the highest bidder—there are powers east of the Combine who would pay a fortune for a legal claim on the source—and vanish. New name, new coast, new life. I never intended to burn anything. I never intended to free anything. I intended to *leave*, the same way my father taught me: by controlling the flow."

The fire cracked. Downstream, an owl asked its one question of the dark.

"I'm telling you because Brannoc thinks I'm a Vessian patriot, and you think I'm a thief with a conscience, and both of you are building something on top of me that isn't true. If we're going to run the unmapped water tomorrow, I want the crew to know what it's carrying." She looked at him finally. "So. Now you know. The girl who ran the water ran it for herself."

Odile was quiet for a long time. Long enough that she started composing her exit from the conversation, the graceful withdrawal, the packing of her bedroll—

"I chased you across eighty miles of salt," he said, "because stopping was the only thing I've ever been afraid of."

She blinked. "What?"

"I don't talk about it. Maybe I will someday, maybe not. But here's what I'll say tonight: everybody in this crew is running from something, and none of us gets to decide what another person's running *from* counts as. Brannoc's running toward a dead country. You're running away from a live one. I'm—" he turned his hands palm-up, as if showing her they were empty, "—I'm just running. The question isn't what we were when we started. The question is what the river makes us by the time it lets us off."

"That's not grace," she said. "That's just deferral."

"No," he said, and smiled, and it was the first time she'd seen him smile all the way. "Grace is when someone hears the worst thing about you and doesn't flinch. I didn't flinch. Check."

She checked. He hadn't. Something in her chest that had been clenched since the rooftop of Corren's Landing loosened by one degree, which was not freedom, but was the direction freedom lived in.

"The permits," he said, standing, offering her nothing but the fact of it. "We'll decide together what they're for. All three of us. That's the crew's law, if you'll have it."

"If I'll have it," she repeated, and found she was smiling too, tiredly, against her own policy. "All right, ranger. Crew's law."

He went to his bedroll. She stayed by the fire a while longer, the permit soft in her fingers, the river talking its endless talk below.

And that was when she saw it—downstream, where the black water bent around the bar, a shape caught in the shallows that had not been there at dusk. Pale. Turning slowly. The river, patient as her father's machines, giving something up.

She stood, and reached for Odile's shoulder, and the night tilted toward morning.
