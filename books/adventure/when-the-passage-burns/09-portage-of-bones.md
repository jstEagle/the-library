## 9. Portage of Bones

The gorge announced itself before we saw it—a sound like a held breath, the river's voice swallowed into stone. For half a day the water had been running fast and shallow over shelves of red rock, chattering the way water does when it's in a hurry, and then all at once it went quiet, and I knew we'd reached the place no chart had ever bothered to name.

I beached the raft on a gravel bar the color of old rust and stood a long time looking up at the walls.

They rose sheer on either side, close enough that a strong man with a rope could almost touch both, striped in bands of ochre and ash where centuries of flood had painted them. The sky above was a ribbon of hard blue. Somewhere up there, hawks turned on the thermals, indifferent as gods. Down here the air was cool and mineral-smelling, and it carried a wrongness I couldn't place until Brannoc said it for me.

"Smell that?" The old man had gone gray around the mouth. "That's not river smell."

He was right. Underneath the wet stone and the green tang of algae there was something sweeter, older—the smell of things that had been dead a long time and dried instead of rotted. Desert air does that. It keeps what it takes.

Mara crouched at the waterline, trailing her fingers through the current like she could read it. "No way through," she said. Not a question. She'd grown up with engineers; she knew what a canyon did to a river, and what a river did to anyone foolish enough to argue.

"No way through," I agreed. "So we go over."

* * *

We'd scouted the portage trail the evening before, Mara and I, climbing the talus slope on the north bank while Brannoc dozed against a boulder with his hat over his face. The trail existed—barely. A thread of a path stitched along the rim, maybe four miles of it, dropping back to the river below the gorge. Someone had cut it once. Whoever they were, they hadn't come back this way, because the path was grown over with saltbush and broken by slides, and in places it simply stopped, as if the mountain had decided mid-conversation that it was done talking.

But it was the second thing we found, higher up, that made me sit down on a rock and stay there longer than I meant to.

Bones.

Not scattered, not weathered into anonymity the way desert bones usually are. Arranged. Laid out along the rim in rows, or what had been rows before the wind and the years pulled them apart—rib cages facing the canyon, skulls set upright on stones like men who had died watching the water. Dozens of them. Maybe more than a hundred. The dry air had cured their gear along with them: leather straps gone stiff as iron, canvas packs bleached white, rifle barrels furred with orange rust. And on every pack, every strap, every shield-shaped plate of corroded metal, the same mark stamped deep enough to survive even here.

A circle crossed by three lines. The seal of the Combine.

"The first expedition," Mara said quietly. She didn't touch anything. She stood with her arms wrapped around herself, looking down the rows like a woman reading her own name on a list of the dead. "My father used to talk about it. He called it *the survey*. Forty years ago. They sent two hundred men upriver to find the source." Her voice went flat. "He never mentioned that none of them came home."

"Maybe he didn't know," I said.

"He knew." She said it without heat, which somehow made it worse. "He knew everything about that river. That's how you build a diversion—you learn where the water goes so you can teach it not to." She turned away from the bones. "Come on. We should get back before dark."

I looked one last time at the nearest skull, its jaw gone, its eye sockets full of pale sand. A man who had climbed out of the gorge to die in the open rather than lie where the river couldn't see him. Or maybe just a man too tired to climb any farther. Out here it was hard to tell the difference between a choice and an exhaustion.

I marked the trail in my head the way my mother taught me—notch the landmarks, count your steps, trust nothing twice—and followed Mara down.

* * *

Morning came cold off the water. We broke camp in the dark by habit, and by first light the raft was lashed into its carrying frame—two pine poles under the hull, crossbars lashed with rawhide, packs distributed among us according to weight and stubbornness. Brannoc insisted on taking his share, and his share included the chest. I let him have it because arguing with him cost more energy than carrying it myself would have, and because I understood what the chest meant to him. It was the only piece of Vessia left above water. A man doesn't hand that to someone else's shoulders.

The climb out of the gorge took two hours. The talus shifted under our boots like something alive and resentful, and twice the whole slope groaned and settled, and we froze and listened to the mountain decide whether it cared enough to kill us. It didn't. By midmorning we were on the rim, the river a thin green vein far below, and the portage stretched ahead of us across a plateau of cracked clay and wind-bent brush.

That's when the bones began again.

On the flats they were worse than on the rim, because on the flats there was nowhere to look away to. They lay along the whole length of the old trail, exactly where the trail was easiest to walk, as if the dead had chosen the route for us. Rib cages like the frames of small boats. Boots with feet still in them. A helmet fused to a skull by forty years of rust, so that the man and his armor had become one object, inseparable now, neither able to leave the other.

Brannoc walked slowly between them, and I watched him change.

I'd known him six days, and in six days I'd learned that Brannoc's age was mostly performance—that beneath the wheezing and the complaints about his knees was a frame built for work and a will built for siege. But the plateau took the performance away from him. With every row of bones we passed, he shrank a little, and his steps shortened, and his breathing got that wet, torn quality that I'd heard once before, in a man dying of a fever in a tollhouse loft, though Brannoc wasn't sick. He was grieving. He was grieving a hundred strangers because they had died trying to reach the place he'd been born too late to see, and grief is heavier than any pack.

By noon he was stopping every fifty paces. By early afternoon, every twenty.

"We'll rest," I said, the third time, and he waved me off with a hand that shook.

"I've carried loads since before your father could walk, boy. Don't fuss."

"I'm not fussing. I'm counting." I nodded back down the trail. "You've stopped eleven times in an hour. At this pace we don't make the drop-in before dark, and I am not spending a night on this plateau."

Mara, ahead of us with her own load and her own silence, glanced back but said nothing. She'd been quiet since the rim. Quiet in the way of someone recalculating everything.

Brannoc glared at me, opened his mouth to tell me exactly what he thought of being counted, and then his knees simply ended the argument for him. He went down sideways, slow, almost polite about it, the chest rolling free of his shoulder, and he caught himself on his hands in the clay between two rib cages and stayed there, head hanging, breathing like a bellows with a split seam.

"Brannoc—"

"Don't." The word came out crushed. "Don't say it."

I knelt beside him. Up close I could see what the morning had done: the sweat standing on his forehead in the cold air, the blue tinge under his lips, the tremor in his forearms where the muscles had given everything they had and started borrowing from somewhere else. He was seventy if he was a day, and he'd carried a chest of a drowned nation four miles across a graveyard, and his body had finally presented him with the bill.

"You can't carry it anymore," I said gently. "And you can't walk it alone. So here's what happens instead."

He raised his head. His eyes were furious and wet and proud, all three at once, and I recognized the expression because I'd worn it myself, once, on a night I'd rather not remember, when someone had offered me help I hadn't earned and couldn't refuse.

"Get up on my back," I said.

"Absolutely not. I will crawl before I—"

"It's four miles, old man. Crawl it and the vultures will have you before sundown, and then who finishes the map?" I crouched lower, offering my shoulders. "Besides. You're lighter than the chest."

That got a sound out of him that might have been a laugh if laughter could bleed. He swore at me in Vessian—I didn't know the words, but the grammar of cursing is universal—and then he gripped my collar with both hands, and I stood up with him, and the world settled onto my back.

He weighed less than I expected. That frightened me more than the weight would have.

* * *

I won't pretend those miles were easy, but I'll tell you what they were: they were the truest thing I'd done in years.

The plateau unrolled ahead of us, bone-lined and merciless, and I put my head down and walked. Brannoc rode high on my back with his arms looped under mine and the chest strapped across us both, and after the first mile he stopped apologizing and started talking, low, near my ear, in the voice of a man who has decided that if he must be carried through a field of the dead, he will at least perform the office properly.

"That one," he said, as we passed a skeleton still wearing the rotted remains of an officer's sash. "He'd have been the one who kept the muster roll. See the ink case? They carried ink. Paper, ink, surveying chains. They came to *map*, Odile. Two hundred men with instruments and good intentions, and the Combine wrote them off in a season and paved the story over." His breath caught. "Nobody ever came to bury them. You understand? Forty years, and nobody came."

"Somebody came today," I said.

He was quiet a moment. Then his grip tightened on my collar, and he said, in a voice scraped nearly bare, "Your mother's people. The ones who told you the stories. Were they river people?"

"Ferry folk. Salvagers. Whatever paid." I stepped over a fallen rifle, its stock gone silver with sun. "She told me about Vessia the way you tell children about monsters—except backwards. A country that was real, and the monster was the mapmakers who erased it. She made me swear the seal to memory when I was seven. Said someday I'd see it and I'd need to know what it meant."

"And now you've seen it."

"Now I've seen it."

Behind us, Mara's footsteps kept steady time with ours. She hadn't spoken since the rim, but she hadn't drifted either. When I stumbled once on a hidden root, she was there before I finished falling, one hand flat against Brannoc's spine, holding us both until my legs remembered their business. Then she dropped back without a word. That was Mara. She gave help the way she took it—quickly, silently, and with an escape route already planned.

Somewhere past the third mile, Brannoc's talking slowed, and his head grew heavy against my shoulder, and I realized he'd fallen asleep on my back—actually asleep, in the middle of a graveyard, trusting me with his unconscious weight the way you can't fake trusting someone. I adjusted my grip and kept walking, and something happened in my chest that I didn't have a name for yet. It felt like a door I'd braced shut for fifteen years swinging open an inch, just an inch, letting in a draft of something warm.

I had spent my whole adult life moving because staying meant being found, and being found meant the debt coming due, and the debt coming due meant the name I buried would be dug up and used against everyone foolish enough to stand near me. Moving was mercy. Moving was arithmetic. I'd told myself that so long I'd believed it.

But walking four miles with a dying old man on my back through the bones of two hundred strangers, I did the math differently. Those men on the plateau had moved together or died alone, and the ones who died alone had died first. That was the whole lesson, written in ribs and rust. The Combine sent two hundred men and mourned none of them. I had three people, counting myself, and I was beginning to understand that I would burn a great deal down before I let the number shrink.

* * *

We reached the drop-in an hour before sunset.

The trail descended through a break in the rim, switchbacking down red rock to a wide eddy below the gorge where the river gathered itself again, deep and green and slow, as if it had spent the portage resting too. I lowered Brannoc onto a flat stone at the water's edge and he woke instantly, the way old soldiers wake, one hand already reaching for the chest to confirm it was safe. It was. It had never left us.

Mara was already at the raft, checking lashings, and I went to help her, and for a while the three of us worked in the easy silence of people who have shared a weight. We relaunched as the light went copper. The current took us gentle at first, then quicker, and the walls of the new canyon closed around us, and the bones were behind us, and none of us spoke of them, and all of us were thinking of them.

It was Mara who broke the silence, near full dark, as we made our first camp on a sandbar and Brannoc slept—really slept—under a blanket by the fire.

"Odile." She sat across the flames, sharpening a knife she didn't need to sharpen. "Those men. My father sent them. He was young then, but he was there—he helped plan the survey. And when they didn't come back, he helped write the report that said they'd deserted." The knife whispered along the stone. "I keep thinking about that word. *Deserted.* Two hundred men, and the report said they abandoned *the Combine*." Her eyes came up and caught the firelight. "Standing on that plateau today, it looked the other way round to me."

I fed a branch to the fire and watched it catch.

"Then you know what I know," I said.

"I know more than that." She turned the knife over, and her voice dropped to something careful. "There's a boat behind us. Has been since yesterday. Lantern light on the water, far back, keeping pace. I didn't want to say it in front of him."

The fire popped. Downriver, the dark canyon waited, and somewhere in it, whatever the Combine had sent was waiting too—or following, patient as the water.

"How far back?" I asked.

Mara looked upstream, into the black.

"Close enough," she said, "that tonight we'd better sleep in shifts."
