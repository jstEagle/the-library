## 10. The Combine's Offer

The envoy came at dusk, when the light on the water turned to hammered brass and the river ran slow and gold between the reed banks. He came alone, in a flat-bottomed skiff painted the gray of official paper, and he did not pole so much as glide, as though the current itself had been instructed to carry him.

Mara saw him first. She was mending a tear in the raft's portage wrap with waxed thread, her fingers working by habit while her mind wandered the way it always did now—back to the Combine's halls, back to her father's study with its maps of rivers that existed on no public chart—and when she looked up and saw that skiff, her hands went still.

She knew the boat. She knew the man poling it, too: Corvane Dessel, who had once stood three steps behind her father at every function she'd ever attended, holding his hat and his silence in equal measure. He had taught her cards when she was nine. He had let her win exactly twice.

"Odile," she said quietly. "We have company."

Odile was at the fire, coaxing tea from a tin that had seen better decades. Brannoc sat against a boulder with his bad leg stretched out, whittling something that might have been a bird or might have been nothing at all. All three of them turned to look downriver, and Mara watched the recognition move across Odile's face like weather—the tightening around his eyes, the way his hand drifted, casual as breathing, to the knife at his belt.

"That's a Combine hull," he said.

"It's worse than that." Mara set down the needle. "That's my father's man."

* * *

Corvane beached the skiff with a soft grinding of sand and stepped out onto the shingle as if he were stepping into a drawing room. He was older than Mara remembered—gray had colonized his temples, and there were new lines around his mouth—but he wore the same dove-colored coat, cut in the Vessian style that the Combine had adopted after they'd swallowed the nation whole, wearing its dead like borrowed clothes.

"Lady Vess-Mara." He inclined his head. The old title, the one from before her father had renamed himself for the Combine's registries. It landed on her like a stone dropped down a well; she heard it fall a long way before it hit anything.

"It's just Mara," she said. "There's no lady anymore. There hasn't been since you people erased the country that made me one."

"People," Corvane said mildly, "is a strong word for what I am. I'm one man in a boat, come to talk business." His gaze moved past her to the chest, which sat where it always sat, lashed beneath the oilskin near the raft's center, and Mara felt the weight of his attention settle on it the way a hawk's shadow settles on a hare. "Though I confess the business is not complicated."

"We're listening," Odile said. He hadn't moved from the fire. The kettle steamed behind him, absurdly domestic. "Tea first?"

"I'd be honored."

They sat on driftwood logs around the small flame, four people and a chest full of dead nation, and Corvane accepted his tea with genuine courtesy and drank it without flinching at whatever the river had done to the taste. For a moment—only a moment—Mara could almost believe they were old friends catching up on lost years. That was the thing about Combine men like Corvane. They could make the cage feel like a parlor.

"You've had an eventful season," he began. "A theft in Saltmere. A chase across the flats. A dead courier pulled from the shallows—" his eyes flicked to Brannoc, then back, "—and a journey upriver that no chart has ever survived. My employers find it all very impressive. They also find it inconvenient."

"The Combine can afford inconvenience," Mara said. "It owns everything else."

Her father's man smiled, and it was the smile she remembered from her childhood, patient and faintly sad, the smile of a man watching a clever child say something clever and doomed. "Your father misses you, Mara."

The words went through her like cold water through cloth. She had prepared herself for gold, for threats, for the smooth recitation of terms. She had not prepared herself for that.

"He has a strange way of showing it," she managed. "Sending riders to shoot at us at Fenwick Bend. Sending gunboats into the canyon country."

"He didn't send those." Corvane set down his cup. "I'll be honest with you, because honesty is what I'm paid for and because you deserve better than the version I'd give anyone else. Your father disavowed you six months ago. Officially. There are documents. But unofficially—" he spread his hands, "—unofficially, a man does not stop being a father because a registry says so. He asked me to find you before others did. Others who do not have your childhood in their hearts."

"And what does he want?" Odile asked, quiet and level.

Corvane turned to him, and his manner changed—the warmth folding away like a fan, revealing the ledger underneath. "Amnesty. Full restoration of status for Lady Vess-Mara, her record cleansed, her name returned to the rolls. A stipend. A house in the capital, if she wants walls again." He paused. "And for the two gentlemen accompanying her: five thousand crowns apiece, paid in minted coin, plus safe conduct out of Combine territory and a formal pardon for all prior offenses. In exchange, the chest. Unopened contents irrelevant—we only require the permits themselves, delivered intact, before the equinox."

The fire popped. Somewhere upstream a bird cried out over the water, long and lonely.

"Five thousand," Brannoc said at last. His voice was gravel and smoke. "That's real money. That's a farm in the hill country and never working another day."

"It is," Corvane agreed.

"It's also blood money," Brannoc said, "for a drowned country. Those permits aren't paper, boy. They're the last legal breath Vessia ever drew. You want to buy a corpse's signature and call it commerce."

"I want," Corvane said gently, "to keep everyone at this fire alive past the spring. Because I have seen the other men my employers would send, and they do not bring tea."

* * *

They talked until the light died. Corvane laid out the terms twice more, answered every question with the same unflappable patience, and refused, with equal patience, to answer the only question that mattered: what the Combine intended to do with the source once the claim was filed.

"That's above my station," he said.

"Then you're selling us a locked door," Odile said, "and pretending there's nothing behind it."

"I'm selling you a door that opens away from a firing squad. What's behind it is your father's concern, Lady Mara. Not mine."

When he finally rose to go, brushing driftwood dust from his coat, he paused beside Mara and lowered his voice—not enough that the others couldn't hear, but enough that the words became something intimate, a hand on the shoulder rather than a proclamation.

"He keeps your drawing on his desk," Corvane said. "The one you made when you were seven. Of the river, with the fish in it. He looks at it every night. Whatever you think of him, girl, know that he grieves."

Then he pushed his skiff back into the current and glided away into the dark, and the river closed over his wake as though he had never been.

* * *

Nobody spoke for a long time.

Mara sat with her arms wrapped around her knees, staring at the coals, feeling the envoy's words work through her like splinters seeking bone. Her father grieved. Her father kept a child's crayon river on his desk beside the ledgers that recorded how much water he stole from a dozen dying towns. Both things were true. That was the trap of him—he was never simply a monster, and so she had spent her whole life drowning in the space between what he was and what he should have been.

"Well," Brannoc said heavily. "Someone has to say it, so it may as well be an old fool. Five thousand crowns."

"Brannoc." Odile's voice carried a warning.

"No, hear me." The old forger leaned forward, firelight carving deep the lines of his face. "I love this crew. I love this mad river. But I was born under a flag that doesn't exist, and I have buried more friends than I can count, and I am fifty-nine years old with a leg that screams when it rains. Five thousand crowns is a warm room. It's medicine. It's dying in a bed instead of in a gorge full of Combine bones." He looked at Mara, and his eyes were wet in the firelight. "You'd get your name back, girl. Your father. A home."

"My father ordered me erased," Mara said. "Whatever Corvane carries in his breast pocket, the registry still says I don't exist. Amnesty from the Combine is a leash with a gold collar. And the moment we hand over those permits, the diversion stays sealed forever, and every town downriver—from here to the Saltmere flats—keeps dying of thirst a little more each year so the capital can have its fountains." She heard her own voice rise and couldn't stop it. "You'd sell the water. You'd sell the *water*, Brannoc, for a bed to die in."

"I'd sell paper," Brannoc shot back, "to save three lives I can see, instead of spending them on a promise I can't! You think burning through canyon country with gunboats behind us is a plan? We're three people and a raft!"

"Four," Odile said.

They both turned. He had been silent so long Mara had half forgotten him, sitting at the edge of the fire with his cup untouched, watching the coals with the expression he wore when he was far away from all of them, in whatever country of the past he visited when things grew quiet.

"There are four of us," he repeated, "and the promise is real. I've seen the drowned towns' names in the courier's map margins. I've seen what the Combine's barges left in that gorge. If we sell, we live comfortable and everything stays broken. If we run the river, maybe we die—but maybe the water runs free, and maybe that's worth more than any of us." He looked up, and his gaze found Mara's across the flames. "But I won't decide it alone. That's not how a crew works. So we vote. Tonight or tomorrow, but honestly, and no one punished for their heart."

Brannoc snorted and turned away toward the dark, shoulders hunched, and the sound he made wasn't quite anger—it was grief wearing anger's coat, and Mara recognized it because she knew it intimately. He wanted to go home. He wanted to see the source before he died, she knew that too, and the terrible arithmetic of the offer was that it priced his dream against his life and called it fair.

"I need to walk," she said, and stood.

"Mara." Odile rose too. "Don't go far. Not tonight."

"I'm not running to them, if that's what you think."

"It's not what I think." He hesitated, and in the firelight his face was open in a way she rarely saw, the restless hunter stripped down to something younger and more afraid. "I think a man offered you your whole life back tonight, and I watched you refuse it, and I don't know yet whether that was courage or whether part of you is still standing on that beach deciding. Either way—I'd rather not lose you to the dark while I'm guessing."

The words undid something in her. She had expected suspicion. She had earned suspicion. Instead he handed her his doubt like a thing entrusted, and she found she could not carry it carelessly.

"I'm walking to the water," she said. "Ten minutes. Then I'll come back, and tomorrow we vote, and whatever it says—I'll abide it. Even if it's five thousand crowns and a leash."

He nodded once and sat back down, and she walked down the shingle to where the river breathed against the stones.

* * *

The water was black glass under the rising moon. Mara crouched at the edge and let the cold seep through her boots, and she thought about a seven-year-old's drawing of a river with fish in it, pinned above a desk where a man calculated the death of towns, and she thought about Brannoc's wet eyes, and Odile's open face, and the chest sleeping under its oilskin ten paces behind her.

Five thousand crowns. Her name in the rolls. Her father's door, unlocked.

And behind the door: the fountains running, the diversion sealed, the source caged forever behind Combine machinery, and every promise she had made on this river reduced to ash that someone else got to sweep.

She heard footsteps on the shingle and didn't turn. Only two people could have followed her, and the uneven drag of a bad leg told her which one.

"I'm not changing my mind," Brannoc said, easing himself down onto a rock beside her with a grunt. "I still think we should take the money."

"I know."

"But I wanted to say—" he worked at it, an old man wrestling language older than himself, "—that when I demanded passage to the source, I wasn't thinking about what it would cost anyone else. I was thinking about a boy who got marched out of Vessia with nothing but his mother's sewing scissors. I've been owed something for forty years, girl, and I grabbed at the first thing that looked like payment." He was quiet a moment. The river filled the silence. "Your refusal shamed me. That's not your fault. But I wanted you to know I felt it."

Mara looked at him—at the bent shape of him against the moonlit water, this forged-paper ghost of a drowned nation—and felt the fracture in the crew ache like a tooth. They were not broken. Not yet. But the envoy's gold lay between them like a crack in ice, and somewhere downstream the Combine was already counting the days to the equinox.

"Come back to the fire," she said softly. "Odile makes terrible tea, but he makes a lot of it."

Brannoc huffed—almost a laugh—and let her help him up.

Behind them, far downriver, a lantern kindled on the black water. Then another. Then a third, strung out in a patient line, moving slowly upstream against the current, toward the firelight, toward the chest, toward the vote they would hold at dawn with the enemy already in sight.

Mara counted the lights twice, and her mouth went dry.

"Brannoc," she whispered. "Get Odile. They're not waiting for our answer."
