## 11. Night of the Split Vote

The fire was small on purpose. Small fires tell fewer lies about where you are.

Odile crouched at its edge and fed it a stick of greasewood, watching the flame take the wood with a sound like a whispered secret. Above them the canyon walls leaned together, black against a sky salted with stars, and somewhere below the river went on talking to itself in the dark—that low, ceaseless mutter of water that had become, over the past weeks, the closest thing he had to a conscience. It never slept. Neither, lately, did he.

Three bedrolls around one fire. That was all his crew amounted to now, and the arithmetic of it sat wrong in his chest. Brannoc lay propped against a boulder with his wounded arm bound tight across his chest, whittling at nothing—a sliver of cottonwood that had long since stopped being anything, though his knife kept moving anyway, the way an old man's hands do when they're afraid of what they'll do if they stop. Mara stood apart, at the lip of the bank, her back to them, watching the black water as if it might carry an answer down from upstream.

And between them, on a flat stone where everyone could see it, the chest.

It looked smaller than it had in the ruined cistern, smaller than it had in Brannoc's workshop, smaller than it had strapped to the raft through the Rapids of the Unmapped while gunboat rounds chewed the water to lace around them. Smaller than the thing that had cost Brannoc his blood. Just a box of salt-stained cedar with a drowned nation's seal pressed into its lid—a river coiled around a star—and inside it, papers. Permits. Concessions stamped by clerks who had been dead or scattered for a generation, granting whoever held them the right to file claim on the source of the river before the spring equinox.

Four days from now, the equinox would come, and the permits would die with it.

"You keep looking at it like it's going to bite you," Brannoc said without looking up from his whittling.

"It's bitten enough people already."

"Then let it bite someone rich." The old forger set down the knife and the useless sliver of wood both. "That's all I'm saying, boy. All I've been saying since the envoy's men left their gold on the table and rode off to count how long it takes stubbornness to bleed out."

Odile didn't answer. He'd learned that trick from the desert itself: when the wind starts howling, get low and wait. But the wind tonight was coming from Mara, and he could feel it building at his back like weather.

She turned from the bank. In the firelight her face was half copper, half shadow, and her eyes had the flat shine he'd learned to dread—the look she got when she'd already made up her mind and was only waiting for the rest of them to catch up.

"Say it," Odile said quietly.

"I'm saying we sell." She came forward and crouched opposite him, the chest between them like a border neither would cross. "The envoy offered amnesty. Real amnesty—signed, sealed, my father's own man carrying the offer. And forty thousand in scrip convertible anywhere the Combine trades, which is everywhere, which is *everywhere*, Odile." Her voice stayed level, but he could hear the effort of it, the way you can hear ice creaking under a traveler's weight. "Forty thousand buys boats. Buys passage out of this basin for every downriver family that's been drinking dust since the Combine closed the sluices. Buys Brannoc a doctor for that arm instead of riverbank poultices."

"My arm's fine," Brannoc said.

"Your arm is rotting, old man, and you know it."

"Rotting slow," Brannoc amended. "I'm Vessian. We do everything slow. It's why they drowned us."

Mara ignored him. She kept her eyes on Odile, and there it was under the flatness—not greed, not exactly. Something rawer. He'd seen it before, in the mirror, in the years after he'd stopped using his mother's name: the look of a person who has been hunted so long that safety starts to look like a country, and any map leading there seems worth the toll.

"You think I don't know what that ledger says?" she went on. "I read it same as you. Twelve towns starving because my father's machinery drinks the headwaters. I *know*. I grew up in the house where it was decided." Her jaw tightened. "But burning the permits doesn't open the gates. Selling them doesn't either—but selling them keeps us alive long enough to try something that might. Amnesty means we walk into Combine territory wearing their own seal instead of a target. You want to free the water? Fine. Free it from *inside*."

"That's not freeing it," Odile said. "That's renting it back."

The words came out quieter than he meant, and heavier. He watched them land.

"The permits expire in four days," he said. "You heard the envoy's clerk say it, same as me. After the equinox, the claim dies unfiled, and the source belongs to whoever's standing on it. If we sell, we're not keeping the water alive—we're handing the Combine a legal knife and asking them to please only use it kindly. They've had thirty years of kind. Look at Fenwick Bend. Look at the portage." He nodded downstream, toward the gorge they'd crossed on foot, past the bones of the first expedition, men who had come to map the source and been left where they fell because maps are dangerous things. "The ledger doesn't need our help. What it needs is witnesses. And witnesses don't take the money."

Brannoc made a sound in his throat—half laugh, half cough. "Witnesses. Listen to him. He sounds like my grandmother, may the river keep her. She used to say a nation isn't land. It's people who remember out loud." He picked the knife back up, turned the sliver of wood. "I told you my price at the tollhouse, girl. I'm riding this river to see the source before I die, and I'll see it free or I'll see it fenced, but I won't see it sold. Not with Vessia's seal on the paper. We were drowned once already. I won't sign the second drowning myself."

"You were three years old when Vessia flooded," Mara said. "You don't even remember it."

"I remember what my mother remembered," Brannoc said simply. "That's how nations survive. Somebody remembers out loud."

* * *

The argument wore on the way arguments do when everyone has already lost—circling, repeating, wearing grooves in itself. Odile laid out the ledger again, the columns of diversion figures, the towns marked with the Combine's own shorthand for *sacrificed*: Darrow Flats, Nine Wells, the string of settlements along the old channel whose names he'd grown up hearing in his mother's stories as places where the water ran sweet. Mara countered with logistics, with numbers, with the cold geometry of survival, and she was good at it—he could admit that, hating it—she'd been raised in the Combine's counting houses and it showed in every sentence, the way a scar shows where the wound healed wrong.

What she never said—what circled underneath everything she did say, unspoken and enormous—was the thing Odile had understood weeks ago, watching her stand watch at night with her face turned upstream: that the offer wasn't forty thousand scrip to her. It was a door back into the house where she'd been born. It was her father's handwriting on a document that said, in effect, *you were never really disavowed; it was all a test, and you passed.*

He knew what it was to want your name back. He'd burned his own name himself, deliberately, and still some nights he reached for it like a tongue reaching for a missing tooth.

So he tried to be gentle, which was a mistake, because gentleness reads as pity to the proud.

"Mara." He waited until she looked at him. "Whatever you think is waiting behind that door—it isn't. I chased you across the saltflats, remember? I saw what they sent after their own daughter. Riders with burn orders. Your father didn't lose you. He *filed* you."

"You don't know anything about my father."

"I know what his envoy offered us. Amnesty for two of us and a chest. He didn't mention you by name, Mara. He mentioned *the courier's effects*. You were inventory."

The fire popped. Somewhere upstream an owl called, once, and got no answer.

Mara stood very slowly. When she spoke again her voice had gone smooth and cold, polished like river stone, and Odile recognized the polish for what it was—the last defense of someone trying not to bleed in front of witnesses.

"Then vote," she said. "You wanted a crew. Crews vote. So vote."

They voted. There was no ceremony to it; Brannoc raised his hand for refusal without even setting down his knife, and Odile raised his own, and that was the whole of it, two hands against one, the fire throwing their shadows huge and wavering against the canyon wall like the ghosts of a much larger crew.

Mara looked at their hands. Then at the chest. Then at Odile, and what was in her face then he would spend many nights afterward taking apart and examining, because he missed it in the moment—missed the hurt under the anger, missed the way her eyes went wet and furious about being wet, missed the single heartbeat where she seemed to be waiting for him to say something else, anything else, some word that would make staying possible.

He didn't find the word. He was tired, and proud in his own way, and certain—certain the way only a man who has never had a crew to lose can be certain—that rightness would hold her the way it held him.

"Then I'm done voting," she said.

She took her bedroll, her knife, her share of the food, and she walked upriver along the bank, into the dark, and none of them followed, because following felt like chasing and chasing felt like admitting they'd been wrong, and pride is a currency that spends easiest in the dark.

Brannoc watched her go. "Boy," he said, after a while.

"Don't."

"She was inventory to them. You just reminded her."

"I know what I—" Odile stopped. Fed the fire another stick. The flame took it greedily, and he hated how warm his hands were when everything else was turning cold. "She'll cool off. She always circles back. She's a hunter; hunters loop their own trail."

"Hunters also walk into other people's snares when they're angry," Brannoc said. "I've forged documents for both kinds."

* * *

Sleep wouldn't come, so Odile gave up pretending and took the middle watch, sitting with his back to a stone, the chest heavy against his knees, the river murmuring its one long syllable below. He thought about his mother's stories—the forbidden ones, told in whispers over the cookfire when he was small, about a nation that lived on the water and wrote its promises in ink that outlasted the flood. He'd thought those stories were gone with her. Then a thief led him across the saltflats to a sealed chest, and here they were, in his lap, demanding things.

Demanding, specifically, that he choose. Loyalty or truth. His crew or the water. He kept turning it over like a stone with something underneath, and every time he lifted it, the thing underneath was the same: *why does it have to be a choice? Why can't the truth have a crew?*

The moon cleared the canyon rim a little after midnight and laid a pale road down the middle of the river. It was the light that finally showed him what his ears had been too dull to catch: no owl answering the earlier call. No night birds at all. The bank upstream had gone quiet in the particular way of a place where men are holding their breath.

He was on his feet before he knew he'd moved.

"Mara's watch," he breathed. "She never came back for second watch."

Brannoc was awake instantly—old men sleep like knives in drawers, ready. "How long?"

"An hour. Maybe more." Odile was already moving along the bank, low, following the pale road of moonlight, reading the ground the way his mother taught him to read sand. Her footprints ran clear along the firm clay above the waterline, quick and angry, stride long. Then shorter. Then—

Then they stopped making sense. A scuff. A heel-drag. A patch of clay churned by more boots than hers, boot prints with the squared heel and deep tread of Combine issue, and beside them, pressed sharp into the mud where someone had knelt, the round brass butt-plate of a carbine.

A strip of dark cloth hung from a mesquite branch, torn, still swinging faintly.

Odile stood very still in the moonlight and felt the guilt arrive ahead of the fear, the way pain arrives ahead of the knowledge of the wound. *Two hands against one,* he thought. *We outvoted her straight into their arms.*

Upstream, maybe half a mile, a lantern blinked twice in the dark below the ruins of an old weir—signal, acknowledgment, signal—and he heard, very faintly, carried on the water the Combine had spent thirty years teaching to lie, the sound of men laughing around a fire.

They hadn't killed her. Killing the courier's effects would waste the leverage.

"Brannoc," Odile said softly, when the old man caught up, breathing hard, knife already in his hand. "Get the raft. Get the chest. Keep it out of lantern-light."

"And you?"

Odile pulled his own blade and tested its edge with his thumb, watching the weir ruins, where the firelight moved and the laughter rose and fell.

"I'm going to go steal back the vote," he said.
