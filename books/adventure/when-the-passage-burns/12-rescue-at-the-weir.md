## 12. Rescue at the Weir

They took my boots first.

That was how I knew it was serious. The envoy's men had been almost gentle on the trail—rope around my wrists, a hood that smelled of lamp oil, a voice in my ear saying *no harm, no harm, the Master wants you home*—but at the weir camp they stripped me of everything, even the cracked leather boots I'd stolen from a dead courier two years ago, and set them by the fire to burn.

Boots meant they didn't expect me to walk anywhere again.

I sat where they put me, in the lee of the old weir house with its sagging timber roof and its walls furred white with salt bloom, and I watched my boots curl and blacken, and I thought: *you fool.* I thought it in my father's voice, because his voice was the only one that had ever lived inside my head long enough to make itself comfortable there. *You had one job, Mara. You had one thing you were good at, and you let a saltflat ranger and a dying forger talk you out of it.*

Except that wasn't true either. Nobody had talked me out of anything. I'd walked away from the envoy's fire on my own two unbooted feet, straight into their arms, because Odile had looked at me across the flames and said *we don't sell people* and I couldn't bear the weight of what he meant. It was easier to be taken. Easier to be a prisoner than a traitor.

The camp was small—eight men, maybe nine, and the envoy himself, a grey man named Corvane whose voice never rose above the level of a man reading accounts aloud. They'd built their fire against the weir's old stonework, where the river used to spill through the sluice gates before the Combine sealed them, and the stones still wept a little, a dark damp line down the mortar like an old scar that ached in cold weather. Beyond the firelight the desert went on forever, flat and starless under high cloud, and somewhere out in all that dark were two men with a raft and a chest full of paper and no idea what they'd lost.

Or maybe they did know. Maybe Odile was already counting the days until he could stop looking over his shoulder for me.

A guard brought me water in a tin cup. He was young, with a sunburn peeling off his nose in strips, and he wouldn't meet my eyes. "Drink," he said. "You'll want your strength."

"For what?"

He looked at me then, just once, and whatever he saw in my face made him flinch. He set the cup down between my bound hands and walked away fast, like a man leaving a room where something bad was about to happen.

I drank anyway. Fear is no reason to be careless with water; my father taught me that too, along with everything else.

* * *

Corvane came at moonrise.

He didn't sit. He stood at the edge of the firelight with his hands folded behind him, a lean grey heron of a man, and he studied me the way my father studied flow rates—not with cruelty, not with kindness, but with the flat attention of someone solving a problem. I'd seen him at Combine functions when I was a girl, always at the edge of my father's circle, always holding the ledger.

"You ran well," he said. "Three years. Your father said you would. He built you to run."

"Where is he?"

"Upstream." A pause, precise as a knife laid on silk. "At the source. Where he has always been. Where you were going, I think, with those permits." He tilted his head. "You should know that we found Brannoc's raft tracks at the second bend. We know exactly where they are. We could have taken them tonight. We chose not to."

The cold that went through me then had nothing to do with the desert night. "Why not?"

"Because your father asked me a question," Corvane said, "and I am here to give you his answer. He asked whether you had seen the permits. Whether you understood what they were worth. And I told him yes—I told him you'd held them, slept beside them, run the river with them strapped to your back." He unfolded his hands. "And he said, *then she knows too much.*"

The fire popped. Somewhere beyond the weir wall, wind moved through dead reeds with a sound like whispering.

"He said—" My mouth had gone dry. "Say it plainly."

"He said you were to be disposed of. Quietly, before the equinox, so that no claimant could ever say the Vessian heir's daughter had touched the documents. Your name comes off the rolls tomorrow. By the next census you will never have existed." Corvane delivered it the way he delivered everything, without inflection, a line item in a ledger. "I want you to understand that this is not punishment. Your father does not punish. He optimizes. You are simply the most efficient solution to a problem he created."

I laughed. I couldn't help it—it came out of me like something breaking, a short ugly sound that made the young guard look over from the fire. Because of course. Of course. All those years inside the Combine compound, learning ciphers and current tables and which of my father's colleagues smiled with teeth, and I had told myself I was stealing the permits as an escape plan, my escape, my one clever thing—and the whole time I had been nothing but a variable in his arithmetic, and now the equation had solved itself and the answer was a ditch somewhere downwind of the weir.

"I'm his daughter," I said. My voice sounded far away, like someone else's.

"You were," Corvane agreed. "Now you're a liability. There's a difference, and your father taught me it." He turned to go, then paused, and for one moment something almost human crossed his face—pity, or its accountant's equivalent. "For what it's worth, I argued for amnesty. Gold, a new name, passage east. He said no. He said—" and here Corvane's voice did change, just barely, taking on the careful flatness of a man quoting scripture he doesn't believe— "*Mara was always going to be water. Best she goes downhill.*"

He left me with the tin cup and the burning boots and the sound of the dead reeds whispering, and I sat in the dark and did not cry, because crying was another thing he'd trained out of me before I was ten, and hated myself for being grateful for it.

* * *

Here is what I learned growing up in the Combine's house above the diversion works: love is a resource allocation problem. My father kept a garden once—a real garden, green things in a walled courtyard fed by a private pipe—and when I was seven I asked him why the garden got water when the towns below the dam were rationed to a bucket a day. He knelt down, put his dry warm hand on my head, and said, *because the garden is ours.* That was the whole lesson. That was the curriculum. Everything else—the cipher drills, the etiquette, the way he never once raised his voice—was homework.

I had spent three years telling myself I'd escaped him. Sitting in the dirt with my wrists raw from rope, I finally understood that I hadn't escaped him at all. I'd only ever been running on his map.

But maps can be burned. I'd watched a chest of them burn once, or part of one, and I remembered what Odile's face looked like in that light.

* * *

They came an hour before dawn, which told me everything about who they were.

Combine raids happen at midnight, when sentries are drowsy and the fire is low, or at dusk, using the long shadows. An hour before dawn is the worst hour there is—the hour when the night watch has stopped believing anyone will come and started believing in their own beds. Only desperate men raid then. Or men who have decided that the person in the ropes is worth the risk, which amounts to the same thing.

I heard the guard dog first—or rather, I heard the dog stop. One moment the young sentry's cur was grumbling at the dark, and the next there was silence with a shape in it, and then the sentry himself made a small wet sound and folded down out of sight behind the weir wall, and I was on my feet with my heart slamming before my mind caught up.

Then the world came apart. Brannoc's old river-horn went off somewhere in the reeds—a long braying note that every Combine man in camp had been trained to answer with alarm, because the horn meant gunboats, meant inspectors, meant the Master's own authority—and eight soldiers boiled up out of their bedrolls toward a threat that didn't exist while two shadows came over the weir wall from the opposite side, quiet as spilled ink.

Odile reached me first. He didn't say anything—he put a knife in my bonds and cut, and his hands were steady, and when my arms came free they shook so hard I nearly dropped the rope, and he caught my wrists and held them a moment, feeling the tremor, reading it.

"They told you," he said. Not a question. He'd seen my face.

"How did you know where—"

"Mara." His grip tightened. "You walked into their fire to keep us from having to choose. You think I wouldn't come get you?" Something moved behind his eyes, quick and fierce and gone. "We're leaving. Can you run?"

"My boots are—"

"Brannoc." Odile whistled, low, and the old forger materialized out of the smoke carrying a bundle. He dropped it at my feet: boots. Not mine. Someone else's, pulled off some unconscious soldier, too big by a size and stuffed with straw at the toe.

"Borrowed," Brannoc rasped, grinning through his grey beard. "Consider it interest on what they owe you."

I got the boots on. They smelled of someone else's sweat and the fire's smoke, and they were the finest things I had ever worn.

* * *

We went out through the sluice tunnel.

It was Odile's idea—I learned later he'd scouted it the evening before, swimming the flooded lower channel with a reed in his teeth while the camp sat around its fire counting its prisoners—and it was the kind of plan only a man who has spent his life alone in bad country would dare. The old sluice gates had been sealed with masonry a generation ago, but the Combine's engineers, ever economical, had left a maintenance crawlway beneath, a stone throat barely wider than a man's shoulders, half-choked with silt and running with a finger's depth of cold black water that the sealed gates still wept.

We crawled it in single file, Odile first, me last, the stone scraping our backs, the darkness total except when Brannoc struck a shielded match to check the forks of the passage. The water was snowmelt-cold and tasted of iron and deep rock, and it was the first water I had touched in years that hadn't passed through Combine pipes first, and I wanted to laugh and sob about it at the same time.

Halfway through, Brannoc stopped.

"Listen," he whispered.

We listened. Above us, muffled by yards of stone, came shouting, horns, the crackle of the camp discovering its prisoner gone and its horn-trick played. And beneath it, faint but unmistakable, a sound none of us had heard clean and unmanaged in our whole lives: water moving under its own will, finding its own old path through the foundations of the sealed gates, patient as memory.

"The river remembers the way," Brannoc murmured. "Even here. Even after everything they did to it." He struck another match, and in its small trembling light his face was wet—not from the seep. "My mother sang songs about that. About the water coming home. I thought they were just songs."

"Keep moving," Odile said gently. "Sing them to us on the raft."

* * *

We came out of the tunnel's mouth into a wash a mile downstream, where the raft waited under a tarp of woven saltbush, and the sky in the east had gone the grey of wet ash. Behind us the weir camp still burned its alarm-fires against an enemy already gone. Ahead the river ran narrow and quick between banks of black stone, and the current, when I put my hand in it, pulled like a living thing that had been waiting for us.

I sat in the stern with the straw-stuffed boots dripping off my feet and watched the camp shrink, and Odile crouched across from me, coiling the painter, not looking at me, giving me the silence the way he gave everything—without being asked.

"I voted to sell," I said finally. "Twelve hours ago I would have handed them the chest myself if it bought me a head start."

"I know."

"And my father ordered me killed, and the first thing I felt wasn't grief. It was relief. Because at least now I knew where I stood." My voice cracked on the last word and I let it. "You should have left me. It would have been cheaper."

Odile finished the coil, set it down, and looked at me straight on, and in the pre-dawn grey his face was as open as I'd ever seen it—that restless, competent, lonely face I'd first seen across a saltflat with a stolen chest between us.

"When I was nineteen," he said, "I had a name that wasn't this one, and a debt I couldn't pay, and a crew that sold me to cover it. I've been moving ever since because stopping means trusting, and trusting means giving someone the knife." He shrugged, a small motion, almost embarrassed. "Then a thief led me across forty miles of salt to a dead nation's seal, and I found out I'd rather be hunted with company than safe alone. So no. I'm not leaving you. Get used to it."

Brannoc, at the steering oar, snorted. "He practiced that speech the whole crawl. Twice."

"Shut up, old man."

I laughed—really laughed, the first time in three years, an ugly honking sound that scattered birds off the bank—and then, because I am my father's daughter after all, and because some lessons are worth keeping, I made myself say the rest of it plainly, the way he never could:

"The envoy knows where you are. He let you take me because my father wanted me gone, but Corvane keeps his own ledger, and he'll use us. He'll follow us upstream and let us do the dangerous work of finding the source, and take it from us at the end. And there's something else." I pulled the folded scrap of paper from my sleeve—the guard's water ration chit, which I'd lifted off the tin cup while everyone watched my face instead of my hands. Stamped across it in the Combine's cipher-key, plain as day for anyone raised on my father's knee: the source garrison's strength, the gunboat rotation, and the equinox deadline circled twice.

"We have nine days," I said. "And I know the way in."

Odile took the chit, read it, and looked up at me with something kindled behind his eyes that I recognized, because I'd seen it in mirrors all my life—except his was lit from the other side, from the direction of the water.

"Then we run the river," he said, and pushed us off into the current, upstream, toward the source, with the whole Combine waking behind us and nine days to outrun it.
