## 13. The Envoy's Ledger

The weir camp burned behind us like a second dawn.

Odile Ishikawa stood at the edge of the escarpment with the fire's heat on his back and Mara's weight against his shoulder—she could walk now, mostly, though the rope burns on her wrists had opened again during the climb and she refused to let Brannoc bind them properly. Below, the Combine's camp was a scatter of orange light and black figures, tents collapsing in on themselves as men who had never expected resistance scrambled to save what mattered most to them: their ledgers, their orders, their little paper gods.

"Let them have the tents," Odile said. "We got what we came for."

He patted the oilcloth-wrapped bundle strapped beneath his pack. It had cost them forty minutes inside the envoy's command tent while Mara's screams were still echoing in his skull and his hands would not stop shaking—not from fear, he told himself, but from the effort of not killing every man between him and her cell. The bundle was heavy for its size. Paper always is, when it carries that much weight.

Brannoc crouched at the rim of the rocks, old knees cracking like green wood, watching the fire with an expression Odile couldn't read. Grief, maybe. Or recognition. The forger had spent sixty years making false things look true; perhaps he knew better than anyone what a document could do to the world.

"You took more than the chest papers," Brannoc said quietly. It wasn't a question. He'd seen Odile pry up the floorboard of the envoy's writing desk, seen the flat iron box beneath, seen the way Odile's face changed when he opened it.

"I took everything," Odile said.

* * *

They made six miles before dawn forced them into cover—a slot canyon the river charts called the Throat of the Heron, where the water ran narrow and cold and the walls rose so sheer that the stars became a ribbon overhead instead of a sky. They built no fire. They ate hardtack and dried fish and drank from the river itself, which tasted of stone and snowmelt and something older, something that Odile was beginning to think of as the source's own flavor, faint even this far downstream, like a voice heard through a wall.

Mara sat apart, wrapped in Brannoc's spare coat, working the stiffness out of her wrists. She hadn't spoken much since the rescue. Odile understood why. There was a particular silence that followed learning your own father had ordered your disposal—the silence of a house falling down around you while you're still standing in it. He'd heard that silence before, in his own head, years ago, in another life under another name.

He didn't offer comfort. Comfort would have been a lie, and lies were the one currency he'd sworn off. Instead he sat down across from her, unwrapped the oilcloth, and set the iron box on the stone between them.

"What is it?" she asked. Her voice was rough from the dust of the cell they'd pulled her out of.

"The envoy's ledger." He lifted the lid. Inside, protected by waxed leather, lay a book bound in gray calfskin, its spine stamped with the Combine's mark—the interlocked rings, the river sigil turned on its side like a closed eye. Beside it, a sheaf of loose dispatches tied with wire, and a folded map so fine it could only have come out of the Combine's own cartography office in Vessia City—or whatever they called that city now, since they'd scrubbed the old name off everything.

Mara looked at the ledger for a long moment. "I know that binding," she said finally. "My father's office used them. Gray calf, waxed corners. I used to hide under his desk when I was small and read the spines." A pause. "I thought they were shipping manifests."

"Maybe some of them are."

Odile opened the book.

The first pages were exactly what a suspicious mind would expect: grain shipments, toll receipts, payroll for the weir garrisons. Bureaucratic sediment, laid down layer over layer to bury anything worth finding. But the envoy had been careless in one respect—he'd kept his private record interleaved with the official one, or perhaps he'd simply never imagined anyone would live long enough to read it. Beginning at page forty, in a smaller, tighter hand, the entries changed.

*Diversion schedule, spring quarter. Gates One through Four at full draw. Downriver allocation reduced per directive 11-C. Note: Fenwick Bend wells failing. Recommend continued reduction; population mobile.*

"Fenwick Bend," Brannoc said. His voice had gone very quiet. "That's the tollhouse. That's where we met."

"Population mobile," Odile repeated. He said it flatly, because if he didn't say it flatly he would say it loudly, and loud voices carried down canyons. "They're draining the towns dry and calling the people *mobile*. Like they're weather. Like they're birds."

He turned pages. The entries went back eleven years. Eleven years of arithmetic performed on human lives—acre-feet here, households there, a column headed *attrition, natural causes* that grew longer every dry season. Twelve towns named in the margins, twelve names Odile knew from ranger postings and caravan gossip and the kind of quiet news that never reached the capital because the capital didn't want it to reach anywhere: Saltmere. Low Crossing. The Anvil. Harrow Flats. Places where the river had been shrinking for a generation and everyone blamed the drought, the sky, the gods, anything except the four great gates caged around the source, gates that appeared on no map because the maps themselves were part of the diversion.

Directive 11-C. He found it referenced a dozen times before he found the original, copied into the back matter in full: a single sheet bearing three signatures, the topmost one executed in a hand Odile recognized without ever having seen it before—because Mara had described it, once, on the river, in the low voice people use to describe wounds. *His letters look like architecture,* she'd said. *Everything squared off. Nothing alive.*

Aldous Vane-Marrek, Director of Waters. Mara's father.

"He signed it," Odile said. Not to Mara—to the canyon, to the dark, to the dead courier whose body the river had given up weeks ago with a partial map clutched in his drowned fist. "Eleven years ago. First quarter. He sat at a desk and signed the order that started killing these towns, and then he went home to a daughter he taught to read from ledgers just like this one."

Mara didn't flinch. That was the thing about her that Odile had learned to trust above all her sharpness: she did not flinch. She leaned forward instead, into the worst of it, and read the signature herself, and her face did something complicated and final, like ice deciding whether to become water or stay ice forever.

"He told me the downriver towns were dying of drought," she said. "When I asked why we couldn't open relief channels. I was nineteen. I stood in his office with the rainfall reports in my hands and asked him, and he told me the desert takes what the desert takes, and that mercy at scale is just inefficiency with a conscience." She laughed—one short, terrible note. "I believed him. I built my whole escape plan on top of believing him. I stole those permits thinking the worst thing the Combine did was hoard a birthright."

"They do worse than hoard," Brannoc said. The old man had taken the loose dispatches now, reading by feel in the starlight, his lips moving. "Look here. *Reclamation survey, Harrow Flats district.* They're not just starving the towns, boy. They're waiting for the towns to empty so they can file new concessions on vacant land. The permits you carry—the Vessian ones—they expire at equinox because after equinox, the Combine intends to hold the source under its own claim, uncontested, with nobody left alive downriver to argue."

The wind moved through the Throat of the Heron, and somewhere far below, the river answered it with a sound like slow breathing.

* * *

They read until the stars wheeled past the canyon's ribbon of sky and began to pale. Odile catalogued as he read—it was the ranger habit, the one discipline that had survived every other discipline he'd ever abandoned—and what he catalogued frightened him more thoroughly than any gunboat or gorge.

Because the ledger was not merely proof. It was a *pattern*.

The diversions weren't random greed. They were sequenced. Gate openings timed to market prices for grain in the eastern provinces. Well failures logged alongside land-option purchases. Attrition columns reconciled, quarter by quarter, against the Combine's expansion ledgers, so that every death by thirst had a corresponding entry in a column labeled *acquisition*. Somewhere in a tower office, men had built a machine in which human suffering was not a cost but a *yield*, and they had balanced its books for eleven years without a single line going red.

"We could sell this," Mara said at last.

Odile looked up.

"Not to the Combine." Her jaw was set. "To the guilds. To the downriver councils. To anyone with a printing press and a grudge. This book ends them, Odile. One copy in the right hands and the Combine is finished—every concession they've filed becomes theft on paper, every signature becomes a confession. We wouldn't need to burn anything. We'd just need to be paid enough to disappear afterward, all three of us, somewhere the warrants don't follow."

"That's what you wanted," Odile said. "Back at the split vote. Before."

"Yes." She met his eyes without apology. "And I was wrong about the permits, and I know it, and I'm not wrong about this. There's a difference. The permits were a claim—paper that says someone owns the water. This is evidence. Paper that says someone committed murder. You don't burn evidence. You spend it."

Brannoc made a sound in his throat, half cough, half laugh. "Spend it on what, girl? Justice? Justice is a town with a well. Justice is the gates standing open. You put this ledger in a guildhall and it becomes a weapon in somebody else's war, and wars end, and archives burn, and in twenty years some director's grandson files a fresh concession on the same water and calls it precedent." He tapped the calfskin cover with one cracked fingernail. "I was born Vessian. I watched a whole nation get erased because the wrong people held its papers. Papers don't free water. People free water. At the source. With their hands."

"And get us killed," Mara said.

"Eventually," Brannoc agreed placidly. "Everything gets us killed eventually. The question is only what we're doing when it happens."

Odile said nothing for a long time. He turned the ledger over in his hands, feeling its weight, and thought about the man he had been before the saltflats—before the debt, before the name he no longer used, before he'd learned that the surest way to survive was to keep moving and care about nothing heavier than the next mile of trail. He had chased a thief across the flats because chasing was what he did, because stopping meant thinking, and thinking meant remembering. And now here he sat, stopped, thinking, holding a book that weighed less than his rifle and more than everything he'd carried in ten years of running.

The mission had been simple once. Recover the chest. Appraise the permits. Decide. Sell or keep—that was the whole moral universe of it, two doors and a coin toss.

But the ledger had kicked both doors open onto the same room, and the room was full of the dead of Harrow Flats and Saltmere and Low Crossing, and they were all looking at him.

"It's bigger than the permits now," he said. "It was bigger the moment we pulled that courier's body out of the river, and I was too stubborn to see it. The permits say who may claim the source. The ledger says what's been done to keep the claim secret. Burning the permits breaks the Combine's *future*. But this—" he lifted the book— "this breaks their *past*. And a lie can't survive its own history."

"So we take it to the source," Mara said slowly, testing the shape of the idea.

"We take it to the source, and when the gates come down—if they come down—we leave the ledger where the water can testify. Every town downriver will see the river rise and know it rose on purpose, and someone will ask why, and the answer has to be written somewhere they can find it. Not sold. Not hidden. *Posted.* Like a warrant." He almost smiled. "I've been on the other side of enough warrants to know how they work. Nobody forgets their name once it's nailed to a post."

Brannoc chuckled, low and dry, and settled back against the canyon wall. "An outlaw publishing outlawry. Your mother would have liked you, boy."

Odile went still. "What did you say?"

But the old man's eyes had already drifted shut, or seemed to, and the question hung in the dark among the stars like a moth with nowhere to land. Odile let it go. There would be time. There had to be time, though the ledger's last dispatch, the one tied at the bottom of the wire bundle, argued otherwise in the envoy's cramped hand:

*Source garrison reinforced. Director arriving in person before equinox. All approaches sealed. No survivors expected or required.*

"No survivors expected or required," Odile read aloud, because keeping it silent gave it power.

"Then we'll be unexpected," Mara said.

Far upstream, beyond the sealed approaches, beyond the gorge of silence, the water waited behind its cage—and for the first time since he'd picked up a thief's trail on the white salt, Odile Ishikawa felt no urge at all to run.
