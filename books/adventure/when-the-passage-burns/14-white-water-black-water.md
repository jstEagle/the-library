## 14. White Water, Black Water

The river told us before the sky did.

I'd learned its moods in the Combine's hydrology halls—on paper, in ledgers, in my father's careful voice reciting flow rates like scripture. But paper rivers don't warn you. This one did. All morning it had been running a half-tone darker than the water above Fenwick Bend, a bruised green-black that meant depth and silt and something big gathering below. The current had thickened too, gone syrupy at the edges where it dragged against stone. Odile stood at the raft's bow with his pole planted, reading the surface the way Brannoc read seals, and I watched his shoulders change.

"Brannoc," he said quietly. "How far to the falls your map doesn't show?"

"There are no falls on my map." The old man didn't look up from the chest. He'd hardly looked away from it since we took the ledger from the weir camp, as if the two of them were bound now, permit-box and forger, each keeping the other honest. "There are no falls because no one who mapped this stretch came back to say so."

"That's not comforting."

"It's not meant to be."

I took the second pole. We'd fallen into the rhythm without discussing it—Odile forward, me aft, Brannoc amidships with the chest lashed between his knees and the ledger wrapped in oilcloth against his heart. Three days since the raid on the weir. Two since we'd buried nothing of the envoy's men but their pride. The river had been kind so far, and kindness, in my experience, was always a loan.

The canyon walls had been closing all morning. What had been a corridor was becoming a throat, red rock rising sheer on either side until the sky was a bright ribbon overhead and the air smelled of wet stone and something mineral, almost metallic, like blood held in the mouth. The water's voice changed too. It stopped being speech and became argument—a low, continuous grinding beneath us, boulders turning over in the dark of the channel, the river chewing on the mountain's bones.

Then the sound came down the canyon.

Not thunder. Thunder rolls. This arrived whole, a single sustained roar like a door thrown open on a furnace, and under it, fainter, another sound that made my stomach drop before my mind caught up: the rhythmic *tuk-tuk-tuk* of an engine working hard against current.

"Gunboats," Odile said.

"I know."

"You know how many?"

I closed my eyes and listened the way my father had taught me, back when listening was a daughter's duty. "Two. Maybe three. They're behind us, close. They'll have put in at the weir the moment they found the camp empty."

"They can't run this water any better than we can," Brannoc said.

"They don't have to run it," I said. "They just have to run it longer. Those boats are armored. They carry twenty men each and a swivel gun apiece. We carry a raft, an old man, and a box." I heard my own voice go flat and clinical, the Combine cadence creeping in, and hated it. "We cannot outrun them. We can only out-die them."

Nobody laughed. That was the thing about our crew—we'd passed beyond laughter into something leaner, some shared animal understanding that lived in the space between glances. Odile met my eyes across the chest, and what I saw there wasn't fear. It was arithmetic. He was already doing what I was doing: counting the water ahead, the boats behind, the odds in between, and finding them ugly.

"Then we read the river better than they do," he said. "Mara. You grew up on this water's numbers. Where does it want to kill us?"

*Where doesn't it,* I thought, but I said: "Black water means deep. Black water beside white water means a hole. Stay off the seams. If the current stacks up against the outside of a bend, the inside is rocks. And if—" I hesitated.

"If?"

"If you see foam standing still, that's a keeper hole. It holds things. Boats. People." I swallowed. "My father called them the river's hands. He said they close."

Odile nodded slowly. "Then we give the hands nothing to hold."

* * *

The first rapid came at us like a decision we hadn't made.

One moment the river was fast; the next it was gone—dropping away beneath us into a chute no wider than the raft, walls of red stone pressing in, and we were falling, actually falling, the bow lifting as the stern dropped, Brannoc's arms locked around the chest, my pole ripped from my hands by water moving faster than wood could argue. Spray hit us sideways, cold enough to burn. The roar filled everything—not just my ears but my ribs, my teeth, the hollows of my skull, until I couldn't have heard my own name.

And then the river set us down, almost gently, in a long black pool, and the silence rang.

Odile was laughing. Actually laughing, breathless, hauling his pole back aboard. "Again!" he shouted at the canyon, and the canyon gave him back his own voice in pieces.

"Don't invite it," I said, but I was shaking, and I knew—I knew—he could see it, and I let him see it, which was its own strange gift. With Odile I never had to be the Combine's daughter, the disavowed one, the woman who ran. I could just be afraid, and afraid was allowed.

Brannoc wasn't laughing. He'd gone gray around the mouth, and his knuckles were white on the chest's iron bands. "That was the small one," he said.

He was right. The sound ahead kept building.

* * *

We ran three more rapids through the afternoon, and each one taught us the same lesson harder: the river did not care about permits or gunboats or fathers. It cared about weight and angle and luck. We lost a paddle. We lost most of our drinking water when a wave came over the bow and found the cask. We lost, for one endless moment in the third rapid, Brannoc himself—thrown clear as the raft surfed sideways off a wave train—and then Odile had him by the collar with one fist and the chest strap with the other, holding both above water while I poled us into the eddy, and none of us spoke about how close it had been, because speaking would have made it real.

Behind us, the engine sounds multiplied.

By late light we could see them: two gunboats coming down the last pool, riding low, their armor plates streaming, the swivel guns lashed and covered for the rapids but unmistakable. Men lined their rails. Through the canyon's failing light I saw, with a clarity that felt like a wound, that they weren't hunting us anymore. They were herding us. Keeping pace. Letting the river do its work, saving their powder for whatever wreckage washed out the bottom.

"They know something we don't," Odile said.

"Or they know exactly what we know," I said, "and they're patient."

It was Brannoc who went still. He'd turned the chest toward him and was looking at the Vessian seal by the last of the light, and his face had changed—not fear, something older and sadder. Recognition.

"Mara," he said. "Your father engineered the diversion. Your father knows these waters better than any living man." His thumb moved across the seal. "Why would he send armored boats into a canyon that killed his own first expedition? Unless he isn't worried about the boats."

The cold moved up my spine one vertebra at a time.

"Unless," Odile said slowly, "he knows where the river ends."

"The gorge," I whispered. "The Gorge of Silence. If the river goes underground—if the maps end because the water ends—then he doesn't need to catch us at all. He needs only to seal the far end and wait. Every rapid we run brings us closer to a door he controls." I looked at the chest, at the stamped permits expiring at equinox, at the beautiful legal machinery of claim and concession that my father's world ran on. "He let us take it. He *wanted* us running. A thief can be followed. A claimant can be cornered. But a dead crew in a drowned boat—that's just the river taking back what was stolen. No warrants. No witnesses. No scandal."

The chest sat between us, iron-banded, sealed with a nation's ghost, and for the first time I saw it as my father must have seen it from the beginning: not treasure. Bait. Bait with our names attached.

"So we abandon it," Odile said. "Sink it. Take the ledger and swim for it."

"And lose the only proof that the source can ever legally belong to anyone but the Combine?" Brannoc's voice cracked like dry timber. "Boy, I was born Vessian. That chest is the last lawful breath of a murdered country. You don't sink a country because the water gets rough."

"Then we don't sink it," Odile said, and his jaw set in the way I'd learned meant the argument was over before it started. "We run it. All of it. Tonight, in the dark, while their pilots hug the banks afraid of the holes. Mara—can you read black water in starlight?"

I looked up. The ribbon of sky had opened above the canyon, and the stars were coming out hard and cold, and the river below them ran black indeed—black and singing, full of every hand that had ever closed.

"No," I said honestly. "But I can try."

"Try," said Odile Ishikawa, "is what we've got."

* * *

The night rapid had no name on any chart, which meant it had names on every chart that never survived to be printed. We lashed everything down—chest, ledger, ourselves—double-knotted, Brannoc's wrists tied to the frame because he refused to be tied and then refused, after the third rapid, not to be. The gunboats' lanterns glowed far behind us, cautious, slow. In front of us the river made a sound I had never heard water make: a continuous, tonal roar, almost musical, the note of a canyon breathing in.

We entered the dark.

What follows, I remember in pieces, the way drowning people remember weather. A wave like a moving wall, and the taste of my own blood where my lip split against the pole. Rocks appearing out of starlight like the backs of animals. Odile shouting bearings in that calm, carrying voice—*left seam, hard right, hold, HOLD*—and my body obeying him before my mind agreed. Once, the raft stood nearly vertical and I saw the stars straight down the throat of the drop, and I thought, absurdly, of my father's study, of the neat blue lines of his diversion schematics, and how none of them had ever shown anything true.

Then the impact.

Not water—wood and iron. A submerged sweeper, a dead tree anchored in the channel, and the raft struck it broadside with a crack I felt in my teeth. The world tilted. Brannoc cried out—not a word, just a sound, old and startled—and then the chest was sliding, straps snapping one by one, sliding toward the black seam where the river's hands waited, and the old man did the last thing anyone expected.

He threw himself on it.

He wrapped his arms around the iron bands and let his body become the anchor, and the river took the price gladly. I heard something tear that was not canvas. I heard Odile screaming my name, and I was already moving, both of us hauling at Brannoc's legs while the current tried to peel him away like bark, and we got him—God, we got him—back onto the tilting deck with the chest pinned under three bodies, and the river, cheated, spat us out into a pool below the rapid as if disgusted with us.

The gunboat lanterns, far upstream, stopped moving. Even they, I think, needed a moment.

* * *

We grounded on a gravel bar under the first pale suggestion of dawn, and I knelt over Brannoc in the gray light and understood, before my hands confirmed it, what the tearing sound had been.

The sweeper had not torn canvas. It had torn him—open along the ribs where the iron band had bitten and the wood had driven through, a wound that bled with the slow, terrible patience of deep damage. His lips were gray. His hands, still curled around the chest's bands even now, were shaking.

"Foolish," Odile whispered. "Old man, you foolish, brave—"

"Worth it." Brannoc smiled with bloody teeth. "Chest is dry. Check the seal. Check the seal, boy."

It was dry. Of course it was dry. The river had wanted the man, and taken its price, and left the paper.

We worked on him with what we had—the boiled linen, the resin-pine pitch, the needle Odile carried for sail repair, my father's field-surgery training finally used for something I didn't despise. Brannoc endured it telling us about Vessia, about the spring festivals on the water, about his mother's garden drowned forty years and more, his voice going softer and softer as the blood left him. At the end of it he caught my wrist with surprising strength.

"He sent those boats knowing," he rasped. "Knowing the water. Knowing you'd run the chest into the dark. That's the Combine entire, girl. They never fight. They *arrange.*" His grip tightened. "So stop running their arrangement. Stop running at all. Get to the source, and end the claiming forever—burn the permits, burn the ledger, burn every paper that says the water belongs—"

"Hush," I said, because I couldn't bear the rest. "Save it. You're going to finish the map yourself. You promised."

His eyes, fever-bright, went to the canyon ahead, where the river's roar had begun to change again—deepening, steadying, gathering itself toward whatever door waited downstream. Then they came back to me, and there was peace in them, which frightened me worse than the blood.

"Aye," he said softly. "If I can't walk there, I'll draw you there. Every bend. Every hand. Everything my mother's people knew before the maps burned." He coughed, and held on to consciousness by will alone, and Odile and I looked at each other over his body and over the chest between us, and I saw the same understanding land in Odile's face that had landed in mine.

The chest was bait. The chase was the trap. My father had arranged it all—and the only way out was forward, through the Gorge of Silence, to the one place the Combine could not afford to let us stand.

Downstream, around the next bend, the canyon walls drew together like a closing hand, and the river slid toward the dark of the tunnel, black and silent as a held breath—and on the far rim, tiny in the dawn light, we could see the glint of armor. They were already sealing the door.

Odile picked up his pole.

"We climb, then," he said.
