## 16. The Gorge of Silence

The river began to whisper before it began to disappear.

Odile heard it first as a change in the water's voice—a hollowness beneath the rush, like a word spoken into a cupped hand. He'd been poling the raft through a long slack stretch where the canyon walls leaned so close overhead that noon came down to them as a single blade of light, and when he lifted his head to listen, Mara was already listening too. She had her father's ears for water. She hated that he knew it.

"Listen," she said anyway.

"I'm hearing it."

Brannoc would have said something about how a river that whispers is a river with secrets, but Brannoc was three days buried on a bend of gravel upstream, under a cairn they'd built with bleeding hands, and the silence where his voice should have been was louder than anything the gorge could make.

The walls closed. That was the only way to describe what happened over the next hour—the canyon did not narrow so much as *decide* to become something else. The red sandstone gave way to black basalt veined with white mineral scars, and the walls drew in until Odile could have touched both sides with an oar, and the sky shrank from a ribbon to a thread to a memory. The air went cold and mineral-smelling, the way a cellar smells, the way a tomb smells. The water slowed. It pooled. It moved with the deliberate, heavy patience of something being funneled somewhere it did not want to go.

And then, around a shoulder of rock polished smooth as glass, the river simply ended.

Not dried up. Not dammed. *Ended.* It slid into a hole in the canyon wall—a tunnel mouth forty feet high, its rim worn round as a bottle's lip, and beyond it there was nothing. Not darkness exactly. A darkness with texture, a darkness that breathed. The sound of the water changed at the threshold: the broad voice of the river compressed into a low, resonant moan, like wind across the mouth of a jar, and it came back to them off the stone in slow echoes that arrived seconds late, as if the dark were considering each sound before returning it.

Mara shipped her pole. "There it is," she said quietly.

"There what is?"

"The throat." She stood at the raft's bow, and the light caught the side of her face, and Odile saw that she wasn't looking at the tunnel—she was looking past it, up the sheer face above it, reading the rock the way other people read letters. "My father called it the throat. I used to think it was a figure of speech. Engineers' talk." Her jaw tightened. "It isn't. Everything the Combine takes comes through here. Every stolen gallon of the source passes through this throat on its way down to their channels. This is the door."

"Then we knock," Odile said, and even as he said it he knew how hollow it sounded, because you do not knock on a door like that. You do not enter a throat like that. The river itself went in there every day of the world, and the river came out the other side, but the river was not two people on a battered raft with a dead man's map and a chest full of burning permits lashed under oilcloth amidships.

He let the raft drift to the near wall and set a line around a spur of basalt, and they floated there in the cold shadow, looking at the mouth of the tunnel, and for a long time neither of them spoke.

The echoes were wrong. That was what unsettled him most, once he started cataloguing the ways it was wrong. Sound went in and took too long coming back, and when it came back it came back *changed*—their own breathing returned to them flattened and distant, as though someone else were breathing in there, someone patient, someone waiting. He thought of the wrecked barge in the Rapids of the Unmapped, the ribs of it, the rusted winch still holding a coil of cable like a fist that had never learned to open. Men had come this far before. Men had looked at this same black mouth. And the gorge had taken them, or the men behind them had, and either way the rock remembered nothing and told nothing.

"We can't run it," Mara said at last. It wasn't despair in her voice; it was worse than that. It was assessment. "Even if the channel inside is navigable—and it might be, the flow's too steady for it not to be—we'd be running blind in the Combine's own pipe. They'll have grates. Winch points. Places to stand with rifles where the current funnels you straight to them. My father doesn't build doors without locks, and he doesn't build locks without killing floors." She turned. "And even if we got through—even then—they know we're coming now. After the weir camp, after the ledger, they've had days. They won't be waiting at the far end. They'll be waiting *in* it."

Odile looked at the tunnel a while longer. Then he looked up.

The wall above the tunnel mouth rose sheer for perhaps sixty feet—black stone slicked with spray-mist where the pooled river breathed against it—but above that, the character of the cliff changed. Ledges stepped out. Cracks ran diagonal, pale with old water-stain, the kind of breaks that held handholds if a man was willing to trust them. And at the top of the visible climb, maybe two hundred feet up, the rim of the gorge cut a hard line against a sky gone the color of hammered tin.

"They sealed the far end," he said. "You said it yourself. Which means they're thinking about the water, not the rock. Nobody seals a tunnel and then guards the ceiling."

Mara followed his gaze. He watched her understand it, watched the understanding move through her like cold water through a channel, and he watched her hate it.

"No," she said.

"It's the only way."

"It's two hundred feet of wet basalt carrying everything we own, Odile. Carrying the chest. Carrying—" She stopped. He knew which name had risen in her throat, and he knew she'd swallowed it, and he loved her a little for trying to spare him, and hated her a little for thinking he needed sparing.

"Brannoc mapped us here," Odile said. His voice came out lower than he intended. "Last thing he did with his hands was draw the way in. He didn't draw a tunnel with a gate at the end of it. He drew the source. So the way in exists, and it isn't floating down a pipe built by the man who killed him." He untied the line from the spur, coiled it slowly, giving his hands something honest to do. "We climb."

* * *

They spent the rest of the daylight preparing, because preparation was the only prayer either of them trusted anymore.

The raft they broke down at a gravel shelf below the tunnel mouth, working in the blue half-light that leaked into the gorge's throat. Odile stripped the frame to its lightest members and lashed them into a drag-sledge; the chest went into a canvas wrap with the oilcloth doubled over it, and the whole bundle rode high between his shoulders on a harness cut from the raft's rigging. Mara took the ledger and the maps, wrapped in waxcloth against the spray, strapped flat against her chest where a fall would drive them into her ribs rather than tear them away. They ate the last of the smoked fish cold. They drank, deliberately, more than they wanted—the climb would sweat it out of them faster than thirst would warn them.

Twice, during the work, Odile caught himself speaking aloud to no one—to the shape of a man who wasn't there, asking Brannoc whether the lashings were true, whether the harness sat right—and twice he stopped himself mid-word, and both times Mara pretended very hard to be busy with something else, and both times he was grateful to her for the pretense.

Grief on a river is a strange cargo. It doesn't weigh what you expect. Some hours it's nothing at all, just a smooth stretch of water, and then a bend comes—a bend where the old man had laughed at something, or cursed the current, or named a star—and the whole weight of him arrives at once, broadside, and all you can do is keep the hull pointed downstream and wait for it to pass. Odile had lost people before. He had a debt and a dead name and a decade of never stopping moving precisely because stopping meant feeling things like this. But Brannoc had been different. Brannoc had been the first person in ten years who'd known what Odile was running from and chosen to sit beside him anyway, in a leaky tollhouse, mending nets, talking about a drowned country as if remembering it were a form of resistance.

"He'd have made the climb," Mara said, out of nowhere, as they tested the first anchor point. "Complaining the entire way. About his knees, his lungs, the ingratitude of stone. But he'd have made it."

"You don't know that."

"I know he crossed the Salt Teeth at seventy-one with a forged passport sewn into his coat and a price on his head." She pulled the anchor line taut, testing. "I read his file, Odile. The Combine kept one on him since before I was born. Forty years they've been afraid of an old man with a pen." Something moved across her face—not quite a smile. "He'd have made the climb. We're the ones who have to live up to it."

Dusk came down into the gorge like water filling a cup. Above them, the last light left the rim, and the tunnel mouth across the pool became a perfect circle of deeper black, and the river's moan rose slightly, as if the dark were clearing its throat.

They slept in shifts, back to back against the basalt, and Odile dreamed of his mother's kitchen and woke with salt on his lips that might have been spray and might not have been.

* * *

They crossed the pool at first gray light, ferrying the sledge and bundles on a line strung from a flake of rock below the tunnel mouth to a horn of stone on the far side, and the water was so cold it burned, and the echo of their own splashing came back to them from inside the tunnel seconds late, flattened, patient, like applause from an empty house.

The climb began where the wall above the tunnel mouth offered its first ledge—a shelf of basalt a hand's width wide, running diagonally up and left, greased with a film of spray. Odile went first, unburdened, to set the rope. He climbed the way he did everything: without letting himself think about the height, without letting himself think about the drop, keeping his eyes on the next hold and only the next hold, because a climber who looks down has already begun to fall.

Sixty feet up, the easy rock ended.

Above him the wall steepened into a section where the ledges pinched out entirely and the only way on was a vertical crack, two fingers wide, seeping cold water, running fifteen feet to the next good stance. He hung there with his boots on a ledge barely wider than his heels and looked at the crack and felt the gorge look back at him. Below, Mara was a small figure on the bank, paying out his rope, watching him with her father's eyes for water and, apparently, for stone. The river moaned beneath him. Somewhere far above, wind moved along the rim and made a sound like a vast slow breath, and the whole gorge seemed to inhale.

*It's just rock,* he told himself. *Rock doesn't want anything.*

But that was the lie of the desert, and he'd known it since he was a boy: rock wants everything. Rock wants your grip and your nerve and eventually your body, and it is patient the way only water and stone are patient, and every year it takes somebody who trusted a hold that had been waiting all its life to fail.

He climbed the crack. There was no alternative worth discussing, so he didn't discuss it, even with himself. Fifteen feet of two-finger jamming, forearms screaming, water wicking cold up his sleeves, and then his palm slapped onto the stance above and he lay against the warm-dry stone—it was dry here, above the spray line, the first kindness the gorge had shown him—and breathed until his heart agreed to continue.

The rope went down. Mara came up.

She climbed well—he'd known she would; everything she did was precise, economical, angry at inefficiency—but halfway up the crack she stalled, and her voice came up to him thin and stripped of all its usual armor.

"Odile."

"I'm here."

"If I fall—"

"You're not going to fall."

"If I fall, the chest stays with me. Cut me loose. Don't you dare try to hold me, the rope will take you off the wall, you *cut me loose*—"

"Mara." He waited until her breathing steadied. "Look at your feet."

A pause. "What about my feet?"

"Are they where you put them?"

Another pause. "...Yes."

"Then you haven't fallen yet. Climb."

She climbed. She came over the stance with her hands shaking and her face gray and her eyes furious—at the rock, at herself, at him, at the whole architecture of a world that made daughters of engineers climb cliffs to undo their fathers' works—and she knelt on the stone and pressed her forehead against it for a moment, just breathing, and Odile crouched beside her and said nothing, because some mercies are made of silence.

They hauled the bundles up on the rope, chest and ledger and sledge-frame, and rested, and climbed again.

The middle hundred feet were almost kind—a long rising traverse along a system of ledges, exposed but steady, the kind of ground where a body finds a rhythm and the mind is allowed to go quiet. Odile let it go quiet. He thought about nothing: about the weight of the sun on his neck, the smell of stone warming, the small bright flowers growing out of cracks a thousand feet from any soil anyone had ever claimed. Cliff flowers. Things that bloomed where nothing was supposed to live. He thought: *Brannoc would have liked these,* and then he thought nothing again, and it was almost peace.

Then they rounded a buttress and saw the far end of the gorge, and the peace died.

Two hundred yards downstream, where the tunnel swallowed the river, the Combine had been busy. Scaffolding clung to the cliff above the tunnel mouth like a white fungus—fresh timber, bright rope, the glint of new iron. A winch platform jutted out over the water. And across the tunnel's entrance, half-lowered, hung a grate: an iron portcullis the size of a chapel door, its bars thick as a man's waist, dripping river. Even from this distance Odile could see the mechanism of it—chains running up into a housing cut into the rock, a capstan, figures moving on the scaffolding. Small figures. Patient figures. Men whose job was to stand between the desert and its own water, and who would stand there all day, every day, forever, because someone paid them to.

And beyond the grate, deep in the tunnel's throat, a light burned. Lanterns. A camp inside the mountain. They weren't just sealing the door.

They were living in it.

Mara lowered the spyglass slowly. Her face, when she turned to him, had gone very still—the stillness of a woman doing arithmetic she doesn't like.

"That's not a patrol post," she said. "That's a garrison. Twenty men at least. Maybe more inside." She looked back up the gorge, the way they'd come—two hundred feet of cliff, days of rapids behind it, and beyond those, the Combine's riders spreading across the desert like ink in water. "We can't go forward. We can't go back. And we can't stay on this wall forever."

Odile took the glass. Through it, the garrison resolved: the capstan, the chains, the bored sentries, a cookfire's smoke rising thin from the scaffolding. Ordinary men. Men with names and blisters and mothers, probably. Men who would kill him without losing any sleep, and who would sleep fine afterward, because the Combine had spent a generation teaching the desert that the water's theft was simply *how things were*, and men defend how things are the way they defend their own skin.

He lowered the glass and looked up instead. Above them the traverse continued, angling back toward the near wall, toward a place where the gorge's rim finally came down to meet the climbing stone—a saddle of broken talus, maybe another four hundred feet, where a determined pair with a rope and no sense might pull themselves out of the canyon altogether.

Out of the gorge. Onto the rim. Overland, then—across whatever plateau lay above, around the sealed throat, down whatever approach existed on the far side. Toward the source. Toward the machinery. Toward Mara's father, and twenty guns, and the equinox now fewer than six days out.

It was a terrible plan. It was the only plan. He found, examining it honestly, that he felt no despair at all—or none that mattered. Despair was a luxury for people with alternatives.

"Mara," he said, and pointed up at the saddle, at the broken sky-line where the gorge finally ended. "How are you at walking?"

She followed his finger. He watched the arithmetic run behind her eyes—the distance, the days, the guns—and watched her arrive, as he had, at the only answer there was. Something hardened in her face. Not hope. Harder than hope. The thing that carries you when hope runs out: plain, stubborn, unglamorous will.

"I walked out of the Combine," she said. "I can walk around it."

They climbed.

The afternoon went gold, then copper, then ash. The gorge fell away beneath them foot by foot, the river shrinking to a dull bronze ribbon, the garrison at the tunnel mouth dwindling to toys around a toy door, and the wind at the rim grew louder, cleaner, tasting of open country and distance. Odile's hands were torn and his shoulders were fire and the chest rode his spine like a sleeping animal, and none of it mattered, because above him the rim was close enough now to see individual stones, and above the stones was the sky, and above the sky was whatever came next.

He reached up for the final hold, pulled his head level with the lip of the gorge—

—and froze.

Horses. A picket line of them, tethered among the rim rocks not fifty yards from where he would emerge, saddles on, steam rising off their coats in the cooling air. And beside the nearest horse, checking a hoof, a rider in Combine gray, close enough that Odile could hear him humming.

Close enough to hear Odile's own breath catch.

Below him, feeling the rope go taut and still, Mara whispered up through the stone:

"What is it? What do you see?"
