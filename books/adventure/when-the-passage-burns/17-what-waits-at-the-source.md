## 17. What Waits at the Source

The tunnel breathed cold.

Mara felt it before she saw anything—a current of air sliding out of the rock like the exhale of something enormous and long-held, carrying the smell of wet stone and iron and, beneath it all, water. Living water. After eleven days in the Gorge of Silence, where the river had vanished under their feet and the only sounds were their own boots on scree and Odile's rope creaking against the cliff face, that smell went through her like a blade finding the gap in armor.

She stopped climbing and pressed her palm flat against the tunnel wall. The stone was damp. Not seep-damp, not the thin sweat of condensation, but *wet*—saturated, weeping, alive.

"It's close," she said, and her voice came out smaller than she intended.

Odile looked back down the line at her. He was roped above her, chalk-pale with dust, his hands wrapped in strips of hide gone gray from the rock. Eleven days of climbing had carved him down to wire and stubbornness, but his eyes were still steady—those flat ranger's eyes that gave away nothing except, sometimes, when he let them, a kind of fierce tenderness he probably didn't know was visible.

"How close?" he asked.

"Close enough to smell." She lifted her hand so he could see it shining. "This isn't runoff. This is the aquifer itself. We're inside its throat."

Above them, where the tunnel bent into darkness, Brannoc would have said something. Would have made a joke about Vessian masons or the arrogance of men who thought they could pipe a god. But Brannoc was buried two hundred miles downstream under a cairn of river stones, and the map he'd finished with his dying hand was folded against Mara's heart, soft as cloth now from handling, and there was no one left to make the dark feel navigable except the two of them.

So she climbed.

* * *

They came up out of the rock the way drowned things come up out of lakes—slowly, painfully, blinking.

One moment the tunnel was a throat of black basalt, narrowing until they had to move sideways, shoulders scraping, breath loud in their own ears. Then the ceiling cracked open into a seam of gray light, and Mara hauled herself through a fissure no wider than a coffin, and lay gasping on warm stone with the sky suddenly enormous above her.

Not open sky. Cavern sky. They had emerged onto a ledge inside a vaulted hollow vast enough to have weather—the air moved in slow currents, cool and mineral, and somewhere far above, a shaft of true daylight fell through a chimney in the rock and lit the center of the chamber like a column holding up heaven.

Mara got to her knees. Looked down.

And forgot how to breathe.

The source was not a spring. Springs were what she'd known her whole life—seeps, trickles, the shy beginnings of things. This was a *sea*. A lake of water so still and so deep it was black, filling the floor of the cavern from wall to wall, and in the center of it, directly beneath the falling column of daylight, the water rose. She could see it rising—a great slow boil, a blossoming upward pressure, as if the earth itself were exhaling through a wound. The light came down and the water came up and where they met, the surface bloomed silver, endlessly, silently, like a flower opening that never finished opening.

"Oh," she heard Odile say behind her. Just that. One syllable, stripped of everything.

Her mother had sung her a song once, before the Combine took even that away—a lullaby about the water under the mountain, the water that dreamed, the water that remembered every name it had ever been called. Mara had thought it was a fairy tale for children who lived too far from any river. Now she knelt above a black sea in the belly of the world and understood that her mother had been singing geography.

Then she saw the machinery, and the song died in her throat.

* * *

It ringed the near shore like a crown of thorns fitted to the mountain itself.

Pumps—she knew pumps, had grown up among them, had drawn their schematics at her father's knee before she could write her own name—but nothing like this scale. Twelve of them, maybe more, squatting on iron platforms bolted into the living rock, each one tall as a temple gate, their pistons descending into the black water on stone caissons. Pipes ran from each pump mouth toward the far wall of the cavern, pipes wide enough to drive a wagon through, and where they entered the rock they were banded with brass collars stamped with the sigil she knew better than her own heartbeat: the interlocked rings of the Combine.

Gauges. Valves. A catwalk system strung along the shore on stanchions, lamplit, manned. She counted figures moving on it—small at this distance, unhurried, going about their work with the terrible ordinariness of men feeding a furnace. Feeding the diversion. Every one of those pistons swallowing the source and driving it sideways, into the hidden channels, away from the old riverbed, away from Fenwick Bend and the drowned towns and the saltflats where Odile had grown up learning which wells would lie to you.

Twelve towns starving while this sea rose and rose and rose, caged, and the only thing ever allowed to drink was the Combine's own hidden channel.

"We're standing on it," Odile said quietly. He had come up beside her without her hearing, which after eleven days of trusting him with her life should not have surprised her and did anyway. "The whole time. Everything the river used to be—it's all been here. Being held."

"Not held." Mara's voice came out flat and hard, and she recognized her father's cadence in it and hated it. "*Taken.* There's a difference. Held implies you intend to give it back."

Below them, a whistle sounded—one short note, then another—and the nearest piston began to cycle. Even at this distance she felt it in her teeth, in the ledge under her knees: a slow seismic pulse, the mountain's heartbeat replaced by an engine's.

And then she saw the figure on the central platform, and stopped feeling anything at all.

* * *

He stood apart from the workers, at the rail of the highest catwalk, and even across the whole width of that black sea she knew the set of his shoulders the way you know a scar.

Aldous Vane-Erris. Director of Waters. Her father.

He was thinner than she remembered. That was the first obscenity—that he had grown *thin*, that the man who had engineered the theft of a nation's blood had been eating poorly while her friends bled in rapids for the right to look at what he'd locked away. His coat was Combine gray, unadorned, the coat of a man past ceremony. His hands rested on the rail. His head was bowed slightly, as if in thought, or prayer, or the particular posture of a man listening to a machine he loved.

He had taught her to read water tables before she could read words. He had shown her, when she was seven, a diagram of the diversion and told her it was medicine—the desert was sick, he'd said, and the river was the treatment, and the Combine was the physician, and physicians must be permitted to decide where the dose goes. He had believed it. That was the worst part, the part she'd spent her whole disavowal refusing to examine: he had never lied to her. He had simply built a world where the lie wasn't needed, because the truth had been redesigned.

"He's here," she said. "Odile. He's *here*. He doesn't come to the field. He hasn't left the capital in nine years—he sends envoys, he sends men like the ones who took me at the weir. If he's standing on that platform—"

"Then the permits mattered enough to bring him," Odile said. "Or the equinox did." He was beside her now, flat against the rock, and his eyes had gone from wonder to work, sweeping the shore, counting ladders, cataloguing guards with the same attention he'd once given her trail across the saltflats. "Two ways off this ledge. Down the talus to the west shore, where the catwalk ends. Or back into the tunnel and around, if there is an around. Your call. You know how he thinks."

"I know exactly how he thinks." She laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "That's the problem. I've spent three years making sure I don't."

"Mara."

She looked at him. Dust in the lines of his face, rope burns on his palms, a man who had burned a dead nation's claim papers rather than sell them, who had carried a dying forger on his back through a gorge full of bones, who had come for her at the weir with a stolen knife and no plan worth the name. He was looking at her now with that steadiness, and underneath it—not pity, Odile didn't do pity—something more dangerous. Recognition.

"You don't have to go down there," he said. "Whatever you're telling yourself right now about owing him a confrontation—you don't owe him anything. Not your fear. Not your explanation. Not your face."

"That's not why I'm going."

"No?"

"No." She turned back to the black sea, to the silver bloom where the light met the rising water, and felt the old anger settle in her chest—not hot anymore, not since Brannoc died, but dense, compressed, load-bearing. Anger as architecture. "I'm going because he built all of this believing no one would ever stand where I'm standing and see it whole. Because he designed the diversion so that no single mind would ever hold both ends of it—the towns that thirst and the sea that's hoarded. Everyone sees their piece. No one sees the crime. That's how it survives. That's how *he* survives—by never having to stand in the room where the arithmetic becomes bodies."

She stood up. Her legs shook. She let them.

"So I'm going to stand in the room," she said. "Both ends. Out loud. Where he can't redesign it."

* * *

The talus slope took them down in silence, stone whispering under their boots, and the closer they came to the shore the larger the machinery became, until it stopped being machinery at all and became architecture, industry, a cathedral built to a god of suction. The air grew colder near the water and louder near the pumps, and between those two pressures Mara walked with the map against her ribs and her father's name in her mouth like a stone she couldn't swallow or spit.

A sentry found them forty yards from the catwalk stairs.

To his credit—and she would remember this, later, in the way you remember small mercies in bad rooms—he hesitated. He saw two gaunt, dust-caked figures coming down the talus out of a sealed tunnel that appeared on no chart, and instead of shouting he raised his rifle halfway and said, in a voice pitched between authority and disbelief:

"This level is restricted. How did you—" And then his lamp swung fully onto her face, and he stopped.

She watched him recognize her. It happened in stages: the narrowing, the flicker, the way his grip shifted on the rifle—not raising it, lowering it an inch, the instinct of a man who has just realized the person in front of him outranks whatever orders he was given. She had seen that look her entire childhood. Daughter of the Director. The girl who ran the water. She had hated it her whole life and discovered, at the weir, tied to a chair in her father's own camp, that it had never once protected her.

"The Director will want to know I'm here," she said. Her voice did not shake. She had learned stillness the hard way, in a chair, watching men wait for instructions about her. "Take me to him. Both of us. Unarmed." She unbuckled her knife and let it fall to the stone, and beside her, after a moment, Odile did the same, and the sound of his blade hitting rock was somehow louder than the pumps.

The sentry looked at Odile—at the salt-flat tan, the ranger's braids, the eyes that had already measured the distance to the man's throat—and made the calculation of his career.

"This way," he said.

* * *

The catwalk rang under their boots, iron notes swallowed by the vastness, and the black sea slid past below them, close enough now that she could see the water truly was clear where the light touched it—clear and impossibly blue-green, glacial, ancient, moving upward in columns she could almost see, the whole lake breathing rise. Her mother's song turned over in her skull. *The water that remembers every name it has ever been called.*

Vessia. It had been called Vessia.

At the central platform, the workers drew back, and the space around the rail emptied the way water empties around a dropped stone, and Aldous Vane-Erris turned to face her.

Up close, the thinness was worse. His hair had gone entirely gray at the temples. There were new lines around his mouth, cut deep, the grooves of a man who had spent years saying difficult things to other difficult men. But his eyes were unchanged—pale, precise, patient—and when they found her they did not widen, did not soften, did not do any of the things eyes are supposed to do at the return of a daughter the world told you was dead.

They focused. The way a lens focuses. The way an instrument focuses.

"Mara," he said.

"Father."

Behind him, the nearest piston completed its stroke, and the whole platform shuddered, and the black sea below them rose another invisible fraction of an inch, patient as geology, patient as everything he had never been.

"I reviewed the weir incident," he said, conversationally, as though they were in his study and she were fourteen and late with her hydrology exercises. "My envoy exceeded his mandate. I've corrected that. You'll want to hear the details, I expect. There are arrangements—"

"There aren't." She heard her own voice come out level and was grateful to it. "I'm not here for arrangements. I'm here because you've spent your whole life making sure no one ever saw both ends of the river at once. So look at me. I've seen both ends. I carried a man through a gorge full of your first expedition's dead. I've drunk from wells in towns your diversion is killing, and I've stood over this—" she gestured, and her hand shook now, finally, and she let it, "—this sea you've caged. Both ends, Father. In one mind. Yours, if you want it. Mine, whether you want it or not."

Something moved behind his eyes. Not guilt. She had waited her whole life to see guilt there and understood, in that moment, with a clarity that felt like falling, that she never would. What moved there was assessment. Recalculation. The same motion she'd seen him make over a failing pump: identify the fault, isolate the component, determine whether the component could be repaired or must be replaced.

"Mara," he said again, gentler now, and the gentleness was the most frightening sound in the cavern, "you're tired, and you've been among people who filled your head with—"

"Don't." Odile's voice, low, from beside her. Not raised. Simply placed, like a stone in a wall.

Her father's gaze shifted to Odile, took his measure in half a second, dismissed him in the next—and that dismissal, that casual arithmetic of worth, settled the last question Mara had carried up the gorge. She stepped forward to the rail, between her father and the black sea, so that the rising water was at her back and the man who made her was in front of her, and she reached inside her coat and drew out the folded map—Brannoc's last work, the source charted in a dying man's careful hand—and held it where he could see it.

"I know what you're calculating," she said. "You're deciding whether I'm a fault to be isolated or a component to be recovered. So let me save you the trouble." Below her, beneath the platform, the source bloomed silver in the falling light, opening and opening and never finishing, remembering every name it had ever been called. "I'm neither. I'm the witness. And the equinox is in two days."

For the first time in her life, she watched her father's hands go still on the rail.

Far across the cavern, faint but unmistakable, a door boomed shut, and boots began to move along the far catwalks—many boots, converging, and her father did not turn to look, which was answer enough.

"You brought more than yourself," Mara said quietly.

"I brought the claimants," Aldous Vane-Erris said. "The permits expire at equinox, Mara. Someone will file the claim. It might as well be family."
