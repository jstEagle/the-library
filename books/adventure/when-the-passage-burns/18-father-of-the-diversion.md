## 18. Father of the Diversion

The tunnel breathed.

That was the only word for it—Odile had stood in mines and caves and the throat of every dead thing the desert kept buried, and none of them breathed like this. The air came out of the dark in long, slow exhalations, cool and mineral-sweet, carrying the smell of deep stone and older water. It pressed against his face like a hand checking whether he was still alive.

Behind him, Mara crouched at the mouth of the passage they'd clawed their way through, binding her scraped palms with strips of her own sleeve. Three days of climbing since the Gorge of Silence. Three days of hauling themselves up chimneys of rock while the Combine's lanterns burned on the far rim, patient as vultures. Brannoc was two days dead and riding in a folded map against Odile's chest, and Odile had not yet decided what to do with that weight except carry it.

"Listen," Mara said.

He listened. Beneath the tunnel's breathing there was another sound now, rhythmic, mechanical—a great slow *chunk*, *chunk*, like a heart that had been taught to count. Iron moving against iron somewhere ahead in the black.

"The diversion gates," she said. "We're close."

*Close.* The word sat strangely in his mouth when he tried it. His whole life he had been far from things—far from the towns that wouldn't have him, far from the name he'd buried with his mother, far from any place that could be called home by anyone who knew him. Now he was close to the source of everything, and it felt less like arrival than like standing at the edge of a wound.

They went in.

* * *

The tunnel ran straight for half a mile before it opened, and what opened was not a cavern. It was a wound made deliberate.

Odile stepped out onto a gallery of cut stone and stopped breathing for a moment. The source of the river filled a chamber vast enough to swallow the whole town of Fenwick Bend and have room left over for its graveyard. Water rose from the living rock in a spring the size of a lake, blue-black and glass-still in its depths, boiling white where it surged up from vents no diver would ever reach. In the old stories his mother had told him—the forbidden ones, told by candle stub with the shutters barred—it had been called the Mother's Eye, and it was said you could see your whole life in it if you looked too long.

He did not look too long. Because rising out of that water, caging it, strangling it, stood the machinery.

It was worse than anything he had imagined, and he had imagined plenty. A forest of iron legs strode down into the spring itself, holding up platforms and gantries and sluice-gates thick as fortress walls. Great pipes as wide as houses drank from the Eye and turned away into the mountain, carrying the river off into channels it had never chosen. Chains the size of ship's anchors hung slack and dripping. And everywhere—everywhere—the seal of the Combine, stamped in brass on every beam: three rivers braided into one fist.

"They built it inside the mountain," Mara said quietly beside him. "My father designed it when I was six years old. He used to bring home drawings. I thought they were beautiful." Her voice did something complicated on the last words. "I built models of them. He put one on his desk."

Above the machinery, cut into the chamber wall in letters tall as a man, ran an inscription. Odile couldn't read the old Vessian script, but he didn't need to. He'd seen the same letters on the permits, on the courier's map, on Brannoc's dying sketches. The drowned nation's own words, carved by hands a generation gone, watching the Combine's iron strangle the water they had died defending.

*Chunk,* said the machinery. *Chunk.*

And then, because the world had never once let Odile Ishikawa arrive anywhere quietly, a voice rolled down from a gantry high above the spring, amplified by some brass-throated horn, filling the chamber the way thunder fills a canyon.

"You climbed well."

Mara went rigid beside him. Odile had seen her face down Combine riders, gunboats, drowning, and her father's name spoken aloud—and she had not gone rigid like this. This was different. This was the body remembering something the mind had tried to bury.

A platform descended on chains from the upper galleries, slow and ceremonial, lamplit. On it stood a single man.

Serafin Voss-Adler, First Engineer of the Combine, was smaller than Odile expected. That was the first obscenity of him—that the man who had caged a river should be so ordinary a size, silver-haired, straight-backed, dressed in a plain engineer's coat with the braid stripped from the cuffs. Disavowed men wore stripped cuffs. Odile knew the look. He'd worn it himself, once, under a different name.

But the eyes were not ordinary. They were Mara's eyes, and they were cold as the deep of the spring.

"Mara," the man said, and the horn carried it gently, almost tenderly, which was somehow worse than shouting. "You've grown thin."

"I've grown free," Mara said. Her voice came out steady. Odile gave her that; he wasn't sure his own would have.

Her father's gaze moved past her, unhurried, cataloguing. It settled on Odile with the particular attention of a man reading a ledger. "The ranger. Ishikawa—or whatever name you're using this decade. You cost me a great deal of money at Fenwick Bend."

"Happy to hear it," Odile said.

"And you're carrying Brannoc's map." Not a question. "I knew Aldric Brannoc when we were apprentices together. Did he tell you that? Before the erasure, he was the finest hydrographer the Combine ever trained. Then he chose a dead country over a living one, and spent forty years forging paper for smugglers." Serafin Voss-Adler sighed, and the sigh was performed, Odile was sure of it, calibrated for the acoustics of the chamber. "Everyone I have ever valued ends up standing on that ledge."

"Everyone you ever valued," Mara said, "ends up disavowed."

Something flickered across the engineer's face—there and gone, fast as a fish under ice. "Come up," he said. "Just you, Mara. Bring the ranger if you must. We'll talk where my voice doesn't need a horn."

* * *

The platform rose on groaning chains, and Odile hated every foot of it. There was no cover on the gantry, no way back down but the same slow ascent, and he counted the armed men along the upper walkways as they climbed—twelve, fourteen, sixteen, rifles slung but hands near. Above the spring, suspended over the Mother's Eye itself, they came level with the topmost sluice-gates and Odile saw for the first time how the whole monstrous arrangement worked: the gates held the river's head back, throttled it, metered it out through the pipes in exactly the quantity the Combine allowed. Downriver towns got what fell to them from a bureaucrat's arithmetic. Everything else went where the fist pointed.

Brannoc had died asking him to swear the water would run free. Standing here, Odile finally understood the full size of the promise.

Serafin Voss-Adler received them on a wide platform overlooking the spring, and dismissed his guards with a flick of two fingers—all but two, who took up posts at the platform's edge with the ease of men who had done this many times. Father and daughter faced each other across ten feet of iron grating, and between them, on a surveyor's table, lay spread a map of the chamber. Odile looked at it and felt the hair rise on his neck: it showed the tunnel they'd climbed, the gorge, the sealed far end. Every route marked. Every one of them annotated in a precise hand with the word *closed*.

Except one. A single line, drawn fresh, ink still faintly shining, running from the platform they stood on down through the machinery to the master control house at the spring's edge. Beside it, in the same hand: *open*.

"I'm going to offer you a trade," Serafin said to his daughter. "And I want you to understand that I am offering it to no one else alive. Not the envoy. Not the council. Me."

"We're listening," Odile said.

"I wasn't speaking to you." But the engineer's eyes flicked to him, and there was something in them that wasn't contempt. Calculation, maybe. Or recognition—one hunted animal taking the measure of another. "Mara. You stole those permits because you wanted out. I know. I've known since the night you left—I read your room before the servants touched it. The false-bottomed case. The forged travel papers. You were always competent; that was never in question. What was in question was whose side competence serves." He stepped closer. "So here is what I offer. Give me the permits and the map. Walk back through that tunnel with me tonight. And in the morning the Combine announces the restoration of its disavowed daughter—full name, full rank, full inheritance. Within five years, the council seat. Within ten—" he smiled, and it was Mara's smile turned inside out, all charm and no warmth, "—the Combine itself. My work, my machine, my rivers, yours. Everything I built, handed to the only person alive fit to hold it."

The chamber roared softly beneath them. *Chunk. Chunk.*

"And them?" Mara asked. Her voice was very quiet. She nodded backward, without looking, toward Odile.

"What about them?"

"If I say yes. What happens to the ranger? To Brannoc's grave? To the twelve towns your arithmetic is starving?"

Serafin Voss-Adler spread his hands, and Odile watched him do it—the gesture of a reasonable man, worn smooth by use. "Towns fail. Rivers are managed. That is not cruelty, Mara, that is engineering. Sentiment is what happens when people mistake water for a birthright. The Combine keeps order. Order keeps more people alive than freedom ever has—you've seen the downriver towns, you've smelled them. Would you drown a thousand lives in the lowlands so a few hundred can feel righteous?" He shook his head slowly. "You were raised better than that. I raised you better than that."

"No," Mara said.

Odile heard it land and saw the engineer absorb it, and what struck him was that neither of them seemed surprised.

"You haven't even considered it," Serafin said.

"I considered it for eleven years," Mara said. "Every night, in your house, under your roof, doing my lessons while you drew your beautiful machines. I considered it every time you told me the erasure was necessary, every time you signed another permit, every time a courier went upstream and didn't come back and you called it attrition." She stepped forward, and Odile saw her father's guards shift, and she did not stop. "You're offering me the Combine, Papa. But I've seen what the Combine does to the people who hold it. It held *you*. Look at you. You're standing on top of a chained river telling your daughter that drowning towns is arithmetic. I don't want your machine." Her voice cracked—not with weakness, Odile realized, but with the effort of keeping grief and fury in the same throat. "I want the water to run downhill. That's all. That's the whole of it. Water runs downhill, and nobody owns it, and everybody drinks."

For a moment—for one long breath—Serafin Voss-Adler looked at his daughter and Odile saw something human move behind the cold. Regret, maybe. Or the memory of a girl building models on his desk.

Then it closed over, like ice forming, and he said, "Take them."

* * *

The guards came fast, and the gunfire started before Odile finished reaching for his rifle.

The first shot went wide because the platform was narrow and the guards were afraid of hitting the engineer—that hesitation bought them the two seconds Odile needed. He fired twice, low, not to kill but to make men dive for cover behind the sluice housings, and grabbed Mara's arm. "The control house! The map!"

She was already moving. Of course she was already moving—she'd read the fresh ink line on her father's survey table the same instant Odile had, and she ran the gantry like a woman running a route she'd dreamed her whole life, boots hammering iron, ducking under pipes, vaulting a chain-hoist while bullets sparked off the railings around her. Odile followed, firing behind them, feeling the strange calm come over him that always came when the shooting started—the restless competence that had carried him across a thousand miles of saltflat, finally aimed at something worth the aiming.

Below them the Mother's Eye boiled against its chains. Above them, on the upper galleries, horns began sounding—alarm calls rolling through the mountain, and beneath them, distantly, the grind of the far tunnel being unsealed. They had minutes.

The control house squatted at the spring's edge, a blockhouse of iron and glass fronting the master gates, and its door was locked, and Mara hit it shoulder-first twice before Odile put his rifle butt through the glass and reached the bar inside. They spilled in among levers and brass dials and walls of gauges, and Mara stood in the middle of it, breathing hard, blood on her temple from a graze Odile hadn't even seen happen, and stared at the controls the way a woman stares at a language she was forbidden to learn.

"Talk to me," Odile said, reloading. "Your father's designs. You built the models."

"He numbered everything." Her hands hovered. "Everything was numbered. The head gates are—" Her finger stabbed at a bank of levers stenciled with figures. "There. Those four. The bypass valves are below, the red wheels—but the sequence matters, if you open the bypasses before the head gates drop, the pressure surge will tear the pipe manifold apart and flood the lower galleries—"

"How long to do it right?"

"Two minutes. Maybe three."

Boots on the gantry outside. Voices. Odile slammed the door and dropped the bar and turned to face the room's single window, rifle up, and behind him he heard the first red wheel begin to turn, groaning, reluctant, a valve that hadn't moved in a generation waking up.

They came at the window in twos and threes and Odile shot the glass out around them and made the doorway a place where men learned caution. He was aware of everything with terrible clarity—the recoil in his wrists, the smell of cordite and deep water, Mara's voice counting sequences behind him, the *chunk* of the great gates stuttering, changing rhythm, changing *mind*. Somewhere above, Serafin Voss-Adler was shouting orders in a voice stripped of all its reasonableness, and the shouting sounded, for the first time, like an ordinary man's fear.

"Head gates dropping!" Mara cried. "Odile—the bypasses—help me turn—"

He left the window, because the door was barred and the glass was empty for the moment, and put his hands on the second red wheel beside hers. It fought them like a living thing, like the river itself objecting to one more delay, and then it broke loose and spun, and the third, and the fourth—

Deep beneath the platform, in the roots of the mountain, something enormous let go.

The sound came up through the iron and through their boot-soles and through their bones: a groan, a shudder, then a rushing roar as the bypass valves opened and the river found, for the first time in twenty years, a path of its own choosing. The gauges swung. Steam screamed from a joint somewhere. And through the shattered window, across the black glass of the Mother's Eye, Odile watched the great head gates tremble on their chains—held, still held, but shaking now, shaking like a door about to give.

Mara leaned against the console, panting, laughing or crying or both. "The gates can't hold against full bypass. When they go—"

"When they go," Odile said, "the water runs downhill."

Outside, on the upper gallery, Serafin Voss-Adler's voice rose over the alarms, ordering the gates reinforced, ordering charges set, ordering his daughter taken alive—and then, cutting through all of it, from the mouth of the far tunnel, a sound that froze Odile's blood:

Gunfire. A lot of it. And a horn call he knew—the Combine's own signal, three notes, repeated: *the claimants are coming. The equinox is tomorrow.*

The head gates groaned. The chains began, link by link, to sing.
