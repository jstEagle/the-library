## 19. The Burning of the Permits

The last night before the equinox, the source did not sleep.

Odile heard it through the stone even as he tried to rest—a vast, throttled breathing beneath the canyon floor, the spring straining against the machinery that caged it. The Combine's engineers had worked through the dark hours by lamplight, patching what Mara and her father's gunfire had broken, but the great gates that turned the water aside still stood, groaning under pressure they were never built to hold back forever. The sound was like a heart beating inside a closed fist.

He lay on cold rock with his bedroll unused beneath him and watched the stars wheel over the rim of the gorge, and he thought about his mother's voice telling stories she should not have told—stories of Vessia, the green nation at the river's head, drowned in ink and erased from every map a generation before he was born. She had told them like contraband, in whispers, with the shutters drawn. *They didn't just take their water,* she'd said once, when he was small enough to believe the world could be argued with. *They took the idea that the water belonged to anyone at all.*

Tomorrow, if the permits lived past dawn, someone would own the source again.

He got up quietly so as not to wake Brannoc's grave-marker—a cairn of river stones they'd raised two bends downstream, too far to see from here, close enough to feel—and walked the ledge toward where Mara kept watch above the machinery floor.

She heard him coming, of course. She always did. "Can't sleep either?" she said without turning.

"Didn't try."

Below them, torchlight moved among the Combine's men. They had come in force since the fight—two hundred at least, ringing the basin of the source with lanterns and rifles, and at their center, under a canopy pitched with insulting neatness, sat the claimants: three men in Combine grey with document cases chained to their wrists, and behind them, pale and bandaged and silent, Mara's father, watching everything with eyes like drilled slate.

"They'll file at first light," Mara said. "Father told me that much, before—" She stopped. Before I saved him from his own flood, she might have said. Before I pulled him out of the water he made. "Equinox sunrise. That's the law they wrote themselves. Claim presented at the equinox is uncontestable for ninety days."

"Then we have until first light," Odile said.

"You have a plan."

It wasn't quite a question, but he let it hang anyway, because the truth was that the plan had been forming in him for days now, ever since Brannoc died with the finished map pressed into his hands and made him swear the oath. *The water will run free.* Not *the water will be ours.* Not *the water will be fairly divided.* Free. He had carried those words up the gorge like a coal cupped in both palms, afraid to look at them directly, because looking meant understanding what they would cost.

"I'm going to burn them," he said.

Mara turned. In the starlight her face was all angles and shadow, and for a long moment she said nothing at all. Below them the spring breathed against its cage.

"The permits," she said finally. "The only thing standing between the Combine and this place being theirs forever. The only proof anyone has that Vessia's claim ever existed. You're going to burn the one thing we bled for."

"That's exactly why." He crouched beside her at the ledge's edge. "Listen to me. If we file those permits—if we claim the source ourselves—we become the Combine. A kinder Combine, maybe. A Combine with better manners. But the water gets an owner again, and owners build gates, and gates are how you starve a dozen towns downriver and call it administration. Brannoc didn't walk a thousand miles to see a new flag over the same cage."

"And your crew?" Her voice was flat. Careful. "Sable voted to sell. Torv voted to sell. You know what burning them means for us. There won't be amnesty, there won't be gold, there won't be anything except warrants with our faces on them from here to the coast."

"I know."

"And you're doing it anyway."

"Yes."

Mara looked away, out over the torchlit basin, and when she spoke again her voice had changed—something loosened in it, or broken loose. "When I stole that chest, I told myself I was escaping my father. I wasn't. I was trying to become him. Take the claim, hold the power, make the world owe me." She laughed, short and joyless. "You're about to burn the only inheritance I have."

"It was never yours," Odile said gently. "That's the whole point. It was never anyone's."

* * *

They gathered the crew in the lee of the machinery housing an hour before dawn, while the stars were going grey and the Combine's camp below was quiet in the way of men waiting for a ceremony. Sable came with her arms crossed and her jaw set. Torv came with his rifle slung and his eyes on the ground. Little Wren, who had joined them at Fenwick Bend and never once complained through the portage of bones, came holding the chest itself, because she had appointed herself its keeper somewhere in the rapids and no one had argued.

"You're really going to do it," Sable said. Not a question either. This crew asked very few questions anymore; they had learned that Odile's silences answered most things.

"At first light," Odile said. "When they present the claim, I burn the permits in front of them. Unfiled, the claim dies. No owner. No ninety days. Nothing for their lawyers to stand on."

"And then what?" Torv's voice was rough. "We're outlaws. Real ones. Not running-from-riders outlaws—burned-the-Combine's-claim-outlaws. Every garrison from here to the saltflats will have our names."

"I know."

"Say something else, then. Give me one reason."

Odile looked at each of them in turn—at Sable, who had lost a brother to a diverted river that dried her town's wells; at Torv, who had nothing but the road and the raft and the people on it; at Wren, thirteen years old, born after Vessia vanished, who had never seen the source run free because she had been born into a world where it never had.

"Because downhill," he said, "there are twelve towns drinking dust. Because the ledger we pulled out of the envoy's camp proves the Combine has known for twenty years what the diversion would do, and filed it under costs. And because tomorrow morning, if we do nothing, the water stays in a cage and we go home rich, and I have been rich enough in my life to know it doesn't keep you warm at night. It just keeps you moving."

Silence. The spring breathed beneath them.

Then Wren stepped forward and held out the chest. "I'll carry it down," she said. "If we're doing this, I want to see their faces."

Sable closed her eyes. When she opened them, something in them had settled—the look of a woman setting down a weight she'd been carrying so long she'd forgotten it was heavy. "Burn them high," she said. "Where everyone can see. If I'm going to be hanged for this, I want a good view."

Torv spat over the edge of the ledge, an old habit, a benediction. "First light, then."

* * *

Dawn came up the gorge like a slow fire, gilding the rim rocks, filling the basin of the source with amber light. The Combine's men assembled in ranks. The canopy came down; ceremony required open sky. The three claimants stood forward with their cases unlocked, and behind them Mara's father rose from his chair, bandaged, upright, refusing to lean on anyone, and began to speak in the carrying voice of a man who had spent his life making large things happen to small places.

"By authority of the Combine's Charter of Concessions, presented at the hour of the equinox—"

"Now," Odile said.

They came over the rim in a line, five of them, walking openly down the switchback trail with the chest borne ahead of them like something in a procession, and the Combine's riflemen swung toward them and hesitated, because no one fires on a procession without orders and no orders came. The claimants' voices faltered. Mara's father stopped mid-sentence.

Odile walked to the center of the basin, to the flat shelf of stone above the caged spring where the machinery's shadow fell shortest, and he set the chest down and opened it one last time.

The permits lay inside in their oiled sleeves, stamped with the seal of Vessia—the wave-and-star, the mark his mother had whispered about across a kitchen table—dated a generation ago, expiring today. Up close he could see the fine work of old Brannoc's authentication, the master forger's final gift, making the dead nation's paper live long enough to matter. Beside them, wrapped in oilcloth, lay the envoy's ledger, the true account of twelve towns starved on purpose.

He lifted the permits out and held them high.

"My mother told me about these," he called, and his voice carried strangely well in the stone bowl of the gorge, the way voices do over water. "In secret. In whispers. Because a whole country was made to forget it existed. Well—I'm done whispering."

Mara's father took a step forward. "Ranger. Whatever you imagine you're owed—"

"I'm not owed anything." Odile drew the lantern from his belt, the one Wren had filled before dawn with lamp oil and prayer. "Neither are you. That's the point. Nobody's owed the source. Not the Combine. Not Vessia's ghost. Not me."

He touched flame to the corner of the topmost permit.

The paper caught with a soft rush, and he held it up burning, and took the next one from the stack and lit it, and the next, feeding them to the fire one by one so that each had its moment—a generation of signatures curling to ash, seals blackening, dates dissolving—and the wind off the spring carried the sparks upward in a slow bright spiral, and two hundred riflemen watched a nation die a second death, freely given this time, and understood that they were watching something else be born.

"No claim," Odile said, as the last permit went. "Unfiled. Expired. Dead. The source belongs to no one—which means it belongs to everyone downstream of here. Which means it runs free."

"Seize them," Mara's father said, but his voice cracked on it, and in the hesitation that followed, Torv moved.

Torv had spent the night, while the others slept or kept watch, doing what Torv did best: quiet work with tools and grudges. While the claim burned, he stood at the diversion gate's control housing with a pry bar, and when Odile shouted his name he threw his whole weight onto the bar, and the sabotaged coupling—the one weakened during the firefight, the one the Combine's engineers had patched too hastily—gave with a crack like the world's spine breaking.

The gates shuddered. Groaned. Began, inch by screaming inch, to swing open.

What came through was not water at first but sound—a rising roar from deep in the rock, the spring's held breath finally released—and then the water itself, white and furious and ancient, bursting through the opened gates and flooding the dry channel that had waited a generation for it, tearing at the machinery, ripping scaffolding away like paper, sending the Combine's men scrambling for high ground with their ceremony scattered behind them.

Mara's father stood his ground in the flood's path, staring at it, and Odile saw in his face—for one unguarded instant—not rage but something worse: recognition. The engineer watching his life's work come apart, and knowing, in whatever was left of his soul, that it had always deserved to.

"Downstream towns get their water," Sable said beside Odile, watching the channel fill. Her voice shook. "Twelve towns. All of them."

"Yes."

"And we get warrants."

"Yes."

She was quiet a moment. Then: "Worth it."

The fire in Odile's hands had gone out; only ash swirled upward now, mingling with spray, falling on the stone like grey snow. He looked at the empty chest, at the freed water thundering down the channel toward the lowlands, at his crew—Wren laughing, actually laughing, spray-soaked and wild-eyed; Torv leaning on his bent pry bar like a pilgrim at a shrine; Sable with tears cutting tracks through the dust on her face—and he felt the strangest thing, a thing he had not felt since childhood, since before the debt and the discarded name and the years of never stopping:

Stillness. Standing perfectly still, in the middle of the loudest place on earth, and wanting nothing except to stay.

Then a rifle shot cracked off the rim rocks, and the moment shattered.

"Riders on the north rim!" Wren screamed, pointing. "A whole column—they were waiting for the filing—"

And from the chaos below, through the smoke and spray, Mara's father's voice rose one last time, hoarse and carrying, aimed like a blade:

"Take them alive. *All of them.*"

Odile grabbed the empty chest and ran.
