## 21. Hunted, Together

The warrants found us before we did.

We came down off the high water in the last week of the equinox month, four of us on a raft that had been three boats and a barge's worth of luck, and the first town we touched—Carrow's Ferry, all mud walls and rope bridges and the smell of wet clay after a decade of thirst—had our faces already nailed to the tollhouse door.

Not painted. *Nailed.* The Combine did not waste ink on men they meant to find. The paper was heavy stock, the kind that survived rain, and the likeness of my face was good enough that an old woman selling melons by the landing squinted at me for a long moment before she looked away and said, very loudly, to no one in particular, that the melons were fresh today, very fresh, and wouldn't the traveler like to buy one and be on his way.

I bought three. I paid too much. Neither of us said anything else, and I understood that I had just been given the only kind of gift the desert knows how to give: silence, bought and paid for, no questions asked.

Mara read the warrant over my shoulder, her breath stirring the paper's corner. *Odile Ishikawa. Mara Vessia-Calden. Brannoc Hale, deceased. Accomplices unknown. Reward: five hundred crowns, dead or alive. The Combine thanks all loyal subjects of the river.*

"Brannoc's on there too," she said quietly. "Deceased."

"He earned that word," I said. "We didn't."

Below the names, in smaller type, the charge: *Conspiracy against the lawful waters. Destruction of Combine property. Theft of sovereign claim.*

Sovereign claim. I thought of the permits curling into ash at the source, the sparks climbing the updraft like startled birds, and I thought of the sound the water had made when the diversion gates cracked—not a roar, at first, but a groan, deep and mineral, the sound of a thing that had been held back for a generation finally being allowed to remember its own weight. Then the roar. Then everything else.

"They got the charge wrong," Mara said. "We didn't conspire against the water. We just stopped conspiring *for* them."

"That distinction won't hold up at a hanging," I said.

* * *

Carrow's Ferry fed us anyway.

That was the thing I could not make fit in my head, that first night, sitting in the back room of a rope-maker's shop while the whole town pretended not to know we were there. We were outlaws. The warrants were on every post from the source to the sea, or near enough. The Combine's riders moved in pairs now, in threes, asking their questions in the flat polite voice of men who had been told the answer didn't matter. And still the rope-maker's wife set out stew still warm from the pot, and still the rope-maker himself sat across from me sharpening a blade he clearly had no intention of using, and still the children were sent to bed *before* we arrived, so that no small ears could hear names that might be repeated.

"You don't understand what you did," the rope-maker said at last. He was maybe sixty, hands like braided hide. "You think you broke something. You didn't break anything. You *returned* something. My father farmed this bend when the river still came through. I was a boy when the water started going thin. I watched them dig the diversion channels at night, with lanterns, like thieves. Like *thieves*, ranger, in their own country." He set the blade down. "For thirty years I have carried water four miles to keep eight rows of beans alive. Last month my daughter swam in the old channel. *Swam.* Do you understand? There is water under the bridge my grandfather built, water that was promised to this town in a charter nobody can find anymore, and my daughter swam in it."

He was crying, I realized. Not dramatically. The way old men cry, without changing expression, as if the tears were simply a leak in a vessel too old to hold.

"I can't shelter you," he said. "If the riders come asking, I'll tell them the truth, that strangers passed through and I fed them because I feed everyone, it's known, ask anyone. But you'll sleep behind my shop tonight, and in the morning my brother will walk you to the ferry pool, and there will be a boat, and no one in this town will remember your faces, because we have all had a great deal of practice not remembering things."

* * *

It went like that, town after town, all the way down the revived channels.

We were not traveling as fugitives anymore, though the warrants said we were. We were traveling the way water travels—finding the low ground, the kindness, the crack in the wall of the world—and the desert, which had been dying of thirst for a generation, had suddenly developed a flood of small mercies and nowhere to put them.

At Sallow Bend, a ferryman took us across at night and refused payment, then took payment anyway when Mara pressed her father's gold into his hand—"for the boat," she said, "not for us," and the ferryman looked at the coin, Combine-minted, and spat into the river and kept it anyway, because gold is gold and spite is free. At the ruins of Old Vessia itself—or what the maps called nothing, the blank stretch where a nation had been erased—we found people living in the drowned foundations, fishing the restored channels, and an old man with a Vessian accent thick as silt took one look at Brannoc's name on the warrant and began to weep, and asked us, over and over, whether it was true, whether the source was free, whether the water at the source ran *downhill* now, the way the old songs said it used to.

"It runs downhill," Mara told him. "It runs all the way to the sea."

"Then Vessia isn't drowned," the old man said. "Vessia is just upstream."

I have carried a lot of words across a lot of salt, and I will carry that one the rest of my life.

* * *

The cost came due in smaller coins, at first.

We could not use the ferries at the Combine garrisons. We could not sleep in inns, buy supplies in market towns, or send word ahead by courier, because every courier was a Combine courier now, or could be. My face was worth five hundred crowns, and in the desert five hundred crowns was a farm, a herd, a decade of safety, and I did not blame a single soul who studied me too long at a well. Twice we had to move at night when a settlement's hospitality curdled into calculation. Once, a boy who had guided us for a day's wage disappeared at dusk, and we spent a bad night in a dry wash listening for riders that never came—though whether that was loyalty or luck, I never learned.

Mara took it hardest. She had spent her whole life inside the Combine's architecture, and now she lived in the negative space of it—the gaps between patrols, the hours between checkpoints, the towns too small or too stubborn to matter. She had betrayed her father, her name, her inheritance, and the Combine answered by making her a ghost with a price on her head.

"They'll never stop," she said one night, at a fire we'd built in a culvert, small and smokeless. "You understand that. My father doesn't forgive. The Combine doesn't *forget*. We'll be running this river until we're old, and then we'll be running it until we're dead."

I fed the fire a stick and watched the sparks do their brief climb, the same climb the burning permits had made, and I thought about what the rope-maker had said, and what the old man at Old Vessia had said, and what Brannoc would have said, which was probably something about how a man gets one death and should spend it on something.

"Brannoc wanted to see the source before he died," I said. "He saw it. It cost him everything he had left, and he'd tell you it was a bargain." I looked at her. "You keep talking like we lost something. What did we lose? A name? I lost mine years before the Combine ever printed it on paper. A home? I never had one. What we had before was movement without destination. What we have now is exile with an address."

She frowned at me. "That's not a home."

"No," I admitted. "It's the *down payment* on one. And every town that feeds us, every ferryman who looks the other way, every kid who doesn't sell us to the riders—that's another payment. We're not buying safety, Mara. We're buying something better. We're buying *witnesses*. You can't hang four people the whole river has decided to remember kindly. The Combine knows it too. That's why the warrants say *dead or alive* and the riders keep their distance. They're not afraid of us. They're afraid of what happens if they make martyrs of us in a town full of people whose daughters just went swimming."

* * *

The letter came at Tarn's Landing, and it was the first thing in weeks that frightened me.

Not a warrant. A letter, hand-delivered by a girl of maybe ten who said a man on a gray horse gave her a coin and asked no questions, which should have been the first warning, because men who ask no questions have already had their questions answered. The letter was unsigned. The hand was careful, educated, and cold.

*You believe the river is free. You believe the towns will shelter you. Towns are weather, ranger; they change with the season. The Combine has been patient for thirty years. We can be patient for thirty more. The girl's father asks after her health. He is not well. Consider what her name will be worth when he is gone, and what it will cost when the protection of his office dies with him. There are men below you on the river who are not patient, who are not official, and who do not issue warrants. You have until the harvest moon to come in. After that, you are simply a problem, and problems of your kind are solved quietly.*

Mara read it twice, her face doing nothing at all, which was how I knew it was doing a great deal.

"It's my father's handwriting," she said finally. "Or his secretary's, writing what he dictated. He's telling the truth, though. The part about the unofficial men. There's a division—the Quiet Water, my mother used to call it, half as a joke. They don't wear colors. They don't file reports. They solve problems."

"Then we solve them first," I said, "or we outrun them, or we make sure that every town between here and the sea knows their faces too. Warrants cut both ways, Mara. If they can print our names, we can print theirs."

"You want to fight the Combine with *paper*?"

"I want to fight them with the only thing they can't buy back," I said. "The truth, notarized. Brannoc taught me that. A forgery only works if it's *better* than the real thing—and their whole government is a forgery of a country that used to be real. We have the envoy's ledger. We have the map of the diversion channels, in Brannoc's hand, dated and witnessed. We have a dead man's sworn statement that I intend to have copied in every market town from here to the delta, with the Quiet Water's faces drawn underneath, so that the next time one of them buys silence from a child, the child's mother knows exactly what she's looking at."

Mara was quiet for a long time. The culvert dripped. Somewhere above us, a night bird called across the water, and the water answered the way water does now, running, always running, downhill and free.

"You really believe the towns will hold," she said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I believe the towns are the country," I said. "The Combine is just the paperwork. And paperwork, in my experience, burns."

* * *

We left Tarn's Landing before dawn, and the whole town, in its way, came to see us off.

Nobody said goodbye. That wasn't how it was done, we'd learned. The melon-seller's cousin loaded our raft with food that had fallen off a cart. The rope-maker's brother walked us to the pool and checked our lashings with a professional eye and said nothing at all. A woman pressed a bundle into Mara's hands—dried fish, a jar of honey, and, wrapped inside, a scrap of cloth with an old Vessian border stitched around the hem, the kind of thing a grandmother keeps against the day it means something again.

Mara held it like it might dissolve.

At the last bend, looking back, I saw them on the bank—maybe thirty people, maybe more, standing in the gray light among the reeds, not waving, not calling out, just *standing*, the way you stand for a funeral or a birth. Witnesses. And I understood, finally, what Brannoc had tried to tell me with his last map, and what the water had been trying to tell me since the gates broke, and what the old man at Old Vessia had meant.

You don't find home. You don't even build it, not exactly. You *irrigate* it. You put the truth in the high ground and you let it run downhill, town to town, hand to hand, and you accept that some of it will soak away and some of it will be stolen and some of it will be muddied past recognition—but enough of it arrives. Enough of it always arrives. And where it arrives, things grow.

We were hunted. That was the price, and I paid it without haggling. Five hundred crowns for my face, a quiet division of killers on our trail, a lifetime of looking over my shoulder. In exchange: a river that ran, a nation un-drowned, a daughter unmade and remade, and a string of towns from the source to the sea that would lie for us, feed us, and remember us.

I had spent my whole life moving because stopping meant being found. Now I was the most found man in the desert, and for the first time, I understood that being found was not the same as being caught.

Mara stood at the stern, the Vessian cloth folded against her chest, watching the bank recede.

"Where do we go now?" she asked. "The Combine will seal the garrison towns. The Quiet Water will work upriver. We can't stay ahead of them forever."

I looked downriver, toward the delta, toward the sea, and then back upriver, toward the source, toward the spring that now ran open to the sky, unclaimed, unguarded by anything but distance and the memory of what it had cost.

"We go where the water goes," I said. "And then we go where it came from. There's a spring up there with no name on any map, and the Combine's paperwork says it belongs to them, and our paperwork says it belongs to no one—and I think it's time we stopped arguing with their documents and started writing our own. Brannoc wanted it mapped openly so no one could claim it in the dark. So we map it. We map it, and we post it, and we put copies in every town that ever carried water four miles for eight rows of beans. Let the Combine explain to a hundred witnesses why a spring needs an owner."

Mara was quiet. Then, slowly, she smiled—the first real one since the weir, tired and crooked and entirely hers.

"You know they'll hunt us harder for that."

"They're already hunting us," I said, and pushed off from the bank. "Might as well give them something to hunt us *for*."

Behind us, Carrow's Landing stood in the dawn with its people on the shore, and the river carried us down, and the water—free, unowned, running the way it had always meant to run—went with us.
