## 1. The Chest That Shouldn't Exist

The river kept its dead the way a miser keeps coins — close, and counted, and never willingly surrendered. Marguerite Winterbourne had learned that in her first season on the water, at seventeen, when her father's dredge came up with a church bell still ringing faintly in the imagination, green as pond scum, heavy as guilt. Twelve years later she still went down expecting the river to lie to her.

The *Coriander* had been lying on her side in the silt off Grayhaven Bend for eleven years, and the navy had spent three of those years pretending she wasn't there. Marguerite knew the choreography of official forgetting: the survey boats that came at dusk with their instruments humming, the divers who sealed the hatches with government wax, the notice nailed to the harbor board in language so bloodless it might have been describing a fence repair. *Vessel decommissioned. Cargo of no recoverable value. Site closed to salvage.*

No recoverable value. Marguerite had salvaged enough navy-scrubbed hulls to know that phrase was a lock, not a description. Locks meant something behind them worth locking.

She went down at dawn, when the light came through the water in long amber columns and the patrol boats were still asleep in their berth. The *Coriander* rose out of the silt like a whale's ribcage, her rail furred with zebra mussels, her name plate crusted but legible. Marguerite ran her gloved thumb along the letters and felt the small tremor she always felt — the river's dead deserved at least a moment of acknowledgment before she went through their pockets.

Inside, the hold had been swept. That was the first wrongness. Navy scrubbing was thorough but never *this* thorough; there should have been chain, dunnage, the bones of cargo. Instead the deck was scoured to bare timber, except for one corner aft where the silt had drifted back in over the years, patient as snowfall, and buried something the sweepers had missed — or been ordered to miss.

Her knife found the corner of it before her eyes did. Iron banding. Oak. The shape of a chest, not a crate — a chest meant to be carried by hand, meant to matter.

She worked it free in a haze of stirred silt, and when she hauled it up into the amber light and turned it, her breath fogged her mask and her heart did something complicated in her chest.

Stamped into the lid, deep and deliberate, was a seal she had only ever seen on the gravestones in the drowned cemetery upriver: the double-headed heron of the Vellmark Republic, wings spread over a river rendered as a single unbroken line.

The Republic had been dissolved before she was born. Its concessions, its charters, its very name had been absorbed into the shipping company that now owned every berth, every toll, every mile of navigable water from Grayhaven to the head of passage. The heron was a ghost. And here it was, stamped in iron, on a chest that the navy had scrubbed a wreck to hide.

* * *

She didn't open it on the boat. That was discipline, and discipline was the only inheritance her father had left her that couldn't be repossessed. She brought the chest up in her lifting net, hauled it over the gunwale of the *Magpie* with a groan of winch cable, wrapped it in her storm tarp, and ran her boat up the creek to the boathouse where the reeds grew tall enough to hide a hull from anything but a heron's eye.

Only there, with the door barred and the light coming through the knotholes in dusty blades, did she cut the seal.

The lock was old Republic work, a warded thing that surrendered to her pick with a sigh, as if it had been waiting. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, were two things.

The first was a document. She spread it on the workbench and read it three times, because the first two readings refused to be true. It was a deed of inheritance — a grant of the upper passage, from Grayhaven Bend to the source, to one *Aurelio Vantrease and his heirs in perpetuity*, witnessed, sealed, signed in a hand that trembled slightly on the final letter. The date was the year the Republic fell.

The second was a map. Hand-drawn, ink faded to the color of weak tea, showing the river from its mouth to a place marked only with the heron and two words in old Vellmark script: *where it begins*.

Marguerite sat with it a long time. The boathouse dripped. Somewhere out in the reeds a bittern boomed, once, like a door closing.

Because she knew the story every river kid knew. Aurelio Vantrease's inheritance was the founding stone of the Vantrease Shipping Concern — the paper that turned one family into the owner of the water and everyone on it. And she knew the communities upriver, the ones the toll gates had strangled, the ferry families and net-casters whose grandparents had worked those waters free and woken one morning to find the river belonged to a dead man's son. She had grown up downstream of their grievance the way you grow up downstream of a tannery: you stopped smelling it, but it was in everything.

And the deed in front of her was wrong. She couldn't have said at first how she knew — it was the sum of it, the ink too uniform, the wax poured over a seal already pressed, the signature's tremor *repeated* in three places as if traced. Forgeries were like the river's lies: they held until you looked at them from below.

She was still looking when the knock came.

* * *

Two of them, navy, in shore clothes that fooled no one. The taller one had a voice like a man reading regulations to himself in the dark.

"Miz Winterbourne. The *Coriander* site is closed. We have reason to believe salvage activity occurred this morning. Anything recovered is government property under the writ of abandonment. You'll surrender it now, and there'll be no further inquiry."

Marguerite leaned in the boathouse doorway with the river at her back and the chest behind her, under the loose floorboard beneath the tar pot, and she thought about how many times she had heard versions of that sentence. *The site is closed. The matter is settled. The river belongs to the Concern.* The whole world, it seemed, was a locked hatch with a notice nailed to it.

"I salvage for a living," she said. "I don't keep inventories for the navy."

"Then you won't mind if we look."

"I mind on principle."

Something shifted in the tall one's face — not anger. Something wearier and more dangerous, something that looked almost like regret. He glanced past her at the tar pot, at the floorboards, and for a half second she was certain he saw straight through the planks to the oilcloth and the heron seal. Then he touched his cap brim, and the two of them walked back down the creek path, their footfalls swallowed by the reeds.

She watched them go. Then she watched the water, where their launch idled at the mouth of the creek, and she understood, with the cold clarity of a diver at depth, that the *Coriander* had not sunk. Ships don't take on water along one clean seam, in calm weather, within sight of three navy picket boats, unless someone opens the sea-cocks. The wreck was no accident. It was a burial. And she had just gone and dug up the grave.

They would come back. Not the two polite men in shore clothes — someone with less regret and more tools. The Concern did not write letters when it could send fire.

Marguerite went inside, pried up the floorboard, and looked at the deed a last time. *Aurelio Vantrease and his heirs.* Somewhere upriver there were heirs of a different kind — the ones the paper had erased, families who had never stopped knowing the river was stolen, even if they'd lost the words to prove it. And somewhere on that faded map, at the place marked *where it begins*, was whatever the Concern had drowned a ship to keep from being found.

She wrapped the deed and the map in fresh oilcloth, sealed them in a tarred canvas roll, and thought of her father's last lesson, the one he'd given with a diver's knife flat on his palm: *The river gives up nothing for free. Whatever it hands you, you carry, or you drown carrying it.*

Marguerite Winterbourne rolled the canvas tight, slung it across her back, and went to find out who the Vantrease deed had erased — because by nightfall, she suspected, she would need a crew.
