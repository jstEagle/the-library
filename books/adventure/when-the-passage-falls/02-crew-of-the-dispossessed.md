## 2. Crew of the Dispossessed

The chest sat in Marguerite's cellar like a sleeping animal, breathing salt.

She had wrapped it in oilcloth and buried it under a false floor of rotted planking, beneath three decades of her father's junk — cracked dive masks, a rusted winch, jars of pickled eel nobody would ever eat. Every night she came down with a lantern and told herself she was being paranoid. Every morning she woke with the deed's words printed on the inside of her eyelids: *the inheritance of Aldous Vane, granted by decree of the Provisional Crown, in perpetuity along the waters of the Meridian Passage* — signed in a hand that trembled at the descenders. Forgers always rushed their flourishes. The dead king's chancery had never rushed anything.

Three days after she'd surfaced with it, a navy launch had idled past her mooring twice, slow as a shark considering. On the third day, an officer in a gray coat climbed her dock and asked, politely, whether she'd recovered any artifacts of historical interest from the *Coriander*. She said no. He looked at her hands — diver's hands, scarred, honest-looking — and thanked her for her service to the river's memory.

That night she started making lists of people who hated the Vane Concession Company more than they feared it.

* * *

Tomas Ferreira sold bait and boat repair off a sagging pier at Halfway Bend, where the river widened and forgot itself. He was twenty-six, built like a bollard, with his grandmother's stubborn jaw and ink-stained fingers from mending nets. When Marguerite laid the forged deed on his counter between two trays of worms, he read it once, slowly, and then again with his lips moving, and when he looked up his eyes were wet and furious in equal measure.

"This is my family's name," he said. "Vane took it off the rolls. My grandmother used to say we owned water once. Everyone thought she was senile."

"She wasn't." Marguerite tapped the seal. "This grant is fake. Which means somewhere there's a real one, and whoever holds it owns everything the company pretends to own. There's a map in the chest. It points upstream, past the last survey marker, to the source."

"The source is forbidden," Tomas said. "Company water. They shoot first and file permits later."

"Then we go fast." She held out her hand. "I need crew. I can't read seals or run rapids alone, and I can't fight enforcers with a pry bar."

Tomas wiped his eyes with the heel of his palm, a rough, angry gesture, and shook her hand hard enough to hurt. "My grandmother died owing rent on land that was ours," he said. "I'm in. Whatever this costs."

* * *

The pilot found them, which should have worried Marguerite more than it did.

Sable Okonkwo ran cargo up the Passage in a flat-bottomed skiff called the *Liar's Prayer*, dodging company toll stations by running the narrows at night with her lights doused. She was fifty, silver-haired, missing two fingers to an old propeller accident, and she laughed like a woman who had already survived everything worth fearing.

"You want to run the upper reaches during flood season," she said, turning the map over in her remaining fingers. "With navy patrols out because somebody robbed a wreck the navy scrubbed. With company gunboats behind you." She grinned. "What's the pay?"

"Justice," Tomas said.

"Terrible currency." Sable studied them both, then folded the map into her coat. "But I've been paid worse. I'll take you as far as the gorge. Past that, even I don't know the channels — the maps end at the falls."

"They don't," Marguerite said. "Not all of them."

* * *

Callum Reyes stood in the archive annex of the Naval Hydrographic Office and felt the truth of his career settle over him like cold water.

He was the officer who had walked Marguerite's dock. He had filed the report on the *Coriander* himself, four years ago — *hull loss due to structural failure, cargo manifest sealed per concession agreement* — and he had believed, then, that sealing manifests was ordinary procedure. Bureaucracy. Paperwork. Tonight, cross-referencing the wreck's registry against the ledger of the Office of Concessions, he found his own signature reproduced eleven times down a column headed *DISPOSITION AUTHORIZED*, beside entries that were not wrecks at all but *removals*: barges burned at anchor, a ferry lost with witnesses aboard, a customs sloop scuttled with its logs intact.

Beside each entry, in the margin, small neat initials: C.R.

His initials. His authorization. Four years of them, backdated, some dated to days he could prove he'd spent ashore with fever. Someone had made him the ghost author of a cover-up so thorough it had its own filing system.

He sat down slowly on a stool among the charts. Outside, rain worked at the windows. The lamp hummed. Callum Reyes, thirty-one years old, decorated, dutiful, orphaned by the same river he served, understood that he had two choices: report the anomaly and be quietly disappeared like the ferry, or find out who had written him into the ledger — and what else the ledger held.

He copied three pages by lamplight, folded them inside his coat, and went home to pack a bag.

* * *

The archivist came last, and nearly didn't come at all.

Wren Halloway worked in the Records Hall at Copperside, cataloging pre-Dissolution documents for a salary that insulted her, in a basement that flooded every spring. She was small, sharp-eyed, allergic to sunlight, and the only person in the province who could distinguish a genuine Provisional Crown seal from a forgery at ten paces — a skill she'd developed out of spite, she said, because half the "historical treasures" donated to the Hall were fakes made by bored nobles.

Marguerite brought her a wax impression of the chest's seal, and Wren turned it under her loupe for a long time without speaking.

"This is good," she said finally. "Very good. The matrix was cut from an authentic die — probably stolen from a provincial chancery during the Dissolution. But look here." Her fingernail, bitten to the quick, touched a point at the seal's edge. "The wax was poured too hot. It's shrunk away from the impression at the rim. A period seal would have been poured cool, pressed slow. Whoever made this had the die but not the craft." She looked up. "Where did you get this?"

"A drowned ship."

"And you want me to come with you." It wasn't a question. Wren set down the loupe and began, methodically, wrapping instruments in cloth. "I've spent nine years cataloguing lies that people call history. Nine years of stamping *authentic* on documents I knew were forged because the donation letters came with contributions attached." She tied off the bundle. "I would like, just once, to be on the side of the truth. Even if the truth is wet."

* * *

They were loading the *Liar's Prayer* at dawn when the enforcers came.

Marguerite saw the launch first — black hull, no registration, six men in gray with the Vane Company's otter-and-crown stamped on their shoulders. She had time to shout one word before the first flare arced over the water.

The *Kestrel's Grace* — her boat, her father's boat, the only thing she had owned outright in thirty-one years of living on the river — went up like a struck match. The enforcers had soaked her deck in lamp oil while she slept; the fire caught the tarred seams and climbed the mast and ate the sail in seconds, and the heat of it pushed her back against Sable's rail while her whole life burned down to the waterline. She heard the timbers crack. She smelled thirty years of her father's work dissolving into smoke, and she did not cry, though something in her chest tore loose and sank.

Tomas held her arm. Sable cast off, cool as morning, and put the skiff's bow upstream. The enforcer launch circled the burning wreck once, a man in the bow taking photographs for the company's files, and then turned away, satisfied.

"They wanted to leave you nothing to come back to," Tomas said quietly.

"Then they've made a mistake." Marguerite watched the fire shrink behind them, a red coin on gray water. Her livelihood was ash. Her name would be on a list by nightfall. Everything measured in miles — moorings, routes, the radius of a safe life — had just been taken from her, and what remained was the chest in the hold, the map in Sable's coat, and four strangers standing shoulder to shoulder on a smuggler's deck.

It was, she realized with a strange lightness, the first crew she had ever trusted.

From the stern, Wren cleared her throat. "There's something you should see." She had the forged deed spread on a crate, weighted at the corners, and beside it a magnifying glass and one of her wrapped instruments. "The forgery is dated the ninth of Thawmonth. But the Provisional Crown fell on the fourth." She looked up, and her eyes behind the smudged lenses were bright with terrible certainty. "Whoever wrote this didn't just forge an inheritance. They forged it *after* the authority that could legally grant it had ceased to exist — which means the entire concession chain downstream rests on a document that was never valid for a single day."

Sable whistled low. "So the company doesn't own the river."

"The company never owned the river," Wren said. "And someone upstream has been hiding the proof for sixty years."

Marguerite turned her face upriver, where the gorges narrowed and the flood-swollen water ran brown and fast, toward the source no chart dared name.

"Then let's go collect it," she said, "before the water does."

Behind them, far downstream, a navy launch slipped its mooring and turned to follow — and at its rail stood a gray-coated officer, watching them go, with stolen pages hidden inside his coat.
