## 3. Upstream Against the Clock

The river woke them before dawn, drumming rain on the tarped deck of the *Kestrel's Mercy* like a fist demanding entry. Marguerite stood at the wheel with her hands wrapped around spokes gone slick and cold, watching the water rise in the gray half-light. Two days ago it had run green. Now it ran the color of old bronze, thick with mountain melt, carrying whole trees past them like matchsticks.

"Permit expires at midnight," Tomas said, coming up beside her with two cups of chicory that steamed against the wet air. He handed her one without asking. Three days of running the river together had taught him her habits better than most people learned them in years.

"Then we make the narrows by midnight." She drank. The chicory was terrible. She drank it anyway. "After that we're pirates."

"We were already pirates," said Odile from the pilot's bench, where she sat with her boots up and her smuggler's charts spread across her knees, weighted down with brass fittings. "The difference is paperwork."

Marguerite almost smiled. Almost. Her boat — her first boat, the one the enforcers had burned to the waterline at Vellacourt dock — still ached in her chest like a missing tooth. The *Mercy* was Odile's vessel, borrowed, patched, and ugly as a mudskipper, but she answered the wheel honestly, and honesty was more than Marguerite had gotten from anyone official in a month.

Below decks, she could hear Père Anselme reading aloud from the old deed, his voice a low liturgy of legal phrases in a dead dialect, translating for Tomas line by line. *The waters from the source to the sea, granted in perpetuity to the family Verane and their heirs entire.* Forged in perpetuity, too. But the forgery only mattered if they could prove what it had replaced.

The map said the original waited upstream, where the river began. The map also said the river began somewhere no permit in the republic reached.

"Hard to starboard," Odile said suddenly, sitting up. "We've got company."

Marguerite heard it before she saw it: the flat, patient slap of an outboard motor working upstream against the current, somewhere behind the willow screen of the far bank. Navy hulls didn't work that quietly. These were the other kind.

"Kill the lamp," she said. "Tomas, get below. Anselme — nothing leaves this boat that can be read."

The motor sound faded, circled, faded again. They held their breath through four full minutes of rain. Then it went away downstream, hunting some other ghost, and Marguerite let the air out of her lungs slowly, the way she'd learned to do at forty feet down when the tank gauge lied.

They were being herded, she thought. Not caught — herded. Someone wanted them tired, wanted them shallow on supplies, wanted them making mistakes.

She turned the wheel upstream and gave the river more throttle.

* * *

Callum Reyes found his own name on the third page of the ledger, and it undid him more thoroughly than any court-martial ever could.

He'd taken the file from the district records office under a legitimate requisition — that was the bitter joke of it; everything he'd done to bury this had been done legitimately, stamped and countersigned and filed. He sat now in the stern of the patrol launch with the ledger open on his knees while the rain came down and Ensign Braithwaite steered them upriver after the smuggler's boat, and Callum read his own handwriting confirming receipt of sealed materials recovered from the wreck of the *Ardent Grace*, disposition: destroyed.

Disposition: destroyed. Except one chest hadn't been, because a salvage diver with quick hands and a quicker conscience had pulled it out of the dark before the burn team arrived.

"You all right, sir?" Braithwaite asked.

"Fine." He closed the ledger. His name was on page three. On page seven was Commander Vasquez, who had ordered the write-off. On page eleven, in ink older than the rest, was the name of the concession company's founding director — and beside it, in the same hand, the word *Verane*, struck through once, cleanly, like a man crossing out a debt he'd decided never to pay.

Callum had grown up on the lower river. He had eaten at tables in the drowned villages — not yet drowned then, just poor — where old men still spoke of land they'd lost to lawyers instead of floods. He had joined the navy partly because the navy was the only institution on the river that had never cheated his family. That was the belief he'd carried for twenty years, and here it was, disproven in eleven pages of his own filing system.

"Sir, contact ahead. Port side, behind the shoal marker."

Callum looked up. Through the rain he saw the *Mercy*'s blunt silhouette sliding into the white water above the shoals, riding high and reckless, exactly where no sane pilot would take a loaded boat.

"They're running the Narrows," Braithwaite said, incredulous.

Of course they were. The permits died at midnight, and the flood was coming, and every hour they waited was another hour for the concession's hired boats to close the net. Callum understood the arithmetic perfectly. He'd helped write it.

"Break off," he heard himself say.

Braithwaite turned. "Sir?"

"Break off, Ensign. Log it as mechanical failure. I'll sign the report." He was already pulling off his dress cap, already reaching for the waterproof bag he'd packed in secret three nights ago, telling himself he was only gathering evidence. "I'm going ashore at the bend."

"Alone? In this?" Braithwaite stared at him, young and honest and baffled, and Callum felt the full weight of what he was about to do settle onto his shoulders like the rain.

"Yes," he said. "In this."

* * *

The Narrows took everything the river had and asked for more.

Marguerite drove the *Mercy* through standing waves taller than the gunwale, water hitting the deck in cold slabs, Odile calling depths from memory and chart while Anselme clung to the mast step and prayed in a dialect older than either. The map — the true map, the one from the chest — showed a channel through the shoals marked in a dead nation's hydrographic code, and Anselme had spent the whole night decoding it, and now Marguerite bet all their lives that a scholar's translation beat a smuggler's instinct.

"Left of the broken spire!" Anselme shouted. "The old chart says left!"

"The old chart is eighty years out of date!" Odile shouted back.

"Trust it or drown, choose quickly!"

Marguerite chose. She put the bow left of the broken limestone spire, into water that looked like certain death, and the river dropped away beneath them — a hidden channel, dredged deep by some forgotten engineering corps, running clean between the shoals like a road under a blanket. The *Mercy* shot through with the engine screaming and everyone aboard laughing or crying or both.

Then the ambush hit them at the top of the rapids, where the channel narrowed to a stone throat.

The first shot took out the fuel line. The second shattered the lantern. Marguerite saw them then — three men on the cliff shelf above, rifles braced, professional and unhurried, the concession's enforcers picking their moment the way they picked everything. The *Mercy*, her engine dying, swung broadside into the current and began to drift toward the rocks.

"Tomas!" Marguerite screamed it over the water. "Get down—"

But Tomas was already moving, because Tomas had spent his childhood on this river and knew what a drifting boat needed: weight aft, fast. He threw himself across the stern to hold the rudder true, and the rifleman adjusted, and the shot meant for the engine found him instead, high in the shoulder, spinning him into the scuppers with a sound Marguerite would hear for the rest of her life.

They got through. Barely. Odile ran the last of the rapids on current alone, kissing rocks by inches, and grounded them hard in a willow-choked eddy two miles beyond, where the bank rose steep and the enforcers' rifles couldn't reach. Anselme stanched the wound with river salt and prayer. Tomas, gray-lipped, gripped Marguerite's wrist and said, "Don't stop. Whatever it costs. Don't stop."

They lost the fuel, most of the food, and the spare charts. What remained of the crew sat in the dripping willows while the rain kept falling and the river kept rising, and Marguerite counted what they had left: half a tank, a decoded map, a wounded heir, and midnight coming.

That was when the man stepped out of the trees with his hands raised — a navy officer, soaked through, dress cap long abandoned, holding a leather satchel above the waterline like an offering.

"Commander Callum Reyes," he said. "Page three of your enemies' ledger has my name on it. I'm here to help you make them regret writing it down."

Marguerite looked at him for a long moment, rain streaming off her chin, the river roaring behind them like something impatient to be obeyed.

Above the gorge, far upstream, the first thunder of the flood season rolled down out of the mountains — and beneath it, faint but unmistakable, the sound of more engines starting.
