## 4. Where the River Begins

The river stopped being a river two miles before it began.

It thinned into a chain of pools strung like dropped coins through a gorge so narrow the sky was a rumor overhead, and then even the pools gave out, and there was only wet stone and the sound of water coming from somewhere beneath the world. Marguerite climbed the last of it with the map pressed flat against her chest inside oilskin, Tomas's rope taut at her waist, and thought: *this is where everything I ever measured myself by runs out.* No depth gauge for this. No fathoms. Just altitude, and the thin cold air, and the ache in her shoulders that had nothing to do with hauling.

The chapel appeared all at once, the way drowned things do.

One moment there was only gorge wall and grey spray; the next, a bell tower rose out of the rock like a bone from silt — half-swallowed, its arches bricked over with centuries of flood-deposited stone, but unmistakably a tower, and unmistakably ringing with water. The source didn't gush from a mountainside as the company's charts claimed. It rose *through* the chapel, welling up inside the old nave and spilling out its broken doors, so that the building stood hip-deep in its own christening, forever.

"They flooded it," Tomas said hoarsely beside her. "When they forged the deed. They drowned the chapel so no one could ever read what was carved here."

He was crying without seeming to know it. Marguerite put a hand on his shoulder and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that the stone wasn't already saying better.

* * *

They waded in at dawn.

The water inside was impossibly clear — snowmelt filtered through a mile of mountain, lit from the broken roof in pale shafts, so that wading through the nave felt like wading through light itself. Pillars rose around them, each one carved, and Marguerite understood within ten steps why the company had needed this place dead. Every pillar bore names. Hundreds of them. Generations of river families cut into the stone in the old script — the script the archivist, Wren, had spent three years learning to read off salvaged seals.

Wren stood in the shallows with her sleeves rolled and her fingers hovering an inch from the carvings, not touching, the way she touched documents. "It's a register," she whispered. "Marguerite. It's not a chapel. It's a *ledger*. They kept their claim here, carved in stone, renewed every generation. This is the original deed."

"Where's the paper?" Callum asked from the doorway, navy coat gone, rifle slung, looking like a man who had finally stopped apologizing with his posture.

"Paper rots," Wren said. "Stone doesn't. But there'll be a copy — there's always a sealed copy. Look for—"

Tomas shouted.

He was at the altar, chest-deep, and below him, visible through the impossible clarity, the floor opened into a crypt. And in the crypt, on a stone shelf above the flood line of some older, crueler century, sat a lead box the size of a hymnal, stamped with the seal of a nation dissolved decades ago.

The same seal that had been on the chest Marguerite raised from the wreck.

* * *

She dove for it herself. Of course she did. Some habits are the whole skeleton of a person.

The crypt water was colder, blacker; her lamp beam swung across carved walls, across the shelf, across the box — and across the fresh drill-marks in the stone beside it. Someone had been here recently. Searching. Failing to find.

She brought the box up into the light and set it on the altar, and they crowded around it dripping while Wren worked the seal with a conservator's patience and Tomas shook and Callum watched the doors. Inside, wrapped in waxed linen still supple after eighty years: the original grant. The true deed. Signatures of the river families, witnessed by the dissolved crown, granting them the passage rights in perpetuity — rights the company's founder had erased with a forgery so clean that three governments had signed off on the lie.

And beneath it, a second document. A confession. The forger's own hand, naming names, naming the company, naming the wreck he'd been paid to sink with the evidence aboard.

Marguerite stared at the last line and felt the river roar through her. *"Sunk at Marker Nine. Let the water keep my sin."*

The water had kept it. She had pulled it out.

* * *

The enforcers came at noon, when the light was worst.

Six of them down the gorge path, and two more on the ridge with rifles, and Voss himself in front — the company's quiet man, the one who burned boats and wrote polite letters afterward. He stopped at the flooded doors and looked at the four of them standing among the name-carved pillars, and he did not draw a weapon, because Voss understood something Marguerite was only beginning to: that some positions cannot be won by force.

"You're standing in a registered concession," he said. "Everything in this building is company property."

"Then the company owns eight generations of the families it robbed," Marguerite said. "I'd let that go to print, if I were you."

Wren was already working. That had been the plan since the rapids, argued through wet nights and cold meals: not to flee with the deed, not to sell it, not to trade it — but to make it *everywhere at once*. The portable scanner Callum had requisitioned with his own clearance codes, the relay smuggler Renna had wired into the chapel's old bell tower, the port-office addresses Wren had memorized from forty years of shipping manifests. Every harbor master downstream. Every customs house. Every newspaper office with a wire terminal. Every one of them would open tomorrow morning to find both deeds side by side — the truth and the forgery — timestamped from the source itself.

Voss's men started forward into the water.

"Scan's running," Wren said calmly. "Batch one of nine. You can shoot us, Voss. But the packets queue automatically. Kill the operator, the machine finishes. Flood the chapel, the machine finishes. It's very simple machinery. It only knows how to send."

What happened next took less than a minute and Marguerite would remember it in fragments all her life: Voss's face going carefully blank; the ridge rifleman lowering his weapon because Callum stepped between it and Tomas with his hands open and his old rank spoken aloud like a shield; the water rising — the spring rains arriving early, the source surging up through the nave floor as if the river itself had chosen this hour to reclaim its ledger — and Marguerite wading to the altar through knee-deep, then thigh-deep water, holding the original deed above her head while the clear water climbed her body, reading the signatures aloud, all of them, every carved name made flesh again in her voice, until the water took the paper gently from her hands the way the river takes everything it is owed.

The scans were already downstream. All of them. There was no getting them back. There never had been, not really — not once the water decided to carry something.

* * *

The concession collapsed the way empires do: quietly, administratively, between one fiscal quarter and the next. Permits revoked. Charters voided. Voss's employers dissolved into lawyers and silence. By the time the flood season peaked, the passage belonged — on paper, in ink, in law — to the families whose names were carved in a drowned chapel that the new river council voted, in its first act, to preserve exactly as it was: flooded, legible, visited by boat in high summer when the water ran clear enough to read the pillars from above.

Marguerite stayed.

She told herself it was temporary. She told herself she'd salvage the chapel properly, document the carvings, train the community's own divers, and be gone by autumn. But Tomas asked her to teach a diving class in the shallows, and Renna needed help refitting the burned boat's sister ship, and Wren wanted a second pair of hands cataloguing the crypt, and Callum — Callum simply showed up each morning with coffee and no explanation, and eventually she stopped asking for one.

Her old life had been measured in miles: bottom time, distance run, wrecks logged, ports behind her. She caught herself, one evening in late summer, measuring differently — in Tomas's laugh carrying across the water, in the number of students who surfaced grinning, in the way Callum handed her the coffee cup handle-first now, without being asked, which was its own kind of language.

The chapel bell tower, Renna had discovered, still held a working bell. On the anniversary night, the whole community came upstream to ring it — once for each generation the forgery had stolen, and once more for the ones that wouldn't be.

Marguerite stood in the shallows with the whole river gathered around her, listening to the bronze roll down the gorge toward the sea, and understood that she had spent her whole life diving for things the water kept.

She had finally found the thing it gave back.
