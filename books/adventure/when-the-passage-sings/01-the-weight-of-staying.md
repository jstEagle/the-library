## 1. The Weight of Staying

The last crate went into the ground at dusk, and Ines Joubert let herself believe, for one full minute, that the season was over.

The dig at Angkor Borei had been profitable in the way that paid for diesel and rice and the slow repair of the *Mekhala*'s engine—unremarkable in every way that mattered to anyone but her. Pottery shards. A burial jar with a cracked lip. The kind of find that filled a report and emptied a bank account back into nothing. Her crew had worked it well: Ratana cataloguing with his meticulous, half-superstitious care; Dara hauling buckets with the cheerful resentment of a man who complained about everything and quit about nothing; old Vireak, who could read soil strata the way monks read scripture, tapping the earth twice before he trusted it.

Ten years she'd been running crews like this. Ten years of digs chosen from maps with her eyes half closed, of contracts signed under names that weren't quite hers, of a reputation built deliberately small—a competent field archaeologist, unaffiliated, unremarkable, *unrelated*. She had gotten good at being no one's daughter.

She was standing at the edge of the trench watching the light go amber over the paddies when the car came.

It came fast for this road, throwing dust in a long banner behind it, and Ines knew before it stopped that the season was not over. Cars like that did not come to Angkor Borei by accident. They came because someone had decided something, and someone else was going to have to live inside that decision.

The woman who stepped out wore linen the color of bone and boots too clean for the mud, and she crossed the site the way people cross rooms they already own. Two men stayed with the car. The woman carried a leather folder under one arm like a verdict.

"Dr. Joubert." Not a question. "Marguerite Voss."

Ines knew the name the way you know the name of weather coming. Voss ran salvage-adjacent surveys out of Singapore, published fast, credited slowly, and had spent fifteen years building a career on the ruins of other people's reputations. Ines had made a point of never meeting her, the way she'd made a point of never saying her father's surname aloud.

"You've finished here," Voss said, glancing at the backfilled trench as if confirming an appointment.

"We finish tomorrow. Then we're done for the season."

"Good." Voss opened the folder. "Because I need your crew on the water within the week, and I don't have time to interview anyone who isn't you."

"I'm not taking contracts I haven't read."

Voss handed her the papers without ceremony, and Ines read them standing there while the mosquitoes found her arms and the crew drifted near, pretending to pack tools, listening with the whole surface of their bodies.

River survey. Delta province. A cave system above the flood line on the Nam Kham branch—karst, unusual for the delta, a limestone tooth the river had been gnawing at for ten thousand years. Permits issued through Phnom Penh and the Vietnamese side both, valid until the monsoon made the waterways impassable. Compensation figures that made Ines's chest tighten with something she refused to call relief.

And then the site designation, printed in a bureaucrat's indifferent typeface:

**NAM KHAM CAVE COMPLEX — SECTOR 3.**

Ines did not move. Somewhere behind her, Dara set down a shovel very carefully, the way you set down something you might need to throw.

"You know the site," Voss said. It wasn't a question either. This woman asked no questions; she simply stated things and waited for the world to agree.

"I know the name."

"Your father surveyed it twenty years ago." Voss's voice didn't sharpen; it softened, which was worse. "Before the collapse."

The word landed in Ines's stomach like a stone dropped down a well. She heard it fall a long way before it stopped.

"The collapse killed Émile Roche," Ines said. "His mentor. His—" She stopped. *His only friend,* she almost said, and hated how easily the words came, after all these years of not saying anything at all. "He walked away from it. He never went back. There's a reason."

"There are several reasons," Voss said. "Most of them are wrong. The geology is sound where he stopped digging—the collapse was localized, a pocket failure in Sector 1, nowhere near the galleries he mapped in Sector 3. I've seen the old survey notes." She paused, and Ines understood, with a cold sliding feeling, that Voss had seen notes she herself had never been allowed to see. "There is occupation material in those galleries, Dr. Joubert. Trade goods. Possibly Funan-period, possibly older. Enough to rewrite the river trade chronology, if anyone ever goes back in."

"And you want my crew to go back in."

"I want *you*," Voss said. "You're the best field archaeologist working the delta who doesn't have a university telling her what to publish. And—" the softness again, surgical now, "—I imagine the compensation matters to you. Your boat leaks. Your crew hasn't been paid properly since the Takéo contract fell through. I do my research."

The crew had stopped pretending. Ratana stood with his catalogue box against his hip. Vireak had come up out of the trench and stood beside him, and Dara stood slightly in front of both of them, arms folded, looking at Ines the way they all looked at her when the decision was hers: patiently, completely, as if their next six months lived in her mouth.

That was the trap of it. Not Voss's money, not even the cave. It was this—six people who had followed her from site to failing site because she fed them first and paid them second and never once lied to them about the odds. They were broke. The *Mekhala* leaked below the waterline in two places she'd patched with resin and prayer. The monsoon was coming early this year; everyone on the river said so.

Say no, and she kept her name buried and her father's ghost quiet and lost the season. Say yes—

Say yes, and she went back to the one place on earth her father had forbidden her to go, wearing her own face, carrying his methods in her hands like inherited knives.

"It's just money," she heard herself say, and her voice came out flat and practical, a stranger's voice, a voice with no history in it at all. "Sector 3 only. Nothing above the flood line gets touched. We work Voss's permits, we take Voss's pay, and when the monsoon comes, we're gone. That's the whole contract."

Voss smiled the way bankers smile. "Then we understand each other."

Ines signed the folder against her own knee, the pen scratching, and told herself the cold in her hands was the evening air.

* * *

They loaded the boat by lantern light, and the crew talked the way crews talk when they've agreed to something they haven't discussed—loudly, about nothing, about rope and rice and whether the engine would turn over. Ines sat on the gunwale checking the bilge pump for the fourth time and heard Sophea before she saw him.

The guide stood at the bow rail, looking upriver into the dark, toward the delta branches, toward Nam Kham. He was a quiet man, sixty or so, hired in Kratie on Vireak's recommendation, who spoke five languages and volunteered none of them. Now he said, in English, without turning around:

"You signed for Sector 3."

"You were listening."

"I was listening." He turned. In the lantern light his face was unreadable, which she had always taken for age, and tonight looked suddenly like practice. "Twenty years ago, I carried baskets out of that cave. I was seventeen."

Ines's hands went still on the pump handle.

"I knew your father," Sophea said. "Everyone on that expedition knew him. He was the only one who—" He stopped himself, glanced back at the crew, at the lanterns, at the dark water sliding past the hull, and what was in his face then was not unreadable at all. It was grief, held so long it had fossilized into manners.

"He ordered us out," Sophea said quietly. "Minutes before. You should know that before the river takes us there."

Below her feet, faint but unmistakable, the *Mekhala* began, very softly, to take water.
