## 2. What the River Keeps

The river had been brown for three days before anyone said it aloud.

Ines noticed it first from the bow of the *Mekhala's Daughter*, watching the water change its mind about itself—muddy gold one morning, then the color of strong tea, then something darker, thicker, carrying whole trees past them like the river was clearing its throat. The monsoon was supposed to be a week out. The permits ran to the end of the month. She'd done the arithmetic in her notebook four times, and every time the numbers came back the same: enough, if nothing went wrong.

Nothing ever went wrong, on paper.

"Water's rising faster than the charts," Sophea said behind her. He stood at the tiller with his weight settled low, the way he always did, as if he and the boat had agreed long ago on whose job was whose. "My cousin runs fuel up from Tan Chau. He says the gauge at the confluence is a meter over where it should be."

"A week early."

"The river doesn't read our permits." He said it without bitterness, which somehow made it worse.

Behind them, the deck had become a small floating village. Ratha was re-coiling rope that didn't need coiling, because Ratha coiled rope when he was nervous the way other men smoked. Dara sat cross-legged over the sediment cores they'd pulled at the cave mouth two days ago, labeling tins with a marker going dry. Malis had rigged a tarp between the mast and the rail against rain that hadn't come yet but would, and under it she'd set up the camp stove, and the smell of frying garlic had made everyone briefly, dishonestly calm.

They were nine days upriver from the site. The Nam Kham cave waited in the limestone spine above the floodplain like a held breath, and Ines had spent those nine days not thinking about it, which was a full-time occupation that left no room for anything else.

Her father had drawn this river once. She'd found the sketch after he died—a pencil rendering of the delta in wet season, the channels braiding and rebraiding, annotated in his cramped hand: *the river keeps what it wants; you only borrow.* She'd thrown the sketch away. Then she'd dug it out of the bin. Then she'd burned it, properly, in a steel drum at a dig outside Battambang, and told herself that was the end of it.

She still knew the drawing by heart.

* * *

The hull started taking water on the fourth night.

It began as a sound Ines half-heard in sleep—a soft, rhythmic knocking below the waterline, patient as a clock—and woke fully to find her bunk corner dark with seepage, the wood swollen and weeping where the caulking had given up. By the time she got a lamp below, there were three fingers of bilge water sloshing over the floorboards, and Ratha was already down there with the hand pump, working it in steady strokes, saying nothing, because Ratha never said anything when it mattered most.

They bailed through the small hours. The rain arrived around two in the morning—not gradually, but all at once, as if someone upstream had tipped the sky. It came down in ropes, hammering the tarp, drowning conversation. Malis cooked nothing. Dara moved the core tins to the highest shelf and stood looking at them as though labels could keep water out of tin.

By dawn the pump was running in shifts and the river had climbed into the reeds on both banks, erasing the shoreline, making the delta into something borderless and moving.

"We should turn back," Dara said. He said it carefully, the way you set down something breakable. "We could make Vinh Long by nightfall. Wait out the worst, come back when—"

"When the permits are ashore and Voss has filed her preliminary report without us?" Ines heard her own voice come out harder than she intended. She softened it. "I know. I'm doing the math too. I just don't like any of it."

"You've been doing the math since Phnom Penh," Malis said quietly. Not accusing. Just true.

Ines looked at her crew—her crew, hers, the only family she'd chosen rather than been issued—and saw them waiting, the way they'd waited in Chapter One of every disaster she'd ever walked them into. Ratha with his hands still wet from pumping. Dara with his careful face. Malis with her arms folded, reading Ines the way she read stratigraphy: layers down to bedrock.

Sophea stood apart at the tiller, and he was watching her with an expression she hadn't catalogued yet. Something older than patience.

"Tell me about my father," she said.

The rain filled the silence for a while.

"I thought you might never ask," Sophea said.

* * *

He steered them into the lee of a drowned copse—dead acacias standing hip-deep in floodwater, their branches holding sheets of rain—and cut the engine so the story wouldn't have to compete with it. They gathered under the tarp without being asked. Even Ratha came, dripping.

"I was fifteen," Sophea said. "My uncle got me on the crew as a basket carrier. Twelve riel a day and all the rice I could eat, which at fifteen was considerable." A few of them almost smiled. He didn't. "Your father's expedition. Nineteen ninety-eight. The Nam Kham cave."

Ines felt the name go through her like cold water through cloth.

"He talked to the laborers," Sophea went on. "You understand how unusual that was. Most of the farang professors treated us like equipment that ate. Your father learned our names. He asked about our villages. And when the survey data started coming back strange—the karst readings, the water table—he didn't hide it from us. He stood us all down at midday and explained, in Khmer, badly, beautifully, that the cave was breathing wrong. That the river had gotten inside the limestone somewhere and was hollowing it from within, and that the instruments said the chamber above the main gallery was saturated."

He paused. Rain drummed the tarp.

"There was pressure on him. You know this? The funding committee, the university, the man whose money it all was—they wanted the gallery opened before the season ended. There was a rival team, French, coming up from the coast. Your father argued with them for two days. We could hear it through the tent canvas at night. And then on the last morning he did something no professor had ever done for people like us. He ordered everyone out of the cave. Everyone. Paid wages for the full day. Stood at the mouth himself and counted us out like a schoolmaster, twice, because one boy had gone back for his jacket."

Sophea's eyes found Ines's and held.

"Forty minutes later, the chamber came down. Half the hillside went with it. The entrance your father had mapped ceased to exist. If we had been in the main gallery—" He shook his head slowly. "There were thirty-one of us. I have counted that number my whole life. Thirty-one."

The bilge pump knocked away below decks, keeping its patient time. Nobody moved.

"They said it was his fault," Ines said. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears. "The report. Shoddy surveying. Negligent—"

"That is what the report said." Sophea's voice stayed level, but something moved underneath it, deep, like the river under its brown surface. "Because he wrote it. They gave him a choice—blame the collapse on unstable ground nobody could have predicted, lose the funding, or sign a statement taking responsibility for faulty surveying and keep some shred of the expedition's reputation intact. He signed it. He took the disgrace so the families of thirty-one living men would not have to watch the project die in scandal, and so the cave would be written off as dangerous and left alone, because he believed—I know he believed—that the river would take more if anyone went back."

Ines stared at him. Twenty years of a single sentence lived in her chest—*my father killed his mentor and ruined his own name with bad science*—and she felt it crack along a fault line she hadn't known was there.

Her father had sworn off Nam Kham. That much she'd known, the family legend, the reason they never spoke of the delta. She'd built her whole drifting career on the assumption that the man had run from his own failure. That he'd been a coward with a compass.

He hadn't run. He'd carried thirty-one people out on his back and then carried the blame so no one would look too closely at why the cave had fallen.

"Why didn't anyone say?" she asked. "Why did it stay buried?"

"Who would publish it?" Sophea said simply. "Basket carriers? The men who survived scattered across three provinces. And your father—" he hesitated, and Ines watched him choose honesty over kindness, and respected him for it, "—your father asked us not to. He came through the villages afterward, before he left the country. He said the truth would reopen the permits. That other people would go back into that hill. He said let the river keep it."

Below, the pump knocked. Above, the rain kept falling on a world gone borderless and brown.

"And now Voss has permits," Malis said softly, watching Ines.

"Now Voss has permits," Ines said.

She thought of Marguerite Voss's immaculate dossiers, her grant language, her careful mention—in passing, deniable, poisonous—of *the Joubert methodology*, of *controversial antecedents*. Voss wasn't chasing pottery shards. Voss was chasing vindication, and she meant to get it by proving the discredited theory right while leaving the discredited man exactly where the record had buried him.

Or worse. Leaving him buried as the cautionary tale that made her discovery shine brighter.

The boat rocked. Somewhere below, Ratha resumed pumping without being told.

"Ines," Dara said. "What do we do?"

She looked at the rain, at the drowned trees, at the brown water carrying everything it wanted toward the sea, and understood—for the first time, completely, with the whole weight of twenty years landing at once—that she had not come up this river for money. She had come because some part of her had always known the story was wrong, the way you know a load-bearing wall is hollow.

"It's not just geology," she said. "It never was. There's a lie down there, and I've been carrying it since I was nine years old."

The river rose. The permits burned down like a fuse. And forty kilometers upstream, the hill called Nam Kham waited with its throat full of water, keeping what it kept.
