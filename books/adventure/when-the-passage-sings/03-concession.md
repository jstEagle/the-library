## 3. Concession

The river had turned against them by dawn.

Ines stood at the bow of the *Mekhala's Daughter* and watched the water climb the hull in slow, patient inches, the way a debt collector climbs stairs. The monsoon had come three weeks early, and the Nam Kham channel—brown, swollen, carrying whole trees like matchsticks—had swallowed the landing shelf where her father's expedition had moored twenty years ago. Somewhere beneath that churning surface lay the mouth of the cave. Somewhere beyond it lay the truth.

"Two hours of permit left," Marguerite Voss said behind her, checking her watch with the crisp finality of a woman closing an account. "The water won't wait for paperwork."

"The water doesn't care about your paperwork either," Ines said.

Voss smiled thinly. She was dry where everyone else was soaked, immaculate in a way that seemed almost hostile against the chaos of the flooding deck. "No. But I care about mine. And you should care about what happens when they expire, Ines. Everything we find after that belongs to the river."

Behind Voss, at the rail, Ines's crew watched her. Not Voss. Her. That was the weight she'd been carrying since Phnom Penh—the way Danai, Preah, little Kiri with his cracked glasses—all of them waited on her word as if her word were still worth more than their wages. They were broke. They'd been broke for months. The Nam Kham contract was supposed to fix that, and instead it had delivered them into a rising flood on a leaking boat with a guide who kept looking at Ines like he was reading a letter she hadn't opened yet.

Sophea stood apart, one hand on the tiller, his eyes on the water. He'd barely slept. When he did, he dreamed out loud, in fragments of Khmer that Ines half understood: *the lamps going out, the sound like the mountain clearing its throat.*

"Take us in," she said.

*

The cave mouth was exactly where her father's field notes said it would be, which frightened her more than anything else. Twenty years of silt and flood seasons, and the limestone arch still rose from the waterline like a door someone had meant to come back to. Sophea brought the boat alongside it with hands that shook only once.

"I was seventeen," he said, as they rigged the lights. His voice was low, meant for her alone over the drumming rain. "Your father paid me in rice and stories. He taught me to read a stratigraphy the way you read weather." He looked at her then, and his eyes were old in a young face. "He ordered us out, khun Ines. All of us. Minutes before the collapse. He carried the last man up the slope himself. Then he stood in front of the officials and said the survey was his fault, so the families would be compensated and no one else would ever dig here and die."

Ines's hands went still on the lamp housing. "You never told anyone."

"I told the river," Sophea said. "It keeps things better than archives do."

She wanted to say something—to deny it, to demand proof, to do anything but stand there feeling the floor of her whole life tilt. Instead she clipped the lamp to her harness and waded into the dark.

*

Inside, the cave sang.

That was the only word for it. Water moved through chambers above and below them, and the acoustics turned every drip and shift into something almost melodic, a low resonant hum that her father had written about in the margins of his notes—*the passage sings, Ines-girl, I swear it sings*—and she had thought it poetry. It was physics. It was also, somehow, both.

Preah found the burial niche first. Her shout came back doubled and tripled off the stone, and then they were all crowding around a recess in the chamber wall where ceramic vessels sat in rows undisturbed, their glazes intact, their forms unlike anything in the regional typologies. Danai lifted one with cotton-gloved hands and made a sound Ines had never heard him make—a small, broken laugh.

"This rewrites the trade routes," Voss breathed. She had descended with them despite everything, and now her composure cracked clean through, and underneath it Ines saw the real Marguerite Voss: hungry, brilliant, twenty years of being told her theories were fantasy. "This vindicates everything. The pre-Angkorian network, the river cults—"

"And the collapse?" Ines asked quietly.

Voss's face closed like a door. "What about it?"

"You know what happened here. You've known since before you hired me. You didn't buy my crew for our shovels." Ines stepped between Voss and the niche. "You bought me because I'm Emile Joubert's daughter, and publishing this site under the shadow of his scandal makes your correction look even more heroic."

The hum of the cave filled the silence. Water dripped somewhere far back in the dark, keeping time.

Voss straightened. "I intend to publish the find. Accurately. Completely." A pause, precise as a scalpel. "The 2004 incident will be footnoted. Surveying error. Negligent leadership. It's context, Ines. It's history."

"It's a lie."

"It's *my* lie to spend," Voss snapped, and for a moment her voice echoed off ten thousand years of stone. "Do you know what your father cost me? My mentor died in that collapse too—or have you forgotten? I spent two decades building a career on the certainty that Emile Joubert was either a fool or a fraud, because the alternative was unbearable. Now you want me to rewrite my own grief?"

"Yes," Ines said. "Because it wasn't yours to grieve like that. He saved eleven men that day. Sophea was one of them. He's standing right there, and he'll testify."

Danai's head came up. Preah looked from Ines to Sophea and back, and Ines saw the moment the crew understood—that the job was no longer a job, that their employer had been carrying a name like a knife in her boot the entire time, and that she was about to use it in public.

"You knew," Danai said. Not angry yet. Worse: asking.

"I found out on the boat. Two days ago."

"You let us walk into a cursed site—" Kiri started.

"There's no curse. There's a dead man who took the blame for saving lives, and a rival who wants to keep him dead for her own reasons, and permits that expire in ninety minutes while the river eats our boat." Ines heard her own voice go flat and hard, her father's cadence, and hated it and used it anyway. "I'm not hiding it anymore. Publish the find without the truth and I'll publish the truth myself, separately, loudly, with Sophea's testimony attached. Your name goes on the discovery, Marguerite. His name gets cleared, or neither of us publishes anything worth reading."

Above them, muffled through forty meters of limestone, came the groan of the *Mekhala's Daughter* shifting on her lines.

Voss stared at her for a long moment. Rain-sound. Cave-song. Then, very quietly: "You'd burn the whole excavation. Your crew's wages. Everything."

"No," Ines said. "I'd finally pay for it."

*

They surfaced into a world coming apart. The sky had gone the color of wet slate, and the river had climbed the landing shelf entirely; the boat sat canted, taking water faster than Kiri could bail. They ferried crates of samples and the first four vessels through chest-deep current, Sophea anchoring the line, Danai hauling, Preah counting bodies aloud like a prayer—*five, six, seven*—until all of them stood on the high shelf beneath the arch, soaked and shaking and rich in a way none of them had planned.

Voss stood apart, clutching a waterproof case to her chest. When she spoke, her voice carried strangely well under the arch, almost gentle.

"Joint publication. Full account. Testimony, field notes, the collapse—everything." She held out her hand. "But it goes out under both our names, Ines. Joubert's daughter, in print, by name. No more drifting. If we clear his name, you stand next to it."

Ines looked at the hand. At her crew, watching. At the water swallowing the last of the landing shelf, and the cave behind them singing its low, patient song.

She took it.

"One condition," Ines said. "We go back in tomorrow. Permits or no permits—I know a provincial officer who owes Sophea his life. There's a second chamber. My father's notes mark it *incomplete.*"

Far upstream, thunder rolled down the valley like something enormous turning over in its sleep, and Sophea, watching the water rise, said quietly:

"That's not thunder."
