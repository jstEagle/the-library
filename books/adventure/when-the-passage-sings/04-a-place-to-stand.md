## 4. A Place to Stand

The river took the cave back at dawn.

Ines stood on the deck of the *Mekhala*, watching the brown water climb the limestone throat of Nam Kham, swallowing in minutes what had taken her father twenty years and her crew three weeks to open. The monsoon had stopped pretending. Rain came down in sheets that turned the world to gray silk, and the boat groaned beneath her like something alive and tired.

They carried out almost nothing. Two crates of pottery, wrapped in Sophea's tarps. The sealed aluminum case of field notes they'd found wedged behind the collapse line—her father's handwriting on water-stained pages, dated the day before everything ended. And the survey maps Voss had brought, now warped into soft waves that mirrored the river itself.

"Nothing else we can do," Tam said beside her, his voice hoarse from hauling crates through chest-deep water for six hours. He was thirty-one and had followed her across four countries without ever asking why she moved so fast. "The lower gallery's gone. Whatever's left can wait a hundred years."

Ines nodded. Her hands wouldn't stop shaking—not from cold, though she was soaked to the bone, but from the thing she'd done yesterday, standing in the rising dark with Marguerite Voss's flashlight in her face, saying the words aloud: *My father ordered them out. He carried the last man up the ladder himself. Then he signed the report that blamed him.*

She had said it in front of her crew. That was the part that still burned.

* * *

Voss came aboard at midday, poling a skiff through water that should have been dry land. She looked terrible—hair plastered flat, mud to her knees—and Ines thought with some surprise that she had never seen the woman look more honest.

"The permits die tonight," Voss said, without preamble. She dropped a waterproof satchel on the deck between them. Inside were copies: the original 2004 incident report, correspondence Ines had never known existed, and a letter from the provincial authority confirming the evacuation order had been logged—filed by Émile Joubert, forty minutes before the collapse, over the objection of the expedition's funders.

"I know," Ines said. "I read my father's notes."

"You understand what I'm offering." Voss's voice was flat, professional, and underneath it something rawer. "We publish the find together. The ceramics alone will make both our careers. The collapse stays a footnote—geological instability, regrettable loss of life. Clean."

"And his name stays dirty."

"And your name stays clean." Voss met her eyes. "You've spent ten years making sure no one connects you to him, Ines. I'm offering you the chance to keep it that way. Most people would take it."

Ines looked past her, to where Sophea sat mending a torn tarp with the patience of a man who had waited twenty years for this conversation and could wait twenty more. Behind him, Tam and Lin and old Phirun pretended not to listen, which meant they were listening completely.

Her crew. Her only family. People who had signed on for money and stayed for reasons none of them named.

If she published the truth, every one of their names would be dragged into the story too—the crew that worked for Joubert's daughter, the dig that turned political, the permits that died in a flood. Funders would flinch. Work would dry up for a season, maybe two.

If she didn't, her father stayed a cautionary tale forever, and Sophea stayed a footnote in a lie, and Ines went on standing nowhere.

"I'll write the full account," she said. "All of it. The find, the collapse, the evacuation order, the cover-up by the funders, and his name cleared. My name attached. Yours too, if you want it—or not, if you'd rather keep your distance from a Joubert."

Voss was quiet for a long moment. Rain drummed on the deck like fingers tapping out a decision.

"My grudge was never with your father," she said finally. "It was with the men who buried him and kept their funding. I was young. I let it go too long." She picked up her satchel. "Write it well, then. If you're going to burn ten years of anonymity, don't do it badly."

At the rail, pausing, she added, without turning: "He pulled my brother's best friend out of that cave. I was twelve when they told us he'd killed him with bad surveying. I built a career on hating the wrong man." A breath. "Thank you is insufficient. It will have to do."

Then she was gone into the rain, and Ines stood alone with the shape of what she'd chosen settling over her like the weather itself.

* * *

The writing took eleven days, anchored in a stilt house outside Kratie while the flood peaked and began its slow retreat. Sophea sat across from her each evening, correcting dates, giving names to the laborers history had numbered. Tam read drafts in silence and handed them back with one word: "True." Lin argued about the geology until two in the morning and was right twice. Phirun cooked. Nobody talked about leaving.

When the paper went out—under both their names, because Voss surprised her in the end—it went out quietly, the way real things do. No press conference. Just the journal, and then the slow ripple outward: a retraction request from one of the old funders' institutions, a letter from a man in Lyon who had been seventeen in 2004 and had never stopped writing letters to editors asking someone to look again.

Ines read that letter on the deck of the repaired *Mekhala*, moored now at a bend in the river where the water ran gold in the late light. Her father's name, restored, sat in the middle of the page like a stone set back in a wall.

She had lost the thing she'd guarded most carefully. Strangers would say *Joubert* and mean her now. There would be questions at conferences, eyes on her in the field, the particular loneliness of being visible after a decade of moving like smoke.

It should have felt like ruin. Instead it felt like ground.

Tam came up the gangplank carrying two beers and the season's next permit applications—sites in Laos, modest ones, nothing spectacular. He set them beside her without ceremony.

"We voted," he said. "While you were sleeping. We're staying. Not because you'll stay put—we all know better—but because you're done running, and honestly, the running was exhausting for all of us."

Ines laughed, and it cracked something open in her chest that had been closed for ten years.

Sophea appeared at the rail, older than the river and just as unhurried. "Your father," he said, "once told me the delta gives back what the current takes, if you're willing to wait where you stand." He looked out at the water. "I think he meant you."

Downriver, far off, thunder rolled over the hills where other caves waited, other rivers rose and fell, other truths lay folded in limestone like letters never opened. Ines picked up the top application, and for the first time in her life, she wasn't choosing a place to hide.

She was choosing a place to stand.
