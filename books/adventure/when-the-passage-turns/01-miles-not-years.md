## 1. Miles, Not Years

The supercell was forty miles west of us and climbing like it had somewhere better to be, which was more than I could say for myself.

I'd been counting miles since before dawn. That's how I keep time — not in years, not in birthdays, not in the slow accumulation of gray hairs I pretend not to see in the rearview mirror. Miles. The odometer on my truck rolled past 214,000 somewhere outside Amarillo, and I marked it the way other men mark anniversaries. Two hundred fourteen thousand miles of chasing weather for people who paid by the hour and billed by the minute.

"Contact," said Marisol from the passenger seat, not looking up from her laptop. "Rotation's tightening. Wall cloud's dropping."

"How tight?"

"Tight enough that if you're going to get us close, now would be the time."

Marisol Delgado had been riding shotgun with me for three seasons. She was twenty-six, brilliant with radar, and had the emotional range of a barometer, which is exactly what you want in a partner when the sky is trying to kill you. She didn't ask about my life. I didn't ask about hers. We chased storms together and split the hazard pay down the middle, and that was the most honest relationship either of us had ever managed.

I swung the truck off the blacktop onto a farm road that hadn't seen a grader since the last administration. Wheat stubble rattled against the undercarriage like applause from a hostile crowd. Ahead, the storm filled half the world — a green-black bruise of a thing, its updraft base rotating with the lazy menace of a shark deciding whether you're worth the trouble.

"You're smiling," Marisol observed.

"I'm concentrating."

"You're smiling. It's disturbing. Stop it."

I didn't stop it. Here's the truth about me, the one thing I'll admit without a gun to my head: everything else in my life was a mess, but inside a storm, inside the ten-minute window where skill actually matters and the sky grades you pass or fail, my life made sense. A tornado doesn't care about your credit score. Lightning doesn't ask for references. Out here, I was exactly as good as my hands and my eyes and my judgment, no more, no less. Everywhere else, I was a man with 214,000 miles on his truck and $61,000 of other people's money burning a hole through his chest.

The hail started at 4:17 p.m. Golf balls first, then baseballs, then things I chose not to name because naming them makes them real. The windshield took hits that sounded like God throwing gravel. Marisol called out wind speeds in a voice so flat she could have been reading a grocery list, and I drove into it, because the client wanted mesonet data from the inflow side and the client paid double for data nobody else could get.

The client. Right. Let's talk about the optimists.

That's what Voss called them, the people I owed. "Your optimists," he'd say, in that voice like dry paper tearing. "They lent you money because they believed in your future. Optimists." As if lending money to a storm chaser were an act of faith rather than arithmetic. As if they hadn't run the numbers themselves and found them delicious.

* * *

Here's the ledger, since we're being honest, and I figure a man who lies to himself about his debts deserves every storm that finds him:

$18,400 to First Plains Credit, for the truck. The truck. The one vehicle in America that has both saved my life and bankrupted it, armored and modified and worth maybe a third of what I owe on it.

$12,000 to my mother, which is the debt that hurts worst, because she lent it out of her retirement and never once mentioned it, and I found out about it secondhand from my cousin, who mentioned it the way cousins do, casually, like handing you a knife handle-first.

$9,750 in equipment leases. Radar units don't come cheap and neither do the men who repossess them.

$14,200 spread across four cards whose interest rates I've stopped reading because reading them felt like watching myself bleed.

And the rest — call it seven thousand — scattered across old friends, old favors, and one very patient veterinarian in Oklahoma who fixed my dog Biscuit after the twister season of two years ago and let me pay in installments until the installments stopped coming. Biscuit died anyway, eighteen months later, of nothing related. I still owe Dr. Patel. Some debts outlive the reason for them.

Sixty-one thousand dollars. I did the math once: at my average take-home, paying it off honestly would take me eleven years, assuming nothing went wrong, assuming the sky stayed generous, assuming I never ate anything more expensive than gas station taquitos. Eleven years. I'm thirty-four. I'd be forty-five when I came up for air, and the air would smell like ramen.

So when the collectors call, I do what I do best.

I drive.

* * *

We got our data. Of course we got our data; Marisol is a machine and I am, whatever else is true, good at this. The wall cloud tightened, dropped a funnel that touched down for ninety seconds over empty pasture, lifted again like it had just been stretching, and dissolved back into the parent circulation. Ninety seconds of tornado. Four hundred dollars of hazard pay, minus fuel, minus the new windshield I'd need, minus the hail dents that would knock another chunk off the truck's resale value, which mattered only to people who thought the truck had a future.

We limped into a town called Faxon — population sign said 212, actual population probably 190 and a grain elevator — as the light went amber behind the retreating storm. The motel had a vacancy sign where only the V worked. I liked it immediately. Honest signage.

Marisol took her half of the pay and drove home to Lawton. She always drove home after a chase, even at midnight, even exhausted. "You live in that truck," she told me once. "I sleep in a house. It's a character difference."

She wasn't wrong. The truck had a cot in the back, a cooler, a hot plate I wasn't supposed to have, and a photo taped to the dash of a river I'd never seen, taken by a father who'd left before I could ask him why he kept it there. Green water, white stone, jungle pressing in from both banks. When I was a kid I used to think he'd gone to heaven and this was the postcard. Later I figured out it was just some river somewhere, and that my father had been the kind of man who photographed beautiful places instead of staying in them.

Genetics are a terrifying thing.

My phone rang at 9:40 p.m., while I was eating taquitos on the motel bed and watching the storm cell die on radar like a slow exhale.

I knew before I answered. Collectors have a sixth sense for payday. You get four hundred dollars of hazard pay direct-deposited at six, and by nine-forty the phone rings, because somewhere a computer has noticed money moving in your account the way sharks notice blood in water, and the computer tells a man, and the man calls.

"Noor Reyes."

"Mr. Reyes. This is Voss."

Voss. Of course. Of all the tentacles of the debt octopus, Voss was the one that had learned my name. He worked for a recovery firm that contracted for First Plains, and he was — I'll give him this — not like the others. The others shouted. Voss never shouted. Voss spoke the way accountants speak at funerals, gently, with total confidence that the numbers will win in the end.

"Evening, Voss."

"I see you had an active day. Tornado warned cell near Faxon. Congratulations."

"You watch the weather?"

"I watch you, Mr. Reyes. Weather is just the medium." A pause, the sound of paper. He still used paper. I'd decided long ago this was theater, a prop for men who wanted to feel like they were keeping books on your soul. "Your payment posted today. Four hundred dollars. Your minimum due is three hundred eighty-two. Which means, after this month's interest accrual, your balance increased by nine dollars."

I looked at the taquito in my hand. Nine dollars. I had just risked my life inside a rotating thunderstorm for a net gain of negative nine dollars.

"That's math," I said. "It's not really news."

"It's news to the optimists. They believed the balance would go down. That's what optimism is, Mr. Reyes — a belief about the future. And I'm afraid I have an update about your future." More paper. "First Plains is reviewing your file next week. There's a conversation happening about whether continued forbearance serves their interests, or whether referral to asset recovery serves them better. Asset recovery, in your case, would mean the vehicle."

The truck. They meant the truck. My house, my office, my church, my escape pod, the only roof in America I trusted.

"You can't take the truck," I said. "It's my livelihood. Taking it is cutting off the income that pays you."

"Yes," Voss agreed pleasantly. "That is the paradox we've been discussing in the office. Some of my colleagues feel you're too important to repossess. Others feel you're too expensive to keep. I've been asked to determine which you are." Another pause, longer this time, and when he spoke again his voice had shifted into something almost gentle, which from Voss was worse than shouting. "Mr. Reyes, can I offer you some professional advice? Free of charge."

"Sure."

"Stop running north. Start running toward something. In eleven years of this work, I've learned that men who run toward something eventually arrive, and men who run away from something eventually tire. The optimists forgive a lot of things. They don't forgive tiredness."

He hung up. No threat, no deadline, no drama. Just the numbers, delivered like a diagnosis.

I sat on the motel bed for a long time, holding a cold taquito, listening to the wind rock the dead V of the vacancy sign outside. Somewhere south, the storm was gone. Somewhere in a filing cabinet in Oklahoma City, my future was being weighed by men with calculators, and the calculator was winning.

Eleven years, I thought. Or the truck. Those were the options. Chase until I'm forty-five and broke and gray, or stop now and find out what I am without the road.

Neither option included the word *arrive*. Voss was right about that much, and I hated him for it. I ran away from a father who left, away from a town that remembered, away from a mother whose kindness I couldn't afford to look at directly. I ran on blacktop and dirt roads and the shoulders of highways at three a.m., and I called it a career, and sometimes, when the lightning walked across the horizon like something alive, I even called it a life.

But tonight, with the radar showing nothing but fading green and the phone dark and silent, the running felt less like freedom and more like a treadmill with scenery.

I slept badly, the way I always sleep in motels, one ear open for weather that wasn't coming.

* * *

Morning brought a job offer, because the universe has comic timing.

Marisol called at seven: a research outfit out of Norman needed a chase team for a three-week deployment, tracking moisture transport off the Gulf. Good money — real money, fifteen hundred a week plus per diem. But the staging point was a border town eight hours south, and the deployment started in four days, and between here and there lay nothing but distance.

"Take it," I told her.

"You sound terrible."

"I sound rested. This is what rested sounds like."

A pause on the line. Marisol's pauses were load-bearing structures. "Noor. What happened?"

"Nothing happened. A man did math at me."

"Voss?"

"Voss."

Another pause. Then, quietly: "Take the Norman job. Take the money. And Noor —" she hesitated, and I braced, because Marisol hesitating was like a barometer falling — "there's a bar in that border town. La Sirena. Guys at the university talk about it. A prospector drinks there, old guy, Aurelio somebody. He's been asking around for a driver. Somebody willing to go upriver. Says he'll pay."

"Upriver where?"

"He wouldn't say. Just — upriver. Río Sereno, he keeps saying. Like everybody should know what that means." She snorted. "Probably a con. Old man, tall tale, wants free rides. But you need money, and I need you solvent, so at least hear him out. What's the worst that happens? You drink a beer and say no."

What's the worst that happens. I've built a whole life on that question and I still haven't learned to answer it correctly.

I packed the truck while the morning fog burned off the wheat fields. On the dash, the photograph of the green river caught the light, and for a second — just a second — the water in it seemed to move.

Eight hours south, I told myself. Hear the old man out. Say no. Drive to Norman. Take the honest money. Run toward something, like the collector said.

I pulled onto the highway heading south, odometer ticking, and told myself I was in control of where the road ended.

I want it noted, for the record, that I almost believed it.
