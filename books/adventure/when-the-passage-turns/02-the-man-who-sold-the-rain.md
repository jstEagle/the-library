## 2. The Man Who Sold the Rain

The bar in Puerto Frontera was called La Espera — The Wait — which struck me as the most honest piece of naming I'd encountered in years. Everything in that town was waiting: the trucks waiting for cargo that might never come, the dogs waiting for scraps, the river waiting at the edge of everything like a debt collector with better manners than most.

I'd been there three days, drinking cheap beer and pretending it was a plan. The supercell season had collapsed early over the eastern plains, and with it my last shot at hazard pay. You learn to read a sky for a living and then you learn to read a ledger, and between the two of them you don't have much room left for reading anything else. Forty-one thousand dollars, give or take the interest that grew overnight like mold. I owed an outfit called Meridian Capital, though nobody there had ever seen a meridian or cared about capital in any sense that involved building something. Voss handled my account. Voss had a voice like a dial tone and the patience of a man being paid by the hour to be patient.

My phone buzzed on the bar at nine that night. VOSS. I let it ring out. It buzzed again. I answered, because ignoring him only ever cost more.

"Reyes." Dial tone voice. "You're a hard man to find when you're broke."

"I'm easy to find when I'm paid."

"Then we have a scheduling problem." A pause, papers shuffling somewhere in a city I'd never see. "Your balance is forty-one two hundred as of this morning. The optimists are getting restless."

He always said *the optimists*, meaning the investors who'd bought my debt, the way you'd say *the weather* — something vast, impersonal, capable of ruining your week. "Tell the optimists I'm working on it."

"You're drinking beer in Puerto Frontera."

"That's research. Drought conditions. Very valuable data."

He didn't laugh. He never laughed. "Ninety days, Reyes. Then we stop being optimistic." And he hung up, because men like Voss understand that silence after a threat is worth more than the threat itself.

I turned my phone face-down and looked at my beer like it owed me money too.

That's when I noticed the old man.

* * *

He sat at the far end of the bar where the light didn't reach properly, and he had the look of weather about him — not a storm exactly, but the aftermath, the washed-out gray of a sky that has spent itself. Thin as a fence post. Hands folded around a glass of something amber like they were keeping it warm. His jacket was oilcloth, the kind prospectors used before Gore-Tex made suffering optional, and it was patched in places with stitching so old the thread had gone the same color as the fabric.

I'd seen a hundred men like him in border towns. Prospectors, mostly, or men who'd been prospectors until the rivers stopped giving and the companies started taking. They had a way of sitting that said they'd made peace with losing.

Except this one kept looking at me.

Not rudely. Not the way drunks look or the way men look when they want trouble. He looked at me the way I look at a cloud formation that doesn't quite match what the models predicted — with recognition, almost, like he'd been expecting me and was checking me against some internal forecast.

I raised my glass slightly. He nodded back, unhurried, and that should have been the end of it. In towns like this you acknowledge strangers and then you leave them alone; it's a courtesy that keeps everyone's secrets where they belong.

But he got up. Walked the length of the bar with the careful economy of a man rationing his own body, and set himself on the stool beside mine like he'd rented it in advance.

"You're the one who chases storms," he said. His Spanish carried the rounded vowels of the upper Sereno country, three hundred kilometers upstream from anywhere I'd ever needed to be.

"Sometimes. When the pay's right."

"And when the pay is wrong?"

"Then I chase whatever pays."

He smiled at that, or did something with his mouth that in a healthier face would have been a smile. "Honest. Most men lie to an old man. They think it's kindness." He signaled the bartender with two fingers. "What are you drinking?"

"Whatever's cheapest."

"He'll have the good rum," the old man told the bartender, "and I'll have water." He caught my look. "Doctor's orders. Though the doctor gave up on me in March, so now it's just orders."

The rum arrived, dark and honest. I drank it because refusing felt ruder than accepting, and accepting felt like opening a door I hadn't checked behind.

"Aurelio Reyes," he said, and offered his hand.

His grip was dry and light, bird-boned, but steady. And the name went through me like a current through wet rope.

Reyes. My name. My father's name, and my father dead eleven years now, and before him a grandfather nobody spoke of except in the past tense and the negative — *he left, he chose the river, we don't talk about him*. My mother had scrubbed the surname out of our house so thoroughly that I'd grown up half-believing Reyes was just a word, a label, a thing that happened to other people. I'd kept it anyway, out of stubbornness or laziness, and spent twenty years introducing myself to strangers without thinking about what I was handing them.

There were thousands of Reyeses in this country. It meant nothing. I told myself it meant nothing with the particular energy of a man telling himself something that isn't true.

"Noor," I said. Just Noor. Let him make of it what he liked.

"Noor." He tried it once, nodding. "Good name. Unusual." His eyes stayed on me a beat too long, and I had the distinct, uncomfortable sensation of being measured — not my debts, not my boat-handling, but something else entirely. Something I couldn't put a number to.

"So," I said, to break it. "You're a prospector."

"Was. Fifty years. Gold, mostly, then water rights, which is gold with paperwork." He wrapped both hands around his water glass. "Now I'm mostly a man who sits in bars and buys rum for strangers."

"What happened in March?"

"The usual thing that happens. My heart filed its resignation and my body refused to accept it." He said it lightly, the way you'd describe a busted radiator. "I've got maybe three months. Maybe less if I keep drinking rum, which is why I'm drinking water, which is its own kind of joke."

Three months. Ninety days, give or take. The same arithmetic Voss had done to me an hour earlier, from the opposite direction. I didn't know yet how much those two numbers would come to mean the same thing.

"Why buy a stranger rum?"

"Because you're not a stranger." He said it simply, without drama, and then took a sip of water like a man buying time. "You're the storm chaser. There was a piece about you in the paper in Villanueva, two years back. The tornado on the flats near Santa Rosa. You drove toward it when everyone else drove away, and you pulled a family out of a drainage culvert afterward."

"That was luck."

"It was also you." He turned on his stool to face me fully, and up close I could see the geography of his dying — the yellowed whites of his eyes, the tendons standing in his neck like guy-wires. "I've watched men run from weather my whole life, boy. And I've watched a few run toward it. The ones who run toward it are either fools or they're looking for something. Which are you?"

"Fools," I said. "Definitely fools. We have a union."

He laughed — a real one this time, rusty, costing him something, and he pressed a hand flat against his chest until it passed. "Ah. God. Don't make me do that again." He breathed. Settled. And then, quieter: "I need a courier."

And there it was. Every bar in every border town has one old man with a proposition, and I'd sworn off propositions the way reformed drinkers swear off the bottle — completely, sincerely, and with a private list of exceptions I preferred not to examine.

"I don't smuggle," I said. "Whatever it is."

"It's not contraband. It's worse. It's a promise." He reached inside the oilcloth jacket, slow, giving me time to object, and I didn't, and out came a roll of something heavy and stiff, tied with waxed cord gone soft with age. He laid it on the bar between us but kept his hand on it. "This goes to the source of the Río Sereno. Before the concession permits expire. Ninety days."

"The Sereno runs through Halcyon Terra's concession. Whole upper basin. Nobody gets up there without company paper."

"Somebody does." His thumb moved on the roll, absently, the way you'd touch a scar. "Somebody always does. The families up there have been doing it for generations. But their time is running short, and so is mine, and the two of those together is why you're drinking my rum."

I looked at the roll. Oilcloth, like his jacket. Old. Weighted with something inside besides paper — I could see the bulge of it, dense, deliberate.

"What's in it?"

"A map. And letters. And the answer to a question you haven't asked yet." He smiled again, that ghost of one. "I'm not going to tell you what the answer is. That's part of the terms. You carry it sealed, you open it at the source or not at all."

"Terms," I repeated. "So there are terms."

"There are always terms. Anyone who tells you different is selling you the rain without the clouds." He counted them off on fingers that trembled slightly, and I understood the trembling wasn't fear. It was the engine running down. "One: you carry it yourself. Not by post, not by proxy. Your hands. Two: you reach the source before the permits lapse — ninety days from tomorrow. Three: you give it to no one but whoever's at the source when you arrive. Whoever's there. That's the whole of it."

"And in exchange?"

"In exchange—" He paused, and for the first time since he'd sat down, the old prospector looked uncertain. Not of the deal. Of me. Like a man reading a sky that could go either way. "In exchange, nothing. No payment. That's the term underneath the terms. You do it for free, or you don't do it at all."

I laughed. I couldn't help it. "Mister, I'm forty-one thousand dollars into a hole with a company that employs people whose job title is basically *ruin*. Free is the one currency I don't have."

"I know what you owe," Aurelio Reyes said quietly. "Meridian Capital. Voss's desk. Forty-one two hundred as of this morning."

The bar noise seemed to drop away, the way sound does in the seconds before lightning. "How—"

"Because I've been sitting in this bar for six days waiting for you, and a man waiting six days learns things. Because Voss drinks at the Hotel Comercio and talks to his collectors like they're children. Because I asked." He leaned closer, and his breath was thin and smelled of hospitals. "I'm not offering you money, Noor. I'm offering you the one thing Voss can't price."

"Which is?"

"A reason." He tapped the oilcloth roll once, softly. "Everything in here belongs at the source of the Sereno. Not with me — I can't get there. Not with the company — they'd burn it. With someone who knows how to read weather and isn't afraid of rivers, and who owes enough people that he understands what a promise costs." His eyes held mine, yellow-rimmed and absolutely clear. "Carry it home for me. That's all. Carry it home."

Home. He said it like the source of the Sereno was a kitchen with a light on in the window, and something in my chest pulled sideways, some old unhealed thing, and I hated him a little for finding it.

"I'm not the sentimental type," I said.

"No," he agreed. "But you're the type who keeps driving toward the thing everyone else drives from. Sentimental is just another word for it, if you live long enough to check."

I looked at the roll of oilcloth. I looked at my phone, dark on the bar, holding Voss's ninety days inside it like a fuse. I thought about the supercell season ending early, about the empty calendar, about the fact that upstream was exactly the direction I ran from everything else.

And underneath all of it, quiet as groundwater, the name. Reyes. The grandfather who chose the river. The father who never explained. The mother who went silent whenever the surname came up, and the shape of that silence, which I'd carried so long I'd mistaken it for furniture.

It was a common name. Thousands of us. Coincidence is the national pastime of border towns.

"Say the terms again," I heard myself say.

He said them again. Slowly, formally, like a man reciting something he'd written out long ago and rehearsed in the dark. And when he finished, he held out his hand across the bar, and I understood that this — the handshake, skin on skin, witness — was the actual contract. Paper could be burned. A grip between two men in a bar called The Wait was harder to dissolve.

"If I do this," I said, "and I'm not saying I will — what do I call it? What am I carrying? A deed? A confession? Somebody's ashes?"

Aurelio Reyes smiled, and this time it reached all the way up into his eyes, and what lived there was so much older and sadder and more certain than anything I'd seen in a mirror in years that I felt it like a change in pressure before a storm.

"You'll know when you open it," he said. "At the source. If the river lets you keep the appointment."

I shook his hand.

* * *

He died at dawn, eleven days later, in the room above the bar, and the bartender found me because my name was the only one in the old man's effects besides his own. By then I'd already bought the map's passage a place in my dry bag and told myself it was a job like any other — freight with unusual handling instructions.

But that last night, before the dying started in earnest, Aurelio had gripped my wrist at the door of his room with the last of his grip strength and said, "You'll meet my grandson on the road. Elian. He thinks I'm a liar. Let him think it a while longer — it's easier on him than the truth, until the truth is ready."

I hadn't agreed to that. It wasn't in the terms. I was still deciding whether to be angry about it when the door of La Espera opened behind me and a young man with a surveyor's case and a jaw set like a property line walked in, looked directly at me across the empty bar, and said:

"You're the one who took the map. We need to talk about my grandfather."

The rain started against the windows, soft and steady, like the river already counting us downstream.
