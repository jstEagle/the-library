## 3. Terms of a Promise

I found the con artist at six in the morning, asleep in the waiting room of the San Fidelio bus depot, with his boots on a plastic chair and the map folded into his jacket like it was worth something.

I'd been watching him since four. That's not a confession of obsession; that's just what cartographers do. We watch things until they give up their shape. I had watched this man — Noor Reyes, according to the hospital visitor log I'd bribed off an orderly, and yes, *Reyes*, which was either coincidence or the opening move of a very long game — come out of Hospicio del Carmen at dusk, walk three blocks in the wrong direction for someone with anywhere to be, and then double back to the depot like a man circling a wound.

He slept the way people sleep when sleep is a debt they're paying down in installments: badly, one ear open, hands twitching at weather only he could feel. Storm chaser, the orderly had said. The American who paid Aurelio's bar tab on Tuesday and sat with him through the coughing fits on Wednesday. The one Aurelio called *mi muchacho de las tormentas*.

My grandfather had known this man for eleven days before he died, and he gave him the map.

Eleven days. I had given Aurelio Reyes twenty-six years of silence, and he gave a stranger the thing my mother spent her whole life pretending she didn't grieve.

So yes. I was watching him. And when he finally stirred, scratched his jaw, and checked the map's hiding place with the furtive little pat of a man confirming his own guilt, I crossed the waiting room and said, "You're going to want coffee for this conversation."

He came awake the way I imagine he does everything — all at once, no transition, eyes already cataloguing exits. Up close he looked worse than the orderly's description. Gray under the eyes. A healing split in his lip. The particular leanness of a man whose body has started eating itself for fuel because there's nothing else on the budget.

"Noor Reyes," I said, though he hadn't asked.

"Elian." He didn't offer a hand. Smart. "You've got me at a disadvantage. Which is usually my trick."

"I know what you are."

"Do you?" He sat up slowly, and there was something almost pleasant in his voice, the way water is pleasant right before it goes over the falls. "Because I've been awake twenty minutes and I already know you're the grandson. The resemblance is a hell of a thing, by the way. Same chin. Same way of standing like the floor owes you money."

That landed harder than it should have. I let it show for exactly half a second, then put it away.

"Aurelio Reyes," I said, "was my grandfather. He walked out on my mother when she was nine years old. She got two letters in her whole life from him, both postmarked from towns that don't appear on any survey I've ever worked. And eleven days before he died of whatever was chewing through his lungs, he handed a total stranger a map to the source of the Río Sereno and made him promise to carry it home." I crouched so we were eye level, which is a dominance move and I knew it. "Home isn't yours. So before you get on that bus with ninety days of somebody else's deadline ticking in your pocket, you're going to tell me exactly what he promised you, and exactly what you promised him, and whether there's a finder's fee buried somewhere in the fine print."

He looked at me for a long moment. Then he laughed — short, unamused, aimed mostly at himself.

"You think I'm running a scam on a dying man," he said. "That's rich. You want to see the scam? Here it is." He pulled the map out of his jacket and unfolded it across his knees, and I hated how carefully he handled it, like it was scripture. "A dying prospector trades a piece of oilcloth to a stranger in exchange for a promise. No money changes hands. The stranger gets nothing but a debt to a dead man and a river full of rapids between here and nowhere. If that's a con, it's the worst-structured one in history. Where's my payout, Elian? Show me the angle."

"The angle comes later." I kept my voice level. "It always comes later. That's what makes it a con. Nobody runs the long game on day one."

"And what's the long game?"

"That's what I'm here to find out."

We stared at each other over the creased oilcloth. Between us, drawn in faded ink and annotated in a cramped, slanted hand I refused to look at too long because it looked like the handwriting on two letters in a shoebox in my mother's closet, ran the Río Sereno. Upstream past the toll bridge, past the gorge marked with three small skulls — Aurelio had a sense of humor, apparently — into blank paper. Actual blank paper. A valley that existed as nothing but a promise.

"He didn't leave it to family," Noor said quietly. It wasn't defensive anymore. Something underneath the words had gone tired. "He could have tracked you down. He had eleven days and a phone at the hospice and a grandson who draws maps for a living. Instead he picked up a stranger in a bar. You want to know what that tells me? It tells me whatever he owed your mother, he knew better than to try paying it with a map."

"Don't psychoanalyze my grandfather. You knew him for eleven days."

"Long enough to watch a man try to trade the only thing he owned for a clean death." He folded the map along its old creases, precise, respectful, infuriating. "He wasn't selling me anything. He was hiring me. There's a difference."

"There's always a difference," I said, "in the version where you're honest."

* * *

The café across from the depot opened at seven, and by seven-fifteen we were sitting across from each other with coffee neither of us drank, because the first rule of interrogating a suspect is you don't let them enjoy anything.

I laid out what I knew, because knowing things is the only weapon I've ever trusted. "Halcyon Terra filed concession permits on the upper Sereno basin fourteen months ago. Ninety-day exploration window, renewable once, expiring on the fifteenth. Their survey teams have been buying up every scrap of prior documentation on the region — diaries, field notes, old claims. Anything that might locate the headwaters before their window closes. Two weeks ago a man named Voss was seen in San Fidelio asking after Aurelio Reyes." I watched his face. Nothing moved in it, which told me he already knew the name. "If that map leads where Aurelio claimed, Halcyon Terra would pay more for it than either of us will earn in ten years. So when I ask what you promised him, understand that I'm not asking out of sentiment. I'm asking because everyone who touches that map eventually gets offered money, and I want to know your price in advance."

"My price." He turned his cup a quarter turn. "My price is forty-one thousand dollars, Elian. Give or take. That's what I owe, itemized, to a collection of lenders my friend Voss used to work for before Halcyon Terra upgraded his skill set. Forty-one thousand dollars is the number I do arithmetic against every single day of my life. Miles per dollar. Dollars per mile. I chase storms for hazard pay because hazard pay is the only kind that comes fast." He smiled without any humor in it at all. "So yes. If someone offered to clear that ledger for a piece of oilcloth, I'd be lying if I said I wouldn't think about it. But nobody's offering. They're offering to buy the map *from* me, which means the promise dies, which means—" He stopped. Recalibrated. "Which means I don't know what, actually. I never asked him what happens if the promise breaks. I was too busy watching him die."

The honesty of it disarmed me more than charm would have. Charm I had defenses against. I'd grown up around men who could sell rain to a flooded town — it was practically the family inheritance. But this man sat in front of me doing arithmetic on his own corruption in real time, out loud, to a stranger, and something about that made my chest hurt in a way I decided not to examine.

"You realize," I said, "that everything you just told me is exactly what a good con artist would say. Vulnerability is a technique. Trust me, I've read the manuals — Halcyon Terra writes them."

"Then come verify." He leaned forward. "Ninety days. The permits expire on the fifteenth, which means everything Aurelio drew on this map has a shelf life, and I don't even fully understand why. He said the source 'belongs to whoever holds the promise,' and I thought that was morphine talking. But you're the cartographer. You tell me — what kind of land claim works like that?"

And there it was. The hook, set clean through my professional soul, and he probably didn't even know he'd cast it. Because I did know. Every surveyor knows the ghost stories: pre-registration commons, ancestral occupancy trusts, the old legalisms that predate the modern cadastre, land held not by deed but by *continuity* — by someone keeping faith with it. Land that belongs to a promise the way other land belongs to a signature. My professors had called them curiosities. Aurelio Reyes had apparently called one of them home.

"It's not supposed to exist anymore," I said slowly. "Those tenure systems. The registries were consolidated decades ago. If there's a parcel still held under an occupancy trust—"

"Then it's worth whatever Halcyon Terra thinks it's worth," Noor finished. "Or worth more to whoever holds the promise. Which is why they sent Voss. Which is why the clock matters." He rubbed his face with both hands. "Okay. Here's my problem, Elian. I don't trust you. You showed up at dawn to interrogate a stranger, you bribe orderlies, and you look at me like I'm a variable you're trying to solve for. But I need someone who reads terrain the way I read sky, and you need—" He gestured vaguely at me, at all of me. "Whatever it is you need. Closure, revenge, a reason to be angry at a dead man in person instead of in theory. So make your case. Why should I take you upstream?"

Because I wanted to say *because he's mine to grieve and you stole that too*, but grief makes terrible negotiating posture, so instead I said:

"Because you'll fail without me. The lower Sereno isn't navigable past the first gorge without someone who knows the survey benchmarks. Half those rapids have killed boats carrying men who thought adrenaline was a skill. And because if you're honest, you'll want a witness — someone with no stake in your ledger, who can stand at the source and confirm you kept the promise instead of sold it. And if you're lying, I'll find out somewhere around the second rapid, when it's too late for you to do anything about it." I stood, dropped bills on the table for coffee I hadn't touched. "Either way, I win. That's not trust, Reyes. That's just good cartography. Redundant systems."

He looked up at me for a long moment, and I watched him run the same math I'd run — risk, reward, the specific cost of being alone with a decision this heavy.

"One condition," he said. "You carry the map."

"What?"

"Alternate days. Your pocket, my pocket. That way neither of us can sell it without the other knowing, and neither of us can lose it without the other killing him." He almost smiled. Almost. "Redundant systems."

I should have said no. Every instinct I'd built over twenty-six years of estrangement said no — don't get on the river with a debtor and a dead man's map, don't mistake proximity for answers, don't go looking for a grandfather in the company of the stranger he chose over you.

Instead I heard myself say, "Fine. But I navigate, you bail."

"We'll see who bails."

* * *

We bought the boat that afternoon — or rather, I watched Noor negotiate for it, and learned more about him in twenty minutes of haggling than in the whole morning's interrogation. He lied constantly, fluently, and always in the direction of getting less than he needed, which is the opposite of every liar I've ever studied. He talked the seller down to a price the boat barely deserved, then paid the original amount anyway, pocketing the difference in silence. When I raised an eyebrow, he shrugged and said, "Guy's got a kid in school," and that was the entire explanation.

Provisions took longer. Rice, fuel, rope, a tarp with more patch than tarp. At the chandlery I caught him standing in front of the satellite phones for a full minute, doing the conversion — the one I was starting to recognize, dollars to miles, miles to days — before walking away without one.

"You'll want that by the gorge," I said.

"I'll want a lot of things by the gorge." He hefted a crate onto his shoulder. "Wanting's free. Everything else has a rate card."

It was dusk when we loaded the boat at the landing below the town, the Río Sereno moving slow and brown-green under the last light, patient as a creditor. Noor stood at the bow checking the fuel line, and I sat on the gunwale with the map in my jacket — my day to carry it — and tried not to feel the weight of it as anything other than paper.

But paper is never just paper. I'm a cartographer. I know that better than anyone. Every line on a map is a decision somebody made about what matters, and every blank space is a decision too. Aurelio Reyes had spent fifty years filling in blanks, and at the end, dying, he'd chosen to spend his last blank on a stranger with storm-chaser's hands and forty-one thousand dollars of reasons to fold.

"Why him?" I asked, before I could stop myself. "Eleven days. Out of everyone. Why give it to him?"

Noor didn't stop working. "Maybe because I didn't want anything from him," he said. "Everybody else who came through that bar wanted the map. I just bought him a beer and listened. Turns out that's rare enough to qualify a man for sainthood, or at least for courier duty." He tested the throttle; the engine coughed, caught, settled into a rough idle. "Get in, Elian. Water's flat now. It won't stay that way."

I stepped aboard. The boat rocked, steadied. Behind us San Fidelio's lights were coming on one by one, and ahead of us the river ran dark toward the blank space on the map, toward the ninetieth day, toward whatever my grandfather had decided mattered more than twenty-six years of silence.

I told myself I was coming along to catch a con artist.

I was already starting to worry I was coming along to catch a ghost.

And downstream, at the landing office, a light burned late — and a man with a collector's neat handwriting was signing the guest register with a name that wasn't his.
