## 4. Ninety Days

**Noor**

The boat was called the *Mariposa*, which was optimistic, because nothing about her suggested flight. She was thirty feet of rust-streaked aluminum with a diesel engine that sounded like a man clearing his throat before a difficult conversation. The seller, a one-eyed river rat named Chelo, kept calling her "a project" in the way people call a divorce "a fresh start."

"She'll hold," Chelo said, slapping the hull hard enough to raise a hollow boom.

"So will a coffin," Elian said.

I liked him a little for that. I didn't tell him so. We were two days past his ambush at the bus depot, two days of him auditing my every move like an accountant who suspected the books were cooked — which, to be fair, they were. My books were always cooked. That was sort of my signature move.

"Ninety days," I said, running my thumb along the gunwale where the paint had gone the color of old teeth. "Permits expire in ninety. Aurelio said so."

"And you believe him." Elian crouched by the transom, checking welds with a penlight he produced from somewhere inside his jacket like a magician with trust issues. "A dying man sells you a map to a river nobody's mapped, and your due diligence is *he seemed sincere*."

"My due diligence is I'm broke and hazard pay doesn't cover boat rentals." I looked upriver, where the Río Sereno went green and slow between walls of cane. "You're the one paying for half this expedition, remember. If you think it's a con, you can walk."

He stood, pocketed the penlight, and gave me a look I'd come to know well over the following weeks: the look of a man doing arithmetic he hated. "I'm not walking," he said. "If it's a con, I want to watch it fail from inside. And if it isn't—" He stopped himself. Whatever came after *if it isn't* was grief-shaped, and he wasn't ready to say it out loud. "Provisions," he said instead. "We need provisions."

So we bought provisions, and that's how we learned the ninety days were real.

* * *

**Elian**

The trading post at Puerto Ceniza had the particular atmosphere of a place where everyone knew something you didn't and had agreed on a price for telling you. The owner, a broad woman named Doña Fermina, weighed rice and beans and lamp oil onto the scale while answering my questions with the enthusiasm of a woman reading her own eulogy.

"The concession permits," I said. "Halcyon Terra. When do they lapse?"

She set down the weight. Looked at me. Looked at Noor, who was pretending to study a rack of fishing lures with the focus of a scholar.

"Sixty-one days," she said. "Not ninety. Your friend's dying prospector was generous with his numbers, or bad at math."

I felt the floor tilt a degree. Sixty-one days. The source of the Sereno was, by Aurelio's map, better than four hundred kilometers upstream through water that got meaner the higher you climbed. In a boat like the *Mariposa*, that was tight even without complications.

"What kind of complications are we talking about?" Noor asked, materializing beside me with the timing of a man who'd been listening the whole time. He did that. It was going to be insufferable right up until the moment it saved our lives.

Fermina lowered her voice, though the store held only us and a parrot eating sunflower seeds like a shareholder. "Halcyon Terra has boats on the water now. Enforcement contracts. Men in gray who check papers at the narrows and ask questions about maps." She glanced at the oilcloth tube strapped across Noor's chest, where it rode like a bandolier. "You want my advice? Don't have one of those."

"We don't," Noor said smoothly.

"You do," she said. "Everyone who comes through asking about the headwaters does. Last month there was another one. Big man, quiet voice, paid cash for fuel and asked about the same stretch of river." She wrote nothing down, but her eyes wrote plenty. "He called himself Voss."

The name landed on Noor like weather. I watched it happen — the small stillness, the jaw tightening, the way his hand drifted to the map tube not protectively but the way a man touches an old scar. I filed it away. I was getting good at filing away the things Noor Reyes didn't say.

"Old friend?" I asked him as we carried crates down to the dock.

"Debt collector," he said. "Formerly mine. Apparently the optimists I owe have diversified."

"Your creditors hired enforcers?"

"No." He set a crate of diesel cans down harder than necessary. "Voss works for whoever pays. Halcyon Terra pays better than a bar full of deadbeats, apparently." He managed a grin that fooled neither of us. "Small world. Bad neighborhood."

* * *

**Noor**

Here's what I knew about Voss that Elian didn't: he never chased. He arrived. You could run for years and Voss would simply be standing at the end of the road when you got there, patient as a tollbooth, holding a ledger and a smile that had been calibrated in a laboratory to be just friendly enough. Three years ago he'd sat across from me in a diner in Las Tortas and explained compound interest with the tenderness of a priest. He'd said, *Mr. Reyes, the people you owe aren't monsters. They're optimists. They believed in you.* It was the cruelest thing anyone had ever said to me, and I'd thought about it most nights since.

Now he was on the river ahead of us, or behind us, or both. With sixty-one days.

That evening we loaded the *Mariposa* under a sky going bruise-colored, and Elian spread the map on the deck and weighted the corners with fuel cans. I watched him work. He had a cartographer's hands — precise, unhurried, the fingers moving over the oilcloth like he was reading braille. He'd already fixed the route in his head, I could tell. He did that thing where his eyes went slightly unfocused and you could practically hear the coordinates clicking into place.

"Fuel gets us to the second gorge," he said. "After that we're poling and paddling, or we resupply at San Rafael de las Nubes, if it exists."

"If?"

"It's on the map in Aurelio's handwriting. It's on no other map I've ever seen, and I've seen most of them." He tapped a bend in the river where the ink had faded to a ghost. "Which means either the town died, or it never existed, or it exists specifically to be invisible. All three options interest me."

"You're enjoying this," I said.

"I am *not*." But the corner of his mouth betrayed him, a small mutiny. He smoothed it flat. "There's a checkpoint at the Narrows del Cobre. Fermina wasn't guessing — I checked the concession registry online before I found you at the bus depot. Halcyon Terra holds extraction rights on everything below kilometer three-twenty. Above that, the land is listed as *sin registrar*. Unregistered."

"Meaning what?"

"Meaning legally it belongs to no one, which in practice means it belongs to whoever shows up with the most guns and the best paperwork." He rolled the map with more force than the oilcloth deserved. "Aurelio sent us racing toward exactly the place a company like Halcyon Terra would want to keep empty. Either he didn't know, or—"

"Or he knew exactly," I finished.

We looked at each other across the map, and for the first time since the bus depot we weren't arguing. We were two men looking at the same storm cell on the horizon, doing the same grim math about whether to drive toward it.

"I've driven toward storms for a living," I said. "For pay. There's a trick to it. You don't decide once. You decide every ten minutes, and every ten minutes you get to change your mind. Most people don't take the exit because they've already told themselves a story about being brave."

"And your story?"

"I don't have one yet." I tied off the last line. "Ask me in sixty days."

* * *

**Elian**

We left Puerto Ceniza at dawn, and by noon I had learned three things about Noor Reyes.

One: he handled a boat like he'd been born on one, which contradicted his storm-chaser résumé until I realized rivers and supercells obeyed the same physics — pressure gradients, shear, the patience required to let chaos organize itself before you moved. He read the water the way I read contour lines, and he read it faster than I did, and admitting that cost me something I wasn't prepared to itemize.

Two: he talked constantly and said almost nothing. His conversation was a system of locks and dams; information entered and was impounded behind some interior structure, released only in controlled amounts, usually as jokes.

Three: he slept badly. The first night at anchor, in the lee of a bluff where the fireflies stitched the dark together, I took the midnight watch and heard him above me on the deck, awake, talking low — to the map, I thought, or to someone the map reminded him of. I couldn't make out the words. I decided not to try. A man's locked doors were his own business until they endangered the vessel.

On the third day we reached the Narrows del Cobre, and the vessel's business became everyone's business.

The checkpoint sat where the canyon pinched the river to fifty meters of brown muscle between copper-stained cliffs. Two gray-hulled patrol boats, a floating platform with a canvas awning, and four men whose posture announced their employment more clearly than any badge. A drone hung overhead on station like a patient wasp. On the platform, stenciled in white: **HALCYON TERRA — CONCESSIÓN 7 — PROTECCIÓN DE ACTIVOS**.

Protection of assets. The assets being, presumably, the river itself.

"Papers," said the one who boarded us, a heavyset man with a clipboard and the eyes of someone who'd stopped seeing rivers as anything but jurisdiction years ago. "Concession access permit, transit registration, cargo manifest."

Noor produced a laminated fishing license and a smile of tremendous warmth. "Sport fishermen. Catching whatever the river feels like giving up."

The man looked at the license. Looked at Noor. Looked at me, sitting at the tiller with a nautical chart open on my knees — a real chart, published, boring, depicting nothing interesting whatsoever. I'd laid it there that morning like setting a table.

"Where headed?"

"Upstream a ways," Noor said. "Then back. Nothing above the third gorge — we heard the water's ugly up there."

Something flickered in the inspector's face. Not suspicion. Recognition — the look of a man hearing a phrase he'd been briefed on. "Nobody goes above the third gorge," he agreed. "Restricted zone. Unregistered terrain. For your safety." He handed back the license and paused, one boot on the rail, and delivered the rest like a man reciting lines he'd been given and half-believed: "Also, advisory. Persons transporting unregistered survey documents in Concession Seven are subject to detention and forfeiture. Company policy. Have a good fishing trip."

He stepped back onto the platform. The drone made a small adjustment overhead, and I understood with a cold settling feeling that it had photographed us, the boat, the chart, our faces, and that somewhere those images were already traveling toward a desk where a man named Voss or someone like him would look at them.

We idled through the Narrows in silence. The cliffs closed around us, red and dripping, and the river ran quick and green beneath the keel, carrying us upstream at eight kilometers an hour toward a place that was, officially, for our safety, forbidden.

"That was close," I said finally.

"That was routine." Noor kept his eyes on the channel, reading the seams in the current. "They weren't looking for us. They were looking for *anyone*. Which means somebody at Halcyon Terra already knows what's up there and wants the approaches swept clean before the permits die." He glanced at me, and there was something new in his face — not fear, exactly. Velocity. The look he must have gotten on the plains when the mesocyclone tightened and the sky went green and every sane instinct screamed left while his foot pressed the accelerator. "Sixty-one days, Elian. And they've got boats, drones, and a head start."

"They've also got a weakness," I said.

"Yeah?"

"They think the valley is empty." I unrolled Aurelio's map between us on the deck, careful of the spray, and put my finger on the blank space above the third gorge where the river dissolved into unnamed tributaries and, eventually, into the annotation Aurelio had written in a hand gone shaky: *el principio* — the beginning. "An empty valley needs sweeping. A populated one needs lawyers. If there are people up there — farms, families, anyone — then Halcyon Terra's whole play collapses the moment anyone official notices them. That's why the permits matter. That's why the clock matters. Ninety days, sixty-one now, and then the land reverts to whoever can prove a claim."

"And we're the proof," Noor said slowly.

"We're the couriers," I corrected. "Don't flatter yourself."

But he was staring at the map with an expression I couldn't parse — hunger and dread fighting for the same square centimeter of face — and I remember thinking, in the way you notice weather changing before the wind arrives, that Noor Reyes knew something about that map that I didn't. Something that scared him worse than the rapids.

I didn't ask. I was saving my questions. We had sixty-one days, four hundred kilometers, and a company between us and the beginning of the river.

Ahead, the Narrows opened into the long green reach, and the first thunder of the upper gorges came down the water to meet us like a greeting from something that had been waiting a long time.
