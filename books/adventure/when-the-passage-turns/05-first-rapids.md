## 5. First Rapids

The Río Sereno had a sense of humor, and it was not a kind one.

For three days it had been all gentle bends and tea-colored water, the kind of river that lulls you into thinking you've got its number. Elian spent those three days bent over his charts like a monk over scripture, calling out depths and bends in a voice that made me want to steer straight into a rock just to hear him stop talking. I spent them at the tiller of our battered river boat — *La Promesa*, which was either sentimental or sarcastic depending on who you asked, and Elian asked constantly — learning her moods. She pulled left when the current got greedy. She hated headwinds. She forgave nothing.

I knew rivers the way I knew storms: not from books but from the particular education of having your life depend on reading what the sky and water were about to do to you. Twelve years chasing supercells across the plains teaches you things no chart can. It teaches you that green light in a storm means hail. That the wind dying suddenly is worse than the wind screaming. That everything beautiful and terrible arrives with about ninety seconds of warning, if you're paying attention, and most people aren't.

Elian paid attention to paper. I paid attention to the world. We hadn't yet figured out that we might need each other's attention.

That morning the birds told me first. The kingfishers that had been working the banks for days went quiet and moved downstream in a hurry, all business, no fishing. Then the water itself changed its mind about something. The current picked up half a knot without any wind to explain it, and the color shifted from tea to milk-coffee — sediment coming down from somewhere above us, a lot of it, fast.

"Rapids," I said.

"I know," Elian said, not looking up from his chart. "According to the survey, the next significant gradient change is eleven kilometers upstream. Class two at most."

"Your survey's old."

"The survey is *authoritative*—"

The sound reached us then, rolling up the valley like distant thunder that had gotten lost and decided to take up residence in the river gorge ahead. Not class two thunder. Class four, maybe five, the kind of sound that makes your back teeth hum.

Elian looked up. For the first time since I'd met him, he looked genuinely uncertain, and I'll be honest — some ugly little part of me enjoyed it.

* * *

We tied off at a gravel bar and walked the bank to scout, because I wasn't raised stupid even if I'd been living like it. The gorge narrowed ahead into a slot maybe forty meters wide, and the river went through it like a fist through wet paper. Standing waves the size of trucks. A hole below the second drop that swallowed driftwood whole and spat out splinters. Two channels split around a midstream fang of granite, and both channels looked personally offended by the concept of boats.

"Class four," Elian said quietly. He'd gone pale in a way that had nothing to do with fear of drowning, I realized later, and everything to do with being wrong. "The survey said—"

"Surveys are photographs of the past," I said. "Rivers are alive. They rewrite themselves every flood." I crouched at the waterline, watching the pattern of the waves, the way the foam streaks bent left before the first drop. "There's a tongue. See it? Smooth green water going in left of center. If we hit that line exactly, the river does most of the work."

"And if we miss it?"

"Then the river does all the work, and none of it is ours."

He stared at the water for a long moment. I watched him do the thing he did — measuring, calculating, trying to turn chaos into numbers. And I watched the moment the numbers refused to cooperate, and something in his jaw set.

"We could portage," he said. "Carry around."

"It's three kilometers of jungle on each side with the boat. We'd lose two days." Two days out of ninety. The permits expiring. Aurelio's map riding against my chest in its oilcloth, patient as a debt. "And there's a trading post downstream we need to reach before the supply barge leaves Thursday."

"So we run it."

"So we run it. Unless you want to wait here and I'll run it alone and pick you up Thursday."

That landed exactly where I aimed it. He flushed and started untying the bow line. "Get in the boat, Reyes."

"That's my name too," I said, and immediately regretted how much it pleased me to say it.

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about running big water: it's mostly waiting. You spend an hour terrified and thirty seconds doing the thing you were terrified of, and the thirty seconds feel longer than the hour.

I put Elian amidships with the bail bucket and the spare paddle, because he weighed less than me and because I wanted him where he couldn't grab the tiller. I took us into the tongue at exactly the angle the foam streaks had promised, and for about four seconds it was almost peaceful — the smooth green lane, the gorge walls sliding past, the roar building underneath us like a train coming through the floor.

Then the river remembered it was a river.

The first drop hit like the world changing its mind. La Promesa stood up on her stern, hung there in a way boats are not designed to hang, and came down into the trough hard enough to compress my spine into my boots. Water came over the bow in a solid sheet. Elian shouted something — I'd find out later it was a bearing correction, the absolute lunatic — and I hauled the tiller over to follow it, because through the spray I could see what he'd seen: the left channel was collapsing, a new sweeper log laid across it where the survey would never find it.

"Right channel!" I yelled back.

"Right channel has the hole!"

"Left channel has the tree! Pick your death!"

There was a half-second where I felt him actually weighing it — this man, this cartographer, this stranger with my grandfather's surname and his grandfather's stubbornness — and then: "*Right.* Ferry angle, thirty degrees, now, or we don't clear the eddy fence—"

I gave it thirty degrees. The boat slewed sideways across the current, which is the single most unnatural thing you can ask a floating object to do, and for one long suspended moment we were surfing sideways toward a hole in the river that ate driftwood.

The hole took the bow.

Not all the way. Just enough. The front third of La Promesa dropped into that churning pit and the river tried to keep her, tried to fold her in half the way it folds logs, and I did the only thing the plains ever taught me that applied — I read the water the way I read a wall cloud, found the seam where the circulation broke down, and threw my weight and the tiller against it. The engine screamed. Elian threw himself forward onto the bow deck, all sixty-some kilos of him, ballast with a paddle, and the boat shuddered, groaned, and tore free like a tooth coming out.

We shot through the rest of the rapid sideways, backwards, and once briefly vertical, and then the gorge opened up and the river set us down in a long calm pool as gently as if nothing had happened, as if it hadn't just auditioned us for a drowning.

* * *

We drifted. Both of us breathing like we'd run the rapid ourselves, on foot, carrying the boat.

"You saw the log," Elian said finally. His voice was shaking. So was mine. "Before I did. From the tiller, in the spray. How?"

"Storms. You learn to see what's coming instead of what's there." I wrung out my shirt over the side. "You called the ferry angle off a chart you'd memorized. In moving water. While upside down, more or less."

"The chart was in my head. Charts are always in my head." He was staring at me with an expression I hadn't seen on him before, and it took me a moment to identify it because I'd never been on the receiving end. Respect. Actual, unwilling, furious respect. "That was the worst helming I've ever survived."

"Best navigation I've ever drowned with."

We looked at each other and — I blame the adrenaline, I blame the sun off the water, I blame a dead man's map and ninety days and genetics — we laughed. Real laughter, ugly and gasping, the kind that comes out of you whether you permit it or not, and somewhere in the middle of it I noticed that Elian Reyes, soaked to the skin, hair plastered flat, furious with relief, was beautiful, and I thought: *oh no,* with the same tone of voice I use for hail on the horizon.

Because attraction, I know from storms. Attraction is the green light before the hail. Attraction is the wind dying. It looks like calm. It is the exact opposite of calm.

I steered us to the bank and put the whole river between me and that thought.

* * *

We camped that night on a sandbar, because the trading post was still a day off and neither of us trusted the boat's seams after the hole incident. Elian bailed and inspected and re-caulked while I built a fire, and we worked in a silence that had changed texture. Before the rapids, silence between us had been a weapon. Now it was just quiet.

He emerged from the boat holding the bail bucket and a look.

"She took water fast on the starboard bow. Seam opened. I've caulked it, but—" He hesitated, and I braced myself, because hesitation from Elian was always the preamble to precision. "Noor. When you threw your weight against the hole. You angled the tiller *against* the current's rotation. Every instinct says to fight downstream of the pull. You fought upstream of it."

"Yeah."

"Why?"

I fed a stick to the fire and watched the sparks go up, and thought about how to explain reading a rotating wall cloud to a man who thinks in contour lines. "A storm isn't a thing, it's a negotiation. Air arguing with air. There's always a seam where the argument breaks down — a place where the wind can't make up its mind. You find the seam, you live. Same with that hole. The water spinning into it was fighting the water feeding it, and right at the edge they cancel out for about a meter. You thread the meter."

He sat down across the fire from me, slowly, like a man approaching a wild animal he'd previously classified as livestock. "That's not in any hydrology text."

"Nope."

"You learned it chasing weather."

"Learned it the hard way. The plains charge tuition."

He was quiet a while. The fire cracked. Somewhere across the river something large and indifferent moved through the undergrowth, and neither of us reached for the machete, because after the day we'd had, a jaguar seemed manageable.

"My grandfather used to write about seams," Elian said abruptly.

I kept my face very still. The map rode against my chest, oilcloth warm from my body heat, full of annotations in handwriting I'd recognized in the bar and said nothing about. "Did he."

"In his letters. He wrote that every dangerous thing has a seam, and that finding it was the only honest skill he ever had." Elian poked the fire, not looking at me. "My mother burned his letters when I was fourteen. Said he was a liar and a drifter. But I'd already memorized most of them. I didn't have charts then, so I memorized letters instead."

Something in my chest did a thing I didn't authorize. "Sounds like he knew what he was talking about."

"He abandoned my mother." Flat. Final. A door closing. "Whatever he knew, it cost everyone around him."

I wanted to say: *maybe it cost him more.* I wanted to say: *he's dying in a border-town bar right now, and he gave me his life's work to carry, and his handwriting makes my hands shake.* I said: "We should sleep. Early start."

He nodded and rolled into his bedroll, and lay facing away from me, and I lay facing the fire, and the river talked to itself all night in the dark, running downstream toward everything, and I lay there counting — not miles, for once, not debts — counting the seconds between the fire's pops, listening for the seam in my own argument with myself, and not finding it.

Because here was the trouble, and I knew it even then: I'd spent twelve years outrunning weather, and weather always finds you. And Elian Reyes slept six feet away with his grandfather's stubborn jaw and his mother's grief and my grandfather's handwriting folded into his childhood memory, and every instinct I had — the ones that kept me alive in hail and flood and collection notices — every one of them was telling me the same thing the birds had told me that morning.

Move. Move now. Something's coming upstream, and you are standing directly in its path.

I didn't move. I watched the fire die and listened to him breathe, and for the first time in years I wasn't counting miles.

I was counting heartbeats, and I didn't like the total.

In the morning, at the trading post, the supply barge captain would tell us that strangers had been asking after us at every landing for two days. Men in a company boat. Men asking about a map.

But that night I didn't know that yet. That night there was only the fire, and the river, and the man asleep across from me, and the slow, terrifying discovery that trust, like weather, doesn't ask permission before it changes everything.
