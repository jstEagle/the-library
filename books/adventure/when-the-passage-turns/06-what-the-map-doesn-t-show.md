## 6. What the Map Doesn't Show

I learned cartography from a man who believed that every map is a confession. Professor Ibarra, third-year surveying, chain-smoking over his light table: *A mapmaker tells you what he thinks you need to know, señor Reyes. The truth is in what he leaves out.* I was twenty and thought it was just something old men said to justify their mistakes. Now I sat on the deck of a rented river boat with my grandfather's — my *abuelo's* — map spread across my knees, and Ibarra's voice came back to me with the patience of all true curses.

The annotations had been bothering me since day two. Not their content — Aurelio Reyes had annotated like a man who distrusted even his own memory, every bend of the Río Sereno triple-marked with depths, current speeds, landmarks described twice in case one description rotted. No. What bothered me was the hand itself.

I'd been telling myself it was coincidence for three days. The slant of the letters. The way the capital R kicked out at the bottom like a leg mid-stride. The peculiar flat-topped 4s, drawn like little flags on poles. The way he crossed his t's low, almost apologetically, as if the letter hadn't earned its full height. I knew that hand. I had grown up with that hand. Every birthday until I was eleven, an envelope arrived at our house in Puerto Cabral with no return address, containing money folded inside a page of looping, careful script — *for the boy's books, tell him rivers are older than roads* — and my mother would read it, go quiet in a particular way, and burn it in the kitchen sink while I watched the ink curl.

She told me the sender was nobody. A stranger who owed my father something. She told me this with her jaw set the way she set it when she was protecting us from something, which in our family meant lying beautifully.

I had burned those letters. But I had read them first. A boy who reads everything — and I read everything, I was the child who read cereal boxes and tide tables and other people's mail — does not forget a handwriting. Handwriting is a fingerprint made of habit. You can forge a signature. You cannot forge thirty years of the way a person makes their small letters without thinking.

So there I was, on the deck of the *Mariposa* — Noor had named the boat, because of course he had, everything about the man was borrowed romance layered over desperate arithmetic — holding a map worth nothing or everything, and the handwriting matched. Not similar. Matched. The same tired apostrophes. The same 4s flying their little flags.

And Noor knew Aurelio's surname. I'd caught it the night we met, the flicker when the old man said *Reyes*, the half-second where Noor's face did something complicated and then lied about it. I'd filed it under *con artist recognizes mark's family name, adjusts pitch accordingly*. That was the generous interpretation. The ungenerous one I didn't let myself finish.

Noor was forward at the tiller, running us upriver through the morning haze, shirt off despite the fact that we were the only two humans within fifty kilometers, because Noor Reyes had the relationship to modesty that other men have to religion — he believed in it privately and practiced it never. He drove the boat the way he did everything, at a speed suggesting the past was gaining on him.

"Elian." He didn't turn around. "You've been staring at that map so hard it's going to file a complaint."

"I have a question."

"That sounds ominous."

"It usually is." I stood, folding the map along its worn creases — the creases themselves were data, the same ones refolded ten thousand times, a map carried against a body for years. I walked to the stern and held it out. "Read me the annotation at the second oxbow."

He glanced down, hands steady on the wheel. "'Oxbow two. Water drops four feet over gravel bar, keep left, the right channel eats propellers.' Why?"

"Now the one at the falls."

"'Falls of the Virgin. Portage north bank, two hundred meters. Do not trust the south path, it floods.'" His eyes came up. "Elian, what is this?"

"Whose handwriting is that?"

"An old man's," Noor said. Too fast. Too smooth. "Aurelio's. It's his map."

"It's Aurelio's *handwriting*, yes." I kept my voice level, which took work, because my pulse had started doing something arrhythmic and unprofessional. "But it's not just any Aurelio's handwriting. I know this hand. I grew up with this hand. This exact hand wrote me letters every year of my childhood until it stopped, and everyone told me the man who wrote them was a fiction, a debt-collector's trick, a ghost. And now his ghost is navigating your boat."

The engine noise filled the silence the way water fills a boot — completely, and then you notice how cold it is.

Noor looked at the map. Then at me. Then back at the river, which was, I'll grant him, a reasonable thing to look at instead of me.

"I don't know what you want me to say."

"The truth would be a novel starting point."

"There's nothing to tell." He said it lightly, but lightness, I was learning, was Noor's native dialect of lying. "Old prospector, old map, old handwriting. Half the men out here write the same. Schoolhouse cursive, all of them—"

"The 4s, Noor." I stepped closer. "Show me another man on this river who draws his 4s like flags on poles. Show me one. I'll wait. We have ninety days; I'm prepared to spend forty of them on this."

His jaw worked. For a moment — one real moment — something moved behind his eyes that wasn't deflection. Something that looked almost like grief wearing a disguise it hadn't chosen well. Then it shut, door and deadbolt.

"You're reading ghosts into ink," he said. "You said it yourself. Your grandfather abandoned your mother. Abandoned *you*. Men like that don't leave maps. They leave holes." He turned back to the channel ahead. "We should make the narrows before the wind turns."

That was the moment suspicion curdled. Because here's the thing about being lied to by someone you've started to — I didn't have a word for it yet, and I resented the wordlessness — someone whose opinion of you had started to matter. It doesn't feel like anger first. It feels like arithmetic. You sit there recalculating everything, every shared meal, every laugh at the trading post, every time he'd gone quiet when Aurelio's name came up, and the numbers stop coming out even. The ledger won't balance. And I am a cartographer; I am professionally incapable of leaving an unmapped discrepancy on the chart.

* * *

We tied up at midafternoon below the narrows, in a cut of red clay bank shaded by a strangled fig tree, and Noor went off to check the fuel line, which was either genuine maintenance or an excuse to not be looked at, and I chose to believe the latter because it meant I could be angry with intention rather than accident.

I sat with the map and did what I do. I measured.

Not the river. The margins.

Maps lie in their margins. That's where the draftsman hides what he can't put in the body — the doubts, the corrections, the things too dangerous or too tender for the official geography. Aurelio's margins were dense. Little notes in that flag-topped hand: distances estimated by days' walk, not kilometers, because he'd mapped this before he owned instruments. Weather observations dated across — I checked — nineteen years. Nineteen years of dry-season levels and flood marks, recorded by a man who came back to the same river annually the way some men visit graves.

And in the lower margin, half-erased by water damage but legible if you tilted it against the sun, a line I hadn't noticed before:

*If the boy ever reads this — the source isn't gold. Tell him the source isn't gold.*

The boy. My skin prickled. I photographed the line with my phone, zoomed, enhanced, the modern cartographer's rosary. The handwriting was the same. The phrasing was the same as the letters — *tell him rivers are older than roads* — the same imperative, the same assumption that whatever mattered would pass through someone else's mouth to reach me, because the man himself never would.

I heard Noor's footsteps on the deck and didn't look up.

"Fuel line's fine," he said. "We're good for the gorge approach if we—"

"*Tell him the source isn't gold.*"

Silence. The specific silence of a man deciding whether to play dumb, and knowing the odds are bad.

"What?"

"Lower margin. Water-stained. Nineteen years of annotations, Noor. Annual visits. A message addressed to 'the boy' — and I am the only boy attached to the name Reyes who was raised on stories of a grandfather who supposedly never sent so much as a postcard." I finally looked up at him. He was standing in the shade of the canopy with the light behind him, and I hated that even now, even mid-interrogation, some traitorous part of me registered him aesthetically. "So one more time, and then I start drawing my own conclusions, and I promise you my conclusions are worse than anything you could say. What is your connection to Aurelio Reyes?"

Noor sat down across from me. Slowly. Like a man sitting down across from a debt.

"He was a client," he said. "I met him in the bar. He was dying. He paid me — was going to pay me — to carry the map. That's it. That's the whole story."

"That's the whole story the way a road atlas is the whole territory."

"Elian—"

"And here's what I can't get past." I turned the map toward him, finger on the margin. "This note is old. The water damage predates the recent annotations — see how the newer ink crosses over the stain? He wrote this years ago. Maybe decades. *If the boy ever reads this.* He carried this map for years waiting for a boy to read it. And then, dying, in a border-town bar, out of every courier on the continent, he handed it to *you*. A stranger with storm-chaser's hands and a collector named Voss circling him like weather. Unless—" I stopped. Made myself say it. "Unless you sought him out. Unless you knew who he was and what he had, and you played the dying man for a payday, and now you're playing me, because I'm the sentimental idiot grandson who'll carry the con all the way upstream."

Something crossed Noor's face then that I'll be honest about, because I promised myself I'd be honest in this account even where it costs me: it hurt him. Whatever mask he wore, and he wore them well, that sentence got under it. I saw it land. And part of me — the part that had already, unforgivably, started to care — wanted to take it back immediately.

But the larger part had spent a childhood watching envelopes burn in a kitchen sink, and that part held the floor.

"You think I conned a dying man," Noor said quietly.

"I think somebody's lying," I said. "And the map says it isn't him."

He laughed. It wasn't a good laugh. It was the laugh of a man finding the one dry spot on a sinking boat. "The map. Right. Trust the paper, distrust the guy keeping you alive on the rapids."

"Keeping us alive. And yes. Paper doesn't flinch. I've built a career on the difference between what people say and what they draw. People lie with their mouths, Noor. They tell the truth with their lines." I stood up. The fig tree dropped a leaf between us, ceremonially, like a judge. "Here's what happens now. Nothing dramatic. We keep going. The permit clock doesn't care about our feelings, and neither does the river. But you don't touch the map again. I navigate. You drive. And when you're ready to tell me the actual story — the one that explains why a dying stranger's handwriting matches the only letters I ever got from a man who never existed — you know where I sleep. Which is six feet away from you, which is going to be *fascinating* for both of us."

I rolled the map, tied it, and stowed it in the dry box with more force than the instrument strictly required.

Behind me, Noor said nothing for a long time. When I finally glanced back, he was looking at the river with the expression of a man doing sums he couldn't make come out, and his mouth opened once — closed — opened again.

"Elian," he said. "There's something you should know about why the name Reyes—"

The radio crackled. Static, then a voice, flat and official, the voice of a man paid not to care: *"Vessel Mariposa, vessel Mariposa, this is Halcyon Terra patrol. You are approaching restricted concession waters. State your business and permit numbers or divert downstream."*

Noor and I looked at each other across the deck, the unfinished sentence hanging between us like a bridge out.

He reached for the radio. "I'll handle this," he said. "But after — you're going to want to sit down for the rest of it."

Which, given everything, was either honesty finally arriving, or the best-set trap yet.
