## 7. The Company Man

There's a particular silence that follows the last collection of a working life. I'd imagined it would feel like victory. It felt instead like a man standing in an empty warehouse, holding a clipboard, waiting for someone to tell him where the next shipment was.

My name is Voss. For eleven years I collected debts for people who preferred not to be seen collecting them. The optimists, we called them — the lenders, the funds, the quiet men behind quiet desks who advanced money against futures that mostly never arrived. I was the arrival. I knocked on doors, sat in kitchens, drank coffee I didn't want, and explained, with all the warmth of a utility bill, that optimism has terms and those terms had come due.

I was good at it. Not because I enjoyed it — enjoyment is a luxury of amateurs — but because I understood something most collectors never learn: nobody believes they're going to die, go broke, or lose their house until the exact moment they do. My job was to be the moment. To arrive punctually, politely, and irreversibly. A man who is his own deadline doesn't need to raise his voice.

Then the optimists consolidated. New management from up north, algorithms instead of ledgers, drones instead of men with briefcases. They let me go on a Tuesday, with severance calculated to the day and a card for a counseling service whose number, I later confirmed, had been disconnected. Eleven years, and my goodbye was an automated email with my name spelled wrong. *Vosk.*

I kept the misspelled email longer than I should have. There was something honest about it. Even the machine that discarded me couldn't be bothered to know who I was.

* * *

Halcyon Terra found me in March, through a headhunter who spoke the way real estate agents speak about floodplains — everything a euphemism. "Asset protection specialist." "Field liaison." "Community interface."

"What it actually is," I said, "is enforcement."

The headhunter smiled like I'd passed a test. "Mr. Voss, Halcyon Terra doesn't enforce. Halcyon Terra acquires. Enforcement implies resistance, and resistance implies publicity. We prefer situations that resolve quietly."

I took the job because the alternative was a pension and a small apartment where I'd learn the names of the neighbors' dogs. Also because there was a contract on the table with a number in it that had one more digit than any number I'd ever earned honestly or otherwise. I told myself it was pragmatism. Pragmatism is what ambition calls itself when it wants to sleep at night.

My briefing materials were thin, which I recognized as deliberate. A river: the Río Sereno. A permit window: ninety days, expiring in eighty-one as of my first morning. A man: Noor Reyes, storm chaser, debtor, currently upstream with a boat, a stranger, and — this was the part that made the number in my contract make sense — a map.

"Whose map?" I asked.

"Aurelio Reyes," said the regional director, a woman named Okonjo who wore her competence like other people wear cologne. "Prospector. Registered claims across four concessions along the upper Sereno. Dying, as of six weeks ago. Before he died, he gave Reyes the younger a document and a deadline."

"And Halcyon Terra cares because?"

Okonjo slid a satellite overlay across the table. River systems, concession boundaries, cadastral grids. Most of it meant nothing to me. But there was a shape near the headwaters — a basin, maybe forty square kilometers — that sat inside no boundary at all. No concession color. No registry shading. Just blank, the cartographic equivalent of a room with no lock.

"Unregistered?" I asked.

"Unsurveyed," she corrected. "Which is worse. Or better, depending on your position." She folded her hands. "If Aurelio Reyes registered anything before he died — a claim, a trust, a filing of any kind — then whatever sits in that basin belongs to whoever holds his paper. If he registered nothing, then legally it belongs to whoever gets there first with lawyers. Either way, Mr. Voss, it does not belong to the families currently farming it, because families without papers are, in the eyes of every institution that matters, weather. They're there, they matter, and no one records them."

I looked at the blank space on the map for a long time. In eleven years of collections I had developed a professional instinct for value — you learn to see a debt not as a number but as everything the number could become. A defaulted truck loan is two years of a man's labor. A missed mortgage is a widow's kitchen. I had never once looked at a piece of land and seen nothing.

This was nothing worth a fortune.

"The permits," I said. "When they expire, the concessions revert. But the basin—"

"The basin was never theirs to revert. That's the elegant part. The company spent thirty years buying up every registered parcel around it, assuming the center would eventually come up for sale. It never did. Aurelio Reyes sat on it like a dragon on one egg." She almost smiled. "Now the dragon is dead, and the egg is walking upstream in a river boat with a man who owes the optimists sixty-two thousand dollars."

I did the arithmetic without meaning to. Sixty-two thousand against a valley. It wasn't even a trade. It was a rounding error wearing a man's face.

"So my assignment," I said slowly, "is to catch a courier."

"Your assignment," Okonjo said, "is to resolve a situation quietly. How you do that is your department. You were recommended because you resolve things. Bring back the document, or bring back certainty that no document exists. Ideally both. The company prefers paper, but it will accept absence."

Absence. I wrote the word down in my notebook, underlined it twice, and understood that I had just been hired to make something not exist. It was, I reflected, the mirror image of my old work. For eleven years I had made debts exist — dragged them out of denial and into the light, made men sign their names to what they owed. Now I would be paid more than double to erase.

There is a version of me that walked out of that office. He's still standing on the sidewalk outside, I think, deciding. The version that stayed signed the contract in blue ink, because blue photocopies better than black and everyone knows it, and a man who notices details like that tells himself he's careful, not compromised.

* * *

I caught up with them eleven days later, or rather I caught up with the shape of them, because catching a river man is like catching smoke with a ledger. Trading posts keep books; fuel sellers keep memories; bartenders keep both. I moved downstream to upstream the way water moves — patiently, finding every low place.

At the third post, a woman selling diesel out of repurposed drums described them without being asked. "Two men. One talks like a textbook and draws in a book all night. The other one —" she paused, wiping her hands, choosing — "the other one looks like he's listening to weather that hasn't arrived yet."

That was Noor Reyes. I knew him already, of course, from the file. Photographs don't do justice to debtors; they flatten men into their balances. In the file photo he had the look of a man photographed at a funeral he couldn't afford to attend. The diesel woman's description was better. Weather that hadn't arrived yet. I put that phrase in my notebook too. I was beginning to collect phrases the way I used to collect signatures.

"He pay you?" I asked.

She laughed. "He tried. His card declined twice and then his friend paid cash and apologized for both of them." She shrugged. "The polite one pays. The weather one promises."

Promises. I turned that over on the walk back to my launch. In my former profession, a promise was a liability with a heartbeat. Men promised constantly — next month, next season, when the harvest comes in — and I had built a career on the principle that a promise is only as heavy as the consequences attached to it. An unsecured promise is a wish. A secured promise is a contract.

But Aurelio Reyes had secured his promise somehow. That was the thing the file wouldn't say plainly, the thing Okonjo's overlay implied and her mouth refused to confirm: the company was afraid of the paper. If it were only a map, we'd have let the river take it. Maps are cheap. But they'd hired me at consultant rates to intercept a dying man's last errand, which meant somewhere in a Halcyon Terra legal department, someone had read the words *registered trust* and gone pale.

You can learn a lot about an organization by what frightens its lawyers.

* * *

I found their camp on the twelfth night, or close enough to it — a fire glow around a bend, two voices carrying over water the way voices do when the air is cooling and the river is low. I didn't approach. Approach is for amateurs and missionaries. I sat on the far bank with a pair of field glasses I'd bought with the first installment of my consultant fee and watched two men eat beans out of the same pot, and I confess I felt something I hadn't felt since the Tuesday they let me go.

Recognition.

Not of them — of the posture. The tall one, Elian, sat with his maps weighted down by stones, correcting them, annotating, a man building order against chaos. I knew that posture. That was me with a ledger, thirty years old, believing that if the columns balanced, the world would. And Noor — Noor sat facing the water, not the fire, the way a sentry sits, the way a debtor sleeps: half-present, always listening for the knock.

I had been the knock for eleven years. Sitting there in the dark, watching a man relax by degrees into a fire he hadn't yet noticed was warm, I understood with uncomfortable clarity exactly what I was to him. Not a person. A term coming due. Somewhere ahead of him on this river, in his mental ledger of miles and money, there was a line item named Voss, and he didn't even know my face yet.

The strange thing — the thing I've turned over every night since — is that I felt no satisfaction in it. Only the flat professional calm of a man doing arithmetic, and beneath the arithmetic, faint as a draft under a door, a question I couldn't invoice:

*What happens to a debt when the debtor becomes the creditor?*

Because that was the hypothesis I was carrying upstream alongside my gear. Sixty-two thousand owed. A valley potentially worth millions held — if the rumors in the legal department's panic were true — by a trust payable to the keeper of a promise. If Noor Reyes kept the promise, he wasn't a courier anymore. He was an heir. And you cannot collect from a man richer than the debt. You can only decide whether you're his collector or his competition.

Or, said a voice I didn't recognize as mine, his colleague.

I closed the field glasses. Downstream, the fire flickered. Two men, one pot, one map, eighty-one days minus twelve.

In the morning I would buy fuel, hire a guide who hated Halcyon Terra on principle, and start making up the bend. I told myself it was pragmatism: complete the assignment, secure the fee, retire on a number with two extra digits. Cold, clean, rational. The kind of reasoning that photocopies well.

But I'll record here, in the privacy of my own notebook, what I actually thought as I watched their fire gutter: that Okonjo had called the farmers *weather*, and that I had spent my whole career treating people the same way — as conditions, as line items, as climate rather than climate's casualties. And that the blank space on the satellite overlay, the basin with no color in it, was starting to look less like an opportunity and more like the only honest thing on the entire map: a place that belonged to no one, which is another way of saying it belonged to whoever loved it enough to stay.

I am not a sentimental man. Sentiment is a rounding error in human form. But I have learned to watch for the moment a calculation develops a pulse, because in my experience that is precisely when a careful man should cut his losses.

Instead, I bought the fuel.

* * *

The guide's name was Tomás, and he took my money with the expression of a man doing charity work. "You're the company," he said, not a question.

"I'm contracted to the company."

"You're the company." He spat over the side of his launch with great precision. "Fine. I'll take you upriver as far as the Toll Bridge. Past that, the water belongs to people with better reasons than yours."

"Which are?"

He looked at me the way you look at a tourist asking why anyone would live somewhere with winters. "Aurelio Reyes mapped this river before your company had a logo," he said. "Every eddy, every sandbar, every spring. When the floods came in '09, his charts got eleven villages moved to high ground before the water did. Nobody paid him. Nobody thanked him. He just kept walking upstream, year after year, writing down a country that didn't exist on paper so that someday it might." Tomás gunned the engine. "Your company owns the paper, señor. Aurelio owned the country. You figure out which one holds."

We ran upstream through the morning, past terraced hillsides and children waving from rope ferries, and I sat in the bow with my notebook open to a page I hadn't written in yet, and for once the columns wouldn't balance. On one side: a fee, a retirement, a clean resolution. On the other: a dead man's charts, a valley of weather-people, and two men upstream sharing a pot of beans, one of whom owed sixty-two thousand dollars to institutions that had already forgotten his name was Reyes and not Vosk.

Eighty-one days minus thirteen now. The Toll Bridge ahead, where enforcers I'd helped train in a previous contract held the last crossing, and beyond it, somewhere in the blank space, an egg that had started walking.

I wrote one line in the notebook and underlined it twice, the way I underline things I'm not ready to believe:

*The optimists think they're buying the valley. They haven't met the promise.*

Then I closed the book, and we went upstream to find out which of us was right.
