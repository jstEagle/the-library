## 8. River of Small Betrayals

The trading post at Puerto Ceniza was the kind of place that existed because a river happened to slow down there, and everything that slowed down got taxed. Fuel, rope, rice, batteries — all of it marked up three times what it cost in the city, and nobody complained, because the alternative was floating on with empty tanks and pretending optimism was a provisioning strategy.

I'd been pretending for two days.

We tied up at the rickety dock just past noon, the *Mariposa* knocking her hull against pilings softened by decades of rot, and I told Elian I'd handle the resupply while he re-inked his charts. He gave me a look — the look he'd perfected by then, the one that said he knew I was lying about something but hadn't decided which lie to call out — and went below with his drafting kit like a surgeon retiring to his theater.

Here's what you need to understand about me: I count things. Miles, gallons, hours of fuel burn, days left on a ninety-day clock. It's how I keep the world from ambushing me. And somewhere around kilometer four hundred of the Río Sereno, my counting had stopped adding up.

I stood at the counter of the trading post with a list in my hand and did the arithmetic three times, hoping the numbers would get embarrassed and change. They didn't. Fuel for the outboard: most of what was left in my pocket. Rice, beans, oil, salt: another chunk. The water filter cartridge we desperately needed, because Elian had been boiling everything and making faces about it: more than I had. The list didn't fit the money. The money fit maybe sixty percent of the list, and the remaining forty percent was sitting there on the counter between us like a debt collector.

Which, in a way, it was.

"Problem, señor?" The shopkeeper was a broad woman named Doña Fermina who ran Puerto Ceniza the way weather runs across a plain — inevitably, and without apology. She'd watched me total my list twice. She wasn't fooled either. Nobody in this chapter of my life was fooled.

"Just deciding what I can live without," I said.

"Fuel," she said. "You can't live without fuel."

"I was thinking more along the lines of the filter cartridge."

She looked at me the way you look at a man who says he can live without clean water, which is to say she looked at me like a problem she'd solve eventually, when she had time.

I paid for what I could and carried it down to the boat in two trips, hating every step, because carrying supplies you could afford is just logistics, but carrying supplies you can barely afford is a confession. Elian was bent over his chart table, ruling lines with a straightedge, and he glanced up as I stowed the sacks.

"No filter cartridge?"

"They were out."

"You're lying again." He didn't even put down the pen. "You've got a tell. You touch your jaw. You've touched your jaw four times since you came aboard."

I put my hand down. "It's an old injury."

"It's a tell." He set the pen down after all, which was worse. Elian giving you his full attention felt like being x-rayed. "Noor. How much money do you have?"

"Enough."

"How much?"

There are conversations where lying is still an option, and conversations where the other person has already closed every exit and is just waiting politely for you to notice. This was the second kind. I sat down on the supply crate across from him, and the crate creaked under me like it had opinions too.

"Forty-one dollars," I said. "And some coins. Maybe fifty with the coins."

He blinked. "We have six weeks of river left. Minimum."

"I know how long the river is, Elian. I've been counting."

"Then you know fifty dollars doesn't buy fuel past the next confluence." He wasn't angry. That was the strange part. I'd braced for anger — I'd earned anger — but what came off him instead was something quieter and much harder to defend against. Concern. Actual, unarmored concern, aimed at me like I was worth aiming it at.

"It'll be fine," I said. "I've got a line on—"

"On what? A storm? Hazard pay doesn't grow on rivers, Noor. What exactly is your plan?"

And here's where I should have told him everything. Here's where a better man opens the ledger and shows the whole ugly accounting: the debts, the collectors, the name Voss, the reason a storm chaser with a bad knee takes a stranger's map and a dead man's promise instead of a paying contract. But shame is a hoarder. It doesn't want company; it wants to keep everything locked up where the rust gets in.

"My plan," I said, "is to figure it out."

Elian looked at me for a long moment. Then he nodded slowly, stood, and went to the small lockbox bolted under his bunk — the one I'd noticed before and catalogued, in my counting way, as *his savings, don't ask* — and I thought, with a lurch, *oh no.*

"Don't," I said.

He opened it anyway. Inside was a roll of bills held with a rubber band, a passport, and a photograph of a woman I'd never asked about. He counted out bills onto the chart table with the same precision he brought to contour lines. Two hundred. Three hundred. He kept going until there were five hundred dollars fanned out between us, and I felt each bill land like a stone dropped into my chest.

"Elian. Put it back."

"It's not a loan."

"It's absolutely a loan."

"It's not a loan," he repeated, "because I'm not lending it to you. I'm spending it on the trip. On *our* trip. There's a difference, and the difference is that I'm allowed to spend my own money on my own expedition, and you happen to be the other person on it." He rolled the rest back up, tucked the box away, and met my eyes. "You'd do the same for me."

"I would not," I said honestly. "I'd make you fill out paperwork."

That almost got a smile out of him. Almost. "Why won't you let anyone help you?"

Because help has a price tag, I wanted to say. Because everyone who ever helped me kept the receipt. Because a man named Voss taught me that generosity is just interest wearing a smile. But what came out was nothing, because my jaw-tell had apparently migrated to my whole face.

* * *

Doña Fermina's post had a cantina attached, because every trading post on every river in the world has a cantina attached, and that evening I sat at its corner table with a beer I couldn't afford and watched the door, because watching doors is a habit you pick up when people start looking for you.

Elian had gone to bed early, or pretended to, and I sat alone with my beer and my ledger-brain running its nightly audit. Forty-one dollars minus the beer. Five hundred dollars of someone else's trust in the lockbox. Ninety-day clock down to fifty-three days. Kilometers remaining: roughly nine hundred, if the map's scale could be believed, and maps lie less than men but they still lie.

The door opened at nine-forty.

Two men came in, and I knew them before I knew why I knew them. Not their faces — I'd never seen these particular faces — but their posture. There's a way collectors carry themselves, a way of entering a room like they already own part of it and have come to collect the rest. I'd spent years being on the other side of that walk. You don't forget the grammar of it.

They ordered rum and took a table near the window, and one of them unfolded a piece of paper, and even upside-down and across a room, I recognized the letterhead. Halcyon Terra. Below it, in a hand I knew even better: Voss.

Voss had found the river.

I shouldn't have been surprised. Voss was the kind of man who treated a debtor's disappearance not as a loss but as a puzzle, and he was very good at puzzles, mostly because he refused to believe any puzzle was unsolvable — only insufficiently motivated. When I'd skipped town with a dying prospector's map and no forwarding address, I hadn't fooled him. I'd just bought time, and time was the one thing Voss billed for.

The two men weren't looking for me specifically, or if they were, they were doing it badly, showing the paper to the shopkeeper's daughter behind the bar and getting shrugs. They were casting a wide net. Which meant Voss didn't know exactly where I was yet. Which meant I had a margin. Margins were my native language. I lived my whole life inside margins — margin of error, margin of safety, margin between the lightning and the thunder.

But margins close. That's the thing about them. That's practically their job description.

I finished the beer, left exact change, and walked out past their table without looking at them, which is the hardest thing I did that day, harder than any rapid. One of them glanced up as I passed. I gave him the blank nod of one stranger acknowledging another, and I felt the moment slide past like a truck on a wet road — close enough to feel the wind of it.

Back at the dock, I sat on the *Mariposa*'s gunwale in the dark and listened to the river work. Somewhere upstream, the Río Sereno was doing whatever it did at night, patient and enormous, indifferent to ledgers. Aurelio's map was rolled in its oilcloth under my bunk, and I put my hand on the deck above it without quite meaning to.

*Carry it home,* the old man had said. As if home were a place on a river instead of a balance I owed.

Fifty-three days. Nine hundred kilometers. Five hundred borrowed dollars and two collectors drinking rum a hundred meters away. Any reasonable auditor would have flagged this account as insolvent months ago.

So why did I stay? Why didn't I slip off the boat that night, walk up the bank, and vanish into ash country until the heat died? I asked myself that honestly, sitting in the dark, and the honest answer scared me more than Voss did: because leaving would mean leaving *him*. Because somewhere between the first rapids and the flood, the expedition had stopped being a delivery and started being a life, and I didn't know how to abandon a life. I only knew how to abandon debts.

Footsteps on the dock. I tensed — old habit — but it was just Elian, barefoot, squinting against the dark.

"You weren't in your bunk," he said. "I checked."

"Couldn't sleep."

"Liar." He sat down beside me, close enough that our shoulders touched, and followed my gaze toward the cantina, where the two men were still visible through the window, silhouettes over their drinks. He was quiet a moment. "Those two asked Doña Fermina about us this afternoon. About a boat heading upstream. Two men, one older, one younger. She told them we were missionaries."

"Did she?"

"She told them what I paid her to tell them." He shrugged. "I grew up in border towns, Noor. I know what information costs and who's selling. Those men are looking for someone. Are they looking for you?"

The river knocked against the pilings. I could have lied. My jaw was right there, ready for touching.

"Yeah," I said. "They're looking for me."

"Who are they?"

"Collectors. Debt collectors. I owe—" I stopped, restarted. Saying the number out loud felt like opening a vein, but he'd already seen the lockbox, so what was one more wound. "I owe thirty-eight thousand dollars. To a fund that finances storm chasing contracts. When a chase pays out, they take their cut. When it doesn't, the cut keeps growing anyway. Interest doesn't care about weather." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "There's a man named Voss. He used to be a collector. Now I think he works for Halcyon Terra. He's the one who sent those two, or he will be. He doesn't give up. It's his best quality and his worst one."

Elian absorbed this in silence. He was good at silence — he treated it like terrain, something to be read rather than filled.

"The money I gave you," he said finally. "It wouldn't even cover the interest."

"Not even close."

"And you were going to pay it back how? By chasing storms until your knees give out? Until the debt grows faster than the pay?" He shook his head. "That's not a plan, Noor. That's a treadmill with a sky on it."

"I know what it is."

"Do you?" He turned to face me, and in the dark his eyes caught the cantina's light. "Because I've been watching you count things since the day we met. Miles, dollars, days. Like if you tally it all correctly, the sum will finally come out fair. But it won't. Some sums don't. And the question isn't whether the math works." His voice dropped. "The question is what you do when it doesn't."

I didn't have an answer. I sat there with the river talking underneath us and the collectors' silhouettes in the window and thirty-eight thousand dollars' worth of gravity pulling at me, and for once in my life I didn't reach for a number.

"Why are you helping me?" I asked. "Really. Five hundred dollars, lies to strangers, a trip you think might be built on a con. What do you get?"

Elian considered it with the seriousness he brought to everything, and then he said, "My grandfather died alone in a bar handing a stranger a map, because the family he actually had wouldn't take his calls. I'm not going to watch the second Reyes in a row do everything alone. Somebody has to break the pattern." He stood, brushed off his shorts, and headed for the cabin. At the hatch he paused. "Also, you snore, and I wanted the financial leverage. Goodnight, Noor."

I stayed on deck another hour. Upstream, thunder muttered over some ridge I couldn't see — dry season storms, all bark — and I counted the seconds between flash and sound out of pure reflex. Eleven seconds. Three and a half kilometers away. Far, but closing. Everything far was closing.

In the morning we'd buy the rest of the list with Elian's money, and I'd hate myself efficiently and quietly, and we'd point the *Mariposa* upriver into the narrowing channel. And somewhere behind us, patient as interest, Voss's net was drifting downstream to meet us.

What I didn't know yet — what I couldn't have counted, though God knows I tried to count everything — was that the net wasn't after the debt at all anymore. Voss had read the map's margins too. And he'd decided the valley at the end of the Río Sereno was worth more than anything I could ever owe.
