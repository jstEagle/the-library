## 9. Night of the Flood

**NOOR**

The sky had been lying to us all day.

That's the thing about weather most people never learn: it doesn't announce itself. It rehearses. A few clouds that look like nothing. A wind that shifts two degrees and holds. Heat pressing down flat like a hand on the back of your neck. I'd spent eleven years reading those small lies for a living, chasing storms across three states of debt, and I knew what the afternoon was doing before it did it.

"Rain by four," I told Elian, squinting upriver at a wall of cloud stacked over the canyon rim like dirty laundry.

"It's one-thirty," he said, not looking up from the chart. He'd taken to navigating with a pencil tucked behind his ear like a professor who'd wandered onto the wrong set. "And the forecast model puts precipitation at—"

"The forecast model," I said, "has never been rained on."

He looked up then, and there it was — that expression he wore when he was deciding whether to be insulted or intrigued. With Elian it was usually both. "Fine, meteorologist. What do we do?"

"We find high ground. Now."

We didn't find enough of it.

* * *

**ELIAN**

I want it on record that I argued with him. Not because I thought he was wrong — because I needed him to be wrong. Because if Noor Reyes could read the sky the way other men read street signs, then everything else I'd decided about him might also need revising, and I wasn't ready for that. I had a whole architecture of suspicion built up over six weeks of river, and I liked my architecture. It kept the rain off.

It did not keep the rain off.

The storm came down the canyon like something with an opinion about us. One minute the air was hot and close; the next the world went green-black and sideways, rain so heavy it stopped being water and became a substance, a place you could drown standing up. The river answered. I heard it before I understood it — a sound like a freight train deciding to leave the tracks — and then the water came around the bend carrying half a hillside, whole trees spinning end over end like toys in a bathtub.

Noor grabbed the bowline and my collar at the same time. "Cave. Left bank. Run."

I ran. I am not proud to say I didn't think; thinking is supposed to be my department. But there are moments when the body understands what the mind is still drafting, and mine understood *run*.

The cave was a seam in the limestone maybe eight feet deep, above the old flood line — barely. We dragged the provisions up the slick rock while the river below climbed ten feet in as many minutes, brown and furious, tearing the mooring lines off our boat and taking the boat itself somewhere downstream to die. I watched forty days of charts, fuel, and Elian Reyes's carefully budgeted savings disappear into the murk, and I laughed. Actually laughed, high and unhinged, until Noor put his hand on the back of my neck like I was a spooked animal.

"Breathe," he said. "Boats can be replaced."

"That boat cost me—"

"I know what it cost." His voice did something quiet. "Breathe anyway."

So I breathed. And the storm breathed back, all night long.

* * *

**NOOR**

Here's what nobody tells you about fear: it's honest work. Chasing storms, I got paid to be afraid on schedule, in daylight, with a truck to drive away in. This was different. This was fear with no invoice attached, no hazard pay, no exit. Just two men, a wet cave, and a river outside conducting its business like we owed it money.

Which, historically, everybody owed somebody money. Me more than most.

Elian shivered beside me in the dark. We'd gotten our shirts off and wrung out, huddled under the one tarp that survived, and even so the cold came up through the stone like the mountain itself was indifferent to us. His teeth were chattering hard enough to hear over the rain.

"You're hypothermic," I said.

"I'm *fine*."

"You're chattering in morse code. You just asked me to send help to the *Santos family*, whoever they are."

A shaky breath that might have been a laugh. "My grandmother's neighbors. 1998. I haven't thought about them in twenty years."

"Body's digging deep." I shifted closer. There's a protocol for this — shared warmth, skin contact, no dignity required or available. I'd done it with strangers after tornadoes. Doing it with Elian was going to be a problem, and I did it anyway, pulling him against my side, his cold shoulder jammed into my armpit, my arm around him because the alternative was watching him shake apart.

He stiffened. Then, slowly, like a man testing ice, he didn't pull away.

"This is medical," he said.

"Strictly medical."

"Good. Because otherwise it would be—" He stopped.

"Otherwise it would be what?" I asked, and hated how careful my voice came out.

The river roared. Somewhere above us, thunder rolled down the canyon walls like furniture being moved on the floor of heaven. Elian turned his head, and in the gray light leaking into the cave I could see his eyes, and they weren't looking at the flood.

They were looking at me like I was a map he'd finally found the legend for.

* * *

**ELIAN**

I had planned, at some point in this journey, to tell him what I suspected. About the handwriting. About the letters. I had rehearsed it in the dark of a dozen nights — *Noor, the annotations on your precious map are in my grandfather's hand, the same hand that wrote me birthday cards until I was nine and then stopped forever, and either you're working with a ghost or you know something I don't.* Every version ended with him confessing and me feeling vindicated.

None of the versions ended like this. None of them accounted for the way he held me like I was something worth keeping warm, or the fact that I'd stopped wanting vindication somewhere around the second set of rapids and hadn't admitted it even to myself.

"Otherwise," I said, "it would be a terrible idea."

"Why's that?"

"Because you're hiding something from me. Because I'm angry at you about it. Because we're stranded, broke, boatless, and possibly concussed." I paused. "Because if I start something now, I'll never know whether I meant it."

Noor was quiet for a long moment. Rain hammered the rock above us. When he spoke, his voice had gravel in it.

"And right now? Do you mean it?"

The honest answer terrified me, so naturally I gave it. "Yes."

"Okay," he said. "Then we stop pretending."

"For the record," I managed, "I hate pretending."

"I know. You're terrible at it. You've been looking at me for three weeks like you're auditing my soul."

"I *have* been auditing your soul. You have discrepancies."

"Yeah." Something crossed his face — fast, gone, like a fish under the surface. "I do."

I should have pressed. That was the moment to press, and some part of me knew it, and I let it go, because his hand had come up to my jaw and the flood was screaming outside and none of the questions seemed to matter as much as the answer already forming between us.

He kissed me first. Or I kissed him. Honestly, the forensics are unclear. It was clumsy — noses colliding, teeth clicking, both of us shaking too hard for grace — and it was the least clumsy thing I've ever done. Like the whole journey had been a long approach and this was the summit, except the summit was a freezing cave and the view was a drowned canyon and neither of us cared.

When we broke apart, he rested his forehead against mine. His heart was going like a drum line.

"Still medical?" I whispered.

"No," he said. "This one I mean."

* * *

**NOOR**

We stayed tangled together while the storm worked itself out overhead, talking in the low way people talk at three in the morning when sleep won't come and honesty has nothing left to lose. He told me about his grandfather — the shape of the absence, the birthday cards that stopped, the mother who said *he chose the river over us* and never explained. I listened and said nothing, and every word I didn't say sat in my chest like ballast.

Because here's what I couldn't tell him yet: I didn't know what I knew. Aurelio Reyes had said *Reyes* in that bar and I'd felt it land in me like a dropped anchor, but I still didn't know why. My father never talked about his family. My mother changed the subject. There was a hole in my history shaped exactly like an old man dying in a border-town bar, and I'd carried the map out of that bar telling myself it was about the money, the promise, the ninety days — anything but the name.

Elian fell asleep against my shoulder somewhere past four, his breathing finally slow, one hand fisted loosely in my shirt like even unconscious he didn't trust the river not to take me. I sat awake and watched the water drop, foot by foot, revealing the wreckage of the banks, and I made myself a deal: when we were safe, when the valley was reached or the promise broken, I'd tell him everything. Whatever *everything* turned out to be.

Dawn came up gray and washed. The river had shrunk back into its channel, chastened, littered with trees and mud and the occasional unidentifiable piece of our boat. Elian woke, blinked at the light, at me, and — to his credit — didn't pretend last night hadn't happened. He just squeezed my hand once, businesslike, like signing a contract.

"So," he said, voice rough. "We've lost the boat, most of the food, and roughly a third of our supplies. We're four days behind schedule with seventy-one left, on foot until we can barter passage." He looked out at the ruined canyon, then back at me, and there was something new in his face — not softness exactly. Resolve wearing softness's clothes. "Any other forecasts I should know about?"

I opened my mouth to say something flippant, and that's when I saw it. The tarp had slipped in the night, and the map — which I'd kept inside my jacket, because I always kept it inside my jacket — lay open on the stone between us, oilcloth backing glistening with damp, one corner peeled loose where the water had gotten in.

Elian saw it too. And I watched his face change as he looked at that loosened corner, watched the cartographer wake up in him, the man who noticed things, the auditor of souls.

"Noor," he said slowly. "What's sewn into the lining of your map?"

Outside, the river ran on toward everything we'd left behind, and I had no answer that would hold.
