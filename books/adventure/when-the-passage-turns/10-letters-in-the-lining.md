## 10. Letters in the Lining

The map began to come apart on the ninth day, which I took personally.

Not the river's fault. Not really the map's fault either, if I'm honest, though I'd been blaming it for everything since the border town — the wrong turns, the extra portages, the general condition of my life. Oilcloth doesn't like water. This is the kind of thing a cartographer knows and a storm chaser apparently doesn't, because Noor had spent the last week using the thing as a lap desk in the rain, eating beans off it, spreading it over the gunwale like a tarp whenever the spray came sideways. By the time we tied up below the narrows, the backing had gone soft as old skin and the seams were weeping thread.

"We need to dry it," I said. "Properly. Flat. Away from the fire."

"It's been fine for sixty years," Noor said, which was the kind of thing he said when he wanted to be wrong with confidence.

"It's been *rolled* for sixty years. There's a difference between surviving and being handled by you."

He gave me that look — the one that had stopped being hostile somewhere around the first rapids and had since curdled into something I refused to name — and handed it over without another word. That was the trouble with him lately. He kept agreeing with me. It made arguing feel like dancing with a partner who kept stepping in the right direction on purpose.

We made camp on a gravel shelf above the water, under a leaning alder, and I laid the map out on the flat rock I'd cleared, weighting the corners with stones. The light was good — that low, rinsed gold the river gets in the hour before dusk — and I sat cross-legged with my repair kit and my grandmother's patience and began to work.

Here's what nobody tells you about restoring an old document: you have to touch it the way you'd touch a wound. Slowly. Without flinching. You learn where it hurts.

The oilcloth backing was stitched at the edges with waxed linen thread, the kind prospectors used because it didn't rot. Someone had sewn it with a saddle stitch, tight and even, the work of hands that had done it a thousand times. I snipped the first knot and eased the thread free, working my way along the western edge, and that's when I felt it.

A thickness. A ridge under the cloth, running parallel to the seam, about the width of my finger.

I told myself it was a fold. A doubled hem. A repair patch, some earlier mending. My hands kept working anyway, because hands are honest even when the rest of you is lying, and I peeled the backing back from the map proper — carefully, carefully, the way you'd lift a bandage you were afraid of what was under — and there they were.

Letters.

Four of them, folded into long strips, sewn flat against the inside of the oilcloth like a man had built a secret into the very skin of the thing. The paper was thin, airmail stock, the kind that used to come in blue-and-red-striped envelopes. The ink had faded to the color of weak tea. The handwriting was a tight, slanted cursive, all forward motion, the letters of a man who wrote the way he walked — like he was late for somewhere and intended to arrive anyway.

I knew that handwriting.

I want to be precise about what happened in my body when I recognized it, because I've spent a lot of years pretending I don't have a body, just a pair of eyes and a set of opinions. But there it was: a drop, like an elevator between floors. My stomach arriving somewhere a half-second after the rest of me.

I knew that handwriting because I'd seen it on eleven envelopes, aged six to fourteen, addressed to *Elian Reyes* in a fist that slanted forward like it was fleeing. Letters from a grandfather I was told had died. Then, when I was old enough to ask better questions, told had *left*. Then, when I was old enough to stop asking, not mentioned at all. My mother kept them in a biscuit tin under the winter coats, and once a year, some anniversary she never named, she'd take the tin out and sit with it and put it back, and I'd lie on the floor of my room and hate a man whose face I didn't know but whose handwriting I could have picked out of a lineup.

Aurelio Reyes had sewn his letters into a map. Of course he had. A man who couldn't say a true thing to a living person would say all of them to a dead river, in ink, and hide the ink where only someone patient enough to mend what he'd broken would ever find it.

I should have read them. That's what I keep telling myself now, weeks later, in the version of events where I'm braver than I was. I should have sat right there on the gravel and read every word and known everything, and spared us all the slow-motion disaster of the next three days.

I didn't read them. Not then.

I read the first line of the first one. Just the first line, the way you test bathwater, the way you take one drink and call it the last.

*To whoever is mending this: I am sorry. I was always sorry. I just never found a direction to send it.*

And then I folded the letters back along their old creases — I remember being careful, absurdly careful, aligning the paper to its own history — and I sewed the oilcloth shut again, stitch by stitch, the saddle stitch, matching his tension, my hands steady in a way the rest of me was not.

Then I looked up at Noor.

* * *

He was down at the waterline, washing the dinner pot, and I watched him the way you watch weather you've decided not to run from yet. He had his sleeves shoved up — he always had his sleeves shoved up, like a man permanently ready to fight or fix something — and he was scrubbing at a scorch mark with his thumb, and the evening light was doing that thing on the water that even I, a man who distrusts beauty on principle, had to admit was doing something.

Here is what I knew, sitting on my rock with a mended map and a mouth full of swallowed words:

I knew Aurelio had a son he never claimed. I knew it the way you know a thing you've just read — completely and not at all, the knowledge sitting on the surface of me without soaking in. The letters would say more. The letters would say *who*. But I already knew who, and I knew it the way you know the last line of a song before the singer gets there, and the knowing had a name, and the name was currently scrubbing a pot.

Noor Reyes. Aurelio Reyes.

I'd told myself the shared surname was a border-town coincidence. Reyes is common the way Smith is common, the way river is common. I'd told myself the old man's fixation on Noor — the way his eyes had tracked Noor around that bar like a compass needle that finally found its north — was the fever, the dying, the ordinary desperation of a man who wants to be believed before the lights go out.

But the fever hadn't written eleven letters in a slanting hand. The fever hadn't sewn a confession into the lining of the only thing he owned that mattered. Dying men are confused. Dying men are not *architects*.

He'd chosen Noor. Out of every face in that bar — out of every drifter and dealer and down-on-his-luck fool — he'd put the map in Noor's hands, and Noor had taken it, and I had watched him take it, and I had thought: *con artist*. I'd thought: *here's another man taking from my grandfather, the way everyone always took, the way the man himself took, walking out on a daughter-in-law and a grandson and a biscuit tin of unsent words.*

And the whole time, Noor hadn't been taking anything. He'd been *receiving*. He just didn't know what.

I sat with that. I want it on the record that I sat with it, alone, for the better part of an hour, while the light went from gold to copper to that bruised purple the canyon does at dusk, and Noor came up from the water and built the fire and cooked the rice and said something wry about my face that I answered on autopilot, and through all of it the letters sat sewn against the map's spine not four feet from his elbow, holding the one sentence that would reorganize his entire life.

*You have a grandfather. He chose the river over you. Here is why. Here is how sorry he was. Here is what he left you, which was never the map at all.*

I didn't say it.

I've been asking myself why ever since, and I keep arriving at answers that sound noble and knowing they're not. The noble version: I wanted to be sure. I wanted to read the letters whole, in daylight, with a steady hand, before I handed a man a grenade with the pin already pulled. And there's truth in that — you don't tell a man his father was unclaimed by his own father based on one line of bathwater-tested ink.

But the true version, the one I only admit at altitude, is smaller and uglier. I didn't tell him because telling him would change everything, and I wasn't ready for everything to change. We had nine days of river behind us and something — I flinch even writing it — something *good* growing between us in the space where distrust used to live. He'd started sleeping facing me across the fire. He'd started laughing at my jokes before I finished them, which is either love or an insult, and with Noor it was somehow both. And I knew — cartographer's certainty, the kind I stake whole expeditions on — that the moment I said *Aurelio was your blood*, the map between us would stop being a map. It would become a minefield. Every shared surname, every coincidence, every time the old man had looked at Noor instead of me — all of it would detonate retroactively, and Noor would look at me, the grandson, the *real* one, the one who'd been told the truth as a child and still got left, and he'd ask the question I couldn't answer:

*Why did he pick me? And why didn't you tell me sooner?*

So I said nothing, and the nothing got heavier every hour, the way snow gets heavier on a roof, silent and incremental, until the whole structure is groaning and everyone inside is still calling it a quiet night.

* * *

The next morning the river gave us a long, easy stretch — braided channels, gravel bars, the water running shallow and bright over copper-colored stones — and we made good time, which was its own kind of problem, because good time meant talking, and talking meant proximity, and proximity meant the letters were riding in the dry bag between his knees like a stowaway with a knife.

"You're quiet," he said, around noon, poling us through a slow bend.

"I'm navigating."

"You're navigating with your mouth shut and your jaw doing that thing."

"What thing?"

"The thing where you're composing a sentence and arresting it mid-build." He glanced over. "You've done it three times since the narrows. I count things. Occupational hazard."

There it was — the whole man in one observation, the way he counted miles and debts and my tics like they were all the same currency, and I felt the nothing on my back shift its weight again. I opened my mouth to say something — *Noor, about the map, about the stitching, about the letters* — and what came out was:

"Your stitching's crooked."

"I didn't stitch anything."

"Exactly my point."

He laughed, and the laugh did what it always did, which was make a liar of me, and I poled us onward through the bright water with a dead man's confession at my feet and his grandson's smile three feet away, and I understood, with the clarity that only comes to men who have made the wrong choice and know it, that I was now keeping two secrets: his, and mine.

* * *

That evening we camped in the lee of a bluff, and the wind came up the canyon smelling of rain that hadn't fallen yet, and Noor spread the map on the rock to check our line for tomorrow, and I watched his finger trace the route — his finger, on the paper his grandfather's hand had covered in annotations, the two of them touching across sixty years and neither of them knowing it — and the weight on my back became unbearable in the specific way that weights do, which is all at once.

"Noor."

"Yeah?"

I had the whole sentence built. *Aurelio had a son. I found letters in the lining. I think—*

And then the wind gusted, and the map corner lifted, and Noor slapped his hand down on it to pin it, laughing, saying, "This thing's trying to leave too, huh?" — and the sentence collapsed in me like a cut guy-line, and what I actually said was:

"Tomorrow we hit the toll bridge."

He looked up. "The what?"

"Voss's people won't be the only ones on this river. Concession companies don't build enforcer camps at the source. They build them at the crossings. It's cheaper. One bridge, one choke point, every traveler has to pass through it." I traced the line on the map, my finger crossing his grandfather's ink, feeling like a forger. "There's one crossing left before the high valley. Tomorrow, sometime past midday, we're going to have to talk our way past men whose entire job is making sure people like us don't."

Noor was quiet for a moment, studying the map, and then he said, "You'd tell me. If something was wrong. Wouldn't you?"

The river said nothing. The fire said nothing. Eleven letters in faded ink said nothing, sewn into the skin of the thing we were both leaning over like a family around a dinner table.

"Yeah," I said. "Of course."

He nodded and rolled the map — *my* stitching, *his* grandfather's secret — and went to check the boat lines, and I sat by the fire and watched him move and understood that tomorrow I would face armed men at a toll bridge with a lie in my chest, and that the lie was going to come due soon, the way all debts do.

The river, which had been running easy for two days, was already starting to darken ahead. Somewhere upstream, past the crossing, past the lies, the source was waiting. So was the truth. I just hadn't figured out which one I was more afraid of reaching first.
