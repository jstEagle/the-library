## 14. Sabotage

### Noor

I woke to the sound of water where water shouldn't be.

Not rain — the sky had been holding its breath all night, that flat gray hush that means something is deciding whether to kill you later or much later. Not the river either; we were anchored forty meters off the bank in a backwater eddy, and the river has a voice you learn like a lover's breathing. This was different. This was a small, patient, intimate sound, the kind of sound a boat makes when it's quietly dying.

I lay in my bunk for three full seconds doing arithmetic I already knew the answer to.

Then I was up, barefoot on wet planks, and the bilge told me everything before my eyes did. Six inches of black water sloshing around our provisions. The lantern swinging on its hook threw light across it in long gold stripes, and every stripe moved wrong.

"Elian." My voice came out flat, which surprised me. Somewhere in the last month I'd apparently developed a voice for disasters that wasn't panic. "Elian, wake up."

He was already moving. Cartographers, I've learned, sleep like men who expect the map to change overnight — one eye always open, one hand always on the terrain. He came out of his bunk with the knife he kept under the pillow, which I'd teased him about until this exact moment made me stop teasing anyone about anything forever.

"Leak?"

"Worse. Look at the hull line."

We went down into the flooded stern together, shoulder to shoulder in the dark, and found it by touch before we found it by sight: three cuts in the planking below the waterline, each one clean as a signature. Not rot. Not stress. A blade, drawn twice along the grain, then punched through. Somebody had knelt on our gunwale in the night, taken their time, and gone home dry.

"Sabotage," Elian said. He said it the way he said *contour interval* — a fact, located precisely on the map of the world, requiring no opinion.

"Yeah." I ran my thumb along the cut. Sharp steel, steady hand. "Voss's men caught up."

"You're sure it's them."

"Noor," I said, using my own name like evidence, "who else do we know who hates us this professionally?"

* * *

### Voss

I want it on the record that I didn't order the slashing.

That matters to me more than it should, probably. There's a version of me — the one the optimists at Halcyon Terra thought they were hiring when they pulled me out of collections — who would have ordered it without losing sleep. Slash the hull, strand the courier, let the river do the paperwork. Clean. Deniable. The kind of thing you put in an expense report as *field contingency, minor*.

Instead I told Marquez and Ochoa: find them, watch them, report. Do not engage.

And then I woke up on the ridge above the backwater at dawn and saw the two of them bailing their boat like men fighting for a drowning animal, and Marquez was grinning at me from the treeline with river mud still on his knees, and I understood that my instructions had been received the way instructions are received by men who are paid by results.

"You said don't engage," Marquez offered, when I got down to him.

"I did."

"We didn't hurt anybody. We just slowed them down." He spread his hands, reasonable as a banker. "Four days, maybe five, with what they've got for materials. Permits lapse in nine. You can do that math better than us, Mr. Voss."

The math was simple and I hated it. Nine days minus four left five. Five days to cover distance that had taken us — traveling light, traveling mean, knowing the water — eleven. They would have to run the gorge or quit. Those were the options now, and one of them was written in my handwriting whether I liked it or not.

"They'll run the gorge," Ochoa said. He was younger, and he said it with something that wasn't quite admiration. More like the way you talk about weather coming.

"Nobody runs the gorge," Marquez said.

"Somebody will," Ochoa said, looking up the ridge at the smoke from the boat's little stove. "That one will."

I didn't say anything. I was watching the smoke rise off the water, thin and stubborn, and thinking about ledgers again, which is a bad habit of mine. Every debt has a due date. Every due date makes somebody desperate. And somewhere upstream there was a valley full of people who had never signed anything, whose only crime was living on land worth money, and I was the man sent to collect.

I told myself it was pragmatism. I'm getting worse at telling myself things.

* * *

### Noor

Four days.

Let me describe four days to you, since I lived all of them and would prefer not to live them again alone.

Day one was grief. We dragged the boat onto the shingle bar and looked at the cuts the way you look at a wound — with that stupid helpless tenderness, wanting to apologize to it. The *Marta*, Elian had named her, after a river goddess neither of us believed in but both of us needed. She was thirty years old, cedar over oak, held together partly by caulking and partly by faith, and she had carried us through rapids that should have folded her like paper. Now she had three wounds below her waterline and no doctor within two hundred miles.

"We can't fix this properly," Elian said. "Properly means hauling her out, drying the wood, cutting scarph joints, letting epoxy cure. Properly means a week we don't have."

"So improperly."

"So improperly." He was already pulling the tool roll, and I want to say something about how he looked right then — sleeves shoved up, hair plastered down, kneeling in the mud with a chisel in his teeth like a pirate cartographer — but this is supposed to be a chapter about sabotage, so I'll keep it professional. Mostly.

Day two was improvisation. We cut patches from a spare fuel drum, beat them flat, and bedded them in marine epoxy that had been sitting in the hold since sometime in the previous geological epoch. It cured slow in the damp. Everything cured slow in the damp. The river rose an inch overnight and fell two by noon, and I stood on the bank reading the water like a man reading a letter from someone angry with him.

Day three was the day I stopped being useful with my hands and started being dangerous with my mouth.

Because here's what I knew, standing there with epoxy under my fingernails: five days remaining, eleven days of river between us and the headwaters, unless. Unless the gorge.

Every chart marked it the same way. *Paso del Diablo — impassable at all stages.* Eleven kilometers of granite throat where the Río Sereno dropped ninety meters through a channel that averaged, in places, less than the width of the boat. The old logging company had blasted a flume through it in 1961, lost six men, and never tried again. The maps didn't show a route because there wasn't one. What they showed was a straight line and a dare.

"There's another way," I said, that third evening, while Elian recalculated our drift rate for the fourth time.

He didn't look up. "There isn't."

"The gorge."

Now he looked up. "No."

"It cuts the route to three days—"

"*Noor.*" He set the pencil down with the exaggerated care of a man who wanted very badly to throw it. "The gorge kills professionals. The gorge killed six loggers with winches and dynamite and a plan. We have a patched drum-steel hull, a motor that floods if you sneeze near it, and—" he gestured at himself, at me, at the whole wet ridiculous enterprise "—this."

"And if we don't try it, we lose. Voss wins. The permits lapse, Halcyon walks into that valley with guns, and Aurelio's promise becomes a story we tell in bars about the time we almost mattered." I heard my own voice climbing and couldn't stop it. "I didn't come this far to watch it expire on a calendar."

"Then walk," he said. "Portage around. Ten days overland, brutal, but survivable. We lose the deadline but we keep our lives, which are generally considered load-bearing."

"With what supplies? We ate the margin two days ago. By the time we portaged we'd arrive too weak to stand, let alone argue with enforcers." I crouched so we were eye to eye, which was a mistake, because his eyes were furious and frightened in equal measure and it scrambled something in my chest. "It's the gorge or nothing, Elian."

"That's not a choice, that's a hostage situation."

"Welcome to my entire financial life."

He laughed — one sharp involuntary bark, hatefully charming — and then pressed his palms against his eyes and was silent for a long time. The river talked to itself in the dark. Somewhere upstream, thunder cleared its throat.

"If we run it," he said finally, "we scout it first. On foot. Every meter. I chart the line, mark every rock, every eddy, every place the water folds. We go at first light, at the lowest stage, and we go *my* line, exactly, no heroics."

"Agreed."

"And you wear the life vest."

"I always wear the vest."

"You wore it as a cushion for three weeks."

"That's called multi-tasking."

* * *

### Elian

He meant to go alone.

I want that recorded somewhere, in some archive, in some margin note that survives us both: on the third night, after we agreed, after we shook hands like businessmen over a suicide pact, Noor Reyes began quietly packing a single dry bag. Rope, flare, the map in its oilcloth, two days of food. One bag. One person's weight.

I watched him do it from the darkness past the firelight for ten minutes, feeling something in me go very cold and very clear, the way water goes when it slows down and starts to think.

"You're leaving tonight," I said.

He didn't startle, which I'll give him. He just sat back on his heels and looked at me across the fire, and for once he didn't reach for a joke. That was almost worse.

"At dawn," he said. "You'd slow me down in the gorge. Be honest, Elian. You're a brilliant navigator and a terrible swimmer, and the gorge doesn't grade on a curve."

"And you're going to run eleven kilometers of whitewater solo, in a patched boat, carrying a map, so that if you die, I die too — just slower, and with paperwork." I stood up. My legs were shaking and I made them hold anyway. "No."

"Noor's law," he said softly. "One bag, one man, one chance. It's how I've survived everything."

"It's how you've survived *alone*. It's not the same thing anymore." I crossed to him and took the dry bag out of his hands, and he let me, which told me everything. "You don't get to decide to be a martyr. I'm in this. I was in this before I liked you, and I am extremely in this now, and if you run that gorge without me I will follow your wreck downstream on foot and be furious at your corpse for the rest of my natural life."

"That's a lot of fury to commit to."

"I'm a Reyes. We commit."

He looked at me for a long moment. Firelight does dishonest things to faces, but what I saw in his was honest enough: fear, and under the fear, the specific loneliness of a man who had planned his whole life around the assumption that nobody would insist on staying.

"If we capsize in there," he said, "the water will take us apart in seconds. There's no eddy big enough to hold a boat, no bank you can climb. It's a slot. You understand? It's a throat."

"I've read the charts."

"Charts are prayers, Elian. They're wishes with grid lines." He laughed, low and hopeless, and rubbed his face. "God. Four days ago I thought the worst thing on this river was the company."

"The company is still on the river."

"Yes. But the company wants the map. The gorge just wants us." He looked upstream, toward the black seam in the hills where the granite waited, and I watched him do the thing he does — measure, count, convert dread into distance. "First light tomorrow. We scout the entry on foot today. Your storm instincts, my line. Fifty-fifty."

"Ninety-ten," I said. "My line."

"Sixty-forty and I carry the rope."

"Deal."

We shook on it, and then, because we are who we are now, the handshake turned into holding on, and we stood there in the firelight with the river rising and the clock burning down, two men gripping each other like the last fixed points on a washed-out map.

Above the far ridge, lightning stitched the clouds — silent, distant, counting something of its own.

Tomorrow we run the throat of the world. Tonight I am not afraid of the gorge.

I am afraid of how much I need him to come back, and how much he needs me to say so, and how neither of us says it, and how the water doesn't care, and how we go anyway.
