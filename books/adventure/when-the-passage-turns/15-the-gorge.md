## 15. The Gorge

There's a thing storm chasers say about the moment before a tornado takes your truck: you stop being afraid and start being curious. I'd always thought that was bravado dressed up as philosophy. Standing at the lip of the gorge that morning, watching the Río Sereno fold itself into a green cataract of noise, I finally understood it. Fear has a ceiling. Curiosity doesn't. It just keeps going down.

The gorge was maybe forty meters wide at the top and narrowed to half that within the first kilometer, walls of black basalt rising on both sides like the pages of a book someone slammed shut. The river didn't run through it so much as get forced through it, compressed into a series of drops that Elian — who had spent two days with his maps and my binoculars and a vocabulary of profanity I hadn't known he possessed — had catalogued as "Class V, possibly worse, possibly much worse."

"Officially," he said, "I want to state for the record that this is insane."

"Noted for the record."

"We lost four days to the sabotage. The permits expire in nine. The portage around the gorge is six days if nothing goes wrong, and something always goes wrong."

"So we go through."

"So we go through," he agreed, in the tone of a man signing his own autopsy. He looked at me then, and there was something in his face I'd been seeing more of lately — something that wasn't distrust anymore, hadn't been for a while, and that scared me in a way the gorge didn't. "Noor. If anything happens—"

"Nothing's going to happen."

"That's what people say right before things happen."

"I'm a professional," I said. "Things happen *to* other people."

He almost smiled. That was the trick of him, I was learning. He could be furious with me, betrayed by me, ready to walk into the ash country alone — and still, one stupid joke from me and the corner of his mouth would give him away. I'd built a whole life out of not letting people matter. Elian Reyes had dismantled it in about three weeks without ever raising his voice.

We ran the checks twice. Life jackets cinched, throw rope coiled where I could reach it blind, the dry bag lashed down with everything that mattered — the map, the letters, the satellite phone, our last real food. The boat itself was a battered aluminum skiff we'd named *La Promesa*, which Elian said was sentimental and I said was accurate. She'd taken us through rapids that should have killed us. I put my hand flat on her gunwale and told her, out loud, because I'm superstitious about machines the way sailors are superstitious about the sea, "One more run, old girl."

Elian pretended not to hear. His ears went red anyway.

* * *

The first kilometer was a lie. The river ran fast but clean, big rolling water, and I let myself believe the guidebooks and the gods had exaggerated. Elian called the lines from the bow — "left channel, hard left, stay off the wall" — in that precise cartographer's voice, every word placed like a contour line, and I steered, and for a few minutes we were good. We were better than good. We were the version of ourselves the river had been building since the day we met: two men who argued about everything except the water, because the water didn't care about our feelings.

Then the gorge turned.

I heard it before I saw it — a change in the sound, from roar to something deeper, a sub-bass grinding that I felt in my back teeth. Around the bend the walls pinched together and the whole river dropped into a chute no wider than the boat was long, and beyond the chute, mist. A lot of mist. Mist that meant a drop you couldn't see the bottom of.

"Elian!"

"I see it!" He was already scrambling, throwing his weight to port. "There's an eddy — left side, behind that rock — we can scout—"

The river decided otherwise.

A lateral wave came off the right wall — I've seen waves like it on the plains when a supercell's outflow hits warm air sideways, a wall of moving atmosphere — and it took the stern and swung us like a door. I corrected. Overcorrected. The chute reached up and took us.

What follows, I remember in pieces, the way chasers remember tornadoes: not a movie but a deck of cards thrown at your face.

The bow lifting. Elian's hand finding mine across the thwarts for half a second — not holding on, just touching, confirming, the way you touch a doorframe in an earthquake.

Black water over the top of us.

The sound. God, the sound — the whole gorge was a struck bell and we were inside it.

And then impact, and the world became white.

* * *

I came up gasping. The boat was gone — not capsized, gone, in pieces, I saw half a thwart cartwheeling downstream and an aluminum panel spinning in an eddy like a dropped coin. My life jacket had done its job; my shoulder hadn't, and fire lit up my left arm every time I moved it. The current had me. I did what twenty years of whitewater instinct demanded: feet up, pointing downstream, riding the boils, waiting for the river to spit me somewhere survivable.

That's when I saw him.

Elian was maybe thirty meters ahead, and he wasn't swimming. He was tumbling — the dead, boneless tumble of a body the water owns completely. His head broke the surface once, twice, and both times it went under again without him taking a breath, and I knew what that meant. I'd seen it once before, a spotter named Calloway, on the Pecos, years ago. The water had knocked him out.

The river was carrying him toward a strainer — a fallen tree wedged between two boulders, branches combing the surface, the kind of feature that kills more paddlers than all the holes and hydraulics combined. You go into a strainer, the water goes through and you don't.

My shoulder screamed. I swam anyway.

I've never been a strong swimmer by any honest measure — plains boy, landlocked childhood, and most of my adult relationship with water has been adversarial — but fear does something arithmetic to the body. I angled across the current using every trick I knew, ferrying hard, trading distance downstream for distance across, and the strainer grew in front of us like a slow-motion accident, and Elian's body drifted toward those black branches, and I made a sound I don't want to remember making.

I caught his collar with my right hand.

The current hit both of us then, a freight train of it, and for a long moment I understood with total clarity that I was going to watch him die while holding onto him, that my grip would be the only witness. My legs found something — a rock, a miracle, a rock — and I jammed them against it and held, and held, and the water tried to peel us off like old paint.

"Elian." My mouth was full of river. "ELIAN."

His head lolled. One of his arms dragged through the foam.

There was an eddy behind the boulder I was braced against — I could see it, that smooth inverted V of calm water, close enough to touch. Getting there meant letting go of the rock. Letting go meant trusting the eddy to catch us, and if I read it wrong, we'd both be downstream in the strainer's teeth inside ten seconds.

Storm chasers have a rule: never drive into the rain core, you can't see what's in it. And every chaser I've ever respected has broken that rule exactly once, at the moment it mattered.

I let go.

The river took us — one horrible instant of freefall, my arm around his chest now, both arms, my bad shoulder howling — and then the eddy closed around us like a hand, and the roar stepped back, and we were turning slowly in a pocket of quiet behind the boulder while the rest of the world kept screaming past.

I hauled him onto the boulder's low shelf. Rock under my knees, his weight heavy and wrong in my arms, water pouring off both of us.

He wasn't breathing.

I know CPR. Everyone knows CPR. Knowing it and doing it on a wet rock in a gorge with your hands shaking so hard they barely recognize each other are different countries. I tilted his head, swept his mouth — river gravel, a leaf — pinched his nose, and breathed for him. Once. Twice. Then compressions, counting out loud because the count was the only solid thing in the universe, thirty of them, my voice cracking on the numbers, my shoulder announcing its resignation and being ignored.

On the fourth cycle he convulsed.

He turned sideways and vomited half the Río Sereno onto the rock, and then he coughed, a raw tearing sound, and then he breathed — great ragged gulps of air, eyes still shut, hands scrabbling weakly at the stone — and I made another sound, this one closer to a sob than I'll admit to anyone, and gathered him against me with both arms, bad shoulder and all, and just held on.

"You're okay," I said into his hair. "You're okay. I've got you. I've got you."

His eyes opened after a while. They were unfocused, then focused, and the first thing they found was my face, and the first thing he said, in a voice scraped down to gravel, was:

"The map."

I laughed. It came out broken. "You nearly drowned and your first word is *the map*."

"It's waterproof," he rasped. "I need to know if I'm waterproof."

"You're waterproof," I said, and kissed his forehead, salt and river and shaking. "You're the most waterproof man alive."

* * *

We got off the water as fast as two half-drowned men could move, which is to say slowly, leaning on each other along a shelf of basalt above the rapids until the gorge walls opened enough to let us climb. The sun was low and orange by the time we found a gravel bench above the flood line, sheltered by an overhang, and I got a fire going with damp wood and pure spite.

Inventory: one dry bag, recovered from an eddy a hundred meters downstream by a swim I charged to the adrenaline account. Inside: the map, dry. The letters, dry. The satellite phone, dry, one bar of battery. One emergency blanket. A knife. Half a chocolate bar going soft in its wrapper. Everything else — tent, food, rope, the stove, Elian's instruments, my father's old rain jacket — was somewhere in the gorge or the sea of forest below it, becoming geology.

*La Promesa* was gone. I didn't say a eulogy out loud, but I gave her a moment.

Elian sat wrapped in the emergency blanket, silver and shivering, watching the fire like it might explain itself. There was a cut above his eyebrow I'd cleaned with the last of our water. His lips had color again. Every few minutes he'd take a breath that was too deep, testing his own machinery, and I'd watch him do it and feel the fear come back fresh, a tide that kept returning.

"You stopped breathing," I said, finally. I hadn't planned to say it. It came out anyway.

"I figured," he said quietly. "Everything went... bright, and then quiet. Like the eye of something."

"Yeah." I fed the fire a stick. "I've stood in the eye of a few. Never liked the quiet part."

He looked over at me. Firelight did things to his face I could have watched for years, if years were the kind of thing I believed in. "You came after me."

"You came after me first," I said. "Back on the first rapids. Fair's fair."

"Noor." He said my name the way he drew boundaries on a map — carefully, meaning it. "I was dead. Or close enough that the difference was academic. And you—" His voice caught. He started over. "Nobody's ever come into the water for me. Not literally. Not any way."

"Well," I said, wry because the alternative was not wry, and not-wry was a country I couldn't afford to live in yet, "somebody had to. You're carrying the map."

He threw a pebble at me. It bounced off my chest, and we both laughed, and the laugh turned into something else halfway through — the kind of laugh that's just crying wearing a disguise — and then he leaned over and put his forehead against mine, and we stayed like that while the fire cracked and the gorge roared its one note below us.

"I thought I was going to watch you die," I said into the small space between us. "I need you to know that whatever else happens — the permits, the valley, Aurelio, all of it — that's the thing now. That's the fixed point. I don't lose you. We can argue about everything else, but that's settled."

He pulled back far enough to look at me. His eyes were wet, and he didn't pretend otherwise.

"Settled," he said.

* * *

We walked upstream at first light, following the river the way the map demanded, because the map didn't care that our boat was kindling and our bodies were bruise-colored. Nine days to permit expiry. Maybe five days' walk to the headwaters, if the terrain allowed, and terrain never allows.

But here's what I noticed, somewhere in the second hour, climbing through ash-country scrub with Elian's hand pulling me up a ledge: I wasn't counting miles.

I've measured my whole life in miles. Miles to the storm, miles from the debt, miles between me and everyone who might ask questions. Miles are safe. Miles add up to something you can owe and repay. But walking beside him, my shoulder screaming, my lungs full of yesterday's river, I caught myself measuring in something else entirely. In breaths. In the number of times he looked back to check on me. In the exact weight of his hand when he passed me the chocolate bar and lied that he wasn't hungry.

Somewhere behind us, downstream, I knew Voss was coming. Somewhere ahead, the valley waited with its springs and its families and its secret written in a dead man's handwriting. And somewhere in the lining of that map, in oilcloth and thread, was the truth about whose blood ran in mine — a truth Elian carried silently, I could see it sitting on him some mornings, and I wasn't ready, and the river didn't care.

The gorge fell away behind us. Ahead, the mountains stacked themselves blue against the sky, and the Río Sereno ran thin and cold beside the trail, running home.

Elian stopped at the ridgeline and pointed.

Smoke. Far off, high in the next valley — not wildfire smoke. Cookfire smoke. Thin, domestic, human.

"Somebody lives up there," he said.

I shaded my eyes and looked, and my heart did something strange — not fear, not hope, something older than either.

"Yeah," I said. "Let's go meet them."
