## 16. Permit Expiry

I've spent twenty-two years collecting debts, and I'll tell you the one thing nobody in the business admits: the ledger is a story you tell yourself. Every line item is a character. The debtor is the villain until he pays, and then he's a fool. The creditor is the victim until he gets his money, and then he's a hero who was never greedy at all. I wrote that story for two decades and believed every word of it, because believing it was cheaper than asking what kind of man keeps books on other people's desperation.

The permits expired at midnight.

I knew the exact minute because I'd been watching my watch do it, second by second, in the dark above the valley, with a satellite phone in my lap and eleven men behind me in the treeline and nothing — not one goddamn thing — to show for four months of river miles, bribed ferry pilots, slashed hulls, and a boot I'd ruined crossing ash country that still smelled like a burned church.

At 12:00:04, Halcyon Terra's regional director came through the phone in a voice like a filing cabinet closing.

"Voss. Status."

"They're at the headwaters. Two men. They've been there six days."

"And the occupants?"

"Farmers. Maybe forty people across three settlements. Terraces, springs, irrigation channels older than the company. No papers. No registration. Nothing in any archive I could buy." I paused, because the next part required me to say it out loud to hear how it sounded. "They've been farming that valley since before your concession existed. Legally, under the old land code, continuous occupancy predating registration is a claim. Weak one. But it exists."

"It doesn't exist," the director said, "because nobody has filed it. And at midnight, our permit lapsed, which means we have no legal instrument either. Which means what happens next isn't law, Voss. It's logistics."

There it was. The word they always reached for when the story stopped holding. *Logistics.* As if men with rifles moving families off land they'd worked for generations were just a supply chain problem with weather.

"What exactly are you ordering?" I asked, though I knew. I'd written enough collection orders in my life to recognize one being read to me.

"Secure the basin before word gets out. The survey data suggests mineral rights worth nine figures. If a single registry clerk in the capital hears the word 'trust' attached to that valley before we've got boots on it, this becomes a decade of litigation and we lose. You have enforcers. You have the element of surprise. You have until the rainy season closes the passes. Do I need to say it more plainly?"

"No," I said. "You don't."

"Good. Confirm the order."

I looked at my watch. 12:07. Somewhere below me, past the black shoulder of the ridge, a fire was burning in the valley floor. I could see its glow on the low clouds, an orange stain like a bruise coming up through skin.

"Confirmed," I said, and hung up, and sat there in the dark feeling like a man who has just signed something without reading it, except I had read it. That was the problem. I'd read it twice.

* * *

Let me tell you about Noor Reyes, because I've been chasing him longer than Halcyon Terra has, and I know him better than his own creditors do.

I met him three years ago over a debt of forty-one thousand — hazard pay advances against storm-chasing contracts, medical liens from a hailstorm that nearly took his eye, interest compounding like mold in a wet wall. He was polite about it. That's the thing I never forgave him for. Most debtors beg or rage or vanish. Noor Reyes would sit across from me in some roadside diner, count out what he had, write down what he owed, and ask me to initial the arithmetic. He treated the ledger like a shared document. Like we were both just men keeping accounts.

It made my job easy and made me feel like a fraud, which is a bad combination, so I stopped taking his file personally and started taking it professionally, and when the collections dried up — when the optimists who owned his paper sold it all to a company with helicopters and lawyers — I went with it, because a ledger is a ledger no matter whose name is on the cover.

That's what I told myself, anyway, on the river, in the ash country, on this ridge.

Now I lay on my stomach at the treeline and looked down into the basin, and I want to be honest with you, whoever ends up reading this, if anyone does: I hesitated.

* * *

The valley at night was not what the survey drones had promised. The drones had promised terrain: contours, water tables, ore signatures. What I saw was lamplight. A dozen small fires and lamps strung along terraces that stepped down the walls of the basin like the pages of a book someone had fanned open. Water moved through it — I could hear it, that low constant conversation a river makes when it's young — and along the channels, dark figures were working late, hauling something, maybe the last of a harvest, their voices carrying up the ridge in fragments of a language I didn't speak but understood anyway, because it was the sound of people finishing a day's work together, and there is no translation required for that.

And near the largest fire, close enough that I could pick out two silhouettes against the light, sat the Reyes cousins — or whatever they were; the genealogy in Aurelio's file had been a maze I'd given up solving — sitting shoulder to shoulder, bent over something flat between them. A map. Of course a map. One of them gestured, tracing lines, and the other leaned in, and even at four hundred meters I could read the body language: two men arguing about a drawing with the total seriousness of priests arguing about scripture, and underneath the argument, something else, the way they kept touching each other's arms to make points. That's not how enemies sit. That's not even how partners sit. That's how people sit who have decided, somewhere downriver, that drowning alone is worse than drowning together.

I'd had them in my sights, figuratively, for months. Easier when they were abstractions. *The courier. The cartographer. Obstacles with names.* But abstraction dies hard at four hundred meters in the cold, watching a man warm his hands at a fire he built next to another man he loves, on land he walked half a country to reach because a dying stranger asked him to.

Because that was the part I couldn't shake. Not the map, not the trust, not the nine-figure mineral signature. The promise. Aurelio Reyes had handed a broke storm chaser a piece of oilcloth and said *carry it home*, and the idiot had done it. Through rapids and floods and my own people slashing his boat, he had carried a dead man's paper upstream like it was a sacrament. I know debts. I've spent my life around people who owe things. And I can tell you the difference between a man paying a debt and a man keeping a promise, and it is the difference between a dog on a leash and a dog swimming out to save you. Same motion. Entirely different animal.

Noor Reyes didn't owe Aurelio anything. There was no paper between them, no interest rate, no collector. He owed him a promise, which is the one instrument no court enforces and no ledger holds, and he'd paid it anyway, at full value, in the only currency that can't be restructured.

And here I was, at midnight, ordered to take it from him by force.

* * *

Márquez found me at the treeline around two in the morning. My second. Good man. Bad conscience, which is why he was my second — I'd promoted him years ago precisely because he asked questions, and questions keep a crew honest, or at least keep them tired.

"You heard the order," I said.

"I heard it." He crouched beside me, looking down at the fires. "Voss, those aren't squatters. I talked to the kid at the lower terrace today, while you were on the ridge. Bought eggs off him. His family's been here — he says seven generations. He showed me the irrigation channels. His great-grandfather cut them. There are graves up the slope with dates older than the republic."

"The company's position is that undocumented occupancy isn't title."

"The company's position," Márquez said, "is whatever the director's bonus requires it to be this quarter." He was quiet a moment. "You ran collections for twenty years. You ever seize anything from somebody who didn't owe it?"

I thought about it honestly, which is a habit I recommend to no one in this profession. "Once. Early. A widow in a flood district. The note was fraudulent — her husband's partner forged it. I knew. I took the house anyway, because the client paid, and the client's money spends the same as honest money." I watched the firelight below. "I told myself the same thing I tell myself now. That I'm not the one who writes the orders. I'm the one who executes them. That there's a difference."

"Is there?"

"That's the question I've been avoiding for twenty-two years, Márquez, and I'd thank you not to ask it again at two in the morning on a mountainside."

He almost smiled. "The men are asking it too. Quietly. Delgado already said if it comes to dragging farmers off terraces, he wants his severance. Two others nodded. These are enforcers, Voss. We're hired for show and intimidation, mostly. Nobody signed up for—" he gestured at the valley, at the graves, at the whole moral weight of it "—this."

"Nobody ever signs up for this," I said. "This arrives later, wearing the uniform you're already in."

Below us, the two figures at the fire stood. One of them — the tall one, Noor — raised his arms in exasperation, then dropped them, then laughed, and the laugh carried up the ridge, thin and human and completely indifferent to the fact that eleven armed men were lying in the dark deciding whether his valley belonged to a mining company. Elian put a hand on his shoulder. Noor covered it with his own. Then they sat back down, and the argument resumed, and I felt like a burglar who has broken into a house and found a family singing.

* * *

Here is the arithmetic, since arithmetic is my native language. I'm going to lay it out the way I'd lay out a debtor's file, because it's the only way I know how to think.

Asset: unregistered basin, mineral rights estimated at nine figures, per Halcyon Terra survey. Legal status post-midnight: none. Company claim: none. Permit: expired. Which means the seizure order is not an execution of law but a preemptive theft — the legal term is *adverse action pending registration*, and the practical term is robbery, and the difference between the two is roughly the thickness of a filing.

Liability: unknown trust instrument, referenced in Aurelio Reyes's papers, payable to bearer of a promise. Unverified. Possibly worthless. Possibly the single most valuable piece of paper in the province. If it's real and gets filed first, Halcyon Terra doesn't lose the valley. Halcyon Terra loses the valley, the nine figures, and a decade of litigation fees, and probably several executives' liberty, because companies that order midnight seizures tend to have paperwork problems when the seizures become lawsuits.

Risk: eleven men, most of whom don't want to do it, against forty civilians, two of whom survived the Río Sereno gorge in a season that kills professionals. And somewhere downstream, presumably, a satellite uplink, a land registry, and a storm chaser who has already proven he can outrun weather.

Expected value of executing the order: high, if nothing goes wrong. Catastrophic, if anything does.

But that's not why I hesitated. Any analyst can run those numbers. I hesitated because of the eggs.

Márquez's kid with the eggs. Seven generations. Graves older than the republic. I have spent my career reducing lives to line items, and I stood there on that ridge trying to reduce that valley to one, and the line item wouldn't hold. It kept expanding. It kept turning into a boy selling eggs to the man sent to evict him, and charging fair prices, and saying thank you.

A ledger, I have learned tonight, is a machine for making certain things invisible. It shows you what is owed. It cannot show you what is kept. And that valley was full of what is kept — channels cut by hand, terraces built by generations, promises carried up rivers — and none of it appeared anywhere in Halcyon Terra's books, which meant that as far as the company was concerned, none of it existed, which meant the company could take it with a clean balance sheet and a dirty everything else.

I have been the optimists' man for a long time. Tonight, lying in the wet grass above a valley full of strangers' firelight, I understood that the optimists' optimism had always been simple: that no one would ever make them look at what their money did. That the men who execute would keep executing, and the books would stay clean, and the story would hold.

The story wasn't holding. I could feel it coming apart in my hands like wet paper.

* * *

At four in the morning I called the director back.

He answered on the first ring, because men like him sleep like ledgers — flat, closed, balanced.

"Voss. Progress."

"There's a complication." I kept my voice level, professional, the voice of a man reporting logistics. "The occupants have documentation. A trust instrument, registered provisionally, tied to the original prospector's concession claims. Pre-dates ours. If we move on them now, we're not securing an asset, we're assaulting the holders of a senior claim, and every one of my men becomes a defendant the moment a registry clerk files that trust."

Silence on the line. I watched the firelight below and lied with the calm of a man reading true numbers, which is the only kind of lying I respect.

"Is the trust verified?"

"It's in their possession. Whether it's valid depends on who files it first." I let that sit. "Director, I can execute your order. Eleven men, forty civilians, remote basin, no witnesses. We'd probably hold it. Probably. And then we'd spend ten years and eight figures losing it in court, with video, with names, with the kind of press that ends careers and starts investigations. Or I can report that the operation is not viable and recommend the company pursue acquisition — negotiate with the trust holders, buy access legally, come in through the front door instead of over the wall."

"You're recommending negotiation," he said, in the tone of a man identifying a disease.

"I'm recommending arithmetic."

Another silence, longer. Down in the valley, the fires were burning down to coals. One by one the lamps on the terraces went dark, ordinary as breath.

"You've been in the field a long time, Voss," the director said finally. "Field men go native. It's documented. I'm authorizing a secondary team. They'll be at the pass in five days. You can either bring your people home with a clean report, or you can explain to the board why you refused a direct order."

"Put it in writing," I said, "and I'll decide which explanation costs less," and hung up before he could hear whatever my voice was doing, because I didn't know myself.

Five days. That's what I'd bought them. Five days before a second team came over the pass with fewer questions and colder blood.

* * *

Márquez was waiting when I turned back. He'd heard enough, standing in the dark the way soldiers do, hearing everything and admitting nothing.

"So," he said. "We going native?"

"Ask me tomorrow."

"Tomorrow's five days closer to the second team."

"I know how to count, Márquez."

"Yeah," he said, looking down at the valley, where the last lamp had gone out and only the coals remained, a faint red pulse in the black like something alive and sleeping. "That's what worries me. You've always known how to count. Question is what you're counting for."

I didn't answer him. I lay back down at the treeline and watched the coals fade, and I thought about a widow's house I took twenty years ago, and a forged note I executed anyway, and every clean entry in every dirty book I'd ever kept, and I understood that I had come up this river chasing a debt and was leaving — if I left — owing one.

Five days.

In the morning, I was going to walk down into that valley, knock on the door of the tent where two men were arguing over a map, and tell them everything: the order, the second team, the clock. Hand them the numbers and let them run them, because if there was one thing I'd learned watching Noor Reyes across diner tables for three years, it was that the man treats a ledger like a shared document.

Time to find out if that was true when the ledger was mine.
