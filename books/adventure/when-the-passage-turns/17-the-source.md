## 17. The Source

**Noor**

The last morning of the climb, Elian stopped arguing about elevation.

That's how I knew we were close. For eleven days he'd been correcting my estimates — *we're at nine hundred meters, Noor, not a thousand, your altimeter is sentimental* — but that morning he just stood at the lip of the ridge with his mouth open, and I realized the cartographer had run out of map.

Below us, the land fell away into a bowl so green it looked wet even from two kilometers up. Not the green of scrub or second-growth logging, which is the color of apology. This was old green. Terraces stepped down the basin walls in long curves, each one stitched with water channels that caught the early light and threw it back like someone had laid copper wire across the whole valley. Smoke rose from a dozen cooking fires, thin and unhurried. A river braided through the floor — no, rivers, plural, six or seven silver threads all crawling out of the hillsides and weaving themselves into something wide enough to deserve the name Río Sereno.

I'd spent three weeks imagining the source as a single point. A spring you could cup in your hands. Something you could put on a map with an X.

It wasn't a point. It was everything at once.

"Tell me I'm not hallucinating," Elian said. "Tell me dehydration has finally gotten me."

"You're not hallucinating." My voice came out strange, flattened, like I was talking underwater. "There are people down there."

"I counted forty-one field plots before I gave up." He had his notebook out, hands shaking — from the climb, I told myself, though we both knew better. "Noor. These terraces are pre-concession. Some of them might be pre-*survey*. Do you understand what that means? Halcyon Terra's entire claim framework assumes vacant land. Unimproved. There's nothing vacant down there."

Down in the basin, a woman was walking an irrigation channel with a shovel over her shoulder, moving at the pace of someone who had done this walk ten thousand times and expected to do it ten thousand more. A dog trotted beside her. Somewhere below, faint on the wind, a child laughed, and the sound climbed the valley walls to us like it had been sent.

Aurelio's map sat against my chest where it had ridden for ninety days minus four. I thought about the way he'd said *the source*, back in that border-town bar, coughing blood into a rag while he made me swear. I'd assumed he meant water. Standing on that ridge, watching smoke rise from forty kitchens, I understood he'd never once meant water.

"He sent me to find people," I said.

"He sent you home," Elian said, and then went quiet, because neither of us was ready for what that sentence contained.

We descended. It took most of the day, switchbacking down through cloud forest that smelled like rain and rot and something sweeter underneath — citrus, maybe, or the white flowers that grew in ropes along the trail. Twice we passed stone walls older than any country I could name, moss-eaten, holding up terrace soil that should have washed away centuries ago and hadn't. Somebody had maintained these walls for generations. Somebody had decided, over and over, across hundreds of years, that this place was worth keeping.

My legs were shaking by the time we reached the valley floor. Three weeks of gorge and ash country and half-rations will do that. But I barely felt it, because the first person to meet us was a boy of maybe eight, standing in the middle of the path with a machete too big for him held with total confidence, asking us in Spanish who we were and what we wanted.

"Travelers," Elian said, hands visible, voice gentle. "From downstream."

"Nobody comes from downstream," the boy said. "Downstream is the company."

"Not anymore," I said. "We hope."

He studied us the way Voss studied ledgers — looking for the weakness, the angle, the lie. Then he turned and shouted up the hill in a language I didn't recognize, and within minutes people started appearing out of the fields the way fish rise to a disturbance in still water. Curious. Cautious. Unarmed, I noticed, except for tools.

An older woman took charge without taking center stage — I'd learn her name was Marisol, and that she was, in the complete absence of any election, the person whose word settled things. She looked at our ruined boots, our rope-burned hands, the salt crust on our clothes, and she asked one question:

"Who sent you?"

"A dying man named Aurelio Reyes," I said. "He gave me a map and a promise."

Something moved through the crowd then, not quite a murmur. More like weather changing pressure. An old man near the back crossed himself. Marisol closed her eyes for a moment, and when she opened them they were wet, and furious, and relieved, all three at once.

"That man," she said, "has been sending us letters for sixty years. Warning us. Telling us when the surveyors were coming, when the permits changed, when to hide the terraces from the aerial passes." She looked at me hard. "And now he sends us two strangers?"

"He sent one stranger," I said. "The other one invited himself."

Elian, beside me, didn't deny it.

* * *

**Elian**

They fed us first. That's the thing I keep returning to, weeks later, when I try to explain the valley to people who've only ever seen land as an asset: before they asked us anything else, before they weighed us or questioned us or decided what we were worth, they fed us. Fish from the channels, maize from the upper terraces, a sauce that made Noor's eyes water until he was laughing. We ate in the open air under a roof of woven palm, surrounded by children who stared at us with the frank appraisal of people who had never been taught that strangers outrank them.

I filled twelve pages of my notebook that first evening and every page contradicted the one before it.

Here is what I had believed, professionally, for my entire career: that land becomes real when it is surveyed. That a place unmeasured is a place unfinished, waiting for coordinates the way a sentence waits for grammar. I had built my life on instruments — theodolites, GPS units, the cold authority of the grid. And here was a valley that had never appeared on any official map, that existed in the legal record as a blank space labeled *unregistered*, and it was the most finished, most fully human landscape I had ever stood inside.

The terraces weren't just agriculture. They were archives. Marisol walked us along the upper channels at dusk and read them to us like text: this wall rebuilt after the flood of her grandmother's time; this spring house older than anyone could remember; this field left fallow on a rotation nobody had written down because everyone simply knew. The water itself was governed — there were rules, spoken ones, about who drew from which channel in which season, and the rules had held through droughts and company incursions and at least one armed attempt at seizure that Marisol described with the flat tone of someone recounting bad weather.

"And the permits?" Noor asked. "Halcyon Terra holds concession rights over this whole drainage. Ninety days, expiring soon. When their people come—"

"They have come before," Marisol said. "Twice in my lifetime. Once with papers, once with guns." She paused at a bend in the channel where the water ran quick and clear over red stone. "Papers we can argue with. Guns we survived. But always they go away confused, because they come looking for a mine, or timber, or a headwater to dam, and instead they find—" she gestured, taking in the terraces, the fires, the dogs asleep in doorways, "—us. And *us* does not fit in their ledger."

Noor was quiet for a long stretch of walking. I watched him work the problem the way he worked rapids — reading the surface, calculating what was underneath. I'd learned his silences by then. This one was heavy.

"The permit expires," he finally said. "After that, legally, the concession reverts. And whoever holds prior claim—"

"There is no prior claim," Marisol said. "Not on paper. We have never had paper. Paper is how they take things." She stopped and faced him, and I saw that she had understood something about Noor already that had taken me weeks. "You carried a dead man's promise up a river for three months. You think promises aren't paper?"

I felt Noor go still beside me.

"What do you know about Aurelio's promise?" he asked.

"We know he made one," Marisol said. "To us, years ago, in a letter. He wrote that if ever the company came to take the valley for good, a keeper would come. Someone carrying his word." Her eyes moved between us, unhurried. "We did not know who. We did not know when. We planted anyway. You do not stop planting because the future is uncertain. That is the whole secret of terraces, if you want one."

That night I couldn't sleep. I lay on a borrowed mat under the palm roof listening to the channels run — the whole valley whispering with moving water, day and night, a sound I realized I'd been hearing in fragments for weeks without understanding it was the sound of order, of agreement, of a hundred small decisions made well and kept.

Beside me, Noor was awake too. I could tell by his breathing.

"Elian," he said, very quietly, into the dark. "The map. When we get it out of the oilcloth tomorrow — I want you to look at the bottom margin again. The annotation I told you was just a surveyor's mark."

My heart did something complicated. "The one in Aurelio's handwriting."

"The one that isn't coordinates." His voice was steady, but I knew the cost of the steadiness. "It's a legal reference. I've been turning it over since the toll bridge. I think I know what it points to. And I think I've known since the ridge, and I've been afraid to say it, because saying it makes it real, and if it's real, then everything I planned to do with it—"

He stopped. In the dark, I reached over and found his hand.

"Say it tomorrow," I said. "Tonight, just sleep. The valley's not going anywhere. It's waited longer than either of us."

But sleep didn't come for either of us, and near dawn, when the first fires were lit below and the smell of woodsmoke rose through the palm thatch, Noor spoke again, one last thing, almost too quiet to catch:

"My grandfather built all this. Sixty years of letters, Elian. Sixty years of protecting people he never let himself love out loud." A breath. "And he picked me — the grandson he never met, the one drowning in debt — to be the one who carries it. Why me? Out of everyone in the world, why the one who needed money most?"

I didn't have an answer. But I thought about the letters sewn in the lining, the confession I'd found and kept silent about for a thousand kilometers, and I thought: *because you're the one who would understand what it costs to owe someone everything.* Because debt was the one language Noor and Aurelio already shared.

I didn't say that either. Tomorrow, I promised myself. Tomorrow the map comes out of its lining, all of it, everything I know.

Then the future arrived early, the way it does.

A runner came up from the lower terraces at first light, breathless, barefoot, a boy of fifteen who skidded to a stop in front of Marisol and gasped out words that emptied the breakfast fire of people in seconds.

"Men on the south ridge," he said. "Armed. Company jackets. And they're flying a drone."

Marisol looked at Noor. Noor looked at me. And I watched the awe drain out of his face and something harder take its place — the face I'd seen in the gorge, the one that appears when there's no longer any choice left worth discussing.

"Ninety days," he said quietly. "They couldn't even wait for the ink to dry."
