## 19. Voss at the Headwaters

I'd like to tell you I saw them coming. That some storm-sense tingled in my back teeth, that the birds went quiet, that the river itself whispered a warning up through its gravel throat.

None of that happened. What happened was a kid — one of Marta's grandkids, barefoot, maybe nine years old, with mud to his knees and a mango in each fist — came sprinting down the terrace path shouting *barcos, barcos* in the particular register of panic that doesn't need translation. Then the dogs started. Then the whole valley started, a low human hum rising from the terraces like the first pressure-drop before weather.

So no, I didn't sense it coming. But when it came, I recognized it. You always recognize your own weather.

Elian and I were on the eastern slope, staking out the spring line for the survey he was drafting — his handwriting on fresh paper, mine holding the tape measure, both of us pretending we weren't counting the days since the permits expired since neither of us knew what expired permits actually meant for people who'd arrived without any. We heard the engines before we saw anything: diesel, heavy, more than one, grinding up the old logging spur on the valley's north shoulder where no road should have been passable and apparently now was, because money finds roads the way water finds cracks.

Elian straightened up from his map board. "How many?"

"Three engines," I said. "Maybe four vehicles."

"How many men does Voss have?"

"When I knew him? Six collectors and a lawyer." I coiled the tape measure with hands that wanted to shake and refused to let them. "He'll have upgraded."

We came down through the terraces fast, past families already moving with the practiced calm of people who had been displaced before and would not be moved easily this time. Old Tomás stood at the head of the lower field with a machete he clearly used for harvesting and clearly intended to be seen holding anyway. Marta had gathered the children into the longhouse. A man named Ciro, who hadn't spoken a word to me in three weeks of mapping his land, pressed something into my hand as I passed — an old .38 revolver, rust-freckled, probably older than me.

I gave it back to him. "No," I said. "Not like this."

He looked at me like I was insane. Maybe I was. But I'd spent my whole adult life reading skies, and I knew the difference between a storm you run from and a storm you stand inside of. This one, I had to stand inside of. Running was how valleys got emptied.

The lead vehicle crested the spur at midmorning — a flatbed, then two pickups, then a gray SUV with the Halcyon Terra chevron stenciled on the door. Men got out. Eight of them, nine, hard to count against the glare, most carrying the specific nonchalance of hired muscle trying to look like surveyors. And at the front, walking down the last stretch of trail alone while his men hung back, came Voss.

He'd changed since the border town. Not softer — Voss didn't do soft — but *finished*, somehow. The debt-collector's hungry lean had been replaced by something more corporate, a man wearing a lanyard instead of a ledger. His boots were new. His eyes weren't.

He stopped ten meters from me and looked around the valley — really looked, maybe for the first time, at the springs feeding the channels, the terraces cut generation-deep into the slopes, the smoke from cookfires standing straight in the windless air. Something crossed his face that I chose, generously, to read as hesitation rather than appraisal.

"Noor Reyes," he said.

"Voss."

"You look terrible."

"I've been paddling upstream for two months. How do you look?"

He almost smiled. Almost. "Like a man about to close a very large account." He turned a slow circle, taking in the terraces, the longhouse, Tomás with his machete, the line of farmers who had gathered behind us without being asked — thirty, forty people, silent, watching. "You know why I'm here."

"The permits expired."

"The permits expired," Voss agreed. "Which means the concession reverted to open status. Which means, per the regional land office, this basin is unregistered crown land available for claim. Halcyon Terra filed their claim yesterday morning. First valid filing takes precedence." He said it smoothly, like a man reciting terms he'd rehearsed in the truck. "Survey crews arrive within the week. Heavy equipment after that. The company is prepared to offer relocation assistance—"

"To the people who've farmed here for a hundred years."

"To whoever's squatting here, yes. Relocation assistance. Fair-market consideration. It's generous, Noor. More generous than most."

Behind me, I heard Elian come up the path. I felt him more than heard him, the way you feel a change in air pressure. He stopped at my shoulder and said, quietly, "Tell him."

"Not yet."

"Noor. *Tell him.*"

Voss's eyes flicked between us. "Tell me what?"

And here's the thing I want to be honest about, because honesty is the only weapon I brought to this fight: for about four seconds, I considered not telling him. Four seconds where I did the math the old Noor would have done — the math where information is leverage, where truth is a card you hold until it pays out, where every relationship is a transaction and every promise has a price tag. Four seconds where I thought: if I keep the trust quiet, maybe I can trade it. Maybe I can buy the valley's safety with my silence and walk away owed nothing but clean.

But I'd watched Voss work for years. I knew what he did with cards held close. He called bluffs with guns.

"It's not open land," I said.

Voss waited.

"Aurelio Reyes registered this basin forty-one years ago," I said. "Under a trust. Payable to the bearer of his final promise — carry the map to the source of the Río Sereno. Which I did. Which means the trust executed eleven days ago, when Elian and I walked into this valley. The deed isn't Halcyon Terra's. It never was. Your claim is a filing against property that legally belongs to someone else. It's not a claim, Voss. It's theft with paperwork."

The silence that followed had texture. I could hear the springs. I could hear a dog panting somewhere behind Tomás. I could hear my own pulse doing its storm-chaser thing, that weird calm-panic cocktail I used to pay rent chasing.

Voss's face went through several weathers of its own. Disbelief first — genuine, I think. Then calculation, fast and cold, the collector running numbers. Then something else, slower, that I couldn't name yet.

"That's a story," he said finally. "A dying man's story told by a debtor with everything to gain."

"It's a registry document. Aurelio filed it himself, with a notary in Puerto Cabral, seal and witnesses. Elian has copies. The original goes to the land office by satellite uplink tonight." I took a step closer, which was either brave or stupid, and honestly the distinction felt academic. "You can verify all of it before your survey crews finish unpacking. Or you can seize the valley today and spend the next decade losing the lawsuit, because every court in the country honors registered trusts over raw claims, and Halcyon Terra's lawyers will figure that out approximately six minutes after they read the file."

"And you're telling me this because."

"Because you're not a thief, Voss. You're a collector. There's a difference. Collectors collect what's *owed*. This—" I gestured at the terraces, the springs, the kids peeking from the longhouse, the whole living valley. "This isn't owed to anybody. It's just *here*, and it belongs to the people keeping it alive. Taking it wouldn't be collection. It would be robbery, and you'd know it every day for the rest of your life, and I know exactly what that kind of knowing does to a man, because I carried it for years."

"You don't know what I am anymore," Voss said. "People change employers."

"People change ledgers," I said. "The entries stay."

His jaw worked. Behind him, his men shifted — one of them, the big one with the shaved head, rested his hand on the stock of the rifle slung across his back, casual as anything, and I watched Tomás see it, and I watched Tomás shift his grip on the machete, and I understood with total clarity that we were about ninety seconds from a version of this morning where nobody walked away clean.

"Stand down," I said — to Voss, but loud enough for everyone. "Both sides. Nobody here wins a firefight. You've got nine men and rifles; we've got machetes and home ground and nothing left to lose, which makes us worse odds than you think. And even if you win — even if you clear every farmer off these terraces by nightfall — you'll be holding stolen paper. The company's own auditors will hang you for it eventually, because that's what companies do with men who bring them liabilities. I've watched them do it to better men than you."

"They weren't better," Voss said. Flat. Almost amused.

"No," I admitted. "They weren't."

He looked at me for a long time. And I want to report that I saw the exact moment his conscience woke up, some cinematic hinge swinging, but the truth was messier. What I saw was a man doing arithmetic — the same arithmetic he'd done his whole career, debts and assets, risk and return — except for the first time the columns didn't balance, because I'd added a number he'd never had to carry before: what it costs a man to know he robbed a valley.

"Show me the document," he said at last.

Elian stepped forward and handed over the folder — the certified copy, the notary's seal, Aurelio's signature unmistakable even faded, and beneath it, in the margin, the old man's final annotation in that careful draftsman's hand: *To the one who keeps the promise. Let the river keep the rest.*

Voss read it twice. His thumb stayed on the annotation longer than it needed to.

"He planned this," Voss said quietly. "Forty-one years ago, he planned this."

"He planned for someone to keep a promise," Elian said. "Who kept it was an accident. That's the part that gets me."

Voss closed the folder. For a moment he just stood there, and I swear the whole valley leaned in — the springs, the dogs, the kids, the sky.

Then he turned to his men and said, "Load up."

The big one with the rifle frowned. "Sir, the order was—"

"The order was to assess the claim," Voss said, in a voice that had ended careers. "Assessment complete. The claim is invalid. Load up."

They loaded. Slowly, sullenly, the big one last, glaring at me like I'd personally cost him a bonus. Voss walked back toward the SUV, then stopped, half-turned, and looked at me across the ten meters that had earlier been a battlefield and was now just dirt.

"You burned a ledger once," he said. "In Puerto Cabral. Your own. I read the file when I took this contract."

"I remember burning it."

"So do I." He said it strangely, like the memory belonged to him too. "It's the only entry I ever envied." He opened the SUV door, then paused again. "There's a second file, Noor. Internal. Halcyon's full dossier on this basin — mineral surveys, water rights projections, the works. They'll send a second team when this one reports failure. Legal won't stop them next time. They'll just make the paperwork prettier."

"What are you saying?"

"I'm saying I have that file in my vehicle, and I'm having a very strange morning." He got in. The engine turned over.

And then — as the convoy ground back down the logging spur, as the valley exhaled around us, as Elian's hand found mine without either of us deciding it — Voss's SUV stopped at the tree line. Held there a moment. Backed up twenty meters.

The window came down. Voss held out a black document case, arm extended, face unreadable.

"Take it," he said. "Before I talk myself out of being the man who did."

I walked over and took it. It was heavier than paper should be. Inside, I could see the chevron watermark, the internal classification band, decades of Halcyon Terra's intentions for this valley bound in a single folder.

"Why?" I asked him.

Voss looked past me, up the valley, at the springs and the smoke and the terraces — at everything he'd been sent to take and was, for reasons neither of us fully understood yet, handing back.

"Because somebody once told me the optimists I answer to don't forgive," he said. "Turns out I'm done asking them to."

The window went up. The convoy rolled. And I stood there in the road holding the weight of a company's whole appetite in my arms, while behind me Elian was already saying something about satellite uplinks and tonight and *we have to move fast* —

— and above the tree line, barely visible, a second set of headlights was coming up the spur.
