## 20. The Ledger Burns

**Voss**

I have spent eleven years collecting other people's debts, and I want it on the record that I was good at it. Not good like a surgeon is good — nothing about the work was clean — but good like weather is good at finding cracks in a roof. I could smell desperation through drywall. I knew which men would pay and which would run and which would hand me their mother's ring with an apology for the tarnish.

So understand what it cost me to stand on that ridge at dawn, looking down into a valley I'd been paid to steal, holding a ledger full of names I no longer believed in.

The valley didn't look stolen. That was the problem. It looked like something out of the old survey maps my grandfather kept rolled in a tin tube under his bed — terraces cut green into the slopes, smoke rising from cook fires in thin honest columns, a river braiding itself out of a dozen springs like it had all the time in the world. Children were already moving between the houses. Somebody was laughing. At five-forty in the morning, in a valley that officially did not exist, somebody was laughing.

My orders sat in my breast pocket, printed on Halcyon Terra letterhead: *Secure perimeter prior to third-party awareness. Legal exposure acceptable.* Four words doing the work of a confession. *Legal exposure acceptable* meant people. It always meant people. Companies don't write sentences like that about paperwork.

Below me, two fires burned close together. One belonged to Reyes and his cartographer. The other — smaller, older, built from stones placed by hands long dead — belonged to the valley.

I had watched their firelight for three nights from this ridge. First night, I told myself I was establishing surveillance protocol. Second night, I told myself I was waiting for reinforcements that were never coming, because Halcyon Terra's idea of support was a satellite phone and a lawyer on retainer in a city four hundred miles away. Third night, I stopped telling myself things.

That's the thing nobody tells you about pragmatism. It works fine right up until you have to look at what you've been pragmatic *about*.

I came down off the ridge while the light was still gray, walking slow, hands visible, the way you approach a dog that's been kicked. The enforcers — six of them, armed, bored, badly fed — held position at the headwaters crossing per my instruction. My instruction. Mine. Somewhere between the ridge and the springs I had quietly decided that if this went wrong, it would go wrong as my mistake and not as my obedience.

Reyes saw me first. Of course he did. Storm chasers see everything; they spend their lives reading skies that are trying to kill them, so a man in a company jacket coming down a hill doesn't exactly tax the instrument.

"You're early," he said. "Vultures usually wait for the dying part."

"I'm not here as a vulture." I took the file out of my bag. Halcyon Terra blue, stamped INTERNAL — DO NOT DUPLICATE, fat as a Bible and about as merciful. "I'm here as an accountant."

"That's worse," said the cartographer, Elian, without looking up from the papers spread across a flat rock. He had the trust documents arranged in a fan, every page annotated in Aurelio Reyes's cramped hand. He'd been cross-referencing since before dawn. He looked up then, and his eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely steady. "Accountants are how companies say sorry in advance."

"Fair." I set the file on the rock between us. "Read it anyway."

Noor opened it the way you'd open something that might be wired to blow. Inside: the concession permits, the expiry schedule, the internal valuation of the Sereno basin — a number with eight zeros that made even me flinch, and I'd seen the number before — and beneath all of it, the acquisition memo. *Unregistered land. No competing claim anticipated. Trust instrument presumed void or unenforceable. Recommend quiet consolidation.*

"Presumed void," Noor read aloud. His voice did something complicated on the word *presumed*. "They didn't even check."

"They checked enough to know they might lose," I said. "That's why I was sent instead of lawyers. Lawyers leave paper trails. I leave ledgers."

"And now you're leaving your ledger with us?" Elian said. "Why?"

And there it was. The question I'd been carrying down that ridge like a stone in my boot.

Eleven years of names in that ledger. Men who owed money to men who owned money to men who owned the company that now wanted a valley where children laughed at dawn. Every name a person I'd squeezed until something came out. And last month, in a border-town bar, I'd watched a dying man trade a map for a promise, and I'd thought: *there's a mark.* I'd thought it about Aurelio Reyes, and I'd been wrong, and being wrong had turned out to be the most expensive education of my career.

"Because yesterday I got new orders," I said. "After the permits lapsed. Secure by any means. And I read them standing on that ridge, looking at those terraces, and I understood that I have been the *any means* my whole life, and I'm tired."

Nobody said anything. A rooster somewhere down the slope crowed like it had opinions about corporate malfeasance.

"The trust is valid," I went on. "Your grandfather registered it through a notary in Puerto Almendro fourteen years ago. Payable to the bearer of his promise. I pulled the registry confirmation myself, three days ago, when I still planned to use it to break you." I tapped the memo. "Halcyon knows it too, now. Which means they'll stop sending accountants."

"So we send the documents out," Elian said. "Today. Before—"

"Before what?" Noor asked.

"Before the next Voss," Elian and I said, almost together. We looked at each other with mutual distaste, which I find is often the beginning of working relationships.

"I have a satellite uplink unit in my vehicle," I said. "Company issue. Encrypted channel, direct to the land registry and, if we're feeling generous, to two newspapers whose editors owe me nothing, which makes them the only honest men I know."

"You're going to burn your own exit," Noor said slowly. "You understand that. Once this goes out, you're not a contractor anymore. You're a witness against them."

"I've been a witness my whole life," I said. "I just finally picked a side worth witnessing for."

* * *

**Noor**

Here's what nobody tells you about the moment your debt dies: it doesn't feel like freedom. It feels like taking off a backpack you've carried so long your shoulders have grown around it. You keep bracing for the weight.

Voss's satellite unit sat on the flat rock like a black beetle, antenna up, blinking its patient green blink. Elian fed the trust pages into the scanner one at a time, and each page went out over the uplink with a soft chime, and each chime sounded to me like a door closing on a room I'd lived in for years.

The debt ledger itself lay beside the fire. Voss's own — the real one, paper, the kind collectors keep because digital records can be subpoenaed but ash cannot be interviewed. Names, amounts, dates. Eleven years of other people's worst months, in a neat accountant's hand.

"You're sure?" I asked him. We were alone for a moment; Elian had gone down to tell the valley families what was happening, because it was their land before it was anyone's inheritance and it would be after too.

Voss crouched by the fire. For a man built out of arithmetic, he looked strangely peaceful.

"Do you know what I did with my first collection?" he said. "Man in Cárdenas, owed four thousand. Couldn't pay. Had a daughter in school. I took his truck. He walked her to school after that, both directions, eleven kilometers each way." He turned the ledger over in his hands. "I wrote it down as *recovered*. Four thousand, recovered. You learn a language in this work where everything true gets translated into something false, and eventually you can't remember which language you dreamed in."

The fire crackled. Down the slope, Elian stood in the middle of the village path with the valley's elders gathered around him, showing them the scanned pages on a tablet, and I watched an old woman touch the screen where her grandmother's terrace appeared in a fourteen-year-old photograph, and I had to look away because some things aren't mine to watch.

"Aurelio offered me a way out once," I said. "Ninety days ago. Carry the map, clear my debts. I thought he was buying my labor." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "He was buying my *name*. Making me family whether I signed on or not."

"He was buying a promise," Voss said. "Cheapest instrument in finance. Most durable." He held the ledger over the flames, hesitated — one last twitch of the collector's reflex — and let go.

It caught fast, the way old paper does, greedy with it. Names curling into black. Amounts going up as sparks. I watched a column of figures lift off the page and die in the morning air, and I felt my shoulders come down half an inch, and then another half inch, like a structure deciding it was allowed to stand differently now.

"It's not gone, you know," Voss said, watching it burn. "The debts. They exist in systems. Copies. Halcyon will sell your paper to someone else out of spite."

"Let them." I crouched beside him, warming my hands over a burning ledger, which has to be the strangest heat source of my life. "A debt only holds you if you agree it defines you. Aurelio taught me that. Took him dying to do it, but he taught me."

"Sentimental," Voss said. But he said it the way you'd say *thank you* if thank you embarrassed you.

Elian came back up the path at a jog, face lit like a man carrying news too big to hold alone. "Registry confirmed receipt," he said. "Preliminary validation. Full review within seventy-two hours. And—" he grinned, actually grinned, and I remembered the first day, the border town, the boy who'd looked at me like I was a disease his grandfather had caught, "—the *Meridian Post* wants the story. Both newspapers do. An editor called the uplink operator back within ten minutes."

"Halcyon will know within twenty," Voss said, standing, brushing ash from his knees. "Their monitoring isn't sentimental either."

"Then we'd better be done being quiet," Elian said.

Down at the headwaters crossing, I could see the enforcers. Six men, rifles slung, watching the smoke of our fire and the smoke of Voss's ledger mingle and go up together. Voss raised his hand — some signal, some old grammar of command — and one by one, they shouldered their weapons and stepped back from the water. Not leaving. Just... standing down. Standing *aside*.

One of them, a young one with a sunburned neck, looked at the valley below him for a long moment. Then he sat down on the bank, took off his boots, and put his feet in the Río Sereno like a man remembering what rivers were for before they were assets.

"There it is," Voss said quietly, watching him. "That's how it starts. Not with defection. With somebody taking their boots off."

The uplink chimed its final chime. All pages transmitted. Registry acknowledgment stamped in green.

Elian folded the original trust documents back into their oilcloth, careful as a surgeon, and looked at me across the dead fire, across the ashes of eleven years of somebody's cruel bookkeeping.

"So," he said. "Seventy-two hours until it's official. Six enforcers with nothing left to enforce. One ex-collector who owes us an explanation of every trick Halcyon might still try." He patted the oilcloth roll against his chest. "And a valley that's been waiting fourteen years for someone to read the fine print."

"And us," I said.

Elian looked at me, and the morning light came through the smoke, and he smiled the way the river moved — like it had somewhere to be and all the time to get there.

"Yeah," he said. "Especially us. Now come on. There's a spring upstream I haven't mapped yet, and I intend to be the first Reyes to put *that* on paper honestly."

We started walking. Behind us the ledger smoked itself out, and ahead of us the water ran downhill toward everything we'd escaped, and we climbed, the two of us and our unlikely accountant, toward the beginning of everything.
