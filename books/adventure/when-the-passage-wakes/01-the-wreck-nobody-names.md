## 1. The Wreck Nobody Names

The water off Grayling Point was the color of old pewter that morning, and Coral Zhou loved it the way a man loves a thing that has never once loved him back.

He stood at the rail of the *Marrow*, his forty-foot salvage scow, and watched the swell roll in from the open sound — long, grey, patient swells that carried no warmth from anywhere. The navy's buoys sat a quarter mile out, painted a yellow so aggressive it looked like an insult: **RESTRICTED WATERS — NO ENTRY — BY ORDER OF THE ADMIRALTY**. The letters had been repainted twice since Coral had started working the edge of the cordon. Somebody in the navy cared very much that the letters stayed bright.

Nobody, apparently, cared that the letters were a lie by omission. There was no name for what lay beneath them. On every chart, the wreck was a blank smudge with a survey number. On the navy's incident rolls — Coral had paid a clerk in Port Amity twenty dollars for an afternoon with the public ledgers — it was a gap. A date, a latitude, a longitude, and nothing else. No ship name. No tonnage. No cause. Forty-one years of bureaucracy, and the sea had been given a hole where a story should have been.

The locals called it the Quiet Lady. Coral called it work.

"Bottom's at thirty-one fathoms," Juno said behind him. She was crouched over the winch controls, coffee steaming in a tin mug wedged against her knee, her grey-streaked braid coiled on top of her head like a rope waiting to be thrown. "Visibility'll be garbage after the rain yesterday. You'll be diving by feel."

"I always dive by feel."

"You dive by stubbornness and call it feel." She blew on her coffee. "Tide turns at eleven. That gives you two hours of slack water, and the current on the seaward side of the hull runs like a river even at slack, so don't get clever."

"I never get clever."

"You are the cleverest man I know, which is the whole problem." She set the mug down and looked at him with that flat, unfooled expression she'd worn since the day she'd signed onto the *Marrow* six years ago, a laid-off cannery welder with a diver's ticket and nowhere else to go. "Plate steel today. The section we marked on the port side, aft of the break. You cut, I haul. Nothing else. We are not archaeologists, Coral. We are scavengers with a boat."

"We are," Coral agreed, "the most respectable kind of thieves."

"The only kind the sea respects." She spat over the rail, an old cannery habit, a blessing to the water. "Get in before I change my mind about the weather."

* * *

The descent was a slow drowning in grey.

Coral went down the shot line hand over hand, letting the weight belt and the water pressure do their quiet work on his chest, watching the light die by degrees — pewter to slate to a bruised, grainy dark where his lamp made a small trembling room around him. At fifteen fathoms the cold found the seam of his suit and pressed a single cold finger against his spine, the way it always did, the way he'd stopped minding years ago. At twenty-five fathoms the wreck rose up out of the dark like the shoulder of a sleeping animal.

She was a big one. He'd known that from the first survey run, from the way the sonar shadow lay across the bottom — a hull better than three hundred feet, broken amidships where whatever had killed her had done its work, both sections settled into the silt with their backs to the sky. Forty-one years of soft grey sediment had dressed her in velvet. Anemones hung from her rails like pale lanterns. A school of rockfish drifted through the torn superstructure, unhurried, unafraid, fish that had never known a hook.

And everywhere, everywhere, the navy's fingerprints. The cordon buoys above were only the polite part. Down here the hull was studded with demolition charges' old scarring — Coral had mapped them across four seasons of dives, a constellation of blast points placed with a surgeon's economy, all along the keel line and the engine spaces. Ships that sink don't sink neatly. Ships that are *sunk* do. Somebody had wanted this wreck not merely dead but unreadable, folded shut like a letter no one was ever meant to open.

He'd told himself a hundred times that it wasn't his business. The sea keeps secrets; that was the first thing the sea taught you, before it taught you anything else. His business was what the sea allowed to slip — copper pipe, brass fittings, the occasional porthole with the glass miraculously whole, sold quietly through a broker in Port Amity who never asked and never remembered. A living. A thin one. A living scraped off the margins of a grave, and he knew that too, and he did it anyway, because the alternative was a cannery floor or a debt collector's patience, and Coral Zhou had tried both and found the water kinder.

He worked the port side aft, fins stirring up little ghosts of silt, and found the section Juno had marked on the last run — a stretch of hull plating exposed where the sediment had slumped away, the steel beneath still dark and clean as a fresh scar. He set his cutting torch's tip against it and began.

There was a rhythm to cutting plate underwater, and Coral had it the way some men had a card sharp's shuffle or a carpenter's eye. Spark and hiss, the orange blossom of the exothermic flame swallowed almost at once by the grey, the plate giving up its grip in slow, reluctant pieces. He counted his breaths without meaning to — in through the regulator, out, in, out — a habit so deep it had become a kind of prayer. Count breaths. Trust the line. The water is not your enemy; it is not anything; it is only water.

He was three plates into the section when he saw the stamp.

He'd cut a plate free and was walking it — drifting it, rather, the plate weighing nothing down here and everything at once — toward the lift bag, when his lamp swung across its inner face and stopped.

There was a serial number stamped into the steel.

That alone wasn't strange. Every plate on every hull ever built carried its stamp, the yard's mark, the heat number, the lot. Coral had read hundreds of them, had learned to date a wreck by the typography of its stamps the way Juno could date a weld. But this one was wrong in a way it took his mind a long moment to articulate, the way a wrong note in a familiar song takes a moment to become wrong.

The prefix was a naval construction code. Fine. The yard number was from the Ferndale yards. Fine. But after those, where every plate he'd ever seen carried a standard hull designation — a class letter, a pennant series, the ordinary honest alphabet of ships — this plate carried a code he had never seen, in a format he had never seen, six characters long, the last two of which were not letters at all but a symbol he didn't recognize and a stamped zero with a slash through it. And beneath the code, small and deep and deliberate, a second line:

**SPEC 7-K. DO NOT DUPLICATE.**

Coral hung in the grey water with the plate in his hands and felt the cold finger on his spine become a whole cold hand.

He knew naval steel. He had spent six years reading this wreck's bones, and before that three years working legitimate salvage on legitimate wrecks, and he was telling himself *you misread it, the silt blurred it, your lamp flickered* even as his lamp burned steady and the stamp sat in the steel as crisp as the day the yardman's die had struck it. Spec 7-K. Do not duplicate. That wasn't a ship's plate. That was a plate from something that had never officially existed — or from something that had existed once, and been made to stop existing, thoroughly, on paper and in the water both.

The navy hadn't just sunk her. The navy had sunk a name.

Coral looked up from the plate, along the wreck's long broken back, into the dark where the bow section lay. The rockfish drifted. An anemone opened and closed its pale fingers in the current. Somewhere far above, muffled by thirty fathoms of grey, the *Marrow*'s engine turned over, Juno keeping station, keeping time, keeping him tethered to the world of people who said what things were called.

He thought: *the water is keeping something from me.*

He had felt it before, in a small way, all the years he'd worked her — the sense of a room with the furniture covered in sheets, of a house where someone had gone through ahead of him and taken every photograph off the walls. Wrecks told stories. That was the pleasure of them, the secret pleasure, the thing he'd never said aloud to Juno because it sounded too much like poetry for a man who sold portholes by the pound: every wreck was a last sentence, and a good salvager learned to read backward from the ending. But this wreck had been censored. Her last sentence had been redacted by men with demolition charges and a paintbrush for the warning buoys, and Coral had been living off her margins for six years without once asking what the margins belonged to.

Until now. Until a stamped plate sat in his gloved hands like a word torn out of a letter.

He should put it down. He knew that with the flat, cold certainty of a man who had survived this long precisely by knowing what to put down. Restricted waters. Restricted *wreck*. A serial number that shouldn't exist was not a porthole; it was not brass; it was not the kind of thing a broker in Port Amity bought without asking questions, and the men who repainted buoys twice a season were not the kind of men who forgave salvage.

He put it in his mesh bag anyway.

He told himself it was for the copper content. The plate was steel; there was no copper in it worth the air he'd spent. He told himself he'd throw it back on the next dive. He told himself, finally, the truth, which was that Coral Zhou had spent forty-one years — his whole life, all of it spent in sight of this same grey water — being competent at a small thing in a small life, and that some deep and restless part of him had been waiting, without admitting it, for the water to hand him something too big to hold.

The tide was turning. He felt it before his gauge confirmed it — a subtle lean in the water, a long slow breath the sea drew in along the wreck's seaward side. Time to go up. He clipped the lift bag, sent the plate rising into the grey, and followed it up the line, hand over hand, counting his breaths, the wreck falling away beneath him into its forty-one years of silence.

At the surface, the world was hard and bright and ordinary. Juno hauled him over the rail with a grip like a dock winch, and the *Marrow* rolled under him, and the sky was the color of unwashed tin.

"Well?" she said. "Plate steel?"

"Plate steel."

"Any good?"

Coral looked at the mesh bag. Through the weave, the stamp was a dark smudge, a secret folded into itself.

"Odd lot," he said. "We'll see."

Juno's eyes narrowed — she could read him the way he read wrecks, backward from the ending — but the tide was turning and the swells were building out of the southwest, and she let it go, the way she let most things go until they were worth not letting go. He stowed the bag in the hold under a tarp, under a coil of chain, under the ordinary weight of an ordinary day, and stood at the rail while Juno brought the *Marrow* about for the run back to Grayling Point.

The navy buoys slid past, yellow and bright and lying. Beyond them the grey water lay over the Quiet Lady like a sheet over furniture in a house where nobody lived and everybody visited.

* * *

They ran in ahead of the weather. By the time the *Marrow* was tied off in Coral's boathouse — a lean, salt-bleached shed on pilings at the edge of the Grayling mudflats, half workshop, half home, smelling always of diesel and tar and old rope — the first spit of rain was ticking against the windows, and the light was going out of the day like a lamp turned down.

Coral waited until Juno had gone up the hill to her room above the chandlery, then unlocked the hold and took the plate out.

In the lamplight it looked worse. Or better. He turned it under the light and read the code again, and the second line again, and then did the thing he had been not-doing all the way in from the cordon: he took down the battered naval registry he'd bought from a Port Amity bookseller, and the salvage yearbooks, and the scrap of ledger paper with the clerk's neat copy of the incident roll, and he laid them all out on his workbench like a surgeon laying out instruments.

The Ferndale yard number belonged, according to the registry, to a hull laid down and cancelled — *cancelled*, the word written in the registry's flat bureaucratic ink — eleven years before the Quiet Lady had gone into the water. A yard number for a ship that was never built, stamped on a plate that was, on a ship that had no name, sunk by charges placed like a surgeon's sutures, in waters the navy repainted warnings on twice a season.

Coral sat back on his stool. The rain ticked. Somewhere out on the mudflats a heron cried once, harsh and lonely, and was quiet.

He thought about throwing the plate into the channel. He got as far as carrying it to the door. The tide was out; the mud lay bare and silver under the dying light, ribbed like the palm of an enormous hand; and the channel water beyond it moved slow and grey and patient, the way it always did, keeping what it kept.

He carried the plate back inside instead, and wrapped it in canvas, and put it in the bottom of his tool chest, under the wrenches, which was where a man put things he was not ready to have decided about.

That was how he left it, that night, when the rain came down in earnest and the boathouse creaked on its pilings like an old ship dreaming. He banked the stove, hung his suit to drip, and was halfway up the loft ladder to his bunk when he heard the sound.

At first he took it for the water — the flats at low tide made a hundred small noises, gurgle and slap and the sucking of mud — but this was different. This was rhythmic. A knocking. Faint, irregular, from the seaward side of the boathouse, where the ladder came down to the mud and the tide was out and no boat could reach.

Coral stood in the dark with his hand on the loft rail and counted his breaths, the old habit, the old prayer. In. Out. In. Out. The knocking came again, weaker now, and with it — carried thin through the rain — a voice.

"Hello the boathouse. Hello. In the name of God, somebody."

Coral went down the ladder two rungs at a time.
