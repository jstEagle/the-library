## 3. What the Navy Won't Discuss

The grey water gave up its dead slowly, and Coral Zhou had learned to work in that rhythm — patient, unhurried, letting the sea decide what it would surrender. He was forty feet down on the wreck's starboard side, working a corroded fitting loose with a wrench he'd wrapped in cloth to keep it from ringing against the hull, when he heard the engines.

Not fishing boats. Fishing boats came in shallow and hesitant, their props stuttering as they felt for the sandbars. These engines came deep and steady, two of them, running the channel like they owned it. Which, Coral supposed, they did.

He hung in the cold green light and watched the shadows slide across the surface above him — long, low hulls, navy-grey even seen from below, moving toward his anchorage with the unhurried certainty of something that had never once been told no.

The fitting came free in his hand. He let the wrench drift, caught the line, and began the slow ascent, counting his breaths the way he always did, because breaths were the only currency down here that couldn't be counterfeited.

* * *

They were waiting on his dock when he broke the surface.

Two men in civilian clothes that fooled nobody — the cut too sharp, the boots too polished, the way they stood with their weight balanced like men who'd spent years on decks that moved under gunfire. Behind them, at the mouth of the cove, a patrol boat sat on its anchor with the casual menace of a held breath.

Coral climbed the ladder, stripped his mask, and said nothing. He'd learned young that silence cost nothing and bought time.

"You're Zhou." The taller one had a face like a folded map, all creases and no country marked on any of them. "Corvette Investigator Dane. This is Investigator Sorel."

"Salvage license says I can work this wreck," Coral said. "That's all the talking I do."

"We're not here about the wreck." Dane's eyes went past him, cataloguing the boathouse, the rusted crane, the skiff tied alongside. "We're here about a man. Merrick Hale. Prospector. Sixty-odd years, white beard, half-mad by most reports. His mule was found grazing on the coast road three days back."

The word *Hale* landed in Coral's chest like a dropped anchor. He kept his face still. Down below, keeping your face still was survival; panic burned air you might need later. He breathed the way the water had taught him — slow, measured, betraying nothing.

"Don't know him."

"No?" Dane produced a notebook, but didn't open it. That was the trick, Coral realized. The notebook was theater. The real instrument was patience. "Fisherman at Gull Point saw a man matching his description come ashore near your cove. In the water. Alive, barely. Said someone dragged him out."

"Lot of things wash into my cove," Coral said. "Driftwood. Dead fish. Navy lies."

Sorel laughed — one short bark, quickly smothered. Dane didn't smile.

"Mr. Zhou. A man died somewhere on this coast, possibly on your property, and we are attempting to establish the circumstances. Obstruction of a naval investigation carries penalties you cannot salvage your way out of."

"He didn't die on my property." True. Hale had died in his boathouse, which was legally the same thing and practically a different universe. Coral heard himself say it and knew, even as the words left his mouth, that it sounded exactly like what it was — a man splitting hairs over a corpse.

Dane's creases deepened. Somewhere behind those folded planes, a decision was being made, or unmade.

"Where is he now?"

"I don't know."

"You said he didn't die on your property. That implies you know where he did die."

Coral looked out at the water. The patrol boat's wake had long since flattened; the cove lay smooth as poured lead. He thought about the map folded inside his shirt, grease-stained, smelling faintly of kerosene and old man. He thought about a hand gripping his wrist with the last strength it owned, and a voice like gravel sliding downhill: *Ada Voss. Harrowgate. Before the snows. Swear it.*

He had sworn. He'd sworn because a dying man doesn't lie about what matters, and because some part of Coral — the part that had spent ten years pulling other people's secrets off the seafloor and calling it a living — was tired of being paid to not know things.

"I found a man in the surf," he said. "I pulled him out. He was hurt bad. I put him in my boat to run him up to the mission station, and he died before we cleared the point. I buried him where the ground was soft. That's the whole of it."

It was mostly true. It was also, in every way that mattered, a lie — because he'd buried Hale with the map still warm from his own shirt pocket, figuratively speaking, and because the ground he'd chosen was soft precisely so he could find it again.

Dane wrote something. Sorel watched Coral's hands, which Coral noticed, and stilled, and hated himself for noticing.

"Burying bodies without reporting them," Dane said mildly, "is a crime."

"So is sinking ships full of sailors and pretending the sea did it," Coral said, before he could stop himself.

The silence that followed had weight. It pressed on his eardrums like depth. Dane closed the notebook without writing whatever he'd pretended to write.

"The wreck you dive," Dane said quietly, "doesn't exist. You understand? Not officially. Your license permits salvage of an *unidentified* derelict. There is no serial number on any plate of that vessel, because there is no vessel. If you have recovered anything bearing markings — anything at all — the correct procedure is to surrender it to the naval station at Port Ceren and forget its existence. Am I understood?"

"Understood," Coral said, and meant none of it, and knew his voice gave away exactly how much he meant none of it, because Dane held his gaze a moment longer than comfort allowed before turning to go.

At the head of the dock he paused. "The man Hale. Whatever he gave you — and men like Hale always give somebody something — it isn't worth what it will cost. The sea keeps its secrets, Mr. Zhou. We prefer to help it."

They rowed back to the patrol boat. Coral stood on his dock until the grey hull shrank to a sliver, then to a suggestion, then to nothing, and the cove was just a cove again, and his hands were shaking so badly he had to grip the rail.

* * *

Juno found him packing.

She leaned in the boathouse doorway with her arms crossed, watching him fold a spare regulator into an oilcloth roll with hands that almost didn't tremble anymore. She was small and wiry and dark as a knot in pine, and she had been his dive partner for six years, which meant she could read him underwater at thirty feet and read him twice as easily standing in a doorway.

"Going somewhere?"

"Fishing trip."

"You hate fishing." She came in, stepped over the coiled mooring line, and picked up the oilcloth roll. Turned it over. Set it down. "You're taking the good knife. You only take the good knife when you think someone's going to make you use it."

"Juno."

"Navy boats don't scare fish, Coral. They scare divers." She sat down on the crate across from him, elbows on knees, in the exact posture she used when she'd decided a conversation would continue whether he participated or not. "Two investigators and a patrol boat, and you stand on the dock looking like a man who's swallowed the hook along with the bait. So either tell me, or I start guessing, and my guesses are worse than the truth."

He kept folding. Shirt. Rope. The little brass compass that had belonged to his father and pointed, as far as Coral could ever determine, wherever it pleased.

"They asked about Hale," he said finally.

"And?"

"And I lied."

"How badly?"

"Badly."

Juno nodded slowly, as if this confirmed something she'd suspected for years. "So they'll be back. And next time there'll be more of them, and they won't ask politely, and they'll search the boathouse, and they'll find—" Her eyes flicked to his chest, to the shape beneath the shirt that a stranger wouldn't see and a partner couldn't miss. "There it is."

"It's a map."

"To what?"

"I don't know yet. Mountains. Kestrel Range. Something the navy sank that ship to bury, and Hale bled out on my floor making me promise to carry it to a woman named Ada Voss before the snows close the passes."

Juno was quiet for a long moment. Outside, the tide was turning; the water in the cove sighed against the pilings, that long exhalation the sea made when it changed its mind.

"You swore," she said. Not a question.

"On his death. On his hand around my wrist. Which, for the record, was freezing and smelled like kerosene."

"And now you're leaving tonight, alone, into mountains you've never seen, carrying the one object two different sets of killers want badly enough to send a patrol boat to a salvage diver's dock." She ticked the points off on her fingers like items on a salvage manifest. "And your plan is what, exactly? To outswim them?"

"My plan is to walk fast."

"That's not a plan. That's a eulogy with extra steps." She stood. Went to the wall where the gear hung, and took down her own pack — the one she hadn't touched since the season ended, still smelling of neoprene and lamp oil. She began filling it with the economy of a woman who had packed for emergencies her whole life and expected this one like a debt coming due.

"What are you doing?"

"Packing."

"Juno—"

"You're going to say it's dangerous." She didn't turn around. "You're going to say it's not my fight, and I should stay here and keep diving the wreck and keep my nose clean until the navy gets bored. And I'm going to say that the navy doesn't get bored, Coral, it gets thorough, and the first place they'll be thorough is the partner of the man who lied to them. They'll lean on me to get to you. Everyone knows I'm the soft spot. So either I come with you, or I stay here and become the hostage that makes you come back, and we both know which of those ends with both of us in a cell at Port Ceren."

He opened his mouth. Closed it. She was right, and they both knew she was right, and the knowledge sat between them like a third person.

"Why?" he asked instead. "Why come? This isn't your promise. You never met him. You owe a dead prospector nothing."

Jono stopped packing. She turned, and in the lamplight her face was unreadable in the way the deep water was unreadable — calm on top, everything important underneath.

"Six years ago I washed up on this coast with nothing," she said. "No license, no name worth using, no family that admitted owning me. And you — who owed me less than nothing — taught me the water. Gave me half your catches. Never once asked what I was running from." She shrugged, and the shrug carried more weight than most speeches. "Somebody chose me once, Coral. When it would've been easier not to. So now somebody's chosen to carry a dead man's promise into the mountains, and I'm choosing back. That's the whole arithmetic. Don't make it complicated."

Coral looked at her — at this wiry, stubborn, impossible woman who had just dismantled every objection he owned with the efficiency of a cutting torch — and felt something shift in his chest that wasn't fear. Fear was still there, braided through everything now, a cold strand he suspected would never fully work itself loose. But underneath it, threaded tight, was something warmer and far more dangerous.

Loyalty. His own, returned.

"The passes close early this year," he said. "Everyone says so."

"Then we'd better walk fast." She slung her pack. "Boat or road?"

"Road. If they're watching the water, the water's a trap."

"Then we walk tonight, before the moon's up." She paused at the boathouse door and looked back at him, and for the first time since the investigators left, she smiled — quick and crooked and real. "Fair warning, though. I've never seen a mountain. If I die of altitude, I'm haunting you specifically."

"Noted," Coral said, and killed the lamp, and together they slipped out into the dark, two shapes moving north along the coast road with a dead man's map between them and the first snows already gathering somewhere high and white and waiting.

Behind them, far out in the black water, the wreck they were forbidden to name settled another inch into the silt, keeping its secrets — for now.
