## 4. Harrowgate Road

The road to Harrowgate was the color of old rope, stitched through the foothills in long, sagging loops that seemed to have been laid down by someone who hated both travelers and horses. Juno Reik had walked worse roads. She had walked roads that weren't roads at all, just the memory of one, where the gravel had washed out and left a spine of shale you had to pick your way across like a heron in shallow water. But she couldn't remember a road that had made her this tired, and she suspected the tiredness wasn't in her legs.

It was in the way Coral kept touching the inside pocket of his coat.

He'd been doing it since the navy men left the boathouse. Every hour or so his hand would drift up, casual as a man scratching his chest, and press flat against the oilcloth-wrapped bundle that rode over his heart. Checking. The way a diver checks his air gauge without thinking about it — not because he doubts it, but because the habit is the only thing between him and the dark.

"You're going to wear a hole in the oilcloth," Juno said.

"I'm going to wear a hole in my ribs," Coral said. "The oilcloth will be fine."

They walked on. Behind them, the sea had folded itself away into the haze, and ahead the land climbed in slow green wrinkles toward a line of grey peaks that looked, from this distance, like the teeth of something asleep. The air had changed overnight. It was thinner up here, drier, with a bite to it that hadn't been there on the coast, and Juno found herself breathing deeper without meaning to, as if her body knew something her head didn't.

Merrick Hale had been dead six days. Juno had helped Coral sew him into his tarp and sink him off the point, in water deep enough that no one would drag him up, and she had said the few words she knew, and Coral had said none at all, just stood at the rail with his hands opening and closing like a man learning to let go of something. Then they had packed in the dark, before the navy could come back with better questions, and now here they were: two salvage divers on a road built for mule trains, carrying a dead man's map to a woman neither of them had ever met, in a town that existed mostly as a smudge on freight manifests.

Juno had agreed to come for reasons she hadn't fully examined. The navy had looked at Coral like he was a stain on a deck plate. They had asked their questions in that flat, patient voice that meant the questions would keep coming until they got the answers they'd already decided were true. And Coral — Coral, who could hold his breath four minutes and read a tide table like scripture and never, ever lost his temper — Coral had lied so badly that Juno had to step on his foot under the table to make him stop.

Someone had chosen him. That was the thing. Merrick Hale, drowning, half his blood in the bilge water, had looked at a stranger in a boathouse and chosen him. Juno had watched it happen, and something in her had said: *if a dying man can see it, you can see it too.* So she'd packed her gear bag and her knife and the good rope, and she'd come.

"You're doing it again," Coral said.

"Doing what?"

"Thinking at me. I can feel it. Like standing next to a stove."

"Then walk faster and get out of range."

He almost smiled. It was a small thing, barely a shift in the weather of his face, but Juno counted it. Six days since Hale died, and it was the first one.

* * *

Harrowgate announced itself by smell before it announced itself by sight.

The wind came down off the shoulder of the mountain carrying woodsmoke and ore dust and the sour-sweet reek of the stamp mill, and underneath it all something warm — bread, or grease, or both. Then the road bent around a granite shoulder and there it was: a scatter of buildings wedged into a river valley like stones in a creek bed, built wherever the ground was flat enough and nowhere it wasn't. A headframe leaned over an adit in the hillside like a drunk man against a wall. Tailings ran down to the water in pale fans. The whole town had the look of a place that had been built in a hurry by people who weren't sure they'd be allowed to stay, and then stayed anyway, out of spite.

The stamp mill was silent. That was the first wrong thing Juno noticed. A town like this lived and died by the rhythm of its mill, and this one stood with its cams frozen mid-fall, its timbers grey, weeds waist-high in the ore yard. Half the cabins had that same abandoned look. But there was smoke from a good number of chimneys, and there were people — moving slow, watching the road the way small animals watch a treeline.

"Half a town," Coral said.

"Half a town that doesn't want to be seen," Juno said.

They came down the main street, which was really just the road widened and churned to mud, past a feed store with its windows boarded, past a assay office with a new lock on an old door, past three men sitting on a porch who tracked them with their eyes and said nothing. Juno felt the weight of the watching. In a town that serves everyone the law forgot, strangers aren't a curiosity. They're a question. And questions in a place like this get answered carefully or not at all.

The roadhouse stood at the far end, where the street gave up and became trail again. It was a long, low building of timber and river stone, two stories, with a deep porch and a sign that had once said something Juno couldn't read and now said nothing at all, just a board of weathered ghosts. But the windows were clean. That was the detail that caught her. In a town of boarded glass and shuttered doors, the roadhouse windows had been washed, and behind them lamps were already lit against the early dusk, and the smell of bread came off the place like a welcome that hadn't quite decided whether it meant it.

"You're staring," Coral said.

"You're staring harder."

"I'm deciding if we knock or just walk in."

"We knock. We're not animals."

They were still deciding when the door opened on its own, and a woman stood in the gap with a lantern in one hand and a shotgun in the other, and she looked at them the way a jeweler looks at stones she's been asked to appraise in the dark.

* * *

Ada Voss was not what Juno had expected, though Juno couldn't have said what she had expected. Not a frontier widow with a face like a fence post. Ada was maybe fifty, broad through the shoulders in the way of someone who has carried real weight for real years, with grey shot through her black hair and hands that were clean but not soft. She wore an apron over a man's shirt and a man's coat, and the shotgun came down to rest against her hip without ever quite pointing at the floor, and her eyes went from Coral to Juno to the road behind them and back again, reading the empty street the way Coral read water.

"You're not from here," she said.

"No," Coral said.

"You're not from the valley at all. Coast people." She said it like a diagnosis. "Salt in your collars. You walk like the ground owes you money." Her gaze settled on Coral. "You're the diver."

Juno felt Coral go still beside her — that particular stillness, the one he got in the water when something moved at the edge of the light. "I don't think we've met."

"No. But a man came through here three weeks ago with a limp and a story, and he said if a diver ever came looking for me, I should feed him and hear him out before I shot him." She stepped back and opened the door wide. "He was specific about the order. In. Boots off. There's stew."

The common room was warm and low-ceilinged and smelled of woodsmoke and onions and tallow, and it was nearly empty — a card game in one corner that stopped when they entered and resumed a half-beat too late, an old man asleep by the stove with a dog across his feet, a girl of maybe twelve hauling a stack of plates who looked at them with frank, unafraid curiosity. Ada took their coats without asking, hung them on pegs by the fire, and set down two bowls of stew that were better than anything either of them had eaten in a year. Juno ate like it was a job. Coral ate like a man remembering how.

It was only when the bowls were empty and Ada had refilled them without being asked — a second helping, unearned, which in Juno's experience was either charity or a trap — that Ada pulled a chair around backward and sat, arms folded on its back, and said:

"Now. The name of the man who sent you."

"Merrick Hale," Coral said. "Prospector. He washed into my boathouse half-drowned. He'd been shot."

Something crossed Ada's face — not surprise, exactly. Confirmation. The look of a woman who has been braced for bad news for years and receives it the way a wall receives weather.

"Dead?"

"Six days. We put him in the water off the point. It's what he asked."

Ada was quiet a moment. The fire ticked. The dog by the stove sighed in its sleep. Then she said, "Hale was a fool and a liar and the best surveyor I ever met, and he owed my father a debt he spent thirty years trying to pay. What did he give you?"

Juno watched Coral make the decision. She watched it happen — the small pause, the breath drawn through the nose, the way his hand went to the inside of his coat and this time did not come away empty. He unwrapped the oilcloth on the table between them, and the map came out into the lamplight, grease-stained and water-swollen, covered in Hale's cramped, confident hand.

"Deliver this to Ada Voss in Harrowgate before the first snows seal the passes," Coral said. "His words. He didn't finish the rest."

Ada looked at the map.

Juno had spent her life around charts — tide tables, salvage surveys, the navy's own hydrographic sheets, which she'd once spent a profitable week copying out of a locked drawer. She knew what a map was supposed to look like, and she knew what Hale's was: hand-drawn, obsessive, dense with elevation figures and bearings and little annotations in a shorthand she couldn't read. She expected the woman to study it the way anyone would, tracing lines, working out the route.

Instead, Ada Voss went white.

Not pale. Not startled. White — the white of paper, of snow, of a face from which the blood has been recalled for some emergency deeper than embarrassment. Her hand went to the edge of the table and held on. The girl with the plates stopped in the middle of the room and stared. In the corner, the card game had gone quiet again, and this time it stayed quiet.

"Ma'am," Juno started, "should we—"

"Eleven days," Ada said.

Her voice came out flat and strange, like a voice heard through water.

"You're eleven days late already."

* * *

She took them through the kitchen, past a stockpot the size of a lifeboat, into a back room that was part office and part fortress: a desk buried in ledgers, a rifle over the door, a window shuttered with an iron bar. She lit a second lamp, and the map went onto the desk under the light, and for a long time she didn't touch it at all. She just looked, the way you look at a grave.

"My father," she said, "was an engineer. Third assistant, marine systems, out of the Corvane yard. He worked on a ship I am not going to name in this room, because this room has ears, and the last man who named it in a room with ears was my father." She touched one of Hale's annotations with one finger, not quite resting on it. "The navy tells everyone that ship went down in a storm. Forty men. They gave the families medals and closed the file, and when my father started asking questions — he'd seen the loading manifests, you understand, he'd signed for cargo that had no business being on a passenger run — they retired him. Early. Generously. With a letter reminding him of his oath."

"And he didn't stop asking," Coral said.

"He stopped asking out loud. He started writing. And he started keeping things." Ada looked up, and in the lamplight her eyes were dry and very bright, the way the sea looks just before weather. "Hale was his friend. Hale helped him move things into the high country, year by year, a box at a time, up past the treeline where nobody looks because there's nothing there worth looking at. When my father died — heart, they said, and maybe it was — Hale kept the route. He was supposed to bring this map to me in the spring. Instead he ran into the wrong people in the wrong valley, and they shot him, and he gave it to a stranger on the coast."

She straightened. The whiteness had gone out of her face now, replaced by something harder and more useful, and Juno recognized it, because she'd seen it on Coral's face a hundred times at the rail of a boat at first light: the look of someone who has stopped grieving and started working.

"Eleven days," Coral said. "Late for what?"

"Late for the window. The Kestrel crossing closes with the first real snow, and the first real snow was forecast for the twentieth. It's the ninth." Ada began rolling the map, careful as a surgeon. "You came up the low road. You can go back down it. The navy will find you inside a week — they'll have your names off the ferry manifests and your faces off the Harrowgate road, because there are men in this town who sell what they see, and one of them saw you come in tonight."

"And if we don't go back down?"

"Then you go up." She set the rolled map on the desk and put her hand flat on top of it, claiming it. "And you go with me, because neither of you has ever been above the treeline in your life, and the mountain does not care that you can hold your breath."

Coral opened his mouth, and Juno saw the objection forming — she knew him well enough by now to read the shape of his refusals before he made them — but Ada talked over him the way water talks over stone.

"I spent nine years waiting for that map to walk through my door," she said. "I am not sending it up the Kestrel with two divers and a prayer. Get some sleep. We leave at first light, and before we leave, you're going to tell me everything Hale said, every word, in order, and you're going to start with why the navy came asking about a dead prospector in a boathouse four hundred miles from anywhere."

She picked up the lamp. At the door she paused, and looked back at them, and for one moment the hardness went out of her face and Juno saw what was underneath it — nine years of a woman setting an extra bowl at her table for a father who was never coming down the mountain.

"He finished it," Ada said quietly. "Whatever he was trying to say when he died. He finished it. He always did."

She closed the door, and Juno sat in the lamplight listening to the wind come off the peaks, and thought: *we were two people this morning. By first light we'll be three, and the mountain is counting.*
