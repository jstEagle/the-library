## 5. The Woman Who Kept the Ledger

Ada Voss kept the roadhouse ledger the way other women kept prayer books — nightly, in a careful hand, with the lamp turned low so the ink wouldn't glare. She had done it for eleven years, since the night her father's name stopped appearing on any manifest anywhere and started appearing only in her memory. Every soul who slept under her roof went into that book. Every debt forgiven, every meal given away to a miner too proud to beg, every stranger who paid in coin that rang wrong. She wrote it all down because writing things down was the one thing she knew how to do that the sea could not undo.

The sea had taken her father's ship. The navy had taken the truth of it. But no one — not the Admiralty, not the syndicate men who drank in her taproom and tipped too well and asked too many questions about the Kestrel Range — could take what she wrote in her own hand.

She was on the third page of the evening's entries when she heard the boy, Pell, come pounding down the stairs from the loft.

"Miss Voss," he said, breathless, "the diver's awake. And he's asking for you by name."

She set down her pen. She did not hurry. Hurrying told people what you feared, and Ada Voss had spent eleven years teaching herself never to show fear to anyone who might be watching — which, in Harrowgate, was everyone.

* * *

The common room smelled of woodsmoke and tallow and the wet wool of men who'd come in off the pass road. Coral Zhou sat at the corner table nearest the stove like a man who had been placed there rather than seated there, upright despite exhaustion, his hands wrapped around a mug of chicory he hadn't touched. His dive partner — Juno, the loud one, the one with the laugh like a dropped anchor chain — hovered at his shoulder with the coiled tension of a dog that smells weather coming.

Up close, Ada saw what the water had left in him. The salt still crusted at his hairline. A rawness around his eyes where mask-glass met skin, day after day after day. Hands scarred in the particular way of salvage divers: rope burns across the palms, a missing crescent from the left index finger, knuckles gone smooth from cold. He looked at her the way men look at a door they've been told is locked.

"You're Ada Voss," he said. Not a question.

"And you're the man Merrick Hale died in front of." She said it plainly, because plainness was mercy. Men flinched harder when you made them guess.

Coral didn't flinch. That interested her. "He asked me to bring you something."

"Then bring it."

He hesitated — one heartbeat, two — and she watched him make the calculation every man who came through her door eventually made: whether she was safe, whether the room was safe, whether anything in this broken country was safe. Then he reached inside his coat and laid the map on the table between them.

It was worse than when Hale had shown it to her father's old crewmate, years ago — or so she imagined; she had never actually seen it before tonight. Grease-stained, water-blistered along one edge, drawn in a hand that shook with either age or urgency. The Kestrel Range rendered in careful contour lines, the passes named in a cartographer's shorthand, and there — high above the treeline, in a valley that appeared on no survey map she'd ever bought or stolen — a small square marked with three letters in faded ink:

**A.V.**

Her initials. Her father's hand.

The room went very quiet, or perhaps the room stayed exactly as loud as it had been and Ada simply stepped out of it for a moment. Eleven years. Eleven years of writing names in a ledger, of keeping a roof over the heads of anyone the law forgot, of telling herself that if she just stayed useful and stayed quiet and stayed *here*, then someday something would wash up out of the grey water and ask for her by name.

She heard herself say, "You're eleven days late already."

Coral blinked. "Late?"

"Hale drew that map in spring. I know because he sent word ahead — half a sentence, carried by a mule driver who drinks too much and remembers too little. 'Tell Ada the cache holds.' That was all. I have been waiting since the thaw for someone to walk through my door carrying it." She touched the edge of the paper, lightly, the way you touch a wound to learn if it still hurts. It did. "Eleven days past the last safe crossing of Sawtooth Pass, if the weather holds kind. And the weather in the Kestrels has never once held kind."

Juno leaned over Coral's shoulder, squinting. "That mark. A.V. That's—"

"Me. Or my father. Both of us answered to those initials." Ada straightened. "His name was Elias Voss. He was an engineer. Third assistant engineer, aboard a ship whose name you will not find in any registry, because the navy struck it from every record the week after she went down."

Coral's hands went still around the mug. She watched the diver in him surface — the man who spent his life reading what the water concealed. "The wreck off Cape Merrow," he said quietly. "The cordon. The one nobody's allowed to name."

"You've dived her."

"I work her. What the tide shakes loose." He paused. "Last month I brought up a hull plate with a serial number that doesn't exist in any book I can buy, steal, or bribe my way into."

"No," Ada said. "It wouldn't."

* * *

She took them into the back parlor — the room she used for ledgers and for conversations that shouldn't be overheard — and locked the door, and poured three fingers of plum brandy into glasses that had belonged to her mother, and then she did the thing she had rehearsed in the dark for eleven years without ever once saying aloud to a living soul.

She told them.

"My father signed aboard as an engineer because the pay was triple the merchant rate and because they told him it was a trial run for a new class of engine. He believed them. He was a man who believed systems worked — that if you did your part correctly, the larger machine would function as designed. He wrote me letters from every port. Forty-one letters. I still have them." Her voice did not shake. She had practiced that too. "In the last letter he said the trials were going badly. That the weapon they were testing — he called it *the arrangement*, as though naming it plainly would get him reported — had failed twice, catastrophically, and that the crew had begun to understand what they were actually carrying. He said some of them wanted to refuse further tests. He said the officers were drawing lots for shore leave, because nobody wanted to be aboard for the third attempt."

Juno had stopped pretending to drink. Coral sat forward, elbows on knees, a man braced against a swell.

"The third attempt was scheduled for the fourteenth of Frostmonth. On the thirteenth, the ship put out from the naval yard at Corvane with her full complement — including six civilian witnesses. Engineers from the contracting firm. A journalist who had been granted access to write a piece about naval ingenuity. My father among them, because the engine was his responsibility and he would not leave it." Ada set down her glass. "On the fourteenth, the navy announced that the vessel had been lost with all hands in a navigational accident. Rocks. Weather. Tragic. They paid out pensions to the families with one hand and sealed the wreck site with the other. There was no storm on the fourteenth of Frostmonth. I have checked the harbor logs of every port within two hundred miles. The sea was flat as poured glass."

"You think they scuttled her," Coral said. "Deliberately. With witnesses aboard."

"I don't think it. I *know* it the way you know a thing you cannot yet prove." She met his eyes. "My father saw the failure coming. He was a careful man, and careful men keep copies. Before the final trial he packed his engineering logs — the real ones, the ones with the test failures recorded in his own hand — into a sealed strongbox and shipped them up-country with a trusted friend, to a cache in the high valleys of the Kestrels. A valley that isn't on the maps. A cache marked with initials only I would recognize." She tapped the grease-stained paper. "Hale was the friend's cousin. When the friend died two winters back, Hale inherited the knowledge and evidently decided the knowledge needed to move. To me. Before someone else found it, or before he died of the lung-rot that was eating him alive — which, from the sound of your account, is exactly how it went."

"He made me swear," Coral said. "Before the first snows seal the passes."

"Then we are both bound to a dead man's timetable." Ada rose and went to the window. Below, Harrowgate lay in its bowl of dark — the mine-heads, the shanties, the road winding south toward the lowlands and law and everything that had failed her family. Snow already dusted the highest ridgelines, early and wrong, the mountain clearing its throat. "There is a federal judge in Verity. Judge Ansel Crane. I have been cultivating his trust for six years — correspondence, small favors, testimony on local disputes, always honest, always documented, because I understood that someday I would need a man of law who owed me nothing but believed me completely. If that strongbox reaches Crane, the logs become evidence. Evidence becomes inquiry. Inquiry becomes—" She stopped herself. Hope was a currency she refused to spend unwitnessed. "It becomes whatever truth can survive contact with power. Perhaps nothing. But the dead deserve the attempt."

* * *

"I'm going with you."

She said it while repacking the brandy bottle, not looking at them, and she felt both men go rigid behind her the way rooms go rigid.

"No," Coral said.

"Yes."

"It's a hard crossing even in good weather. We lost— " He caught himself. "Two people barely made Sawtooth Pass last year. You're a roadhouse keeper, not a mountaineer."

"I'm the daughter of a man who surveyed half these valleys before the navy ever issued a chart of them." Now she turned. "You have a map drawn from memory by a dying prospector. I have my father's own route notes, memorized, plus eleven years of feeding and listening to every guide, hunter, and smuggler who's crossed the Kestrels out my front door. I know which passes the syndicate watches, which springs run foul in autumn, which slopes shed rock when the frost gets into them. Without me you'll take three weeks finding the cache. With me, nine days. And Mr. Zhou — the snows are already early. Nine days may be too many."

"She's right," Juno said, and got an elbow for it.

"That's not the point." Coral stood, and Ada saw the exhaustion in him finally, the cost of the water and the running and the promise sitting on him like ballast. "Those men who came asking about Hale today — they weren't navy. Navy sends uniforms. Those were hired eyes, and hired eyes report to someone with money, and whoever buried that ship has had eleven years to hire more of them. They'll follow us. Anyone with us becomes a target. I won't—"

He stopped. Recalibrated. Ada watched him do it — watched a man accustomed to weighing risk underwater try to weigh it on land, where the currents were less honest.

"You won't what?" she asked gently. "Won't let another person die for your promise? Mr. Zhou, Merrick Hale died in your boathouse holding your wrist. My father died in grey water holding a wrench. Everyone in this story is already dead or already committed. The only question left is whether the dead get their names back." She crossed to the ledger on its shelf and laid her hand flat upon it — eleven years of names, debts, debts forgiven, strangers sheltered. "I have kept accounts for everyone else in this valley since I was nineteen years old. This is the last entry outstanding. I mean to close it myself."

The stove ticked. Somewhere upstairs, Pell's footsteps crossed the loft. Outside, far off, the mountain made a sound like a door being tested.

Coral looked at her for a long moment — the look of a man taking a sounding, dropping his line all the way down to see what the bottom was made of. Whatever he found there, he nodded once, slow, like a verdict.

"Nine days," he said. "We leave before dawn, ahead of anyone watching the road. And if I say turn back, you turn back."

"If you say turn back," Ada agreed, "I will consider it."

Juno laughed — that dropped-anchor laugh — and the tension in the room broke like thin ice.

Ada blew out the lamp and opened the ledger to tomorrow's page. In her careful hand, by feel in the dark, she wrote the date, and beneath it: *Departing. Elias — hold on. I'm coming to carry your name home.*

Then she went to pack, and downstairs, faintly, she heard Pell calling up from the yard in a voice pitched wrong — the voice of a boy bringing bad news.

"Miss Voss! Miss Voss, there's men riding in — three of them — and they ain't stopping at the gate!"
