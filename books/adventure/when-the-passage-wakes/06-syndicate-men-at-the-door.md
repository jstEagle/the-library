## 6. Syndicate Men at the Door

The rain came in off the flats two days after Ada read the map, and with it came the man in the grey coat.

Coral saw him first from the roadhouse porch, where he'd gone to check the tie-downs on Juno's gear. Harrowgate sat at the seam between the mining country and the long wet valley below it, and the road that fed the town ran straight as a plumb line for three miles before it bent toward the coast. Anything coming up that road announced itself early — and this thing had been announcing itself since noon, a dark motorcar grinding through the mud with the patient confidence of something that had never once been stuck.

Nobody in Harrowgate owned a car like that. Nobody within fifty miles did.

He watched it come the last half mile without pretending otherwise. There was no point. The man behind the wheel had already seen him; you could feel it, the way you felt a shark turn in green water, some pressure shifting in the air between you. Coral set down the tie-down rope and stood on the boards with his hands loose at his sides and let the car come.

It stopped in front of the roadhouse with a hiss of steam and brakes. Two men got out. The driver was big through the shoulders, soft through the middle, wearing a city suit gone wrong at the hems from road spray. The passenger was another animal entirely — lean, silver at the temples, dressed in a coat too good for the weather and boots too good for the mud, and he stepped over the puddles like they were beneath his notice rather than around them.

"Morning," the lean one said. His voice was pleasant, unhurried, the voice of a man reading terms aloud. "Looking for the proprietor."

"You found her," Coral said. "She's inside."

"And you are?"

"A guest."

The lean one smiled. It was a good smile, well maintained, like everything else about him. "Corliss," he said, and offered a hand that Coral didn't take. Corliss let it hang there a moment, then folded it away without embarrassment, which told Coral more than the smile had. A man who could absorb a small insult without flinching was saving himself for larger ones. "I'm told there's been some traffic through here lately. Coast men. Divers."

"Harrowgate sees all kinds."

"It does." Corliss looked past him at the roadhouse — the sagging porch, the smoke-stained eaves, the sign that said VOSS'S in letters faded to ghosts. "Charming place. My employers have interests in the region. Mining interests. Survey interests." He said the words lightly, but each one landed like a weight on a scale. "They've misplaced an item. Paper. Old. Greasy, I'm told, as though that helps. They'd very much like it back, and they're prepared to be generous with whoever helps them."

"I don't know anything about paper."

"No," Corliss agreed pleasantly. "You wouldn't."

* * *

Inside, the kitchen smelled of woodsmoke and onions, and Ada was standing at the stove with her back to the room in a way that meant she'd heard every word through the window glass. Juno sat at the corner table with a cup of coffee she wasn't drinking, her knife hand resting flat on her thigh, casual as a drawn curtain. Hale's map was upstairs under a floorboard in Coral's room, wrapped in oilcloth, and Coral thought about that floorboard the way a drowning man thinks about air.

Ada turned. She looked at Corliss the way she must have looked at every hard case that ever walked through her door — a long, level, unhurried look that took inventory and gave nothing back.

"We're closed," she said.

"For lunch, perhaps." Corliss removed his hat and set it on the table, uninvited, claiming territory. "But we're not here to eat. We're here to make an offer, and offers go stale. So let me lay it out plainly, madam, because I respect plainness." He drew a chair out and sat, crossing his legs, arranging himself like a man settling in for a negotiation he'd already won. "There is a document in this building. It came up the coast road eleven days ago in the pockets of a dead prospector. My employers want it. They will pay five hundred dollars for its delivery, no questions asked, no names recorded. Five hundred dollars is a fortune in a town like this. It would buy this roadhouse twice over."

Nobody moved. The fire ticked in the stove. Outside, the rain thickened against the windows.

"And if nobody has it?" Ada said.

"Then nobody gets paid, and we keep looking, and looking is expensive. Expensive things make my employers irritable." Corliss produced a cigarette case, opened it, closed it again without taking one — a little performance of restraint. "I'll be at the Kestrel Arms until tomorrow evening. I do hope the document finds its way to me. It would spare everyone a great deal of unpleasantness." He rose, retrieved his hat, and paused at the door. "Ms. Voss. Your father was a careful man. Careful men leave careful records. Records are exactly the sort of paper my employers misplace." He tipped the hat to the room. "Good day."

The door shut behind him. Juno exhaled slowly through her nose.

"He knows about her father," Coral said quietly.

"He knows *of* him," Ada said. Her voice was steady, but her knuckles were white around the ladle. "There's a difference. Barely." She set the ladle down. "That man didn't drive three days through mud on a rumor. He knows the map exists. He knows it reached Harrowgate. Which means someone talked, or someone followed Hale longer than any of us guessed."

"The navy?" Juno asked.

"Navy doesn't hire men like that. Navy sends uniforms and paperwork." Ada untied her apron with sharp, deliberate motions. "That was money talking. Money that paid for the ship to sink and paid for everyone to forget it." She looked at Coral, and for the first time since he'd met her he saw fear in her face — not for herself, but banked and controlled, aimed somewhere else. "They won't stop at offering. Men like that offer first because buying is cheaper than burying. When the buying fails—"

"It'll fail tonight," Coral said. "We leave before dawn."

* * *

They spent the evening preparing the way people prepare for funerals: quietly, thoroughly, saying less than they meant. Juno broke down and repacked their gear by lamplight, checking straps twice. Ada counted out food from her own stores — hard bread, smoked fish, a sack of dried apples — and pressed a canvas-wrapped bundle into Coral's hands that clinked faintly: tools, a pry bar, a coil of good rope. "For the cache," she said. "If it still exists. If the mountain left it alone."

Coral went up to his room one last time and lifted the floorboard. The map lay where he'd hidden it, grease-stained and patient, Hale's careful pencil lines crawling across the Kestrel Range toward a valley that had cost forty men their lives and one old man his. He thought about Merrick Hale's hand closing on his wrist in the boathouse, the salt-crusted lips shaping the words *Ada Voss, before the snow*, and the promise he'd made to a dying stranger because refusing a drowning man seemed like the kind of sin the sea kept accounts of.

Five hundred dollars. Half a year of salvage, maybe more. Enough to re-rig the boat, replace the compressor, stop diving the forbidden wreck altogether.

He wrapped the map tighter and put it inside his shirt, against his skin, where a knife would have to mean it.

Below, the floorboards creaked. Then stopped. Then creaked again — wrong rhythm, wrong weight. Coral had spent years listening to hulls talk in the dark, learning which sounds were the water's and which sounds were trouble. This was trouble.

He was halfway down the stairs when the lamp in the kitchen went out.

* * *

Later, Coral would understand that Corliss had never intended to wait until tomorrow evening. The offer had been theater — a civilized performance staged for witnesses, so that afterward, when the unpleasantness came, it could be called something else. A burglary. A tragedy. A fire started by careless guests. The syndicate had buried a ship and forty men once already; burying a roadhouse and its stubborn keeper would hardly strain the budget.

But Corliss had made one miscalculation, and it was lying awake in the loft above the stable with a knife in her boot: Juno.

Coral came off the stairs into darkness and smelled it immediately — lamp oil, fresh-poured, sharp under the onion-smell of the kitchen. Two shapes moved by the stove. One held a can. The other struck a match, and in the small bloom of flame Coral saw the big driver's face, slack and ordinary, doing ugly work without passion.

The match touched the oil and the floor went up in a blue-orange whisper, fast as spilled water finding a crack.

"Fire!" Coral roared it, and threw the nearest thing — a cast-iron pan — into the second man's skull. The clang was enormous. The man dropped the can and staggered, and then the kitchen was all noise and orange light, and Coral learned in the next thirty seconds what kind of fighter he was when it wasn't sport: not a good one, but a desperate one, and desperation has its own technique.

The big man recovered faster than a man his shape should. He came in low with a blade of his own — a short, wicked hook of steel — and Coral caught the first pass on his forearm and felt it open him like a can opener, hot line of pain, and drove his forehead into the man's nose. Cartilage crunched. They went down together across a table that collapsed, and the knife skittered somewhere into the dark, and Coral's world narrowed to breath and weight and the roar of flame climbing the wall behind them.

Juno came off the loft ladder like weather. She didn't hesitate — later he'd understand she never hesitated, that hesitation was a luxury her life had never stocked — and put her boot into the big man's head twice, precise as a diver counting strokes. He rolled off Coral and didn't get up quickly enough to matter.

"Coral—" Ada's voice from the hall, ragged. "The map—"

"Got it." It was against his ribs, warm from his body. "Out. Now. The whole wall's going."

The kitchen was lost — flames had found the dry pine of the walls and were running upward with the joy of a thing finally unleashed. Smoke layered down thick and black. Coral hauled the unconscious driver clear of the doorway — he'd ask himself why, later, and never find a clean answer except that leaving a man to burn was the kind of debt the sea collected on — dumped him in the mud of the yard, and turned back for the house.

Ada was standing in the yard in the rain, watching twenty years of her life breathe fire through the windows. She wasn't crying. That frightened Coral more than tears would have.

"My father's letters," she said, very quietly. "In the east room."

"They're ash," Juno said, brutal and gentle at once. "So are we, in about four minutes, if those other two come back. Ada. Look at me. The letters are ash. You aren't. Not yet."

Somewhere up the street, a bell began to ring — the mine watchman, roused by the glow. Voices. And down the road, headlights: the grey motorcar, returning, unhurried, its lamps cutting twin cones through the rain and smoke.

"They're coming to see it burn," Coral said. "And to see who runs out."

"Then we don't run out the front." Wren's voice — no. Not Wren. Not yet. It was Juno, jerking her chin at the stable. "Horses are saddled. Have been since sundown. I don't trust men who arrive in cars."

They went through the stable's back gap single file, into the treeline, into the black and the rain, four horses moving quiet on soft ground while behind them the roadhouse that had sheltered everyone the law forgot lit the underside of the storm clouds like a second, terrible dawn.

* * *

They climbed for two hours before anyone spoke.

The trail left Harrowgate through cut timber and climbed into foothills where the rain turned to sleet and the sleet turned to a fine, needling snow — too early, weeks too early, the mountain's opening argument. Coral rode with his wounded arm bound tight against his chest, the cut throbbing in time with his pulse, and the map a small stiff rectangle against his skin, and behind him the glow of Harrowgate fading under the clouds like a coal going out.

At a saddle where the trees opened, Ada reined in and looked back a long time. Snow gathered on her shoulders and she didn't brush it off.

"That man knew my father's name," she said at last. "Not from files. From *memory*. Someone who worked with him. Someone who signed the same papers." She turned forward. Her face in the grey light was carved down to essentials. "Corliss isn't hunting a map anymore, Coral. He's hunting witnesses. And now we're both on the list."

Coral looked back once, himself. Below, the valley was dark and the road was empty and everything he'd had two days ago — the boat, the claim, the wreck that kept its secrets, the small crooked life he'd built on the edge of other people's lies — was already gone, dissolved into smoke and ash and a wanted man's future.

He'd made a promise to a dying stranger. He'd refused five hundred dollars without even hearing himself refuse. Somewhere between the boathouse and the burning kitchen, the choice had stopped being a choice, and he hadn't noticed the moment it happened.

"Kestrel Pass closes with the snow," he said. "So we don't let it close."

Juno grinned at him through the sleet, blood on her knuckles, and kicked her horse uphill.

Behind them, far down the black valley, headlights swung onto the mountain road — patient, deliberate, following.
