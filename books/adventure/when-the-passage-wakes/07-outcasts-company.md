## 7. Outcasts' Company

Juno had learned to read people the way she'd once read tide tables: by what they hid, not what they showed. Coral was easy — he wore his worry like ballast, low and steady, and you only noticed it when the weather turned. Ada Voss was harder. Three days out of Harrowgate and the woman still hadn't told them everything the map meant to her, though she carried it like a bone she'd swallowed, something that ached in every weather.

And then there were the strays.

The first one found them at a place called Cold Fork, where the trail crossed a stream so pale with glacier milk it looked like the mountain had bled into it. Juno saw him before anyone else did — a habit from her years running cargo through country nobody honest traveled. A man sitting on a rock above the ford, rifle across his knees, watching them come with the patience of something that had been waiting a long time.

"Don't," she said quietly, when Coral's hand drifted toward his belt knife. "If he wanted us dead we'd already be."

The man stood as they reached the water. He was fifty, maybe older, built like a fence post left out too many winters — lean, weather-cracked, one shoulder sitting lower than the other. His coat was army surplus, dyed civilian brown but not well enough; the buttons gave him away. So did the way he stood: weight even, eyes moving, always moving, cataloguing them the way a quartermaster catalogued stores.

"You're headed up-valley," he said. Not a question.

"We're headed wherever we're headed," Coral said.

"Through Kestrel Pass." The man's voice was flat as slate. "Ahead of the snows. With two mules and no high-country experience between you." He looked at Ada. "You I don't know. You—" he nodded at Coral and Juno— "I've watched since Harrowgate road. You walk like dock rats. Nothing wrong with that down here. Up there it'll kill you."

Juno felt her hackles rise, and beside her she felt Coral's do the same. But Ada stepped forward, studying the man with an expression Juno couldn't parse — recognition without warmth.

"Tam Osgood," she said.

Something flickered across the tracker's face. "You know me?"

"I know the name. Fort Meridian, six years back. The tracking detail that lost the Verity girl in the Whitefall country." She paused. "You were the one who kept going after they called it off. Forty hours alone. They said you found her body and carried her out yourself."

"They also said I disobeyed a direct order," Tam said. "Which is the part that ended my career." He shouldered his rifle. "So. Now you know what I am. A man the army threw away for caring about the wrong thing at the wrong time."

"And what are you doing here?" Coral asked.

"Watching you walk toward a pass I've crossed eleven times, badly equipped, badly led, being followed." He let that last word sit in the cold air. "You've got riders behind you. Two days back, maybe less. I've seen their sign since the Fork."

Juno's stomach dropped. She thought of Corliss's smile in the burning kitchen, the way he'd tipped his head like a man doing arithmetic. *Everything can be bought, Miss Juno. Even regret.*

"How many?" she asked.

"Four. Maybe five. Good horses, bad manners." Tam looked at each of them in turn, and Juno realized he wasn't asking to join them. He was deciding whether they were worth saving. "I'm not offering friendship. I'm offering the difference between dying stupid and dying slow. Take it or leave it."

Coral glanced at Juno. It was a glance with a whole conversation folded inside it — *can we trust him, do we have a choice, does it matter* — and Juno answered it with a shrug that said *we never have a choice, that's the joke.*

"Walk with us," Coral said finally. "But you carry your own weight."

"Always have," Tam said, and came down off the rock, and that was that.

* * *

Wren they didn't find. Wren found them — or rather, Wren had been finding them for two days without any of them noticing, which was its own kind of lesson.

It was Tam who caught her, naturally. They'd made camp in a hollow of wind-bent pines below a ridge called the Shepherd's Tooth, and Juno woke in the grey hour before dawn to Tam standing at the edge of the firelight, very still, looking at a shadow that resolved itself into a girl. Sixteen at most. Small, sharp-faced, wrapped in a coat three sizes too big with the sleeves rolled six times. She stood the way animals stand when they've decided running isn't worth it yet.

"There's someone in your camp," Tam said calmly, to no one in particular. "Been there since midnight. Ate half our salt pork."

"That was mine," the girl said. "I paid for it."

"With what?"

"Information." She grinned, and it was a feral, brilliant thing. "You're being followed. Four men, maybe five. Good horses, bad manners."

Juno heard Coral sit up. "That's what Tam said."

"Yeah, well, your soldier counts noses. I count boot prints." The girl crouched by the dead coals, warming her hands with a shamelessness Juno found almost admirable. "They stopped at Cold Fork. Talked to the ferryman. Ferryman says you bought mule feed and asked about the Kestrel route. That's worth more than salt pork. We're square."

"What's your name?" Ada asked.

"Wren."

"Just Wren?"

"Just Wren." But her eyes went to Ada when she said it, quick and hungry, and Juno understood that names were currency this girl had learned to spend carefully.

"Why are you following us?" Coral asked.

Wren picked up a stick and poked the coals. "Because you're going somewhere. Because everyone in Harrowgate either stays or runs, and runners are interesting. And because—" the bravado flickered, just for a second, like a lamp in wind— "because you've got a woman, a diver, a dock rat, and a disgraced tracker, and none of you look like you belong anywhere either. I figured misfits travel safer in company."

Nobody said anything for a moment. The fire ticked. Somewhere upslope a pine dropped its load of snow with a soft whump.

"She knows the high country," Tam said abruptly. Everyone looked at him. "I've been reading her tracks since yesterday. She moves like she was born up here. Trapper's kid, or prospector's. Am I right?"

Wren's chin lifted. "My father ran traplines above the treeline for nine years. Died in a whiteout two winters back because the army closed the beacon stations." Her voice didn't waver, but her knuckles whitened around the stick. "So yes. I know these mountains better than your maps do. And I hate the same people you hate. Can I come or not? I'll eat less than the mules."

Coral looked at Juno again — that folded-conversation glance — and this time Juno answered out loud, because some things deserved air.

"We're already carrying trouble," she said. "One more mouth won't tip the boat."

* * *

By the third evening the company had found its shape, the way water finds the shape of whatever holds it.

They camped in a cirque below a hanging glacier, five humans and two mules tucked against a wall of granite that held the day's last heat like a banked stove. Tam set snares without being asked. Wren vanished for an hour and returned with an armload of fatwood and a look of smug satisfaction. Ada cooked, silently, competently, and Juno noticed she'd started making portions without counting heads first — just making enough, the way you did for family.

Coral sat sharpening his dive knife, and Juno settled beside him with the mule harness she was mending.

"She's good," Coral said, nodding at Wren, who was teaching herself Tam's snare knots by firelight, failing, swearing creatively, failing better.

"She's starving," Juno said. "There's a difference. Starving kids learn fast."

"So did we."

"So did we." She threaded the awl through cracked leather. "You ever think about how none of us chose each other? Hale washes into your boathouse and dies. Ada opens her door because of a promise she made to a dead man. I follow you because—" she paused, surprised at herself— "huh. I actually don't know why I followed you."

"Loyalty," Coral said. "You said it yourself. Someone chose you, so you chose back."

"That's not loyalty, that's infection."

Coral laughed — really laughed, the first time since the boathouse burned — and the sound carried across the cirque, and Juno saw Tam's shoulders ease a fraction, and Wren look over with curiosity instead of calculation, and Ada smile at the stew pot like the smile was private.

Later, when the dark had gone total and the fire had shrunk to a red eye, they talked. It happened without anyone deciding it would. Wren asked Tam if the army story was true, and Tam, to everyone's surprise, told it — flat, unadorned, ending with: "They gave me a discharge and a letter saying I put personal attachment above mission. As if finding a dead child and carrying her home was a *personal* problem." He fed the fire a stick. "I've decided to be proud of it. Took four years."

"My dad used to say the mountains keep score different than towns do," Wren said. "Towns remember your mistakes. Mountains only care if you show up tomorrow."

"The mountains and the sea agree on that," Coral said.

Juno lay back on her bedroll and listened to them trade small truths the way traders trade small coin — nothing valuable enough to fight over, everything necessary to build with. Ada spoke of her father for the first time: not the engineer, not the dead man, just her father, who sang off-key and burned rice and believed that machines could be honest if people were. Tam mentioned a wife, past tense, no details, and nobody pressed. Wren admitted she was sixteen, not seventeen as she'd claimed, and dared anyone to make something of it, and nobody did.

At some point Coral said, "When this is done — whatever's in that cache, whatever happens — what then?"

"Then we find out who we are when we're not running," Ada said.

"Speak for yourself," said Wren. "I'm going to be rich and insufferable."

"You're going to be asleep in an hour," Tam said, "like everyone else."

But nobody slept quickly. Juno lay listening to the glacier creak somewhere above them, a sound like a giant clearing its throat, and thought about how strange it was — that a week ago her whole world had been Coral and the boat and the grey water, and now it was this: five strangers becoming a shape, a company, a thing with a name none of them had spoken yet but all of them could feel coming.

She was nearly asleep when Wren's voice came, low, from the edge of the firelight.

"Juno. You awake?"

"Mm."

"The riders. The ones following us." A pause. "They made camp tonight two miles back. I checked. But that's not what's bothering me."

Juno opened her eyes. Across the coals, she saw Tam's silhouette go still — he'd been awake too, then, listening all along.

"What's bothering you?" Juno asked.

"There were five of them yesterday," Wren said. "Today there's four. One peeled off sometime after noon, heading north, fast. Toward the pass." She poked the fire, and sparks rose like startled birds. "He's not following us anymore. He's going ahead of us."

Above them, the hanging glacier groaned in the dark, ice shifting under its own weight, patient as anything that has all the time in the world and intends to use it.
