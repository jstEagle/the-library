## 8. The First Pass

The mountain announced itself three days before they saw it.

Coral felt it first in his teeth, a pressure like the deep-water squeeze he'd known a hundred times below the surface, and then in the mules, who grew sullen and quick-eared, refusing the trail at places where nothing moved but wind. Wren said the animals could smell the pass. Coral had spent enough years listening to water to believe that a mountain could have a smell too — cold stone, old ice, the mineral breath of scree fields that had not decided yet whether to hold together or let go.

Sawtooth Pass showed itself at dawn on the fourth morning: a grey notch in a wall of granite, hung with dirty snow, narrow as a wound. The sun came up behind it and made a blade of light down the eastern face, and for a long moment nobody in the company said anything at all. Tam stood with his good hand shading his eyes. Juno counted under her breath, the way she did when she was doing arithmetic she didn't like. Ada had her father's map out of its oilcloth, though she wasn't reading it — she was looking at the pass the way a woman looks at a grave she's only ever heard described.

"That's it," Wren said, and her voice came out smaller than she meant it to. "Sawtooth."

"It's early," Coral said. "We're ahead of the snows. Hale's map said the crossing was good through the third week of the month, and we're—" he did the count in his head, the way he counted breaths underwater, "—we're four days clear. Five, maybe."

"Maybe," Juno said. She said it the way she said everything, flat and certain, a woman who had hauled him out of grey water enough times to distrust the word *maybe* from anyone, including herself.

They made camp below the last stand of pines, in a bowl of granite where the wind came through in long, slow pulses, like the mountain breathing in its sleep. Tam got a fire going small and hot, shielded behind a wall of stone he built with a tracker's patience, fitting rocks together so no light escaped. Wren hobbled the mules — Biscuit, Juno's grey; Marrow, the big bay that carried most of the food; and the two younger animals, Sorrel and Nettle, that they'd bought off a rancher in the foothills for more than they were worth. The mules stood with their heads down, ears swiveling, listening to something none of them could hear.

Coral took first watch because he wanted it. Sleep had been coming to him in shallow pieces since Harrowgate, since the fire, since the roadhouse kitchen had turned into a knife-fight and a burning roof and a life that belonged to other people now. On watch, at least, the waiting was his to do. He sat on a flat rock above the camp with his knees drawn up and watched the pass turn silver under the climbing moon, and he thought about the sea.

It was a habit he couldn't break. Ten years of salvage diving will do that — the body learns to measure everything against water, against pressure and current and the long patience of things that sink. And the more he looked at the mountain, the more he understood that it was not so different from the wreck he'd been stripping when Merrick Hale washed into his boathouse. Both of them were graveyards. Both of them were full of metal and bone and the residue of decisions made by men who never expected to be held accountable. And both of them, he was beginning to understand, kept their own time.

The navy was counting. Corliss was counting. And now, he thought, looking up at that cold notch in the granite, the mountain was counting too — and of the three, the mountain was the only one that never hurried, never bargained, and never missed.

* * *

They started for the pass at first light, before the sun could soften the snow bridges, and the first two hours were almost kind. The trail climbed through a corridor of stone where the wind funneled and sang, a low moan that Wren said was the pass "talking in its sleep." Tam led the second mule. Ada walked beside Coral and said, once, quietly: "My father crossed here twice a year for eleven years. He said the pass forgives nothing, but it forgets everything. He liked that about it."

"Did he like anything else about it?"

"He liked that it didn't care who you were," Ada said. "Navy, syndicate, judge, thief. Up there it's all the same weight on the rope."

Coral looked at her. She'd said *rope* without emphasis, but he heard it — the word they'd all been avoiding since the trailhead, the word that meant what it had meant on the water: you tie yourself to another person, and their weight becomes yours, and yours becomes theirs, and if one of you goes over the edge the only question is how many you take along.

They roped up at the treeline. It was Tam who insisted, and Tam who rigged it, showing Wren how to coil and flake the line, showing Juno how to anchor a piton in a crack that looked like nothing. Coral watched the disgraced tracker work and understood, again, why Juno had argued for taking him. There were men who had been broken by the army and men who had been sharpened by it, and Tam was the second kind wearing the first kind's coat.

Above the treeline the world went simple. Rock, snow, sky, rope. Coral had spent his whole adult life in environments that reduced everything to essentials — the wreck had been the same, a black corridor where the only truths were air, line, and time. He felt something in himself go quiet and competent, the old diver's calm settling over him like a second skin, and he led them up through the corridor of stone with his breath counted, his steps even, his eyes moving from hold to hold the way they used to move from beam to beam inside a drowned hull.

They made the crest of Sawtooth Pass a little after noon.

For a moment — Coral would remember the moment for the rest of his life, and would never quite be able to explain it — the wind stopped. All of it, everywhere, the long moan in the corridor and the high whistle off the cornices, everything, gone at once, and the silence was so complete that he could hear the creak of the rope and the mules' breathing and his own heart. They stood on the spine of the range with the world falling away on both sides, east into the country they'd fled, west into the country that didn't know they were coming, and the sky above them was so blue and so close it felt like a held breath.

"Beautiful," Juno said. "Now get us off it."

The descent began on the western side, down a long scree fan that spilled from the pass like something poured. It was good going, faster than the climb, the mules picking their way with that mule wisdom that made them worth their price, and Coral allowed himself — briefly, foolishly — to think they might actually make the valley floor by dark.

He heard the mountain before he understood it.

It was not a sound so much as a change in sound — a deep, grinding note underneath the crunch of their boots, a note like the hull of a ship taking on water, and every hair on Coral's body stood up because his body knew that sound. He'd heard it through the soles of his boots on a drowned deck once, the day the wreck finally shifted, the day the sea decided it had let him borrow long enough.

"Off the trail," he said. Then louder, with the voice he used below the surface, the one that carried through water and fear alike: "*Off the trail — move!*"

The rockslide came off the eastern face with no more warning than a wave.

It was not the great cinematic thunder he'd imagined avalanches to be. It was worse — it was quiet at first, a whispering, a hiss, the scree field above the trail deciding all at once to become a river, and then the roar arrived and swallowed everything. Coral had Juno by the arm and was dragging her toward a granite fin that stood off the trail like a ship's rib, and the world behind them turned to white dust and grinding stone.

He felt, more than saw, what happened to the mules.

Biscuit went first — the grey simply vanished into the dust, and Juno screamed a word that the mountain ate. Marrow fought. Coral saw the big bay turn uphill, ears back, eyes rolling white, and for one impossible second man and mule looked at each other through the flying grit, and then the slide took Marrow the way the sea takes a man off a deck — completely, without malice, without even noticing. The food went with him. The spare rope, the canvas, the medical kit Tam had packed with a surgeon's economy — all of it, down the mountain, in the space of ten seconds.

Then it was over. The roar thinned to a rattle, the rattle to a hiss, and the dust rolled down the slope and hung in the air like fog, like the grey water he'd been born to, and Coral stood against the granite fin with his heart slamming and the rope still taut in his hand and counted.

One. Two. Three.

Checking his people the way he checked his air.

"Sound off," he said. His voice came out of him rough, scraped.

"Here." Juno, beside him, coughing dust, her hand a bruise-tight grip on his forearm.

"Here." Tam, further down, with Wren — the girl had a scrape along her jaw that was already bleeding freely, and she was staring back up the trail at the place where the mules had been.

"Here." Ada, at the rear, white to the lips, both hands locked on the rope. She was looking not at the slide but at the mountain above it, at the fresh scar on the eastern face, and her expression was not fear. It was recognition.

"The food," Wren said. Her voice cracked on the second word. "Coral. The food was all on Marrow. Everything. We had — we were going to re-supply on the far side, we said—"

"I know."

"We have what's in the saddlebags. That's it. Four days if we're careful. Three if the weather—"

"I *know*," Coral said, and made himself soften it, because she was sixteen and bleeding and had just watched an animal she'd named die in a river of stone. "I know, Wren. We'll count it. We'll count everything, tonight, down in the trees. Nothing gets eaten that isn't weighed first."

They picked their way down through the dust to what remained. It wasn't much. One saddlebag from Sorrel, half-torn, with a sack of flour gone to paste and a tin of tea and Tam's flint. The mules themselves were gone — buried, or thrown clear and fallen further; Tam scouted the slide's runout for an hour in the fading light and came back with his hat in his hand and his face saying what his mouth didn't bother to.

"Two mules," Juno said, when he reported. "And most of the food. And we're still four days from the valley if the weather holds."

"If the weather holds," Tam said, and looked west, where the sky over the far peaks had begun to build a different kind of cloud — high, white, feathered, the kind that Coral had never learned to read but that made both the tracker and the mountain girl go quiet.

* * *

They camped that night in the first stunted pines below the scree, and Coral sat apart from the fire, doing the arithmetic he'd been avoiding.

Four days of food, stretched. Five days of travel, if the trail was kind. And behind them — always behind them, somewhere down in the green country they'd crossed — Corliss and his riders, who did not have to cross the pass at all if they didn't want to, who could wait at the bottom of every descent like a current waiting for a diver to come up.

Three enemies, Coral thought. Three clocks.

The navy, which wanted the logs back in a dark drawer. The syndicate, which wanted them in ashes. And now the mountain itself, which didn't want anything at all — which was the worst of the three, because you couldn't buy it, couldn't outrun it, couldn't lay a false trail for it through a burn scar. The mountain only counted. It had been counting since the day Merrick Hale washed into his boathouse with a dying man's map in his fist, and today, on Sawtooth, it had taken its first payment: two mules and most of their food, in ten seconds, without malice, without even noticing.

He heard footsteps and didn't reach for the knife, which was how he knew it was Juno. She sat down beside him on the cold rock without asking, the way she'd been sitting down beside him since they were two kids diving wrecks the law forgot, and for a while neither of them said anything.

"We could still turn back," she said at last. "Go down the east side. Take our chances with Corliss on open ground."

"Open ground is his ground."

"Then we could turn around and go home, and let the dead stay dead, and let the navy keep its secret until the sea gives it up on its own. Which it will, eventually. It always does." She pulled her knees up. "I'm not saying it, Coral. I'm just saying it out loud so we both know it was on the table."

He looked at the fire, where Wren was showing Ada how to stretch the flour with pine nuts, and Tam was re-wrapping the girl's jaw with a strip of shirt, gentle as a man defusing something. Ada was laughing at something Wren said — a small, surprised sound, the laugh of a woman who hadn't laughed since her father's name became a number on a navy manifest.

"No," Coral said. "It was on the table. It's off now."

"Because of the map?"

"Because of them." He nodded at the fire. "Hale gave the map to me. But nobody gave these people to me. They just — came. And I'm not carrying them down the east side to get shot on open ground, and I'm not carrying them home to wait for the navy to knock. So we go through. We go through and we ration hard and we move fast, and we treat this mountain with the respect I treat deep water. Because it's the same thing, Juno. I finally see it. It's the same thing. The whole range is one big wreck, and we're inside the hull, and the hull has opinions."

Juno huffed — almost a laugh. "The hull has opinions," she repeated. "Ten years underwater and that's the philosophy you come up with."

"Ten years underwater taught me the only philosophy that matters." Coral stood, and brushed the dust of Sawtooth Pass off his trousers, and looked west, where the feathered clouds were building higher and whiter over the far peaks, and where, four days and one hard valley away, a high pass called Kestrel waited with its own arithmetic. "You count your air. You trust your line. And you get out before the weather changes."

He was still looking at those clouds when Tam came and stood beside him, and the tracker was quiet a long moment, reading the sky the way Coral read water.

"Those are snow clouds," Tam said finally. "Early ones. Ahead of forecast, the way things have been going." He turned, and in the firelight his face was grim and steady. "Get some sleep, Coral. Tomorrow we ration. And the day after, I want to show you something I found on the back of Hale's map — a mark your dead prospector made that I don't think was a mistake."

Coral looked at him. "What kind of mark?"

"The kind," Tam said, "that might save us four days — or kill us in a canyon nobody's ever mapped."
