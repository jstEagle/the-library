## 9. Corliss on Their Trail

Tam Verrin had spent eleven years learning to read ground the way other men read letters, and four of those years learning what happened to a man who read it too well for his superiors' comfort. Disgrace had a taste, he'd found — copper and ash, like blood in the mouth after a bad fall. He tasted it every morning now, waking on frozen dirt with a bedroll that smelled of mule and Juno's terrible coffee, and every morning he waited for the shame to arrive on schedule.

It hadn't come in three days. He was starting to suspect why, and the suspicion sat in his chest like a coal he didn't dare touch.

He was two hundred yards ahead of the company, as he'd been since dawn, walking the spine of a granite shoulder above the tree line's ragged fringe. The others moved below him along the creek — Coral and Ada leading the surviving mule, Juno sweeping the rear with the rifle she carried like an extension of her arm, Wren ranging wherever Wren pleased, which was everywhere. Tam watched them the way he'd once watched patrol columns from ridgelines: not because they needed watching, but because watching was how you kept something.

The burn scar announced itself before he saw it. A smell first — old char leached into the soil, that sweet-bitter tang of carbon and rain that could linger a decade in high country — then the color, a wash of grey and bone-white snags standing among the green like a congregation struck dead mid-prayer. It swept down from the ridge to the east and pooled in the valley the trail would follow for the next six miles. Lightning fire, by the shape of it. Three summers old, maybe four. The lodgepoles were dead but standing; the brush underneath had come back furious and dense, manzanita and ceanothus woven into a lattice that would swallow a horse to the girth.

Tam crouched at the edge of the scar and did what the army had trained into him and then punished him for doing honestly: he thought about who else might be looking at this ground.

* * *

He'd seen the dust at midmorning yesterday and told himself it was weather. That was the soldier's oldest lie, and he was ashamed of it within the hour. Weather didn't hold formation. What he'd glimpsed through a gap in the trees — three points of pale light where sun caught glass or steel, moving parallel to the company's line of travel, half a mile east and holding — that was discipline. That was men keeping spacing without being told, checking their backtrail without breaking stride.

Syndicate riders. Corliss's people, or Corliss himself; Tam couldn't tell at that distance and hadn't said anything yet, because saying *someone's following us* without proof was how you burned a group's nerve like paper in a stove. And this group — he studied them now, the five of them strung out along the creek — ran on nerve the way a lamp ran on oil. Coral drove himself like a man paying off a debt to a dead prospector. Ada walked with her father's ledger wrapped in oilcloth against her chest as if it were an infant she'd learned to carry in one arm. Juno trusted no horizon. Wren trusted nothing at all except, lately, Tam's judgment, which frightened him more than the riders did.

So he'd held his tongue and watched, and this morning the watchers had made their mistake.

It was small. It was beautiful. Tam nearly laughed aloud on the empty ridge.

They'd crossed the creek. All three of them, at the same ford, maybe forty minutes ahead of him — he could see the churned mud even from here, see the water still carrying a faint ribbon of silt downstream. Amateurs crossed water at fords. Professionals knew that a ford was a signature written in mud, legible for hours, and that any tracker worth his salt would find it inside a day. Which meant either they didn't have a tracker —

— or they wanted him to find it.

Tam went very still, the way he'd gone still in the Keshari hills with an ambush wire under his boot, and let the cold move through him. Consider it straight. Corliss was a fixer, not a fool. The man had run them out of Harrowgate with a knife and a torch; he didn't chase blind. If he had a tracker — and a syndicate that could buy a packer out from under you, as they'd learn later, could certainly buy a tracker — then the ford was bait, and the trap was somewhere past it, in the green timber where visibility closed to thirty feet and a rifleman could hold a narrows all afternoon.

And if there was no tracker, the ford told him nothing anyway, and Tam had lost only time.

Either way, the answer was the same. Don't take the bait. Give them something else to chew.

He looked at the burn scar again, really looked, and began to smile. It wasn't a kind smile. Eleven years ago it would have been; the army had beaten the kindness out of his craft early. Now it was just arithmetic with teeth.

* * *

He backtracked off the ridge, cutting downslope through the deadfall until he reached the creek a quarter mile below the company's route, and walked upstream in the shallows themselves, boots finding the gravel bars, letting the water do what water does — erase. Where he had to leave the creek he stepped on stone, always stone, choosing each footing the way a miser chooses coins. It cost him twenty minutes to cover ground he could have covered in five. He didn't care. Time spent invisible was never wasted; time spent visible was always borrowed, and borrowers paid interest.

He came up behind Wren, who heard him anyway — of course she did, the girl moved through brush like a rumor — and turned with that flat, unimpressed stare she'd perfected somewhere in whatever life she'd run away from.

"You're wet," she said.

"I'm quiet," Tam said. "There's a difference, and I'm about to spend it."

Coral turned at the sound of voices, hand already drifting toward the hatchet at his belt, and Tam raised both palms. Behind him, Juno had already swung the rifle off her shoulder, casual as breathing. Ada stood beside the mule with her chin up and her eyes narrow, reading him the way she read everything — for the angle, for the cost.

"Riders," Tam said quietly. "Three of them, maybe more behind. They've been paralleling us since yesterday morning, east side, half a mile out. This morning they crossed the creek at the lower ford and left it churned like a cattle crossing."

"They want us to see it," Coral said. Not a question. Tam felt something shift in his chest at that — the diver had a tracker's mind after all, or at least a hunted animal's caution, which amounted to the same thing in the mountains.

"It's either sloppy or it's a door," Tam agreed. "Corliss doesn't strike me as sloppy."

"So we don't go through the door." Coral looked at the valley ahead, at the green timber closing in, and Tam watched him weigh it — the food they'd lost at Sawtooth Pass still bleeding out of their margins, the mules gone, the days counting down toward snow. "Options?"

"The burn scar." Tam pointed east, up the slope, to where the dead snags stood silver against the sky. "Six miles of open ground, ugly going, no water after the first mile. But nobody tracks well in a burn. Char eats sign. The ground's black, the light's flat, and everything that looks like a trail is a ghost — old fire lines, game paths that died with the trees. A tracker can work it, but slow, and slow is what we need."

"And the risk?"

"No cover. If they catch us in the open—" Juno said it for him, flat and unbothered, sighting along the ridge as though the riders were already there.

"If they catch us in the open," Tam said, "we're done. But they won't, because I'm going to make sure they're looking somewhere else." He crouched, pulled a stick from the fire circle they'd abandoned an hour early that morning, and drew in the ash-dirt: the creek, the ford, the valley trail, the scar climbing east. "We go up into the burn here. But first—" he tapped the ford, "—somebody walks through that ford. Boots in the mud, mule tracks, a camp ring, the whole theater. Make it look like we took the bait. Then we climb, and when their tracker reads that ford, he reads us going up the valley trail, fat and careless, and he sets his ambush where the narrows pinch at Miller's Bend. And we're six miles east laughing at him."

Silence. Wren was grinning. Juno's mouth did the thing that was almost a smile, the thing Tam had decided privately counted double coming from her.

"That's a lot of theater," Coral said slowly. "Who plays us?"

"I do," Tam said. "All of us. It has to look real — drag marks, spilled grain, a dropped ration wrapper. People believe litter. Litter's honest. Nobody fakes litter." He paused. "Except me. That's rather the point."

Ada spoke for the first time. "You've done this before."

It wasn't a question either, and Tam heard what lived under it — not accusation, exactly. Curiosity with an edge. Everyone in this company carried their disgrace openly except him; he'd offered his in fragments, the court-martial reduced to a shrug, *the army and I disagreed about what counting things was for.* He looked at her, and at Coral, and understood that this was the toll. They'd carried him this far on credit. The bridge was here. Pay or turn back.

"Keshar," he said. "Third year of the border war. My section got cut off, eleven of us, and the way home was through a valley full of men who wanted our heads. I laid false trails for nine days. Sign for a column of sixty, moving north, while eleven of us went south through the reed beds." He shrugged, and hated how small the shrug sounded against what those nine days had been. "We lost two. We should have lost all eleven. Afterward the inquiry asked me how I'd known the enemy trackers would read the trails the way I wrote them, and I said because I'd write them the way I'd read them, and the major said that was circular reasoning, and I said yes sir, tracking is circular, that's why it works. They gave me a commendation and then, two years later, they gave me a discharge, because a man who thinks that clearly about deception makes his officers nervous."

The silence stretched. Somewhere above them a jay worked a pinecone loose and sent it rattling down through the branches.

"So the army broke you for being good at your job," Wren said, with the directness of the young, "and now you're going to be good at it for us instead."

Tam looked at her. Something in his throat he declined to examine. "Something like that."

"Then let's go be dishonest," Coral said, and there it was — the corner of a grin, the first Tam had seen on him since Sawtooth Pass took their mules. "Show us how a liar walks."

* * *

The theater took two hours, and Tam directed it like the stagecraft it was.

Juno led the mule through the ford twice, three times, churning the crossing into honest confusion, while Coral dragged a dead branch to score long furrows in the mud — the mark of a heavy load, a strongbox-sized suggestion. Ada scattered a handful of oats by the water's edge, then thought better of wasting grain and substituted crushed pinecones, which Tam approved with genuine professional respect: *she thinks like a forger.* Wren, who could cry on command, a skill she demonstrated without being asked and refused to explain, sat on a rock and wept convincingly at nothing, and Tam marked the spot where her tears fell and ground them into the dust with his heel, because a tracker who found salt traces in a rest camp would build a whole story around it — fear, exhaustion, a girl at the end of her rope. Let them build it. Every brick wrong.

Then the litter. This was the scripture, and Tam recited it aloud as he worked, because teaching them was part of proving himself, and some stubborn pride in him wanted witnesses: "A dropped wrapper says carelessness. A torn sleeve on brush says haste. A latrine site says a halt of hours. Never fake all three in one place — pick two, always two, because three is a story and stories get doubted." He tore a strip from his own shirt hem and snagged it deliberately on manzanita at eye height. He cracked a ration tin and buried it half-exposed, glinting. He built a cold camp ring with stones pulled from the creek so they'd still be damp, and pissed on the coals of nothing so the smell of a doused fire hung faint and plausible in the air.

At Miller's Bend, two miles up the valley trail, he planted the masterstroke: a single boot print in soft ground beside the narrows, deep-heeled, fresh-looking, pointing onward. One print. Trackers trust single prints the way priests trust scripture — one clean print is a sentence from God, and they'll follow the sentence into whatever ambush geography suggests.

Then they climbed.

* * *

The burn scar took them the way the sea takes swimmers — indifferent, enormous, smelling of endings. Underfoot the ground was black crust over grey ash that rose in ghosts at every step, coating their throats, silvering their eyebrows, turning the whole company into a procession of the drowned. The dead lodgepoles stood in their thousands, bare and white as ribs, and when the wind moved through them they groaned in chorus, a low organ note that Wren said sounded like whales and Tam, who had never heard a whale, chose to believe her.

It was brutal going. The fire had killed the soil's skin; every slope shed scree under their boots, and twice the mule went down to the hocks in ash drifts and had to be hauled out swearing — or rather Coral swore for it, in two languages, with feeling. There was no shade and no water, and by midafternoon their canteens were hollow and their lips split, and still Tam pushed them east and slightly south, along a contour he'd chosen not for ease but for sightlines: from here he could look back across the whole green valley they'd abandoned and watch it work.

And it worked.

He saw it happen at the fourth hour, from a knob of granite at the scar's edge, and he made no sound, only touched Juno's arm as she passed and pointed, and she dropped beside him without a word, and one by one the others folded down into the ash like a congregation at prayer — Coral last, crawling the final yards, which told Tam more about the man than any speech.

Down in the valley, at the mouth of Miller's Bend, three riders had appeared.

They came along the trail at a trot, unhurried, confident, men following a script someone else had written. Tam watched them reach the narrows, watched the lead rider dismount and crouch over soft ground, and even at half a mile he could read the body language plain as print: the crouch, the long study, the rise and the gesture — *here, set here* — the unhurried confidence of men who believed they were ahead of their quarry instead of behind a lie. Two dismounted and began working rifles into the rocks above the bend. The third rode back down the trail the way they'd come, to fetch the rest.

"How many total?" Coral breathed.

"Can't know. Six, eight. Enough." Tam kept his voice as flat as the burnt light. "But they'll sit on that bend until dark, and then they'll sit on it till morning, because that's what an ambush is — patience with a trigger. And we'll be over the far ridge by then, drinking from a creek they think is a day behind us."

Juno exhaled slowly through her nose. "Nine days in the reed beds," she murmured. "I believe you now."

Wren was looking at Tam with an expression he'd never been on the receiving end of before — not the flat appraisal, not the runaway's wary arithmetic, but something open and young and startled, the look of a person discovering a room in a house she thought she knew completely. "You *made* them do that," she said. "You made them sit there. With a boot print and a crying trick."

"With your crying trick," Tam said. "I only supplied the mud."

She laughed — actually laughed, a bright cracked sound that the dead forest swallowed and gave back — and Coral turned and looked at Tam across the ash, and what was in his face wasn't gratitude exactly, or not only. It was recalculation. The look of a man revising a ledger, moving a name from one column to another.

"We stop at the far creek," Coral said. "Hot food, real rest, first watch doubled. And Tam—" he hesitated, and Tam braced for the thanks he wasn't sure he could survive gracefully, "—you're on point from here on. Every day. Whatever you say about ground goes."

"Understood," Tam managed.

They moved out. Behind them the burn groaned its long note, and beyond the ridge, unseen, the ambush sat down to wait for a company that had already become smoke.

* * *

They made the far creek at dusk, a cold bright thread running through living timber, and the relief of water and green shade hit the group like wine. Juno got a fire going small and hot. Ada produced tea she'd been hoarding like contraband. Wren washed the ash from her hair with the thoroughness of a cat, and Tam sat apart on a root, cleaning grit from his boot seams, listening to the ordinary music of it — the mule cropping grass, the pot beginning to murmur, Coral and Juno arguing amiably about watch order — and realized the copper-and-ash taste of disgrace had been gone from his mouth all day and he hadn't noticed when it left.

Pride, he thought. So that's what it weighs. Lighter than I remembered.

He was still turning that over when Wren dropped down beside him, close, the way she'd started doing — a proximity she offered no one else — and nodded back the way they'd come, west, where the evening light was dying red along the ridgelines.

"I checked the backtrail," she said quietly. "Like you taught me. High point, long look, ten minutes."

"And?"

Her face, in the firelight, had gone carefully blank — the runaway's face, the one that meant bad news worn calmly. "Dust," she said. "On the eastern ridge. Behind us. A lot of it, and it isn't slowing down."
