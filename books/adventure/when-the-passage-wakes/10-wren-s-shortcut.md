## 10. Wren's Shortcut

Wren had been listening to the mountain for two days before she said anything about it.

Nobody noticed, because nobody ever did. That was the trick of being small and quiet and fifteen — you could stand at the edge of a fire with your knees drawn up and your hood half over your face and hear everything: Tam sharpening his knife in slow, even strokes like a man saying a prayer; Juno arguing numbers with Coral in a low voice that wasn't really an argument; Ada turning her father's map over and over in her hands as if the paper might confess something new. Wren heard all of it. She also heard the creek.

The creek was wrong.

It had been running loud since morning — louder than it should for the melt this time of year, louder than the snowfields above could honestly feed it. And under the loudness there was a hollowness, a sound like water moving through a throat instead of a bed. She'd grown up above the treeline. Her mother had taught her to read water before she'd taught her letters. *When a creek sounds hollow,* her mother used to say, *it's because it's running through somewhere it hasn't decided to be yet.*

So on the third morning, when Coral stood over the map with his jaw set and said, "We go around the ridge. It costs us a day, maybe more," Wren heard herself say:

"Or we don't."

Everyone looked at her. They always looked surprised when she spoke, which was fair, because she surprised herself too.

Coral lowered the map. His eyes were the color of deep water and just as hard to read, but he didn't dismiss her, which was one of the reasons she'd decided — quietly, without telling anyone, without even quite admitting it to herself — that she would follow him off the edge of the world if it came to that. He waited. Waiting was another thing he did right.

"There's a canyon," she said. Her voice came out smaller than she wanted, so she made herself say the rest faster. "Cutting straight through the ridge. Nobody uses it because it doesn't show on the survey maps — it floods out every few years and they figure it's not worth cutting a trail into. But I've been through it. Twice. With my mother." She swallowed. The name still caught sometimes, like a fishhook in her throat. "It saves the whole day. Maybe more."

"A canyon nobody maps," Juno said slowly, "that floods."

"It won't flood today."

"You know that how?"

Wren lifted her chin. "Because I can hear it isn't going to."

That got the silence she expected. Tam stopped sharpening. Ada looked up from the map with an expression Wren couldn't name — something between grief and recognition, as if Wren had reminded her of someone.

Coral studied her for a long moment. Then he did the thing that sealed it, the thing she'd remember later when everything went wrong and right again in the space of ten minutes: he handed her the map.

"Show me."

* * *

She led them down off the benchland before the sun cleared the eastern peaks, along a game trail only she could see, through a stand of frost-burned aspen whose leaves rattled like coins. Behind her the mules complained, and behind the mules came the others, and behind them — though none of them said it aloud — came the knowledge that Corliss's riders were somewhere on their flank, patient as weather.

The canyon mouth opened in the ridge like a wound. Walls of grey limestone rose sheer on either side, streaked with old waterlines, and the light fell into it in a single narrow blade that wouldn't reach the bottom until midday. The creek ran fast and clear over red stone, loud now, very loud, filling the whole gorge with its voice so that they had to lean close to talk.

Wren walked the edge of it with her boots almost touching the current and listened the way other people prayed.

"See the walls?" she called back. "Those black lines? That's where the water gets when it's angry. We're well below them. Today we're fine."

"And tomorrow?" Ada asked, falling into step beside her. Ada had taken to walking near the front since the cache — since the strongbox full of her dead father's handwriting had come down off the mountain and changed the weight of everything they carried. She carried it now in turns with Coral, and Wren had seen her rest her hand on it in the evenings like a person touching a grave.

"Tomorrow we're through it," Wren said. "That's the point."

Ada smiled — a thin, tired, real smile. "Your mother taught you that too?"

"Everything I know." Wren kicked a stone into the creek and watched the water take it. "Mostly."

They made good time through the morning. The canyon floor was smooth stone worn glassy by centuries of flood, and the mules' shoes rang on it like bells, and for a while — Wren noticed it and didn't trust it, but felt it anyway — the company loosened. Tam pointed out bighorn sign on a ledge forty feet up and told a story about tracking a deserter through country like this, back when he'd still been someone the army trusted, and for once the story didn't have a bitter end; he let it stop at the funny part. Juno sang two bars of something filthy and got glared at by Ada and laughed at by everyone else. Even Coral's shoulders came down from around his ears.

Wren walked ahead and let herself feel, briefly, like a person who belonged to something.

That was when the light changed.

Not the sun — the sun was fine, the blade of noon sliding down the eastern wall toward them. It was the sound underneath the sound. The hollow note in the creek's voice had deepened overnight, and now beneath it she heard something new: a far-off grinding, like a millstone turning, coming from the high country upstream where no mill had ever been.

She stopped. Put her hand flat against the canyon wall. Felt it.

A tremor. Tiny. Continuous. The stone was shivering.

"Wren?" Coral had come up beside her. He read her face the way he read water — patiently, completely. "What is it?"

"The slide dam." Her mouth had gone dry. "Up-canyon. There's a place where a rockslide backed the creek into a lake — my mother showed me, three years ago, it was already big then. If the melt's been eating at it—"

"How far?"

"Four miles. Maybe five."

"How long?"

"I don't—" She hated the word *don't*, hated it more every day on this mountain. She closed her eyes and listened past the creek, past her own heartbeat, into the grinding. "An hour. Less if the sun hits the snowfield."

Coral didn't shout. That was the thing about him — even now, she'd remember, even at the worst moment, he never shouted. He turned and his voice carried anyway, flat and hard as the stone under their feet: "Juno. Mules forward, now, don't stop for anything. Tam, rear guard, keep them moving. Ada—" he touched the strongbox strap on her shoulder, "—stay between us."

He looked at Wren last. "How far to the way out?"

"Two miles. There's a side cut where the canyon forks — high ground, solid rock. We can climb out there."

"Then you lead. Fast as you can."

She led.

* * *

Later — much later, lying under a tarp on the high ground with her clothes steaming dry and her hands still shaking — Wren would try to put the next twenty minutes in order and fail. Memory broke it into pieces, the way the flood broke the canyon into pieces, and the pieces never fit back together the same way twice.

The mules' hooves ringing faster. The creek rising around their ankles, then their knees, cold enough to burn. The light in the gorge going strange and greenish as the blade of sun hit the western wall. Tam bellowing at the pack mule that balked, and the mule, incredibly, obeying him. The grinding upstream getting louder, closer, becoming a roar.

And Coral.

She saw it happen and could not stop it and would see it forever: Coral at the rear with Tam, pushing the last mule up the fork, when the wall of water came around the bend.

It didn't look like water. That was the lie of it, the trap. It looked brown and solid, a moving hillside, carrying whole pines and slabs of bank and a boulder the size of a cabin in its teeth, and it filled the canyon wall to wall and came on at the speed of a falling thing. Tam screamed — Wren had never heard Tam scream — and Coral shoved the mule up the side cut with both hands, with his whole body, and the flood took him the way the sea takes a man overboard: instantly, completely, without malice.

"CORAL!"

Her own voice. Juno's voice. Ada's, cracking on the name.

Then the world was noise and spray, and Wren was climbing, clawing at rock with the others, up the side cut as the flood thundered past below them, higher than a man's head, higher than two men, tearing the trail they'd walked to pieces behind them like paper.

And Coral was gone.

* * *

"No." Juno was on her feet on the ledge, scanning the brown torrent below. "No, no, no — he's got air in his coat, he floats, he floats, WREN, where does it open out?"

Wren's mind did what her mother had trained it to do: went cold, went quick, went to work. "Three miles down. The canyon opens into the valley at the old burn — wide gravel flats, the water spreads out, slows down." She was already moving, scrambling up the goat path that switchbacked along the rim. "There's a trail along the top. We can run it faster than the water runs the bottom. Barely."

"Then we run," Tam said. His shoulder was bad — everyone knew it, nobody said it — but he was already running.

They ran.

Wren ran the rim trail with her lungs full of knives and the flood roaring in the gorge below her, and she thought, absurdly, of the wreck stories Coral told late at night when he thought she was asleep — the grey water, the navy line he dove anyway, the hull plate with the impossible number. A man who spent his life going down into dark water after things the world wanted lost. Of course the water had come for him. Of course it had. Some debts follow a man uphill.

The gorge opened. The valley opened. The flood spread across the gravel flats in a hundred braided channels, slowing, spreading, shedding its trees and boulders like a runner shedding clothes — and there, snagged against a fallen spruce in the shallows, facedown and motionless, a dark shape in a soaked canvas coat.

They reached him together and pulled him out and he was not breathing.

What happened next, Wren watched from very close and very far away at once. Juno rolled him and started compressions with the flat mechanical fury of a woman who had done this before and refused to do it a last time. Tam knelt and pressed his forehead to Coral's wet hair and said something low and urgent that Wren couldn't hear and somehow understood anyway. Ada held the strongbox in her lap like ballast and prayed, or counted, or both.

"Come on," Juno snarled, between pushes. "Come *on*. You son of a — you dragged me off that boat, you don't get to — breathe, damn you, *breathe*—"

Coral coughed.

It was the ugliest sound Wren had ever heard and she would treasure it the rest of her life. Water came out of him in a gray rush, and then air, ragged and horrible and wonderful, and his eyes opened — that deep-water color, clouded now, unfocused — and found Juno's face first, then Tam's, then, last, Wren's.

He tried to speak. Coughed again. Tried again.

"Map," he rasped.

Juno made a sound halfway between a laugh and a sob and smacked his shoulder. "The map's dry. Ada kept it inside her coat the whole run. You absolute *idiot*—"

"Good," Coral whispered, and his eyes closed, and he was breathing, just breathing, alive on the gravel with the flood flattening out around him into ordinary water, and Wren sat down hard in the shallows and let the cold take her and shook and shook and could not stop.

* * *

They made camp early, on the high bank above the flats, because nobody suggested going on and nobody had to. Juno built the fire herself, which she never did, and made it huge. Tam rigged a windbreak from the spruce the flood had kindly delivered. Ada got Coral out of his wet coat and into blankets and sat with him, and Wren watched the two of them talking in low voices — Ada's hand resting on his arm, Coral looking at her with an expression Wren had never seen on him, stripped bare, younger than his years.

Wren hung her soaked clothes on branches and sat wrapped in a horse blanket with her knees up, and found Tam settle onto the log beside her without a word. For a while they just watched the fire.

"You heard it," he said finally. "Before anyone. Before the water even moved."

"She taught me. My mother." Wren picked at a loose thread on the blanket. "Everybody always thinks knowing things is reading books. It's not. It's listening. You listen long enough, the country tells you what it's going to do." She hesitated. "I told them it wouldn't flood today. I was wrong."

Tam shook his head slowly. "You were right. You bought us the hour that kept us all alive. You were standing right there when the mountain changed its mind." He looked at her sideways, and his eyes were kind in a way that his gruffness usually hid. "Fifteen years old and you saved eight lives today. Don't you dare apologize to me for arithmetic like that."

Eight lives. Wren turned the number over. She'd counted six — no, seven, counting herself. Then she followed Tam's glance to the far side of the fire, where Juno sat sharpening her knife with unnecessary force, glaring at the sleeping Coral as if fury were the only safe alternative to what was obviously underneath.

Eight. Because Juno had chosen him, back on the dock, and had never once pretended otherwise.

"He scared us," Wren said.

"He did worse than scare us." Tam fed a branch to the fire. Sparks climbed. "He let us find out we need him. And worse than that—" the ghost of a smile, "—he let us find out he needs us. Man like Coral would rather drown than admit it. Nearly did both."

Across the fire, Coral stirred, woke, and looked around the circle — at Juno, at Tam, at Ada, at Wren — doing the thing he always did, counting his people, checking his lines. Diver's habit, Wren thought. Count breaths, trust the rope. Except the rope wasn't rope anymore. It was this. It was them.

His eyes found hers and held, and he didn't say thank you, because he was Coral, and neither did she, because she was Wren, and it was somehow the fullest conversation she'd had in months.

She slept better than she had since the boathouse.

* * *

She woke before dawn to voices, low and urgent, and lay still in the dark and listened, because listening was what she did.

"—saddle tracks on the rim trail," Tam was saying. "Fresh. Shod horses, four of them, moving fast. They cut for sign at the flood line and they found it."

"They'll have seen our fire," Juno said.

"They'll have seen the smoke for miles."

A pause. Then Coral's voice, rougher than yesterday, scraped raw by the water, but steady — steady the way a fixed rope is steady.

"Then we don't give them a trail to find. Wren."

Wren sat up. Across the dead coals, his eyes caught the first gray light.

"You said you know this country. How much of it?"
