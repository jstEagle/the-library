## 11. The Cache Above the Treeline

The trees gave up before the mountain did.

Ada Voss had known they would — her father's letters had described the treeline the way other men described a border, a line drawn by cold and stubbornness where the world simply stopped trying. But knowing it and walking into it were different things. One moment there were whitebark pines twisted like old men arguing with the wind, and the next there was only stone and sky and a silence so complete she could hear her own blood moving.

She stopped at the edge of it and looked back down the trail. Coral came first, moving with that peculiar economy of his, as if he were still swimming through air instead of climbing through it. Juno behind him, pack straps creaking. Tam favoring his left side in a way he probably thought nobody noticed, which meant everybody noticed. Wren last, light as a bird on the scree, turning every few steps to read their backtrail out of habit.

Five of them now, strung along a slope that no sane person climbed for any reason but greed or grief. Ada supposed she was carrying both.

"Rest," Coral said, and it wasn't a suggestion. He'd learned somewhere in the last hundred miles that she would drive herself past sense if nobody made her stop, and he'd appointed himself the one who made her stop. She resented it and was grateful for it in equal measure, which she was beginning to understand was simply what it felt like to be part of this company.

They ate hardtack and dried apples and drank snowmelt gone iron-cold in the canteens. Wren lay on her back and named constellations wrong on purpose until Tam corrected her, and then argued with him about whether correction counted as cheating. The small ritual of it steadied Ada more than the food did. Her father had been a man who talked to fill silences too — engineering jokes, terrible ones, told over supper while her mother rolled her eyes. She had spent twenty years thinking those suppers were ordinary. Now she knew they had been borrowed time, all of them, everyone at that table living on credit the navy intended to collect.

"We're close," she said, when the sky went from pewter to slate. "Another hour. Maybe less."

Coral looked at her a long moment. "You've never actually been here."

"No." She folded the map along its worn creases — Merrick Hale's grease-stained hand, her father's careful annotations copied onto it in pencil so faint she sometimes had to tilt it toward the light. "But I've read his letters four hundred times. He describes this valley the way a man describes his own face. There's a split rock like a door standing open. Below it, a cairn. He said—" her voice caught, and she let it, because these people didn't flinch from grief and pretending would insult them, "—he said whoever found it should know the ground was chosen. High enough that the snow wouldn't crush it. Low enough that a man carrying a box could reach it in one day's climb from the pass road. He built it to be found by *someone*. Just not by everyone."

"And the navy never found it," Juno said.

"The navy never looked. Why would they? They sank the evidence." Ada stood, brushing grit from her knees. "You don't dig up a thing you buried yourself."

* * *

They climbed the last stretch in the blue hour, when the rock gave back the last of the light and everything looked carved rather than grown. Ada's lungs burned. The air up here was thin and clean and tasted of nothing at all, and she thought: *he breathed this.* Her father had walked this same slope with forty pounds of sealed steel on his back, in autumn, alone, knowing what he carried and why. Knowing that the men who'd ordered the sinking might already be looking for him.

The split rock appeared exactly as the letters promised — two slabs of granite leaning apart like a door ajar, black seam between them running down into shadow. And below it, half-swallowed by decades of frost-heave and lichen, the cairn.

Ada knelt. Her hands shook so badly the first three stones she moved accomplished nothing, and Wren crouched beside her without a word and began lifting rocks with the calm efficiency of someone who understood that some work is easier done than watched. Stone by stone the cairn came apart, and beneath it, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, bound with wire corroded green —

A strongbox.

It was smaller than she had imagined. That was the first thing. Twenty years of carrying this secret inside herself, twenty years of the map and the promise and the name *Voss* meaning something in certain rooms she'd never been invited into, and the whole weight of it fit in a box she could have hugged to her chest. Steel, double-sealed, the hinges waxed against rust. Her father's initials punched into the lid corner with a center punch, crooked, hurried.

E.V.

Edmund Voss. Chief engineer's assistant, third class, merchant marine certification, age thirty-four at the time of his death — except he hadn't died, not that night, not with the others. He had lived another eleven months just long enough to carry the truth up a mountain and bury it under stones, and then he had died anyway, of something the navy's records would list as pneumonia and Ada's heart listed as a broken thing that could not be mended.

Nobody spoke. Even Wren had gone still.

"Open it," Coral said quietly.

Ada's fingers found the wire. It snapped with a sound very loud in that silence.

* * *

Inside, wrapped in waxed canvas and tar paper, packed with wool greased against the damp: notebooks. Dozens of them. Engineering logs in her father's small, upright hand, the same hand that had written her letters, written *be good to your mother, little wren* — no, that had been a different word, a different bird, but the handwriting was identical, and that was the thing that undid her. She had expected documents. She had not expected her father.

She sat down right there on the frozen ground with the first logbook open across her knees, and the company let her, forming a loose ring around her with their backs to the wind.

The early pages were ordinary. Boiler pressures, fuel consumption, vibration readings from the number three shaft. A ship's heartbeat recorded in ink. Then, dated the ninth of March, twenty-two years ago, the entries changed.

*Test firing scheduled 0400. Civilian observers aboard per contract requirement. I am not comfortable. The weapon is not ready. Mr. Aldous says the schedule is not negotiable.*

Ada read aloud, because she couldn't not, because the words had been locked in steel above the treeline for twenty years and they deserved air:

*"Third test firing. Containment failure in the breech assembly. Fire on the loading deck, suppressed in eleven minutes. Two men burned. The observers took notes and said nothing. I asked Mr. Aldous what happens if it fails during the demonstration and he looked at me as though I had asked him what happens if the sun rises."*

Page after page. The weapon — she had pieced its shape together over years of rumor and redacted newspaper, but seeing it named in her father's plain engineer's shorthand made it real in a way rumor never could — a naval gun of a new order, tested aboard a converted hull, tested again and again because each failure was supposed to be fixed by the next test, and each test required witnesses, and the witnesses were contract men and junior officers who could be trusted to keep quiet because their careers depended on it.

And then, the last week:

*"The demonstration has been moved forward. Full admiralty review aboard. Forty souls additional. I have checked the breech assembly myself twice and the flaw is structural. It cannot be fixed by Thursday. I have said so in writing. My memorandum was returned to me with no comment."*

Ada's voice failed on the next line. Coral took the book gently from her hands and read it aloud in his diver's flat calm, which somehow made it worse and better at once:

*"I believe they know. I believe the test is intended to fail. I believe we are the failure."*

The wind moved over the stones. Somewhere below, a mule stamped.

"There's more," Juno said. She'd taken a second logbook and was reading fast, her lips moving. "After. After the demonstration. Listen — *'Forty-one dead. The official account is an ammunition accident. This morning I was instructed to sign the salvage survey confirming total loss and to certify that recovery operations are impracticable. The wreck lies in ninety fathoms. Nothing is impracticable at ninety fathoms. They are going to leave them there.'*" Juno looked up. "He's describing your wreck, Coral. Your water. Your serial number."

"I know," Coral said. His voice had gone strange and quiet. "The plate I pulled. The stamp that shouldn't exist. It exists because the ship was re-registered before the demonstration — refitted, renumbered, so the accident report would describe a vessel that never served. They didn't just sink her. They erased her first."

"They killed forty people to hide a weapons program that didn't work," Tam said. He'd gone grey around the mouth. In the army, Ada thought, he'd have seen how men were used like this. "And then they killed the ship's name to hide the killing."

"And my father carried the proof of it up a mountain," Ada said, "and died of it."

She hadn't meant to say that last part aloud. It came out anyway, into the thin cold air, and hung there, and nobody tried to take it back or soften it, and she loved them a little for that.

* * *

They made camp in the lee of the split rock, because none of them could face descending in the dark and because, though nobody said it, the box seemed to deserve a night spent nearby. Coral rigged the tarp. Wren built a fire small enough to hide in a hollow. Tam took first watch without being asked, sitting where he could watch the backtrail, his bad shoulder angled away from the firelight.

Ada read logs by firelight until her eyes ached. There were forty names in the final notebook — her father had written them out, every man lost, rank and rating and home port, in a list headed *FOR THE RECORD, SINCE THE RECORD WILL NOT BE MADE.* She read them one by one. Thomas Okafor, stoker, Port Sallow. Henrik Dahl, gunner's mate. A boy of seventeen listed as apprentice, R. Calloway. On and on, forty lives in her father's careful upright hand, and at the bottom, unlisted because he was writing the list, Edmund Voss had left a space and drawn a single line through it, and beside it written: *and the one who carried this, who asks no place on it.*

"You should sleep," Coral said. He crouched beside her, and in the firelight his face was the face of a man who had spent his life pulling things out of dark water and had just learned what the darkest water held.

"So should you."

"I don't sleep much before a dive." He turned a page of the notebook with one finger, careful, as if the paper might bruise. "My mother used to say the sea keeps what it's given. I used to think that meant wrecks. Cargo. Now I think she meant this. Everything anyone ever dropped into the deep comes back up eventually. It's just a question of who's holding the rope."

"Then we're holding it," Ada said.

"Yes." He looked at her. "And you understand what that means. What happens when we bring this down the mountain. There's no putting it back under the stones. Not for us. We'll be carrying it the rest of our lives — Corliss, the navy, everyone who needs this to stay buried, they'll all of them be pulling on the other end."

"I have spent twenty years being the daughter of a dead man in an unnamed accident." Ada closed the notebook. The truth of it settled over her, heavy as ballast, heavier — and underneath the heaviness, unmistakable, a kind of terrible lift, like a held breath finally let go. "I have been polite about it. Patient. I kept a roadhouse and poured whiskey for men I suspected and smiled at officers I despised, waiting for a map to wash into the right hands. Vindication is supposed to feel like triumph, Coral. Nobody warned me it feels like this. Like finding out the grave is exactly as deep as you feared, and having to climb down into it anyway, and carry it back up."

"That's salvage," Coral said. "Everything worth raising weighs more than you want it to."

She almost laughed. Outside the firelight, Wren murmured in her sleep, and Tam's silhouette didn't move against the stars, and Juno's breathing went slow and even, and Ada understood suddenly that this — this ring of strangers on a frozen mountain, guarding a box of dead men's names — was the first family her father's truth had ever had. The navy had given it silence. The syndicate had given it a price. These five had given it witnesses.

That was all vindication ever was, maybe. Witnesses.

She slept, eventually, with the strongbox under her arm like a child, and dreamed of her father climbing this same slope with the wire biting his palms, and woke before dawn with the certainty, cold and complete as the frost on the tarp, that they were being watched.

She said nothing at first. She lay still and listened — wind, mule, the tick of cooling stone. Then, from the darkness below the camp, faint but unmistakable, carried up the clear mountain air:

A voice. More than one. And the thin metallic note of a rifle bolt being worked, patient as a man counting money.

Ada put her hand on the strongbox and reached across to wake Coral, and found his eyes already open, already fixed on the dark below the rocks, already listening.
