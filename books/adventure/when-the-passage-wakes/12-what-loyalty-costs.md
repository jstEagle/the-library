## 12. What Loyalty Costs

The valley narrowed the way a throat does before it swallows.

Coral felt it before he saw it — that diver's instinct, the one that had kept him alive through ten years of black water and bad air. Something upstream of them was wrong. The light had changed. The birds had stopped. The mules' ears were pinned flat, and Tam, riding drag with his arm bound tight against his chest, had gone quiet in the way that meant he was listening with his whole body.

They came around a shoulder of granite streaked with old avalanche scree, and there they were: six riders strung across the valley floor like beads on a wire, navy blue under grey wool, carbines resting easy across their saddles. Behind them, half-hidden in the shadow of an overhang, sat a patrol wagon and two more men holding horses. A lieutenant stepped forward out of their line, leading his mount, and Coral knew him at once.

Lieutenant Commander Aldous Vane. He'd worn the same face three years ago across a salvage office desk, when he'd taken Coral's license with a signature and a sentence about *unauthorized recovery operations*. He looked older now. Colder at the eyes. The kind of man who had learned to enjoy his work or had stopped pretending not to.

"Zhou." Vane said it like a man greeting an old debt. "You always did have a talent for being exactly where you shouldn't."

Coral held up a fist and the company halted. Twenty yards of open ground between them — scree and thin grass, no cover worth the name. He counted the carbines again. Six. Maybe eight with the wagon men. Against five, one of them wounded, one of them sixteen years old.

"Vane." He kept his voice level, the way you keep your breathing level at forty fathoms. "Long way from salt water."

"The mountains belong to the service too." Vane dismounted, unhurried, and handed his reins to a trooper. That was the thing about him — he never hurried, because hurry was for men who feared outcomes. "You've been a busy man, Zhou. Harrowgate. Sawtooth Pass. A burn scar full of false trails that cost my trackers two days. And now this." His gaze moved past Coral, deliberate as a searchlight, touching Ada, Juno, Wren, the mule carrying the strongbox lashed under oilcloth. "A federal judge in Verity. That's who you're running those logs to, isn't it? Ada Voss's little crusade. Her father's little strongbox."

Coral said nothing. Beside him he heard Ada's breath go shallow, and behind him Wren shift her weight in the saddle. Don't confirm. Don't deny. Let him spend his words first.

Vane smiled without warmth. "Here's what happens now. You give me the box, the girl, and the map, and you walk away. All of you walk away. That's the generous version."

"And the other version?"

"The other version is classified." Vane's smile didn't move. "But I'm not here for the other version. I'm here because someone above me has decided you're worth more alive than dead. Which surprises me, frankly, given your record."

He reached into his coat and produced a folded paper, cream-colored, official, the seal broken and re-sealed with wax that gleamed dull red in the flat mountain light. He held it out.

"Amnesty," Vane said. "Full reinstatement of your salvage license. Berth rights on the *Meridian* wreck — the whole field, not the scraps they let you pick at. Your claim back, Zhou. The one they seized. Every plate, every fitting, every ounce of brass the sea owes you. Signed by an admiral. All you have to do is hand over a box of paper that belongs in a government archive anyway, and forget the name of a ship nobody remembers."

*The Meridian.* Coral's hands went cold inside his gloves. Not the nameless wreck — the real one, the mother lode, the hull he'd spent ten years diving blind around because the navy drew its cordon buoys in a circle and shot at anything that crossed it. Ten years of grey water and half-lungs and watching other men get rich off honest claims while he picked the margins of a forbidden grave.

And all it would cost was forty names.

He could see it so clearly it hurt: the license framed on the boathouse wall again. Juno laughing on the deck of a proper boat. No more running. No more looking over his shoulder at syndicate riders and navy patrols. Just work, honest and deep and his own.

"Coral." Ada's voice was quiet beside him. She hadn't moved. She was watching him the way she'd watched her father's ledger pages — reading, not pleading. "It's your choice. It was always going to be your choice."

That was the worst part. She wasn't going to make it for him.

He thought of Merrick Hale dying mid-sentence in the boathouse, grease-stained map pressed into his hands, the promise still warm between them like something just pulled from a forge. He thought of the serial number stamped into a hull plate that shouldn't exist, the water keeping secrets, the navy cordoning off a grave and calling it security. He thought of forty men who had gone to work one morning on a ship their own government had already decided to drown, and whose families had been told *lost at sea, cause unknown*, and who had waited thirty years for a truth that fit in a strongbox the size of a bread oven.

He thought of the moment, three days ago in the cache above the treeline, when Ada had opened that strongbox and read her father's handwriting aloud, and her voice had cracked on the word *witnesses*, and every one of them had understood at once that they were carrying something heavier than cargo.

"You're offering me my life back," Coral said.

"I'm offering you your future back."

"They're not the same thing." He swung down from the saddle. His boots crunched on the scree, loud in the silence. He walked forward, alone, until ten yards separated him from Vane and the paper in his hand. Up close he could see the admiral's seal, the fine clerk's handwriting, the whole machinery of the state reduced to a single page. "I knew a man named Hale. Prospector. Half-dead when I found him. He gave me a map and made me swear an oath with his last breath, and I swore it, and I don't break oaths to drowning men." He paused. "I also knew forty men I never met. They drowned on a ship your service sank on purpose, with witnesses aboard, to hide a failed test. Their families got a form letter. I'm carrying the correction."

Vane's face didn't change, but something behind it did — a flicker, quickly banked, like a lamp turned down. "You're a salvage diver, Zhou. You pull things out of the water. You don't understand what's in that box. Weapons programs. Budgets. Careers. Men far above both of us decided the country is safer with that ship on the bottom, and they were right. Nothing you do changes what happened. All you can change is how many people die learning about it."

"That's the second time someone's told me that," Coral said. "The first one tried to buy me too. Then he burned a roadhouse down around us." He took another step. Close enough now to take the paper if he wanted it. "You want to know what I learned underwater, Vane? The sea doesn't keep secrets. People do. The sea just holds them until somebody's brave enough to go down and get them. And every year I dove that wreck, I could feel it — the truth down there, pressing on the hull plates, waiting. Your cordon buoys didn't bury it. They just marked where it was."

He reached out — and pushed the paper back against Vane's chest, gently, almost courteously.

"No," Coral said. "Keep your amnesty. Keep your license. I'll dive grey water the rest of my life before I sell forty dead men for a berth on their grave."

The silence that followed lasted three heartbeats. Coral counted them, the way he counted breaths at depth. One. Two. Three.

Then Vane sighed, and folded the paper away, and looked genuinely, terribly sorry.

"I was afraid you'd say that." He turned his head slightly. "Sergeant. Disperse them."

The world came apart.

The first carbine shot went high — a warning, or a miss, Coral never learned which — and then the valley was full of thunder. Scree jumped around his boots like startled fish. Somewhere behind him a mule screamed, a terrible human sound, and Juno was shouting his name, and Ada was dragging at his sleeve, and he realized he was standing frozen in the open like a man watching a surface close overhead.

*Move.*

He ran. Bullets kicked dust off the rocks around him, whining away downslope in ricochet songs. He hit the ground behind a granite rib the size of a boat hull and rolled, and Juno crashed down beside him, cheeks bloodied where a stone chip had opened them, rifle already out and working.

"They're not trying to kill us!" she shouted over the din. "They're herding us!"

She was right. He saw it in a heartbeat — the firing pattern, walking along the ridge lines, driving them east, away from the trail to Verity, away from the low route, up and toward—

"The narrows," Tam rasped from cover further back, his face grey with pain. "They're pushing us into the cliffside narrows. Dead-end country. They'll bottle us up and starve us out, or worse."

Wren was already moving, small and fast and low, cutting the strongbox free of the screaming mule with a knife while bullets chewed the rocks above her head. Ada hauled the box onto her shoulder like a woman who had carried grief for thirty years and found it lighter than expected. Coral fired twice — not to kill, just to make the nearest troopers keep their heads down — and the company broke east, running, stumbling, sliding down scree toward the dark slot in the canyon wall where the map ended and the unmapped country began.

Behind them, unhurried as ever, Lieutenant Commander Aldous Vane remounted his horse and did not pursue. He didn't need to. The mountains would do his work for him. Coral looked back once, at the top of the slope — the blue-coated line, the wagon, the man watching him with an expression that was not triumph but arithmetic — and then the narrows swallowed them, and the light went from grey to none.

They climbed for an hour in the failing afternoon, up switchbacks no goat had approved, the strongbox passed hand to hand like a hot coal, until the shooting faded below and behind them and only wind remained. Finally, in the lee of an overhang dusted with the season's first snow, Coral called a halt.

They were five, breathing hard, bleeding a little, alive. The strongbox sat among them, dented at one corner where a bullet had kissed it, its seal unbroken.

Juno cleaned the stone cut on her cheek and didn't complain. Wren checked the mule's legs with gentle hands. Tam sat propped against the rock, white-lipped, hiding his shoulder the way he hid everything. Ada knelt beside the strongbox with her palm flat on the dented metal, as if taking a pulse.

"He offered you everything," she said quietly. It wasn't a question.

"He offered me everything," Coral agreed.

"And you said no."

"I said no."

Ada looked up at him, and in the last violet light of the day her eyes were wet and fierce at once. "My father wrote in his last log entry that someone would come. That someone would choose the dead over themselves. I thought it was a father's hope talking." Her voice caught. "It wasn't."

Coral looked out over the drop, where the valley they'd fled lay filling with blue dusk, and somewhere down in that dusk a patrol was making camp, patient as tide. Above them, the unmapped country rose into peaks that no chart admitted to, and the first snows were coming down thicker now, fat and silent, erasing their back-trail flake by flake.

"We've lost the road to Verity," Juno said, spreading the map on her knee and studying it by the last light. "Vane's cut us off from the low route. If we're still getting those logs to the judge, there's only one way left."

Coral followed her finger to the top edge of Hale's grease-stained map, where the ink trailed off into guesswork and a single handwritten note in the prospector's shaky hand:

*Kestrel Pass. Crossable till deep winter. After that, the mountain keeps what it takes.*

Above them, the pass waited — high, white, and utterly indifferent to what any of them had promised anyone.

"Then we climb," Coral said.
