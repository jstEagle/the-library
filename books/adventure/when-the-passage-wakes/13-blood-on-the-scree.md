## 13. Blood on the Scree

Tam Voss had been shot at in three countries and shot in two of them, and he had learned long ago that the first thing a bullet does is lie to you.

This one told him he was fine.

He heard it arrive — a flat wet slap, like a stone thrown into mud — and felt it punch through the meat above his left shoulder blade, and his body did what bodies do when they have been trained by worse men than God: it kept running. The scree field tilted and slid beneath his boots, grey shale pouring downslope in long whispering sheets, and Tam ran on it the way he'd run on river ice as a boy, weight forward, never stopping, because stopping was how the mountain ate you.

Behind him, rifle fire cracked off the rocks and came back doubled, tripled, the echoes confusing everything — which was the only reason any of them were still alive. Echo country. He'd chosen this route for exactly that reason, back when choosing routes had been an academic exercise instead of a prayer with legs.

"Tam!" Wren's voice, high and thin from somewhere to his right. "TAM!"

"Keep moving!" His own voice came out wrong, gravelly, half-swallowed. "Don't stop for me!"

"I'm not stopping for *you*, I'm stopping because there's a cliff!"

There was, indeed, a cliff.

It rose out of the scree like the shoulder of something buried, banded red and black rock streaked with old snowmelt, and it ran north and south as far as he could see through the dust of their own passage. To the east, downslope, the syndicate riders were working along the treeline in ones and twos, patient as crows, herding rather than hunting. Corliss hadn't spent good money on amateurs. These men knew that a running man on loose stone makes mistakes, and all you have to do is wait for the mountain to make him make them.

Tam skidded to a halt beside Wren and put his back against the cliff's base, and only then — with the wall holding him up, though he'd have denied it to his grave — did he let himself look at the wound.

Left shoulder, high, just below the joint. Entry hole small and dark, soaking through his coat in a bloom that was already cold in the wind. No exit that he could feel. That meant the ball was still in there, which meant it hadn't touched anything that mattered going in, or he'd be dead, but it also meant it was sitting in there like a bad guest, and every jarring stride had been driving it deeper into muscle that his whole life depended on.

He'd taken worse, he told himself. He'd taken a bayonet at Kettle Ridge that had opened his thigh to the bone, and he'd walked eleven miles on it, and the army had given him a commendation and then a court-martial in the same month, so army opinions of his endurance were worth nothing now anyway.

"You're hit." Wren stood in front of him, breathing hard, sixteen years old and furious about it, wind-whipped hair full of shale dust. Her eyes went to the blood and stayed there. "You're hit and you're doing the face."

"What face?"

"The face where you decide nobody gets to know." She said it without heat, the way she said most true things. "Juno does it too. Coral does it worst of anybody. It's like traveling with a family of locked doors."

*Family.* The word landed somewhere under his ribs and sat there, warm and unwelcome. Three weeks ago these people had been strangers he'd attached himself to out of shame and habit — a disgraced tracker following a diver and a dockhand because he had nowhere else his skills were wanted and no name worth using anywhere else. Now a teenage runaway was reading his face like a scout read sign, and calling it family, and meaning it.

"Bullet's in," he said. "High left shoulder. I can still shoot, still walk. That's what matters."

"That's not all that matters."

"It's all that matters today." He pushed off the cliff wall, tested the arm — it answered, sluggish, a hot wire of pain from elbow to neck, but it answered — and looked north along the base of the cliffs. "The pass road is east of us and they own it. We go north. There's country up there nobody maps because nobody's stupid enough to go."

"Nobody's stupid enough," Wren repeated. "Great. Lead on."

* * *

They found the others in a fold of ground half a mile on: Ada kneeling over the strongbox with her arms around it like it was a child, Coral and Juno covering the slope behind, both of them breathing in the controlled way of people who have decided panic is a luxury item.

Coral saw him first. Tam watched the diver's eyes do it — flick to the shoulder, to the blood, to the way Tam held the left arm slightly away from his body, and Tam saw him decide, deliberately, to say nothing in front of Ada. Coral had that kind of courtesy. It was one of the things that made Tam trust him more than he trusted himself.

"Report," Coral said quietly.

"Six riders visible, maybe eight total. They're not pushing. They're pushing us." Tam knelt, wincing as the shoulder took his weight wrong, and dragged a stick through the shale dust to sketch the country as he'd read it. "Pass road runs there. They'll have a picket on it by dark if they don't already. We can't fight eight with four guns and no position. So we don't take the road."

Ada looked up. Wind had stripped her face down to its bones; she looked older than she had in Harrowgate, and harder, and Tam thought: *that's what twenty years of carrying your father's ghost does to a woman.* "Then where?"

"There." He drew the line north, along the cliff base, up into the jumble of peaks beyond. "Unmapped. Old burn country, then granite, then whatever's past granite. No trails. No water we know of. Cold enough at night to kill a careless man."

"And you know this how?" Juno asked.

"I don't. Nobody does. That's the point." Tam met each of them in turn. "I'm telling you the truth the way a tracker tells it: I can get us lost in country where they can't follow, or I can keep us found in country where they can. Lost is living. Found is dying. Pick."

Coral picked fastest, the way he picked everything, with a diver's arithmetic — depth, air, bottom time. "Lost," he said. "We've got food for six days if we're mean about it. After Sawtooth we're already mean about it. Go."

They went.

* * *

The unmapped country began gently, which Tam distrusted immediately.

Gentle country was how mountains lied. They gave you a meadow, a stream, an easy grade, let your guard down and your feet soften, and then presented the bill all at once. He led them north along the cliff base through lodgepole pine blackened by some fire older than Wren, the trunks standing like charred soldiers at parade rest, the ground between them soft with ash and lupine. His shoulder kept its own time. Every hundred yards it sent up a fresh report — a hot pulse, then a deep grinding ache, then a strange cold numbness that frightened him more than the pain, because pain meant the arm was arguing and numbness meant it was starting to give up.

He catalogued it the way he'd been taught to catalogue everything, in the flat voice of the army manual in his head: *wound, through-and-through unlikely, ball retained, bleeding moderate, seepage continuing, range of motion sixty percent and falling, fever onset unknown, prognosis—*

He shut the manual. Prognosis was a word for officers and priests.

What mattered was that Wren kept drifting closer.

She didn't announce it. She did it the way water does — imperceptibly, constantly, always finding the low place. When they stopped to breathe, she stopped near him. When the trail narrowed, she was behind him. When he stumbled once on a root — once, only once, and he covered it as kicking a stone — she was suddenly at his elbow with her hand hovering an inch from his sleeve, not touching, just waiting, the way you hover a hand over a candle flame you suspect is guttering.

"You're favoring it," she said, low, when Ada and the others had drawn ahead around a fallen trunk.

"I'm walking."

"You're walking *crooked*. Your left side doesn't swing. I've been watching you move for three weeks, Tam. You move like a cat that's bored. Right now you move like a cat that got kicked."

Tam almost laughed, and the laugh turned into a grunt of pain, and he hated that she heard it. "Go on ahead. Tell Coral I'm scouting our backtrail."

"You want me to lie for you."

"I want you to let me manage my own business."

Wren stopped walking. She planted herself in front of him in the middle of the burn scar, small and immovable among the dead trees, and looked up at him with those gray eyes that had seen too much too young, and said: "My mother hid how sick she was for a year. Hid it from everybody. Said managing her own business. And when it finally came out, there wasn't anybody left who'd had time to love her while they could, because she'd spent all their time teaching them not to look." Her chin trembled once and steadied. "So I look. That's my business. You don't get to take it away from me too."

The wind moved through the burned pines with a sound like the sea, and Tam Voss, who had faced courts and bullets and his own disgrace without flinching, found he could not meet a sixteen-year-old's eyes.

"Ball's still in," he said finally. "Shoulder. It needs cutting out, and it needs clean hands and boiled steel, and I don't see a surgeon growing on any of these trees. So until we're somewhere it can be done, the smart thing is for nobody to worry, because worry spends strength, and strength is the only supply we've got that doesn't weigh anything."

Wren studied him for a long moment. Then she nodded, once, briskly, and he thought he'd won — and then she said, "Okay. But I'm telling Coral tonight. Not because I'm breaking your secret. Because he's the one who decides how far we push tomorrow, and he can't decide that if you feed him lies. Secrets that get people killed aren't secrets. They're just slow knives."

She turned and trotted ahead, light-footed over the ash, and Tam stood alone among the dead trees and admitted, in the privacy of his own skull, that she was right, and that he would spend the rest of the day trying to think of a way to be righter, and that there wasn't one.

* * *

They camped that night in a hollow beneath an overhang of granite, a fire the size of a cupped hand, no coffee because coffee meant smoke-smell and smoke-smell carried. They ate hard biscuit and a strip of dried elk shared seven ways — six ways, Tam corrected himself, watching Juno divide it, because Juno had quietly been putting the largest pieces on Tam's tin plate for two days and thought nobody noticed. A dockhand's trick. Porters fed their strongest climbers. Juno was keeping her mule sound.

He ate it. He was ashamed of how gratefully he ate it.

Above them the stars came out in the terrible abundance of high country, thick as spilled salt, and below them the valley lay dark and quiet, and somewhere down in that quiet were eight men with rifles and all the patience in the world.

Coral sat down next to him against the rock, close, shoulder to shoulder — the right shoulder, Tam noted, Coral having made the same calculation he would have.

"Wren talked to me," Coral said.

"Did she."

"She used the phrase 'slow knives.' She learned that from you?"

"She learned it from being smarter than me." Tam turned his cup in his fingers. "Coral. Straight answer, soldier to whatever you are. If it comes to a fight tomorrow, put me on the left flank and don't ask me to raise that arm above my heart. I can shoot level. I can't shoot up."

"How bad?"

"Bad enough that I'm giving you straight answers."

Coral was silent a moment. In the firelight his face had the stillness Tam had come to recognize — the diver's stillness, a man counting breaths in dark water. "We find you a doctor in Verity," he said. "That's not a hope, it's a line item. You get us through this country, and the first thing we buy in town isn't food and it isn't ammunition."

"Food's cheaper."

"So are you, apparently. Doesn't matter. You're the line item." Coral knocked his cup against Tam's, a small ceramic click in the vast dark. "Get some sleep. First watch is mine."

Tam slept, or something near it, and dreamed of Kettle Ridge, and woke twice with his heart hammering and his hand finding the rifle stock, and both times Wren was awake across the coals, watching him with her eyes and pretending otherwise, guarding him the way she'd said she would — by looking, relentlessly, refusing to be taught not to.

* * *

On the third day the gentle country presented its bill.

The valley they'd been following narrowed without warning into a gorge where the stream they'd relied on dropped into a chasm and vanished, and the walls closed to a spear's width overhead, and the map — Hale's grease-stained map, which Coral consulted like scripture — showed nothing at all, because no one had ever come here to draw it. Beyond the gorge the terrain broke apart entirely: ridgelines at angles no ridgeline should take, drainages flowing every direction, granite spires leaning like drunks. Country that had shrugged off the last ice age and never bothered to apologize.

Twice Tam climbed to vantage points he had no business climbing with one arm, and twice he read the backtrail and found it empty — no riders, no dust, no glint of glass. Either Corliss had lost them, or Corliss had gotten smart and gone wide, and Tam knew which bet a professional fixer would make.

The fever started on the fourth night.

He felt it come on like weather — a chill under the skin that no fire could reach, a tightness behind the eyes, the world developing a faint cottony edge. He sat close to the flames and let them cook him and said nothing, and when Wren looked over, he made himself hold her gaze steadily and evenly, and he saw her accept the performance with a small grim nod that said: *I know it's a performance. I'll allow it. For now.*

That night he took out his knife, boiled the blade in Juno's tin cup over the fire when the others slept, and probed the wound himself by touch and moonlight, teeth clenched on a strap of leather, and could not reach the ball. His fingers found the channel of the track, hot and swollen, closing, healing *wrong*, sealing the lead inside like a seed in fruit. He bandaged it, packed the dirt away from the fire, and lay looking up at the indifferent stars, and did the arithmetic he'd been refusing to do.

Five days to Verity, if the country allowed it. Maybe six. Fever like this, in a retained ball, in cold country, on short rations —

He'd seen men last nine days. He'd seen men last two.

*Tam,* he told himself, in the flat voice of the manual, *you are now navigating by a clock that only you can hear, and the others cannot know the hour.*

Except one of them already did.

Because when he rolled over, stiff and burning, Wren was sitting up against the pack saddles, wrapped in her blanket, watching him with eyes that had caught the firelight, and she didn't pretend to be asleep, and she didn't say anything either. She just reached over, took his empty tin cup, filled it from the water skin, and set it by his hand — the way you feed a fire you intend to keep burning all night.

And then, in the grey hour before dawn, when Tam woke shivering and found the chill had deepened into something with teeth, Wren was already shaking Coral awake, and he heard her say, low and urgent and not asking permission anymore:

"Coral. Wake up. It's Tam. We have to get him down off this mountain *now* — and I know a way. Nobody knows it. Nobody's ever mapped it. But I can get us there in three days if you trust me."

Coral sat up in the dark, and Tam, drifting on the edge of the fever, heard him ask the only question that mattered:

"Can you carry a map in your head, Wren? Because we just lost ours."
