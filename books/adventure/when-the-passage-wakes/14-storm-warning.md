## 14. Storm Warning

Juno woke to the smell of snow.

Not the sight of it — there was none yet, only the grey pre-dawn pressing against the canvas of the lean-to — but the smell, cold and mineral and faintly sweet, the way the high country announced itself before it arrived. She had grown up on salt water, where weather came in from the horizon with hours of warning, whole theater productions of cloud and swell. Here the mountains kept their own counsel. They told you nothing until they were already telling you everything.

She lay still a moment, listening. Tam's breathing was shallow but even where he slept propped against his pack, his bandaged shoulder rising and falling beneath the wool blanket Wren had tucked around him twice in the night without being asked. Ada was already gone from her bedroll — Juno could tell by the absence of her particular stillness; the woman slept like a held breath. And Coral —

Coral was standing at the edge of the treeline, facing north, hands jammed in his pockets, watching the sky the way he watched water.

Juno knew that posture. She had spent six years diving wrecks beside him, six years reading the set of his shoulders through a neoprene suit and forty feet of green murk. When Coral stood like that — weight forward, chin slightly down, utterly motionless — it meant he had seen something he didn't like and hadn't yet decided whether to say it aloud. On the *Maren* it meant a current running wrong along a hull. Here it meant whatever it meant about that sky.

She crawled out of the lean-to into air that bit through her shirt like teeth. The pines stood black and silent around the little hollow where they'd camped, and above them the peaks of the Kestrel Range went up into a ceiling of cloud so low and so flat it looked poured rather than formed. No wind. That was the worst part. A storm that announced itself with wind at least gave you the courtesy of a countdown. This silence was just the mountain holding its breath.

"Coral," she said quietly.

He didn't turn. "You feel it too."

"Been feeling it since midnight." She came to stand beside him. Their breath mingled and rose and vanished. "The birds stopped. Did you notice? There were nutcrackers working these trees yesterday morning. Nothing today."

"Barometer's been falling since we broke camp at the waystation." He pulled the battered brass instrument from his coat — salvage, of course, everything Coral owned was salvage, this one off a drowned yacht whose owner had never needed to know a storm was coming — and tilted it toward her. The needle sat well below where it had been two days ago, and it had not stopped moving. "Half an inch overnight. Maybe more. I've seen it drop like that exactly once before."

"The *Vespera*," Juno said.

"The *Vespera*." He said the name without flinching, which was how she knew how much it cost him. "Three days after that reading, she went down with all hands. I only survived because I was in the water already when she did."

They stood together in the cold, and Juno did the thing she was best at, the thing nobody ever thanked her for because it looked like nothing: she thought.

* * *

By full light the whole company had gathered around the small fire Ada allowed them — deadfall only, dry wood only, smoke no higher than the trees, because Ada Voss did not believe in advertising their position even now, especially not now, with Corliss somewhere behind them and the navy somewhere worse than behind. Tam sat wrapped in blankets looking grey around the mouth but present, which was more than he'd managed two days ago. Wren crouched at the fire's edge turning a strip of dried meat over the coals with the focused intensity of someone who had learned young that food was not guaranteed. Ada had the maps out, weighted at the corners with stones.

"All right," Coral said. "Juno?"

It was her cue, and she took it, because this was the other thing she was best at: arithmetic. Numbers didn't care who you were or what you'd promised a dying man. Numbers just waited for you to catch up to them.

"We're here." She put her finger on the map, on the fold-marked hollow below the last stands of pine, where the trail bent east toward the shoulder of the range. "Kestrel Pass is here — thirty-one miles by the route Wren scouted, call it thirty-four with the detour around the scree field. In good conditions, loaded as we are, with Tam walking wounded—" she glanced at him, and he gave her the ghost of a nod, permission granted, "—that's four days. Five if the weather turns."

"And if it turns?" Ada asked. Her voice was level. It was always level. But Juno had watched her read her father's engineering logs by firelight, night after night, and she knew what lived under that levelness.

"If it turns," Juno said, "we don't cross."

She let that sit. Around the fire, nobody moved except Wren, who turned the meat again, mechanically, eyes lifted to Juno's face.

"Look at the sky," Juno went on. "That's not weather coming in. That's winter. The forecast at Verity said first snows in three weeks — everyone said it, the packers, the roadhouse, everyone. But the forecasts are written for valleys. Up here the season doesn't consult the almanac. That cloud deck came over the divide sometime last night, and it isn't passing through. It's settling. Once it settles, it snows, and once it starts snowing up there—" she pointed north, at the invisible pass, "—it doesn't stop until spring. We cross in four days or we find a hole below the treeline and live in it until April."

"April," Tam repeated. His voice was rough. "Seven months. On what food?"

Juno spread her hands. "On less than that. We lost most of the stores at Sawtooth. What's left is nine days of eating careful, twelve of eating desperate. Even if we hunted, even if we fished the lakes before they froze — seven months above the treeline with a man carrying a bullet-torn shoulder and a syndicate hunting party that knows roughly where we are?" She shook her head. "I'm good at numbers, Tam. Those ones don't come out."

"So we go fast," Wren said. It wasn't a question. The girl looked around the fire, and Juno saw something in her face she'd seen only glimpses of before — not the wary street-feral watchfulness, but its opposite, something almost like hope, quickly leashed. "There's a way. Through the canyon country past the scree — I told you about it, the slot canyons. It cuts the distance. Two days instead of four, maybe less if the water's low."

"And if the water's not low?" Coral asked gently.

Wren's jaw set. "Then it's high."

"It's autumn," Juno said. "Snowmelt's done. Flash flood risk is rain, and rain means the storm's already on us, which means we're crossing in it either way." She looked at Coral. "Her shortcut doesn't change the math much. It changes the margin. Four days becomes maybe three and a half. It buys us half a day of grace, and it costs us a canyon system nobody knows but a sixteen-year-old."

"I know it," Wren said, sharp.

"I know you do," Juno said, and meant it, and watched the girl decide to accept that.

Ada had been silent through all of it, her eyes on the map, her finger resting on a point south of their position. Now she spoke. "There's another number nobody's said yet."

Everyone looked at her.

"Corliss." She said the name the way you'd spit out a bad tooth. "He's behind us. How far behind, Tam?"

Tam shifted, winced, settled. "Two days. Maybe three. I cut our sign at the burn scar and laid the false trail south, but Corliss isn't stupid — he'll have unpicked it by now. He's got horses and we've got mules and feet. Call it two days if he pushes."

"And if we take four days to the pass," Ada said, "he reaches us where?"

Juno felt the arithmetic close around her throat before she'd even finished drawing it. She picked up a stick and scratched in the ash at the fire's edge: a line for the trail, a mark for the pass, a second line angling up from behind. "Here. The last mile below the crest. The narrows under the headwall. If he pushes hard and we travel careful, we meet him at the worst ground on the mountain — steep, exposed, nowhere to run and nothing to hide behind."

"And if we push hard," Coral said slowly, "and he travels careful—"

"Then we beat him to the crest by hours and come down the far side while he's still climbing," Juno finished. "Everything depends on speed. Everything. Which brings us back to the sky."

She looked up. The cloud ceiling had come down while they talked. An hour ago it had sat above the peaks; now it draped their summits like wet wool, and the highest ridgelines were simply gone, erased, as though the world ended at eight thousand feet. The air had that pressed, waiting quality of a held breath. Somewhere upslope a single branch cracked under its own accumulating cold.

"Four days," Juno said. "That's what we have. Not four days of good weather — four days, period. After that, Kestrel Pass closes, and it stays closed, and every one of us winters above the treeline or dies trying to get below it. So the question isn't whether we go. The question is how fast, and what we're willing to leave behind to be fast enough."

* * *

They spent the rest of the day making themselves lighter.

It was Coral's idea, and it was harder than it sounded, because everything they carried had already survived one culling at Sawtooth Pass. Juno went through her own kit with the unsentimental efficiency of a woman who had once jettisoned her father's compass to keep a sinking skiff afloat, and it still hurt. The spare dive knife went. The oilskin wrapping for her charts went, replaced by waxed cloth torn from the tent fly. The little tin of lemon drops she'd carried since Harrowgate — a foolish luxury, bought on an impulse she'd never explained to anyone — she held for a long moment, then buried in the ash pit rather than abandon it to whoever came tracking them. Some weights you carried because putting them down cost more than the carrying.

Tam surrendered ammunition he couldn't spare and wouldn't discuss. Ada cut her father's logs out of their protective case and sewed them into a flat packet that rode inside her coat, against her body, and burned the case. Wren, offered the chance to shed anything, shed nothing, and when Coral raised an eyebrow at the girl's pack she said only, "I've carried heavier with less reason," and that was the end of it.

The mules were the real question. Three remained, and each one ate, and each one slowed them on the steep ground. Coral stood looking at them in the grey afternoon light for a long time, and Juno watched him do the same merciless math she'd done at the fire, and watched him arrive at the answer he didn't want.

"We keep two," he said finally. "The dun and the bay. The grey goes loose — we drive her as far as she'll follow, and if she won't follow, she's free."

"She's carried half our food," Ada said.

"She's also half our speed on the switchbacks." Coral rubbed his face. "I know. I know what I'm saying. But a mule that founders on the headwall takes the food and us with it. I'd rather have the food arrive late than never."

Nobody argued. That was the thing about their company, Juno thought, watching them break camp in the failing light — they argued about everything, loudly and often, and then when a decision was made they closed around it like water closing over a stone. Nobody got left. Not people, and — Coral's face said — wherever possible, not mules either.

They made six miles before dark, fast miles, the trail dropping and climbing along the flank of the valley with the river audible somewhere below, louder than it should have been. Juno noted that too, filed it away: the river was up. Snowmelt shouldn't have raised it. Something was feeding it from higher country.

Something white, probably. Already falling.

* * *

The snow began an hour after midnight.

Juno woke to it landing on her face — single flakes at first, drifting down through the pines with a softness that would have been beautiful anywhere else, any other year, any other life. By the time she'd gotten the fire cover over the coals, the flakes were coming thick, and by the time she'd woken the others, the world beyond the lean-to had gone silent and white and depthless.

They stood in a row at the edge of the shelter and watched it fall, five people and two mules, saying nothing. There was nothing to say that the snow wasn't already saying better.

"How much?" Wren asked at last. Her voice was very small in all that whiteness.

Juno held out her hand and caught flakes and watched them melt and did the calculation she'd been dreading, the one she'd hoped the sky would give her more time to avoid. Accumulation rate, temperature, elevation gain ahead, the drift patterns that built up on windward slopes, the cornices that grew silently over the lee sides of ridges until they dropped a ton of snow onto the first unlucky thing beneath them.

"A foot by tomorrow night at this elevation," she said. "More up high. Enough to fill the trail, enough to avalanche the steep pitches, enough to make the pass a lottery." She turned to Coral. "But not enough to stop us today. Not yet. The snow's a warning shot, not the war. We move now, we move all day, we move tomorrow, and we pray the storm's front half is worse than its back half."

"And if it isn't?" Ada asked.

"Then we learn to pray faster."

Coral was already kicking snow off his boots. "Break it down. Hot food, then boots on. We eat walking after this." He paused, and Juno saw him look at each of them in turn — Tam grey and upright, Wren cinching her pack with quick fierce tugs, Ada with her father's truth sewn against her heart, and last of all Juno herself, and in his look she read the old diver's creed they'd never needed to say aloud on the *Maren*: count your breaths, trust your line, keep moving, because the water — or the mountain — does not wait.

"One more thing," Juno said, as they shouldered their loads. "Four days. I want it understood what four days means. It means we don't stop for anything that isn't bleeding. It means we don't argue on the trail. It means when I say move, we move, and when I say run—" she looked up at the white dark swallowing the sky, "—we run."

Tam smiled with the side of his mouth that still worked. "Never took you for the inspirational type, Zhou."

"I'm not inspiring," Coral said. "I'm counting."

And they stepped out into the falling snow, five small dark shapes on a white mountainside, racing a winter that had already left the gate.

Behind them, far below and far behind, unseen in the whiteout, something else was climbing.
