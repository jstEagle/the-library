## 15. The Betrayal

The waystation at Cold Larch sat in the crook of a valley like a bone someone had swallowed and coughed back up. Coral saw it first as a smudge of smoke against the grey of late afternoon, then as a low-roofed building of split timber, its walls silvered by forty winters, a corral of leaning fence, and a stable that listed to the east as though it had been trying to walk away for years and had only now given up. A sign hung above the door on a leather strap gone stiff as bark. The letters had been painted twice in its life, in two different hands, and neither had bothered with the same words.

*Cold Larch Waystation. Meals. Bunks. No Credit.*

"Charming," Juno said, reining in beside him. Her breath came out in a plume that the wind shredded sideways. "I've slept in worse."

"You've slept in worse," Tam said from the rear, where he rode with his left arm strapped tight against his chest and his face the color of old tallow. "You told us about the worse. Twice."

"And you'll hear it again if it keeps you awake in the saddle, soldier."

Ada said nothing. She hadn't said much in two days. She rode with her eyes on the ridgelines the way a woman watches a door she knows someone will come through, and Coral had learned not to mistake her silence for calm. It was arithmetic. She was counting things — days, miles, the weight of the strongbox lashed to the pack mule behind their hired man, Odell Brack.

Odell. Coral looked back at him now, the way he'd been looking back at him for three days without quite knowing why. Odell was a big, soft-spoken man with a beard the color of river clay and hands that handled the mules like they were sleeping children. He'd come recommended by a liveryman in Harrowgate, taken their coin without haggling, and said maybe forty words in six days on the trail, most of them to the animals. There was nothing wrong with him. That was what Coral kept telling himself. There was nothing wrong with him, and the itch between his shoulders was just the cold, just the altitude, just six days of being hunted by men who had already burned one building down around them.

But the itch was there, and Coral Zhou had spent eleven years listening to smaller itches than that. The water had taught him that. The sea never announced a hull breach; it just gave you a feeling, a wrongness in the way the current lay against your skin, and the divers who ignored the feeling were the ones the navy never had to name.

The strongbox rode in a canvas sling on the nearside of Odell's lead mule. Coral could see the corner of it, the dull iron showing through worn canvas, and he made himself look away. Looking at it too often was its own kind of tell.

* * *

Cold Larch had one keeper, a woman named Hesk who was seventy if she was a day and had hands like tree roots and eyes that missed nothing, and she fed them barley stew and dark bread and asked no questions, which Coral decided was the finest hospitality he'd ever been shown. The common room was one room, really — a stove, three tables, a counter, and a staircase to a loft with six bunks. The wind found every gap in the walls and played them like a bad flute.

They ate in a silence that wasn't uncomfortable, because they had passed beyond uncomfortable days ago, into the kind of quiet that only people who trust each other can hold. Wren ate with her boots off and her feet toward the stove, seventeen years old and hollow-cheeked and eating like the food might be recalled. Tam ate slowly, favoring his right hand, and Coral watched him hide the tremor twice and pretended not to. Juno ate and watched the window. Ada ate and watched the door.

It was Wren who said it, around a mouthful of bread, casual as commenting on the weather:

"Odell's gone."

Coral looked up. The loft ladder was visible through the open door to the stable passage. "He's seeing to the mules."

"He saw to them an hour ago. He's not seeing to them now." Wren swallowed. "And his kit's not on his bunk. And he took his coat, and it wasn't cold enough for a man to take his coat to the stable."

The stove ticked. The wind played its flute. Coral set down his spoon and found that his hand was perfectly steady, which surprised him, because everything under his sternum had dropped through the floor.

"The mule," Ada said. Just that. Just the mule.

They went out into the blue dusk together, all six of them, and the corral told the story before anyone had to. Odell's tack was gone from its pegs. The lead mule was gone. Not strayed — the gate was latched from the outside, latched the way a man latches it when he's leading an animal out and doesn't want it followed by another animal. And the sling on the remaining mule's back hung empty and slack, the canvas cut, not unbuckled. Cut with a knife, quick and neat, while a man pretended to check a hoof.

The strongbox. Forty pounds of iron and paper and the murdered truth of forty dead men, gone into the dark with a soft-spoken man and a clay-colored beard.

Coral stood in the corral mud and felt the betrayal arrive in stages, the way cold water arrives in a flooded compartment — ankles first, then knees, then the sudden awful lift of the deck under your feet as the ship decides to go. He thought of Odell's hands on the mules. He thought of the forty words. He thought of every time over six days that he'd looked at the man's back and felt the itch and told himself *there's nothing wrong with him*, and he understood, with a clarity that felt like drowning in reverse, that he had known. He had known and he had wanted to be wrong more than he had wanted to be right, because they were seven days into the high country and a company of outcasts cannot run on suspicion, and so he had taken the wanting and called it judgment.

"Coral." Juno's voice, low. "Say something."

"He's been bought." Coral heard his own voice come out flat and cold, the diver's voice, the voice he used at eighty feet when panic was a luxury with a price. "Bought before Harrowgate, maybe. Maybe he was theirs from the start and the liveryman was the first payment. It doesn't matter. What matters is the box and where he's taking it, and how long we have."

"Corliss," Ada said. She was very still, and in the last blue light her face looked carved. "Corliss doesn't buy men to carry boxes. He buys men to deliver boxes. Which means Corliss is close. Close enough to meet a packer in the night."

"Then we ride," Tam said, and swayed, and Wren caught his good arm without looking at him, the way you catch something you've been catching for days.

"No," Coral said.

They all turned. The wind gusted, and somewhere down the valley a wolf said one long sentence to the dark.

"No, we don't ride. Think. It's dark. The trail forks at the bottom of the valley — Wren told us, three ways, and two of them are river canyons. Odell knows we'd ride. Odell *wants* us to ride, tired, in the dark, split up maybe, chasing noise. Corliss has wanted us split up since the burn scar." He looked at Wren. "You said you knew this country. How far could a man lead a mule in a night, staying off the main trails?"

Wren's face changed — the sullen teenage flatness dropping away like a hood, and underneath it something quick and bright and a little wild. "If he knows the stock trails? Ten miles, twelve, if the moon holds. But he doesn't need to go far. He needs to go to a fire." She was already moving, crouching at the corral's edge, her fingers in the mud where the mule had stood. "He'll want a fire. It's freezing tonight. And a man who steals in the night is a man looking over his shoulder, and a man looking over his shoulder doesn't take the hard trails, he takes the ones his mule already knows." She looked up, and the last light caught her eyes, and Coral thought: *she's been running since she was fourteen, and everything she learned running is in her face right now.* "He'll take the low trail. Along the water. There's a drover's camp at the bend, maybe two hours' walk, with a fire ring and a roof. Everyone uses it. He'll use it."

"You can't know that," Juno said.

"I know it like you know your engine room," Wren said, and there was no apology in it and no fear either, just the flat certainty of a kid who had survived on exactly this kind of knowing while everyone else in the world was asleep. "I've been that man. I've been the one walking at night with something that isn't mine, looking for a fire ring and a roof and a mule that knows the way. You don't get clever at night. You get *tired*. Tired men go where the going is easy."

Coral looked at her for a long moment. Then at Ada, whose face had not changed, but whose hands had unclenched, just slightly, at her sides.

"Two hours' walk," Coral said. "We go on foot, quiet, and we leave the horses. Tam—"

"Tam stays," Tam said. "And before anyone argues, I can't walk two miles tonight, let alone two hours, and we all know it, and I'd rather not have it said out loud where the wind can hear." He grinned, and it was a bad grin, gray around the edges, but it was his. "Someone has to hold the waystation if this is a trick. And someone has to be here when you come back, so I can say I told you so."

* * *

They went down the valley in the dark with the moon three days off full and strong enough to lay the trail out in silver and black. Coral led, because his eyes were the ones that had spent a decade reading the sea's small print, and the dark of a mountain valley was, if anything, more legible — every rock and root writing itself in shadow. Juno came behind him with a rifle wrapped in blanket wool to kill the shine. Ada behind her. Wren last and somehow also first, drifting off the trail and back onto it like a bird that knew the air, reading sign that Coral could not see at all.

Twice Wren stopped them with a raised hand, and twice they stood in the freezing dark while she listened, and the second time she said, softly, "Mule, maybe twenty minutes ahead. Moving slow. He's letting it pick its feet," and Coral felt the whole company tighten like a drawn wire.

The drover's camp sat at the bend of the river where the valley pinched, a low pole shelter with a fire ring in front of it, and the fire was burning. Low, banked, careful — a fire built by a man who didn't want it seen — but a fire. The mule stood hipshot at the edge of the light. And beside the fire, wrapped in a blanket, his clay-colored beard silvered by moonlight, sat Odell Brack.

Coral put out a hand and stopped them all in the shadow of a granite shoulder, and they crouched there and looked, and what Coral felt was not anger. He kept waiting for the anger and it didn't come. What came instead was a terrible, patient sadness, the sadness of a man looking at a hull plate with the wrong serial number — the knowledge that the world had been lying to him for a while, and had done it competently, and that competence was the most frightening thing about it.

Then Odell stood up. Stood up and turned, deliberately, to face the darkness where they crouched, and said, in his soft river-clay voice, carrying just far enough:

"You might as well come in. He's been here an hour already."

The fire popped. Down the valley, around the bend, where the trail widened, lantern light moved — two lanterns, three, and the sound of hooves on frozen mud, many horses, coming slow and unhurried, the way men ride when they are certain.

Coral rose out of the shadow with the rifle in his hands and the cold going through him like water through a split seam, and beside him he felt Wren go rigid, and behind him Ada breathe one word that might have been a prayer or a curse or a name.

Corliss rode into the firelight wearing a good coat and a worse smile, and he said, "Mr. Zhou. We really have to stop meeting like this," and Coral understood that the night had just become the narrowest place he had ever been in, narrower than any passage he had ever swum, and that the only way out was through.
