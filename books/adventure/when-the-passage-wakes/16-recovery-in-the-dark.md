## 16. Recovery in the Dark

Wren had learned to read darkness the way other people learned to read letters — slowly at first, then all at once, until it was just another language she spoke without thinking.

The dark above the treeline was not one thing. It was a hundred small darks layered over each other: the blue-black of sky where stars crowded thick as river gravel; the deeper black of the ridge against that sky; the soft, breathing black of pine shadow down in the valley where the packer's camp burned its little orange secret into the night. She lay on her belly on a granite shelf with her chin in her hands and counted what she could see. Two fires, close together. Three bedrolls visible from this angle, maybe a fourth behind the mule line. One man walking a slow circuit with a rifle slung loose, the way men carried rifles when they were cold and bored and had forgotten, for the moment, that anyone might be watching.

She'd been watching for two hours. Her fingers were numb past feeling and she didn't mind. Numb fingers didn't shake.

Below her, breathing slow through his nose the way he did when he wanted to be quiet but couldn't stop being angry, Coral lay flat as spilled water. Juno was somewhere off to the left — Wren could find her by the faint rhythm of her breath if she tried, and by the fact that Juno breathed like a person who had decided long ago that fear was a luxury item she no longer purchased. Tam was back at the waystation with Ada, his shoulder wrapped and feverish, arguing in a low voice about whether he should come until Ada threatened to sit on him. He'd lost that argument. He'd been losing arguments since the scree, and every loss made him smaller and more stubborn, like a knot pulled tight.

*Four days,* Juno had said that morning, crouched over the snowline with her bare hand pressed flat to the drifts, reading them like tide tables. *Maybe five if we're lucky. After that, Kestrel closes and we winter up here eating our own boots.*

And now the strongbox — Ada's father's voice, forty dead sailors' names, the whole heavy truth of the *Kestrel's* sinking — was sitting in a thief's camp half a mile below, guarded by three men who hadn't stolen it so much as sold it, and Corliss was out there in the dark somewhere between here and the pass with orders nobody had said aloud yet but everyone could feel coming like weather.

Wren had tracked the packer by instinct, they'd said. That wasn't quite true. She'd tracked him because she'd watched him for six days and never once seen him look at the mountains. A man who came into high country and never looked up was a man already leaving in his head. When the strongbox went missing from the waystation storeroom in the night, she hadn't needed sign or spoor. She'd needed only to remember which direction a man looks when he's counting money instead of ridgelines.

Southwest. Down toward the old mining cut, where the trees started again and a man could meet a wagon.

She'd found the camp by its smell before she found it by its light — woodsmoke with a chemical edge to it, the kind of pitch-soaked lumber they stacked at the syndicate depots in Harrowgate. That smell had told her something else too, something she hadn't said yet because saying it would make it real: these weren't the packer's guards. These were Corliss's men who had come *up* to meet him. Which meant Corliss knew exactly where they were, and had known since the theft, and had let them walk all day believing they still had time.

They didn't have time. They had a night.

* * *

They went over it twice, crouched in the rocks above the camp, speaking in the low grammar of people who had learned each other's silences.

"Three I can see," Juno murmured. "Maybe four."

"The fourth sleeps by the mules," Wren said. "He snores. Left side, under the grey tarp."

Juno looked at her — that long, level look she gave when Wren said something that reminded her Wren had been living wild in the high country since she was twelve, and that the things Wren knew weren't tricks, they were just Tuesday. "You've been listening to snoring for two hours."

"Somebody has to have standards."

Almost, Juno smiled. It didn't get all the way there, but it got far enough. Then Coral's hand came up — the flat-palm signal, hold — and both of them went still, because below them the walker had stopped at the fire and crouched, and lifted something from beside a stone.

Even at distance, even in firelight, Wren knew the shape of it. The strongbox sat in the man's lap like a sleeping animal, iron-grey, salt-stained, its lock plate scarred where someone had taken a chisel to it and given up. The packer stood nearby, hat in his hands, talking fast, the body language of a man selling something he'd already spent.

Coral watched for a long moment. In the starlight his face was all planes and hollows, and Wren thought — not for the first time — that he looked like the water he'd come from: calm on top, everything happening underneath.

"He's going to open it," Coral said quietly. "Or Corliss is going to open it. Either way, by morning those logs are ash and we're done."

"Then we go now," Juno said.

"I know." But he didn't move yet, and Wren understood why. Below them, the plan was simple and terrible: four people against four, in the dark, with a box they could not drop, could not damage, could not leave behind, and a retreat uphill through scree that would announce them to anything with ears within a mile. The mountain had been their enemy for weeks. Tonight it might be the only thing that saved them — or the thing that buried them.

"Coral." Wren kept her voice down near the ground. "There's a game trail. Comes up behind the horse lines, through the deadfall. I used it last summer running traplines. It puts you inside their picket before anyone hears you."

"You used it running *from* something?"

"Used it running *to* something. Doesn't matter. It holds."

He looked at her then, really looked, the way he'd started doing somewhere after Sawtooth Pass — like she was a piece of equipment he'd underestimated and was recalibrating around. "How do you know it holds?"

"Because I'm still here."

* * *

The descent took everything Wren had and paid it back double.

She led them down through the deadfall with the moonless dark pressing close, her boots finding stone by memory and by the small animal certainty that lived in her feet. Behind her came Coral, moving better than she expected — a diver's balance, weight always centered, never a wasted motion, as though gravity were just another current he'd learned to swim in. Juno came third with the coil of rope over her shoulder, silent as falling snow. They left Tam and Ada at the waystation with the mules and the rifles they could spare, and Ada had gripped Wren's arm hard enough to bruise and said nothing at all, which from Ada was a speech.

The game trail threaded between fallen pines silvered with age, their bark gone soft as felt. Wren moved through them the way water moves through a wreck — finding the gaps without looking, flowing where the structure allowed. Once a branch caught her sleeve and she froze, held up one finger, waited ten heartbeats while the camp noise below went on unchanged: a cough, the shift of a horse, the mutter of fire.

At the bottom of the trail the trees opened onto the horse lines, and the world smelled of dung and canvas and cold iron.

The sleeper under the grey tarp snored on, ten feet away, his rifle propped against a log an honest arm's reach from his hand. Wren knelt beside Coral in the shadow of a deadfall and pointed: the tent where the fires' glow didn't reach — that was where the strongbox had gone, she'd watched them carry it. The walker had finished his circuit and sat back down at the main fire, and now there was laughter, low and ugly, the sound of men dividing up work they thought was finished.

Coral put his mouth close to her ear. "You take the horses. If this goes wrong, I want you cutting them loose and getting them upslope. You hear me? Whatever happens down here, you don't come back for us."

"And if it goes right?"

"Then you bring them back down and we ride out of here like ghosts."

"That's not a plan, that's a wish."

"It's the best one I've got." And there it was, the thing underneath his voice that she'd been hearing for days now and hadn't named: he was afraid. Not of dying — she'd watched him nearly drown in the canyon flood and he'd been furious, not scared. He was afraid of failing them. Of being the reason the box was lost, the reason Ada's father stayed a nameless dead man, the reason any of them ended up on this mountain forever. He carried it the way Tam carried his wound — quietly, badly, and where everyone could see if they bothered to look.

"We're not ghosts," Wren whispered. "Ghosts are dead people who don't know it. We're alive people who do. There's a difference."

He stared at her. Then, very briefly, he touched the top of her head, the way you'd touch something you were afraid of breaking, and moved off into the dark toward the tent, and Juno peeled away toward the firelight, and Wren went to the horses.

* * *

What happened next happened in pieces, the way bad things and good things both do.

She was sawing through the second picket line when the first sound came — not a shout, which she'd braced for, but a *thud*, dull and final, the sound of a body meeting the ground in a way it hadn't planned. Then Juno's voice, low and hard: "*Down.* Both of you. Hands where I can see them."

Wren froze with her knife in the rope. Through the trees she could see firelight and shapes: the two men at the fire rising, one reaching for a rifle that Juno's was already covering; the walker on his knees with his hands laced behind his head, breathing in whistles; and Coral standing over the packer, who lay sprawled at the tent flap with Coral's boot pinning his wrist and Coral's knife — she saw it glint — resting against the man's throat.

Inside the tent, visible through the gap in the canvas, the strongbox sat untouched. The chisel marks on the lock were fresh, but the lock itself had held. Her father's engineering, Wren thought wildly — no, Ada's father's. Good iron keeps faith longer than good men do.

"Where is he?" Coral was saying, and his voice had dropped into a register Wren had never heard from him, flat and cold as deep water. "Corliss. Where is he waiting?"

"Nowhere," the packer gasped. "I swear — I was supposed to take it down to the mining cut, that's all, there's a wagon—"

"There's no wagon." That was Juno, from the fire, where she'd been going through saddlebags. "There's a depot stamp on this tack. Syndicate issue. You weren't running cargo, you were making a delivery." A pause, papers rustling. "Coral. There are orders here."

Wren heard it in the silence that followed — the particular quality of it, the way the fire popped and nobody breathed. She tied off the last horse, looped the lead ropes over her shoulder, and moved toward the voices because some things you have to see.

Coral had taken a folded paper from Juno and was holding it to the firelight. His face did something complicated. Wren had spent her life reading faces in places where reading them wrong got you beaten, and what she saw cross his was not surprise. It was confirmation. The last door closing on the last hope that this was all still somehow about a map and a price.

"Read it," Ada would have said. So Wren said it. "Read it out loud."

Coral's jaw worked once. "'All witnesses to be silenced on recovery of materials. No exceptions. The woman Voss is priority.'" He lowered the paper. Looked at the packer, who had gone the color of tallow. "You knew this. You read your payment and you knew what 'no exceptions' meant, and you took the money anyway."

"They weren't going to tell me anything!" The man's voice cracked apart. "I owed them, that's all — I owed Corliss from before, from Harrowgate, he holds my paper, he owns my boat, I didn't have a choice—"

"Everybody says that." Coral crouched down, slow, until they were eye to eye, and Wren saw his knife-hand change — saw the blade turn flat, angle away, become something that was no longer promising anything. "Here's yours. Get up. Walk south tonight and keep walking, and when you get wherever you're going, you remember that a man whose brother they drowned still didn't give them your life. That's the choice you didn't make. Carry it."

The packer stared at him. "You're — you're letting me go?"

"I'm letting you go." Coral stood. "Juno, take their rifles. Break the firing pins on the rocks. Leave them a day's food — I'm not killing a man for being a coward, and I'm not feeding him to the mountain either."

For a moment nobody moved. Then Juno began snapping rifles over her knee with the brisk efficiency of a woman closing accounts, and the walker and the fire-tender gathered their bedrolls with shaking hands, and the packer stood in the trampled snow of his own betrayal and looked at Coral like a man looking at a language he'd never learned.

"Why?" he said finally. "Why let me go? They'll just send more."

"Yeah," Coral said. "They will." He turned to Wren, and the firelight caught the salt-scars on his hands, the ones he'd gotten hauling himself out of the sea and never talked about. "Get the horses. We're burning daylight we don't have."

* * *

They rode out of the mining cut at midnight with the strongbox lashed tight behind Juno's saddle and the stars wheeling overhead, indifferent and enormous.

Wren took point, because the trail back was hers and because riding point meant she didn't have to talk. But halfway up the switchbacks, hooves drew level with hers, and it was Coral, and for a while neither of them said anything. The mountain breathed around them. Somewhere below, an owl asked its one question and got no answer.

"You think I was wrong," he said at last. Not accusing. Asking.

"No."

"You're the only one who won't say it, so say it anyway."

Wren chewed on it. Below them, the others strung out along the trail — Juno solid as a fencepost, and behind her the torch-glow of the waystation where Tam and Ada waited, where a woman who'd spent nine years keeping a ledger of the dead was about to learn that the men who killed her father had written her name on the same page.

"I think mercy isn't soft," she said finally. "I think it's the hardest work there is. Anybody can pull a trigger. My pa could. Look where it got him." She glanced sideways at Coral, at the set of his shoulders, the way he held the reins like they might break. "You gave that man a heavier thing to carry than a bullet would've been. That's not weakness. That's just a different kind of fight."

Coral was quiet for a long moment. "My grandmother used to say the sea forgives nothing and forgets less."

"She sounds like the mountain."

"She'd have liked you." Almost a smile. Then it faded, and he nodded back down the trail, toward the dark country southwest, where the mining cut ran out into the long valley and the syndicate's wagons ran like veins. "But here's what tonight bought us besides the box. Those orders. *No exceptions.* Corliss isn't hunting a map anymore. He's hunting witnesses. Every one of us. Ada most of all." His voice went low and even, the diver's voice, the one that came out when the water got deep. "Which means from here to Kestrel Pass, we're not racing weather anymore. We're racing him. And he knows exactly where we are."

Wren looked north, up-valley, where Kestrel Pass hid behind its shoulder of black rock, and felt the snow up there the way animals feel it — a pressure, a promise, a countdown. Four days. Maybe five.

Behind them, far below, a single lantern kindled in the mining cut. Someone had reached a telegraph wire, or a rider had already gone out into the dark.

"Wren," Coral said. "How fast can you get us to the pass?"

She put her heels to her horse.

"Faster than he thinks," she said. "Hold on."
