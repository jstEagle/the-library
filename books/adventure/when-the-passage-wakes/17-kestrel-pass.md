## 17. Kestrel Pass

The snow began as a whisper and became a sermon.

Ada Voss had lived her whole life within earshot of mountains, and she knew their voices the way she knew the creak of her father's old chair — the dry rattle of scree in summer wind, the groan of ice settling at dusk, the particular hush that fell before a storm like a congregation rising. This was that hush. It had been falling for two hours now, thickening by degrees, until the world above the treeline had been reduced to a grey-white room with no walls and no floor, only the six of them moving through it like figures on a page someone had forgotten to finish.

She counted them out of habit, the way her father had taught her to count rivets: *Coral ahead on the rope, Juno behind him, Wren third, Tam fourth, herself fifth, the mule — no, they'd lost the last mule at Sawtooth — just the sled dragging the strongbox.* Five people and a box of dead men's words, climbing toward the sky.

"Talk to me, Tam," Wren said from her place on the rope. Her voice came thin through the wind, stripped of its usual edge of mockery. "Tell me about the worst place you ever tracked through."

"Worse than this?" A cough, then a laugh that didn't quite make it out whole. "Girl, I once spent nine days in the Chuska country with nothing but a dead man's boots and half a canteen."

"And?"

"And I kept his boots polished." Another cough. "Respect costs nothing."

Ada smiled despite herself, despite the cold that had gotten into her jaw an hour ago and refused to leave, despite the weight of the strongbox harness cutting grooves into her shoulders. That was the thing about Tam — he spent jokes the way other men spent blood, freely and when it mattered most. She had known him eleven days. Eleven days ago he'd been a stranger with a disgraced uniform and eyes that flinched from campfires, and now she could not imagine the rope without him on it.

The wind shifted and came at them sideways, carrying grains of ice that stung like thrown sand. Coral's silhouette ahead blurred and sharpened, blurred and sharpened, a dark stitch holding the white together.

"Rope tight!" he called back, and the line went taut between them, five lives knotted at ten-foot intervals, each one responsible for catching the one behind.

They climbed.

* * *

The pass had a name on no map Ada trusted, but her father had called it Kestrel, after the birds that rode the updrafts off the eastern face in summer. He had crossed it twice, he told her once, in the years before the *Meridian*, and both times he'd said the mountain let him through rather than welcomed him. *"Remember that about high places, Addie-girl. They don't hate you. They don't love you either. They just weigh you, and if you're not worth carrying, they put you down."*

She thought of that now with every step, her boots punching through the crusted surface into knee-deep powder beneath, hauling each leg free again. The strongbox rode her back like a second spine, forty pounds of iron and paper, the engineering logs inside worth more than every claim in the Kestrels and heavy as sin. Sometimes she caught herself talking to it silently, the way she'd talked to her father's grave. *Hold on. We're nearly there. I'm bringing you home.*

Juno dropped back beside her, breath ragged, frost in her eyebrows making her look suddenly older, suddenly like a woman who had survived things. "How's your hands?"

"Attached."

"That's not what I asked."

"They're fine, Juno." They were not fine. The cold had taken feeling from three fingers on her left hand somewhere below the shoulder of the ridge, and she worked them secretly against her thigh to keep them honest. But Juno looked at her the way a roadhouse keeper looks at a customer lying about how much he can drink, and said nothing more, which was its own kind of mercy.

Above them, Coral stopped and held up a fist. The company bunched against the wind and waited while he studied something in the murk — a cairn, Ada realized when they reached it, one of her father's, the stones stacked in the particular interlocking pattern he'd used so the weather couldn't take them apart. Someone had built these sixty years ago and the mountain still hadn't won.

"He marked the route," she said, touching the top stone. Her glove left a print in the rime. "He always said if you're going to ask a mountain for passage, leave something behind so the next fool knows where you asked."

"Sentimental engineer," Tam said fondly, from lower on the rope.

"The worst kind." She meant it as a joke. It came out sounding like a prayer.

They moved on. The grade steepened. The snow thickened until twenty feet might as well have been twenty miles, and the world contracted to the rope, the boots ahead, the sound of breathing.

That was when Tam fell.

* * *

It happened without drama, the way the worst things do. One moment the rope paid out steady behind her; the next it went slack, then jerked, and Ada turned into the white to find Tam down on one knee, then both knees, then sitting entirely, as though he'd simply decided the mountain made a reasonable chair.

"I'm fine," he said, before anyone spoke. "Caught my boot. Give me a minute."

Wren was already kneeling beside him, and Ada saw the girl's face change — saw her go very still, the way she went still around wounded animals, all the sharpness draining out of her and leaving something older underneath. "Tam. Your side."

"It's the cold. Everything looks bad in the—"

Wren pulled his coat open before he finished, and the wind gusted, and Ada saw.

The wound he'd taken on the scree, the bullet-gouge below his collarbone he'd laughed about and bound himself and never once complained of, had gone wrong. The binding was soaked through, dark frozen stripes across the cloth. The flesh around it was swollen and wrong-colored even in the flat grey light, and there was a smell under the antiseptic reek that Ada knew from the roadhouse, from men who'd waited too long to send for the doctor in Verity.

"How long?" Coral asked quietly. He'd come back down the rope, and his voice had the diver's calm in it, the flat even tone she'd learned meant the opposite of calm.

"Since Sawtooth," Tam said. "Maybe since the burn scar. Doesn't matter."

"It matters." Coral crouched. "Fever?"

"A little."

"Cough?"

"A little."

"Liar?"

Tam grinned, and the grin cost him visibly. "A little."

"We camp here," Coral said. "Get him warm, redress it, push at first light."

"No." Tam put a hand against the snow and pushed himself upright, and failed, and Wren and Ada caught him between them. For a moment he hung there, a big man made suddenly light as ash, and when he spoke again the joking had gone out of him entirely, and what remained was worse. "You listen to me. All of you. You've got maybe four hours of light and less than that of weather, and this pass closes behind you whether you cross it or not. You stop here, you winter here. You know what that means."

Nobody answered, because everybody knew.

"So here's what happens." He said it steadily, the way a man reads his own eulogy. "You cut me loose. Leave me the tent fly, the stove, whatever food you can spare. I'll hold this spot. When spring comes—" he coughed, wet and long, and Ada's chest hurt hearing it "—when spring comes, somebody sends word down to Harrowgate, and old Marta Kess will come up with a sledge, because Marta owes me a debt she'll never admit. And you—" he looked at Coral, then at the strongbox on Ada's back "—you get those logs over the pass. That's the whole mission. That's why any of us are freezing to death on this rock. Don't you dare spend five lives on a wound I've already spent."

The wind filled the silence after him, shrieking softly over the cairn-stones.

"You're wrong about one thing," Wren said.

"Which thing? I've been wrong about plenty."

"You haven't already spent it." The girl's voice cracked and she hated it, Ada could see her hating it. "You don't get to decide you're done. That's not — we don't do that. We don't *do* that."

"Wren—"

"Nobody gets left on this mountain."

She said it simply, without theater, the way you'd state a fact about the weather or the price of flour, and Ada felt the words go through the whole company like a current through wire. Because it wasn't new. It had never been said aloud before, but it had been true since the roadhouse fire, since the false trail through the burn scar, since the night Coral came up out of the flood-canyon coughing water and Wren had held his head and Juno had built the fire and nobody had slept until he did. They had been living by this law for weeks. Tam had just never heard it applied to himself.

"Girl," Tam said gently, "I'm telling you as a tracker. I can't make the distance. My legs are done and my blood's going bad, and every hour you carry my weight is an hour the storm takes back double. Do the arithmetic."

"I did the arithmetic," Coral said.

Everyone turned. He had been quiet through all of it, crouched at Tam's side with snow gathering on his shoulders, and now he stood, and Ada watched him do the thing she'd watched him do in the canyon flood — go inward, go down, the way a diver goes down, checking his air, checking his line, finding the number that keeps a man alive.

"Here's mine," he said. "We rope together, all five, close intervals. We rig Tam into the middle of the line — sling under his arms, his pack and rifle distributed. Four of us walking, one of us riding. We lose speed, yes. Maybe half. But we don't lose the stove stops, because we don't have to pitch camp — we rest standing, roped, backs to the wind, and that keeps us alive as long as we keep moving. And the storm—" he tilted his head back toward the white roar above them "—the storm is blowing *up* the pass, not down. Which means it's at our backs on the climb. It's pushing us. The mountain is doing half our work."

"You're guessing," Tam said.

"I'm a salvage diver. Guessing is the whole profession." Coral's mouth did something that was almost a smile. "You want to know what I know? I know that water doesn't care how tired you are. It doesn't care how sensible it would be to let go. You tie yourself to the work and you count your breaths and you move, and you move, and you move. That's all survival ever is. So we tie ourselves to you, and you count your breaths, and we move."

"And if I slow you past saving?"

"Then we'll be slowed past saving together," Juno said, "which is a much better story."

Ada unshouldered the strongbox and set it down in the snow, and began, with her half-numb fingers, to rig the sling.

* * *

It took twenty minutes that felt like two hours. They wove the rope through Tam's coat and under his arms, cinched it, tested it with all four of them hauling at once. They split his load — Ada took the rifle slung across her chest beneath the strongbox straps, Wren took his pack, Juno took the ammunition, Coral took nothing extra because he was breaking trail and breaking trail was enough. Then they shortened the intervals to eight feet, close enough to touch, and stood in a ring around the man they were about to carry like pallbearers waiting on a resurrection.

"This is undignified," Tam announced.

"This is Tuesday," Wren said.

He laughed, and the laugh turned into a cough, and then he let them lift him, and the strange thing — the thing Ada would remember all her life, she suspected, in warm rooms far from here — was how little he weighed. Not truly; he was solid as a fence post. But the rope took him, and the four of them leaned into the slope, and he came up out of the snow like something being raised from deep water, and the company became a single animal with five hearts and one purpose.

They climbed.

The storm ate the light an hour early. Ada lost the horizon entirely and then lost the ridge-line and then lost everything except the dark shape of Coral ahead, appearing and vanishing like a lighthouse seen through fog, and the pull of the rope in her mittened hands, and the heat of Tam's body swinging gently at the center of their web whenever the ground tilted. She talked to him as they walked, because Wren had said fevered men drift and voices anchor them, and because her own fear needed somewhere to go.

"Tell me about the Chuska country," she shouted into the wind. "The dead man's boots."

"What about them?"

"Whose were they?"

A pause, long enough that she thought he'd drifted. Then: "My brother's."

The word landed in her chest and stayed there.

"He died at Serpent Fork," Tam went on, his voice coming dreamy and level, the fever talking now but the truth underneath it, "and the army wrote him up as a deserter to cover an officer's mistake, and I spent six years trying to get the record fixed, and the record broke me instead. But I kept his boots polished the whole time. Every morning. Even in the stockade." A gust hit them and the whole rope leaned and recovered. "You understand? It wasn't about the boots. It was about not letting them decide what he was."

"I understand," Ada said, and found her eyes were hot and freezing shut, and blinked them clear. "That's what's in the box, Tam. Forty men the navy decided what they were. We're carrying them over this pass to say otherwise."

"Then they picked the right mule team," Tam murmured, and laughed again, softer, and Ada tightened her grip on the rope and walked.

They rested four times, standing, backs to the wind, exactly as Coral had said, passing a flask of the terrible blackberry brandy Juno carried for emergencies, each of them taking one swallow and giving one swallow, Tam getting two. Between rests there was only the rhythm: step, breathe, haul, step. The cairns appeared out of the white like blessings — her father's stones, one after another, exactly where his old notes said they'd be, and Ada greeted each one silently. *Hello, Papa. Still here. Still asking.*

Once, near what she judged must be the crest, the wind dropped for perhaps thirty seconds — a pocket of terrible silence — and in that silence Ada heard, faint and far below and behind them, carried up the throat of the pass: a whistle. Two notes, repeated. The kind of signal men use when shouting would carry too far.

Juno heard it too. Ada saw her head come up like a bird dog's.

"Company?" Wren breathed.

"Or weather," Juno said. "Could be weather."

"It's not weather," Coral said quietly. "Weather doesn't repeat itself."

The wind rose again and swallowed everything, and they stood roped together on the shoulder of the Kestrel Range with the crest somewhere above them in the roaring dark, a wounded man slung at their center, a box of the dead on Ada's back, and pursuit whistling somewhere in the white below.

"Count your breaths," Coral said. "We move."

And they moved.
