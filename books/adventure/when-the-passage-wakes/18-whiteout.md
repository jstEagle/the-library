## 18. Whiteout

The storm did not arrive so much as it was always there, waiting above them like a held breath.

Coral felt it before he saw it — the same pressure change he'd known a thousand times in grey water, that subtle thickening of the air, the way sound went flat and close. On the reef it meant surge coming. Here, at the shoulder of Kestrel Pass with the sky pressed down to fifty feet of screaming white, it meant something worse: the mountain was about to swallow the road behind them and the road ahead and any idea they'd had of choosing between the two.

"Rope!" he shouted, and the wind took half the word and gave back only *ope*, but Juno was already moving, already uncoiling the line from her pack frame because she'd been diving with him long enough to read his hands as much as his voice. He tied himself in first — bowline around his waist, checked twice by feel alone since his fingers had gone past feeling — then passed the line back hand over hand. Ada. Wren. Tam, who was leaning on Wren more than either of them pretended. Juno last, anchor of the chain, the way she'd always been.

Six souls on one rope. Forty fathoms of nothing on every side.

He had been in whiteout before, once, off the Sable Shoals when the silt came up from the bottom in a wall and turned his lamp's beam into a mirror. Every diver learned the first rule of zero visibility early or didn't survive to learn anything else: *you cannot trust your eyes, so you trust your line.* The line is truth. The line is the only thing between you and the open dark that wants you.

The mountain, it turned out, ran on the same rules.

* * *

They had made the crest at what Tam swore was midday, though the light said nothing at all — just a uniform pearl-grey glare with no sun in it and no shadows, the kind of light that erased depth and distance and made the snow underfoot look solid until it wasn't. The pass itself was a saddle maybe two hundred yards wide between two walls of black rock furred white, and beyond it the trail dropped toward the eastern valley in a series of switchbacks that Wren had described the night before with the flat confidence of someone who'd walked them at eleven years old.

That was yesterday. Yesterday there had been a trail.

Now there was wind, and the wind was moving snow the way current moves silt — not falling but *flying*, horizontal, hissing against his goggles like grit. He could see Ada three feet ahead of him as a smudge, a suggestion, a coat-colored ghost. Beyond her, nothing. Behind him, Juno's grip on the rope tugged once, twice — their old signal from the wreck days, *I'm here, keep going* — and he answered with the counter-tug and stepped forward into the blind.

His breath counted itself without his permission. It always did. Four seconds in through the scarf, six out, the rhythm drilled into him across ten years of cold-water work where panic was the fastest way to die. In the black water he'd learned that fear was just air spent too fast, and air was the only currency that mattered. Up here the currency was the same. Four in, six out. Step. Four in, six out. Step.

"Coral!" Ada's voice, shredded by wind, arriving from somewhere left of where she should have been. "The cairns! Wren says there are cairns every forty paces along the crest — we need to find the first one or we'll walk off the north edge!"

He couldn't see Wren. He couldn't see the edge. He could see, if he squinted into the glare, a darkness that might be rock and might be his own eyelashes freezing.

"Wren." He pitched his voice low and even, the voice he used below, the one that carried calm whether or not he owned any. "You're closest to me. Talk me to the cairn."

A pause. Then her voice, young and steady and scared in a way she was trying hard not to show: "Left. Your left. Ten steps, then feel for the stones — Papa showed me, they're stacked taller than a man's knee, you'll know them."

Ten steps. He counted them aloud, because counting aloud was a rope too, and heard the others' boots crunching behind him in time. On nine his boot struck stone. On ten his gloved hand found it — a stack of rocks packed into ice, thigh-high, real, *there*. Something in his chest unlocked a quarter turn.

"First cairn," he called back down the rope. "Forty paces, bearing whatever Wren says. We go cairn to cairn. Nobody moves without the line."

"And after the last cairn?" That was Tam, further back, his voice roughened by the fever he still thought none of them could hear in it.

"After the last cairn," Coral said, "we're on the switchbacks, and the switchbacks go down, and down is the whole point."

* * *

The second cairn took sixty paces instead of forty, because the wind had built drifts across the saddle and walking a straight line in a whiteout was a lie the legs told themselves. He knew that too. Underwater, divers drifted without knowing it — current took everyone, and the ones who trusted their sense of direction over their compass died smiling at things that weren't there. So he navigated like a diver: pick the bearing, count the kicks, correct, count again. Wren called directions from the middle of the rope, her knowledge of this country threading through his discipline like warp through weft.

Third cairn. Fourth. At the fifth, Juno caught up beside him — the rope slack between them now, which meant the others were bunched, which meant they were slowing — and shouted directly into his ear, the only way speech worked anymore.

"Tracks behind us! Back at the fourth marker — I saw them filling in. Boot prints. A lot of them."

Coral's stomach did the slow cold roll it did when a hull groaned the wrong way. Corliss. Of course Corliss. The man had chased them across three days of scree and two nights of frozen camps with the patience of something that fed on winter, and now the storm that should have blinded everyone equally had become a hunting blind, and the rope-line of six people crawling cairn to cairn was leaving exactly one trail through otherwise unmarked snow.

"How far back?"

"Couldn't tell. The snow eats everything. But they were fresh enough to see at all."

Which meant close. Which meant minutes, not hours.

He stood a moment in the roaring white and let the arithmetic run. Ahead: five more markers, then the descent, then shelter somewhere below the treeline if Wren's memory held. Behind: armed men who wanted the strongbox on Ada's back and every witness attached to it dead. Between: a storm that killed both parties impartially.

"We can't outrun them on this rope," Juno said. She wasn't asking. She was telling him what he already knew, the way she'd told him for ten years: *the tide's turning, captain, decide.*

"No," he agreed. "But we don't have to outrun them. We have to *vanish*."

She looked at him. Even through rime-crusted goggles he could feel the weight of the look.

"The trail," he said. "It's the only thing they can follow. So we stop making one." He turned back down the line, found Wren's shape, drew her forward by the wrist until her face was a foot from his. "Your canyon country. Is there any way off this saddle that doesn't leave tracks?"

Her eyes went wide, then narrowed, calculating — and he watched the fear in her get shoved aside by the thing underneath it, the competence, the same competence he'd seen in her since the day she'd appeared at their fire with a stolen rifle and an opinion about everything. "There's the wind-scour," she said slowly. "East side of the saddle, where the rock funnels the gale through a gap. Snow never lies there — it blows clean down to bare stone. If we cross the scour, no prints. It comes out at the head of the second switchback."

"How wide?"

"Fifty yards. Maybe more. And the wind through it—" she grimaced "—it'll take your breath away. Literally. Papa called it the Throat. Said sheep wouldn't use it."

"Sheep are sensible," Tam muttered from behind.

Coral closed his eyes for one breath — four in, six out — and saw it the way he saw a dive plan: the route, the hazards, the point of no return. A gap of screaming wind and bare stone, crossed blind, on a rope, with a wounded man and a girl and a woman carrying forty pounds of dead men's testimony. Behind them, killers. Above them, weather with murder in it.

Below them, the valley. The truth. Verity, and the judge, and Hale's promise kept or broken forever.

"Then we use the Throat," he said. "All of us. Now, while our trail's still fresh enough to pull them to the cairns while we cut away."

Ada's voice came up the line, thin and hard: "And if the wind takes someone?"

"It won't," Coral said. "Because I'm going to rig this rope the way I rig a descent line. And nobody lets go. Not for anything."

* * *

He worked fast, fingers stupid in the cold, doing it by touch and memory the way he'd spliced lines on a pitching deck at dawn. The rope came off their waists and got re-run as a single traveling line: anchored to the sixth cairn, passed through a carabiner on each person's belt, so that the whole company hung from one continuous lifeline and could slide along it hand over hand rather than being dragged. It was how salvage crews moved along a shot line in current — nobody swimming free, everybody clipped to the truth.

"Hear me," he said, and made each of them look at him, one face at a time, ghost-pale in the glare. "In the Throat, you will want to let go. The wind will make you believe letting go is the only way to breathe. It is lying. The line is true. The line has never once lied to me. Whatever happens — hand over hand, one pull at a time, and you come out the other side. Say it back."

"The line is true," Wren said.

"The line is true," Ada said, and her voice cracked on it, and he understood: she was thinking of her father, of engineers and ledgers and proof, of all the true things men had drowned to bury. He put his mitten briefly on her shoulder.

"The line is true," Tam rasped, and then, quieter, almost to himself: "Wish I'd learned that twenty years ago."

Juno squeezed his arm once. She didn't say it. She'd never needed to.

He went first. Of course he went first; he would not ask any of them to enter that howling gap behind a leader who waited at its mouth. He clipped to the line, set his bearing on the black notch of the gap — the only fixed point in the universe, a seam of darker dark in the white — and stepped into the wind.

* * *

It was like entering a waterfall made of knives.

The Throat did not blow at him; it blew *through* him, a river of air compressed between rock walls, and for the first three steps he could not breathe at all — the wind simply stole each breath out of his mouth before his lungs could close on it, the way deep water stole heat. His goggles iced instantly opaque. He tore them off; better blind than blinded. The world became pure sensation: stone underfoot, iron-cold rope in his fists, the roar, the roar, the roar.

So he did what he had done at eighty feet down in a silt-out with his lamp dead and his partner's life on his gauge. He stopped trying to see. He closed his eyes inside the blindness. He counted.

Four in — a stolen half-breath, snatched sideways from the lee of his own shoulder. Six out. Pull. The rope slid through the carabiner with a sound like a bell underwater. Four in. Six out. Pull. He kept the count aloud in his head and let it be the metronome, the shot line, the one true thing, and he matched his body to it the way he'd matched a thousand ascents, slow and deliberate, because the men who died in whiteouts and blowups and black water were always, always the ones who rushed.

Behind him, faint through the gale, he heard it: the scrape and jingle of the others following the line. One pull, a pause, another pull. They were keeping his rhythm. They were breathing when he breathed. Somewhere in the roar Juno was counting too — he'd taught her the cadence years ago on the wreck, and he could hear her voiceless version of it in the spacing of her pulls, steady as a pump handle.

Halfway. The wind shifted and hit him broadside, a fist of snow and air that lifted his boots and slammed him against the rock wall, and for one lurching instant his hands skated on the wet rope —

— and the training caught him, the ten thousand hours of it, the body's older wisdom: *never grip with your hands alone, wrap, lock, hang from your skeleton.* He wrapped. He locked. He hung in the screaming dark from a quarter inch of hemp and counted four in, six out, and the moment passed the way all moments pass if you refuse to feed them.

He pulled. The rope paid through the carabiner. Somewhere behind him, small and fierce, he heard Wren shouting something that was probably encouragement and probably profanity, and he laughed — actually laughed, alone in the throat of the storm, because that was the other thing the deep had taught him: terror and laughter lived in the same chest cavity, and you got to choose which one made noise.

Twenty pulls. Thirty. The roar began to change pitch, opening up, losing its walls. Forty. And then the wind fell off him like a dropped cloak, and he stumbled out of the Throat into a hush so complete it rang, onto a slope of wind-scoured snow beneath a sky the color of pewter, and turned, and clipped off, and braced the line, and hauled.

Ada came through first, staggering, snow-plastered, alive, clutching the strongbox strap with one hand like it was a child's wrist. Then Wren, who emerged grinning wildly with her hair frozen into a white corona and immediately turned to shout back into the gap. Then Tam, gray-lipped, swaying, held upright by the rope and by Juno behind him, whose hands never stopped moving — checking his clip, checking his breathing, hauling him the last yard into the lee.

Six of six. Coral knelt in the snow and shook, quietly, the way a man shakes after surfacing from a bad dive, and let no one see but Juno, who saw everything and said nothing, which was her whole genius.

* * *

They did not rest long. Rest was a luxury the mountain priced in lives.

From the mouth of the Throat, looking back, Coral saw what he'd hoped to see and dreaded seeing: figures. Small, dark, wrong-angled against the white, working along the cairn line they'd abandoned. Five of them. Six. Pausing at the sixth marker where the rope had been anchored, where the trail simply *ended* — and he watched, with a diver's cold satisfaction, the confusion spread through them like ink through water. Men casting about. Men splitting up. Men walking to the edge of the wind-scour and stopping, because even Corliss's professionals knew what the Throat was, and knew what it cost.

"They'll go around," Tam said hoarsely, dropping to one knee beside him. "South rim. Adds them two hours, maybe three, if the drifts let them ride at all."

"Two hours," Coral repeated. He looked east, down the mountain, to where the switchbacks must be — invisible now, the storm closing again behind them like a door, the light dying toward whatever passed for evening up here. Two hours of lead. Maybe. And below them, somewhere in the falling dark, the second switchback, and beyond it the third, and beyond those the treeline, and beyond that the valley, and beyond that a rail town called Verity where a federal judge was waiting for a strongbox full of names.

"Then we spend them well," he said. He stood, coiling the rope with hands that had finally stopped shaking, and looked at his people — Juno rolling her shoulders loose, Wren stamping feeling back into her feet, Ada holding the box, Tam leaning on his rifle and hiding it badly. Strays, all of them. Outcasts. The family the sea and the mountains had assembled out of spare parts and handed to him without instructions.

"One more thing the water taught me," he said. "When you surface, you don't celebrate at the surface. Sharks live at the surface. You keep swimming until you're aboard." He jerked his chin down-slope, into the gathering murk. "We swim."

They started down. And thirty yards into the descent, Wren — ranging ahead the way she always did, quick as a wren indeed — stopped dead, and raised one mittened hand, and pointed at the snow below the switchback where the wind had momentarily thinned.

Tracks. Fresh ones. Heading *up* the mountain.

Not Corliss's men. Someone else. Someone who had been below them all along, climbing toward the pass through the very storm — and whoever it was, they were between the company and the valley, and they were moving fast.

Coral counted four in, six out, and reached for the rope.
