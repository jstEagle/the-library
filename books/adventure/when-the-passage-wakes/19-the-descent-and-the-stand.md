## 19. The Descent and the Stand

Juno had learned to read Coral's silences the way she'd once learned to read weather off the water — by what was *missing* from them. All morning he'd been quiet in a way that wasn't tiredness. It was the quiet of a man counting something. Breath, maybe. Steps. The distance between them and the sound that had followed them down out of Kestrel Pass since dawn: the far, rhythmic knock of boots on scree, patient as a tide.

They were below the crest now, on the eastern shoulder where the mountain shed its snow in long white aprons and the wind came off the ice fields smelling of stone and old cold. The trail narrowed to a shelf barely wide enough for two abreast, cut into a cliff face that dropped away to their left in a tumble of grey slabs and frozen waterfall — three hundred feet of it, ending in a bowl of shadow where the mist hadn't yet burned off. To the right, the rock rose sheer, streaked with meltwater that had refrozen into ribs of blue glass.

"Tam," Juno said quietly, falling back beside him.

Tam was walking like a man carrying a full pail of water he refused to spill. His left arm hung close against his ribs, bound tight under his coat since the fight on the scree two days ago, and the fever had come back with the altitude — she could see it in the shine of his skin, the way his eyes took an extra half-second to find her face. But his rifle was across his back, and his hand rested near it the way other men rested hands on a railing.

"I'm here," he said.

"You're cooking."

"I'm *warm*," he corrected. "There's a difference. One of those is useful."

Juno almost smiled. That was Tam all over — rationing his honesty the way they rationed food, giving her just enough truth to keep her from worrying and not one grain more. She'd watched him do it for days now, hiding the wound from Wren by walking at the rear of the line, hiding the stiffness from Ada by taking the downhill side of every slope, hiding everything from Coral because Coral already carried enough weight to bend him.

She looked ahead. Coral had stopped at a place where the trail bent around a spur of rock, and he stood very still, head tilted, listening the way he listened underwater — she'd seen him do it in the boathouse a hundred times, whole body gone quiet, as if sound were something you caught rather than heard.

Then he raised a fist, and the company stopped.

The wind moved. Snow squeaked under six pairs of boots settling still. And beneath the wind, unmistakable now, came the knock of boots on stone — closer than it had been an hour ago. Not chasing. *Herding.* They'd been hearing it wrong all morning, Juno realized with a cold slide in her stomach. Corliss wasn't behind them. He'd split his men at the pass, sent riders along the lower switchbacks, and now he was coming up the trail from the east while his others closed from above.

"They've got us between," Tam said. No fear in it. Just arithmetic.

Coral turned. In the flat white light his face looked carved, older than it had any right to look, and Juno thought: *he's twenty-six years old and the sea made him this.* She'd known him four years — hauling salvage, splitting catches too small to split, sleeping in the boathouse loft through storms while he sat up charting tides nobody paid him to chart. She had never once seen him afraid until Hale washed ashore. Now she saw it every day, and what frightened her was how well he wore it.

"Narrows ahead," Coral said. "Fifty yards of single file, then it opens onto the ice shelf. If we run it, we're exposed on open ground with nowhere to go."

"And if we don't run it?" Ada asked. Her voice was steady but her knuckles were white around the strap of the pack that held the strongbox — her father's logs, forty names' worth of proof, heavier than lead and worth more.

"We don't run it," Coral said. "We hold it."

Wren made a small sound. Juno glanced at her — sixteen years old, all elbows and watchfulness, hair frozen into a wild halo — and saw the girl doing sums of her own. "Two of them coming up from below," Wren said. "I counted the echoes. Maybe five from above, moving slow. They don't know these rocks."

"No," Tam agreed. "But they have rifles and we have a corridor." He unslung his rifle with his good arm, and there was something in the motion — deliberate, unhurried, final — that made Juno's chest tighten. "Narrows like this favor one man with a steady position over ten men in a queue. I'll take the bend. You get everyone past me and down to the shelf."

"You can barely lift that thing," Juno said.

Tam smiled, and the fever made the smile strange and bright. "I don't need to lift it. I need to lean it on a rock and be patient. Patience is the one part of me that never got wounded."

"That's not true," Wren said suddenly. Everyone looked at her. She stared at Tam with a fierce, wet-eyed anger. "You've been wounded everywhere. We all know. You think we don't see? You think I didn't see the blood on your shirt two nights ago when you thought the firelight hid it?" Her voice cracked and she pushed through it. "So we're not leaving you at the bend alone. That's not — that's not how this works anymore."

For a moment nobody spoke. The wind filled the silence, keening off the ice ribs, and somewhere below them the boots kept climbing.

Coral crossed to Tam and put a hand on his shoulder — the bad one, gently. "Nobody gets left on this mountain," he said. It wasn't the first time he'd said it; he'd said it at the pass, roped together in the storm. But saying it again here, with guns closing in, made it something else. A creed stops being words when you say it twice in the teeth of death.

"Then here's how it works," Tam said, and Juno heard the tracker come back into his voice, the army man who'd once read sign for a living before disgrace burned that life down. "Wren's right — I don't stand alone. But nobody stands in my firing line either. Juno, you're the fastest on rock. You take the strongbox and the ladies down the narrows first. Coral holds the second position, ten yards behind me, where the wall juts. Anything gets past me, it meets him. Wren—" he hesitated, and Juno saw him decide to stop lying, "—Wren stays with Coral. She knows the descent route off the shelf better than any of us. If it goes wrong, she gets you all down the goat path to the trees."

"And you?" Ada asked.

"I come last," Tam said. "Or I don't come. Either way, the logs reach Verity."

"No," said Coral.

"Coral—"

"*No.*" It came out low and absolute, the voice he used on green divers about to touch something that kills. "We rephrase. You come last, and *last means with us*. You hold the bend until we're through, then you fall back and we cover you. Nobody dies holding a door today. We die, if we die, all facing the same direction." He looked around at each of them, and Juno felt the words land in her like stones dropped in still water. "That's the whole plan. That's the only plan I've got. Are we clear?"

A pause. Then Tam, softly: "Clear."

Wren wiped her face with a mittened fist and nodded. Ada drew a breath that shook only slightly. And Juno found herself saying, "Clear," in a voice steadier than she felt, because someone had to be, and because four years ago she'd chosen this stubborn diver over dry land and safer work, and she would choose him again here, now, at the end of the world, without hesitation.

Below them, the boots stopped climbing.

* * *

The silence that followed was worse than the noise.

Juno pressed herself into the notch Coral showed her — a cleft in the rock wall just past the bend, deep enough to hide her, angled so she could see the narrows stretch away below: fifty yards of shelf no wider than a wagon track, walled on the left by air and on the right by ice-slick stone. The strongbox rode against her spine inside its oilskin wrap, and every breath she took she felt its corners press her like fingers.

Coral crouched ten yards behind her at the jut of wall, rifle braced, Wren folded small beside him. Beyond them, at the bend itself, Tam lay along a fallen slab with his rifle resting in the fork of two stones, exactly as promised — leaned, not lifted. From here Juno could see only the top of his hat and the barrel, and the barrel did not tremble.

Ada knelt at Juno's feet, hands folded around a climbing axe, because Ada Voss did not wait to be protected; she armed herself with whatever the mountain offered.

Time went syrup-slow. Juno counted her own heartbeats the way Coral had taught her to count breaths on the wreck dives — *in two three four, out two three four* — and watched the sun crawl a hand's width up the ice ribs opposite. Frost formed on her eyelashes. Somewhere a raven called once, harsh, and did not call again.

Then the first man came around the lower bend.

He was young, syndicate-issue coat, rifle up, moving careful — and he walked straight into the corridor Tam owned. The shot cracked off the cliff walls and doubled and redoubled, thunder in a stone throat, and the man spun and sat down hard against the outer edge and slid, and slid, and was gone into the mist below without a cry. The sound of his going — rock ticking after him, fainter and fainter — was the worst sound Juno had ever heard, and she had heard men drown.

Shouts below. Rifles barking from cover, rounds sparking off the ice ribs overhead, showering them with grit and shattered frost. Tam fired again, unhurried, and another shape broke and scrambled back down out of sight. But from *above* — from the trail they'd descended — came answering shots, and Juno understood with sick clarity that the upper party had arrived at their rear and found the narrows empty and were now working down along the rim, looking for angles.

"Moving!" Coral's voice, sharp. "Juno — go, go, go!"

She went. Low and fast along the shelf, boots finding the packed snow, Ada's breath hot at her shoulder, the drop yawning white and bottomless to her left so that she fixed her eyes on the wall and let her body remember what Coral had drilled into her a thousand times on the wreck: *trust the line, trust the next hold, never look at the water.* Fifty yards. Forty. Behind her the gunfire stuttered and echoed, and once she heard Tam's rifle speak again and once she heard it *not* answer when a burst chattered back, and her heart climbed into her throat and stayed there.

Thirty yards. Twenty. The narrows began to widen, the shelf opening onto the great ice shelf below the eastern face — a vast tilted field of glacier, blinding in the sun, running down toward the tree line a mile below.

Ten yards. Five. Open ground.

And Corliss stepped out of the ice fog at the far end of the shelf.

Juno skidded to a halt so hard Ada collided with her back. The fixer stood alone, which somehow was worse than if he'd brought ten men — standing on the glacier in his city coat with snow gathering on his shoulders, pistol held low and easy, as though he'd been waiting for a train. Behind him, up-slope, shapes moved in the fog: the rest. Cutting them off from the descent to the trees.

"Well," Corliss said, and his voice carried strangely across the ice, flattened by wind, intimate anyway. "You are a difficult man to buy, Mr. Zhou."

Coral came out of the narrows at a walk, rifle slung, hands loose and empty at his sides. Juno wanted to scream at him. He stopped ten feet from Corliss, and the two men regarded each other across the ice like the last two figures left on a stage.

"The logs go to a federal judge," Coral said. "That's already decided. You can't buy what's already given away."

"Everything can be bought. I've bought judges." Corliss tilted his head. "You're a salvage man. You understand recovery operations. This *is* one. The navy sank a liability; my employers insured the sinking. You're diving a wreck that wants to stay sunk. Every man who goes down to it stays down. That's not cruelty, Mr. Zhou. That's procedure."

"Forty crew," Coral said. "Was procedure their name for it too?"

Something flickered across Corliss's face — the first honest thing Juno had ever seen there. "The crew knew the risk profile," he said. "Everyone signs something."

Behind Corliss, up-slope, the fog shapes spread wider, flanking. Juno saw Tam emerge from the narrows at last — alive, swaying, rifle braced against the rock at the mouth — and saw Corliss's men mark him and mark Coral and begin to converge, and understood that talk was over, that it had perhaps always been over, that Corliss had only needed it long enough to position his men.

"Last offer," Corliss said. "Walk away. All of you. The girl keeps her father's name out of the newspapers and her own name out of the ground."

"My father's name," Ada said, stepping forward, "has been in the ground eleven years. You're the ones who put it there."

Corliss sighed, and raised the pistol —

— and Tam shot it out of his hand.

The crack was enormous. The pistol spun away ringing across the ice, two fingers of Corliss's hand suddenly bloody, and the shelf erupted — rifles from the fog, Tam firing from the narrows mouth, Coral dragging Ada down behind a ridge of pressure ice as rounds shrieked off the glacier in white gouts. Juno hit the snow with Wren on top of her, small and fierce, shoving her toward a crevasse shadow. "The goat path!" Wren screamed. "Down and left, the blue ice — GO!"

Juno went. The world became noise and glare: muzzle flash blooming in fog, the shriek of ricochets, Ada's coat tearing as a round clipped it, Coral roaring something she couldn't parse. She slid down the blue ice on her hip, strongbox grinding beneath her, and fetched up behind a serac the size of a chapel, lungs full of knives.

Through the fog she saw it happen — the way you see a wreck through bad water, in pieces.

Corliss, bleeding, furious, abandoning the gunfight entirely, closing on Coral with a knife drawn from his boot — because Corliss, she understood dimly, was a man who finished things personally. Coral meeting him. The two of them going down together onto the slick ice, rolling, the knife flashing, Coral's hands — those hands that had pulled a hundred things out of the sea — locked on Corliss's wrist. They fought the way drowning men fight, silent and total, sliding across the glacier in a grinding tangle, and the flankers couldn't shoot for fear of hitting their principal, and Tam's rifle covered the narrows so no more could come down, and it was just the two of them, alone on all that white, the whole war shrunk to a circle of churned snow ten feet wide.

Juno crawled to the serac's edge, rifle up, and could not fire. They were welded together, turning, turning.

Corliss got the knife free.

She saw the blade rise. She heard Ada scream. And then Coral did the thing he had done his whole life, the only thing he had ever truly mastered: he stopped fighting the current and let it take him. He threw his weight backward, both of them, off balance, off the edge of the shelf where the glacier calved away in a blue cliff over the valley —

— and let go.

Corliss, knife raised, momentum committed, went over the edge into the fog. There was a moment — Juno would carry it forever — where the man seemed almost to hang, coat flaring, face turned up with an expression not of fear but of pure astonishment, as though the mountain had broken a rule he'd believed applied to everyone else.

Then the fog took him, and the mountain received him, and the sound of his arrival came up the ice a long few seconds later, soft and final, like a door closing.

Coral lay on his back at the lip, arms spread, chest heaving, snow steaming off him. Alive. Two inches from the edge. Alive.

* * *

Afterward, Juno could never reconstruct how the rest of it ended — only that it ended. The syndicate men, seeing their principal fall, lost whatever held them together; Tam's rifle picked two more off the ice before the rest broke uphill and vanished into the fog, and the fog, mercifully, closed behind them like water over a sinking thing. The company gathered at the calved edge, six becoming five, and no one spoke for a long time.

Tam sat down heavily in the snow, and this time he did not pretend it was warmth. Wren knelt and unwound his bandage without asking, and the stain beneath it made even Ada look away. "I'm fine," Tam said, which fooled no one, and Wren said, "You're a liar," which was truer, and bound it tighter anyway.

Coral stood at the edge and looked down into the valley a long time. When he turned, his face was grey and there was blood on his collar — Corliss's or his own, Juno couldn't tell — and his eyes went around them all, counting, the way he counted breaths.

"Verity's a day and a half down," he said. "Trees by dark. We walk."

They walked. Down the long glacier, down through the mist that thinned as they fell, down past the treeline where the snow gave way to wet black earth and the first pines rose around them like a congregation. Nobody sang. Nobody celebrated. They stumbled and caught each other and stumbled on, and somewhere in the blue dusk Juno found herself supporting Tam on one side and Wren on the other, and realized she was humming — some old salvage-boat song, tuneless, endless — and that Tam was humming it too, feverish, half-asleep, keeping time.

Alive. Changed. Five of them descending out of the clouds with forty dead men's names on Juno's back.

It was full dark when they saw the lights of Verity in the valley floor, scattered and gold, and heard — faint on the cold air, impossible, unmistakable — the whistle of the night train pulling into the rail town, sounding, to Juno's ears, exactly like a bell being rung at the end of a very long dive, telling them all: *surface now. Surface now. Come up slowly, and live.*
