## 20. Deliverance

The valley opened the way a wound does — slowly, and all at once.

Ada Voss had spent eleven years imagining this descent, and in every version of it she had been alone. She had pictured herself coming down out of the Kestrels with her father's strongbox strapped to her back like a pilgrim's burden, walking into Verity at dusk with frost in her hair and no one to tell her she'd made it. In those imaginings there was always silence at the end of the trail, because grief is a country you travel solo whether you want to or not.

Instead there were four of them stumbling down the switchbacks behind her, half-frozen, hollow-eyed, smelling of woodsmoke and blood and mule, and Tam riding slung across Juno's spare horse like cargo they refused to treat as cargo. Wren was singing under her breath — some tuneless thing she'd invented on the glacier, three notes repeated like a charm against bad luck — and Coral walked at the rear as he always did, counting them the way he counted breaths underwater. Ada had watched him do it for two hundred miles. He never seemed to know he was doing it. His lips moved faintly: *one, two, three, four.* Making sure the mountain hadn't taken anyone else.

The trees came first. After days above the treeline, the sight of actual timber — black spruce standing shoulder to shoulder down the slopes, snow smoking off their branches in the wind — nearly undid her. Then the smell of the valley reached them: cut lumber, coal smoke, horse dung, the yeast-sour breath of a brewery. Civilization has an odor, and after the sterile cold of the high country it hit like brandy.

Then the rails.

They appeared around a bend of the river as casually as anything in the world — two lines of rust-brown steel running straight through the snow toward the town, humming faintly with the memory of trains. Ada stopped when she saw them and could not, for a long moment, make her legs work.

Rails meant schedules. Rails meant the outside world, telegraph wires, newspapers, men in good coats who would take one look at a gaunt woman leading a band of fugitives down out of the Kestrels and reach for their conclusions. Rails also meant Judge Ellery Marsh, who took the morning train from the territorial capital on the second Tuesday of every month, sat in the rented courthouse office over the feed store from ten until four, and drank his tea scalding hot because he said lukewarm tea was a lie about time.

It was the second Tuesday.

"Verity," Wren announced, pointing with her chin at the town strung along the river below them — forty buildings, three streets, a church steeple wearing snow like a borrowed hat. "That's it? That's the whole thing?"

"That's the whole thing," Ada said.

"It's small."

"So are matches."

Behind her, she heard Coral huff something that might have been a laugh. When she glanced back, his face was grey with exhaustion, cracked lips, eyes rimmed red from wind — and still he was counting. *Four.* All four. The mountain had tried its best and come away empty-handed, and whatever that had cost him, he was carrying it privately, the way divers carry pressure in their ears until they're safely up.

They went down into Verity as the lamps were being lit.

* * *

The town received them the way small towns receive strangers in winter: with narrowed eyes and open stoves. Ada led them past the assay office, past the saloon where a piano stumbled through a waltz, past the livery where a boy gawked at Tam's blood-soaked sling and was called inside by his mother before he could ask questions. She knew these streets. She had walked them for six years, patient as erosion, buying flour from the miller whose brother-in-law worked in the judge's office, mending the church's altar cloth, sitting with Widow Carrow through two bad winters so that when the widow's nephew — a clerk in the federal survey — mentioned Judge Marsh liked honey in his tea, Ada heard about it within the hour.

Six years of small kindnesses, banked like cordwood. Today she intended to draw on all of it.

First, though: the hotel. A real floor. A doctor for Tam, who had gone frighteningly quiet in the last mile, his fever finally burning through the bravado he'd used to hold it off. The hotel keeper, a broad woman named Ostrey, looked at the five of them — mud to the eyebrows, frostbitten, armed, one of them unconscious — and Ada braced for the door to close.

Instead Mrs. Ostrey stepped aside and said, "Baths are extra," which in Verity was as good as a welcome hymn.

While the others were fed and scrubbed, Ada sat by Tam's bed while the town doctor — old Ferro, hands like tree roots — cleaned and repacked the wound in his shoulder and muttered the way doctors mutter when they mean *you should have died twice already.* Tam surfaced from the fever enough to grip her wrist with his good hand.

"The logs," he rasped. "Don't let go of them till they're in his hands. Not his office. His *hands.*"

"I know."

"And Ada." His eyes found hers, fever-bright but level. "The ones on the ship. Say their names. All forty. Don't let it be a number. Numbers get filed. Names get remembered."

She held his gaze until he slept, and then she sat a while longer in the lamplight, and what she felt was not triumph. It was closer to the moment at the end of a long dive — if she understood Coral right — when you finally start up and the weight begins to lift off your chest, and you discover how much of it you'd simply stopped noticing.

* * *

Judge Ellery Marsh kept his office above the feed store, up a staircase that smelled of grain and lamp oil, behind a door with frosted glass and his name painted in gold flaking at the corners. At nine minutes past ten on Wednesday morning, Ada knocked.

"Come."

He was exactly as she remembered: seventy-odd, spare as a fence rail, white hair cropped short, reading spectacles pushed up into his hairline. He did not rise. He looked at her over the top of a legal journal for a long moment, and Ada understood that he was taking inventory — the frost-scars on her cheeks, the way she held her left arm close, the mud of the high country still dried in the seams of her coat.

"You look," he said, "like a woman who has been somewhere I told you not to go."

"You never told me not to go anywhere, Judge. You only declined to help."

"I decline to be complicit. It's different." He set the journal down. "Sit. You're dripping on my floor."

She sat. She did not drip; she had made sure of that, standing in the alley behind the hotel for ten minutes beating the worst of the trail off her clothes, because Ellery Marsh respected preparation the way other men respected rank. From the oilcloth satchel she had carried for two hundred miles she brought out the ledger — her father's engineering logbook, water-stained, its spine broken, wrapped now in oilskin and string — and beneath it the sealed strongbox of duplicate records, charts, and correspondence recovered from the cache above the treeline.

She laid them on his desk the way you lay cards you've been losing on for years.

Marsh did not touch them at first. He took off his spectacles, polished them on his sleeve, put them back on. Then he opened the logbook to the first page and read her father's name — *Elias Voss, Chief Assistant Engineer, hull and ballast* — written in Elias Voss's own upright hand, and Ada watched a man who had spent thirty years keeping his face flat fail, briefly, to keep it flat.

"This is the *Kestrel* material," he said quietly.

"This is the *Kestrel* material."

"The navy reported that ship lost in weather. Storm damage. There was a board of inquiry. I read the findings when I was a young attorney and thought, *how tidy.*" He turned a page. Another. His finger stopped on a dated entry, and he read it twice. "Forty souls aboard."

"Forty-one," Ada said. "My father signed on as replacement for a man who broke his ankle loading coal. Forty died. My father survived because he was in the aft ballast gallery when they opened the seacocks — deliberately, from the inside, on orders. He got into the water with a hatch cover. He lived nine more years and never once slept a whole night, and he wrote everything down because he believed someone, someday, would have the courage to be angry about it." Her voice stayed level. She had rehearsed this speech in mirrors, in kitchens, on trails, for eleven years, and it came out now worn smooth as river stone. "The weapons test failed. The new torpedo warhead detonated early during trials. The ship was compromised — hull fracture below the waterline, systems contaminated. Rather than report the failure and lose the contract, they scuttled her with the crew aboard and recorded it as weather loss. The logs prove it. Manifests, timestamps, my father's own account of the order given. Witnesses' names. Two of the officers who refused the order and were confined below."

Marsh closed the logbook with great care, as though it might bruise.

"There were inquiries," he said. "There was a widow's petition, years ago. It went nowhere. Do you know why?"

"Because there was no evidence."

"Because there was no *admissible* evidence, and because everyone who might have produced some was either dead, bought, or frightened." He looked at her over the spectacles. "And now a woman walks into my office with primary documents recovered from a sealed cache in sovereign territory, carried out of the mountains by persons unknown, pursued — I'm guessing — by persons better funded. Where are these persons unknown? Because if I act on this, Miss Voss, and I am telling you plainly that I intend to try, then every soul who touched these papers becomes a witness, and witnesses become targets. You've seen what they do to targets."

"They burned a roadhouse in Harrowgate," Ada said. "They shot at us on the scree above Sawtooth Pass. One of our people took a bullet for these papers and is lying in the Verity hotel with fever. A man named Merrick Hale died in a boathouse on the coast giving me the map that led here, and his last words were a promise he asked a stranger to keep." She leaned forward. "You want to know where the persons unknown are, Judge? They're downstairs eating eggs. They gave up everything they had to carry this. So yes. I've seen what they do to targets. We came anyway."

Silence. Outside, a dray rattled past; somewhere below, the feed store scales clanked. Marsh removed his spectacles again and pressed the bridge of his nose, and when his hand came away Ada saw that the years had caught up with his face all at once, the way they do.

"Eleven years ago you wrote me your first letter," he said. "You were twenty-four. You wrote that your father had been murdered by his own government and asked me to help you prove it. I replied that without evidence, belief was just grief wearing a suit. I have regretted that sentence for a decade, Miss Voss. I want you to know that."

"I don't need the apology."

"No. You need the subpoena, the certified copies, the affidavits, and a federal hearing before the territorial commission — none of which exists yet, and all of which I am going to spend the rest of my career creating." He stood, and this time he rose fully, an old man unfolding himself with deliberate dignity, and he extended his hand across the desk. "Give me the originals. I'll have copies made under lock by my own clerk — a man I'd trust with my mother's grave. The originals go into the territorial vault today, witnessed and sealed. And you—" he paused, hand still out— "will need to testify. Formally. On the record. With your name attached in print."

"I've had my name attached for eleven years," Ada said. "It's the dead who've been anonymous."

She shook his hand. His grip was dry and strong and trembled very slightly, and she chose to believe it was not age.

At the door she stopped. "There were forty names in that manifest, Judge. When this goes public — however it goes — I want every one of them read aloud. Not summarized. Read. My father kept a list of who they were. Fireman, cook, gunner's mate, a boy of seventeen from Harrowgate named Peter Lassiter whose mother waited nine years for a body that was never coming home because there wasn't one." Her voice frayed at last, one thread of it, and she let it. "Numbers get filed. Names get remembered."

Marsh was quiet a moment. "I'll read them myself," he said. "All forty. Slowly. Let the room get uncomfortable."

* * *

She came down the stairs into the street and the light hit her like a wave, hard white noon off the snow, and for a moment she stood blind on the boardwalk with her hand on the rail, breathing.

It was done. Or begun — begun was truer, and truer was enough. Eleven years of carrying, and the weight had changed hands. Somewhere upstairs a careful old man was unlocking a vault, and her father's handwriting was going to sit in the dark behind iron no longer dependent on her pulse to protect it.

She found them at the hotel dining room, crowded around one table meant for two, because none of them had yet learned to sit apart. Juno was teaching Wren to play cribbage with a deck missing two cards and arguing the rules as she went. Tam sat propped in a chair by the stove, grey but awake, one arm bound, watching the game with the expression of a man relearning that the world contained small things worth watching. Coral stood at the window with coffee he wasn't drinking, looking out at the rail line, and when Ada came in he turned, and he didn't ask. He just looked at her face, and whatever he saw there made him nod once, slow, like a diver confirming depth.

"Well?" Juno said, fanning the cards.

"It's done. Copies by tonight. Originals in the vault. Testimony on the record, my name in the papers." She pulled a chair to the table and sat among them, among the cribbage board and the missing cards and the smell of burnt coffee, and felt the strangeness of a future with no map in it. "Hale's promise is kept. My father's coming home, in the only way left to him."

Nobody cheered. That wasn't their way. But Wren abandoned the game to throw both arms around her neck, and Juno raised her teacup in a salute — "To the late, unlamented map" — and Tam lifted his good hand off the blanket and held it out flat, palm up, and Coral set his own hand on it, and then Juno's, and Wren's, a stack of scarred and frostbitten hands above the cribbage board like something being sworn or something being built.

"To names," Tam said hoarsely. "All forty."

"All forty," they said.

Later — much later, near dusk, when the others had gone up to rooms with real beds — Ada stood at the hotel window watching the evening train come in from the east, one headlamp swinging through the blue snow-light, smoke standing straight up in the still air. Passengers spilled onto the platform: drummers, miners, a mail clerk. And at the edge of the light, apart from the crowd, a man in a fine dark coat who did not go to the hotel, did not hire a sled, did not greet anyone — who simply stood beside the baggage car and looked, unhurried and thorough, up the length of Verity's single main street, the way a man looks over merchandise.

Ada had seen that coat before, in a roadhouse kitchen in Harrowgate, moments before the knives came out. Corliss was supposed to be dead on the ice of Kestrel Pass. But Corliss had employers, and employers, she thought with a cold settling in her chest, rarely send only one man.

She turned from the window and went to find Coral.
