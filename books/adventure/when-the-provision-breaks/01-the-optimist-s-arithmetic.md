## 1. The Optimist's Arithmetic

The pie at the Blue Flapjack was the kind of pie that had never seen an actual blueberry. It was a rumor of pie, a pie-shaped promise, and I ordered a second slice anyway because when your life is going badly you learn to take comfort where it's cheap.

Marisol Vane sat across from me in the corner booth with her hands folded like a woman about to say grace, which in a sense she was. She wore a butter-yellow blazer that probably cost more than my truck, and she smiled the way she always smiled—warmly, patiently, like the whole world was a slightly slow child she'd taken a personal interest in.

"Gideon," she said. "You look terrible."

"Thanks. I've been working on it."

"No, really. You've got that look. The one men get when they've spent a season chasing weather and caught nothing but fuel receipts." She picked up her coffee, sipped, set it down with the delicacy of someone handling evidence. "Tell me about the season."

"You were there for the numbers."

"I was there for the *payments*, honey. The numbers are different." She opened a leather folio—I'd never met anyone else who carried a leather folio to a diner off Highway 93—and smoothed a page flat with two fingers. "So let's do the arithmetic together. It'll be fun. I love arithmetic."

Nobody on earth loved arithmetic less than Marisol Vane claimed to.

I looked out the window instead. The parking lot held four vehicles: my rig, a rancher's dually, a family sedan with a cracked taillight, and Marisol's silver Escalade, parked at an angle that suggested it owned the lot and everything adjacent to it, including the highway and possibly the horizon. Beyond the lot, the land ran flat and brown toward the Sawtooths, which stood up in the distance like a jaw full of broken teeth. From here they looked harmless. That's the trick mountains play on you from a hundred miles out. Everything looks manageable from far away. Debt included.

"The season," I said finally. "We chased eleven cells. Intercepted four. Got good data on two."

"And sold that data to?"

"One university. One insurance consortium. And a documentary outfit that stiffed us on the final invoice."

"Mm." She made a small note. The pen made a sound like a insect dying. "And the truck repairs after the Hailstone County rollover?"

"That wasn't a rollover. It was a controlled descent into a ditch."

"It cost nine thousand dollars."

"It cost eight seven fifty, and I haggled."

She smiled wider, and I understood, not for the first time, that I was a small animal negotiating with something patient. "Gideon. Sweetheart. Do you know what I admire about you?"

"My honesty."

"Your *optimism*. Genuinely. Most people in your position would have stopped believing in themselves around April. You kept believing clear through September. That takes a special constitution." She turned the folio around so I could read it, though I didn't need to; I knew the number the way you know your own blood pressure, or the exact weight of a stone in your chest. "One hundred forty-two thousand. Give or take interest accrued since we last spoke, which—let's be precise—is now one hundred forty-six thousand three hundred and ten dollars."

I did the thing where you don't react. I've gotten good at it. Storm chasers learn early that panic is just another form of bad data collection. Your hands shake, your judgment goes, and then you drive into the thing you should have driven around.

"Pie's good here," I said.

"It is not," said Marisol, "and we both know it, and I think it says something lovely about you that you keep ordering it." She took the plate away from me—not rudely, exactly, more like a mother clearing crayons before a serious conversation—and folded her hands again. "Now. Here's what I want to talk about. Not the debt. The way out of it."

"There isn't one. That's how debt works. I've read about it."

"See, that's pessimism, and pessimism is just laziness wearing a trench coat." She slid a single sheet of paper across the formica. It was heavier stock than diner paper. It had a letterhead. "I have a client with a problem, and you are uniquely positioned to solve it, and if you solve it, your number stops being one-forty-six and starts being zero. Not reduced. Zero. Paid. Closed. A clean slate, Gideon. I know how much you want one."

I should have pushed the paper back right then. I want to be honest about that, because later—when things got complicated, when people started using words like *vanishing* and *hunted*—I told myself I hadn't known. And that was true. But some part of me, the part that reads barometric pressure drops and gets a metallic taste at the back of the tongue, that part knew the paper was too heavy and the offer was too clean and Marisol Vane had never once in her life done anything without a second ledger running underneath the first.

I read it anyway.

*Salvage and recovery contract.* Government survey equipment, abandoned in the high country of the Sawtooth Range following the disappearance of a contracted field team. Client: Meridian Recovery Partners LLC. Scope: locate and retrieve specified assets—survey instruments, data storage units, communications equipment—in intact condition. Compensation: full discharge of outstanding obligations plus operational expenses.

"Meridian Recovery Partners," I said. "Who are they?"

"A client."

"What do they recover?"

"Equipment, mostly." Her smile didn't move a millimeter. "Gears, valves, occasionally reputations."

"And the government team? The one that abandoned all this expensive equipment?"

"Went missing." She said it lightly, the way you'd say *went to lunch*. "Back in August. Terrible business. Weather, most likely. An early storm caught them. The range is unforgiving in autumn."

"They went missing in August?"

"Early storm."

"In *August*."

"Gideon." She reached across the table and patted my hand once, twice, like tamping soil over a seed. "You're a weather man. You know better than anyone that storms don't check the calendar. Now—the equipment. There's a manifest attached. Survey-grade instruments, satellite uplinks, hard drives full of hydrological data. The client wants it recovered before snowfall makes the high country inaccessible. You have, conservatively, three weeks. Maybe less."

"Why me?"

"Because you're good in the mountains, you're desperate enough to say yes, and—" she counted these off on manicured fingers, "—because you're discreet. I've never known you to ask questions you weren't paid to ask."

"I'm asking them now."

"And I adore it. Keep going. It's very healthy." She retrieved the paper, squared its edges against the table, and tucked it back into the folio with the tenderness other people reserve for photographs of their children. "There's a condition, of course. There's always a condition; that's what makes it a contract and not a gift. You recover the equipment and deliver it to a location we'll provide. All of it. Inventory verified. If items are missing or damaged, the deal reverts to standard terms and your number keeps growing at its current rate, which I'll remind you is a rate you agreed to, with a signature, while sober, in this very booth, fourteen months ago."

"I remember the booth."

"I know you do. You looked at it the way a man looks at a jail cell he's about to decorate." She stood, buttoned the yellow blazer, and left cash on the table—too much cash, obviously, because leaving exact change would have been beneath her. "Think it over tonight. Call me by noon tomorrow. And Gideon?"

"Yeah."

"You're going to say yes. We both know it. But I want you to know that I hope you say yes quickly, because winter doesn't negotiate, and neither, really, do I." She smiled at me one last time, warm as a heat lamp, and walked out to her Escalade, which pulled onto the highway going ten under the limit, because Marisol Vane never hurried for anybody. People who hurry, she told me once, are people who aren't sure they'll arrive.

* * *

I sat in the booth for a long time after she left, long enough that the waitress refilled my coffee twice without asking and gave me the particular look waitresses give men who are doing math with their lives.

Here's the honest version of what happened in my head, in order:

First, relief. Ugly, immediate, shameful relief. Zero. The word lit up in me like a instrument panel at night. One hundred forty-six thousand dollars gone, erased, forgiven, in exchange for a hike. I'd done harder hikes for free. I'd done harder hikes for *fun*, back when fun was still a category available to me.

Second, the questions. Because the questions were already lining up, patient as weather. Why does a shell company care about used government gear? Why is the insurance angle absent—no replacement valuation, no claim paperwork? Why hire a broke storm chaser instead of a licensed recovery firm? And why, why, why, did Marisol—a woman whose entire business model was making sure debts stayed alive and breeding—offer to kill mine outright?

Third, and last, and quietest: the Sawtooths through the window, going blue as the light dropped. Somewhere up in that country a team of government scientists had walked away—or been made to walk away—from every piece of equipment they owned, in August, during what every record I could pull would later confirm was the driest, calmest stretch of the summer.

People don't leave gear like that. I'd learn exactly how true that was, later, standing in the middle of it. But I knew it even then, in my gut, the same place I know a wall cloud from a shelf cloud, the same place that has kept me alive through eleven years of driving toward the thing everyone else drives from.

I called Dee Okafor from the pay phone outside, because my cell had one bar and no faith. She answered on the fourth ring.

"Quintana." Her voice was flat as the valley floor. "Somebody die?"

"I need a guide into the Sawtooths. High country. Three weeks' window, maybe less."

A pause. In the background of her line I could hear wind and a dog and something metal being worked on. "What's in the Sawtooths?"

"Government equipment nobody's come back for."

Another pause, longer. When Dee Okafor paused like that, it meant she was reading the sentence underneath your sentence. She'd spent twelve years as a park ranger reading the country that way, until the country and the service both chewed her up and spat her out, and she'd landed in a cabin outside Stanley fixing generators and not talking about why.

"Whose money?" she asked.

"Mine. Sort of. It clears a debt."

"So somebody else's money." I could hear her deciding. I could hear the exact moment she decided, the way you hear a river decide to go around a boulder instead of over it. "Pickup runs or it doesn't. Mine burns oil like a politician burns promises. I want sixty percent."

"Forty."

"Fifty-five, and I don't ask what you're really hauling out, and you don't lie to me when I figure it out. Because I will figure it out, Quintana. I always do. It's the only thing I was ever actually good at."

"Deal," I said, and hung up, and stood there in the cold with the receiver humming, looking north at the teeth of the range catching the last orange light.

Zero, I thought. A clean slate. All I had to do was walk into mountains that had swallowed a whole team of professionals, collect their belongings, and not ask what ate them.

The first snow of the season was supposed to hold off three more weeks.

It didn't.
