## 2. Contract of Convenience

The contract came in a manila envelope with no return address, which should have been my first clue. My second clue was that Marisol Vane paid for the envelope to be couriered to my motel room by a kid on a bicycle who wouldn't look me in the eye and left before I could tip him. People who want you comfortable don't send contracts by courier. People who want you committed do.

I sat on the bed—the motel's only chair had a broken leg and I'd learned that lesson the hard way two nights ago—and spread the pages out on the comforter like a dealer laying out a losing hand.

The paper itself was nice. Heavy stock, crisp laser printing, a letterhead that said **HIGHLINE RECOVERY SERVICES, LLC** in a font designed to make you think of mountain air and clean government work. Highline Recovery Services, LLC. I said it out loud to the empty room, testing it for weight. It weighed nothing. That was the problem.

Here's what fourteen years of chasing storms teaches you about paperwork, and it's the same thing it teaches you about supercells: the dangerous ones are the ones that look organized. A storm that's all chaos and no structure will scare you but probably won't kill you. It's the one with the clean, rotating updraft, the crisp striations, the tidy little hook echo on radar—that's the one that drops an EF-4 on your truck while you're admiring its geometry. Structure is what danger looks like when it's serious.

Highline Recovery Services, LLC was very structured.

I started where you're supposed to start, at the beginning, even though I already knew how this movie ended because Marisol had told me the plot over eggs. *Salvage and recovery of government survey equipment, Sawtooth Range, Idaho. Client seeks retrieval of specified assets prior to seasonal closure of access routes.* Standard language. Professional language. The kind of language that gets written by someone billing four hundred dollars an hour to make sure nobody gets sued.

Page three was where the money lived, so page three is where I went first, because I'm not a fool, just broke, which is different in important ways.

*Compensation Schedule:* Base fee of eighteen thousand dollars upon verified recovery of listed assets. Plus per-diem. Plus a hazard multiplier of 1.4x "for operations above 8,000 feet MSL." Plus—this was the part that made me sit up straight enough to hear my spine pop—a completion bonus of nine thousand if the full manifest was recovered within twenty-one days of contract execution.

Twenty-seven thousand dollars, all in. I did the arithmetic twice, then a third time hoping the number would shrink out of embarrassment. It didn't. Twenty-seven thousand against a debt that Marisol had cheerfully recalculated, over breakfast, at forty-one thousand and change, "and that's being generous with yourself, Gideon, which I always am."

It would nearly clear me. Not quite—Marisol's percentages had a way of growing like mold in a wet basement—but close enough that the light at the end of the tunnel stopped looking like another train.

Which is exactly when I did what any sensible person does with money that looks too good. I asked why.

*

The answer was on page five, and it took me twenty minutes to find it because it was dressed up as boring.

*Insurance Valuation of Recovered Assets,* the section header read, followed by a table. And here's the thing—I know equipment. I've spent half my life buying, breaking, repairing, and reselling weather and field gear, because storm chasing pays like a slot machine that mostly eats quarters, and you learn to stretch. Radar units, GPS suites, satellite messengers, hydrological instruments, cold-weather kit. I can price most of it from memory within ten percent.

The survey team's gear, according to Highline Recovery Services, LLC, was worth one point six million dollars.

I laughed. Actually laughed, alone in a motel room in Mountain Home, Idaho, loud enough that somebody in the next room knocked on the wall. One point six million. Let me tell you what one point six million dollars of field equipment looks like: it looks like a small research station. It looks like a helicopter with instruments bolted to it. What it does not look like is the manifest attached to this contract, which read like the inventory of a very well-funded university field course. Two multispectral scanners—fine, those are pricey, call them sixty grand each new. A stream-gauge telemetry array. Soil moisture probes. Four satellite phones. Drones, batteries, tents, generators. Even pricing everything at retail, brand new, with the generous math of a man trying to impress an insurance adjuster, I couldn't get past three hundred thousand. Probably less. The gear had been sitting in the high country through a summer and into fall; half of it would be water-damaged or dead-battery ruined.

So the client was paying me twenty-seven thousand to recover equipment they'd insured—and were therefore claiming to some insurer—at more than five times its actual value.

I put the pages down and looked out the window at the parking lot, where my chase truck sat under a sky the color of old dishwater, wearing its debt like a second paint job. And I thought about what it means when the numbers on a piece of paper disagree with reality that badly.

There are innocent explanations. There really are. Insurance valuations run hot sometimes—people pad them, adjusters negotiate down, everybody knows the game. Maybe the client was sloppy. Maybe the valuation included replacement costs, deployment costs, the cost of the science lost along with the hardware. Sure. I've heard smarter lies than that told with straighter faces.

But innocent explanations don't usually come wrapped in shell companies.

Because that was the other thing, the thing I'd been circling since the courier kid pedaled away. I flipped back to page one and looked again at the client block. *Client:* Highline Recovery Services, LLC. *Contracting Officer:* R. Delgado. *Client Address:* a suite number in Boise that I happened to know, because I'd once chased a hail core through that part of town, was a mail drop above a nail salon. No phone number in the contract. No email. All communications routed through—"routed through" is such a clean phrase—all communications routed through the office of M. Vane, Financial Services.

Marisol Vane, who held my debt. Who had smiled at me across a diner booth with the warmth of a heat lamp and offered me this one clean way out. Who was now, apparently, also the sole communication channel between me and my mysterious employer.

I got up and made the motel coffee, which is to say I boiled water and poured it over something that had once dreamed of being a bean, and I drank it standing at the window, and I let myself think clearly for about ninety seconds, which is longer than I usually allow.

Here's what ninety seconds bought me:

One. The client didn't exist, or existed only as a mailbox. Whoever actually wanted this gear recovered was hiding behind two layers of paper.

Two. The insurance valuation was fiction, which meant either someone was committing insurance fraud on a grand scale, or the valuation wasn't really about insurance at all—it was about establishing, on paper, that this equipment was worth whatever someone later needed it to be worth. Numbers as weapons. You write a big enough number next to an object and suddenly the object matters more than anything near it. More than, say, the people who were carrying it.

Three. Nobody had mentioned the survey team. Not Marisol, not the contract. Eight pages of legal prose about equipment, liability, access routes, weather windows, and chain of custody, and not one word—not one—about the four human beings who had walked into the Sawtooths in June and never walked out. Search and rescue had stood down in August. The story had run for two days on Boise news and died. And this contract treated them like they'd never existed, which, given everything else on these pages, struck me less as an oversight than as a policy.

I stood there holding my terrible coffee and I felt the feeling I get maybe twice a year, the one I've learned to trust more than radar, more than the NWS, more than my own eyes. Storm chasers call it the smell. Some people say a tornado has a smell—green, they say, ozone and cut grass and something else—and scientists roll their eyes, but every chaser I've ever respected has some version of it, a prickle at the base of the skull that says *the atmosphere is organizing itself around something violent and you are standing in the wrong place.*

The contract smelled green.

*

And here's the part I'm not proud of, the part I've gone back and forth about telling you because a narrator who flatters himself is worse than useless. I signed it anyway.

Not immediately. Let me be fair to myself for one more paragraph. I did due diligence, or the broke man's version of it, which is mostly free. I looked up Highline Recovery Services LLC on the motel's Wi-Fi, which crawled like a wounded animal, and found exactly what I expected: a registered entity, incorporated eight months ago, no website, no filings beyond the minimum, officer names that led nowhere. R. Delgado was either a ghost or a name shared by eleven people in Idaho, none of whom answered the number I found. I called the mail-drop suite anyway. It rang four times and went to a voicemail box that hadn't been set up. The robotic voice said the mailbox was full, which somehow felt like the most honest thing I'd encountered all day.

I drove to the diner—because my room had become unbearable with thinking in it—and I ordered more coffee I couldn't afford and I laid the contract out on the formica like a patient on a table and I went through it line by line looking for the trap, the clause, the thing that would let me say *see, there it is, I can't sign this, my hands are tied.*

You know what I found? A clean contract. That's the hell of it. Liability waivers—standard. Indemnification—standard, and honestly mild; they weren't even asking me to waive claims for gross negligence, which any real lawyer would have flagged, except I can't afford a real lawyer and neither, presumably, could whoever drafted this, or they didn't care, because—and this thought arrived cold, halfway through my third cup—they didn't expect there to ever be a dispute. Contracts get litigated when both parties survive to argue. A contract written by people who assume you'll come back is careful. This one was polite the way a long fall is polite.

Chain of custody provisions: recovered assets to be sealed, documented, and delivered to a designated point, unopened. Fine. Photograph everything, log everything—actually good practice, and it protected me as much as them. Non-disclosure: I couldn't talk about the contract's terms for ninety days after completion. Slightly unusual, not damning. Weather contingency clauses that showed someone knew the range: access windows closing mid-November, snow-load assumptions, helicopter exclusion zones above certain ridgelines. Whoever wrote this knew the Sawtooths. Knew them specifically. There was a paragraph about avoiding "designated watershed infrastructure corridors" that read, to a normal person, like environmental boilerplate, and read, to a person who had grown up around irrigation districts and diversion rights, like a map drawn in negative space. Don't go here. Don't look at this. The contract was telling me where the secret was by telling me not to trip over it.

I sat with that for a long time. Long enough that the waitress refilled me twice without asking and started giving me the look waitresses give men who've been sitting alone too long with documents.

Forty-one thousand dollars. That was the number sitting on my chest. Forty-one thousand owed to a woman who smiled like sunshine and collected like gravity, and winter coming, and a chase truck worth maybe nine thousand running, and a season of nothing behind me—a season of dry lightning and storms that died over the divide, of driving twelve hundred miles for a shelf cloud that never organized, of eating gas-station food and calling it strategy. I was good at my work. That's the bitter joke of my life. I am genuinely, verifiably excellent at reading the sky, and the sky spent this entire year not paying me for it. Talent doesn't service debt. Debt doesn't care how well you forecast.

Twenty-seven thousand dollars. Ninety days of non-disclosure. A ghost client. An insurance number that lied. A silence where four missing people should have been.

I took out my pen.

I want to be precise about the moment, because precision is the only honesty I have left in this story. I didn't sign in a rush of greed, and I didn't sign in ignorance—I'd read every page, I'd done the math, I'd smelled the green in the air and named it correctly. I sat in that diner knowing, with the specific clarity of a man watching a wall cloud rotate, that this contract was not what it claimed to be, that I was being used for something, that the money was bait and I was the kind of animal that takes bait, and I signed it anyway. Three signatures, initial here, here, and here, date, print name. My handwriting came out steady, which surprised me. I'd have expected it to shake.

Why? Because winter debt has teeth, and I'd been bitten enough to know the difference between a risk and a certainty. Marisol was a certainty. Her interest accrued like weather in July, inevitable and total. Whatever this contract was, it was money moving toward me instead of away from me, and a drowning man doesn't audit the rope.

That's the version I told myself, anyway, in the diner, signing. The drowning man and the rope. It's a good story. It has sympathy built into it.

The truer version is smaller and uglier: I wanted my life back. I wanted to walk into that diner one last time, slide an envelope across to Marisol Vane, and watch her face do something other than smile. I wanted to owe nothing—to no one, ever again—and I wanted it so badly that when the price of wanting it turned out to be complicity in something I couldn't yet see the shape of, I paid. I told myself I'd figure out the shape later. Later, I'd learn, is where complicity lives. It's always later. You sign now and understand later, and the understanding arrives with interest, same as everything else in my life.

I folded the contract into its envelope. Outside, the dishwater sky had begun to organize—there was a line building over the Owyhees, flat and mean, the kind of front that means business without drama—and some old reflex in me checked it, graded it, filed it. Marginal. Dry underneath. Nothing for me there.

Nothing for me anywhere, except north, into mountains where four people had vanished and a ghost company wanted its million-dollar fiction back.

I finished the coffee, left a tip calculated to the dime, and stepped out into the cold. Then I took out my phone and pulled up the one contact I'd been avoiding, the name I'd sworn I'd never need: Dee Okafor. Last I'd heard she was living outside Stanley, doing odd jobs, drinking too much, knowing things about the Sawtooth backcountry that no map admitted to and no agency would certify anymore.

If I was going to walk into a lie, I figured, I wanted someone beside me who'd already been lied to by professionals.

She picked up on the sixth ring. She didn't say hello. She said:

"Whoever this is, it better be worth me stopping what I'm doing."

"What are you doing?" I asked.

"Sharpening an axe," Dee Okafor said, "and resenting the world."

"Dee," I said, "how do you feel about the Sawtooths in November?"

There was a pause long enough for me to hear wind on her end of the line, and something else under the wind, rhythmic, patient—the sound of steel on stone.

"Gideon," she said finally, "you sound like trouble with a budget."

She wasn't wrong. But she said yes, and that's how it started.
