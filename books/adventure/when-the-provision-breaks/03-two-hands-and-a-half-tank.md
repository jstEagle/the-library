## 3. Two Hands and a Half Tank

There are exactly three people in the Sawtooth country who will talk to me without a lawyer present, and two of them are dead. The third is Dee Okafor, who lives in a single-wide outside Stanley with a wood stove, a dog named after a failed government program, and a reputation that got shredded in the same year my marriage did. We have that in common, plus about forty pounds of gear each and the mutual understanding that neither of us asks questions we don't want answered.

I found her the way you find anyone in that country: I drove to her place and sat in my truck until she came out.

It took an hour. She knew I was there the whole time. That's the thing about Dee—she makes you wait so she can decide what mood to meet you in. When she finally opened the door she was wearing canvas coveralls with the park service arrowhead still stitched on the shoulder, which was either sentiment or spite, and with Dee it was always both.

"Gideon Quintana," she said. "Heard your truck died twice on the Williams Creek grade last month."

"Heard you got drunk at the Stanley bar and told a Forest Service biologist exactly what you thought of his survey methodology."

"That's not why they fired me."

"I know why they fired you."

She looked at me for a long moment, and something moved behind her eyes—not warmth, exactly. Recognition. The look of one drowning person noting another.

"You want something," she said.

"I want to hire you."

"I don't do anything anymore."

"Good. This job doesn't exist."

* * *

We ended up at her kitchen table because it was November and neither of us wanted to stand outside being honest. Her dog—a rangy shepherd mix with the official name of RECOVER, which she'd gotten from a K-9 program that washed him out for being too independent—lay under the table with his chin on my boot, which I took as a better reference than any Dee could give me.

"Salvage contract," I said, and slid the folder across the table. "Government survey team went missing up in the Sawtooths in September. Equipment left in the field. Client wants it recovered before snow closes the passes."

"Client being?"

"Shell company." I said it flatly because lying to Dee Okafor was like lying to a bloodhound; she'd find the lie and then find you. "Meridian Recovery Partners. Out of Boise on paper, nowhere in fact."

"And the insurance valuation on this equipment is—" She flipped through the pages, and I watched her eyebrows climb. "Gideon. This is a hundred and forty thousand dollars of radios and flow meters. Who insures a water survey at three times replacement cost?"

"Somebody who wants it found badly."

"Or somebody who wants *you* to find something else." She closed the folder and pushed it back across the table, and for a second I thought she was going to refuse, and I felt something in my chest loosen that I didn't want to examine—the relief of being let off the hook.

Then she said, "What's the split?"

And the hook set.

"Fifty-fifty," I said. "Minus my fuel and my debt to Marisol Vane, which comes out of my half, not yours."

"Vane." She said the name the way you'd say a weather warning. "You're working for Marisol Vane now."

"I'm working *adjacent* to Marisol Vane."

"Gideon." She leaned back and crossed her arms, and I watched her run the arithmetic that people like us always run—the one where you calculate how much of yourself a job costs before you take it. "That woman smiles at you while she's counting your teeth. Whatever this salvage job actually is, it's hers. You know that."

"I know that."

"And you're taking it anyway."

"I owe her eleven thousand dollars and a season of interest, and winter has teeth, Dee. You of all people should understand winter having teeth."

She was quiet for a while. Outside, the wind came down off the peaks and rattled something loose against the side of the trailer, a sound like someone tapping on glass. RECOVER sighed from under the table.

"The survey team," she said finally. "The four people who vanished. You read the incident report?"

"Twice."

"Then you noticed what I noticed."

"That there were no tracks," I said. "No signs of struggle. Gear intact. Four adults don't walk out of camp in September without leaving a footprint, and four adults don't get taken by anything in this range that leaves the tents standing."

"So you know this isn't a salvage job."

"I know it might not be. And I know that if I turn it down, I lose everything I have, which currently fits in a pickup bed. So I'm going anyway. The only question is whether I go alone, which would be stupid, or with the one person alive who knows the Sawtooth backcountry better than the cartographers do."

"That's flattery."

"That's arithmetic. Same as yours."

* * *

She made coffee then, real coffee, and we talked terms over the steam, and I'll be honest: watching Dee Okafor negotiate was one of the small pleasures of a bad year. She didn't haggle like a person who needed money. She haggled like a person testing whether I'd flinch.

"Sixty-forty," she said. "My favor. I navigate, I make the calls on route and timing, and when I say we turn around, we turn around. No storm-chaser heroics. I've seen what your kind does in weather."

"My kind?"

"You chase storms for money and cameras. You think the sky owes you a show." She sipped her coffee. "I spent nine years learning that the sky doesn't owe anybody anything. It just kills the unprepared and calls it natural selection."

"That's a grim philosophy for a ranger."

"I'm not a ranger anymore. That's the point."

"Sixty-forty works," I said. "One condition. Nobody touches the journal if there is one. Survey teams keep field journals. If we find one, it rides out with us, and we both look at it together before anyone else does."

Something flickered across her face—surprise, maybe, or respect wearing surprise's coat.

"Why?" she said.

"Because a shell company paying triple insurance to recover radios is a company that cares about paper more than property. And because whatever those four people wrote down before they vanished, somebody thinks it's worth enough to build a trap around. I want to see it first. Don't you?"

Dee looked at me for a long time. Then she laughed—one short, rusty sound, like a gate opening after years shut.

"There he is," she said. "The guy who used to read cloud decks for fun instead of sponsors."

"I never did it for sponsors."

"No," she said. "That's what got you broke." She stuck out her hand across the table, callused palm, grip like a closing trap. "Sixty-forty. Journal comes out together. And Gideon—if I say run, you run."

"If you say run, I'll already be running."

* * *

We loaded her truck the next morning in gray light, frost on the tailgate, our breath hanging between us like small confessions. Her gear was military-neat: ropes coiled in perfect loops, radios labeled in waterproof ink, a medical kit that had been restocked with the care of someone who'd once needed one and hadn't had it. My contribution was less impressive—a half tank of fuel, a case of energy bars, and a satellite messenger I'd bought used from a man in Mountain Home who swore it worked fine, which is the kind of sentence that should come with its own weather advisory.

"Half tank?" Dee said, looking into my fuel gauge like it had personally insulted her.

"Station in Stanley opens at seven."

"It's six forty."

"Then we're early."

She shook her head, but she was almost smiling, and I counted that as the day's first victory. We drove north as the sun came up over the range and lit the Sawtooths pink and gold, the whole escarpment glowing like something freshly forged, beautiful and indifferent, the way mountains always are right before they ask you what you're willing to lose.

Neither of us talked much. Two people with something to prove don't need conversation; we need silence we can trust. But somewhere past the last ranch gate, Dee spoke without looking away from the road.

"For the record," she said, "if this goes sideways, I want it known I said the valuation was wrong."

"Noted."

"And if we find out what happened to those four people—and we will, because you've got that look, the one where you won't leave a question alone—I want it known I said we should have sold the story to a newspaper instead."

"Noted that too."

"Okay." She shifted into low gear as the road steepened. "Just so we're all honest. You, me, and the half tank."

The road climbed. The trees thickened. And somewhere behind us, in a valley town, a woman who smiled while she counted other people's teeth was no doubt checking her ledger, pleased with how neatly her arithmetic was working out.

At the time, I thought the worst thing waiting for us in the Sawtooths would be the weather.

I want that noted too.
