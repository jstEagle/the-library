## 4. Where the Trail Forgets Itself

The Sawtooth doesn't welcome you. It tolerates you, the way an old woman tolerates a salesman at the door—polite enough, but already counting the seconds until you leave. I'd spent eleven years learning that tolerance, first as a seasonal tech, then as a ranger with a badge and a truck and the particular arrogance of someone who thinks the land owes her honesty because she gave it hers. Then I lost the badge, and it turned out the land was the only one who'd kept its side of the bargain.

So when Gideon Quintana offered me a cut instead of cash to walk back into those canyons, I said yes faster than pride would have liked. Pride can wait in the truck.

We left the pavement at six-forty in the morning, when the light comes down the east walls like something poured rather than shone, all at once and gold to the riverbed. The lower canyon is limestone and cottonwood, water talking to itself over gravel bars, and if you've never been there you should know that it is beautiful in the specific way of places that have killed people without ever raising their voices.

"Talk to me," Gideon said from the passenger seat, because they'd figured out somewhere around mile ten yesterday that I narrate when I'm nervous, and they'd decided—correctly—that my nervousness was useful data.

"Okay." I took the switchbacks slow, letting the engine do the complaining. "This is Miller Fork. The canyon narrows for about four miles, then opens into what the old grazing maps called the Punchbowl. There's a trailhead there that hasn't had a maintained trail since the nineties. After that we're on foot."

"Wildlife?"

"Bighorn up high, maybe. Elk moving down early this year—the snowline's already at eight thousand and it's not even October. That's wrong, by the way. File that away."

"Filed." Gideon had a notebook, an actual paper notebook, and they wrote things in it with the seriousness of a person who'd learned the hard way that memory lies about weather. Storm chasers are the only people I've met who treat the sky as a colleague instead of a ceiling. It was going to be either very useful or very annoying. Possibly both.

"Mountain lions exist but will pretend they don't," I went on. "Rattlesnakes are mostly done for the season but 'mostly' is doing work in that sentence. And the river's down maybe forty percent from where it should be for this time of year."

Gideon looked up from the notebook. "Forty?"

"Give or take. USGS gauge on Miller Fork ran nine-year median flows around two-sixty CFS in late September. I drove past it yesterday. Call it one-fifty, and the gauge looked newer than the road." I let that sit. "A river doesn't lose forty percent of itself by being shy about it. Something upstream is drinking."

"Something like drought?"

"Drought takes twenty, thirty percent, and it takes it gradually, and it takes it from everything evenly. This is—" I hunted for the word. "This is selective. Like somebody put a straw in it."

Gideon wrote *straw* in the notebook, which I chose to find charming rather than alarming.

* * *

The trouble started, as trouble in that country usually does, with a road.

Miller Fork Road had been closed to vehicles three years ago—a washout took out the culvert at mile two and the Forest Service, in its infinite budget, posted a sign and called it management. Hikers could walk it. Horses could walk it. Nothing with an axle was supposed to roll past that sign except maybe a rescue rig in an emergency.

Somebody had graded it.

I stopped the truck fifty yards short of the sign and just looked for a while. Fresh blade work, and I mean fresh—not last week fresh, not even yesterday fresh. The berms were still crisp-edged, the cut banks still showing raw soil the color of a wound, no rain on it, no dust settled into the crown. The grader operator had pulled the windrows off to the downhill side neat as you please, and there were tire tracks in the new surface, multiple sets, overlapping, the kind of pattern you get when more than one vehicle has come through recently and nobody bothered to be careful about it.

"Gideon," I said. "Look at these tracks."

They got out and crouched over them the way I've seen people crouch over tracks of a different sort, reading. "Wide," they said. "Really wide stance. Duals?"

"Dual rear axles, yeah. That's a flatbed or a heavy service truck. And look at the depth." I pressed my thumb into one of the prints. The soil compressed maybe half an inch under my thumb alone; the tires had gone twice that. "These aren't pickups out for a hunt. These are loaded. Ten, twelve thousand pounds empty, more than that carrying whatever they carry."

"Recreation vehicles don't weigh that."

"Nobody recreates with duals and a grader." I stood and brushed off my hands. "And here's the part I love. This road is closed. Officially. There's a federal sign right there saying so. Which means whoever graded it either didn't know—and impossible, because you can't get a grader up here without knowing—or knew and didn't care."

"Which means they had permission or power."

"Permission or power. Yeah." I got back in the truck. My hands wanted to be busy, so I put them on the wheel and pretended they were calm. "There's a third option. They owned the people who'd give permission."

Gideon looked at me sideways. "You sound like you've met this before."

"I've met versions of it before." I put us in gear and rolled past the closed sign like the hypocrite I was, though in my defense, our contract did technically authorize vehicle access, and hypocrisy weighs less when it's contractual. "Small county, big ranching money, land agencies staffed by people whose cousins work the outfits they're supposed to regulate. It's not corruption with briefcases. It's corruption with potlucks. Everybody's at the same barbecue. Nobody wants to be the one who brings up the thing."

"And you brought up the thing once."

"Once." I kept my eyes on the road. "We'll get to that story around the campfire, if we have a campfire, and if I decide I like you by then."

"I'm very likable."

"You owe a loan shark money, Quintana. Likability is clearly not your problem."

That got a laugh out of them, short and real, and I'll admit I'd been fishing for it. There's a thing that happens between two people heading into bad country—you start trading small laughs the way climbers trade protection, placing them at intervals, testing whether they hold. Gideon laughed easy and worried hard, which is the correct ratio. I was starting to think the ratio might hold.

* * *

The canyon narrowed the way it always does, walls coming in until the sky was a blue ribbon overhead and the air went cool and mineral-smelling, seep water threading down the rock faces in dark stains. Cottonwoods along the creek had started turning, whole groves lit up yellow-green like something radioactive and lovely. A dipper worked the shallows ahead of us, bobbing on a stone, diving, popping up downstream. Dippers always struck me as the only honest birds in the West—they live exactly where the water is good and nowhere else, and if the water goes bad they simply leave. No press conferences. No committees. They just withdraw their business.

I thought about that a lot over the next few miles.

Because the water was telling on somebody. Every hundred yards or so I found myself doing the math without meaning to—creek width, depth, velocity, the little algebra you learn to run in your head after enough years of gauging stations. And every time, the answer came up short. Not catastrophically. Not the way a dam failure is short. Just... sipped. A river being drunk through a coffee stirrer, patiently, by someone who understood that the trick isn't taking the water, it's taking it slowly enough that no single measurement ever looks like theft.

"Gideon," I said, at the third gravel bar in a row that should have been wet and wasn't. "How much do you know about water rights?"

"Enough to know people kill over them in the history books."

"The history books are generous. People kill over them in the present tense; they just use lawyers and bulldozers instead of guns, most of the time." I parked us at the mouth of the Punchbowl, where the canyon opened up into that broad scoured amphitheater, and killed the engine, and the silence came in like a tide. "Here's what's bothering me. The survey team we're salvaging for—hydrology crew, right? Flow measurements, sediment sampling, the whole suite. Government contract."

"That's what the paperwork says."

"And the paperwork also says their equipment valuation is roughly triple what any of it costs retail, and the client is a shell company with a lawyer's name, so forgive me if I take 'that's what the paperwork says' with a grain of salt the size of this canyon." I got out and stretched, working the stiffness out of my knees. "But suppose the survey was real. Suppose they were really measuring Miller Fork and its tributaries. You know what a proper flow study finds, if somebody upstream is running an unlicensed diversion?"

Gideon came around the hood. "It finds the diversion."

"It finds the diversion. It timestamps it, quantifies it, and puts it in a federal record with a contract number attached. It turns a rumor into a document." I looked up-canyon, toward the country we couldn't see yet, the high granite shoulders gone pink with morning. "So the interesting question isn't why the survey team vanished. Teams vanish all the time out here—one twisted ankle, one flash flood, done. The interesting question is what a vanished team's data is worth, and to whom, and whether the salvage contract we signed is really about tripods and satellite phones."

Gideon was quiet for a moment. When they spoke, their voice had that flat careful quality I'd already learned meant they were rearranging furniture inside their own head. "You think we're not the first people looking for them."

"I think the grader didn't grade itself."

* * *

We packed for the hike—Gideon carried more than their share and pretended it was nothing, another point in their favor—and started up the old trail out of the Punchbowl. The trail itself was a lesson in neglect: brush grown hip-high across the switchbacks, cairns toppled, one long stretch where a slide had taken the tread entirely and you just picked your way up the talus and hoped your ankles held opinions of their own. Eleven years ago I'd helped maintain this stretch. There's a particular grief in walking a trail you once repaired and finding it gone back to wilderness, like visiting a student you taught who's forgotten your name.

But the wrongness kept pace with us.

At the second switchback I found boot prints—not hiker boots. Uniform-tread, deep-lugged, the kind issued by the caseful to security contractors and wildland crews, pressed hard into the dust of a trail that officially saw maybe a dozen feet a season. Two individuals, stride length suggesting tall men, moving uphill at a patrol pace. Days old, maybe three, maybe four, the edges just starting to crumb.

I didn't say anything for a quarter mile. I wanted to see whether Gideon noticed on their own.

They did. At the fourth set of prints, Gideon crouched, touched the edge of one, and said, "Same boots again. Somebody's walking this trail regularly."

"Somebody with a uniform allowance." I crouched beside them. "And look—here, and here. They stop at the same spots. Facing the same direction." I pointed up-canyon, toward the drainage where Camp Four sat per the survey team's filed itinerary. "They're not hiking. They're checking. This is a route somebody walks on a schedule, looking at something."

"Like a fence line."

"Like a fence line," I agreed, "except the property line runs through federal wilderness, and the livestock, as far as I know, is information."

We climbed on. The canyon walls fell away behind us and the country opened into the high benchlands, sage and bunchgrass going bronze, the first real cold breathing down off the peaks. Somewhere above us a raven said something unkind and flapped off with the commentary. It was, by every objective measure, a gorgeous afternoon—the kind of light that makes you forgive the West everything, the droughts and the land barons and your own personal history with both. I felt it working on me anyway, that beauty, loosening something in the chest. That's the trap of this country. It seduces you right up until it doesn't.

Then we topped the bench below Camp Four's drainage, and I stopped so fast Gideon nearly walked into my pack.

Below us, maybe half a mile out, the trail crossed a meadow before climbing into the trees. And across that meadow, plain as a signature, ran tire tracks. Vehicle tracks. Up a drainage where the nearest drivable road ended eight miles and two ridgelines south.

ATV tracks, two machines, wide knobby tread, cutting straight across ground that the Wilderness Act and three separate agency policies said no machine had any business crossing. And they weren't old. The grass lay flattened but not yet sprung back; the tread edges were still sharp; in one low spot, water hadn't yet finished silting into the impressions.

"Dee," Gideon said quietly. "Tell me there's a legal explanation."

"There's an explanation," I said. "Legal is doing a lot of hopeful work in that sentence." I scanned the tree line beyond the meadow, the way you learn to scan when you've spent years reading country for what shouldn't be there. Movement? Nothing. Sound? Wind, and the creek, and my own pulse deciding to make itself known. "Whoever's running machines up here has been doing it long enough to not care about tracks. That's not a one-time trespass. That's a commute."

Gideon pulled out the notebook, then stopped, pen hovering. "If people commute up here," they said slowly, "and the survey team camped up here, and the survey team vanished—"

"Then we might not be walking toward abandoned equipment." I resettled my pack straps, buying my hands something to do. "We might be walking toward a scene somebody's been maintaining. Keeping tidy."

The light was going long and amber across the benchland, and the peaks above us had that clean cold look they get before weather, and everything was beautiful, and none of it fit. A graded closed road. A river on a diet. Patrol boots on a dead trail. Machines in a roadless meadow. Four facts, each one small, each one deniable alone—and together they added up to a shape I recognized from the worst year of my life, the year I learned that the wilderness keeps better records than the government does, and that sometimes the only witness willing to talk is the ground.

"We can still turn around," Gideon said. Not cowardly—just honest, checking the math out loud, the way storm chasers check a storm. "Contract says recover equipment. Equipment's probably right there in camp. We grab it, we walk out, we get paid, nobody has to know anything about straws or commutes."

"Yeah," I said. "We could do that."

Neither of us moved.

Ahead, up in the timber where Camp Four waited, the ravens had gone quiet—all of them, at once, the way they do when something in the woods has stopped being scenery. I'd spent eleven years learning that silence too.

"Camp's twenty minutes," I said, and started downhill.
