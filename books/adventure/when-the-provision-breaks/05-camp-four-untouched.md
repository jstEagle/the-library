## 5. Camp Four, Untouched

The last mile, Dee stopped talking.

That was how I knew we were close. For two days she'd narrated the Sawtooth like a woman introducing me to her extended family—*that's a hanging valley, that's a beaver pond gone silted, that ridge will lie to you about its grade*—and now the commentary had dried up entirely. She just walked, and watched the ground, and every so often her jaw did this small tight thing like she was chewing on a word she refused to swallow.

We came up out of the drainage into a bench of lodgepole and granite, the kind of flat spot that mountains offer grudgingly, and there it was.

Camp Four.

I'd been picturing wreckage. I want to be honest about that. Two days of climbing through country that wanted us dead had built up an expectation in my gut: torn nylon in the trees, scattered instruments, maybe a bear problem, maybe something worse. You don't get hired to salvage gear from a vanished government team without your imagination doing push-ups at night.

What I saw instead was a camp that looked like the surveyors had stepped out to check a gauge and would be back by lunch.

Three tents, pitched taut, guy-lines dressed with the neatness of people who took pride in their work. The rain flies were still on. A tarp was strung between two trees over the cook area, angled correctly against the prevailing weather—which came out of the southwest, which meant whoever pitched it knew what they were doing. A stack of split firewood sat under a slab of bark, dry and orderly. Food hung in a bear cache between two trees, bagged and counterweighted, twelve feet up, ten feet out, textbook.

Nobody walks away from a hundred pounds of cached food. Not in September. Not with winter coming down the pipe like a freight train you can hear but not see yet.

"Dee," I said.

"I see it."

She hadn't moved past the tree line. She stood with her hand resting on the trunk of a lodgepole like she needed permission from it before entering, and I understood then that I wasn't the only one who'd been expecting wreckage. Wreckage, you can process. Wreckage has a story with an ending. This had a story with the middle ripped out.

"We should look around the perimeter first," she said. "Before we go in."

"You think somebody's—"

"I think I want to know if anyone else has been here." Her voice was flat and careful, a ranger's voice, the voice of a woman who'd once told me she could read sign so well she'd once identified a specific illegal outfitter by his horse's shoeing pattern alone. "Fresh tracks. Cut brush. Anything."

So we circled, wide, boots soft on duff, and the camp sat in the middle of its clearing being perfectly, horribly normal.

The perimeter told us nothing, which was itself information. No tracks newer than a season of melt and regrowth. No cigarette butts, no shell casings, no boot prints but the surveyors' own, crisscrossing the bench in the patterns of working people—a trail to the water source, a trail toward the creek gauge downstream, a single set heading up-slope that just ended where the rock began. The ground had kept their last days faithfully and offered no interpretation.

Dee finished the circle where we'd started it. She looked at me.

"Okay," she said. "Okay."

We went in.

* * *

Up close, the camp got worse.

Not messier—worse. Because everything was *finished*. That was the word my brain kept snagging on. The dishes in the mesh drying bag were clean. The fuel bottles were capped and stowed. One tent—the big dome, probably the gear and comms tent—had its door zipped shut from the outside, which is a thing you do when you're leaving for good or when you're leaving for an hour, and there is no way to tell which from the zipper alone, and that fact was going to live in my head rent-free for a long time.

A camp table held a spreadsheet of the team's life printed out and weighted under a rock: duty rotations, radio check schedules, a column labeled NEXT RESUPPLY with a date eleven days after the last entry anywhere. Somebody had planned to be here eleven more days. Somebody had bought those days in advance, in food and fuel, and then hadn't used them.

"Sat phone's in there," Dee said. She'd unzipped the gear tent and was crouched inside. "Come look at this."

The satellite phone lay in its padded case beside a charging setup—a folding solar panel, a battery bank, cables coiled with the fussy precision of people who understood that in the backcountry, communications equipment is religion. Dee lifted the phone out and turned it over in her hands.

"No damage," she said. "Screen's fine. Case isn't even scuffed."

"Dead battery?"

"That's the thing." She held it up so I could see the charge port. "It's dead, yeah. But it didn't die. Look—" she pointed at the battery bank, its little LED display dark, then at the solar panel, laid out flat on the tent floor rather than propped at an angle toward the sun. "Somebody turned the panel face-down. Or it fell and nobody picked it up. Either way, the phone ran down because nobody charged it. And nobody charges a sat phone they're planning to keep using."

"So they stopped using it."

"They stopped using it *on purpose*, or they stopped existing. Those are the options." She set it down gently, the way you set down something that might be evidence. Which, I was beginning to understand, all of it was. We weren't salvagers anymore. We were the first witnesses at a scene, and the scene had no body.

I hate scenes with no body. Storms have bodies. Storms leave them, or carry them off visibly, and either way the sky signs its work. This was a signature-less crime, and my skin crawled with the wrongness of it.

"Food cache next," I said, mostly because moving helped.

Dee lowered the bear bags and we went through them methodically. Dehydrated meals, counted. Coffee, half gone. A ziplock of someone's homemade jerky with a name written on masking tape—*R. OKONKWO, TOUCH AND DIE*—which under any other circumstances would have made me laugh. Everything accounted for, everything portioned, nothing grabbed in haste. When people flee a camp, they take food. It's the first law of panic. Your hands go for calories before they go for anything, including your own children sometimes, God forgive us.

This food had been left like an offering. Like the team expected to come back and eat it.

Or like they'd been given no time to pack—but no, that didn't fit either. Haste leaves a different signature. Torn stuff sacks, spilled rice, a stove left assembled. There was none of it. This wasn't flight. This was *cessation*. As if three human beings had simply been subtracted from the equation and the variables left standing.

"Journal," Dee said quietly.

"What?"

"The field journal. Survey teams keep one. Daily entries, data, decisions. If they logged a reason to leave—or logged anything strange before they left—it'll be in there." She was already moving toward the dome tent, and I noticed her hand wasn't quite steady, and I decided not to mention it because mine wouldn't have been either.

* * *

The journal wasn't in the dome tent.

We searched it anyway, thoroughly, because thoroughness is free and regret is expensive. Sleeping bags rolled. Boots by the door—*boots by the door*, three pairs, and I stood looking at them for a long moment while something cold worked its way down my spine. People do not hike out of the Sawtooth Range barefoot. People do not go anywhere barefoot. Three pairs of boots, sitting neatly side by side, belonging to three people who were not in this camp and had not, as far as the ground remembered, walked out of it.

"Gideon." Dee's voice came from the gear tent again, odd. "Waterproof case. It's here. It's—"

She backed out holding it: a hard-sided Pelican-style case, yellow, latched, with SURVEY 4 stenciled on the lid. She set it on the camp table, under the tarp, in the gray afternoon light, and we both just looked at it for a second.

"Could be empty," I said.

"Could be."

"Latch it open, then."

She did. Inside, wrapped in a dry-bag, was the field journal—a fat Rite-in-the-Rain book swollen with use—and beneath it, a slim folder of printouts, and beneath that, a GPS unit and a small digital camera. All of it dry. All of it intact. All of it *left behind*, deliberately, in the most protective container in camp, as if someone had wanted it found—or wanted it safe—from whoever came next.

Dee opened the journal with the care of a surgeon and read the last page. Then the page before it. Then she went back further, faster, her lips moving, and I watched her face do something complicated.

"Last entry is nine days ago," she said. "Routine. Flow measurements at the lower gauge. Then—" she turned pages backward, hunting, "—here. Six days before that. Listen to this." She cleared her throat and read aloud, and her voice, which had been steady through two days of bad country, wobbled on the second sentence:

*"Flows at Gauge 3 are down forty percent against seasonal norm. Upstream gauges show normal discharge. Water is going somewhere between marker 12 and the confluence, and it isn't evaporating. R. wants to push up-canyon tomorrow to find out where. M. thinks we should report first and let the agency handle it. Vote was 2-1. We go up tomorrow."*

Silence. The kind of silence that has texture, that presses on your eardrums.

"And then?" I asked.

"And then nothing about what they found up-canyon. Just—" she flipped forward, page by page, "—entries getting shorter. Here: *'Found it. Details to follow when we've documented everything.'* And here, the last one: *'M. says we should not transmit. Says radios may not be ours alone. Keeping everything offline until we're out. We move down-valley tonight, all together.'*"

Dee closed the journal. She didn't look at me right away. When she did, her eyes had that flat bright quality I'd started to recognize as the look of a woman re-evaluating every institution she'd ever trusted.

"They found something," she said. "Something worth stealing water over. And they figured out their communications weren't private. So they went quiet, packed their data away, and planned to walk out together and raise hell in person." She tapped the case. "And then they walked out of their boots and left their food hanging in the trees."

"Nobody walks out barefoot," I said. My voice sounded far away to me.

"Nobody walks out barefoot," she agreed.

The wind moved through the lodgepoles above the bench, a long sighing sound, and somewhere down the drainage the creek kept running—running at forty percent of what it should have been, carrying somebody's stolen water toward somebody's cattle, while three people's boots sat in a row pointing at a door none of them had used.

I thought about the contract folded in my jacket. About the inflated insurance valuation, the shell company, the client who'd paid a fortune to recover *equipment*—and whose crew chief had never once asked whether anyone had found the team. I'd filed that away as corporate callousness. Insurance math. People on paper.

Standing in Camp Four, with the journal warm in Dee's hands and the boots lined up like shoes at a door after a funeral, I felt the file in my head reshuffle itself with an almost audible click.

They hadn't sent us for tents and gauges.

They'd sent us because the tents and gauges were still here—and the people who'd left them hadn't reported to anyone, and hadn't surfaced anywhere, and someone with money needed to know what remained. Needed to know if the data survived. Needed to know what three barefoot ghosts might have hidden in a yellow case before they were subtracted from the mountain.

"Dee," I said. "We need to talk about who actually hired us."

She was already ahead of me. She always would be, from that day on. She held up the journal, and the late light caught the cover, and she said:

"First we read every page. Then we decide who we're afraid of."

Down in the drainage, faint but unmistakable, carried on the wind like a rumor: an engine.
