## 6. The Field Journal

The dead don't pack well. That was my first thought, standing in the middle of Camp Four with the morning sun coming through the tent nylon like a church window, turning everything inside a soft, forgiving gold. Sleeping bags rolled and strapped. Stove cleaned and nested in its pot. Boots paired by the door flap like a couple who'd been married forty years.

Nobody walks away from boots. Not in country like this. Not in October.

Gideon was outside doing their inventory thing, counting solar panels and survey tripods with the grim focus of someone reconciling a receipt against what they actually ordered. I'd seen that look before on rangers doing evidence walkthroughs after bad seasons. It's the look of a person building a case against hope.

I went for the tents. Not because I expected anything—habit, mostly. Twenty years of walking into campsites teaches you where people hide things from themselves: under sleeping pads, in boot liners, in the false bottom of a duffel where they keep the letters they're not ready to reread. People are predictable in their hiding places. It's one of the few comforting things about them.

The lead tent was Reyes's—I knew that now from the name tape stitched inside the vestibule, A. REYES, in thread gone gray from sun. Neat as a drill sergeant's footlocker. And at the bottom of her gear bag, wrapped in a spare base layer like it was embarrassed to be found, a hard-sided waterproof case the size of a thick paperback. Pelican-style, yellowed latches, a strip of surveyor's orange tape around it with something written in grease pencil:

**IF FOUND — NOT LOST — DO NOT TURN IN**

Well. That's not ominous at all, I thought. Thanks for the clarity, Ana.

I sat down cross-legged on her sleeping pad, which felt rude, and worked the latches, which fought me, because everything built to survive water is also built to survive being opened by hands that are shaking. Mine weren't shaking yet. But my hands have a sense of dramatic timing.

Inside: a field journal, Rite-in-the-Rain bound, swollen slightly from humidity but intact. A folded topographic map, soft as cloth from handling. Two data cards in labeled sleeves. And a letter, sealed, addressed to *Whoever is reading this because we couldn't.*

I didn't open the letter first. I know how that sounds, but there's an order to things. The journal tells you what happened. The letter tells you why it matters, and you want the facts loaded before somebody else's fear gets to do your thinking for you. That's a lesson I learned the hard way, in a hearing room in Boise, watching a man in a good suit explain that my incident report had been "recontextualized." Facts first. Then let the bastards spin.

The journal started standard. Date, station number, weather, crew initials. Flow measurements on the upper West Fork—discharge readings, gauge heights, sediment notes. Ana Reyes wrote like she talked, if the careful, clipped entries were anything to go by: no wasted words, numbers doing the heavy lifting. For the first week it was textbook hydrology. Pretty river, healthy flows, boring as oatmeal.

Then the numbers started lying.

Not obviously. That's the thing about data—it doesn't lie loud. It lies quiet, in trends, in the gap between what a watershed should be doing in mid-September and what the page says it's doing. Discharge dropping twelve percent over six days with no precipitation change, no temperature spike, nothing upstream on the gauges they could access. Then another eight percent. Reyes flagged it in the margin: *Where is the water going?*

She started hiking upstream on her own time. You can see it in the entries—the stations shift north, the timestamps get earlier (somebody getting up before dawn), the handwriting gets tighter, angrier. She found the first anomaly on September 19th. A side drainage that showed up on the USGS quad as seasonal, dry by August, carrying *substantial* flow in late September. She followed it up-canyon for four hours and then stopped writing measurements and started writing sentences.

*There is a diversion structure at the head of this draw. Concrete, engineered, recently maintained. Unpermitted—no signage, no filing stamps, no state or federal markings of any kind. It taps the West Fork above our survey reach and feeds a buried pipeline running northeast. Estimated capacity: enough to irrigate several thousand acres of land that officially has no water rights to speak of.*

*This is not a seep. This is not a spring development. This is theft, industrial scale, and someone spent real money making sure it would never appear on paper.*

I turned pages faster. The map came next, unfolded across my knees. Reyes had drawn it herself—the pipeline route in red grease pencil, snaking northeast along a contour line, cleverly, following drainages so it would never need an exposed crossing, terminating at a boxed area maybe nine miles out. I knew the box before I read the label. Anybody who'd worked this district knew it. Corliss Ranching Group. Forty thousand acres of alfalfa and cattle in a valley that the state had classified as marginal rangeland for a reason: there wasn't water. There hadn't been water since the thirties.

Except there was now. Green circles on satellite imagery that ranchers will tell you are "improved pasture management." Alfalfa standing fat and emerald in a valley that should've been sagebrush and apology. I'd flown over it in a patrol helo years ago and thought, huh, somebody's got a deal with God. Turns out the deal was with a backhoe and a concrete intake and a river that didn't know it was being robbed.

And here's the part that made me set the journal down on my knee for a second and just breathe: none of this required malice to discover. That's what got me. Reyes wasn't digging for scandal. She was doing her job. The river told on itself. All she did was listen.

The trouble with listening is that other people hear you doing it.

September 22nd: *Vehicle observed on closed Forest Road 417. Heavy, dark, two occupants. They asked what we were measuring and why we kept going upstream. Told them standard survey work. They did not ask again. They watched us for twenty minutes before leaving.*

September 24th: *Same vehicle, different plates. This time they photographed our camp. When I approached, the driver said, "You folks might want to wrap up early this year. Weather's coming." It was 74 degrees and cloudless.*

September 26th: *Called the district office to report harassment. Spoke to someone named Dale who said he'd "pass it along." Called the county sheriff's non-emergency line. Voicemail. Left message. No return call as of today.*

I knew Dale. Everybody who'd ever reported anything in that district knew Dale. Dale was the sound a complaint makes when it falls into a hole.

By September 28th the entries changed character entirely. Less science, more survival. *Moved the data off the primary laptop onto cards. Copies made. One set stays with me, one with Marcus, one cached separately.* And then, October 1st, the entry that rearranged the whole shape of the story Gideon and I had driven up here to tell ourselves:

*Decision made tonight. We are not leaving the way we planned. Hear me out, whoever finds this.*

*They came at dusk. Three men, no uniforms, no explanation, asking us to come down the mountain with them—"for safety" —while offering no reason any reasonable person would accept. We said we'd hike out on schedule. They said the schedule had changed. Marcus got between them and the instruments and one of them put a hand on a pistol grip, casual, like resting his arm. Like it was a fence post.*

*I understood then that we were never going to be allowed to file this report. The question was only whether we'd be attached to it when it surfaced.*

*So we became unattached. We broke camp in the dark—our real camp, not this one—and we left this one dressed to look abandoned-by-choice: bags packed, stove clean, everything squared away, because people who leave voluntarily tidy up, and we wanted anyone who came looking to think exactly that. We want them looking for five hikers headed downslope toward the trailhead, because that's where they'll watch. We are going the other way. Up. Into the high country nobody patrols in October.*

*If you're reading this, we're alive. Cold, probably hungry, definitely furious, but alive. The data is real. The diversion is real. Corliss Ranching Group is stealing a public river and someone in at least two agencies is helping them do it, and when three men with guns ask a survey crew to come down the mountain early, that is not a weather advisory.*

*Do not turn this in to the district office. Do not call the sheriff. Find someone outside the water. Find someone who can't be bought with grazing allotments and campaign donations and the promise of a quiet retirement.*

*And check the cache coordinates on the last page. If we didn't make it back for the copies, they're still there, and they're still true, and truth doesn't freeze.*

I sat there a long time with the journal open on my knees while the gold light moved across the tent floor and somewhere outside Gideon's voice drifted in, narrating inventory: "—two rock hammers, one appears ceremonial, one cooler containing condiments exclusively—"

They were alive. Five surveyors, missing three weeks, presumed dead by every agency that had bothered to presume anything, and they were alive, because Ana Reyes had looked at three men with guns and chosen the one direction everyone knew was suicide. Up. In October. With frostbite waiting at every elevation gain and winter coming down the passes like a debt collector.

It was either the bravest thing I'd ever read or the craziest, and having once done something in between those two things myself—something that cost me a badge and eleven years of identity—I recognized the handwriting on the wall of it. Sometimes the institution is the bear. Sometimes the only move is off the map.

I heard Gideon stop moving outside. The particular silence of a person whose brain just caught up with their eyes.

"Dee," they called, too casually. "Come look at this."

I zipped the journal into its case, tucked the case inside my jacket—sorry, Ana, custody transfer pending—and crawled out into the light.

Gideon was crouched at the edge of camp, by the trampled grass where the surveyors' real departure had been staged. They were holding a boot print casting frame, empty, and pointing at the ground beyond it, where a line of prints led away from camp toward the treeline. Small party. Light loads. Moving upslope.

And crossing those prints, fresher, deeper, tread pattern like a construction boot with attitude: at least two sets of tracks, same direction. Following.

"They didn't vanish," Gideon said quietly. "Somebody chased them out of their own camp."

"Somebody's been chasing them for three weeks," I said, and handed over the journal. "Read the last page first. Trust me."

Gideon read. I watched their face do what mine had done—the flicker at the diversion, the stillness at the guns, and then something harder settling in behind the eyes when they hit the part about agencies that couldn't be trusted. Because Gideon had signed a contract ten days ago with a client that was a shell company and an insurance valuation that made no sense, and I could see the arithmetic landing behind their face like weather.

"The salvage job," Gideon said slowly. "Recover the equipment. Gear intact, no bodies." A short laugh with nothing funny in it. "We weren't hired to recover equipment. We were hired to find out if anybody else had found *this*."

"Or to find the journal for them," I said. "One or the other. Maybe both."

The wind came down off the ridge and moved through the camp, rattling the dead tents, and for a moment the whole place sounded like breathing. Somewhere north and east, nine miles of stolen river away, green circles of alfalfa glittered in a valley that should have been brown, and I thought about Ana Reyes up in the high country right now, if she was still alive, if frost hadn't already collected on the interest of her courage, holding pages that proved the whole rotten arrangement.

"Okay," Gideon said, closing the journal with a care that surprised me. They looked up at the ridgeline, then at me, and the storm-chaser look was on them—that flat, bright focus I'd seen once before, in a diner, when Marisol Vane offered them a way out. Except this time it was pointed somewhere better. "The diversion's upstream. If we're going to understand what we're carrying, we go see it."

"Gideon—"

"They drew us a map, Dee."

"Yeah," I said, pulling my jacket tighter as the wind sharpened, coming colder now, smelling faintly of snow from a front that hadn't shown itself yet. "That's what worries me. Everything about this job came with a map."

* * *

We climbed for two hours through lodgepole and then scree, following Reyes's red line in reverse, and the whole way I kept cataloguing wrongnesses the way I used to on patrol. A game I hated playing. Fresh cut stumps where someone had cleared sight lines. A plastic flagging ribbon, new, tied at eye height—not surveyor's pink but hunter's orange, the color of people who want to find things again. And once, faint on the wind, carried up from the drainage below: the sound of an engine working steadily, patiently, like a pump.

Like a pump maintaining something.

We reached the saddle above the head of the draw at midafternoon and Gideon dropped flat behind a granite rib and pulled me down beside them, and I saw why.

Below us, wedged into the throat of the canyon, sat the intake. Concrete, low-slung, deliberately ugly, built to be mistaken from the air for a natural rock bar. Water slid into it smooth and silent, vanishing under the earth without a ripple worth noticing. Beside it, a small maintenance shed, metal roof, and parked outside the shed—

A truck. Dark, heavy, new tires. And a man in a canvas jacket walking the channel with a clipboard, unhurried, checking his river the way a farmer checks a field he planted.

"There it is," Gideon breathed. "The whole lie. Right there."

And that's when we both heard it at the same time—a second engine, higher, thinner, coming from the north. Not a pump. Not a truck.

Rotors.

Gideon grabbed my sleeve. "Ridge. Now. Move."
