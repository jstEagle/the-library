## 7. What the River Owes

The journal had a smell. I don't mean the paper-and-plastic smell of a thing that's been sealed in a waterproof case for weeks—I mean it had a *smell*, a presence, like a lit match carried into a room full of people pretending not to notice. Dee felt it too. She'd gone quiet after reading the last entry, which for Dee Okafor is roughly equivalent to a normal person screaming into a pillow.

We ate breakfast at Camp Four without tasting any of it. Freeze-dried eggs, coffee boiled over a whisper of flame because smoke travels in this country like gossip in a small town. The tents stood around us doing their patient dead-man impression. Somewhere above us, water was moving where water shouldn't be.

"Say it out loud," Dee said finally. "I want to hear how it sounds."

I turned to a fresh page of my own notebook—the cheap spiral one I use for chase logs—and wrote what we'd already mapped in our heads: flow readings from six stations along the upper Sable Fork, all trending down over eleven days while snowmelt should have been pushing them up. Then the surveyors' note, dated three days before they stopped writing: *"Diversion confirmed. Unlicensed intake approx. 4 km upstream of Camp 4. Corliss equipment. Photographed. Reporting to district office Monday."*

There was no Monday.

"It sounds like four people found out who owns the river," I said, "and then the river kept them."

Dee looked up at the ridgeline to the north, where the channel would be if the channel existed. "It exists," she said. "I scouted this drainage two seasons ago for the Forest Service. There was nothing up there but talus and goat sign." She stood, brushing frost off her knees with the brisk violence of someone who needed to hit something and had settled on her own pants. "So either the goats got organized, or somebody built a dam in the last eighteen months."

We broke camp fast. Not panicked-fast—Dee would have lectured me about panic being a luxury item—but with the particular efficiency of two people who have both, at different points in their lives, packed a vehicle while someone they didn't trust was driving toward them. We cached what we didn't need under a cairn Dee built with an engineer's spite, took the journal, took cameras, took the satellite phone even though it was deader than the camp's fire ring, and started up.

* * *

The diversion channel was exactly as invisible from below as whoever built it wanted it to be. From the valley floor you'd never see it—a cut maybe two meters wide running along the contour of the slope, lined with shot rock so it read as natural talus chute unless you were standing in it. Which, by mid-morning, we were.

Walking inside someone's secret does something to your spine. It's not fear exactly. It's the feeling of reading another person's mail, except the mail is a river and the envelope is a mountain and the person is a ranching consortium with its own security force.

"They cut this with an excavator," Dee said, crouching to touch the channel floor. Dry now—whatever water it carried was being taken higher up—but the bed was graded smooth, the boulders placed rather than dumped. "That's not a weekend project. That's permits, or the absence of them, plus money, plus somebody high up deciding the absence of permits was fine."

"How much water are we talking?"

She paced off the width, eyeballed the grade, did ranger math. "At full diversion? Enough to green up ten thousand acres of pasture that the water rights say should be sagebrush. Enough to make hay in a drought year when your neighbors are selling cattle." She straightened. "Enough to be worth killing for, if you're the kind of person who thinks in those units."

"I'm trying very hard not to think in those units."

"Good luck. I've been thinking in them since yesterday."

We climbed. The channel climbed with us, contouring along the slope in long patient switchbacks, and every hundred meters I kept expecting it to end in some half-finished embarrassment, some abandoned pilot project. It didn't. It was finished. That was the part that sat wrong in my stomach—not the crime but the *craftsmanship* of it. Whoever built this expected it to last decades. They'd built it like a cathedral.

Near noon the channel bent around a shoulder of granite and we heard it before we saw it: water. A lot of water, moving with the flat roar of something confined and resentful.

We came around the shoulder low, bellies to rock, and there it was.

The intake.

It sat in a narrow gorge where the Sable Fork dropped through a slot maybe eight meters across. Someone had poured a concrete weir across the slot—low, unobtrusive, camouflaged with rock dust so from any distance it read as natural bedrock. Behind the weir the river pooled, quiet and deep, and out of that pool a headgate fed a steel pipe the size of a highway culvert, angling down into the hillside toward the channel we'd followed. There was a trash rack to keep the logs out. There was a small control shed bolted to the rock above the gate, solar panel on the roof, antenna on a mast. There was even, I swear on my mother's anemometer, a little gravel parking pad.

Somebody commuted here.

"Corliss?" I asked.

Dee had binoculars up. "No branding. But that pipe spec, that gate actuator—" she lowered the glasses, and her jaw did the thing it does when she's swallowing words she'll regret later, "—that's state-of-the-art. This isn't a rancher with a backhoe and a prayer. This is engineered infrastructure."

"Built illegally."

"Built *quietly*. Different skill set entirely." She passed me the binoculars. "Look at the shed door."

I looked. The door was new paint, weather-gray, and hinged to swing inward against the rock face so it didn't flash bright when opened. And below it, printed small and professional on a laminated card:

**CORLISS RANCHING GROUP — FACILITY 3 — AUTHORIZED ACCESS ONLY**

Facility 3. Plural. As in, there were at least two others.

"Well," I said. "That's a whole sentence."

I got the camera out and started shooting. Wide shots of the weir in its gorge, tight shots of the placard, the headgate, the serial plate on the actuator motor—which, because the universe occasionally rewards the diligent, was legible: a manufacture date fourteen months ago. Dee photographed the pool, the pipe, the channel alignment, everything, working with the methodical calm of a woman building a case file in her head, and I understood suddenly that this wasn't her first time documenting something that powerful people didn't want documented. Her hands didn't shake. Mine were steady too, but only because I was treating them like someone else's hands, borrowed for the job.

Then Dee said, quietly, "Gideon."

Not the tone you want.

"Boot prints," she said. "On the pad."

I came around the granite shoulder and looked down at the gravel lot, and there they were: boot prints, crisp-edged, hours old at most—the morning melt hadn't yet softened their rims. Two sets, one large one smaller, coming up from a trail on the downstream side, going to the shed, going in, coming out. One set walked the length of the weir and back, inspection-style. And near the edge of the pad, a cigarette butt, still holding its ash, tucked neatly under a stone against the wind.

"Somebody maintains it regularly," Dee said. "Weekly, maybe. Look at the trail wear." She pointed downstream, where a faint path switchbacked toward the valley—a path worn into the scree by many boots over many months, discreet, never crossing open ground. "This isn't a construction site anymore. It's a facility. Staffed."

I did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding since the diner. If Facility 3 had staff, Facilities 1 and 2 probably did too. If Corliss maintained illegal infrastructure across federal land, they maintained silence about it the same way—routinely, professionally, with schedules. And somewhere in that schedule, fourteen months ago, four government surveyors had taken photographs on a Monday morning and stopped existing.

"We should go," I said. "Now. While the maintenance crew's having lunch wherever they have lunch."

"One more frame of the—"

The engine was distant. That's the only way I can describe it honestly: distant, and getting less so. A diesel, heavy, down-valley, the kind of sound that carries through granite canyons the way church bells carry through a town. Dee's head snapped toward it with the reflexes of someone who used to listen for engines for a living, and mine snapped toward hers, and for one full second neither of us moved, both of us listening the way you listen when the sound might be weather or might be a decision.

"Truck," Dee breathed. "Big one. Coming up the maintenance trail."

"The one that doesn't exist on any map."

"That one."

We went. Not down—we'd come up the exposed southern flank and going back down meant crossing two hundred meters of open slope in full view of anyone with eyes and a grudge. Instead we went up and east, onto the ridgeline, into the broken granite, moving the way Dee moved: fast, low, deliberate, each step chosen. I'm not built for stealth. I'm built for sitting in a truck watching radar loops and drinking gas-station coffee. But terror is an excellent personal trainer, and I discovered reserves of quiet-footedness I did not know I possessed.

Behind us and below, the engine climbed. Stopped. Doors—two, maybe three, the heavy chunk of vehicle doors on a machine built for work. Voices, too far to parse, close enough to count. Then the specific metallic complaint of the shed door swinging inward on its hinges.

They were checking on their river. Right on schedule. And we had been standing in their schedule's blind spot for the better part of two hours, touching things, photographing things, breathing their air.

We gained the ridgeline and dropped behind it, and Dee pulled us into a crease between two slabs of granite where the wind covered whatever noise we made and the sky above us was a strip of hard blue. Below, muffled by rock, the voices continued. A radio crackled—someone reporting in, routine as a milk run.

I lay on cold stone with my heart going like a hailstorm on a tin roof and thought about the word *fugitive*. An hour ago I'd been an investigator. A documentarian. A guy with a camera and a righteous little glow in his chest. Now I was lying flat on a mountain hiding from a maintenance crew, and the difference between those two men was about forty minutes and one laminated sign.

"They'll sweep the perimeter," Dee whispered. "Standard. Check the trail, check the sightlines. We've got maybe twenty minutes before they look uphill."

"Can they see the camp from here?"

"No. But they'll see our tracks eventually. Melt's soft on the south faces." She was already moving, worming along the crease toward the eastern descent. "We go down the north side. Burn area from the '19 fire—deadfall, no trails, miserable. Nobody patrols miserable."

"Miserable it is."

We moved. Down through the granite, across a snowfield where Dee taught me, in hand signals, how to walk on the shaded side of the slope where our prints would refreeze behind us, down into timber, down until the intake and its tidy gravel pad were a memory and a folder of photographs. My legs shook. Not from the climbing—from the specific exhaustion of sustained fear, which burns fuel like a storm cell burns heat.

By late afternoon we were deep in the burn scar, among silver snags and knee-high regrowth, and Dee finally let us stop in a hollow where a fallen trunk made a wall against the wind. I got the camera out and reviewed the shots. All clean. Serial numbers legible. The placard sharp enough to read from orbit.

Somewhere in that folder was the end of Marisol Vane's cheerful arithmetic. Also possibly the end of us, but I was choosing, for the moment, to be an optimist. It's a family curse.

"Gideon." Dee was looking back the way we'd come, up at the ridgeline we'd abandoned. "Look."

Above the trees, on the skyline we'd left, a figure stood silhouetted against the afternoon sun. Just one. Standing still. Not searching—*looking*, the way you look when you already know where to look. Even at that distance I could read the posture: hands on hips, weight settled, unhurried. A man admiring his view.

Or counting us.

Then he raised something to his face—binoculars or a radio, impossible to tell—and the light shifted, and he was gone from the skyline like he'd never stood there at all.

Dee didn't say anything. Neither did I. We just picked up our packs and went deeper into the burn scar, faster now, while behind us and above us the mountain held its new tenants, and the river ran on below, quietly, obediently, paying out to men who had never asked it politely.
