## 8. Debt Collectors Wear Boots Too

The thing nobody tells you about being hunted is how quiet it starts.

I want to be clear: at the time, I didn't know I was being hunted. I thought I was having a bad afternoon on a ridgeline above a river that was missing most of its water. There's a difference, and the difference is mostly hindsight.

* * *

Halloway had learned to read reports the way other men read weather—looking past what was written for what was being hidden.

He sat in the Corliss Ranching Group's field office outside Stanley, a building that had been a ranger station before it had been anything else, and listened to Trent Mabry describe two people on foot moving north along the diversion channel with the confidence of men who belonged there and the bad luck of men who didn't.

"Two," Halloway said.

"Two. One built like a runner, one carrying survey gear. They went up the intake cut around fourteen hundred, spent maybe twenty minutes inside the structure, then bugged out when our patrol unit came up the maintenance road." Mabry shifted his weight. He was thirty-one and built like a refrigerator someone had left out in the rain, and he reported everything with the same flat competence he'd use to report a fence line down. "They took the ridge west. We lost them in the timber."

"You didn't follow."

"You said don't spook them unless they touch the intake again."

"They touched the intake, Trent."

"They touched it before we got eyes on them." Mabry paused, and Halloway watched him decide whether to say the next part. He said it. "There's something else. The structure's been photographed recently. Fresh camera flash residue on the housing bolts, and somebody handled the flow gauge—oils from bare hands on the dial glass. Whoever they are, they weren't sightseeing. They were documenting."

Halloway turned his coffee mug a quarter turn on the desk. It was a habit, not a ritual. He told himself it was a habit.

The office still had the old ranger station's bulletin board, and Corliss's people had never taken down the fire danger sign, so every day Halloway walked past a green placard reading LOW that hadn't been accurate since the consortium started running water uphill, figuratively speaking, three years ago. He'd signed on eighteen months ago. Guarding property, the ad had said. Range security, watershed infrastructure, competitive salary. At forty-four, after twenty years with the Forest Service ending the way his had ended, *competitive salary* had sounded like a moral position.

"Description," he said.

"Mixed pair. The big one's Black, female, short hair, moved like she knew the country. The other one—" Mabry consulted his notes, though Halloway suspected he'd memorized them already, "—smaller, tan jacket, moved like an athlete who hates hiking. Our spotter caught a vehicle two days back at the Copper Basin trailhead. Older pickup, storm-chasing antenna array on the bed, Idaho plates registered to a Gideon Quintana of Boise."

"A storm chaser."

"That's what the registration says."

Halloway looked at the fire danger sign. LOW. He thought about the salvage contract—the one Corliss's lawyers had dressed up through a shell company called Meridian Asset Recovery, the one with the insurance valuation so inflated it read like a ransom note written by an optimist. He'd seen the paperwork cross his desk in the spring and asked, once, why a shell company would pay triple market rate for the recovery of some government radios and a couple of GPS units. He'd been told it wasn't his lane.

It had not, at the time, felt like a lie. It had felt like rich people being rich people. There was a difference, and the difference is mostly hindsight too.

"Quintana," Halloway said. "Run him."

"Already did. Debt. A lot of it. Chasing storms doesn't pay like you'd think." Mabry almost smiled. "His lender is Vane Capital Partners."

Something cold walked up Halloway's spine and stood there.

Vane. Marisol Vane, who sent Christmas cards to half the valley with handwritten notes in looping cheerful script, who sponsored the 4-H livestock auction, whose money was woven through the eastern counties so thoroughly that pulling one thread would unravel the quilt. Corliss's biggest lender. Corliss's biggest *friend*.

"And Vane Capital," Halloway said carefully, "is the one holding Quintana's paper."

"Yes sir."

"So we have a debtor, sent into the mountains by his creditor, under a recovery contract funded by a company that doesn't exist, to retrieve equipment that isn't worth a tenth of its insured value, from a camp where four federal employees vanished without leaving tracks." Halloway set the mug down and stopped turning it. "Trent, does any part of that sentence sound like salvage to you?"

Mabry was quiet for a moment. "Sounds like fishing," he said. "Chummed water."

"Yeah." Halloway stood. "Get me the line to Mr. Corliss. And Trent—nobody follows those two tonight. Nobody gets clever. If they're carrying what I think they're carrying, I want to know exactly where they sleep before anybody breathes on them."

"You think they found the journal."

Halloway looked at him. "I think four surveyors didn't walk off a mountain naked of their gear because they got bored. And I think somebody paid good money to make sure that if anyone ever came looking for that journal, it would be someone findable." He pulled his coat off the hook. "That's not salvage, son. That's a lure. And we're standing on the hook too, we just haven't looked down yet."

* * *

Here's what I knew at sundown, sitting in the black skeleton of a burn scar with Dee Okafor while the temperature fell like it had somewhere better to be:

I knew the intake structure was man-made, maintained, and illegal. I knew boot prints on its catwalk meant regular visits. I knew a distant engine had come up a road that shouldn't exist, and that Dee and I had gone from investigators to meat in the space of about ninety seconds.

And I knew—I had known since the diner, if I'm honest, known the way you know a storm cell has rotation even before the hook echo shows—that Marisol Vane had smiled at me across a table laminated with decades of coffee rings and offered me exactly one clean way out.

Let me tell you about that smile. Marisol Vane smiles like weather. Not like sunshine—like a front. You watch it come in over the horizon and you think, *that's beautiful,* and you keep watching, and you don't move, and by the time you understand what you're looking at you're already standing in it.

"She flagged it," Dee said.

She sat against a dead lodgepole, knees up, working a hand warmer between her palms like she was trying to get a confession out of it. In the last light her face was all angles and judgment.

"The contract," she said. "Think it through, Quintana. Meridian Asset Recovery. Who hires a shell company to recover *government* equipment? The government recovers its own equipment—it's got people for that, it's got me, formerly. And the valuation. You told me yourself the insurance numbers were insane. Triple replacement cost for radios and a Garmin. Nobody insures junk at ransom prices unless the junk isn't the point."

"The point being?"

"The point being whatever else was at that camp." She nodded toward my pack, where the waterproof case rode against the frame, heavy as a verdict. "The journal. Somebody wanted it found, and they wanted it found by somebody desperate enough to go get it and predictable enough to bring it back down a specific set of trails. You're not a salvager, Gideon. You're a retriever. You're a bird dog."

"I don't fetch," I said, mostly to hear something that wasn't the truth.

"No," Dee agreed. "But you were bred for it, and the person holding your leash knows it."

I did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding since the ridgeline. Marisol recalculating my debt with that pen she clicked when she was pleased, click-click-click, like a geiger counter near something radioactive. The failed season. The number she'd named—forty-one thousand, give or take, "give or take" being the operative cruelty—and then the contract sliding across the laminate like a gift. *One clean way out, Gideon. Gear retrieval, high country, six weeks' work, debt cleared plus a bonus.* She'd even wished me luck. She'd said, and I quote it now in my head with new acoustics, *"You've got such good instincts. That's why I thought of you."*

Good instincts. Findable instincts. Desperate instincts.

"She didn't send me to recover equipment," I said. "She sent me to locate a document. The equipment was the receipt. Proof I went where she needed me to go. And then I walk back into her office with the journal in my pack, and she clears my debt, and Corliss's problem walks out the door in her handbag, and I never even know what I carried."

Dee was quiet a moment. Out past the burn scar edge, the living timber made a sound like the mountain breathing through its teeth.

"There's a version of this where you just do it," she said. Not accusing. Testing. "Hand it over. Debt's gone, winter's paid, you live. It's paper. It's measurements. It's not your river."

"It's Reyes's river," I said. "Whoever Reyes is. Four people wrote that journal and then stopped existing, and the last entry says *if we don't come down, it's because coming down isn't safe*, and somebody made sure it wasn't." I looked at my hands. Storm chaser's hands, currently useless, currently cold. "Marisol bought my debt the way you buy a dog. And the contract wasn't a job, it was a collar and a direction to point me."

"So what's the play?"

I should have said *we turn it over anyway*. I should have said *we go down the east side, find a reporter, eat the loss, start over*. What I actually said was smaller and more honest:

"We don't go back the way we came. Every route she'd predict, we don't take. If she wants this journal, she's priced in my desperation, which means she's priced in the low passes, the trailheads, the roads. So we stop being predictable." I looked at Dee. "How bad is the high route?"

Dee smiled, and it was not Marisol's smile. It was the other kind—the kind that means the next words will cost you.

"Worse than you're imagining," she said. "And you imagine professionally."

* * *

Halloway drove the maintenance road alone that night, headlights off, moon doing the work, because he wanted to see the intake the way the intruders had seen it.

It looked worse in the dark. Concrete and steel where the river used to run free, a wound in the canyon with a pipe feeding eight miles of private ditch. He'd inspected it a dozen times as a matter of routine and never once let himself call it what it was. Theft. Grand, patient, well-armed theft, wearing a watershed improvement district like a coat.

His radio crackled. Mabry. "Corliss called back. He wants the pair located by tomorrow night. His words were 'before they become a story.'"

"Before they become a story," Halloway repeated.

"He also said—if they have the journal—we recover it. His word was 'recover.'"

"Was it."

A pause. "No sir. His word was 'make sure it doesn't reach anyone.' He didn't specify method. He never specifies method. That's the arrangement."

Halloway looked up the canyon, toward the high country, where two strangers were presumably deciding right now whether to be birds or bait. Twenty years ago he'd have been on their side of the ridge. Twenty years ago he *had* been—he'd filed a report once, about grazing allotments and a dead creek, and watched it die in a drawer, and watched his career die shortly after, and learned the lesson the range teaches everyone eventually: the land keeps records, but institutions shred them.

Four surveyors had learned the same lesson harder.

He keyed the radio. "Copy. Locate, don't engage. I'll take the morning sweep myself."

"Roger. Halloway—you okay?"

He thought about the fire danger sign. LOW. He thought about a woman named Dee Okafor, whose name he recognized from an old internal memo, a ranger who'd reported Corliss once before and been buried for it, and who was now up there in the dark doing for free what he got paid to prevent.

"Trent," he said, "do you know what we call it, in the Service, when you find evidence of a crime and your supervisor tells you to lose the paperwork?"

"Sir?"

"We don't call it anything. That's the whole trick." He shut the radio off before Mabry could answer, and sat in the dark with the stolen river running quiet below him, and understood that whatever he decided by morning, he had already decided, and the deciding was just the long painful process of catching up.

Up on the ridge, a light blinked twice and went out. Two people, moving north, away from every road.

Halloway started the truck and, for the first time in eighteen months, pointed it the wrong way.
