## 9. Radio Silence

The satellite messenger died at 11:47 in the morning, and it did not die politely.

I'd been checking it every twenty minutes since Camp Four, the way you check a wound you're pretending isn't infected. Press the button, watch the little screen blink its little green blink, watch the little satellite icon hunt for a signal it never finds. Gideon had named the thing "Percy," which told me everything I needed to know about a person who names their equipment. Percy had worked fine in the truck. Percy had worked fine through the lower canyons. Percy had stopped working somewhere between the survey camp and the diversion channel, and he was not coming back.

"Jammed," I said.

Gideon looked up from the topo map they'd spread across a granite slab, one corner pinned under a water bottle, another under their boot. "You can jam a satellite messenger?"

"You can jam anything if you've got the budget." I turned Percy over in my hands like that would help, like the answer was printed on his underside. "It's not cheap and it's not subtle—you need a vehicle-mounted rig or a fixed site—but it blanks out a whole band of sky. Everything uplink goes into the noise floor."

"So whoever's running it—"

"Doesn't want anyone talking to anyone." I tucked Percy back into my chest pocket, where I could feel him sitting there like a stone. "Which means they know someone's in these mountains who might want to talk to someone."

Gideon was quiet for a moment. They have this way of going still when the math turns ugly—I'd seen it twice already on this trip, once at the camp and once at the intake structure with all those fresh boot prints pressed neat as signatures into the mud. Storm chasers, I was learning, are people who spend their lives reading systems for the exact moment a system stops being survivable. You could watch them do it. The eyes go flat, the jaw sets, and somewhere behind the face a clock starts ticking.

"Okay," Gideon said finally. "So we can't call out. Can we be tracked?"

"Not by satellite. Radio direction-finding, maybe, if we transmit—and we won't. Visual, definitely." I nodded east, toward the ridgeline we'd ducked below when the engine sound came over the saddle yesterday. "They know roughly where we are. They don't know exactly. That gap is our whole world right now."

"That's a small world."

"It's the only one on offer."

* * *

We moved north along the drainage through the late morning, keeping to timber where we could, moving fast in the open stretches because standing still is how you get found. Gideon carried the journal now, in a dry bag against their spine, and I noticed they'd started sleeping with it too, curled around it like a body pillow, which was either touching or pathological and probably both.

I heard the helicopter before Gideon did. That's not a flex; it's just what happens when you spend eleven years listening for engines in places where an engine means trouble. It was a faint chop-chop-chop riding down the wind from the southwest, still miles out but closing, and the wind was doing what wind does in these canyons, which is lie about direction.

"Gid." No more than that. Didn't need more.

We were in open lodgepole on a bench above the creek—bad ground, no cover taller than knee-high, no time to reach the trees to the north before the bird was on us. I scanned the slope and saw it: the burn scar. Two drainages over, a black tongue of dead snags and blowdown climbing the south face, the legacy of some lightning strike three summers back. Burn scars are ugly and they're dangerous—widow-makers overhead, ankle-breakers underfoot—but they do one thing beautifully. Nothing looks natural in a burn scar. Every shadow is ambiguous. Every snag could be a person or a post. From the air, at speed, it's visual static.

"There," I said. "Burn scar. Go."

We went. I'll be honest about what that descent was, because I believe in honesty the way other people believe in lottery tickets: it was not dignified. We dropped off the bench through waist-high manzanita that grabbed at our packs and opened a seam on my forearm, slid down a scree chute on our backsides, splashed across the creek without breaking stride because stopping meant thinking and thinking meant slowing. The chop of the rotor grew teeth. Then it grew a shape—a dark speck sliding along the ridgeline we'd just left, patient as a hawk.

Then we were in the burn, and the world turned to charcoal and broken light.

* * *

A burn scar has a smell. People expect ash, and there's ash, but what stays with you is the sweetness underneath—rotting snags, sap bleeding from scorched bark, the mushroomy breath of ground that's starting to come back. Three summers of recovery and the fireweed was already up in patches, purple flags planted in the black, and the downed logs wore coats of moss like the forest trying to apologize for the mess.

We moved through it bent double, snag to snag, and the helicopter circled twice—long, slow orbits out west, hanging over the ridge we'd abandoned—and then, mercifully, swung away south and faded into engine-noise memory.

Gideon straightened up beside a fallen lodgepole thick as a silo. "Did it see us?"

"No."

"How do you know?"

"Because we're not being shot at, chased, or arrested." I leaned against the log and let my legs remember they were mine. "Also because those birds fly a grid when they've got a fix. That was a sweep. They're looking for movement, not people."

Gideon slid down until they were sitting, back against the snag, and laughed—one short, airless syllable with no humor in it anywhere. "Yesterday morning I was a salvage contractor. Today I'm hiding in a forest fire's grave from a helicopter."

"Career arc."

"What's yours?" And then, before I could dodge: "Dee. You knew the Sawtooth district like the back of your hand. You knew the roads, the drainages, the burn history. You read that graded road yesterday like you'd personally watched them pave it. A ranger who knows a district that well doesn't end up driving storm chasers around for gas money unless something happened."

I should have lied. I'm good at lying; I had years of practice telling the public cheerful, hollow versions of the truth, which is most of what public-facing land agency work is. But we were crouched in a burn scar with a jammed radio and a stolen journal and a helicopter making sweeps over our heads, and there's a kind of company that makes lying feel like a waste of good fear.

"I reported Corliss," I said.

Gideon didn't move. "The ranching group? Before any of this?"

"Eight years ago. I was a range tech then, fresh badge, still thought the arrow on my uniform pointed at something true." I picked up a chunk of charcoal and turned it in my fingers. It crumbled like old bread. "There's a tributary up in the north fork country—Willow Creek, if you care—that runs through a mix of Forest Service allotment and Corliss private lease. I was doing riparian surveys and I kept getting readings that didn't close. Flow gauges downstream reading lower than upstream, no accounting for it. Same trick your dead—or missing—surveyors documented, just smaller scale, eight years earlier. Somebody was pulling water out of Willow Creek and nobody had filed a damn thing."

"And you filed something."

"I wrote it up. Full report. Photos, measurements, the works. Sent it up the chain." I smiled, and I felt my mouth do it without any input from me, the way it does. "You know what happened next? Nothing happened next. My supervisor thanked me for my diligence. Six weeks later I got reassigned to the far side of the district, away from Willow Creek, to work on—get this—recreation signage. Then my report came back to me redlined. Not rejected. Redlined. Every measurement flagged as 'methodologically inconsistent.' My gauges, which I'd calibrated myself, suddenly couldn't count. Then a performance review noted I had 'difficulty accepting established findings.' Then a complaint appeared in my file—an anonymous complaint—from a 'member of the public' alleging I'd been harassing permit holders. Corliss holds permits in that district. Lots of them."

Gideon said, softly, "They buried you."

"They buried me so deep I needed a headstone. And here's the part I didn't understand until later, the part that cost me the badge: I fought it. I appealed. I went over my supervisor's head, which in a bureaucracy is less like going over someone's head and more like putting your own neck on the block and handing everyone the axe. There was a hearing. There were lawyers whose salaries I recognized, indirectly, because their firm also did work for agricultural interests across three counties. In the end I was written up for insubordination, my report was formally destroyed per records policy—which means it was shredded, which means every number I ever collected on Willow Creek exists nowhere except in my head—and eighteen months after I filed the cleanest hydrology data anyone in that district had produced in a decade, I was cleaning out a locker."

The fireweed moved a little in the wind. Somewhere upslope a snag settled with a crack like a distant door.

"I used to think it was corruption," I said. "Big bad corporation buys big bad officials. Simple story. But it wasn't simple. It was worse than simple. It was *ordinary*. My supervisor wasn't evil—he coached Little League, I checked, I was so angry I actually checked—he was just a man who'd learned that the institution feeds the people who don't make waves, and Corliss made sure the institution stayed fed. Grants, partnerships, the grazing advisory board, money for the new district office. Nobody had to break a rule. The rules bent themselves. All anybody had to do was look slightly away, forever, and the machine ran smooth." I tossed the charcoal down. "I lost my badge for looking."

Gideon was quiet long enough that I thought they weren't going to say anything. When they spoke, their voice had a different grain to it. Lower. Like they were reading from something.

"When I signed the salvage contract," they said, "the client was a shell. Some LLC registered in Delaware with a name that sounded like a dental practice. I noticed. I noticed the insurance valuation was insane—forty percent over replacement cost on gear that was mostly depreciated anyway. I noticed, and I signed anyway, because Marisol Vane was holding a number over my head that had grown teeth over the winter, and the contract paid enough to make the number go away." They rubbed their face with both hands. "I told myself the shell was just tax cowardice. Rich people do that. I told myself the valuation was sloppy paperwork. I told myself a lot of things in a very organized way, and then I drove into these mountains to fetch equipment for somebody who apparently doesn't want equipment. They want a journal. They wanted us to find it, carry it out, and hand it over at the exchange point so nothing ever traces back to them."

"The debt was the hook."

"The debt was the hook. Vane didn't lend me money. Vane bought me." Gideon laughed again, that same airless syllable. "And the worst part—the genuinely humiliating part—is that I'd have done it. If we hadn't found the camp, hadn't read the journal, hadn't stood at that intake and seen the boot prints. I'd have walked the journal out, handed it to whatever smiling person met me at the trailhead, cashed the check, and driven home feeling *clever*. Three people vanished in these mountains and my plan was to feel clever about it."

"Three people who are probably alive," I said.

Gideon looked at me.

"Think about it," I said. "No bodies. No tracks leaving. Gear intact, food cached, sat phone dead but unbroken—dead battery, Gid, not smashed. People who get murdered don't power down their phones first. Those surveyors went dark on purpose. They hid. Which means they're either dead somewhere we haven't looked, or they're alive somewhere they chose, and I've spent enough nights alone in this range to bet on the second one."

"Where?"

I looked north, up the drainage, toward the high country where the map went pale and the contour lines crowded together like a nervous crowd. "Old mines. Fire lookouts. There's a decommissioned cabin up past the cirque that hasn't been on an official map since the nineties. If I were three scared scientists who trusted no agency because every agency I've ever known bends itself smooth for men like Corliss—" I tapped my chest pocket, where Percy sat dead and silent, "—I'd be up there. Cold, hungry, and free."

Gideon stared at me. Behind their eyes I could see the clock ticking faster, the math reorganizing itself, the plan tearing down and rebuilding in real time. Find the surveyors. Get them out. Get the journal out—not to a shell company, not to Marisol Vane's smiling arithmetic, but to daylight.

"That's five people," Gideon said. "Five people, one injured team minimum, high route, winter coming, helicopters overhead, and a security outfit that already thinks we're theirs."

"Yeah."

"We'd need weather cover. A front. Something big enough to blind the air support for days."

I almost smiled. "You're a storm chaser. Aren't you supposed to be hoping for exactly that?"

Gideon looked up at the sky—really looked, the way they do, reading it like a page—and I watched the expression cross their face that I'd learn to know very well in the days after. Not hope. Not fear. Recognition. Somewhere over the western ranges, a few thousand feet up, the clouds had gone from white to a hard, bruised silver, and they were stacking.

"It's coming," Gideon said quietly. "Two days. Maybe less."

* * *

We climbed out of the burn scar as the light went long and coppery, heading north up the drainage toward the cirque and the cabin that wasn't on any map, two fugitives and a dead man named Percy in my pocket. Behind us the burn scar held our tracks in its black soil like a confession, and ahead of us the mountain stood up into the weather with its collar turned up, waiting to see what we were made of.

I'd spent eight years believing the institution would eventually catch up to the truth, that the truth was patient, that systems self-corrected. Standing there in the last of the light with the wind shifting around into the northwest and the first real cold of the season on my neck, I finally understood that the truth isn't patient at all. The truth is just heavy. Somebody has to pick it up and carry it, and the carrying is the whole job.

Behind us, far to the south, I heard the helicopter again—two beats of it, then gone, like something clearing its throat.

We walked faster.
