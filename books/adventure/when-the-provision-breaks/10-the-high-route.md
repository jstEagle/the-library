## 10. The High Route

Dee Okafor crouched at the edge of the tree line like a woman reading a letter she didn't like, and when she finally spoke, she said the thing I'd been dreading since dawn.

"We go over the top."

I looked where she was looking. Where I'd been trying not to look all morning. The Sawtooth crest ran north from us in a sawblade line of granite and hanging snowfields, white where it caught the sun and blue-black where it didn't, and above it all sat a sky that had been quietly organizing itself into something with opinions.

"That's not a route," I said. "That's a dare."

"It's a route." She tapped the map spread across her knee, though we both knew the map was a formality. Dee carried the Sawtooth in her head the way other people carry grudges—detailed, permanent, and consulted more often than was strictly healthy. "Low passes are watched. Halloway's people have been sitting on Redfish Saddle since yesterday—I counted three trucks and a warming fire. They're not hunting us anymore, Gideon. They're herding us. Every trail that goes down and out is a funnel."

"So we don't take trails."

"So we stop taking trails." She rolled the map and smacked it against my chest, which is Dee's version of emphasis. "There's an old survey line across the crest—the one the Forest Service cut in '78 and then abandoned because it nearly killed everyone who used it. It tops out around ten-four, crosses two cols, drops into the east drainage below the glacier moraine. Nobody watches it because nobody's stupid enough to use it."

"And we are?"

"We are out of alternatives that end with us alive." She said it flatly, without drama, which somehow made it worse. Dee saved drama for things that deserved it, like river gauges and people who littered.

I sat down on a granite slab still holding the morning's cold and did what I do, which is look up.

* * *

People think storm chasing is about driving fast toward ugly weather, and sure, there's some of that—it's half the fun—but the actual job, the craft of it, is arithmetic. You read a sky and you get numbers. Instability, shear, dewpoint depression, cap strength, timing. The sky tells you what it intends to do and roughly when, and your whole career is built on whether you believe it.

The sky over the Sawtooth was telling me plenty.

High cirrus had been thickening since yesterday afternoon, thin as smoke at first, then milky, then the kind of gray-white veil that means moisture is climbing over the ridge from the Pacific and it has no intention of stopping. This morning the wind had swung from southwest to west-northwest and picked up—fifteen knots at the valley floor, which meant thirty-plus at the crest, maybe forty through the cols. The air had that pressed-flat quality, pressure falling in a way you feel in your fillings before any instrument confirms it. And there was a smell under everything, that green-mineral cold-smell that arrives ahead of mountain snow.

"Front hits tomorrow," I said. "Late. Maybe evening if we're lucky, midday if we're not."

"You're sure?"

"I'm never sure. That's the job. But I'd put money on it." I almost laughed at myself. Money. My relationship with money these days was mostly watching other people hold it. "Snow level dropping to eight thousand by tomorrow night, maybe lower behind the front. After that, every pass in this range closes and stays closed until spring."

Dee nodded slowly. "So we cross tomorrow or we don't cross at all."

"Or we hole up and wait out the season, which is the same as not crossing, except colder." I looked back down the valley, south, toward the low country where Marisol Vane's cheerful voice lived and Halloway's trucks idled at the mouths of every drainable canyon. "How long does your death route take?"

"Two days if the weather holds. Three if it doesn't." She paused. "It won't hold."

"Then we need to be off the crest before the front arrives, which means we start tonight and we don't stop." I did the math out loud because saying numbers makes them real: "Ten hours to the first col. We camp short of the second one—there'll be no shelter on the ridge itself—and we drop east tomorrow morning with maybe six hours of margin. If the front comes early, we're on exposed rock in a whiteout with forty-knot wind, and that's not a survival scenario, that's a search-and-recovery scenario."

"You say that so calmly."

"I'm a professional."

"You're a man who owes eleven thousand dollars to a woman who smiles too much."

"Same thing, basically."

She almost smiled. Then it went away, because we both remembered why we were doing arithmetic on a mountainside instead of drinking bad coffee in a diner, and neither of us said Ana Reyes's name or thought about the journal zipped inside my jacket, against my ribs, warm as a second heartbeat.

* * *

We spent the daylight getting ready, and I want to be honest about what that looked like, because the movies get it wrong. Getting ready to cross a range crest ahead of a storm is not heroic. It is inventory.

We laid everything out on a tarp and argued about it. Two sleeping bags rated to twenty degrees, which would be optimistic by twenty degrees. One stove with fuel for six meals if we were disciplined, four if we were honest. Rope—forty meters of it, because Dee insisted, and I've learned not to argue with Dee about rope. A tent designed for two polite people, not two desperate ones. Food that mostly consisted of things shaped like bricks. The satellite messenger, which had been dead since the burn scar and showed no signs of resurrection no matter how many times I shook it or threatened it.

"The messenger's junk," I said.

"The messenger's jammed. There's a difference. Jammed means someone's spending money to keep us quiet, which means we matter, which is information." Dee packed it anyway. "Also it might come back once we clear their coverage. These units bounce off satellites, but the jammers aren't magic. They're local."

"Local to what?"

"To wherever Corliss can afford to park equipment." She cinched a strap. "Which, given what we found at that intake, is apparently everywhere."

That was the thing I kept circling back to while my hands worked buckles and my mind worked angles: the intake structure, the fresh boot prints, the maintained channel bleeding a wild river into somebody's irrigation schedule. Somebody hiked in there regularly. Somebody with a payroll. And somewhere behind that payroll stood men in offices who had decided that five government hydrologists were a rounding error worth erasing.

And somewhere behind *them*, presumably, sat a contract with a shell company name and an inflated insurance valuation, signed in my own handwriting, by me, for money. I'd told myself it was bait only after the evidence made it undeniable, but the truth was I'd felt it in the diner, in the handshake warmth of Marisol Vane's optimism. Debt doesn't just cost money. It costs judgment. It makes you sign things.

Not anymore. Whatever else happened on that crest, I wasn't signing anything.

* * *

We started at four in the afternoon, which sounds wrong until you understand the logic: Dee wanted us hitting the steep stuff in the cool of evening, moving through the timber while light lasted, and arriving at the tree line by dark so we could sleep low and start the exposed climbing at first light with maximum margin. It was the kind of plan that only makes sense to someone who has been cold enough, high enough, long enough to respect the arithmetic of bodies and heat.

The forest took us in and slowed us down. Deadfall from some beetle-kill years back lay in gray tangles that forced us up and over and through, and the going was the kind where you measure progress in arguments with fallen trees. Dee moved through it like water finding its level, and I followed her line because following Dee's line was the single smartest operational decision available to me.

About an hour in, she stopped so abruptly I nearly walked into her pack.

"What?"

"Listen."

I listened. Wind in the crowns. A creek somewhere below us, running quieter than creeks should run in October. And underneath it, far off to the southeast, a sound I knew from a thousand miles of chasing: the flat chop of a helicopter working a grid.

"They're flying the valleys," I said.

"They're flying the *low* routes. Confirming we're not coming down." Her jaw worked. "They know we're still up here. Which means they know we have to go somewhere, and they're patient, because winter is on their side. All they have to do is wait for the passes to close and then sweep the range in spring."

"Cheerful."

"I contain multitudes." She started walking again, faster now. "Come on. We're racing a clock with teeth."

* * *

We broke tree line at last light, and I want to describe it properly, because even now, even knowing what it cost, I can't make myself regret seeing it.

The alpenglow came up the peaks from the east, rose-colored, and turned the whole upper world into something between a cathedral and a hazard warning. Granite spires threw shadows a mile long across snowfields that glowed pink and then violet and then went out all at once, like a switch thrown. The temperature fell through freezing while we watched, and our breath came out in flags.

"There." Dee pointed to a bench of talon-shaped rock below the first col, maybe ninety minutes on. "Camp. We sleep four hours and move at first light."

"Ninety minutes in the dark on scree."

"Ninety minutes in the dark on scree," she agreed, "or we sleep here at nine thousand feet in the open wind and lose body heat all night and start the climb already spent. Your call, weatherman."

"It's not a weather call."

"No. It's worse. It's a legs call." She looked at me, and in the last gray light I could see she was asking a real question: *do you have it in you?*

Here's the thing about commitment. People talk about it like it's a decision you make once, dramatically, at a crossroads. In my experience it's smaller and meaner than that. Commitment is deciding, over and over, in increments, that turning back is not on the menu—not because pride says so, but because the math does. Behind us: watchers, funnels, a debt, a betrayal, and a client who wanted a journal more than she wanted me solvent. Ahead of us: two cols, a glacier moraine, a storm with our names penciled into its margins. Staying still: death by patience. There was no direction on the compass labeled *safe*. There was only the direction labeled *forward*, and it was uphill.

"Legs are fine," I lied, and shouldered my pack.

* * *

The scree in the dark was exactly as awful as advertised. The slope was a river of loose stone frozen just enough to be treacherous instead of merely exhausting, and we climbed it in the blue-black hour with headlamps making little islands of the world, each island containing nothing but the next foothold. Somewhere below us a rock clattered down a gully on its own initiative, and we both froze and listened until the silence came back.

At the bench, we dug in. Dee built a wind wall out of flat stones with the efficiency of someone who'd done it a hundred times, and we ate brick-shaped food and drank water melted over a stove flame that gutted sideways in the wind, and we did not talk much, because talking costs heat and heat was currency now.

But at some point, lying in the dark with the wind worrying the tent fly like a large impatient animal, Dee said, "You could still go back."

"Down? Through Halloway's funnel? With a dead messenger and a journal full of evidence that gets people erased?"

"You could bury the journal. Walk out clean, tell Vane you found nothing, eat the debt another year. Live."

I thought about it, honestly. I gave it the full professional consideration it deserved, lying there listening to the temperature drop. And what I found at the bottom of the consideration was not courage, which disappointed me, and not principle, which surprised me. It was simpler than either. It was the intake structure. It was boot prints, fresh, regular as a commute. It was the image of some maintenance crew hiking in every few weeks to keep a stolen river flowing, unhurried, unafraid, certain that no one would ever come look.

Somebody had come look. Five somebodies. And the world had responded by pretending they'd never existed.

"Yeah," I said. "I could."

"But?"

"But I'm done being the guy who signs things." I pulled my bag tighter. "Besides, you heard the sky. Tomorrow night that front comes through and writes over everything. Tracks, camps, all of it. The range keeps what it keeps. If we want the truth to survive, we have to carry it out ourselves, tonight and tomorrow, over the top, before the weather closes the account."

Dee was quiet a long moment. Then: "That was almost poetic."

"Don't tell anyone."

"Your secret dies on this mountain." A pause. "Gideon. If the front comes early—if we're on the second col when it hits—we might not make the east side. You know that. You read that sky better than I do."

"I know."

"And you're going anyway."

I looked at the ceiling of the tent, at the fabric shuddering in gusts, and beyond it, at the memory of the crest waiting in the dark like a question.

"I'm a storm chaser, Dee. The storm always comes early eventually. You don't get to pick the day the sky decides to test you. You only get to pick whether you're on the ridge when it does." I closed my eyes. "Sleep. First light is in five hours, and we're going to need every one of them."

She didn't answer right away. When she did, her voice had gone soft and flat, the way it did when she meant something completely.

"All right. Over the top."

* * *

First light came up gray and hard, the color of a decision already made. The cirrus had thickened overnight into a solid deck, and the western horizon had gone the particular bruised shade that I have spent my adult life learning to fear and love in equal measure. The countdown was running. I could feel it ticking in the wind's shift, in the pressure sitting on my sinuses, in the way the light refused to warm.

We broke camp, roped up at the base of the first col per Dee's instruction, and started climbing into the teeth of the range with the storm building behind the horizon like a debt of its own, gathering interest.

Somewhere below and behind us, I knew, Halloway was watching the empty low passes and wondering where we'd gone. He'd figure it out. Men like him always figure it out eventually.

By then, I intended to be a rumor on the skyline, moving fast, carrying the truth over the top before the sky came down to collect.
