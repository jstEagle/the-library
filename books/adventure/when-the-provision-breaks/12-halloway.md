## 12. Halloway

I've been reading sign for twenty-six years, and I want to be clear about something before this story gets any further: I'm good at it. Not good the way a man is good at golf—good the way a dog is good at smelling. It's not a skill I chose. It's a condition.

So when I knelt at the edge of Camp Four that October morning and read what was written in the dirt, I didn't need anyone to translate.

Two sets of boot prints, one large and deliberate, one smaller with a hitch in the left stride—somebody favoring an ankle or an old injury. They'd come up from the south drainage, spent maybe forty minutes in camp, and left carrying more than they arrived with. The satellite phone was dead but not broken, which told me they'd found it and taken it anyway, because people who leave working equipment behind are people who expect to come back, and these two had no intention of coming back anywhere.

They'd also done something I almost never see: they'd walked out of camp on rock.

Not carelessly. Deliberately. Forty yards of granite shelf between the tents and the treeline, chosen and used, the way you'd use a door instead of a window. One of them knew tracking. Not book-knew. Knew it the way I know it, which is to say the way you know a language you were raised in.

I stood there longer than I should have, and my radio crackled at me twice before I answered it.

"Halloway. Status."

"Camp Four's been hit," I said. "Salvage crew came through."

"Gear?"

"Gone. Journal too, if it was here." It wasn't here. I'd checked the cache coordinates Corliss's people had given me three weeks ago, the ones that supposedly marked where the survey team's data case had been recovered and destroyed. The cache was empty. Had been empty for a while, by the look of the rust blooms on the hasp. Somebody upstairs had been lying to somebody downstairs, and I was standing in the gap between the lies getting rained on.

A pause on the line. Then: "Mr. Corliss wants them stopped before the divide. Use whatever's necessary."

*Whatever's necessary.* In my experience, when a man says *whatever's necessary* into a radio, he means something specific and he wants plausible deniability about it, and both of those things are your problem now, not his.

"Copy," I said, which is ranger for *I heard you and we'll see.*

* * *

Let me tell you how I ended up here, because I've had time on ridgelines to think about it, and the honest answer embarrasses me.

I was a ranger for nineteen years. District backcountry, Sawtooth and the ranges east of it. I knew every drainage the way you know the rooms of a house you grew up in—which creeks ran cold and clear and which ones ran warm and green in August, which trails the horse packers abused, where the snow bridges rotted first. I arrested poachers. I pulled drowned teenagers out of alpine lakes. I once spent four days looking for a lost diabetic eight-year-old and found her alive in a hollow tree, and that single afternoon bought me more happiness than everything else in my career combined.

Then the district got reorganized. Budget language. My supervisor—a decent man, which made it worse—sat me down and explained that the new funding model involved cooperative agreements with private landholders, and the largest private landholder in three counties was the Corliss Ranching Group, and that certain access policies would be harmonized going forward.

Harmonized. That was the word. I'd spent two decades learning the vocabulary of public service and it took one meeting to teach me the vocabulary that replaced it.

The thing is—and here's the embarrassing part—I didn't even fight it. Not really. I grumbled, I filed one memo nobody read, and when a headhunter called with a number that had a comma in the right place, I took it. Security chief, private lands division. Same mountains. Better boots. I told myself it was the same job: protecting things worth protecting.

And for six years, mostly, it was. Cattle operations have real problems—rustling, vandalism, idiots with rifles and beer. I guarded fences and water rights and I slept fine. Water rights especially. A ranch without water is a ghost town with delusions, and I understood defending water the way I understood defending anything: as a boundary job. Keep the line. Don't cross it yourself.

Then last spring, Corliss asked me to run a perimeter check on a government survey team working the upper basin. Standard request. Escort, observe, report. And the reports I filed started coming back with edits—not my words changed, exactly, but my observations quietly recontextualized until a hydrologist measuring streamflow looked, on paper, like a trespasser documenting nothing important.

I noticed. I want that on the record, wherever this record ends up. I noticed, and I said nothing, because the paycheck cleared and the mountains didn't look different and it's amazing what a man can not-see when not-seeing keeps his cabin heated.

Then the survey team vanished.

Four people. Reyes, Okafor—I'll get to her—Mbeki, and Tran. Experienced. Equipped. Their extraction date came and went, and the official story assembled itself with the smoothness of poured concrete: weather event, probable relocation, agencies notified, case referred. I watched that concrete pour and set, and I knew—knew the way you know a trail that's been rerouted around something everyone pretends isn't there—that no weather event takes four adults and leaves their camp buttoned up.

I told myself I was wrong. For about nine days, I told myself I was wrong. Then Corliss's operations manager handed me a file on the salvage contract—the one Marisol Vane's outfit had cooked up, the one with the inflated insurance valuation and the shell-company client—and said, "If they find anything besides gear, we need to know immediately," and the way he said *anything besides gear* had a weight to it that gravity doesn't produce naturally.

That's when I started keeping my own notes. Separate book. Old habit from the service—every ranger keeps a personal log the agency never sees, because the agency log records what happened and the personal log records what it meant. Mine was getting full.

* * *

We picked up their trail again above the diversion channel, and I'll say this for whoever planned that channel: it was elegant work. Low dam of graded fill, screened intake, buried pipe running eleven miles to the Corliss lateral. No permits on file. No easements. Just a river quietly losing ten percent of itself, year after year, while downstream ranches bloomed and upstream fish counts mysteriously declined and everybody blamed drought.

Drought. We'd been blaming drought for six years for water that was never missing. It was stolen. Stolen so smoothly that the theft had become part of the landscape, and landscapes are the hardest crimes to see because they don't move.

The salvage pair had followed the channel upstream same as us—they'd stood where I was standing, probably within a day of me, and read the same ground. Boot prints at the intake, fresh, someone maintaining the structure on a rotation. Heavy equipment tracks, recent. And then, on the ridge south of the intake, the prints scattered into flight pattern when an engine sounded—our engine, as it happened, though they couldn't have known that. To them it was just a machine coming, and machines coming meant men, and men meant the people who'd made four surveyors disappear.

I stood on that ridge and felt something I hadn't felt since the district reorganization: shame with a location. Not the diffuse kind, the free-floating professional melancholy I'd gotten comfortable wearing. This had coordinates. I was the engine sound. I was the reason two strangers were running through timber with their hearts going like trapped birds, and the only crime they'd committed was reading a river correctly.

"Contact likely west ridge, moving north," my spotter said. Ruiz. Good kid, ex-military, believed in the mission because nobody had yet shown him what the mission actually was. I envied him that. Briefly.

"Negative contact," I said. "Hold position. They're heading for high country and there's nothing up there but weather."

There was a silence on the line, the particular silence of a young man deciding whether to ask his boss why they weren't pursuing, and I let him sit in it. Let him marinate. Some lessons you can't hand to people; they have to find them in the woods themselves.

* * *

Here's what kept me up that night, in the cot section of the truck with the diesel heater ticking:

I'd read the salvage contract. All of it, twice, after the cache came up empty and the arithmetic stopped working. And the arithmetic said this: Vane's company insured the survey equipment at three times replacement value, billed the "recovery" to a client whose registered agent was a law firm that also represented Corliss's water litigation, and structured the payout so that it only triggered on *full* recovery—gear plus documentation. Gear alone paid nothing.

Which meant the journal wasn't collateral damage to this operation. It was the payload. The salvage crew hadn't stumbled into our business. They'd been aimed at it, like a round down a barrel, and the finger on the trigger belonged to a cheerful woman who recalculates debts over diner coffee, and the debt itself had been the lure.

Gideon Quintana. I'd pulled the file. Storm chaser, mediocre season, leveraged to the eyebrows. Dee Okafor—there was the name I'd dreaded seeing—former district ranger, terminated after filing a report on unlicensed diversions in the Corliss lateral. Her report. Her investigation. Buried so deep she'd been buried with it, badge and all.

So the consortium hadn't just stolen a river and disappeared a survey team. They'd baited a hook with a drowning woman's conscience and a bankrupt man's pride, cast it into the low country, and waited to see what swam up. And now those two were in my mountains, carrying the only true document in three counties, being hunted by me, and the whole design of it was so clean and so rotten that I lay there in the dark admiring it and hating myself in roughly equal measure.

Because here's the thing about boundaries. Nineteen years of rangering teaches you one lesson above all others, and it's not survival skills or botany or how to talk a drunk hunter off a ledge, though it includes all of those. It's this: the land doesn't care whose line is where. Water goes where water goes. Fences are fiction. Every property line ever drawn is a story we agreed to tell each other, and the moment you start enforcing a story with violence instead of agreement, you're not a guardian anymore. You're just the biggest kid on the playground, and the playground is on fire.

I signed on to guard property. Property, I could defend. Lines, I could hold. But somewhere between the reorganized district and the empty cache and the poured-concrete story about four vanished scientists, the property had stopped being the thing being guarded and started being the thing doing the guarding—it owned me, my payroll, my radio callsign, my boots on its ground—and the people I was hunting were the only ones acting like the land belonged to anybody at all.

* * *

Day three of the pursuit, we found where they'd gone to ground during the helicopter pass. Burn scar, south face, ugly country—snags everywhere, ash underfoot, visibility like swimming in milk. They'd hunkered in the middle of it, which was smart; burn scars eat tracks and radar both. While Ruiz worked the perimeter, I stood at the edge of the scar and did something I'm not proud of and can't regret either.

I gave them an hour.

Told Ruiz we needed to resupply at the truck before pushing the far side. Told him the terrain analysis favored an intercept at the saddle tomorrow, not today. Both statements were true, incidentally. That's the trick of it—you don't lie, you just choose which truths to say out loud, and a career in uniform will teach you that faster than any academy.

An hour. Maybe ninety minutes. Enough for two tired people to put distance between themselves and a helicopter that would otherwise paint them against black ash like targets on a range. I stood there watching the smoke-colored country and thought about Dee Okafor's personnel file, the commendations, the report she wrote that named names, the meeting where her supervisor—a decent person, they're always decent people, that's how the machine works, it runs on decent people—explained that her findings would be "harmonized."

She'd reported the theft. She'd been erased for reporting it. And now she was walking out of these mountains with the proof, and my job—the job I'd taken to keep my cabin heated—was to make sure the proof stayed in the mountains, along with, if necessary, whatever and whoever carried it.

*Whatever's necessary.* I turned the phrase over like a stone, looking underneath.

Underneath was Mbeki and Tran and Ana Reyes, frostbitten somewhere in the high country or dead, their disappearance a solved problem in somebody's ledger. Underneath was a river running eleven miles through a pipe it never agreed to. Underneath was a young spotter who still believed in the mission, and a payroll that cleared on the first, and a version of myself from six years ago standing at a hiring desk saying *same mountains, better boots,* and wanting, more than I've wanted anything in a long time, to walk back down the years and slap that man's handshake away.

Ruiz came back over the rocks. "Far side's clean," he said. "They're fast, Chief. For civilians."

"They're not civilians," I said, before I could stop myself.

He looked at me. "What are they, then?"

I watched the burn scar, and the wind moved through the dead snags with a sound like the whole ridge exhaling, and I thought about the answer that was true and the answer that was safe and the narrow country between them where a man can live for years if he never looks down.

"Rangers," I said. "One of them's a ranger. Watch your spacing."

We pushed north toward the saddle, and I let them keep their hour, and I wrote one line in the separate book, the old habit, the honest ledger:

*Day 3. Still telling myself I'm choosing which truths to say. Starting to wonder which side of the fence I'm standing on.*

By nightfall I had my answer, though I didn't write it down. The storm front was building over the divide—big, early, mean, the kind of system that closes passes and erases trails and gives two fugitives a fighting chance and gives a security chief a perfect excuse to report conditions impassable and stand down for forty-eight hours.

Forty-eight hours. I keyed the radio and told Corliss's operations desk exactly that: weather closing in, recommending suspension of aerial ops, ground teams holding at the lower saddles. All true. All defensible. All a gift, wrapped in procedure, tied with a bow made of my own cowardice—or my own conscience, depending on which of us survives to write the history.

The desk thanked me. Somewhere below the divide, two people I was paid to stop were moving into weather I'd spent my life respecting, carrying pages that a river's owner wanted burned.

I sat with the map until the light went, tracing the high route they'd have to take, and caught myself doing the unforgivable thing: checking their line for the best campsites. Marking the water sources. Hoping, in the wordless way you hope for strangers, that they'd find the sheltered side of the ridge before the wind swung north.

Then I closed the map, and told myself tomorrow I'd decide what I actually was.

Tomorrow, at the saddle, the decision was going to make itself—and I was going to have to live inside it, whichever side of the fence it put me on.
