## 13. Cold Camp

The snow cave was Dee's idea, which meant that if it killed us, at least the epitaph would be accurate.

I'd dug maybe a dozen of these in my ranger years, back when I still had a badge and an agency that pretended to listen to me. You find a drift with some spine to it—wind-packed, not the fluffy garbage that slumps on you like a tired drunk—and you tunnel in sideways, low, so the cold air pools at the door instead of your sleeping bag. You poke a vent hole with a ski pole. You line the floor with everything you own. Then you crawl into a hole in a mountain and hope morning is a thing that still happens to people like you.

"Cozy," Gideon said, which was generous. The cave was four feet high, six feet deep, and smelled like wet nylon and two humans who'd been sweating fear into synthetic fabric for three days. Outside, the wind was doing something obscene to the ridgeline, a sound like the whole range being dragged across sandpaper. Inside, our headlamps made two yellow coins of light, and between them sat the waterproof case with the journal, orange as a warning sign, sitting there like it knew what it had cost already.

We'd eaten half a sleeve of peanut butter crackers and called it dinner. We'd melted snow over a stove flame we couldn't afford, fuel-wise, and called it hot. We were down to one working radio, one-and-a-half tanks of stove gas, and a satellite messenger that had been dead since the burn scar—a fact I kept turning over in my head like a stone with something underneath it, because messengers don't just die. They get *made* to die. But that was tomorrow's problem. Tonight's problem was simpler: stay warm enough to want tomorrow.

Gideon sat with their back against the packed snow, knees up, hands buried in armpits, watching me the way you watch a fire you're not sure is going to catch. Storm chasers have this look. I'd seen it on rangers too—the look of someone whose whole profession is reading systems that want to kill them, who has learned to be calm inside the calm before the ugly part. Except Gideon's calm had a crack in it tonight, and through the crack I could see the arithmetic running.

"You're doing math," I said.

"I'm always doing math."

"Yeah, but this is the bad kind. I can tell because you've stopped blinking."

Gideon huffed something that wanted to be a laugh and didn't make it all the way. "Vane's cache is at the lower saddle. That message she left—if we take the journal down there, hand it over, we walk out of this range owing nothing. Clean slate. Warm truck. Actual food." A pause. "Alive."

"And if we don't?"

"If we don't, we carry forty pages of hydrology data over the crest of the Sawtooths in front of a storm system that has been building for two days like it's holding a grudge, and we try to find a reporter who isn't already bought, while men with rifles and a helicopter try to find us first." Gideon's voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "So. Math."

The wind screamed past the entrance. Somewhere above us, the cornices were having a conversation about whether we deserved to live, and I didn't love their tone.

"Say what you actually mean," I told them.

"What I actually mean is that Reyes and her people are alive because they hid, Dee. They hid for five months. They let the world think they were dead because every institution that was supposed to protect them was on Corliss's payroll or scared of it. And now we're proposing to do the opposite of hiding, loudly, in winter, with—" Gideon gestured at the cave, at us, at the general situation, "—*this*."

"They hid and it bought them frostbite and five months of eating pine bark," I said. "Hiding wasn't a plan. It was a stall. Somebody still has to walk the truth out of here."

"Somebody could also walk a debt settlement out of here."

And there it was. Laid on the snow between us like a third person neither of us wanted to look at directly.

I pulled my knees in tighter. My toes had been complaining since the saddle—not the sharp complaint of frostbite yet, more the dull grievance of tissue that had filed a formal request for better management—but the cold gets into your judgment long before it gets into your feet. That's the thing nobody tells you about hypothermia. It doesn't start at your fingers. It starts at your decisions. The cold makes every compromise feel reasonable, makes warm trucks and clean slates glow like salvation, makes the truth feel like a luxury item, something for people with heated seats.

Which is exactly why Vane picked winter.

"That contract," I said slowly. "You said the client was a shell company."

"Meridian Ridge Recovery Services. Registered in Delaware. Sure."

"And the insurance valuation on the gear was insane."

"Four hundred percent over replacement cost. I noticed. I signed anyway." Gideon's jaw worked. "Because Marisol smiled at me across a diner table and told me it was one clean way out, and I wanted it to be true so badly I could taste it."

"So follow it forward. Vane fronts the salvage contract. She knows Corliss wants that journal found—or wants to know where it is, same thing. She needs someone desperate enough to go up there in November and dumb enough not to ask questions. Enter: a storm chaser with a boat anchor of debt." I let that sit. "She wasn't offering you a way out, Gideon. She was offering you as bait. You weren't the customer. You were the instrument."

"I know." Quiet. "I did the math in the truck outside her office, after I signed. That's how far behind I am—I do the math *after*."

For a while neither of us said anything. The stove ticked as it cooled. My headlamp beam caught the breath fog drifting between us, and I thought about how everything alive in this cave was burning something to stay that way—fuel, fat, faith, whatever we had left of each.

"Here's my confession," I said. "Since we're doing inventory."

Gideon looked up.

"When I reported Corliss the first time—the grazing allotments, the dead creek below their east pasture—I did it by the book. Filed with the district office. Copies to state. I thought paper was armor. Six weeks later I'm reassigned to 'special projects,' which meant counting culverts in a district four hours from anywhere, and the ranger who took my old beat found her tires slashed and then found herself transferred too, and then nobody found anything ever again because that's how it works. It doesn't come for you with guns first. It comes for you with paperwork. Guns are the backup." I wrapped my arms around my shins. "So when I say I know how this ends if we hand the journal to the system, I'm not guessing. I watched the system eat a whole watershed and burp."

"But a reporter isn't the system."

"A reporter is a person with a byline and a mortgage. Could be bought. Could be buried. Could print it and watch it die on page twelve." I shrugged, and my shoulders cracked like kindling. "But it's the only door that isn't owned. That's the whole argument. Not that it'll work. That it's the last unlocked door, and I have personally stood outside enough locked ones."

Gideon was quiet a long time. When they spoke again, the level voice had gone somewhere else—lower, rougher, honest.

"You want to know the pathetic part? If it were just me, I'd sell it. Tonight. I'd drag this case down to the saddle and trade it for a zero balance and never think about the Corliss Ranching Group except when I drove past their billboards." They laughed, no humor in it at all. "That's who I am, Dee. I chase storms for money. I read the sky because the sky pays better than anything else I'm qualified for. I signed a contract with a shell company because the number at the bottom was bigger than the knot in my stomach. I am not built to be the person who dies broke for the truth. I've met that person. They're always cold and always right and everybody attends a lovely memorial service."

"But?"

"But I keep thinking about Ana Reyes writing in that journal by candlelight, knowing nobody was coming, knowing the river was being stolen a cubic foot per second at a time, and writing the numbers down anyway. Precise numbers. To the decimal. Because if she was going to disappear, the disappearing was going to *mean* something." Gideon's breath fogged and hung. "And I keep thinking about Halloway standing in that saddle looking at us like a man reading his own eviction notice. He read those pages, Dee. He let us walk. Whatever he tells himself he is, he couldn't un-know what he knew. That's what the journal does. It's not paper. It's a debt of its own. Once you've read it, somebody collects."

"Welcome to my last eight years," I said.

"Yeah, well. Your last eight years are contagious."

I almost laughed, and the laugh turned into a shiver halfway out, and the shiver turned into a full-body event that lasted long enough to embarrass us both. Gideon crawled across the gap between us without asking—storm chasers don't ask, they just move toward the center of the system—and we sat shoulder to shoulder, sharing heat like it was the last currency on earth, which under the circumstances it basically was.

"Okay," Gideon said finally. "The reporter. Say we make the crest. Say we survive the descent. Who? Name one name you'd bet Reyes's life on."

I'd been chewing on that for two days, actually, in the gaps between terror. "There's a woman at the capital bureau—Elena Marsh. Did a series two years ago on water rights fraud in the northern basins. Got sued for it, won, and then got quietly dropped by her own outlet for making an advertiser nervous. Freelances now. Angry, unbought, and hungry enough to burn Corliss down just for the pleasure of the arson." I paused. "Also she owes me. I fed her documents once, back when I still had access. Never got used, but she remembers who tried."

"Elena Marsh." Gideon said it like testing ice. "One name. One shot. If she's compromised, or dead, or on Corliss's retainer like everyone else—"

"Then we're five days deeper into the mountains with a worthless case full of paper and a story nobody will print, and we'll have lost everything except the only thing that was ever actually ours."

"Which is?"

"The fact that we know." I tapped the orange case with one numb knuckle. "They can buy the journal. They can't buy that we read it. That's why Vane wants the physical pages, Gid—it's not the paper she's afraid of. It's witnesses. Paper can be burned. People who've read it have to be dealt with forever. So we stop being salvage contractors and start being witnesses, and we make ourselves expensive to deal with by standing next to cameras when we do it."

Gideon was silent so long I checked that they hadn't drifted off into the sleep you don't wake up from. Then: "You realize we're both going to owe Vane either way. If we run this to Marsh instead of paying her, she doesn't just lose the fee. She loses Corliss's business, and Corliss doesn't strike me as a gracious ex-client. We're not choosing between debt and freedom. We're choosing which debt."

"All debts are the same debt," I said. "It's just a question of who holds the paper. I'd rather owe a journalist than a loan shark. At least the journalist wants something true."

Outside, the wind changed. I felt it before I heard it—the pressure shift in my ears, the way the sound against the cave mouth went from screaming to a flat, total roar, like the mountain had put a shell over the world. I'd spent eleven years learning what that transition meant. It meant the front wasn't coming anymore. It was here.

Gideon felt it too. I watched their head come up, watched them go still in that specific way they went still when the sky started talking, and for a second I saw the other Gideon—the one underneath the debt and the diner smiles and the bad arithmetic. The one who loved weather the way monks love silence.

"It's early," they said. "Twelve hours early, minimum. This system moved faster than the models."

"Models lie," I said. "Mountains don't. What does it mean for us?"

Gideon looked at the orange case, then at the cave mouth, where the darkness had gone the texture of solid wool. When they answered, their voice had the calm of someone who has stopped negotiating with reality and started taking notes.

"It means tomorrow we don't choose the route. The storm does. And we pray it hates Corliss more than it hates us."

I was still deciding whether that counted as optimism or a threat when my headlamp swept the cave wall near the entrance—and caught what the wind had uncovered in the last hour. Boot prints. Pressed into the old drift at the threshold, half-filled with spindrift, frozen stiff.

Not ours. Too big for mine, wrong tread for Gideon's. And pointed *inward*. Toward the ridge. Toward the high country everyone sensible had already fled.

Someone had passed this cave today, heading up, alone, in weather no one sane walks in—and unless I'd forgotten how to count, the prints were fresh enough that whoever it was might still be above us, closer to the crest than we were, between us and the only route out.

"Gideon," I whispered. "We have company on the mountain."
