## 14. The Second Camp

The smoke was what saved us, or damned us, depending on how you wanted to file the paperwork later.

Dee smelled it first—she always smelled things first, a habit I'd decided was either ranger instinct or a lifetime of being the last one blamed for the campfire—and she put a hand on my arm without a word. We'd been cutting across a bench of granite above a frozen creek, moving slow because slow was the only speed the altitude allowed, and there it was: a thread of woodsmoke, thin and gray, rising out of a fold in the mountain that had no business holding anything warmer than a marmot's opinion.

"Nobody camps here," Dee breathed. "There's no water access, no windbreak, no reason."

"Except not being found."

She looked at me. I looked at her. Somewhere below us, a helicopter had been circling the low passes all morning like a fly that had learned where the picnic was, and the logic assembled itself without my permission.

"Except not being found," she agreed.

We left the packs wedged under an overhang and came down on the fold of the mountain the way I imagine a couple of very tired, very cold burglars would—badly, but with commitment. Dee led. I followed, cataloguing the things my storm-chaser brain kept trying to file as *weather* and my increasingly reluctant salvage-brain kept re-filing as *crime scene*: the way the trees thinned, the way the wind funneled, the way the smoke didn't rise so much as leak, like someone was being very careful about it.

Careful. That was the word. Everything about that fold of mountain was careful. A fire ring of stones no bigger than a dinner plate. A lean-to of branches and survey plastic. And then, as we came around a shoulder of granite with Dee's hand up and my heart doing a drum solo against my ribs—

A voice.

"Don't." It was a woman's voice, cracked down the middle, but level underneath, the way a bridge is level while it's being rated for load. "I have a flare gun and I have nothing left to lose, and I promise you I'm a better shot than I was three weeks ago."

"Ana Reyes?" Dee said.

Silence. The good kind and the bad kind, all at once.

"Who's asking," the voice said finally, "and how did you find us, and how many of you are there, and are you armed, and—"

"Dee Okafor." Dee stepped out from behind the granite with her hands visible and empty. "Formerly Sawtooth district. You won't remember me, but I sat in the back of a public comment meeting in Ketchum in 2019 while you argued about flow gauges, and you were the only person in that room who'd actually read the hydrology report."

Another silence, longer.

"Dee Okafor," the voice repeated, and something in it unbent by a degree. "You're the one they fired."

"I'm the one who *resigned*," Dee said, with the particular dignity of someone who has rehearsed that correction in the shower for years. "They just let everyone believe the first thing."

"And him?"

"Gideon Quintana. I drive fast in bad weather for a living, which until last week felt like the least moral thing in this state." I stepped out beside her, hands up, doing my best impression of a man who was not carrying a satellite messenger that didn't work and a journal that could get everyone in this clearing killed. "We came looking for your equipment. The government kind. There's a contract on it."

"There's a contract on *us*," Reyes said, and stepped out of the lean-to.

I'd built a picture of her over the past week out of journal entries and absence—a careful, methodical mind, someone who wrote *flow anomaly at station 7, re-checking* in handwriting that never slanted even on a bad day. The real Ana Reyes was smaller than the handwriting. Frostbitten across the cheekbones in waxy yellow patches, wrapped in layers that had been mended with survey flagging tape, eyes that had done arithmetic with survival numbers for so long the numbers had worn grooves in her face.

She was also, unmistakably, still the person in charge.

"Three of us," she said. "Mbeki and Solano. They're in the back. Before you get excited: yes, we're alive, no, we're not well, and no, you cannot have the data."

"The data," I said. "Not the journal?"

"The journal is the data. The journal is the only copy of three years of flow measurements proving that the Corliss Ranching Group has been bleeding the East Fork dry through an unlicensed diversion, and that at least two people in the state water office signed off on it, and that when we filed our preliminary findings, our funding was pulled in eleven days and a man in a gray truck started photographing our camp from the ridge." She said it all in one breath, like she'd been holding it since October. "So. The data."

* * *

The rest of the camp was worse than the first look.

Solano had a foot wrapped in plastic and duct tape that I did not ask to see under, because the smell had already introduced itself. Mbeki had a cough that sounded structural. They'd been living on cached rations, melted snow, and what Mbeki called, with a gallows humor I came to respect immediately, "the world's worst sabbatical." The flare gun Reyes had promised was real. So was a hunting rifle with six rounds and a notebook tallying every aircraft that had passed overhead—dates, times, headings—in handwriting as disciplined as her field notes.

Three weeks. They'd been up here three weeks, hiding above ten thousand feet in a fold of rock with a flare gun and a cough, because every institution that was supposed to catch a thing like this had, in their assessment, already been bought.

"You could have walked out," I said. We were crouched by their careful little fire, and I was watching Reyes feed it a stick the size of my finger, rationing even the flame. "People walk out of the Sawtooth hurt every year. Frostbite, dehydration, worse. Search and rescue doesn't ask who you work for."

"Search and rescue reports to the county," Reyes said. "The county sheriff takes campaign donations from the Corliss family. I checked. I checked everything, Mr. Quintana, that's the whole point of me. When your preliminary findings get your funding pulled and a gray truck starts circling your camp, you don't call the people the gray truck votes with." She fed the fire another finger-width of stick. "We broke camp at night. We took the journal, we left the tents, we left the satphone dead on the table so anyone who came looking would find exactly what a *vanished* team looks like. Vanished teams get searched for. Dead teams get closed files. And teams that walked out get asked questions by people who already know the answers."

"And the questions come with a gray truck," Dee said quietly.

"The questions come with a gray truck," Reyes agreed.

I sat with that. Around us the fold of mountain held its careful little secret, and above the ridgeline the sky had gone the flat pewter color that I have spent my whole professional life reading and that was now, for the first time, reading *me*—because that wasn't just weather up there. That was a front, and it was early, and it was going to close every pass in this range inside of forty-eight hours.

Here's the thing I want to be honest about, because honesty is the only currency I've got left that isn't owed to somebody: for about ten seconds, I did the arithmetic.

Three survivors. One of them half-carried. A journal worth—what? To Marisol, enough to clear my debt and then some. To a newspaper, enough to burn down a ranching empire. To Corliss, enough to justify whatever a man like Halloway did to people in folds of mountains. I stood there in the woodsmoke with my hands warming for the first time in days and I thought, *I came here for gear. The gear is sitting in a camp four miles north. The contract pays on delivery. There is a version of this where I do my job.*

Then Solano coughed, deep and wet, and Reyes adjusted the blanket around his shoulders without looking away from me—she'd been watching me the whole time, of course she had, she'd had three weeks to learn what people's faces do when they're doing arithmetic—and I felt the version of me that does that arithmetic shrivel up and blow away like a bad forecast.

"Relief curdling into fury" is a phrase I'd have called melodramatic a month ago. I stand by it now. Because here's what happened in my chest in the space of that fire-fed minute: first the *relief*, enormous and stupid and almost tearful—they're alive, the missing people are *alive*, the story has a better ending than the one I'd been carrying since Camp Four—followed immediately, on its heels, no gap at all, by the *fury*. Because they weren't rescued. They weren't even lost. They were *hiding*, three educated professionals with frostbite and duct-tape boots, hiding from their own government's paper trail, and the machinery that put them on that mountain and then hunted them off it was the same machinery that had hired me—cheerfully, with a smile and a pen—to come find the evidence.

The debt was the trap. I'd known it in my gut since the ridgeline. But sitting in that firelight, looking at Solano's wrapped foot and Mbeki's structural cough and Reyes's flagging-tape mended coat, I understood it all the way down: Marisol Vane hadn't sent me to recover equipment. She'd sent a broke storm chaser and a disgraced ranger to do what her client's security couldn't do quietly—*locate the journal*. And I had signed the contract. I'd read the inflated insurance numbers and signed anyway, because winter debt has teeth, and my teeth had been the ones on the line, and I'd never once asked whose leash the whole arrangement hung from.

"Mr. Quintana," Reyes said. "You've gone somewhere."

"Yeah," I said. "Sorry. I'm doing math I don't like."

"Tell me the math."

So I told her. The shell company. The inflated valuation. The contract's oddly specific language about *documentation and records, condition notwithstanding*—clause 9(b), which I'd skimmed at a diner table while Marisol Vane refilled my coffee and beamed at me like I was her favorite nephew. Dee added the graded road, the heavy tire tracks, the jammed satellite messenger, the helicopter that flew search patterns over empty passes and somehow never over the one place we'd actually been. Reyes listened the way she read hydrology reports—completely, without blinking, building the model.

"Vane," she said at the end. "Marisol Vane. She holds paper on half the small operators in the valley. Corliss uses her for things Corliss can't be seen touching." She looked at the fire. "We knew about the diversion. We did not know how far up the money went. I think I didn't *want* to know."

"That's the part that's going to get us killed," Dee said. "Not the mountain. The part where it's not one bad ranch, it's a *system*, and systems don't leave loose ends. They send people like Halloway."

"Halloway," Reyes said, and her face did something complicated. "He was on the ridge twice. Through the scope. He had chances. He never took them." She fed the fire its last finger of stick, and the flame leaned and steadied. "I've been telling myself that means something."

* * *

We spent an hour that felt like a day making the plan, which is to say: there was no plan, there was only the least bad sequence of catastrophes.

The low passes were watched—that was settled fact. The east slope was a two-day descent in good conditions and a death sentence in the front that was now visibly assembling itself over the western peaks like a jury. Solano couldn't walk it fast. Reyes wouldn't leave a single page of the data behind, and when I suggested—gently, I thought, though she took it like a challenge—that maybe the journal could be photographed and the originals cached, she looked at me the way a surgeon looks at a man suggesting the operation could be done by mail.

"Paper doesn't have a battery," she said. "Paper doesn't get corrupted. Paper survives being dropped in a creek. Three people died of exposure on this range last year and nobody found them until spring, Mr. Quintana. If we don't walk out, this journal is the only witness. It walks out with us or it doesn't walk out at all."

"The injured man walks out with us too," Dee said. It wasn't a question, and it wasn't negotiable, and nobody treated it like either.

"Both," I said. "Everything walks out. That's the whole point, isn't it? Otherwise we're just the next set of loose ends."

Reyes looked at me for a long moment, and whatever she saw in my face—the arithmetic finally finished, the answer landed on—she nodded once, like a gate opening.

Then Mbeki, from the back of the lean-to, in a voice scraped down to gravel, said the thing that turned my stomach over and set the clock on fire.

"Helicopter," he said. "North ridge. Coming up the drainage."

We killed the fire with snow and went flat among the rocks, and I put the binoculars on it: not the search-pattern bird from that morning, which had flown like a man looking for his keys. This one flew like a man who'd found them. Low, deliberate, working the fold of the mountain methodically, a quarter mile out and closing, and hanging off its skid a load I recognized from a hundred chase-season supply drops and had never once thought of as a weapon.

A line, and a basket. Room for cargo.

Or for a journal. Or for three frostbitten witnesses who'd been missing for three weeks and were about to be found by exactly the wrong people, in exactly the place they'd hidden to avoid them, with a storm coming down that would bury everything—evidence, tracks, testimony, us—under six feet of deniable white.

"Dee," I said, not lowering the binoculars. "How fast can five people move?"

She didn't answer right away, and the pause was the answer.

"Then we'd better start," I said, "before it finishes that drainage."
