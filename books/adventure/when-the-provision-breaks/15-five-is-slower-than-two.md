## 15. Five Is Slower Than Two

Here's a thing they don't put in the ranger training manual, mostly because I burned my copy in a parking lot in 2019: arithmetic changes at altitude. Two people moving fast over broken ground can cover twenty miles a day if they hate themselves properly. Add three more bodies—frostbitten, half-starved, and carrying the collected hydrological records of the Corliss Ranching Group's crimes in waterproof cases lashed to their backs like penance—and that twenty becomes twelve. Then ten. Then whatever number the mountain feels like giving you that morning.

I did the math at first light on the fourth day out of the second camp, standing over Ana Reyes while she re-taped the data cases with the methodical care of a woman wrapping a wound.

"We leave the duplicates," I said. "The raw notebooks stay here, cached under cairn. You keep the copies."

She didn't look up. "There are no duplicates."

"There are always duplicates. That's field science 101."

"The duplicates were in the truck," she said, "and the truck is at the bottom of Alder Creek, and so is everything that was in it." She smoothed the tape down with her thumb. "What's in these cases is every measurement we took for eleven months. Every flow rate, every temperature series, every photograph of that intake structure before it was finished. If we lose it, we're two storm chasers telling a story nobody believes about a company everybody likes."

I wanted to argue. Arguing was free, and it was warm, and warmth was at a premium. But she was right, and we both knew it, and being right at nine thousand feet is just another way of being cold.

So we carried it all. Eleven months of somebody else's truth, forty-some pounds of it, distributed across five backs like a tax nobody had voted for.

* * *

The fifth member of our little expedition was named Tomas Reyes—no relation to Ana, though they'd stopped correcting people around day two—and by the fifth morning his right foot had stopped participating in the group's activities.

Frostbite has stages, and I knew them all from search-and-rescue work, back when I still had a badge and an institution behind it. First there's the white, waxy look, the numbness, the part where the person insists they're fine because fine is all they can feel. Then comes the rewarming, if you're lucky enough to get someone somewhere warm, and the pain—that screaming, blistering, want-to-amputate-it-yourself pain that means tissue might live. Tomas had gotten his frostbite before we found them, in the first bad week of their self-imposed exile, and he'd never gotten proper rewarming. What he'd gotten instead was days of walking on a foot that had quietly died below the ankle and started announcing its death to the rest of him.

By the fifth morning the line of demarcation had gone from pink to angry red to a bruised purple-black, and the smell when we changed his dressing in the lee of a granite wall was the sweetish, wrong smell that every medic learns to dread.

Gideon crouched across from me, holding the bandage roll, her face doing something complicated.

"That's infection," she said quietly. Not a question.

"That's infection going septic," I said. "Or close enough that the distinction is academic."

Tomas lay propped against his pack, gray-faced, sweating in air cold enough to flash-freeze the sweat. He smiled at us anyway. He'd been smiling at us for two days, apologizing for slowing us down, and I wanted to shake him and tell him to stop being so damn polite about dying.

"How long?" Ana asked. She stood a few feet off, arms crossed, and I watched her do the same math I was doing—the one where hours of delay translated into degrees of latitude between her friend and a hospital.

"Without antibiotics?" I said. "Septic shock inside a week, maybe less if his heart's already working this hard. With antibiotics, he needs surgery anyway. Debridement, maybe amputation." I looked at the foot, then made myself look at his face instead. "He needs to be off this mountain in thirty-six hours. Forty-eight at the outside."

Silence, except the wind doing its usual commentary through the notch above us.

Then Gideon said, "So we move faster," in the tone of a woman who has decided that physics is more of a suggestion than a law, and started breaking camp.

* * *

We moved faster. It didn't help.

That's the cruel joke of it. Speed costs energy, energy costs food, and we were rationing food at a level I'd have called criminal back when I enforced things. Five people burning four thousand calories a day on fifteen hundred calories of oats, jerky, and a candy budget Gideon guarded like nuclear material. By noon each day the group's pace wasn't set by the strongest or the weakest but by some grim consensus of everyone's second wind, and the terrain kept voting against us.

The route Dee Okafor had planned—I should say the route I had planned, since I'm the one narrating this and false modesty is just modesty wearing a hat—cut along a bench above the east fork, staying under the ridgeline where Halloway's helicopters couldn't see us without flying into the weather Gideon kept promising was coming. It was good route-finding, if I say so myself. It was also slow. Bench country means side-hilling, side-hilling means traversing, and traversing with a limping man and forty pounds of other people's convictions means you measure progress in fence posts instead of miles.

Around midday we hit the talus field below Point 9,400, and I called a halt just to watch everyone breathe.

Reyes came up beside me and stood looking back the way we'd come, at the valley where a river ran silver and full and lied about itself all the way to the irrigation districts downstream.

"You think I'm killing him," she said.

"I think you're weighing him," I said. "Same as the rest of us."

"He told me to leave the cases. Last night. He said, 'Ana, bury them under a cairn and come back in June.'" Her jaw worked. "And I said no."

"Why?"

"Because in June, either we're heroes or we're fugitives, and either way that cairn is a rock pile with paper in it. Because Corliss has a helicopter and a security chief who used to wear the same uniform I did, and they will find a cairn." She turned to look at me, and her eyes had the flat shine of someone running on conviction fumes. "You reported them once. You know what happens to evidence that waits."

I did know. I'd written a report once, twenty-two pages, with photographs, about water rights violations on grazing allotments, and I'd watched it go into a filing cabinet the way reports go into filing cabinets when the agency's budget gets friendly visits from ranching lobbyists. My report didn't die. It just went to sleep, and I got fired awake.

"So we carry it," I said.

"So we carry it," she agreed.

Neither of us said the third part, which was that carrying it meant Tomas's foot carried it too, strapped to a man whose body was spending its last reserves defending forty pounds of proof. Principle is cheap when it weighs nothing. This principle weighed what a small child weighs, and it rode on the back of a dying man, and every hour we honored it cost him something specific and countable.

Hours and toes. That's the exchange rate. Nobody prints it on the brochure.

* * *

Gideon caught up with me at the far edge of the talus, where the bench narrowed to a ledge with a drop on the left that would kill you professionally rather than personally.

"We need to talk about the fork," she said.

"There's no fork. There's the high route and there's nothing."

"There's the riverbed shortcut. Down through the gorge, follow the east fork to the confluence, saves us six hours minimum."

"And puts us in a slot canyon with one exit, directly beside the diversion channel, which is the one place on this mountain Halloway knows better than I do, because his people built it."

"Six hours, Dee." She held up her hand, fingers spread. "Six hours is the difference between a foot and no foot. Maybe between a friend and no friend."

I looked down the gorge. The river ran loud down there, louder than it should have been for October, swollen with the releases from the intake structure upstream—Corliss's machinery flushing excess water on some schedule known only to accountants. The sound of it filled the canyon like applause for something terrible.

"If we take the gorge," I said, "and there's a patrol at the confluence, we don't lose six hours. We lose everything. They radio ahead, the passes get locked down, and we spend winter explaining ourselves to federal agents who've already had six weeks to decide how much of this they want to hear."

Gideon was quiet for a moment. Wind pulled at her hood.

"You sound like you've done this before," she said. "The losing-everything part."

"I have done this before. Just smaller." I started walking. "Keep the high line. We'll make time tomorrow when we clear the shoulder of the peak and hit snowpack—we can glissade the north bowls, save hours that way, and snow covers tracks better than rock does."

"Snow also kills you."

"Snow also kills you," I agreed. "But it kills you honestly. Rock just lets you fall and takes no responsibility."

Behind us, Tomas laughed—a real laugh, cracked and surprised out of him—and for a second the whole stupid column of us felt like something that might survive.

* * *

We made camp that night in a hollow below the shoulder, walls of stone on three sides, and I sat with Tomas while the others rigged shelter and melted snow.

His fever was up. I could feel it radiating off him like a stove with the door open, which in that cold was almost a comfort, which is the kind of thought you have at altitude and then hate yourself for.

"Tell me about the badge," he said. His voice had gone dreamy at the edges. Fever-dreamy. "Ana says you lost it over the same water."

"I didn't lose it. I gave it back. There's a difference, and the difference is that losing it implies I wanted to keep it." I checked his pulse out of habit. Fast, thready, but regular. "I found cattle grazing a closed riparian allotment. Corliss brand. Reported it. Got told the closure was 'under review.' Reported again. Got transferred to a district office four hours from my own district. Third report, I got a letter about insubordination and a meeting where a man explained to me, very kindly, that I was young and had a career to think about."

"What'd you say?"

"I said a lot of words that ended my career, and then I drove home and drank a beer and realized the only thing I'd actually lost was health insurance." I pulled his sleeping bag higher. "The water kept getting stolen either way. That's the part that sticks. All that righteousness, and the river didn't even notice."

Tomas was quiet a while. When he spoke again, the fever had dropped his voice to almost nothing.

"When we first found the diversion," he said, "before any of this—I remember thinking, this is the biggest story of my life. And now I'm lying in a hole in the ground, and my foot is—" he gestured vaguely at the dark where his boot was, "—and I keep thinking, please, just let it matter. Let it matter more than the foot. More than all of it. Because if it doesn't matter, then—"

"It'll matter," I said.

"You don't know that."

"No," I admitted. "But I know the alternative. The alternative is it doesn't matter, and I've lived in that country, and the rent is worse than anything this mountain charges."

He smiled again, that polite, apologetic smile, and closed his eyes, and I sat there listening to his breath and hating every institution I'd ever trusted, and a few I hadn't yet.

* * *

It was Gideon who woke me, an hour before dawn, with a hand on my shoulder and a look on her face I recognized from a hundred storm intercepts—the look of a person reading bad news in the sky and finding it beautiful anyway.

"Front's early," she whispered. "Look west."

Above the western ridge, where stars should have been, there was a blackness with texture to it, a solid rolling cloud deck eating the sky from the top down, and beneath it the wind had swung hard out of the northwest and turned the kind of cold that bites through everything you own.

"How long?" I asked.

"Twelve hours to the leading edge. Maybe less. It's moving like it's late for something." She crouched next to me, and in the starlight her breath came out in flags. "Which means we have a choice. Hole up and let it pass—two days, maybe three, in terrain this exposed with his fever—and hope Halloway's grounded too. Or race it."

"Race it where?"

"Over the shoulder, down the north bowls, glissade what we can, and reach the saddle before the front hits. After that—" she shrugged, and the shrug contained the entire Sawtooth Range and everything it intended to do to us, "—after that we're in the storm, and the storm doesn't take sides."

I looked at Tomas, asleep or unconscious, his cheeks flushed dark with fever. I looked at the data cases, lashed and taped and sacred. I looked west at the weather coming down on us like a verdict.

"Six hours," I said. "You said the gorge saves six hours."

"Dee—"

"But the storm saves more. A storm grounds helicopters. A storm erases tracks. A storm makes Halloway's whole apparatus useless for as long as it lasts." I stood, joints cracking like green wood. "We don't race the front to the saddle. We ride the front's skirt—stay just ahead of the worst of it, let the leading snow cover our trail, and use the visibility collapse to move in the open. Your specialty, isn't it? Weather as cover?"

Gideon stared at me, and then she grinned, and it was not entirely a nice grin.

"You've been hanging around storm chasers too long."

"Somebody has to corrupt you," I said, and went to wake the others, and tell them the plan, and watch Ana Reyes tighten the straps on forty pounds of truth while a man with a dying foot began, slowly, to sit up.

Above us, the first flakes came down fine and dry as ash, and the mountain went quiet the way it does when it's deciding something.
