## 16. Ambush at the Saddle

Halloway

I'd been a ranger for eleven years before I learned that the wilderness doesn't care who's watching it. That was the part of the job I loved, actually—the indifference. A grizzly doesn't check your badge. A whiteout doesn't ask who you work for. Out there, you were just a body with a pulse and a set of decisions, and the mountains graded you honestly.

Then I took the Corliss job, and I forgot all of that for a while. Or maybe I didn't forget it. Maybe I just stopped looking at it straight on, the way you stop looking at a bad tooth.

The saddle between Peak 9,140 and the ridge they call the Old Man's Teeth is the only reasonable crossing for twenty miles in either direction. If you'd studied the topo maps the way I had—over coffee, over whiskey, over the long gray hours when you can't sleep because you keep doing arithmetic about frostbite and elevation—you'd know that too. So did Dee Okafor, which is why I wasn't surprised when the thermal feed picked up five heat signatures crawling toward the notch at 4:40 in the afternoon, moving like people who'd spent too many days above ten thousand feet and knew exactly what that cost.

Five signatures. The salvage crew plus three extras. Which meant the survey team wasn't dead, and hadn't been since September, and every report I'd filed saying otherwise was a lie I'd written with my own hands.

I sat on the rocks above the saddle with my binoculars and my rifle I had no intention of using, and I watched them come up the switchbacks like a slow-motion film of something breaking apart. Two of them carried a litter between them—a young man, Vasquez from the manifest, his left foot wrapped in what looked like an entire sleeping bag. One woman moved like her hands were ruined; she held them against her chest the way you hold something you've already lost but haven't admitted yet. And leading them, a tall woman with gray in her braid who kept stopping to look back down-valley, not at her people—at the terrain. Reading it. Reyes. Ana Reyes, who'd once given a talk at the Boise field office about riparian survey ethics that half the room slept through and I hadn't, because she'd said one thing in it that had lodged in me like a splinter: *A measurement is a promise. You don't get to break it just because the number is inconvenient.*

They'd been promised measurements. Corliss had broken every one of them.

And then there was Quintana. The storm chaser. I'd read the file Marisol Vane had assembled—credit history, chase footage, a debt load that would make a grown accountant weep—and I'd thought: *hired gun, mercenary, will hand over anything for a clean ledger.* That was the whole design, wasn't it? Vane dangles a contract, Quintana finds the gear, and somewhere in the finding, the journal surfaces, and we buy it back before anyone reads past page twelve. Clean. Tidy. Nobody vanishes twice.

Except Quintana hadn't sold it. Three weeks in the high country, hunted, freezing, carrying a stranger with septic frostbite on a litter made of pack frames and prayer—and they still had the journal. I knew because I'd found the cache copy myself, two days ago, tucked in a waterproof sleeve under a cairn below the burn scar. They'd duplicated it. Page for page, in Reyes's cramped engineer's handwriting, with a note in different ink: *If we don't make it, someone else should have to carry this.*

I'd sat on that rock with the copies in my gloves for a long time. Long enough for my tea to freeze. Because here's the thing nobody tells you about reading evidence of your own employer's crimes: once you've read it, you're holding it. It doesn't stay paper. It gets heavy. It starts asking you questions in your own voice.

I'd signed on to guard property. Water rights, headgates, a few million dollars of irrigation infrastructure that some very cheerful people in Bozeman considered theirs whether the law agreed or not. That was the pitch. Guard property. Nobody said anything about a survey team going into the Sawtooths in October and not coming out while their satellite phone sat dead-but-unbroken in a tent that was still pitched. Nobody said anything about Halloway, write the incident report, cause undetermined, weather event, tragic.

I wrote it anyway. That's the tooth I stopped looking at.

Now here they came, up the last switchback, into the only choke point in twenty miles, where I was waiting with a rifle and a radio and a job description I no longer believed in.

Reyes saw me first. She went rigid, put out one arm—not to shield herself, to stop the others—and I watched her do the math. Five against one, but one of the five couldn't walk, one could barely grip, and the one with the rifle had the high ground and the only exit.

Quintana stepped in front of the litter. Small gesture. Enormous one. You don't put your body between a rifle and a man you're being paid to abandon.

"Quintana," I called down. My voice carried flat in the cold air, the way sound does above the trees. "Don't do anything stupid."

"That's rich," Quintana called back. Even winded, even wrecked, there was that wry edge, like the whole apocalypse was a mildly disappointing storm cell. "Coming from the man who's been trying to kill us since the burn scar."

"If I'd been trying to kill you, you'd know." I stood up slowly, hands away from the rifle slung across my back, and let them see me do it. "I want to see the journal."

Silence. Wind through the notch, that high thin whistle like the mountains breathing through their teeth.

"You've got copies," Reyes said. Her voice was hoarse but steady. "We know you found the cache. We watched your helicopter search pattern yesterday—it flew the cairn line twice."

So much for operational security. I almost smiled. "I've got copies. Copies are paper. Paper burns, gets wet, gets confiscated by people whose whole careers depend on it never having existed. I want to know what the originals say that the copies might not."

"And then?" Quintana. Still planted in front of Vasquez like a fence post. "Then you radio it in and six more of Corliss's boys come up this saddle by morning?"

"And then," I said, "you walk through."

That landed on them like weather. I watched it move across their faces—Reyes's suspicion, Okafor's flat ranger stare, Quintana's flicker of something that wasn't quite trust and wasn't quite nothing.

"Why," Okafor said. Not a question. A demand with a question mark nailed to it.

Because I read forty pages of flow data showing a river being stolen. Because I wrote a false report once and I can't write another. Because I'm fifty-one years old and I signed on to guard property and instead I helped bury five federal surveyors on paper, and the mountains grade honestly, and I am so far behind on my assignments.

What I said was: "Because somebody has to be the first one to stop lying, and it might as well be the guy with the rifle."

Quintana looked at me for a long moment. Then, without a word, Reyes unslung the case, thumbed the latches, and held the journal open against the wind—not handing it over, just letting me see. Pages of measurements. Dates. Coordinates. Photographs printed from a field printer, grainy images of an intake structure built to specifications no permit ever described. And on the last page, in fresh ink, a list. Names. Mine was on it. Under a column headed, in Reyes's careful engineer's print: *Witnesses — status unknown.*

"I've been updating it," she said quietly. "You're the first entry I've been able to close out."

I don't know what my face did. Something, apparently, because Okafor huffed—almost a laugh, the kind that comes out of people when the alternative is crying.

"The pass is watched from the east approach until dark," I said, all business now, because business was the only thing keeping me upright. "Rotating patrol, two-man team, they pull back to the warming hut at 1900 for the shift change. That gives you ninety minutes. Take the north chute off the saddle—it's ugly, loose rock, but it drops you below their sightline and puts you on the east slope by midnight if the litter holds together."

"And you?" Reyes asked.

"I'll report contact." I keyed my radio and let them hear me do it, let them hear the static cough and the channel open. "Contact with hostile elements at the saddle. Engaged briefly. Lost visual due to terrain and deteriorating light. Requesting authorization to continue pursuit at first light." I paused. "By the time they authorize anything, you'll be twelve miles gone, and by tomorrow afternoon the front hits and no aircraft flies and no tracker tracks. I'm buying you a day. Maybe eighteen hours. Spend it well."

Quintana stared at me. "They'll figure it out."

"Yeah," I said. "Eventually. Probably around the time they figure out who leaked the story to the press, which—if you're smart—someone already did." I shrugged, and the rifle shifted on my back, and I watched five exhausted people flinch at the movement and hated myself for the flinch. "Corliss pays me to guard property. Turns out the property I give a damn about is a river."

Reyes closed the case. For a second she just looked at me, and there was something in her face that I recognized from that lecture years ago—not forgiveness, nothing so generous. Recognition. One professional acknowledging another who'd finally read his own instruments.

"Halloway," she said. "When this breaks—and it will break—there'll be hearings. Investigators. People asking questions with lawyers standing behind them." She held my eyes. "Be the man who answers them."

Then they moved. North chute, single file, the litter swaying between Okafor and Quintana like a pendulum counting down. Quintana glanced back once—I raised one hand, not a wave, just an acknowledgment, *I see you, go*—and then the rocks swallowed them, and the saddle was empty except for me and the wind and the lie I was about to tell.

I keyed the radio. "Overwatch, Halloway. Contact at the saddle, negative. Hostiles broke west before my arrival—found cold sign, boot prints heading the drainage. Recommend shifting search assets to the western approaches." A pause on the channel. "Copy, Halloway. Western approaches. Good hunting."

Good hunting. I clicked off and sat back down on the rock where I'd waited, and I looked east, where the sky over the next range had gone the color of a bruise, and I understood—with the calm of a man reading his own obituary and finding it accurate—that I had just spent the only currency I had left. A day. Eighteen hours. Maybe less, if Corliss's people were smarter than I'd built my career on assuming they weren't.

The wind came through the notch and carried the smell of snow that hadn't fallen yet but was already on its way, and I thought about Reyes's list, and my name in the column, and the word she'd written next to it before today: *unknown.*

She'd update it tonight, wherever they holed up. I wondered what she'd write.

I wondered if I'd live long enough to read it.

* * *

Gideon

There's a moment in every storm chase where you commit—where the road forks and you pick the fork that might kill you because the other fork definitely will, and after that the fear stops being useful and turns into fuel. I felt that moment hit at the top of the north chute, with Halloway's voice still hanging in the thin air behind us and Dee hauling the litter over loose scree like the rocks owed her money.

"He let us go," Dee said. She said it the way you'd say *the river flowed uphill.* "He had the high ground, the rifle, the radio, and he let us go."

"People contain multitudes," I said. "Also, I think he contained about forty pages of flow data."

Dee snorted. It was the first sound resembling humor any of us had produced in two days, and it cracked something loose in the group—Vasquez mumbled something from the litter that might have been a laugh or might have been delirium, and even Reyes's shoulders came down half an inch from around her ears.

But I kept looking back. Habit. Storm chasers check their six the way other people check their phones. Behind us, the saddle sat empty against the darkening sky, and beyond it, east, the clouds had organized themselves into that smooth anvil shape I'd spent fifteen years learning to love and fear. The front wasn't coming anymore. It was arriving. And somewhere behind that bruised horizon, men with radios were being told we'd gone west, and every hour they believed it was an hour we spent buying distance.

"North chute drops us into the cirque," Dee said, consulting the terrain the way other people consult scripture. "From there we cut east along the bench above the lake basin. Twelve miles to the tree line on the east slope. If we move through the night."

"We always move through the night," Vasquez croaked from the litter. "It's all we do. Move through the night. I'd like to file a complaint."

"Complaint denied," Reyes said gently. "Keep talking, Miguel. Talking means you're tracking."

He kept talking. Mostly complaints about the litter's suspension, which was fair, because the litter's suspension was two pack frames and a tarp and my increasingly philosophical relationship with gravity. But he tracked. That mattered. Septic frostbite takes the mind second, after it's taken everything else, and Miguel Vasquez was still in there, complaining, which was the most hopeful sound I'd heard in a week.

We dropped into the cirque as the light died. The temperature fell with it—that clear-sky plunge that comes ahead of a front, the atmosphere scrubbing itself clean before it loads up. I checked the sky out of pure reflex and didn't like the math. The anvil was building faster than the models suggested. This wasn't a system that was going to arrive politely tomorrow afternoon. This was a system that was going to kick the door in tonight or early morning, and when it did, the helicopters would be grounded—which was our one advantage—but so would any rescue, any supply drop, any mercy with wings.

One day. Halloway had bought us one day, and the sky was going to eat most of it.

At the bench above the lake basin, we stopped for ten minutes—water, calories, a rewrap of Miguel's foot that made him go quiet in a way that scared me more than screaming would. Reyes worked on him with hands that shook from cold and something else, and I crouched next to Dee at the edge of the bench, looking back the way we'd come.

"Do you believe him?" Dee asked.

I thought about it. About the way Halloway had stood up slow with his hands visible, a man performing the one act harder than betrayal: un-betraying. About the list in the journal, his name under *status unknown*, and how he'd flinched when his rifle shifted.

"I believe he meant it," I said. "Whether he survives meaning it is a different question."

Dee was quiet a moment. "Marisol's cache is two valleys east," she said. "Past the tree line. We'll hit it tomorrow."

"I know."

"Gideon." She turned and looked at me, and in the last gray light her face was all sharp planes and exhaustion. "If Vane's message says what we both think it's going to say—"

"Then I'll read it," I said. "And then I'll decide who I am. Same as Halloway did. Same as everybody does, eventually. The mountain just makes the decision happen faster."

Below us, the lake basin filled with blue dusk. Above us, the first flakes came down—not the storm yet, just its scouts, drifting through the headlamp beams like ash falling upward. Somewhere behind us, a man sat alone on a saddle telling lies into a radio to save people he'd been paid to destroy, and somewhere ahead of us, a woman who owned my debt was waiting to find out if I'd sell the truth or carry it.

The scouts thickened. I shouldered my end of the litter.

"One day," I said. "Let's not waste it standing around admiring the view."

And we moved east, into the falling dark and the falling snow, while behind us the mountains quietly closed the door.
