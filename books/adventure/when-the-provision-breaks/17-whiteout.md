## 17. Whiteout

The storm didn't arrive. It *arrived*—the way a debt collector arrives, the way Marisol Vane arrives, all smiles and arithmetic, already inside your house before you hear the door.

I'd been reading the sky since I was nineteen years old, and I want to be clear about something: I love weather. Not in the way people say they love sunsets. I love it the way you love a dangerous animal that has never once bitten you, mostly because you've learned to read its teeth. And what was coming over the western crest on the morning of our fourth day above the tree line had very large teeth and absolutely no interest in being read.

"Front's accelerated," I said to Dee, watching the lenticular stack build over the peaks like a cairn some giant was too lazy to finish. "It's going to hit us six hours early. Maybe four."

Dee didn't look up from re-wrapping Tomás's feet. Our injured survivor—the one whose frostbite had gone septic somewhere around chapter fifteen of this nightmare, though he didn't know he was living in a book—had stopped complaining, which is the worst thing a hypothermic, septic man can do. Complaint means fight. Silence means the body has started closing accounts.

"How bad?" Dee asked.

"On a scale of one to ten? It's a Gideon."

"That's not a scale."

"It's the only honest one." I checked the barometer on my watch, which had been falling like my credit score. "Wind's going to go from twenty knots to eighty out of the southwest. Visibility goes to zero. Not 'bad visibility.' Zero. The kind of zero where you can't tell up from down, where your inner ear starts lying to you, where people walk off cliffs they're standing next to."

Reyes looked up from the data case she hadn't let go of in three days. Ana Reyes, team lead, keeper of the river's testimony, a woman who had chosen to vanish rather than hand her life's work to any institution Corliss money had touched—and by day four of our escape, that list included basically everything with a letterhead. Her face was wind-scoured and her eyes were the calmest things on the mountain.

"Then we move now," she said. "Before it hits."

"We can't outrun it," Dee said. "Not with five. Not with Tomás on the litter."

"We don't outrun it," Reyes said. "We hide from it until it passes, then we move behind it."

I looked at her. She looked at me. And I understood we were both doing the same math with different numbers: she was counting pages of data, and I was counting hours of Tomás's circulation, and the storm was counting neither because storms don't count. Storms just take.

"There's no shelter between here and the saddle," Dee said. "Nothing rated for eighty knots."

"There's the cornice shelf below Point 9,400," I said, and immediately hated myself for saying it, because saying it made it real.

Dee turned slowly. "The cornice shelf."

"The lee side. It'll cut the wind to maybe thirty. Snow cave against the cornice base—"

"Gideon. Cornices collapse. That's their whole personality. That's what they *do*."

"Yes," I said. "But they collapse when they're loaded, and they load from the windward side, and if we dig in at the base on the lee—" I heard myself and stopped. "Look. Every option today is a bad option wearing a different coat. This one's just the coat with the fewest holes."

Tomás made a sound. Not words. A small, wet sound, like a man arguing with the dark from underneath it. His partner, Okonkwo—the third survivor, quiet as a held breath—put a hand on his shoulder and said nothing, which somehow said everything.

We moved.

* * *

Digging a snow cave into the base of a cornice while the temperature drops thirty degrees is an experience I'd describe as "character-building" only to someone I disliked intensely. We worked in shifts, Dee and me, because Reyes refused to stop carrying the data case and Okonkwo wouldn't leave Tomás, and so the two of us carved a hole in a mountain's overhanging frown while the sky went the color of an old bruise.

By the time we got everyone inside, the leading edge had arrived. Not snow yet—wind first, a freight-train hiss across the crest, and then the world began deleting itself. First the far ridges. Then the near ones. Then the middle distance. Then anything more than ten feet away, erased like a chalkboard under a sleeve.

Inside the cave, lit by one candle stub Dee had been hoarding like a dragon, we were five people in a womb of blue ice, listening to the mountain scream.

Tomás was worse. His right foot had gone from mottled to black past the ankle, and the red streaks climbing his shin had reached the knee. He was shivering less, which Dee kept checking with a hand on his sternum and a look on her face I recognized from my own mirror during debt season: the look of someone doing arithmetic they don't want to finish.

"He needs antibiotics," she said quietly, away from him. "Real ones. And a surgeon. He needs to be off this mountain in twelve hours or the sepsis makes the decision for us."

"The storm gives us twelve hours?"

"The storm gives us nothing. The storm doesn't know we exist." She glanced back at the candle, at the others. "That's the part people never get about weather. It's not malicious. Malice would mean it noticed you."

I thought about Halloway, somewhere behind us in this white roar, and wondered if he was warm. I wondered if he'd found our tracks before the storm ate them. I wondered which of us the mountain would notice first.

We slept in shifts. Or rather, we lay still in shifts, eyes closed, bodies clenched, while the cornice above us groaned like a ship in heavy seas. Around what I estimated was two in the morning, I woke to Reyes shaking my shoulder, and her face in the candlelight told me everything before her mouth did.

"It's moving," she whispered. "The cornice. Listen."

I listened. Under the wind there was a new sound—a deep, structural creak, the sound of several tons of overhanging snow reconsidering its relationship with gravity. And beneath that, fainter, a crack. Then another. Sharp, percussive, like ice giving applause.

"Everybody out," I said. "Now. Quietly. No lights."

Okonkwo got Tomás up without waking him fully, which is a skill I hope never to need explained to me. We crawled out into the storm's full attention—eighty knots, horizontal snow, a darkness with texture—and I had exactly enough time to think "the litter, get clear of the fall line" before the mountain answered the question of whether the cornice would hold.

It didn't.

The collapse wasn't loud so much as total—a white avalanche of roof-sized slabs pouring down the slope we'd been sleeping beneath, burying our cave, our gear cache, and everything within forty feet of it. The blast of displaced air hit us like a door slammed by God. I grabbed Reyes by the pack because she'd turned toward the case—toward the data, always toward the data—and hauled her downslope as the slide thundered past close enough to powder our faces.

When it stopped, the silence afterward was enormous. The kind of silence that has weight.

"The case," Reyes said. Her voice was steady. Her hands, gripping my jacket, were not. "Gideon. The case."

I looked uphill. Where the cave entrance had been there was now a smooth white rampart, featureless, indistinguishable from every other featureless white thing in a world that had forgotten how to show us edges.

"It's buried," Dee said. "All of it. Spare rope, the stove, the med kit—"

"Not the med kit," Okonkwo said. He was holding it. Of course he was holding it. Some men carry grief; Okonkwo carried antibiotics.

"The data," Reyes said again, and there it was—not panic, but something worse: the specific terror of a person watching years of work become geology. Three summers of flow measurements. Proof of a river being bled dry by men in suits who'd never once been cold. Buried under a cornice in the Sawtooths, possibly forever.

And then I remembered, and the memory arrived with the particular shame of good news remembered late.

"Your copies," I said. "The carbon duplicates. You sewed them into your parka liner in chapter thirteen—"

"I don't know what chapters are, Gideon."

"—you sewed them into your parka liner. When we argued at the cold camp. You said 'paper survives what satellites don't' and you spent two hours with fishing line and a sewing needle while I pretended to sleep."

She stared at me. Then her hands went to her own jacket, feeling along the seams, and her whole body changed—shoulders dropping an inch, breath coming back.

"They're damp," she said. "But they're here."

"Good," I said, and sat down in the snow, because my legs had filed a formal complaint. "Because we just lost the stove, most of the rope, and the tent fly, and I needed one piece of good news before I do the next stupid thing."

"Which is?" Dee asked.

I stood up. The wind hit me like a bill collector.

"We move. Now. In this."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about navigating in a whiteout: you cannot. Not with a map—you can't read a map with frozen hands in eighty-knot wind. Not with a compass—you can't hold a compass level when the wind is trying to take your arm home with it. Not with GPS, because ours died with the cache, and even if it hadn't, the screen would be a coin of ice in ninety seconds.

There is only one instrument left, and it's the one I've trained my whole adult life to trust.

The wind itself.

Every storm has structure. It looks like chaos, but it's architecture. On this range, with this front, the prevailing flow comes over the crest from the southwest, gets funneled through the col west of Point 9,400, and spills down the east-facing drainage in a series of predictable eddies—gust, lull, crosswind, gust. I've chased systems like this across half of Tornado Alley. I've stood in the inflow of supercells and felt the rotation before I saw it. The wind isn't noise. The wind is a sentence, and if you've spent enough years listening, you can read it.

So I stood at the edge of our little disaster camp, closed my eyes—which was redundant, since open eyes showed me nothing—and listened.

Gust from my left cheek. Lull. Crosswind brushing my right ear, quartering from behind. Gust left. Lull. Left again, harder—that was the col funneling, which meant the col was to my left-front, which meant the drainage ran to my right-front, which meant—

"East-southeast," I said. "Down the drainage. Thirty degrees off the fall line to stay out of the slide paths."

"You can't know that," Dee said. "You're guessing."

"I'm reading. There's a difference. Guessing is what you do when you have no data. I'm standing in the data."

"And if you're wrong?"

"Then we find out the traditional way."

She looked at me for a long moment through the streaming white, and whatever she saw in my face must have passed inspection, because she nodded once and started rigging the rope we had left—one length, forty feet, five people, Tomás on the litter dragged behind Okonkwo like the world's saddest toboggan.

I went first.

I want to be honest about what those hours were. They were not heroic. They were not cinematic. They were a man walking blind into screaming white, counting seconds between gusts, checking the wind against his cheekbones like a man reading braille with his face, calling course corrections to the rope behind him in a voice the storm kept trying to steal. Two hundred steps, check bearing. Two hundred steps, feel for the grade change that meant the drainage bend. Once I lost the wind pattern entirely—for maybe four minutes, in some rotor of turbulence off a hidden spur—and I stood in the annihilating white with my heart going like a trapped bird, knowing that to my left was a slope that avalanched once tonight already, and to my right was terrain I couldn't see and couldn't name, and that standing still was dying slowly and moving was dying quickly, and I chose moving, because I am, whatever else is true, a man who moves.

Then the pattern came back. Gust left. Lull. Crosswind right. The sentence resumed mid-word, and I could have wept.

Behind me: the drag-hiss of the litter. Reyes's breathing. Dee's voice at intervals, low and steady, feeding me the count, keeping me tethered to the human world when the white one tried to dissolve me into it. At some point Tomás stopped making sounds at all, and Okonkwo's voice said, flat and terrible, "He's breathing," unprompted, twice, like a man answering a question no one dared ask aloud.

I don't know how long we walked. Clocks are meaningless in a whiteout; time goes soft and local, measured in steps and gusts. Four hours. Maybe five. My face froze in a permanent squint. My hands operated at the end of long delays, like signals from a distant planet.

And then, out of nothing, the wind dropped.

Not gradually—all at once, like a held note released. The storm's core had passed its apex and the back side was rolling over us, and in the strange relative calm of the eye's trailing edge, the clouds tore open for perhaps ninety seconds, and I saw it:

Moonlight on a broad white slope, falling away eastward. And below us, maybe six hundred vertical feet down, threaded through the trees like a dropped ribbon, the frozen gleam of Miller Creek.

Which meant the east fork. Which meant we'd made the drainage. Which meant Dee's high route had worked, and my wind-reading had worked, and we were—by the cruelest margin imaginable—alive, and roughly where I'd aimed.

"Ninety seconds," I shouted. "Everyone memorize! Trees at two hundred yards, creek beyond, saddle after!"

The clouds closed. The white came back. But it was a different white now—the white of almost-through, the white of a storm spending its last fury on a mountain that had already decided to let us go.

We descended toward the trees. And that's when Dee, checking Tomás on the litter, went rigid.

"Gideon." Her voice had gone very quiet, which from Dee is louder than shouting. "His fever. Feel his neck."

I felt it. Burning. The sepsis wasn't waiting twelve hours. It wasn't even waiting six.

Below us, through a tear in the clouds, I caught a flicker of light in the valley—small, orange, deliberate. Not lightning. Not a cabin window.

A fire. Someone was burning a signal fire in the valley floor, at the mouth of the east fork, in the exact path we were descending.

Either someone knew we were coming—or someone wanted us to believe they did.
