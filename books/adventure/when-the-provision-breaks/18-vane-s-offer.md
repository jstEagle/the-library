## 18. Vane's Offer

The cache was where Dee said it would be, which was the first thing that should have worried me.

"Old fire-crew supply point," she'd explained the night before, tracing it on her battered topo with a cracked thumbnail. "Rangers used to stage fuel and food up here for backcountry patrols. Nobody's maintained them since the budget cuts, but the boxes survive. Steel, gasketed, bear-proof. Better than any safe in a bank."

We'd come down off the crest at first light, five of us moving like a funeral procession—Reyes out front on legs that had forgotten how to trust ice, the frostbite case (his name was Okonkwo; I'd learned it somewhere around eleven thousand feet and it had felt important to learn it) riding the litter Dee and I had lashed together from tent poles and a shredded fly. The other two survivors, Vasquez and Lindqvist, flanked him without being asked. They'd been doing that since the second camp, taking shifts, guarding their own like the mountain hadn't already tried to take them twice.

I hung back with the pack that held the journal, and every step I took, I could feel it against my spine like a loaded thing. Not heavy. It weighed maybe two pounds. But weight and mass are different animals, and some things have gravity all their own.

The cache sat in a stand of whitebark pine below a granite shoulder, three steel drums half-sunk in duff, lids rusted the color of old blood. Dee popped the first one with her pry bar and pulled out a plastic ammo can, dry as a sermon inside, and taped to the lid of the ammo can was an envelope.

A real envelope. Paper. A wax seal, because of course there was a wax seal, because Marisol Vane had never met a flourish she didn't want to sleep with.

Dee looked at it, then at me, and something went flat and careful in her face. "You didn't arrange this."

"No," I said. "I didn't."

"Then how does she know where you'd be?"

That was the question, wasn't it. I turned the envelope over. My name across the front in Marisol's looping hand, *Gideon, querido*, underlined twice. The paper was good stock. It had been typed, not handwritten, except for the salutation and the signature, because Marisol believed in efficiency the way other people believed in God—fervently, and only when it paid.

I opened it standing there in the cold with four strangers and one friend watching me, and I read it twice, because the first time through I kept snagging on the same sentence like a boot heel on rebar.

*Gideon, querido —*

*I hope this finds you well and ahead of the weather, though knowing you, you're probably behind it and loving every minute. You always did have a genius for being exactly where the trouble is.*

*A small correction to our arrangement: the contract you signed was never about the equipment. The government gear is scrap—the insurance valuation was inflated because inflated valuations make people sign contracts they shouldn't, and you did sign, which I appreciate more than you know. What my client actually wanted was located, and you've located it. The field journal. Every page, please, including whatever copies your charming ranger friend has made.*

*Here is my offer, and I'll only make it once: deliver the journal to the cache coordinates below by tomorrow sundown, and your debt is cleared. All of it. Every dollar, every cent of interest, the whole ugly arithmetic we did over pancakes. Gone. Forgiven. Consider it a professional courtesy between optimists.*

*You're wondering how I knew where you'd be. Gideon. Sweetheart. I financed the truck. I financed the satellite messenger. I know the route options out of that range better than you do, because I helped write the salvage plan that listed them. You've been walking down a road I paved since the day you shook my hand. That's not a betrayal—that's just what roads are for.*

*One more thing, because I like you and I want you to live: Corliss is my biggest client. Has been for six years. Their security chief is competent and their patience is not infinite. If the journal reaches a newspaper, I lose a client, you lose a friend of mine who makes problems disappear, and I lose the pleasure of ever doing business with you again. If the journal comes to me, everyone walks away rich in the ways that matter.*

*Don't be sentimental. Sentimentality is just arithmetic you refuse to do.*

*All my love,*
*M.V.*

*P.S. — The pancakes really were excellent that morning, weren't they?*

I read it twice. Then I read the P.S. a third time, because somehow that was the line that landed, the little knife tucked into the silk. She'd remembered the pancakes. Of course she had. Marisol Vane remembered everything, filed everything, cross-indexed everything, and somewhere in that cheerful ledger of hers my humiliation over a plate of diner pancakes had been logged as leverage, dated, and held for exactly this moment.

"Gideon." Dee's voice came from far away, then close. "What is it?"

I handed her the letter. Watched her read it. Watched her jaw do the thing it does when she's swallowing anger whole so it doesn't come back up as something worse.

"The cache," she said finally. "She gave you cache coordinates."

"There are old ranger caches all along the east side," I said. "She didn't need to guess. She needed me to find one, and she knew I'd find this one, because—" I stopped. Because Dee had known about it. Because Dee had led us here. And Dee had gone pale in a way that had nothing to do with altitude.

"I told you about this cache," she said slowly. "Back at the burn scar. I said if we needed supplies—"

"You mentioned it once. One time. To me." I looked at the envelope. At the wax seal. At the neat little coordinates printed at the bottom in the same looping hand. "And either she's got ears we haven't found, or—"

"Or she knows me," Dee said quietly. "Or she knows my file. I reported Corliss once, Gideon. Somebody buried that report. You don't bury a report without knowing who wrote it. Maybe somebody kept the file. Maybe somebody's been keeping tabs on a disgraced ranger who knows every abandoned cache in these mountains, waiting for the day a debtor with a truck needs a guide." Her laugh had no humor in it anywhere. "Maybe I wasn't recruited. Maybe I was placed."

"That's not true," Reyes said. It was the first thing she'd said in an hour. She'd come up beside us silent as snowfall, and now she stood looking at the letter over Dee's shoulder with eyes that had gone very still. "I've spent eight months hiding from these people. I know how they work. They don't place pieces and wait—they buy what's in front of them. If Vane wanted a guide, she bought one when she saw one. That's not the same as you being a trap." She looked at me. "But it doesn't matter right now whether it's a trap or a coincidence. What matters is what the letter says. Your lender works for Corliss."

"My lender *is* Corliss," I said. "Or Corliss is her. Six years of biggest client. The salvage contract. The inflated valuation. The route plans." I heard my own voice go flat and strange, the way it does when I'm reading instruments during a meso and the numbers stop making sense. "I thought I was digging myself out. I was the shovel. They handed me the handle and called it opportunity."

Okonkwo made a sound from the litter—not words, just a low noise of pain and cold—and it cut through all of it, the way pain does. Vasquez was checking his dressings, lips moving in a count. We didn't have time for this. We had never had time for any of it; we'd just been spending hours we didn't have, and now the bill was due in a currency I understood too well.

"So what's the play?" Dee asked. "Because I've run this both ways. We give her the journal, debt's gone, we walk into town clean, and Corliss buries eight months of data and three witnesses who conveniently froze to death in the high country. Or we keep it, and we come off this mountain owing Marisol Vane more than money, and she collects debts in ways I've read about in her file."

"We keep it," Reyes said.

"I wasn't asking you," Dee said.

"I know," Reyes said. "I'm telling you anyway. I watched a man from the district office take a bribe with one hand and shake mine with the other. I watched my report get buried. I chose to disappear rather than hand my data to anyone with a logo, because every channel was owned. And I will tell you that the moment you start doing arithmetic with the truth—just a little, just this once, just to survive—is the moment you're doing their arithmetic forever. There's no version of handing it over where you get to keep your hands clean. There's no version where it stops there." She crouched, stiff with cold, and put her frost-nipped fingers on the ammo can lid. "They hunted us for eight months because of what's in this box. Don't let it be for nothing."

Nobody said anything. The wind moved through the whitebarks with that sound they make, like the trees themselves are skeptical.

Here's the thing about me. I chase storms. People think that means I love destruction, and I don't—I love the *honesty*. A supercell doesn't pretend to be anything. It doesn't send you a letter on nice paper with a wax seal. It doesn't call itself opportunity. It shows you exactly what it is, wall to wall, and dares you to respect it. I have spent my whole adult life trusting systems that tell the truth about how dangerous they are, and I have been broke, humiliated, and nearly killed by systems that lie beautifully.

Marisol Vane lied beautifully. Pancakes. *Querido.* Sentimentality is just arithmetic you refuse to do.

Except she was wrong about that, and she was wrong in the specific way that broke people are always wrong. Because I *had* done the arithmetic. Both columns. The journal was worth exactly one debt forgiven, and the debt was worth maybe sixty thousand dollars against a life I'd already nearly lost twice, and on the other side of the ledger was a river running full, and a woman named Reyes who'd hidden in the ground for eight months rather than let the numbers win, and a man named Okonkwo who was losing toes right now because principle is slow and sepsis is fast, and a security chief who'd read enough pages to let us pass a saddle he could have closed like a fist.

You couldn't price that column. But you couldn't ignore it either. Some liabilities don't show up on balance sheets. They show up later, in mirrors, at three in the morning, forever.

"Dee," I said. "Give me your knife."

She handed it over without question, and I took Marisol's beautiful letter, and I fed it into the small spirit stove flame we were using to warm water for Okonkwo's dressings. It caught reluctantly—good stock, treated paper—and I held it until the wax seal bubbled and the looping handwriting curled black, and then I dropped the last corner into the snow and pressed it out with my boot.

"That was a bad idea," Dee said. "Now she knows we read it and refused."

"She already knows. That's why there was a deadline. Sundown tomorrow." I looked west, where the sky over the crest had gone the color of a bruise deciding whether to bloom. The front was early. It was going to be much earlier than Marisol's arithmetic predicted, because Marisol did her math in offices. "Which means she expects an answer by then, and when she doesn't get one, she tells Halloway—or whoever's left of Halloway's crew—exactly where we're standing. This cache was the trap, Dee. Not you. The letter was the net closing, and the deadline is the drawstring."

Reyes straightened. "Then we aren't standing here at sundown."

"No," I agreed. "We aren't." I shouldered the pack with the journal in it, and it weighed its two pounds, and it weighed considerably more than that. "East slope. Tonight, ahead of the front. If the storm hits before we're down, it hits while we're moving, because a moving target in a whiteout beats a stationary one in the open, and I'd rather bet our lives on weather I can read than on mercy from people who wax their letters."

Lindqvist, who hadn't spoken in two days, said something in Swedish that Vasquez translated as "she says the tall one finally makes sense."

"Tell her the tall one is terrified," I said.

Vasquez considered. "I'll tell her you said *determined*. It translates better."

We broke camp in nine minutes. As we climbed out of the whitebark stand onto the east-facing slope, I looked back once at the steel drums, at the little black ghost of ash in the snow where Marisol's offer had died, and I thought about her face when she'd recalculated my debt over pancakes—cheerful, warm, generous, a woman delighted to help, a dangerous optimist. I'd thought the danger was the money.

It was never the money. The money was just the hook. The danger was a person who could look at a drowning stranger and see, clearly and without malice, a delivery service.

The first flakes came down while we were still above tree line, fat and slow and wrong, the kind of snow that means the atmosphere has stopped negotiating. Behind us, somewhere over the crest, I heard it—a sound I knew better than my own heartbeat, the deep freight-train mutter of a front arriving ahead of schedule.

Ahead of *my* schedule. Which meant Marisol's sundown deadline was already dead, and dead deadlines make desperate people reach for radios.

I turned east, into the falling white, and started down.

* * *

The descent lasted forty minutes before the world ended.

Not dramatically. That's the thing nobody tells you about whiteouts—they don't arrive like a wall, they arrive like a forgetting. First the horizon goes, then the slope texture, then the distinction between sky and ground, until you're moving through a bowl of milk and the only direction left is the one your body insists on. I'd been in a hundred storms. I'd driven toward them on purpose for a decade. And I will tell you honestly that walking into this one with four exhausted survivors and a dying man on a litter was the closest I have ever come to understanding what fear actually is, which is not a feeling. It's a calculation with no good inputs.

So I gave it inputs.

"Wind's northwest," I shouted over it, to Dee, pressed close so the words didn't shred. "Steady. Feel it on your left cheek—keep your left cheek to it and we hold the fall line. The slope steepens to the south, rock bands under the snow. North side benches out into timber by four thousand feet. We stay north of the drift line and we walk the wind home."

"And if the wind shifts?" Dee shouted back.

"It won't yet. Front's still maturing. We've got maybe two hours of honest northwest flow before the backing starts." I grinned at her, and I knew what the grin looked like, because she'd seen it before and flinched. "This is the part I'm good at, Dee. Let me be good at it."

Something in her face eased—one degree, maybe two. Enough.

We moved. Okonkwo rode the litter with Vasquez and Lindqvist hauling and Reyes navigating by the data case strapped to her chest like a sternum pack, hunched over it the way you'd hunch over a child in the rain. The snow thickened. The temperature fell through freezing like it had a grudge. Twice the slope tried to hand us to the rock bands and twice I called the correction off the wind on my cheek, off the grain of the loading snow, off ten thousand hours of loving weather that tells the truth.

And then, through the roar, Dee grabbed my arm and pointed up-slope, back the way we'd come, and I turned and saw nothing—white on white on white—and then the nothing resolved, briefly, horribly, into shapes. Three of them. Distant. Upright. Moving with the unhurried competence of people who are paid to be outside.

They couldn't see us. In this, nothing could see anything. But they were *there*, between us and the cache, sweeping the drawstring shut on a net whose deadline had already burned, and somewhere behind them a woman who remembered my pancakes was learning that I'd said no.

The wind backed a degree. Just one. Just enough.

"Left cheek," I said to Dee, calm as I've ever been in my life. "Hold the line. And don't stop walking for anything, because in about twenty minutes, the only proof any of us were ever on this mountain is going to be the tracks filling in behind us—and I intend for there to be nothing left to follow."

Below us, faint through the storm, almost imagined: timber. The east slope. The last descent.

I put my left cheek to the wind and led us down into it.
