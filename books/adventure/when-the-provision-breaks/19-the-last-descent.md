## 19. The Last Descent

The east slope of the Sawtooths doesn't descend so much as it commits to a decision. You stand at the crest and look down and the mountain has already made up its mind about you. Four thousand feet of scree, timber, and frozen waterfall, all of it angled like a question with only one honest answer.

I'd been down that answer twice before in my life, once on foot and once on a stretcher, and I remembered both trips with the kind of clarity you reserve for things that nearly killed you.

"Tell me it gets gentler," Gideon said beside me. They had their binoculars up, sweeping the saddle behind us where Halloway's staged ambush had bought us—by my count—about nineteen hours of borrowed time.

"It doesn't."

"Tell me something else, then."

"The snowpack's stable until the sun hits it. After that, everything slides." I shouldered my pack and looked back at our little procession, because that's what we were now, a procession, five souls strung out on a ridgeline like laundry nobody meant to leave out overnight. "We move before it moves us."

Ana Reyes was already tightening the straps on the litter. She hadn't slept more than an hour at a stretch since the second camp, and she wore her exhaustion the way some people wear authority—as if daring anyone to comment. The data case rode against her chest in a harness she'd rigged from pack straps and stubbornness. Every page. That was the deal she'd made with herself somewhere in those months of hiding, and she wasn't renegotiating it now, not for weather, not for pursuit, not for the man dying by inches on the sled between us.

Marcus Voss was that man. Frostbite in both feet, septic by the time we found them, feverish for two days, lucid for maybe twenty minutes this morning when he'd gripped Gideon's wrist and asked—not demanded, asked—if we could make sure his daughter got the photographs from the third cache. Not the journal. Not the evidence. Photographs of rock formations he'd taken for no reason except that they were beautiful. I've carried a lot of things down mountains. That request sat heavier than any of them.

The other two survivors—Priya and Okonkwo—had gone quiet in the way people go quiet when they've stopped believing words will help. Priya walked point behind me because she refused to be last, and I understood that completely. Okonkwo took the rear with the satellite phone strapped to his chest like a talisman, checking it every ten minutes even though we both knew what it would tell him: nothing, nothing, nothing.

Gideon crouched by the litter runners one last time. They'd built it from tent poles, a torn groundsheet, and two ice axes lashed crosswise, and it looked exactly like what it was—a thing desperate people made from the wreckage of other people's plans. But Gideon had waxed the runners at dawn with candle stub, and when they tested it on a patch of crust, it hissed forward smooth as a lie.

"Okay," Gideon said, standing. "Okay. Everybody drink. Everybody eat half a bar. We don't stop again until timberline."

Nobody argued. There's a particular silence that falls over a group that has passed through exhaustion into something past it—a flat country where the body runs on momentum and the mind stops offering opinions. We'd crossed into it sometime around four that morning, and I remember thinking, as I chewed a frozen energy bar that tasted like drywall, that this was the state I'd been searching for my whole adult life without knowing it. No fear left. No hope either, not really. Just the next step, and the step after that, and the strange clean peace of having nothing in reserve.

We dropped off the crest at 7:40 in the morning, into the shadow of the east face, and the mountain began its argument.

* * *

The first hour was scree—loose stone the size of dinner plates, shifting under every boot like the slope itself was trying to shrug us off. We went down it in a traverse, five points of contact for the litter team, Gideon and me on the downhill side taking Marcus's weight in turns while Reyes fed rope through her gloved hands and called the footing ahead. Her voice never rose. That was the thing about Ana Reyes I came to respect most: she rationed her volume like water, and she never spent more than a situation required.

At the second hour the scree gave way to old avalanche debris, house-sized blocks of granite furred with rime, and we threaded between them single file while the litter scraped and jumped. Marcus moaned once, low, and then went silent again, and the silence was worse. I checked his pulse at a rest stop that lasted ninety seconds. Fast, thready, but there. His feet were the color of old bruises above the improvised dressings, and below them they were something I made myself look at anyway, because a ranger who can't look stops being useful.

"How is he?" Reyes asked. She already knew. She just needed to hear it said aloud so she could put it away somewhere and keep moving.

"He needs a hospital today," I said. "Not tonight. Today."

"Then we get him to one today."

That was the whole conversation. I've had longer discussions about lunch.

It was somewhere in the third hour, dropping through a band of krummholz—those twisted, wind-flattened pines that grow sideways out of spite—that Okonkwo caught up to me and touched my shoulder. He didn't say anything. He pointed back up-slope, toward the notch we'd descended through, and I raised the binoculars.

Movement. Small, at this distance, the way ants are small until you realize they're carrying your picnic away. Six figures, maybe seven, working along the ridgeline where our tracks stitched a bright seam across the fresh snow. Behind them, further back, a snowmachine whine drifted down on the wind, thin and mechanical, like a mosquito that had learned ambition.

"They didn't take the bait," I said.

Gideon appeared at my elbow, breathing hard, cheeks wind-scoured raw. "Halloway's report buys a day, not a pardon. Somebody upstairs wanted confirmation." They studied the figures a long moment. "Ground team. No helo—the storm front's eating the visibility below eight thousand. They know it too. That's why they're on foot."

"So we race them down a mountain," Priya said, coming up beside us. She'd been listening without appearing to listen, which seemed to be her primary skill after three months of hiding. "In the dark. With a stretcher."

"With a litter," Gideon corrected, "and yes."

"I want it noted," Priya said, "that I objected."

"Noted," Reyes said, already turning downhill. "Filed. Move."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about being pursued: the fear isn't loud. It's a metronome. It ticks under everything—under the crunch of boots, under the rasp of breath, under Marcus's shallow breathing—and it changes the arithmetic of every decision. A rest stop that would be sensible becomes a luxury. A route that would be prudent becomes slow. You start spending the future to buy the present, and the exchange rate is terrible.

I knew these slopes. That was the one card we held that they didn't, unless Halloway had walked them too—and knowing Halloway, he probably had. But knowing a mountain and racing down one are different sports. I picked our line by feel and memory: the drainage that looked impassable but funneled into a game trail; the bench of timber where the canopy would break up our silhouette from above; the frozen waterfall I'd climbed once in summer and now planned to bypass via a goat path so narrow that Priya laughed at it, one short sharp bark of a laugh with no humor anywhere in it.

"That's not a path," she said.

"It's a suggestion," I said. "The mountain makes the final call."

We sent the empty packs down first, then the litter, lowered on ropes with Gideon riding brake. It took forty minutes to move fifty vertical feet. Below us the valley opened up in stages—first the burn scar from the fire two summers back, silver and skeletal, then the dark river of conifer timber, then, impossibly far away, a smudge of gold that might have been cottonwood along the east fork, which meant road, which meant truck, which meant everything.

"Signal," Okonkwo said suddenly.

I turned. He was holding the satellite phone out flat on his palm like an offering, staring at it. In the gap between ridgelines, in some fluke of geometry, the thing had found a sliver of sky. One bar. Maybe.

He dialed from memory—the number of a fish-and-game friend, the only name he still trusted—and pressed send, and we all stood there in the wind watching a phone the way you'd watch a lit fuse. It rang. Once. Twice. Voicemail. He left thirty seconds of coordinates and a sentence—"Five survivors, one critical, descending the east fork, we are NOT lost, we are being FOLLOWED"—and then the bar died and the phone went dark and we stood in the silence after, each of us privately doing the math on whether a voicemail counted as a plan.

"It counts," Gideon said, reading my face. "It counts. Come on."

* * *

The light started going around four, and with it the temperature fell off a cliff. The storm system Gideon had been reading for two days—the one they'd ridden like a surfer on the high route, using its leading edge to scrub our tracks—was arriving in earnest now, and the first flakes came down fat and unhurried, almost polite, the way a fist is polite right before it isn't.

We were in the timber by then, good going, the litter sliding on the duff between the trees, and for twenty minutes I let myself believe the descent was ours. Then the snowmachine sound came back, closer, and this time it didn't fade.

They'd found the drainage. Of course they had—it was the only sane line down the east face, and desperate men read terrain the same way rangers do. Headlamps bloomed in the timber above us, three of them, maybe four, bobbing through the trees like will-o'-the-wisps with a budget.

"Off the trail," I said. "Now. Into the deadfall."

We hauled the litter sideways into a tangle of blowdown, old fire-killed lodgepole stacked like pickup sticks, and killed our lights and stood there breathing steam in the dark while the pursuit sounds grew. Marcus was shivering badly now, the bad kind, the systemic kind, and Priya wrapped herself around him under the space blanket and became, essentially, a furnace with opinions.

The headlamps passed maybe two hundred yards upslope. Voices carried on the cold air—snatches, clipped and professional. Then one voice, louder, angry, giving orders that were not professional at all, and I heard the word *journal* crack across the timber like a whip, and I felt rather than saw Reyes go rigid beside me.

"They think we have it all," she whispered.

"We do have it all."

"They think it's worth killing for."

"It is," Gideon said quietly. "That's the whole problem with the truth. It appreciates."

The lights swung away, downslope, cutting for the trail below us—they'd guessed we were ahead of them and were racing to cut the angle. Which meant, if I read it right, that for the next hour we were between them and nothing, free to move, except that the only direction to move was straight down through terrain they'd be waiting at the bottom of.

"There's another way," I heard myself say. Everyone turned. Even in the dark I could feel the weight of their attention. "The east fork splits below the burn scar. Left fork goes to the road—open ground, easy travel, exactly where they'll set up. Right fork drops into a box canyon. Steep, ugly, no exit except a boulder field at the bottom that connects to the old mining road. Nobody guards the box canyon because nobody's stupid enough to use it."

"But?" Reyes said.

"But it's a half-mile of forty-degree snow gully with a cornice overhang, and we'd be doing it in the dark, in a storm, with a stretcher." I paused. "And I haven't done it since I was twenty-six and immortal."

Silence. Snow ticking on branches. Somewhere below, faintly, an engine idling—waiting for us at the easy fork like a spider who'd read the field guide.

Gideon was looking at the sky. I watched them do it—read the wind, taste the air, run whatever private instrument panel they kept behind their eyes. Storm chasers are the only people I've ever met who look at a killer storm the way a musician looks at a difficult score: not with dread but with recognition.

"The cornice loads tonight," Gideon said finally. "By morning it's a death trap. By afternoon it comes down on its own." A beat. "Which means tonight is the only night it's merely dangerous instead of suicidal. Dee. Can you find it blind?"

"I could find it asleep," I said, which was a lie, but the useful kind.

"Then we go down the stupid route," Gideon said, "because stupid is the one thing they won't expect, and it's the one thing we're qualified for."

* * *

The box canyon gully was exactly as bad as I'd promised and worse than I'd remembered. We roped up in a team of five with the litter slung center, and we went down it in the dark the way you go down anything impossible—one placement at a time, one breath at a time, the storm pressing in until the world was the width of a headlamp beam and nothing else existed.

Halfway down, the cornice above us groaned—a deep structural sound, the mountain clearing its throat—and everyone froze except Gideon, who did the bravest thing I saw anyone do on that entire mountain, which was nothing at all. They stood in the falling snow and listened, counting seconds, reading the settling, and then said, very calmly, "It's done talking. Move."

We moved.

Near the bottom, Marcus woke—really woke, lucid, burning with fever—and asked where we were, and Priya told him the truth, and he said, "Good. Good," in a voice like a man hearing that the war is nearly over, and then he said, "Left fork. Check the left fork," and no one had the heart to tell him we'd already chosen the harder mercy.

The boulder field at the bottom was a cathedral of frost-covered granite, and the old mining road beyond it was a promise. We came out of the canyon at 11:50 at night, five people and a litter, frosted white from hood to boot, looking—I have no doubt—like the ghosts the Corliss Group had been trying to make of us since June.

The mining road ran down-valley, gentle grade, real footing. Ahead of us, maybe nine miles, the east fork joined the main river, and near the junction, if my memory served and it had better serve, there was a Forest Service lookout tower decommissioned in the nineties, high enough, visible enough, close enough to the road that any aircraft flying tomorrow would see smoke or light or movement.

Behind us, up the canyon we'd just descended, headlamps were arriving at the rim. Too late. Too steep. Cursing carried down on the wind, and then, fainter, the sound of someone radioing ahead—which meant the road below us would not stay empty.

Okonkwo got one more bar of signal in the canyon mouth, long enough for a second voicemail, shorter, rougher: coordinates, the tower, the words "come armed" that he immediately regretted and did not retract.

Reyes adjusted the data case harness and looked down the black throat of the valley. "How far?"

"Nine miles. Downhill. Road the whole way."

"And behind us?"

I looked back up at the rim lights, multiplying now, spreading laterally, hunting for the way down that wouldn't kill them faster than we could run.

"Behind us," I said, "is everybody they could scrape together before dawn."

Gideon pulled the litter runners free of the snow bank, checked the wax, and smiled the thin, weathered smile of a person who has finally stopped fighting the storm and started planning around it.

"Nine miles," they said. "Then let's give them a chase worth remembering. Tower by sunrise. Fire by dawn. If the world wants to find us—"

"—we make ourselves impossible to miss," Reyes finished.

We put our heads down into the wind, all six of us counting on the living and the almost-living, and started down the last descent, the passes closing behind us like a book whose ending hadn't been written yet.

Somewhere below, an engine started.
