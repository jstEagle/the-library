## 20. Signal Fire

The ground ran out of mountain at about eleven thousand feet, and the men behind us did not.

I'd like to tell you I had a plan at that point. What I had was a ridge of broken granite, a sky the color of a bruise going green, five people who should have been in a hospital two days ago, and a data case that weighed less than a sleeping bag and more than a life. Behind us, maybe six hundred yards down the saddle we'd crossed an hour ago, four figures moved with the unhurried confidence of people who knew exactly where we were going, because there was nowhere else to go.

"Tell me you have something," Dee said. She was carrying Ana Reyes's pack along with her own, because Reyes's hands had stopped working sometime around dawn, and she hadn't complained once, which scared me more than the men did.

"I have weather," I said.

"Weather isn't a plan."

"Weather is *always* a plan. Most people just don't read it." I turned a slow circle, doing what I do, the thing that has paid my rent and ruined my life in roughly equal measure: reading sky. The front had been building all morning off the southwest—a wall cloud stacked like dirty laundry, anvil spreading east, that particular flat-bottomed look that says *I am not here to drizzle on your picnic.* The wind had backed from northwest to west to southwest over three hours, veering around us like something circling prey. Pressure falling so fast my ears felt it. Somewhere behind that wall was a downdraft with our name on it, and somewhere ahead of it was the leading edge—cold outflow, rain turning to snow, visibility dropping to nothing.

Most people run from that. Storm chasers drive *toward* it, on purpose, for fun, for data, for the pure stupid joy of standing next to the biggest thing on Earth and saying *I see you.*

Right now I needed it to stand next to me.

* * *

Let me back up forty minutes, because decisions deserve context even when they're terrible.

We'd come over the saddle at first light, five of us moving like a centipede with a limp—Reyes in the middle where the terrain couldn't hurt her, Tomás half-carried between Dee and me, his right foot wrapped in a plastic bag and insulation and hope, the other two survivors, Brandt and Osei, walking on stubbornness alone. Halloway's false report had bought us a day, maybe more. We'd spent it badly, the way you spend borrowed time: too slow on the scree, too long finding water, one wrong drainage that cost us ninety minutes of climbing.

And then, cresting the last shoulder before the long descent into the eastern watershed, Dee had gone still in that way she does—total silence, total attention—and said, "Gid."

I looked down the slope. Four men in matching gray shells, spread in a skirmish line, moving up toward us with the patience of people being paid by the hour. Not running. Running would mean they thought we could escape.

"They're not even hurrying," Dee said.

"That's the part I hate most," I said.

We retreated north along the ridgeline, looking for a gully, a chute, anything, but the Sawtooth up here doesn't do mercy—it does walls and air. By ten o'clock we were boxed against a granite headwall with a drop on three sides and a rising wind on the fourth, and the gray shells had closed to four hundred yards, and Tomás sat down without being asked to and didn't get up, and Reyes said, quietly, "Give them the journal, Gideon. It's paper. You can testify from memory."

"No," I said, and surprised myself with how little argument lived in the word.

"You'll die for paper."

"People already disappeared for this paper. Seems rude to let that be for nothing." I looked at the sky again. The wall cloud had eaten the sun. The light had gone that strange green-gray that makes everything look underwater. And underneath the fear, underneath the arithmetic of four against five and healthy against frostbitten, something in my chest lit up like an instrument panel, because I finally understood what the day was offering me.

Not an escape. A stage.

"Halloway tipped someone," I said. "He told us he leaked it. A reporter, federal investigators, somebody. Which means somebody knows roughly where we are and roughly what we're carrying. They just can't *see* us. Can't prove anything. Corliss's lawyers will call it a hiking accident before the bodies cool." I pointed at the wall cloud. "So we make ourselves impossible to misfile. We make it *public.*"

Dee followed my finger, then my logic, and her face did something complicated. "You want to start a fire. Above the tree line. In a drought year. In a *storm.*"

"In the leading edge of a storm, yes. Rain inside the hour, snow behind it. The fire won't spread—it'll barely survive—but for twenty minutes it'll be the only thing burning in fifty miles, sitting on top of five missing surveyors and a stolen river, directly under a storm cell that every satellite in North America is currently photographing." I grinned, and I knew it was the bad grin, the one Dee called my casino smile. "You don't hide a signal fire, Dee. You *broadcast* one."

"And if the storm arrives before the fire does?"

"Then we're wet, cold, and still cornered, and I'll think of something worse."

"That's not comforting."

"It's not supposed to be. It's supposed to be honest."

* * *

Here's a thing nobody tells you about fire above the tree line: there's nothing to burn. No trees, no brush, just krummholz stunted into bonsai by wind, tundra grass cured brown by autumn, and granite. Granite burns approximately never. But the lee side of the headwall had collected a winter's worth of dead grass and a decade of windblown krummholk twigs in its seams, and Dee—former ranger, former arson investigator's daughter, keeper of skills she never explained—produced from her pack a ziplock bag of dryer lint and a ferro rod and said, "Don't ask."

"I wasn't going to."

"Everyone asks eventually."

We built the fire the way you build a lie that has to be true: small, dense, defensible. A rock ring in a natural hollow where the granite dipped, downwind edge shielded by a slab, fuel staged in graded piles—lint and birch bark shavings, then grass, then twigs, then the split pieces of a dead krummholz trunk that had spent thirty years drying into something like coal. Osei and Brandt hauled fuel while Dee and I built. Reyes kept watch on the gray shells and called their distance like a golf caddy. Tomás lay against the rock with his ruined foot elevated and watched us with the flat calm of a man who has decided the universe owes him an explanation and intends to collect.

The wind was the problem and the answer. Southwest, gusting to forty, cold enough to make sparks behave badly. You light a fire in that and it either dies instantly or tries to eat the county. So we built it low and deep, a fire that burned *down* instead of out, and when Dee struck the rod and the lint caught, I fed it like feeding a nervous animal—one blade of grass at a time, coaxing, until it had a core of coals that wind couldn't bully.

Then I stood up on the highest rock I could find, held my arms wide like a conductor, and gave the storm its cue.

That sounds insane. It wasn't. It was chemistry and timing. The outflow boundary was maybe four miles out—I could smell it, that petrichor-and-ozone signature every chaser learns before they learn to drive—and when it hit, the wind would swing hard, temperature would drop fifteen degrees in as many minutes, and the first cold rain would arrive like a curtain coming down. Until then, the pre-storm gusts were erratic, lifting, and a column of smoke above a burning ridge in that kind of instability goes *up*, vertical, a gray exclamation point against a blackening sky, visible—I'm not exaggerating—from thirty miles. From the highway. From the valley towns. From any aircraft with a window.

The fire took. The smoke went up. And the sky, right on schedule, began to end itself.

* * *

The outflow hit at 12:40 in the afternoon and it hit like a door slamming.

Forty degrees became sixty in a single gust, dust and ice pellets flying horizontal, and the smoke column bent, thrashed, and then—this is the part I'll remember on my deathbed—punched *through*, because the cell's updraft overhead was pulling harder than any gust could push. Our little fire, our handful of lint and grass and stubbornness, was suddenly connected nose-to-tail to the largest engine on the continent. The smoke rose in a rope straight into the belly of the storm, and the storm took it and made it bigger, smeared it across the base of the cloud in a dark smear visible, I would have bet my truck, from space.

"Okay," Dee shouted over the wind, both of us feeding fuel now, gloves smoking, eyes streaming. "It's big. Now what? We can't stand here burning wet wood until the cavalry—"

The helicopter came out of the murk at 1:15.

For one full second my heart performed a maneuver I can't medically describe, because the last helicopter we'd seen belonged to Corliss. Then Dee grabbed my arm and pointed and started laughing, a wild, disbelieving sound torn sideways by the wind, because this bird was white and orange, and stenciled on its flank in letters even I could read through the rain was CUSTER COUNTY SEARCH AND RESCUE, and hanging off its side was a camera gimbal, and riding in its open door was a person holding a very large telephoto lens pointed directly at us.

At the fire. At the five of us. At the gray shells now frozen on the slope below, four men in corporate-issue rain gear caught on camera in the middle of nowhere, pursuing five civilians through a federal wilderness area during a declared storm warning.

"Oh," I said. "Oh, Halloway, you beautiful son of a bitch."

The news chopper came in behind the rescue bird at 1:30—KIVH Boise, the markings said, and later I'd learn the reporter aboard had been sitting on Halloway's anonymous tip for nine days, waiting for coordinates, waiting for confirmation, waiting for exactly this: not a story she could be sued out of, but a picture. Pictures are harder to bury than rivers.

The rescue helicopter hovered, winched a paramedic down to Tomás first—triage by frostbite severity, the universe finally sorting us correctly—and the pilot's voice crackled out of a loudhailer against the wind, telling the four men on the slope, in the flat official tone of people who have backup arriving in numbers, to identify themselves and stop moving or the recording drone would keep doing what it was doing, which was making them famous.

They stopped moving. One of them raised his hands. Even from two hundred yards I could see the exact moment a job stops being a job—the shoulders dropping, the head tilting back to look at the camera, at the storm, at the sheer size of the thing they'd been trying to keep quiet, now roaring overhead in high definition.

The rain arrived for real at 1:45, fat and cold, and killed our little fire in under a minute, which was fine. It had done its work. It had never been meant to warm anyone. It had been a sentence written in smoke across the whole sky: *WE ARE HERE. LOOK AT US. LOOK AT WHAT'S BELOW US.*

* * *

They winched us out in order of damage, not importance, which meant I went nearly last, which meant I got to stand in the freezing rain on the ridge and watch the storm I'd courted all day finally close over the range like a book shutting. Snow behind the rain, heavy and fast. The passes would be gone within hours. Whatever happened next would happen in valleys, in courtrooms, in print—not up here. Up here was finished.

Reyes went before me, refusing to release the data case even to the paramedic, strapped to her chest like a child. At the helicopter door she looked down at me, rain streaming off her chin, and mouthed something I couldn't hear over the rotors. I'm fairly sure it was *thank you.* It's possible it was *you're insane.* With Reyes the two were never mutually exclusive.

Then it was my turn, and the collar harness cinched, and the ground dropped away, and I looked down one last time at the headwall, the dead fire ring, the four gray figures being escorted downslope by men with badges and cameras.

Dee was already in the cabin, soaked through, hair plastered flat, grinning at me like a woman who had just survived something on purpose. She yelled something over the noise. I leaned in.

"I said," she shouted, "*what happens when we land and Marisol Vane is standing on the tarmac?*"

The grin died in my chest. Because I'd been so busy watching the sky and the cameras and the gray shells that I hadn't done the coldest arithmetic of all—the one I should have done in chapter one, in a diner, over pie. Marisol Vane's biggest client was Corliss. The salvage contract was her play. And a story this size, breaking tonight, with cameras and helicopters and federal investigators already moving—

Marisol wouldn't be on the tarmac. Marisol would be *already gone*, scrubbing herself out of the ledger, unless somebody got to her first.

The helicopter banked east, toward the valley, toward lights and microphones and the beginning of everything, and I looked at Dee and shouted the only plan I had left:

"Then we land fast, and we get to a phone before she gets to a lawyer!"
