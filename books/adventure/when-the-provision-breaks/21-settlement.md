## 21. Settlement

The town was called Fairlee, which struck me as a joke the mapmakers had played on themselves, because nothing about it was fair and nobody in it seemed to agree on what "fair" even meant anymore. It sat at the bottom of the east slope like a sock caught on a nail—gas station, church, diner, hardware store with a hand-painted sign that said OPEN MOST DAYS. Population four hundred and eleven, according to the welcome sign, though by Thursday I'd have sworn half of them were reporters.

We came down out of the trees on the ninth day, five of us and a litter, looking like something a glacier had chewed up and reconsidered. The injured one—Marek, the hydrologist with the septic foot—had stopped complaining somewhere around day seven, which is the point where you stop being relieved and start being terrified. Dee carried him down the last two miles on her back because she refused to wait for the litter rotation, and when we hit the trailhead and there were people there—Forest Service, county sheriff, a woman from the *Statesman* holding a recorder like it was a live grenade—Dee set him down gently in the snowmelt mud and then just stood there swaying like a fence post in wind.

I did what any reasonable person would do. I looked for Marisol Vane.

She wasn't there. Of course she wasn't. Marisol never attended her own disasters; she preferred to read about them later, over coffee, with that smile she kept polished like a bar top.

* * *

The story broke the way Halloway's anonymous leak had intended it to break: loud, fast, and impossible to stuff back in the bottle. Signal fires are old technology, but cameras are not, and when the rescue bird swung wide to give the news chopper its shot, we were five small figures around a burning pile of lodgepole pine on a bare ridge, smoke standing up straight as a flagpole against the storm-scoured sky. Ana Reyes held the data case above her head. She didn't know she was doing it for the camera. She'd been holding it above her head for three days every time she thought someone might take it from her.

By the time they had us in the valley hospital—Marek straight into surgery, the other two survivors warming under foil blankets, Reyes giving an interview before they'd finished cutting off her boots—the Corliss Ranching Group had a problem no amount of lawyers could graze down.

I watched it unfold from a hospital bed I didn't need, eating Jell-O of a color not found in nature, while the television in the corner ran the loop. Aerial footage of the diversion intake. Flow charts reconstructed from Reyes's journal pages, animated by some graphics department that made the river's slow murder look very official. A spokesman for Corliss saying the words "internal review" with a face like a man reading his own autopsy report. And then, on day two, the federal thing: EPA, FBI, a joint task force announcement delivered by a regional director whose tie cost more than my truck.

"Corliss operation's frozen," Dee said, dropping into the chair beside my bed. She had a cast on one wrist she hadn't mentioned getting and a paper cup of vending machine coffee she was drinking anyway, which told you everything about her relationship with comfort. "Assets seized. Halloway's in custody."

"Halloway turned himself in."

"I heard." She looked at me over the cup. "You heard why?"

"He read the journal." I shrugged, which hurt more than I expected. Everything hurt more than I expected; hypothermia sends you an invoice with interest. "He signed on to guard property. Somewhere between the saddle and the signal fire he figured out what the property actually was."

"And you're okay with him taking the fall?"

"He's not taking the fall. He's testifying. There's a difference, and it's about forty pages of photocopied field notes long." I nudged the duffel at the foot of my bed with my toe. Three copies of everything, made at a copy shop in Fairlee the morning after we got down, because I had learned exactly one lesson from this entire catastrophe and it was *always make copies*. "He gets to be the man who chose right at the end. That's more than most of us get."

Dee snorted. "Generous of you."

"I'm a generous man with catastrophic financial judgment. Ask anyone."

* * *

Marisol Vane came on the fifth day.

I knew she was coming before she arrived, because the nurse at the station desk got a phone call, went quiet in a way nurses don't, and then knocked on my door frame with an expression I can only describe as *apologetic on behalf of the universe*. Dee, who had appointed herself my security detail without asking, stood up from her chair and didn't sit back down.

"Ms. Vane," the nurse announced, and there she was.

Marisol looked exactly as she always looked: cheerful, immaculate, dressed for a climate kinder than this one in a camel coat that probably cost as much as my salvage contract. She carried flowers. Yellow ones. She set them on the bedside table next to my Jell-O like she was decorating a set.

"Gideon," she said warmly. "You look terrible."

"You should see the other guys. Actually, you hired the other guys, so maybe don't."

Her smile didn't flicker. It never flickered; that was the whole horror of her. "May I?" She gestured at the chair, and when Dee didn't move from beside it, added, "Miss Okafor. Always a pleasure. Still doing the Lord's work for free?"

"Still charging," Dee said. "Just not in money."

Marisol sat. She crossed her legs, folded her hands, and gave me the full wattage—the smile that had talked a hundred desperate people into contracts with teeth. Up close, in fluorescent light, I noticed for the first time that she was tired. Not much. Just at the corners. Like a house that looks fine until you see the crack running up through the paint.

"You didn't call," she said.

"Phone's been dead since the burn scar. You know that. You jammed it."

"I mean after. You've been in this town five days. The whole world knows where you are. And my client—" she caught herself, corrected with a little laugh, "—my *former* client's empire is melting on national television, and you haven't called to settle up."

"I've been busy."

"With what? Interviews?"

"One," I admitted. "The *Statesman* woman. And a couple of federal agents who wanted to hear the whole story start to finish. Twice. They took notes. They liked the part about the shell company."

Something moved behind Marisol's eyes then—not fear, exactly. Recalculation. She was very good at arithmetic; it was practically her love language. I watched her run the numbers on what I'd just said and arrive at the sum.

"The contract," she said lightly. "Gideon, sweetheart. Contracts are complicated things. Shell companies especially. Layers within layers. If you handed those nice agents a piece of paper with a name on it, that name might not mean what you think it means."

"It means what it says. Meridian Recovery Solutions LLC, registered in Delaware, dissolved last Tuesday. Two directors, both dead on paper before the contract was signed. But the funding trace doesn't lie—it comes through a small private lending outfit based in Boise." I smiled at her, and I'll admit it felt good, using her own weapon. "Vane Capital Partners. You really shouldn't fund your traps out of your own pocketbook, Mari. It makes the arithmetic so tidy."

The silence lasted four seconds. I counted. Old storm-chaser habit—we count the seconds between flash and thunder to know how far away the trouble is.

Four seconds meant it was already overhead.

"You'd burn me," she said, and the warmth was gone now, replaced by something almost like wonder. "After everything. After I gave you a way out when the banks wouldn't return your calls. After I fed you when your season failed. You'd walk into a federal building and hand them *me*."

"You walked me into a trap," I said. "You sold me to Corliss as a bloodhound. You figured a broke storm chaser would find the journal, and either Corliss's people would bury him along with it, or he'd bring it to you and you'd trade it to keep your biggest client happy. Either way, Marisol, I was the instrument, not the guest. I've been an instrument before. I didn't care for the tuning."

"That's business."

"So is this." I reached into the duffel, slow enough that Dee's hand drifted toward her jacket, and pulled out a folded printout. I held it out to Marisol. "Agent Ostrander, Boise field office. Her direct line. She asked me specifically whether I thought the salvage contract originated with Corliss or independently. I told her the truth: I didn't know. I told her who *would* know."

Marisol looked at the printout without touching it, the way you'd look at a snake on a trail.

"You haven't given her anything yet," she said slowly. "You're giving me a chance."

"I'm giving myself a chance. There's a difference, and it matters to me." I leaned back against the pillows. My ribs filed another complaint. "Here's how it settles, Mari. You call Ostrander yourself, today, before she calls you. You come in voluntarily. You tell her everything—the contract architecture, the Corliss relationship, the inflated insurance valuation, all of it. You cooperate fully. In exchange, you're a witness instead of a target, and whatever plea deal your lawyer negotiates keeps you out of anything worse than probation and fines."

"And if I don't?"

"Then when the Corliss investigation widens—and it will widen, because Reyes's journal names names and Halloway is talking—I'm one signature away from being the man who identified the contract's architect. You'll go down as the villain of the piece instead of the tragic accomplice, and honestly, Marisol, you're too charming for that role. It would be a waste."

She stared at me for a long moment. Then she laughed—a real laugh, short and surprised, the first honest sound I'd ever heard her make.

"You know what the funny thing is?" she said. "This is the best deal anyone's ever offered me. Everyone else wanted their money. You want the truth on the record." She picked up the printout, finally, and smoothed it on her knee like she was checking it for creases. "The debt, though. You understand the debt doesn't evaporate because you've developed a conscience. Sixty-one thousand dollars, Gideon. Principal, fees, seasonal adjustment."

"Ostrander's offering restitution recovery on fraudulent lending practices. Your books are going to be audited down to the coffee budget. When they find the phantom fees—which they will, because I kept every statement you ever sent me, and I counted, Mari, I count for a living—you'll be refunding me." I let that land. "Paid in full. By you. To me. With a government receipt."

For a moment—one unguarded moment—Marisol Vane looked at me the way I imagined she looked at the ledger entries of people who'd beaten her: with genuine, professional respect, and a kind of grief.

"The optimist's arithmetic," she said quietly. "That's what I called it, that first day in the diner. I told you there was always a way out if you ran the numbers right." She stood, tucked the printout into her coat pocket, and adjusted her collar. "I just never figured you'd run them on me."

"Nobody ever does," I said. "It's my best trick."

At the door she paused. "The flowers are for Marek. Wrong room, but the sentiment travels." And she was gone, heels clicking down the corridor, already dialing, I hoped, or maybe just ordering a car. With Marisol you never knew which call came first. Maybe that was the whole tragedy of her. Maybe it was just the whole business model.

Dee exhaled for what sounded like the first time in ten minutes. "You're insane."

"Probably."

"She could ruin you. She still has legal avenues, friends, leverage—"

"She has an audit coming and a conscience she's going to discover sometime around her second month of probation." I looked at the yellow flowers, absurd against the hospital beige. "Besides, Dee. I owe sixty-one thousand dollars to a woman who's about to become a federal witness. There are worse positions. I've held several."

* * *

They released me on day six. Marek was out of surgery and stable, minus two toes and plus a titanium plate, complaining about hospital food with a vigor everyone found reassuring. Reyes was in Boise under protective detail, prepping testimony. The passes had closed behind us—officially, on the radio, with the highway department's flat voice announcing closures I'd have killed for two weeks earlier.

On my last night in Fairlee, Dee and I ate dinner at the diner, because it was the only place open past eight and because some part of me needed to sit in a booth with laminated menus and feel the ordinary weight of the world again. Coffee. Pie. A waitress who called us "hon" and meant nothing political by it.

"Spring," Dee said, halfway through her slice. "When the thaw opens the range. Reyes wants company for the follow-up survey. Official one, this time. Permits and everything." She turned her cup slowly on its saucer. "She asked for us. By name."

I looked out the window at the Sawtooths, blue-black in the dusk, snow loading up high where the diversion channel cut its pale scar toward the intake. The river down here ran thin and cold, starved all season. Come April it would run full.

"Yeah," I said. "I figure we owe the range a visit."

Dee grinned—that crooked, wary grin I'd first seen across a truck bed full of rope and radios, back when we were two strangers sizing each other up over a bad idea. "Owing," she said. "Story of your life."

"Not anymore," I said, and raised my coffee cup to the mountains. "Starting this spring, I only owe the ones that can't collect."
