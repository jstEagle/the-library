## 22. What the Range Keeps

The truck still smells like the storm.

I noticed it the first week back, sitting in the cab at some gas station outside Mountain Home, and I keep noticing it—wet granite and cold pine and a faint chemical ghost of the signal fire's accelerant, baked into the seat fabric like the mountains signed their name and refused to erase it. Gideon says I'm imagining it. Gideon also talks to weather systems like they're exes who owe them an apology, so I don't put much stock in their objectivity.

We're driving north on a Tuesday in late April, because that's what people do now. They drive toward the Sawtooths in late April and watch the thaw come down the drainages like a slow exhale. Except we're not tourists. We're two people with a shared trauma bond and a cooler full of sandwiches, going to look at a river.

"Left at the junction," I say, even though there's no junction for eleven miles.

"I know," Gideon says. "You've said 'left at the junction' four times."

"It's a good junction."

"It's a dirt turnoff with a cattle guard."

"The best ones are." I lean my head against the window. The glass is cold enough to make my temple ache, and I find I like it. There's something honest about cold that bites you immediately instead of waiting until you're asleep in a snow cave to start its work. "Dee Okafor's Guide to Idaho: if the junction doesn't rattle your teeth, you're not far enough from anything that matters."

Gideon snorts. Their hands are steady on the wheel these days. For about three weeks after we came down off the east slope they weren't—I'd catch them gripping the shifter like it was trying to escape, knuckles white, eyes doing that thousand-yard math thing where they were calculating wind vectors against terrain that wasn't there. The doctors called it an acute stress response. Gideon called it "the invoice," which is the most Gideon thing I've ever heard, and I once watched them negotiate a tow fee with a man holding a shotgun.

"How's the foot?" they ask, which is how they ask how *I* am, because we're both emotionally constipated in complementary dialects.

"Toes report for duty. All ten. Two of them are ugly and one of them files a formal complaint every time it rains, but they're mine." I wiggle them inside my boot as proof. "Yours?"

"Fingers work. Can't feel the scar tissue much. Marisol's lawyer says that's normal."

And there it is. The name we've been circling for six weeks like a bird over roadkill.

* * *

Here's what happened after the cameras found us on that ridge, in case you only read the exciting parts of other people's disasters:

The rescue helicopter took Marta Vasquez first—her foot was gone by then, taken in a Boise operating room two days later, which the surgeons said was the good outcome. Then Reyes, then the others, then me and Gideon, who had to be physically separated from the data case by a flight nurse with forearms like bridge cable. Reyes held onto my sleeve while they loaded her and said, "It has to run in full. Every page. Promise me." I promised. I'm not a person who breaks promises made above the tree line; those ones have weight.

The story broke the way stories break now—fast, loud, and mostly wrong for the first forty-eight hours. SATELLITE SALVAGE CREW RESCUED AFTER EQUIPMENT FAILURE, said the early coverage, which was technically true the way a stick of dynamite is technically a percussion instrument. Then Halloway's anonymous leak landed—the full journal scans, the diversion schematics, the internal Corliss memos where someone with a corner office used the phrase "manage the survey situation" in writing, which is the kind of sentence that ends careers and starts federal investigations—and the story corrected itself in public, in real time, like a river finding its channel.

Reyes testified three weeks ago. Closed session, but she came out of the courthouse into a wall of press and gave a statement standing straight as a fence post, frostbite scars visible on both hands, and said: "We didn't disappear. We were erased. There's a difference, and the difference matters." I watched it on a motel TV and cried into a bag of gas station popcorn, and Gideon pretended not to notice, which is the highest form of respect between partners.

Halloway awaits trial as a cooperating witness. He turned himself in nine days after the signal fire, walked into the county sheriff's office in Stanley, put a pistol on the desk unloaded and cased, and said, "I have documents and a conscience, in that order." His lawyers are fighting the charges. My money's on partial conviction and a reduced sentence, because the system loves a man who helps it catch bigger fish, even when the smaller fish did most of the swimming. I hate that I understand it. Understanding things is the tax you pay for surviving them.

And Marisol Vane—

Marisol Vane is in federal custody awaiting trial on fraud, conspiracy, and being the cheerful architect of a plan that got four people hunted through a mountain range for the crime of measuring water. Gideon sold her out. Not the journal—the journal went to Reyes and the feds—but *her*. The contract's architecture, the shell company, the inflated insurance valuation, the whole elegant machine built to use a broke storm chaser as a bloodhound. Gideon handed it all to investigators in a folder so organized it looked weaponized, and when I asked them how it felt, they said:

"Like paying a bill."

That's it. That's the quote. Six weeks of nightmares and a hand that shook around coffee cups, and the summary was *like paying a bill.* I've decided to find it profound rather than alarming. It's easier that way.

* * *

The junction arrives. It rattles our teeth. I feel briefly vindicated in a way I don't share out loud, because sharing it would ruin it.

The lower canyon road is still graded smooth—that same wrongness from our approach last fall, the road too nice for a place nobody official wanted anyone to go. But now there's a Forest Service sign bolted to a post at the gate: AREA CLOSED — ACTIVE INVESTIGATION, and below it, someone has zip-tied a smaller handmade sign that reads GOOD. The handwriting is furious. I love whoever wrote it.

We park where the road degrades into track and walk the rest, because some distances should be earned. The country is doing its April thing—mud everywhere, snow retreating uphill in ragged sheets, the air smelling like wet earth and running water and the green fuse of things deciding to grow. Willows along the creek are going yellow-green at the tips. A pair of geese argue about real estate overhead. It's obscene, honestly, how beautiful it is. Like the landscape read about everything that happened here and decided to be gorgeous anyway, which, fair. Mountains don't do guilt. That's a human export.

Camp Four is exactly where we left it, or nearly. The investigation team catalogued and hauled most of the gear, but the tent platforms are still there, the flat spots in the lodgepoles where the surveyors slept their last ordinary night before choosing to become ghosts. Someone has left a small cairn on the largest platform, river stones stacked maybe eighteen inches high, with a strip of surveyor's flagging tied to the top stone, fluttering orange in the breeze.

Reyes told me she came up in March, before the snow let go, with a Forest Service escort who owed her a favor. She stacked the stones herself. She wouldn't tell me what she said while she did it, and I didn't ask. Some prayers are load-bearing and shouldn't be inspected.

Gideon stands at the edge of the camp with their hands in their jacket pockets and doesn't say anything for a long time. I've learned to let them. Silence is a tool they use better than anyone I know—it's how they read storms, how they read rooms, how they read me.

"They were smart," Gideon finally says. "Leaving everything. Making it look like abduction. If they'd packed out, Corliss's people would've tracked them in a day."

"They were smart and it almost killed them anyway."

"Yeah." A pause. "Smart's not protection. It's just a slower way to be lucky."

"That's the bleakest fortune cookie I've ever heard."

"I'm saving the rest for the drive home."

* * *

We follow the creek upstream to the diversion intake, because we're the kind of people who visit old wounds recreationally.

The structure is still there—concrete weir, headgate, the pipe running off toward the hidden reservoir that fed the Corliss operation's private water bank. But it's changed. There's a federal padlock on the headgate now, bright yellow, absurdly cheerful. There's a monitoring station bolted to the rock beside it, solar panel tilted at the sun, transmitting flow data to some server in a building where people with integrity presumably work. And stenciled across the concrete in letters you could read from a helicopter: SEIZED — U.S. ATTORNEY'S OFFICE.

The river runs full.

I want to be clear about what that means, because it's easy to miss if you've never watched a watershed get bled. Last fall, this creek ran at maybe thirty percent of its historical flow below the intake—a dying vein, willows browning in July, fish pools gone to warm gravel. Now, with the diversion locked shut and the spring melt coming down, the water hits the weir and goes over it in a single unbroken sheet, white and loud and completely indifferent to human paperwork. The sound fills the canyon. You can feel it in your molars. Downstream, all the way to the valley, the river is remembering what it's supposed to be.

Gideon crouches at the edge and puts their scarred fingers in the current, just to touch it, and grins like an idiot.

"Cold?" I ask.

"Freezing. Snowmelt. Perfect." They shake their hand dry. "You know what this is? This is the arithmetic working. All those measurements Reyes took, all those pages she made us carry at gunpoint of frostbite—this is the sum. The river gets the answer."

"You're sentimental."

"I'm a professional. Sentimental professionals are just professionals with better data."

I laugh, and the laugh echoes off the canyon walls and comes back sounding like someone else's, someone lighter, and I decide to borrow her for a while.

* * *

On the walk down, Gideon asks the question I've been waiting six weeks for them to ask.

"Do you regret it?"

I make them wait while I step over a seep of meltwater, because timing matters in comedy and confession alike. "Regret what specifically? The frostbite? The hypothermia? The part where a helicopter tried to murder us with rotor wash above a burn scar?"

"The whole thing. Signing the contract. Carrying the journal out instead of selling it. Ending up famous-ish and broker than ever." They kick a pinecone off the trail with unnecessary violence. "Because here's my ledger. I own nothing. My credit score is a war crime. My professional reputation is 'that person from the water scandal,' which sounds cool until you realize no insurance company will underwrite a chase vehicle for me. And the person I thought was my friend tried to trade my life for a client relationship." They glance sideways at me. "By any rational accounting, I lost."

"And yet."

"And yet," they agree.

We walk a while. The trail drops through open lodgepole, light coming down in long afternoon slants, snow patches glowing blue in the shadows. Somewhere below us, the river keeps talking.

"Here's my accounting," I say. "Two years ago I got fired for telling the truth about Corliss, and everyone—including me—treated it like I'd failed. Like the badge was the thing and the truth was the cost of keeping it. Then I spent a year guiding rich idiots to lakes they couldn't pronounce, drinking cheap whiskey and rehearsing my bitterness until it fit like a glove." I hop a downed log. "Then you showed up with a truck bed full of rope and a bad contract, and I said yes for the money, and the money turned out to be a trap, but the trap led to Reyes, and Reyes led to the journal, and the journal led to—" I gesture at everything. The canyon. The river. The padlocked weir. The orange flagging on the cairn behind us. "This. I still don't have a badge. But I have the thing the badge was supposed to mean. Turns out those aren't the same object, and I spent years confusing them."

"So no regrets."

"Oh, I have regrets. I regret not punching the Corliss attorney at the press conference. I regret every sandwich we ate in that snow cave, individually and as a category. I regret that Marta lost a foot while I got away with ugly toes, because she was the kindest one on that team and the mountain isn't fair." I stop walking and face them. "But regret and wish-it-hadn't-happened aren't the same thing either. The wilderness forgives nothing, Gideon. You know that. It kept every mistake we made, filed and cross-referenced. But people—" I shrug. "People sometimes start over. That's the loophole. That's the whole miracle."

Gideon looks at me for a long moment with an expression I'd call vulnerable if they were the type to permit it, which they are, occasionally, at altitude or in April.

"Okafor," they say. "When my truck gets un-repossessed—which is a when, I've done the math, the settlement from the *Statesman* interview covers it—do you want to go chase something? Something with no conspiracy in it. Just weather. Just the sky doing sky things."

I think about it for exactly zero seconds. "Only if we're chasing it away from debt collectors."

"There are no more debt collectors. I checked. It's the freest I've ever been and it's deeply disorienting."

"Then yeah. Yeah, I'm in." I spit in my palm and hold it out, because I'm a ranger's ranger and oaths should be gross. Gideon looks at my hand with horror, sighs, and shakes it anyway.

* * *

We reach the truck as the sun drops behind the western ridge and the canyon goes blue and gold at once, that specific hour when the mountains look like they're lit from inside. The river's voice follows us all the way down, getting softer, never stopping.

Before we pull out, I take one last look up-valley. The Sawtooths stand against the sky with their winter still on them—white shoulders, dark faces, patient as ever. In a few weeks the high passes will open, and hikers will come through Camp Four and see the cairn and wonder, and that's right. That's how it should work. The land keeps what happens on it, but it doesn't hoard the story; it leaves it out where people can trip over it.

Gideon starts the engine. The radio, tuned low, catches the tail end of the evening news: *"—hearing resumes Thursday, where testimony is expected from the former security chief, whose attorneys confirm he will—"*

Gideon reaches over and turns it off.

"Thursday problem," they say.

"Thursday problem," I agree.

We roll out onto the graded road, past the furious little sign that says GOOD, past the cattle guard that rattles our teeth one final time like a handshake, and the mountains fold closed behind us—not hiding what happened, just keeping it, the way they keep everything: in stone and water and the long memory of a river running full.

Somewhere ahead, a thunderhead is building over the desert, tall and clean and utterly innocent, and Gideon watches it in the mirror the whole way down.

"See something you like?" I ask.

"See something I *owe*," they say, and grin, and drive us home.
