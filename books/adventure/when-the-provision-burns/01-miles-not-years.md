## 1. Miles, Not Years

The Afon Ddu ran four hundred and eleven meters longer than the Ordnance Survey said it did, and I intended to find every one of them.

I'd been on it nine days. Nine days of hip boots and cold mornings, of walking the river's spine from its spring below the quarry to the confluence at Llanfair, pacing the meanders with a surveyor's wheel and a GPS unit that sulked under the tree cover like a cat dragged out in rain. The client—a watermill restoration trust with more enthusiasm than budget—wanted the true course mapped before they applied for their grants. What they got was me: Coral Underhill, wayfinder for hire, a man who could tell you where a river had been in 1740 and where it planned to be in 2040, but who could not tell you what he'd had for breakfast without checking his notebook.

That morning I'd found something good. A paleochannel, dry as an old bone, cutting across a field of sheep-nibbled grass two hundred meters east of the modern course. The river had once swung wide here—lazy, extravagant—and some farmer centuries ago had probably cursed it and watched it leave anyway. Rivers do that. They don't hold grudges; they just go. I envied them that.

I stood in the middle of the dead channel with my notebook open and recorded everything: bearing, width, depth of silt, the species of moss colonizing the old bed. *Sphagnum*, which meant the water table still remembered this place even if the river didn't. Water always remembers. It's people who forget, and mostly on purpose.

"You'll want to see this," I said to no one, because that's how I work. Nine days alone on a river and I'd developed the habit of narrating to myself, which is either a sign of independence or a warning sign, depending on who you ask. Nobody asks.

* * *

Mrs. Bethan Pryce, chair of the mill trust, found me at half past five, exactly where she'd found me every evening that week: packing up my instruments at the field edge with the efficiency of a man dismantling a life he might need to abandon at speed.

"Mr. Underhill." She came across the stile with a basket over her arm, and I knew the basket meant trouble. "You've not eaten at the house once this week."

"I eat," I said. "Extensively. Just not in company."

"That's what I'm told." She set the basket down on the gatepost with the deliberation of a woman laying siege. "There's a cawl in there. Bread. Some cheese my sister sends up from Carmarthen that's better than it has any right to be. You'll take it back to your room, then."

"I'll pay you for it."

"You'll do nothing of the sort." Her eyes were sharp and kind at once, which is a combination I find genuinely dangerous. "My husband says you're the best river man he's seen in forty years, and he's seen a few. He also says you flinch when folk say your name, like a horse hearing a whip. So you'll take the basket, and next week, when the job's done, you'll sit at our table one time, and that's an end to it."

I looked at the basket. Steam was escaping from under the cloth in a thin ribbon, and the smell of leek and lamb reached me with the specific cruelty of home cooking aimed at a man who hadn't had any in years.

"Thank you," I said, and took it, because refusing twice would have required a conversation about why, and I keep those conversations the way I keep my kit—packed light, never opened.

She watched me shoulder my pack. "You know," she said, "my mother used to say a man who measures rivers all day is measuring his own distance from something."

"Your mother sounds like she'd have made a fine hydrologist."

"She made a fine nuisance, which is nearly the same thing." She smiled, and let me go, and I walked the mile back to the village with the basket warm against my ribs like something alive.

* * *

The room above the post office cost thirty a night and came with a grate, a kettle, and a view of the Afon Ddu through glass so old it warped the water into something impressionist. I ate the cawl standing up, because sitting felt too much like staying, and it was so good I had to put the spoon down twice. That's the trouble with kindness. It gets past the discipline. Discipline is easy against hostility; hostility confirms your worldview. Kindness just makes the walls feel like a choice instead of a necessity.

Afterward I washed the bowl, laid out my maps, and spent two hours on the paleochannel notes, cross-referencing the tithe map of 1841 against my own survey until the old river's ghost lay clean and certain on the page. This is the part of the work I love—the part where chaos resolves into lines. A river is a argument between gravity and geology conducted over ten thousand years, and my job is to transcribe it. Every bend has a reason. Every floodplain tells you what the water forgave. Give me a river over a person any day. A river will show you exactly what it is, if you're patient, and it will never once ask you what you are.

I was drawing the final section line when the knock came.

Post doesn't come to that room after seven. I knew that, because I'd asked on the first night—not because I was expecting anything, but because knowing what won't arrive is a kind of comfort. Nothing arriving is the natural state of my life. I maintain it carefully.

But the knock came again, and when I opened the door there was only the postman's retreating back and, on the mat, a single envelope.

Heavy paper. Cream. My name typed, not handwritten—*Mr C. Underhill*, with a second line beneath it that stopped my breath before I'd read a word:

*Re: Estate of E. Vell-Underhill — Release of Sealed Deposit.*

Elias Vell-Underhill. My father. Dead twenty years this autumn, buried—or rather not buried, scattered, per his own instructions, into the River Vell at Porthvell in Cornwall, because even in death the man couldn't bear to be anywhere but beside that river. I'd been nineteen. I'd gotten the news third-hand, from a cousin, six weeks late, because my father's expedition family—the boatwoman, the diver, the archivist, all of them—hadn't known where I was. Neither, in any sense that mattered, had he.

I sat down on the bed with the envelope unopened on my knee for a long time. The fire had burned low. Outside, the Afon Ddu went on arguing with its banks in the dark, patient as ever.

A solicitor's letter. *Release of sealed deposit.* Which meant that somewhere in twenty years of dust, some instruction of my father's had finally ripened—some condition met, some date arrived, some trigger tripped—and now a stranger in an office somewhere wanted to hand me whatever the man had locked away and left behind. His last words, probably. His last joke, more likely. Elias Vell-Underhill had been incapable of leaving a room without making it a puzzle.

I read the letter three times. Then I did the sensible thing.

I burned it.

* * *

Here is what I want to be clear about, since apparently I'm telling this story: burning the letter was not a loss of control. It was control, in its purest form. Twenty years ago I had made a decision—that Elias Vell-Underhill had chosen a river and a dig and a crew of strangers over a son, and that a son so thoroughly chosen-against owed the man nothing, not grief, not curiosity, not a forwarding address. I had kept that decision the way I keep my instruments: clean, calibrated, ready. Burning the letter was maintenance. You don't let rust into the works.

I fed the envelope to the grate page by page and watched the solicitor's careful language curl and blacken. *We regret to inform.* Burn. *In accordance with the deceased's instructions.* Burn. *The item may be collected at our offices in Falmouth at your earliest convenience.* Burn, burn, burn. The wax seal went last, popping softly like something sighing.

"There," I said to the fire, to myself, to the river muttering outside the window. "Done."

Then I washed my face, banked the fire, laid out tomorrow's survey section, and went to bed at ten o'clock precisely, because discipline doesn't take holidays.

I lasted four hours.

* * *

At two in the morning I was kneeling on the hearthstone in my shirtsleeves with a poker, excavating.

I'd like to report that I hesitated beautifully, that I wrestled with angels, that some dramatic internal war preceded the moment my hand went into the ashes. There wasn't. There was just the ceiling of that narrow room, and the sound of the river, and the knowledge—arriving with the flat certainty of a surveyed elevation—that I had burned the only address in the world where a piece of my father still existed. Falmouth. An office. An item. And I had fed it to a grate in Wales because twenty years of discipline was easier than one afternoon of grief.

The ashes were cold by then, or cold enough. I sifted with the poker first, delicately, like a man defusing something, then gave up dignity entirely and used my hands. Most of the letter was gone—properly gone, reduced to that grey lace ash leaves behind. But paper burns unevenly in a shallow grate, and the corner of the envelope had sheltered against the iron, and there, half-legible, printed in the solicitor's tidy block capitals, survived enough:

**FALMOUTH** — and beneath it, **THE TIDE KEEPS NO SECRETS.**

Just that. No office number, no street. But *Falmouth* I could find on any map, and the rest—

The tide keeps no secrets. I turned the scrap over in my fingers, grey to the wrist, and heard my father's voice saying it, though he'd been dead twenty years and I'd done my best to lose the voice along with him. He used to say it on the estuary at Porthvell when I was small, standing knee-deep in the shallows while I complained about the cold. *Every tide goes out further than the last one, lad, sooner or later. The sea gives everything back if you wait. The tide keeps no secrets.*

I had assumed he meant it as comfort. Sitting there in the ash-smell dark, hands filthy, heart going like a fish in a net, I understood for the first time that he might have meant it as a promise. Something of his was waiting to come back to me on a tide. Something sealed, something timed, something he'd trusted to the sea's patience rather than to mine—because he'd known, hadn't he, exactly what I'd do with a letter. He'd known I'd burn it. He'd raised me; he knew what I was made of, and what I'd made of myself after he left. So he hadn't left a letter to be read. He'd left coordinates to be found, by whoever came looking when the looking finally hurt more than the not-looking.

Which meant he'd expected me to come eventually. Twenty years of my own decision, and the man had simply out-surveyed me, plotted my course like a river's, and waited at the mouth.

I sat back on my heels. The scrap of envelope trembled in my fingers and I told myself sternly that it was the draft from the window.

Here's the thing about discipline: it works right up until it doesn't, and when it fails, it fails all at once, like a weir in flood. I had spent two decades being a man with no ties, and I had believed—I had *measured*, to the meter, the way I measure everything—that this made me free. Kneeling in the ashes of my father's last message, I understood that freedom and distance are not the same measurement. I had simply been very, very far from something, and called the farness a virtue.

I set the scrap on the mantel, weighted with a coin. I washed my hands. I did not sleep.

By dawn I had the map open on the bed, the road south traced in pencil—Wales to Cornwall, one hundred and eighty-some miles, most of them ones I'd sworn never to drive again. By dawn I had also, in defiance of every rule I operated by, written a note to Mrs. Pryce explaining that the survey would resume in a fortnight, and apologizing in advance for missing dinner.

Some promises you make to other people. That one I made to myself: collect the item, read it or burn it properly this time, and come back to finish the job. In and out. Forty-eight hours in Cornwall, no more. I would walk to the solicitor's office in Falmouth, take custody of whatever Elias Vell-Underhill had left in the dark for me, and decide—calmly, alone, with full possession of my faculties—whether it deserved a place in my life or a second, more thorough fire.

I packed my kit the way I always pack: light, fast, nothing that couldn't be abandoned.

Then, because the tide keeps no secrets and neither, apparently, did I anymore, I took the scrap of envelope off the mantel and put it in my breast pocket, over the heart, where I could feel it with every step like a small grey splinter working its way toward something.

Outside, the Afon Ddu was running high with overnight rain, brown and certain, going where it went. I locked the door behind me and followed it south.
