## 2. The Tide Keeps No Secrets

I have a rule about grief: I schedule it the way I schedule everything else. Fifteen minutes on the anniversary, timed to the minute, usually spent staring at a river gauge and thinking about nothing in particular. It works. It has worked for twenty years, which is either a testament to the system or a condemnation of it, and I've never been curious enough to find out which.

So when I stepped off the train at Truro with a solicitor's letter folded into my breast pocket like a splinter working its way toward the heart, I told myself this was a collection errand. Nothing more. A man goes to an office, signs a form, receives a box, gets back on a train. Total exposure to feeling: under fifteen minutes. Well within budget.

The letter had said Porthvell. Not Truro — Porthvell, a village name I hadn't heard spoken aloud since I was nineteen, delivered in the solicitor's careful copperplate as though he were handing me something live. *The item is held at the office of Tregose & Penhale, Fore Street, Porthvell. Collection by appointment.*

An appointment. My father had left me an appointment.

I hired a car because there was no other way to get there, and drove west through country that kept trying to be beautiful at me. Hedgerows in flower. A heron standing in a ditch with the patience of a saint. I kept my eyes on the road and my mind on the survey report due Friday, and if that sounds like a lonely way to travel, well — it's also an efficient one, and efficiency has been my religion longer than anything else has.

* * *

Porthvell announced itself the way small coastal places do: a church tower, then a bend, then suddenly the sea, enormous and indifferent, filling half the sky. The village sat in the crook of the valley where the River Vell gave up its argument with the land and joined the tide. I remembered none of it and all of it at once, the way you remember a house you lived in before you could talk — nothing concrete, just a pressure behind the ribs.

Fore Street was cobbled and sloped toward the water. Tregose & Penhale occupied a narrow granite building between a chandlery and a shop that sold fudge and lifeboat memorabilia with equal solemnity. The solicitor himself turned out to be a Mr. Penhale, seventy if he was a day, with reading glasses pushed up into white hair and hands that trembled slightly until they touched paper, at which point they went steady as bedrock.

"Mr. Underhill." He said it the way you'd say *so it's you*, and looked at me a moment too long. "You have his look about the eyes."

"I have my own eyes," I said. "They came free with the face."

Something flickered across his expression — amusement, maybe, or its funeral. "Yes. He said you were sharp. Come through."

The back room smelled of dust and sealing wax and, faintly, of the sea, though we were forty feet from it. On the table sat a single object: a document case, oilskin-wrapped, tied with waxed cord, sealed with a blob of red wax stamped with a mark I didn't recognize — a spiral, or a coiled rope, or a wave seen from above. Beside it lay a single sheet of instructions in my father's handwriting.

I knew the handwriting before I knew what it said. That's the thing nobody tells you about twenty years: the body keeps its own archive. My hand remembered his hand. The particular forward lean of his letters, the way his *r*s kicked. For three full seconds I couldn't read a word of it because I was too busy drowning in the sight of it.

*To be opened at the cave below Vell Head, at the lowest tide,* the note read. *Not before. He'll know why.*

"He'll know why," I repeated.

Penhale had the grace to look apologetic. "Those were the terms of the deposit. Your father was very specific. Very specific indeed." He paused. "He made the arrangement twenty-two years ago, Mr. Underhill. Before he — before the end. And then he renewed it every year, right up until the last. Paid the storage fee without fail. Whatever's in that case, he wanted you to have it, but he wanted you to have it *there*."

"There being a sea cave."

"There being the cave." Penhale lowered his voice, which struck me as odd, since we were alone. "You'll want to check the tide tables. Tomorrow morning is the lowest water they're calling in forty years. Some are saying since the war." He wrapped the case back in its cloth, having apparently decided I wasn't taking it anywhere yet, or perhaps having never intended that I should. "The locals call it the Hollow. They don't go in. They haven't gone in since your father did."

"They cross themselves," I said, remembering the letter's other line, the one about superstition.

"They do more than that." Penhale slid the case into a drawer and locked it, and I understood that I would not be leaving Porthvell today, or possibly ever, with that case in my possession. "Collection by appointment," he said mildly. "Your appointment is tomorrow. Low water is at 6:14."

Of course it was. Dawn. My father had scheduled my grief appointment at dawn, in a cave, at the edge of the coldest sea in England. Somewhere beyond death, he was still managing me, still laying out the route and marking the campsites, and I stood in that dusty back room hating him for it with a purity of feeling that frightened me slightly, because underneath the hate, unmistakable as a spring under turf, was relief.

He'd left me instructions. Nobody had left me instructions in twenty years.

* * *

I took a room at the Salt & Anchor because it was the only inn, and because the alternative was sleeping in the hire car, and because some part of me — the part I keep locked in a drawer with the waxed cord — wanted to see the place my father's people had drunk in every year on the anniversary of the dig that ended. I didn't know that yet. I only knew the pub had good reviews and bad beer, and I ordered the bad beer because it was local, and drinking local seemed like the kind of thing a man collecting his dead father's legacy ought to do.

The barmaid was young enough to have no memory of my father and old enough to know the stories anyway. She looked at me twice while pulling my pint, and the second time she said, "You'll be an Underhill, then."

"What gave it away?"

"The way you're sitting with your back to the wall watching the door." She set the pint down. "Old Maren says Underhills sit like that. Like the room owes them money."

"Is Maren here?"

"She's always here on the low tides. Won't sail them. Says the sea gives things back on a low tide and you don't want to be on the water when it does." She wiped the counter with a cloth that had seen honest service. "There's a whole bench of them in the snug, come to think. Every year, same week. They'll be asking after you before closing, I shouldn't wonder."

"I won't be here before closing."

She smiled the smile of someone who has heard that exact sentence from exactly that kind of man many times, and whose job security depends on those men being wrong. "Room six. Water's hot till ten."

I took my pint to a corner table and opened the notebook I use for work and wrote nothing in it for an hour. Outside, the light went long and gold and then grey, and the estuary emptied itself with the patience of something that has done this ten thousand times and intends to do it ten thousand more. I watched the river drop. Sandbars surfaced. Birds arrived to pick over the exposed mud like creditors at an estate sale.

Tomorrow the sea would pull even further back. Forty years of holding its breath, let out all at once. And beneath the cliffs of Vell Head, something that had been shut would stand open, and I was expected to walk in.

I finished the beer. I went up to room six. I slept badly, which is standard, and dreamed of nothing, which is better than the alternative.

* * *

Dawn came up pewter-grey and windless, the kind of morning the coast grants maybe once a generation, as if even the weather had agreed to keep the appointment. I walked out of the village along the cliff path with the oilskin case under my arm — Penhale had released it at 5:30 sharp with a handshake that lasted a beat too long and words I pretended not to hear: *"Whatever he meant to say to you, lad, he meant it. Don't leave it in the cave."*

Vell Head was a shoulder of dark rock thrust out into the Atlantic, cliffs falling sheer to a wave-cut platform that on any ordinary day lived entirely underwater. Today the sea had withdrawn almost a quarter mile, exposing a landscape no living person had walked: barnacled gullies, forests of wrack lying flat and gleaming, pools so still they held the sky upside down. And at the base of the cliff, where the map in my head insisted there should be solid rock, there was instead a mouth.

That's the only word for it. An arch of stone perhaps twelve feet high, going back into blackness, its floor already drying to a sheen in the early light. Around the entrance, the rock was worn smooth in long vertical grooves, as if water had poured down it for centuries — or as if something had passed through it often enough to polish the threshold.

I stood at a distance and did the professional thing first, because the professional thing is my armor and I put it on before I put my boots on most mornings. Tide state: two hours past low, roughly ninety minutes of safe margin. Cave floor: rising gradient, good drainage, no visible silt hazard. Approach route: firm shingle, no soft ground. All clear. All measurable. All fine.

Then I stopped doing the professional thing, because there was a woman on the path behind me, sixty-odd, walking a terrier, and she had stopped dead when she saw where I was headed, and she crossed herself. Actually crossed herself, thumb to forehead to chest, quick and automatic, the way you'd swat a wasp. Then she turned around and walked her dog back the way she'd come at a pace just short of running.

"Morning to you too," I said, to nobody.

But it rattled me. I'll admit it. Twenty years of measuring rivers teaches you that water doesn't hold opinions, and here was a village full of people acting like this particular stretch of coastline held a grudge. Superstition is just data you haven't explained yet, I told myself, and walked down onto the platform, and the shingle crunched under my boots with a sound like slow applause.

The cold hit at the cave mouth. Not wind-cold — stone-cold, the deep stored chill of rock that hasn't seen sun since the Bronze Age. Inside, the light failed fast, and I did what I'd prepared to do: clicked on my headlamp, checked my watch, and noted the time. 7:02. Eighty-eight minutes of margin. Plenty.

The passage ran straight for thirty feet, then bent left, and the bend was where my father began.

His marks were everywhere. Chiselled survey points, tiny and precise, numbered in his hand. Old iron rings driven into the rock at intervals, rusted to lace. A wooden shelf bracketed into a crack, still bearing the ghost-circle of a lamp base. He had worked here. Worked here for years, by the density of it — this wasn't a visit, it was an occupation. The man who had missed my graduation, my mother's funeral, my entire twenties, had spent a decade of low tides furnishing a hole in a cliff.

And there, on a natural ledge of stone at the back of the chamber, dry above the highest reach of any tide, someone had built a cairn.

Six stones. Flat ones, stacked with the care of a man stacking something he expected to matter. I lifted them off one at a time, and my hands were steady right up until they weren't.

Beneath the stones lay the oilskin-wrapped journal — the same heavy waterproof stock as the case in my pocket, salt-stiffened, tied with cord — and beside it, a second bundle, longer, heavier, wrapped in oilcloth and greased leather both, the way you wrap something you intend to survive a sinking.

I untied the journal first. The cover was stiff with salt, and when I opened it the pages crackled like frost. His handwriting filled the first page, dated twenty-three years ago, and it was a survey log, coordinates and soundings, and I felt my shoulders start to come down because this I understood, this was legible, this was a language I spoke fluently —

— and then the third page stopped being a survey.

*The Provision answers to the one who holds it.* Just that, in the margin, circled twice, next to a sounding that made no sense — a depth reading from a pool that shouldn't exist at that elevation. I turned pages. The numbers got stranger. The margins got fuller. Words like *source* and *keeper* appeared, and once, underlined hard enough to tear the paper: *it does not give. it lends. and it collects.*

My watch said 7:31. I had no memory of the last twenty minutes.

I sat down on the cold stone of a cave my father had haunted for a decade, holding a book he had written for me and never sent, and the fifteen-minute grief allowance I budget every year blew through its limit in about ninety seconds flat. It didn't come gently, either. It came the way the tide comes — not as a wave but as a rise, silent and total, lifting everything I'd carefully moored off its footing. I put my hand flat on the rock because the rock was something real, and the rock was cold, and the cold was proof I was somewhere, and somewhere was more than I'd managed for most of the train journey down.

He'd known. That was the thing that broke me open. Reading those marginal notes, I could hear him — hear the specific cadence of his dictation, because I'd grown up listening to him dictate field notes to my mother before she stopped coming along, and later to me, before he stopped coming home. He'd written this knowing I'd read it. Knowing I'd be angry. Writing anyway, year after year, renewing the storage fee, keeping the appointment alive on the off chance his sharp-eyed son with the wall-facing posture would someday walk down a beach and collect it.

*I'm sorry* was in there somewhere too. Page fourteen, bottom corner, small, uncharacteristically uncorrected. I didn't stop on it. I couldn't. If I stopped on it I'd still be sitting in that cave when the tide came back, and whatever else was true, I refused to die in my father's study.

So I did the work. Because the work is the raft, and I have never once drowned while doing the work. I photographed every page. I logged the chamber dimensions. I catalogued the iron rings and marked their spacing — evenly spaced, I noticed, at intervals of exactly eleven feet, which is a strange number unless you're using them for something other than mooring. And then I picked up the second bundle, the long one, and unwrapped the oilcloth.

A brass instrument lay inside, nested in wool gone grey with age. It was beautiful and wrong, the way deep-sea fish are beautiful and wrong. Part sextant, part something else — a ring of engraved brass bands that rotated within each other, graduated in units I didn't recognize, set around a central lens of glass so dark it swallowed my headlamp beam rather than reflecting it. Black glass. Cold to the touch, colder than the cave, cold in a way that felt less like temperature than like opinion.

Engraved around the outer band, in letters worn nearly smooth: a spiral. The same mark as the wax seal on the case. The same mark, I realized with a lurch, as the little pendant my father used to wear inside his shirt, the one I'd found among his effects twenty years ago and thrown in a drawer and never once, not once in twenty years, let myself think about.

I wrapped it back up quickly. Too quickly; my fingers had gone clumsy.

And that was when the light shifted at the cave mouth — not the slow shift of the sun clearing the headland, but a shadow, brief and vertical, crossing the entrance from left to right. Someone was out on the platform. Someone who moved like they didn't want to be seen.

I killed my headlamp and stood very still in the dark, listening past my own heartbeat.

Footsteps on shingle. Two sets, unhurried, professional. And then a voice, low, outside the arch, saying words the wind shredded — except for one fragment that came through clean, because the wind, like the sea, apparently keeps no secrets either:

"—the Underhill kid's here. He found the journal."
