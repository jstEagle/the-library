## 3. Admission Charged in Blood

I did not sleep in the cave. I want that on the record, because everything else I did that night was the act of a man who has stopped making sensible decisions, and I'd like at least one entry in the ledger that reads *responsible*.

Instead I took the journal and the oilcloth bundle back to my room above the Salt & Anchor — a room I had booked for one night on the theory that Cornwall was a place you passed through, like weather — and I laid them out on the bed between the two pillows like a guest I hadn't invited.

The lamp in that room was the kind with a green glass shade, the kind that makes everything look like it's happening forty years ago. Fitting. The journal smelled of salt and wax and something older underneath, something like the inside of a stone.

My father's handwriting filled the first third of it. I knew that hand the way you know a scar on your own body: without looking, without wanting to. He wrote fast and slanted left, letters leaning into each other like conspirators, and he underlined nothing, because he considered emphasis a form of begging.

*Day 1. Porthvell. The tide has done what twenty years of argument could not.*

That was the first line. Twenty years of argument. I sat with that a while. My father had been a man who argued with glaciers, with funding committees, with the sea itself, so I suppose the tide winning was plausible.

* * *

The early pages were expedition bookkeeping — depths, bearings, complaints about Teo's dive tables and Maren's cooking, which apparently involved "a spice profile best described as ballast." I read those pages standing up, telling myself I was only skimming for route information, and then I read them again sitting down, telling myself nothing at all.

It was page nineteen where the tone changed.

*Ines found the reference she's been hunting. The Vell source isn't just water. There is a thing in the rock there. The old parish records call it the Provision, which is church-speak for "we don't know what it is but we're afraid of it."*

*What we know: it answers to the one who holds it. Not serves. Answers. Ines says the distinction matters and she's right, she's always right, it's insufferable.*

*The valley's water went where the keeper willed. Drought upstream, flood downstream, or the reverse. The millers paid tithes. The town paid more. And when the last keeper decided the tithe wasn't enough —*

Here the ink smeared, as if the page had been closed on wet handwriting, or as if a man's hand had paused over the paper long enough for the sea air to get at it.

*— he drowned rather than surrender it. That is not a figure of speech. He walked into the pool at the source with the thing in his fist and let the water take the both of them. His name was Aldous Vell. The valley is named for him, or he for it; the records disagree, and so do I.*

Aldous Vell. I said the name out loud to the green-lit room and it sounded less like a man than a warning label.

There was more. There is always more; that's the trouble with journals, they keep going after you want them to stop. My father had copied out fragments of parish records, a coroner's report from 1841 written in the flat bureaucratic voice that makes horror sound like an invoice, and a sketch — this was the part I looked at longest — a sketch of a lens. Black glass, oval, set in a collar of living rock, drawn with the obsessive cross-hatching he used when he was trying to convince himself he'd seen what he'd seen.

Beneath the sketch, in smaller letters, almost squeezed into the margin:

*Coral, if you are reading this, stop reading this.*

I laughed. It came out wrong, one dry note, like a branch giving way. Twenty years dead and the man was still issuing instructions I had no intention of following. That had been the whole architecture of our relationship: him pointing north, me walking south, both of us certain the other was lost.

I kept reading.

* * *

The route began on page thirty-one, and it was encoded, because of course it was. My father never met a piece of information he didn't want to make people work for. He'd told me once, when I was small enough to still sit next to him, that a secret written plainly was a secret already spent. At the time I'd thought he meant treasure maps. Later I understood he meant himself.

The cipher was his own variant of a running-key system he'd shown me exactly once, on a train, using a timetable as the key text. He'd thought I wasn't paying attention. I was fifteen and paying attention to nothing else, because it was the only hour in that entire year he'd spent looking directly at me.

So I decoded it. I'm not going to pretend it was hard. That's the ugly little truth I'll carry out of this chapter of things: it wasn't hard at all. The key text was the parish register dates he'd copied earlier in the journal — he'd built the lock and the key into the same box, trusting the reader to be worthy of it, trusting, I suppose, me — and by two in the morning I had the first half of the route spread across three sheets of my survey paper in my own tidy field hand, which looks nothing like his and everything like his, if that makes sense. Genetics is a cruel archivist.

Forty miles. That was the shape of it. Forty miles upriver from Porthvell, through workings his notes called *the old men* — the abandoned tin mines, driven so deep and so long ago that the miners themselves were legend — past a chapel the river had taken, along markers he'd left in the rock. He'd numbered them. Marker One was somewhere under the cliff behind me, in the cave, and I realized with a slow cold turn of the stomach that I had probably knelt on it without noticing, some carved stone worn smooth, holding up my knee while I read about a drowned man who wouldn't let go.

Half the route. By four o'clock I had half the route, and the second half dissolved into cipher keyed to something I couldn't find — a missing page, maybe, or a second document, or a word I was supposed to already know. He'd done it deliberately. Split the knowledge. Even dead, even dissolved to ash and estate paperwork, the man was rationing himself out to me in installments.

I got up to open the window because the room felt like it was pressing down, and that was when I saw the boot prints.

* * *

Not mine. Let's establish that immediately, because a surveyor notices his own prints the way a writer notices his own typos. Mine were hobnail-less, deep-heeled, left slightly longer than right from an old ankle break. These were different: lighter, newer, a lug sole with a chevron pattern, pressed into the fine silt floor of the cave where the tide's retreat leaves a skin of wet clay that records everything like a confession.

I'd walked past them on the way in. Of course I had — I'd walked past everything on the way in, blind with purpose, a man tunneling toward his father's ghost with his head down. But now, standing at the window with the cold coming off the harbor, I replayed the cave in memory the way I'd replay any surveyed reach, and the prints were there, all right. A dozen sets of them at least, going in and coming out, overlapping, days old at most — the edges still crisp, not yet softened by seepage.

And the cigarette ends.

Three of them, filtered, a brand I recognized because the site manager of every bad development project in Britain smokes it. They'd been stubbed against the wall near the entrance chamber, in a neat row, which told me something worse than carelessness would have. Careless men litter. Neat rows mean someone was standing watch. Someone had stood in my father's cave, smoking in shifts, waiting out the tide window, while another set of hands went deeper in.

Went deeper in and did what? The journal had been wrapped in oilcloth and wedged behind the marker stone. If they'd found it, why leave it? If they hadn't found it, what had they come for?

I dressed. I don't remember deciding to dress; I remember the cold buttons and then I was dressed, and I was back down the cliff path at five in the morning with the tide starting its long gray crawl back toward the cave mouth, and the gulls making their opinions known.

Inside, I did what I should have done the night before: I surveyed it. Properly. Tape, notebook, the whole discipline — because measurement is the only prayer I know, and because a man who measures cannot be lied to by his own imagination. I paced the entrance chamber, logged the boot prints' sizes and directions, collected the cigarette ends into a sample bag like the evidence of a crime, which is what they were, though of what I couldn't yet say.

Then I went to the marker stone.

It was where I'd knelt, of course. A slab of elvan, waist-high, its face carved with a spiral worn nearly to nothing — and below the spiral, cut shallow and recent, a chisel mark. Fresh. The stone dust in the groove still pale, unweathered. Someone had pried at it. Someone had tried to lever the slab and failed, or been interrupted, or — and this was the thought that put the taste of iron in my mouth — someone had pried at it, found what was beneath, and taken part of it.

The oilcloth bundle was still there. But the space around it had been disturbed, swept clean in a way the rest of the cave was not, and at the slab's base, half-hidden in silt, lay a scrap of paper. Site plan fragment. A consortium letterhead I didn't recognize, a contour line of the Porthvell cliffs, and across the top in a draftsman's confident hand: *SOURCE — CONFIRMED LOCATION — PHASE 2.*

Phase 2.

I stood in the cave with the tide whispering closer, holding a piece of someone else's map to the same river I held half a map to, and the feeling in my chest curdled from the warm ache of grief into something quicker and colder. Curiosity is a slow fire. This was different. This was the specific chill of realizing you are not alone in a house you thought was empty — except the house was a valley, forty miles long, and everyone in it was asleep.

They had the destination. I had the road. And somewhere between us was whatever my father had died — no. Whatever my father had *left*, I corrected myself, firmly, because he was dead of a heart attack in a hotel in Truro and I had signed the condolence card myself, I had *seen* the certificate — whatever he had left, split between a sealed estate item and a missing half-map, and a company with a letterhead wanted it badly enough to post sentries in a cave the locals wouldn't look at.

The first wave lapped at the entrance, dark and businesslike, beginning the cave's daily erasure. In an hour there would be no prints, no ends, no evidence but mine.

I took photographs of everything, pocketed the letterhead scrap, and climbed out onto the cliff path as the light came up the color of tin.

One pub in the village. One. I'd noticed it yesterday with mild professional approval — a place like Porthvell supports exactly one pub the way a body supports exactly one heart — and I'd noted its name in my logbook without thinking about why: *Salt & Anchor*. Because my father's expedition reports, the ones I'd catalogued for the solicitor's inventory, had receipts from it. Expense entries. Pints, beds, a recurring line item every year on the same date, twenty-two years running, ending the year he died.

He'd drunk there. His crew had drunk there. And somebody connected to that crew — or to that letterhead — might be sitting in it right now, warming a stool, keeping a watch on a cave.

I told myself I was going for breakfast. I am a good liar on paper and a terrible one aloud, and the walk down the lane, I spoke the truth to nobody: I wanted to find whose boots those were, and I wanted it with an urgency that felt less like investigation and more like inheritance.

The Salt & Anchor's door was propped open with a mooring stone. Inside, smoke and fry-fat and low voices, and behind the bar a woman with forearms like hawser rope who looked up at me and went very still, the way people go still when they've seen a ghost wearing a familiar coat.

"You'll be Coral," she said. Not a question.

The tide was coming in behind me. Ahead of me, three faces at a corner table had turned, and every one of them was looking at me like a door opening onto a room they'd kept locked for twenty years.

I should have turned around. I want it noted that I knew that too.

I stepped inside, and the door swung shut on the sea.
