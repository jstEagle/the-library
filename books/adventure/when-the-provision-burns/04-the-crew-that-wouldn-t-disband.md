## 4. The Crew That Wouldn't Disband

*Coral*

The boot prints led down the cliff path like a confession.

I'd spent an hour in the cave photographing them before I let myself admit what they were: Vibram soles, size eleven, left foot favoring the outside edge the way a man's does when he carries weight on one shoulder. Recent. The tide hadn't touched them, and the tide had been out for six hours. Whoever they belonged to had walked out of my father's last hiding place while I was still on the train from Paddington, reading a survey report and telling myself this was a errand, not a pilgrimage.

Errands don't leave boot prints in your chest. Neither do pilgrimages, if you do them right. I was doing neither right.

The prints went up the coast path, then inland along the lane toward Porthvell village, and I followed them with the particular joylessness of a man who has already decided where they end. There is exactly one building in Porthvell that stays lit past nine, and it has a sign shaped like a fish that someone, decades ago, painted to look less like a fish and more like a boot. The Salt & Anchor. My father drank there. I knew this the way you know things about a house you grew up in and then burned down—vaguely, resentfully, without ever having been inside.

I stood across the lane for four minutes by my watch. Long enough to count the windows, note which were open, and lie to myself twice about walking away.

Then I went in, because the alternative was standing in a lane all night being a man who stands in lanes.

* * *

*Maren*

Forty-one years I've run boats out of Porthvell, and I can tell you the exact weight of every regular who walks through that door by the way the floorboards take them. Heavy heels means Hartley the builder. Shuffle-and-drag means old Penhale, God rot him, still alive out of spite. But there's a walk I only hear once a year, sometimes not even that, and it's the walk of someone who measures the room before entering it—who counts exits the way other people count change.

That walk came through the door at twenty past nine on the anniversary, and I put down my pint before I'd finished hearing it.

He had Elias's face. That was the first thing, and it hit me like cold water off a winch handle. Not the face as it was when we buried him—hollowed, salt-bitten, sixty-two going on ninety—but the face as it was at thirty, at the start, when he'd stand at the bow of the *Kestrel* with his notebook held out of the spray and laugh at things that weren't funny because he was too happy to tell the difference. This young man had that same face, minus the laughing. Same grey eyes doing arithmetic on the room. Same jaw set like he was bracing against weather that hadn't arrived yet.

Coral Underhill. Last seen at fifteen, furious, on a station platform, refusing to shake my hand.

"Sit down," I said, before he could perform whatever speech he'd rehearsed in the lane. "You're letting the cold in."

He sat. Of course he sat. He wanted to be told.

* * *

*Coral*

Three faces turned toward me, and I want it recorded that my plan was to ask two questions and leave. Who else has been in the cave. What did my father keep here besides a journal. Two questions, maybe ninety seconds, gone.

"Sit down," said the woman, and I sat.

Maren Voss hadn't aged so much as weathered—she looked like a headland, all planes and salt damage, with hands like mooring rope coiled on the table. To her left, a small brown man with a diver's build and a saint's candle burning in a jam jar beside his beer, which I decided not to ask about. To her right, a woman with silver-streaked hair cut short and spectacles pushed up into it, who was looking at me the way an archivist looks at a misfiled document: with interest, and a certain professional offense.

"You're Coral," said the candle man, wonderingly, as though I were a rumor made flesh.

"Teo," Maren said, "don't gawp." Then, to me: "You have his chin. And his manners, which is to say none worth mentioning. What'll you drink?"

"I'm not staying."

"You're staying." She raised two fingers at the bar. "Pint of the dark. He'll want the dark. Elias always—"

"Don't," I said.

The word came out harder than I'd loaded it. The room went quiet around us—the kind of pub quiet where everyone pretends to resume their conversations and nobody does. Maren looked at me for a long moment, and something moved behind her eyes that wasn't hurt exactly. Recognition, maybe. She'd seen that flinch before, on a station platform, twenty years ago.

"All right," she said mildly. "Whatever you like. But you'll sit while it's poured, because I say so, and this is my parish."

The pint arrived. It was the dark. I drank it, because refusing it would have required a second sentence and I'd budgeted for one.

"Ines," said the archivist, extending a hand across the table. "Ines Cardoso. I catalogued your father's field notes for eleven years, which makes me possibly the only person alive who knows what his handwriting looks like on a Tuesday versus a Friday. You have his Tuesday hand, incidentally. I can see it in how you're holding that glass. Measuring it."

"I'm not measuring anything."

"You're measuring everything. It's not an insult. It's just a diagnosis." She took off her spectacles, polished them on her sleeve, and put them back on, and I understood I had just been filed. "Now. You've come down from somewhere with rain in it—you've got Welsh mud on your boots, the clay's the wrong color for here—and you've come tonight of all nights, which means either extraordinary coincidence or you found something in that cave the tide opened up. And since you don't strike me as a man who believes in coincidence—"

"I found a journal," I said.

And that was the whole problem with these people, I realized, somewhere around the second swallow of beer. They didn't ask questions so much as dismantle you politely, piece by piece, and reassemble you on their table with all your secrets facing outward. I had crossed two countries to avoid being known, and within four minutes of sitting down I was more legible than I'd been in a decade.

"The journal," Teo breathed. He'd gone pale under his tan. He touched the candle flame lightly, the way you'd touch a rosary. "Elias's journal. From the dig."

"You knew about the cave," I said. "Someone's been in it. Boot prints, fresh. Size eleven, rolls to the outside edge."

The three of them exchanged a look—a whole conversation conducted in half a second, the kind of shorthand you can only buy with thirty years of shared disasters. I hated how fluently they spoke it. I hated that some animal part of me leaned forward, wanting to learn the language.

"Not ours," Maren said flatly.

"We meet once a year," Ines said. "Tonight. We've met every year since—" She stopped, recalibrated. "Since the dig ended. Since Elias. It's not sentiment, before you make that face. It's bookkeeping. Somebody has to keep the record straight, and the record isn't finished."

"What record?"

"That," said Maren, "is the wrong question, asked in the wrong order. Right question is: what did the journal say?"

"And I'll thank you," added Ines, "not to answer until you've had the pint. Answers go down easier with ballast."

* * *

*Teo*

I want to write down what it was like, seeing him, because Ines says a record kept only in the head is a rumor. He came in with the sea still on him—not wet, but the way of him, the way he stood, weight on the balls of his feet like the floor might tilt. Like Elias. When Elias came into a room, the room became a deck. This boy—I know he's forty-something, but the eyes are fifteen, fifteen and locked—the boy had the same thing, except his room didn't become a deck. It became a cell. He counted the doors. I watched him count the doors.

Elias never counted the doors. Elias trusted rooms. That was his flaw and his grace, both, and I lit the candle for him the way I always do, and when Coral saw the flame he looked away fast, and I thought: ah. There it is. He thinks the candle is grief. He doesn't know yet it's navigation.

When he said *journal*, my heart went over sideways like a dinghy in a crosswind. Because I dove that cave in '89, before the collapse sealed it, and I felt what was down there—or felt the shape of the feeling, if that makes sense, which it doesn't unless you've been down in the black places where the water remembers things. The water down there remembered Elias. I told him so afterward and he laughed and bought me a brandy and never wrote it in any notebook, and I have kept it thirty-five years like a stone in my pocket.

Now his son sits in front of me with the same stone, and doesn't know its weight yet.

"He'll need the boat," I said to Maren, quietly, in our own language, which is mostly eyebrows.

"He'll refuse the boat," she said back.

"He'll refuse the boat and take the boat."

"We know," Ines murmured, not looking up from the napkin she was already sketching on. "We've done this before. With his father. It's practically liturgy."

* * *

*Coral*

By the third pint I had told them nothing and they had learned everything. This is not a paradox; it is simply what happens when you sit across from professionals. Ines deduced the journal's existence from my coat pocket's shape. Maren deduced the route involved the upper river from the fact that I kept glancing at the OS map framed behind the bar—the one with the Vell's source marked, wrongly, with a charming cartographic fiction. Teo deduced, by means I decline to speculate on, that I would need a diver before the week was out.

"You're not going upriver alone," Maren said, in the tone of a tide table. Fact, not negotiation.

"I work alone."

"You worked alone for twenty years and look at the state of you."

"There's no state of me."

"You count the doors, love. I've watched you do it four times since you sat down. Men with no state in them don't count the doors." She drained her pint and set it down with the finality of an anchor. "Besides. Whatever's in that cave besides the journal—your size-eleven friend—he'll be going upriver too. And you'll want people behind you when you meet him. Everyone wants people behind them when they meet that sort. The ones who say different are lying or dead."

"I don't need—"

"Elias said the same thing," said Teo softly, touching his candle. "In '88. Word for word. *I don't need.* And then the mine flooded, and he needed, and we were there, and after that he stopped saying it." He smiled, sad and crooked. "It took the water to teach him. I hope it goes faster for you."

I should have left then. I had my answers—partial, but enough to plan on—and every minute at that table was a minute the boot-print man gained. I knew all this. I had calculated it, because calculation is what I do instead of feeling, and I am very good at it.

And then Ines slid the napkin across the table. On it she'd drawn, in about nine seconds, a fair copy of the upper Vell's east fork with three annotations I recognized from the journal's margin sketches—my father's shorthand, the little hook-marks he used for current direction. She'd reconstructed it from memory. Thirty-five years on, she'd reconstructed it from memory.

"We aren't offering you company," she said. "Company is optional and we're not sentimental. We're offering you the only three people alive who can read that map at speed, in bad light, with wet hands. Take it or leave it, but decide tonight, because Maren's boat goes up on the morning tide either way—with or without you, and frankly the boat's opinion matters more than yours."

I looked at the napkin. At the hook-marks. At the candle burning for a man I'd spent twenty years pretending to have finished grieving, in a pub I'd sworn I'd never enter, surrounded by people who had apparently been waiting for me every year on the same night like a lighthouse crew waiting for a ship they had no proof was coming.

"One condition," I heard myself say.

Maren's eyebrows went up. "Go on."

"Nobody calls it a reunion. Nobody says the word *family*. And nobody—" I pointed at Teo's candle, "—nobody tells me what the candle's for until I ask."

Teo beamed at me like I'd passed a test. "Fair terms," he said. "Elias negotiated worse."

"It's settled, then." Maren stood, joints cracking like rigging in a blow, and dropped coins on the table. "Morning tide, six o'clock, the quay below the lifeboat station. Bring the journal, bring your kit, and eat something first, because I don't feed passengers who faint." She paused at the door and looked back at me with an expression I couldn't file anywhere. "And Coral. Whoever's boots those were—they'll have photographed what they found. Which means somebody paid them to find it. Sleep on who that might be."

She went out into the dark. Ines gathered her napkin-map with the tenderness of a rare folio. Teo pinched out the candle between finger and thumb, unbothered, and whispered something in Portuguese that I chose, for now, not to ask about.

I sat alone for a moment in the emptied corner of the pub, and performed my usual audit: time lost, cover given up, autonomy compromised, exit routes reduced to one boat at six a.m.

Every line item was a loss. And underneath the ledger, quiet as groundwater, something that was not loss at all.

I finished the pint. I counted the doors—one, two, three—and for the first time in years, I wasn't counting them to leave.

Then I saw, pinned to the pub's noticeboard between a missing cat and a regatta poster, a printed sheet on thick cream paper. *Porthvell Valley Regeneration Consultation. Aldous Crane Estates Ltd. Public meeting Thursday.* Below the text, a rendering of a reservoir that would drown half the upper river—and stamped across the bottom corner, in blue ink, a site office address.

And beneath that, smaller, the name of the site manager overseeing the project.

I read it twice. Then I took the notice down off the board, folded it into my pocket next to my father's journal, and went to find out why Crane's man had been walking in my father's cave.
