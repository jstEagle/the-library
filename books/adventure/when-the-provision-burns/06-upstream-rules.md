## 6. Upstream Rules

I spread the journal's map across Maren's kitchen table at six in the morning, weighted down at the corners with a salt cellar, a tin of tea, a chipped mug Ines had already claimed, and Teo's elbow, which he moved only after I glared at him twice.

"Forty miles," I said. "Give or take."

"Rivers don't give or take," Maren said from the stove, where she was frying something that smelled like it had opinions about me. "They take exactly what they want."

The map was drawn on waxed linen in my father's hand—small, precise letters, the kind a man uses when he expects his work to outlive him. The Vell ran up the page like a spine, and along that spine he'd marked his route in a code of symbols: a triangle for the first marker, a circle with a cross for the second, a squiggle I hadn't cracked yet that appeared every few miles like a heartbeat.

"The lower estuary is navigable to about mile nine," I said. "After that the river narrows. There's an old leat system—" I tapped the page "—cut into the hillside above Tregowan. Tin workings. Abandoned since before the war. He routed us through them here, here, and here." Three triangles, spaced like stitches.

"Why through mines?" Ines asked. She'd arrived with her own coffee, her own chair, and the settled air of someone who had decided she was staying before anyone had voted on it. "Why not follow the bank?"

"Because the bank is watched." I turned the journal to the page I'd copied out the night before, sitting alone in my rented room with the window open because grief had made the air feel thick. "He wrote: *The river gives one road and takes another. Walk the water's way and you'll be seen; walk under it and you'll be safe.*"

"He means the adits," Teo said. "The mine entrances. Some of them open below the waterline when the tide's right."

"You've been in them?"

"I've been in everything." He said it without pride, the way a man admits to scars.

Maren brought the pan to the table and set it down hard enough to make the mug jump. Fried bread, eggs, something dark and salty I didn't ask about. Four plates. She'd set four plates without asking anyone anything, and nobody remarked on it, which told me this table had been setting itself for twenty years and I was the latecomer.

"Eat," she said. "Then tell us the rest."

*Us.* I ate.

* * *

Here is what I had planned, back when planning meant planning alone: I would take the journal, take the brass instrument wrapped in oilcloth—which I still hadn't shown them, and didn't intend to—and I would be on the river by dawn, alone, moving fast, answerable to no one. That had been the shape of my life for twenty years and it fit me the way a good boot fits, snug and broken in and silent.

Instead there were four plates.

"The drowned chapel," I said, because they were going to hear it eventually and better from me than from the map. "Lower Vell. It flooded when they dammed the upper reaches in the fifties. The village went under whole. His route passes through it—there's a marker inside the chapel itself."

"In the church?" Teo leaned forward. "Underwater?"

"Underwater."

"How deep?"

"The journal says the bell tower clears the surface in dry summers. This hasn't been a dry summer."

Ines wrote something in her notebook. She'd been writing things all morning, small and fast, and I was beginning to understand that Ines processed the world by cataloguing it, which was either comforting or alarming depending on how much of your life you wanted archived.

"And the third marker?" she asked.

"Squiggle symbol. Haven't decoded it yet." I said it flatly, refusing to make it a confession. "It repeats. Whatever it marks, it matters."

"It's a bell," Teo said quietly.

Everyone looked at him.

"A bell curve," he said, tracing the air. "My grandmother rang bells. When she drew one, she drew it like that—a line that rises and falls. A squiggle with weight in the middle." He looked at me. "Your father put a bell at the center of his map."

I stared at the symbol until it stopped being a squiggle and started being what it was. Of course. Of course it was. I'd been staring past it for two days, looking for complexity where there was only a man's memory of a church bell ringing over a valley that no longer existed.

"Lower Vell," I said. "The marker's the bell. Or the bell marks the marker. Either way we go through the drowned village."

Maren snorted. "Elias always did love a dramatic waypoint."

The name landed in my chest like a dropped anchor. Elias Underhill. My father had a first name. Other people had used it, casually, for years, while I'd been using *my father* the way you use a locked door—as a thing to define yourself against. Hearing it in Maren's mouth, fond and unguarded, did something I didn't have a word for yet.

"Eat your egg," Maren said, and I realized I'd been staring at my plate.

* * *

We argued about boats for an hour.

Not really argued. Arguing requires two people who want different things to win. What actually happened was that Maren said "You'll take my boat," and I said "No," and then we spent an hour discovering how many ways a person can say no before it stops meaning anything.

"The estuary's tidal," she said. "You don't know its moods. There's a sandbar off Carrick Point that has eaten better sailors than you."

"I've read the charts."

"Charts." She said the word the way other people say *rumors*. "I've sailed this water forty years, boy. The charts are a rumor the Admiralty tells itself."

"I've run stranger rivers."

"Not this one." She leaned across the table. "This one knew your father. It'll want to talk to you, and it talks rough."

"That's not a reason."

"It's the best reason there is."

Ines, wisely, stayed out of it, annotating. Teo kept looking between us like a man watching weather build. And I sat there with my father's map under my hands and understood, with a clarity I resented, that I was not arguing about boats. I was arguing about whether I could do this—the whole thing, the cave and the journal and the Provision and whatever waited at the source—with witnesses. With people who would see me flinch. Who would remember.

Solitude wasn't just my preference. It was my method. You can't be betrayed by people you never let aboard. You can't be left by people you never let stay. My father had taught me the opposite lesson early and thoroughly—he'd chosen the dig over me, chosen the expedition family over the actual family—and I had spent twenty years building a life where the door only opened outward.

"No," I said again. Final. Done.

Maren looked at me for a long moment. Then she stood, collected the plates, and carried them to the sink as if the matter were settled, which was somehow worse than if she'd shouted.

"Fine," she said, her back to me. "Go alone. Drown alone. Your father nearly did the same, you know. First year after the dig ended, he came round here with some half-baked plan to go upriver solo. Wouldn't say why. Wouldn't take company." She turned on me, dish towel over her shoulder like a bandolier. "Teo found him halfway up the gorge with a busted ankle, crawling. Crawling, Coral. Proud as a king and crawling. We carried him home and he never thanked us and we never needed him to. That's what crew means. It means nobody has to thank anybody."

The kitchen was very quiet. Even Ines's pen had stopped.

"So take the boat or don't," Maren said. "But know that going alone isn't strength. Sometimes it's just the slowest way to crawl."

I looked down at the map. At the little triangles stitching their way upstream. At the bell. At forty miles of my father's handwriting, every letter of it written by a man who, apparently, also tried to do this alone and failed.

And here is the part I will admit now, though I fought it then: some traitor muscle in my chest had already said yes. Had said yes last night in the pub, when Ines recited my own survey work back to me from memory. Had said yes when Teo laughed at something and the sound filled a room I'd been keeping empty on purpose. Saying yes out loud was just paperwork.

"Maren," I said.

She didn't turn around.

"I'll take the boat."

She kept washing dishes. But her shoulders came down half an inch, and I learned later that this was how Maren celebrated—in millimeters, so no one could tax her for it.

"But I navigate," I added. "Nobody touches the journal. And if I say leave, we leave. No votes."

"No votes," she agreed, too easily.

"We're a democracy," Ines said mildly, not looking up.

"You're cargo with opinions."

"Flatterer." She turned a page. "I've drafted a supply list. We'll need rope, lantern oil, spare batteries, and someone should warn the harbormaster we're taking the *Kestrel* upriver, because the last man who borrowed her without asking is still legally dead as far as Maren's concerned."

"That was one time," Teo said.

"He sank her."

"He *nearly* sank her."

The argument rolled on without me, warm and stupid and familiar in a way that frightened me more than any mine tunnel could. I gathered the map, rolled it, tied it, and stood at Maren's window looking out at the harbor, where the tide was turning and the cliffs of Porthvell stood black against the grey, the sea cave hidden somewhere beneath them, holding its secrets until the water gave them up again.

Forty miles upstream. A drowned chapel. A bell under the water. And somewhere ahead, whatever my father had died rather than use.

Behind me, three people were dividing supplies with the ease of long habit, making room for me in the rhythm without ever once making room feel like charity.

I had refused company and accepted it in the same breath, and the strange thing—the thing I turned over all day as we loaded the boat—was that both refusals had felt true, and only one of them had felt honest.

* * *

We cast off at slack water, Maren at the tiller, Teo forward watching the channel, Ines amidships with the journal's transcriptions spread on her knees despite my rule, because rules were clearly suggestions to her. The *Kestrel* was older than me and moved like she remembered every current personally.

By the third bend of the estuary I had stopped checking the chart. Maren read the water the way I read contour lines—instinctively, greedily—and it was easier to watch her do it than to admit she was better at it than my charts.

At Carrick Point the sandbar showed its teeth, a long brown seam of broken water, and Maren took us past it so close I could have touched the bar with an oar, grinning the whole time like a woman introducing me to an old friend of dubious character.

"See?" she called. "Talks rough. But she knows me."

Past the point, the valley opened. Green hills stepping down to the water, gulls working the mudflats, and far upstream—so far it might have been imagination—a thin white thread where the river fell through the gorge toward us.

Toward the mines. Toward the chapel. Toward the source.

Teo stiffened suddenly at the bow and raised one hand—not alarm, exactly. Attention.

"Smoke," he said. "Upstream. Past the old quay. Somebody's camped on the bank."

I took the glasses from the locker and found it in seconds: a thread of woodsmoke rising from behind the ruined walls of the old tin quay, and beside it, moored in the shallows where no local would ever beach a boat, a rigid inflatable with an outboard worth more than the *Kestrel* entire.

Crane's men. Ahead of us. On our river.

Maren cut the engine and let us drift, silent, the current already deciding for us.

"Well," Ines said softly, pen poised. "Shall we go around, or shall we go under?"

I looked at the hillside, where the black mouths of the old mine adits opened above the waterline like held breaths.

"Under," I said.
