## 7. Flooded Chapel, Standing Congregation

I have been diving since I was nine years old, which means I have been drowning, in a professional capacity, for most of my life. There is a difference. Divers will tell you there isn't, that the two are the same activity separated only by outcome, but those divers have never felt the particular theology of a flooded church. In open water you drown alone. In a drowned chapel, you drown in front of God, who has had forty years to get comfortable down there and does not appreciate visitors.

Teo Ferreira knows this. Teo Ferreira is telling you now, from the far side of it, with tea in his hands and his heart still doing that arrhythmic thing it does when it remembers what almost happened to it.

But I'm ahead of myself. Maren would say that's my whole problem. "Teo," she'd say, "you start every story in the middle and end it in the deep end." She's not wrong. I was raised in the middle of things. Middle of six children, middle of the Atlantic, middle of every argument the expedition ever had. My mother said I was born between tides, and that this explained everything, and she never said what it explained, which is the most Portuguese thing a woman can do.

* * *

We put in below the old quarry road at dawn, because Maren does not believe in dawn, she believes in the half hour before it, when the water is honest. "Lies need light," she says. I've never understood it, but I've never argued with her either, not about water. Arguing with Maren about water is like arguing with a priest about guilt. She'll win, and she'll make you feel it was your own idea.

Coral stood at the bow with the journal wrapped in oilcloth against his chest like a baby, or a bomb, and looked at the water the way he looks at everything — like he's measuring it. Which, I suppose, he is. That's the whole man. A wayfinder. He measures rivers for a living, and I thought, watching him, that he measures everything else for free. Distance from the rail to the shore. Distance from himself to us. I could see him doing the sums.

The chapel of Saint Brendan sits — sat — in the bend where the Vell widens before the second gorge. They flooded it in the fifties when they built the lower dam, and the whole village went under, pews and font and all, and the government paid people to leave and the people left, mostly, and the river took the rest. My grandmother was from Lower Vell. She used to say the church didn't drown, it just went where its people went, which is either beautiful or terrifying depending on how much wine has been poured.

Ines had the charts spread on the thwart, weighted with a thermos and my spare mask strap, because Ines organizes chaos the way other people organize sock drawers. "Roof's gone," she said. "Collapsed in the eighties, or the nineties, nobody agrees. Nave is open to the sky, which is to say, to the water. Depth runs four to six meters over the floor. Font's on your left as you come in. The marker stone should be —" she tapped the journal's copied page — "here. Where the altar was."

"And if it's not," Coral said.

"Then your father lied about a church," Ines said, "which I refuse to believe. He lied about everything else, but not churches. He was sentimental about churches."

Something crossed Coral's face at that — the word *lied* doing its work — and Maren, who misses nothing on the water and pretends to miss everything on land, said, "Enough. Teo, you're first down. Coral, you're his second. Ines, you hold the boat. Nobody argues with the boat."

Nobody argues with the boat. That's the first commandment of the Salt & Anchor congregation, and I am its most faithful sinner.

* * *

I'll tell you what the chapel looks like, because I was the one who saw it first, and because describing it is easier than describing what happened in it.

You go down through water the color of strong tea — the Vell carries the peat from the moors, and down there it's amber, everything amber, like being inside a lamp — and the first thing you find is the doorway. The door itself rotted away decades ago, but the arch stands, granite, and swimming through it is the closest I will ever come to walking through a keyhole into another country. The light comes down in columns where the roof fell in, three or four of them, slanting, and the silt on the floor is pale and soft and holds every mark you make like snow.

The pews are still there. Black with weed, furred white in the shadows, but there, in rows, all facing the same direction, forty years of empty attendance. Fish live in them. Small ones, perch mostly, and they scatter when your light touches them and regroup behind you, so that you always feel followed in there, which you are, but only by fish, and only by the past, and those feel about the same.

The font stands on its column like a mushroom. The altar — the slab where the marker should be — is against the far wall under the biggest of the light columns, and it is, I will confess, a beautiful place. Terrible and beautiful. The two of them always travel together. I learned that from Edmund Underhill, and I was about to learn it a second time from his son, though neither of us knew it yet.

* * *

Here is what you need to know about me, so the rest makes sense: I am the one who goes first. Always have been. Maren steers, Ines thinks, Edmund — when he was alive — decided, and I went first. It's not bravery. Bravery is when you're afraid and you go anyway. I'm not afraid down there. That's the problem. The water is the one place my head goes quiet. Six brothers and sisters, a childhood of noise, and then the first time I put my face in the sea, silence, and I have been chasing that silence for forty years like a man chasing a woman who has made it very clear she'll come back when she comes back.

So I went first. Down through the arch, past the font, up the aisle between the drowned pews, my lantern beam swinging, the perch parting around me like a congregation making room.

The altar was there. The slab was there. And the marker stone was there — a flat piece of slate the size of a hymnal, and on it, cut deep, the Vell's old wayfinding mark: the spiral, the river's own signature, the one Edmund used on everything he ever charted. I'd know it blind. I have known it since I was nineteen years old and he pulled me out of a flooded adit in the Wheal Vorset and I threw up on his boots and he laughed and said, "There's your first survey, Teo. You just measured the depth of your own stupidity."

I got both hands on the stone. It was set into the slab — not loose, not resting, *set*, mortared or grown or something, and I want to tell you I thought about that, I want to tell you the sensible part of my brain said *Teo, a stone that doesn't want to come loose is a stone with an opinion*, but the sensible part of my brain has never been invited on a dive. I pulled.

The slab shifted.

Not much. A hand's width, maybe. Forty years of silt went up in a single brown bloom, and my light went from amber to nothing, and in the nothing I heard — felt, felt is the truer word — the far wall of the nave let go. Old mortar, old granite, a roof that had been holding its breath since the eighties, and I had just elbowed it in the ribs.

The wall came down the way old things come down: all at once and without malice. I didn't see it. You don't see the thing that hits you; you see the light go wrong, the water push, and then you're tumbling, and the slate I'd been pulling came free in my hand — I never let go of it, I want that recorded, I never let go — and something caught my ankle, a beam or a pew end or the long arm of Saint Brendan himself, and I was down, face in the silt, in the dark, in the church, with my air going out of me in bubbles I could hear but not follow.

And here is the part I will tell my grandchildren, if I ever get any, and if they ever ask, which children never do: I was not afraid. I was *embarrassed*. Forty years of diving and I was going to die in four meters of water in my grandmother's church, and my last thought was going to be Maren's face saying *I told you, Teo, you always end in the deep end*, and I was angry about that, furiously, stupidly angry, and I pulled at the thing on my ankle and it pulled back, and the dark got warmer, which is the water's oldest lie, and I thought, *ah. So this is it. Between tides after all.*

Then a hand closed on my harness and hauled, and the dark tore open.

* * *

I have been hauled out of water by professionals and by amateurs and once, memorably, by a dolphin, and I am here to tell you that there is a difference between pulling a man up and *deciding* he is coming up. The second one is what Coral Underhill did. He didn't check the wall, didn't check his own air, didn't do any of the arithmetic a careful man does. He measured nothing. For the first time since I'd met him, the man who measures everything did the one thing in the world that can't be measured, which is to reach into a collapsing dark place and take hold of another human being because taking hold of him was the only possible act in the universe.

He got me up through the light columns, through the arch, and Maren and Ines had me on the gunwale and then the floorboards, and I was coughing up the Vell, which tastes the way it sounds, like coins and moss, and Coral was kneeling on the thwart beside me with my sick on his sleeve and the marker stone in his fist, and he was shaking. Not me. Him. I was fine. I'm always fine; it's my defining feature and my doctor's chief complaint. But Coral's hands were trembling around that slate like it was alive.

"You went down," he said. "You went down and you didn't come up and I —" and then he stopped, because Coral Underhill would rather drown than finish a sentence about his own feelings.

"You hauled me out," I said. My voice came out like a hinge. "Without thinking."

"Someone had to."

"No," Maren said, from the tiller, not turning around. "Nobody had to. People let go, Teo. People calculate. I've watched them do it for fifty years on this water." She turned then, and her face was doing the thing it does, which is being a cliff. "He didn't calculate."

Ines said nothing. Ines was looking at Coral the way she looks at a document she's about to reclassify.

I sat up. I got my breath back — it comes back, breath always comes back, that's the deal, that's the whole covenant — and I looked at him, at this stranger with Edmund's jaw and none of Edmund's warmth, and I thought: *you don't even know what you just did. You don't know whose hands you just used.*

So I told him.

"Your father did exactly that for me," I said. "Nineteen seventy-eight. Wheal Vorset. I was a kid, first real job, showing off, went past the line into a flooded adit and the roof gave. Same as this. Same brown bloom, same dark, same thing on my ankle." I held up my left leg, the one with the scar I tell women is from a shark. "He came in after me with no line and no plan. Hauled me out by the harness, exactly like you did, exactly that grip, and then he sat on the rocks and shook for ten minutes while I threw up on his boots."

Coral was very still. "I didn't know that."

"Nobody knows anything about that man," Maren said to the water. "That was his whole religion."

"He talked about you," I said. "Every year. Every anniversary, we'd meet at the Salt and Anchor, and every year, Teo, he'd say, *is Teo still diving? Is Teo still going first?* And when we said yes he'd do this —" I did it, the small exhale through the nose, the almost-smile, Edmund's whole emotional range in one gesture — "and he'd say, *of course he is. He was born between tides.*"

Coral looked at the marker stone in his hands. The spiral cut into it. His father's signature. And I watched something happen on his face that I have watched happen to divers when they finally come up from a deep dive and the sky is still there, still blue, still waiting — the look of a man who had stopped believing in the surface and is being proven wrong.

"He left," Coral said. Quiet. Not an accusation. A fact, laid down like a stone on a chart.

"He left," I agreed. "And every year after, he came back. That's not the same as staying, I know. I've got six siblings; I'm an expert on the difference. But it's not the same as leaving, either. It's its own thing. It's the tide thing. You go out, you come back, and the shore doesn't stop being the shore just because the water's gone off it for a while."

"That's the stupidest metaphor I've ever heard," Coral said.

"It's a good metaphor."

"It's a *wet* metaphor."

"He's Edmund's, all right," Ines murmured, and there was something in her voice I'd never heard before, something almost soft, and Maren laughed, one short bark, and turned the boat upriver.

* * *

We ran on through the morning, past the drowned village's chimney tips standing out of the water like the fingers of people waving goodbye, and I sat in the bow and held the marker stone because Coral had finally surrendered it to Ines for cataloguing and my hands wanted the weight of something.

I kept thinking about the grip. His grip on my harness. Because here is the thing I haven't said yet, the thing I'm only saying now, to you, in the quiet after: it wasn't similar to Edmund's grip. It was the same grip. Same angle, same anchor-point, same *decision* in the wrist. And I don't believe in inheritance of the soul, I'm a Catholic, we believe in much stranger things than that — but I believe hands remember. I believe a boy who watched his father pull a drowning man out of a mine at seven years old filed that grip away somewhere below memory, in the place the body keeps its real archives, and that twenty years later, in a flooded church, the archive opened, and the hand knew what to do.

Courage isn't inherited. But the *shape* of it is. I'm sure of it. I've seen it twice now, in two Underhills, thirty years apart, and both times it arrived without thinking, without measuring, without permission.

We made the second gorge by noon, and that's when Ines, who had been silent over the charts for an hour, said the thing that ended the easy part of this journey.

"Coral," she said. "The marker stone. There's a second spiral under the first. Someone cut it recently — the granite's bright. And there's a letter."

"A letter?"

"A Greek letter. Lambda." She held the stone up to the light, and the fresh-cut mark caught the sun, and my stomach, which is a superstitious organ, went cold. "And I've seen this mark before. On the survey documents. It's Crane's site crew. They've already been here."

Coral took the stone and looked at it for a long time, and then he looked upriver, toward the gorge, toward the mines, toward whatever his father had left him at the end of forty miles of cold water.

"Then we're not following his map anymore," he said. "We're racing it."

Maren opened the throttle, and the Vell carried us upstream like it had opinions of its own.
