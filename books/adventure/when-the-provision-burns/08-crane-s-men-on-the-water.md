## 8. Crane's Men on the Water

I have been sailing the Vell estuary since before I could properly swim, which my mother called reckless and my father called tradition. Forty years of knowing every sandbar like a scar on my own palm, every channel that lies about its depth, every rock that shows its teeth only at half tide. So when I tell you that the morning the strangers came up the estuary, the water itself was wrong, you can take that as either superstition or forty years of data. I take it as both.

They came in two RIBs, black-hulled, new engines, running at a speed the estuary has no patience for. The Vell is a slow river. It meanders the last three miles to the sea the way old men walk to church—unhurried, certain of arrival. And these boats came cutting through it like knives through bread, throwing wakes against the mudbanks that would have gotten any local man a word from the harbourmaster and a cold shoulder from his wife for a month.

I was at the tiller of the *Kestrel*, my boat, my second boat if you count the one I buried, with Coral Underhill at the bow doing that thing he does—measuring the world with his eyes like the world owes him a receipt. Teo was amidships being seasick in the dignified way he manages it, which is to say silently and with great suffering, and Ines had the journal open on her knees under an oilcloth, because the morning was spitting rain and she guards that book like it's the last copy of anything.

"Company," Coral said. He didn't turn his head. He never turns his head at anything; he just announces it, like a lighthouse that talks.

I looked. Two RIBs, maybe six hundred yards back, running our line. Not our line exactly—running the channel, which is our line, because there's only one channel and the river knows it better than any of us.

"Well," I said. "They're keen."

"They've been keen since the pub," Ines said without looking up. "The site manager asked the landlord about us on Tuesday. I heard him through the wall. Very polite. Bought a round he didn't drink."

That was the thing about these people that set my teeth wrong, and I want to be precise about it, because I've been threatened before—poachers, customs men, a man from the council with a clipboard and the courage of ten. Threats I understand. What I didn't understand yet was the courtesy. The RIBs slowed as they came near. Not aggressive. Almost deferential. They pulled alongside at a distance you'd use for a funeral boat, and the man in the bow of the lead one raised a hand in greeting like a neighbour over a fence.

"Maren Tregose?" he called. He knew my name. I'd never seen him in my life, and he knew my name, and that is a specific kind of cold that goes down into the bilge of you.

"Depends who's asking and what they think they know."

"Quentin Hale. Site manager, Vell Valley Development Consortium." He held up a laminated folder the way a bishop holds a Bible. "I've got paperwork, if you'd rather do this properly."

"I'd rather you turn around and drown politely on your way home."

Coral turned then. First time. I watched him take the measure of Hale the way he'd take the measure of a river—width, depth, rate of flow, likelihood of flooding. Hale was fortyish, tidy, weathered just enough to look like he worked outdoors without actually doing it, and he had the smile of a man who has been told his smile works and has never once questioned the intelligence of the person who told him.

"Miss Tregose, I'm not here to make trouble." Hale's voice carried across the water easily, trained for it. "The consortium's acquired riparian access along the lower Vell. We're conducting a survey. I'd ask—politely—that you and your passengers not interfere with our work."

"Your work." I let that sit in the air like a gull over chips. "And what work is that, exactly? Because I've lived on this water forty years and nobody's bought it, because it's not for sale, because it's a *river*."

"The estuary's tideland, actually. And it was for sale. Everything's for sale, Miss Tregose. That's not cynicism. That's just the price list."

I'll give him this: he said it without malice. That's what frightened me. A man who hates you will eventually do something stupid. A man who doesn't hate you, who sees you as a line item, will do exactly the right thing every time, and the right thing for him is never the right thing for you.

Coral spoke. "You were in the cave."

Not a question. Hale's smile didn't move but something behind it did—a shutter, a ledger closing. "I'm sorry?"

"Porthvell cave. Low tide, three days ago. Size eleven boots, left foot worn on the outer edge, cigarette ends—American blend, filter pinched, which means you're a chewer not a smoker, you light them for something to do with your hands." Coral said it the way he says everything, flat as a sounding line. "You went in, you looked around, and you left before the tide came back. Which means you didn't find what my father left there. Which means you've been looking ever since."

The estuary made its small noises. Water on mud. A curlew somewhere up the marsh. Ines had gone very still under her oilcloth, and Teo had stopped being seasick in favour of being afraid, which I suppose is one cure for another.

Hale looked at Coral for a long moment, and I watched him decide something. I've watched men decide things on this water for forty years—whether to run the bar at half tide, whether to lie to the coastguard, whether to marry the girl or the boat. Hale decided to stop pretending, and the pretending came off him like a coat.

"Underhill," he said. "Coral Underhill. Yes, we know who you are. We know your father's name too, and we know what he was looking for, and Mr. Crane—" and here the name came out with the reverence people use for a church or a bank, which for Hale were probably the same building, "—Mr. Crane would very much prefer to do this with your cooperation. There's compensation available. Substantial compensation. The consortium values goodwill."

"The consortium," Ines said, emerging from her oilcloth like a wasp from a jam jar, "values the water rights of an entire valley, and goodwill is what it costs to take them."

Hale looked at her with something almost like appreciation. "Miss Reyes. The archivist. Yes. We know about you too." He said it kindly. That was the worst of it. He said everything kindly. "Look—I'll be straight with you, since straightness seems to be the local currency. We're going upriver. We'd rather you weren't in the way. If you're in the way, there will be injunctions, and the injunctions will be served by people with better lawyers and worse manners than me. Nobody needs that. Go home. Sail your boat. Let the river do what rivers do."

"And what's that?" I asked.

"Get owned," he said, and smiled, and I swear on my mother's grave the curlew stopped calling.

* * *

They didn't leave. That's the part I want on the record, because later there was talk of who provoked whom, and I'll not have it said I started anything. They didn't leave. They fell in behind us at a polite distance—two hundred yards, maybe, close enough to watch, far enough to claim they were minding their own survey—and they ran our line up the estuary like a shadow with an outboard.

And then the drone went up.

I heard it before I saw it, that wasp-whine that doesn't belong on water, and there it was against the grey: a little black cross rising off the second RIB, climbing, hovering, its camera eye—small, dark, patient—swinging toward us.

"They're photographing," Teo said. "Maren, they're photographing the boat."

"They're photographing the *journal*," Ines said, and her voice had gone thin and hard. "Coral, the page—you had it open at the marker notation—"

Coral was already moving. He had the journal out from under the oilcloth and I shouted at him—I did, I'll admit it, I shouted like a fishwife—"Put that away, you great fool, the whole river can read over your shoulder!" But he wasn't reading it. He was doing something to it, quick fingers, and I realized he'd turned to a page of tide tables. Old tide tables. The most boring document ever produced by the Admiralty. He held it open on his knee and looked up at the drone with an expression I can only describe as offering.

The drone hung there. The camera looked. Coral looked back. Somewhere in that little machine a man on a screen a mile away was deciding whether tide tables from 1998 were worth a closer look, and Coral Underhill, who trusts no one and nothing, was gambling the whole expedition on the answer being no.

The drone dropped. Lower. Twenty feet off the water, coming in along our port side, and Ines had her hand flat on the journal under the oilcloth and Teo was reaching for a boat hook and I was doing the arithmetic every skipper does—what can I do with this boat, right now, that changes the story—

And I did it. God forgive me, I did it without thinking, because the fog was there.

You have to understand about the fog on the Vell estuary. It doesn't roll in like fog does in films, all dramatic and cinematic. It *condenses*. It comes up off the water on a warm morning after a cold night, out of nowhere, a mile of clear air gone to milk in four minutes, and every skipper on this coast has a healthy fear of it and I have something better than fear. I have familiarity. I have forty years of sailing it blind, by sound and by the feel of the current on the rudder, by the smell of the mud off the eastern flats versus the salt off the bar. Other people's fog is a wall. Mine is a corridor.

The first wisp came off the water at the bend below Church Creek, and I put the tiller over and ran us straight into it.

"Maren—" Teo started.

"Sit down, Teo."

"Maren, the visibility—"

"*Sit down.*"

The milk took us. In three strokes of the engine we were in a white world with no up and no down and no RIBs and no drone, and behind me I heard the drone's whine climb, confused, its operator losing his toy in the soup. Coral closed the journal and stowed it. Ines let out a breath she'd been holding since the pub on Tuesday. And I sailed.

I won't pretend it was nothing. It was the finest piece of blind pilotage I've done since the wreck of the *Marigold*, and I say that with a full heart and shaking hands. I took us off the channel—because Hale knew the channel now, he'd have its chart, and the channel was the one thing he could follow—and I took us through the middle grounds where the water shoals from four fathoms to eighteen inches in the length of a boat, threading the sandbanks by the feel of them, by the way the current speaks against the hull, by a lifetime of listening to this river the way you listen to a friend talking in her sleep. Twice the keel kissed the mud and kissed it off again, soft as a cheek. Teo prayed. I heard him. He prays in Portuguese and I don't speak Portuguese but I know a man asking for a favour when I hear one.

We came out of the fog at the old oyster beds, four miles upriver and a world away, in air so suddenly clear it felt like being born. No RIBs. No drone. Just the river, brown and calm, and the drowned chapel tower standing out of the water ahead like a finger raised in warning or blessing—I've never decided which.

Nobody spoke for a while. Then Ines said, "They photographed something."

"They photographed tide tables," Coral said.

"They photographed *you holding something*. Hale doesn't need to read it. He needs to know you have it, and that you went to trouble to keep it from his camera, and that tells him everything the page would have." She said it gently, the way you tell someone a death. "Coral. They know the journal exists. They know you have it. And now they know it matters."

Coral looked at the water going by. I watched him from the tiller, this man built out of distances, and I saw him arrive somewhere he didn't want to be.

"Then we stop being careful," he said.

"That's what I was afraid you'd say," said Ines.

* * *

We put in at the weir pool below the old tin workings, under the alders, where the water runs dark and cold even in August, and we had a council. I use the word loosely. A council implies procedure. What we had was four people on a boat arguing in whispers like the alders might inform on us.

"They'll run the east bank path tonight," Coral said. "It's the fast route to the second marker. Hale's got the fitness and the lights for night travel and we don't."

"We've got a boat and he doesn't," I said. "The river's faster than any path, if you know it."

"You'd run the gorge at night?"

"I'd run the gorge in my sleep, and some nights I have." I looked at him and made my offer the way I make all my offers, flat and final, no refunds. "I've run this water since girlhood. Every lie it tells, I know the truth underneath. You want to get to your marker before Crane's men get to it, you don't need a map and you don't need a head start. You need me awake and you need my boat, and you've got both."

He looked at me a long moment. I want to say he was grateful. What he actually was, was recalculating—a man who'd planned to do everything alone discovering that alone was no longer one of the available options, and not entirely hating it, which I suspect frightened him more than Hale did.

"The fog," he said finally. "You lost them in fog that came up out of nowhere in four minutes on a morning with no forecast for it."

"River does that."

"The river doesn't do that. I've read the meteorology. Sea fret needs a temperature differential we didn't have." His eyes were on me, and they were his father's eyes, and for one horrible moment I was twenty years younger and Edmund Underhill was looking at me with that exact expression, that *I've caught the river in a lie* expression, and I understood where the son had learned it.

"Maren," he said. "Was that fog natural?"

I could have lied to him. I've lied to better men for worse reasons. But there's something about this Underhill breed that makes lying feel like throwing litter in church, so I told him the truth, or as much of it as I've ever been able to stand:

"The Vell looks after its own, boy. It always has. You ask me how and I'll tell you I don't know, and I'll be lying, because I know exactly how, and the knowing has kept me off this stretch of river after dark for thirty years." I looked up at the drowned tower, black against the sky. "Your father knew too. Ask him. Oh—" and the words came out of me before I could stop them, bitter and old, "—that's right. You can't."

The silence after that was the worst kind, the kind with an apology in it that nobody knows how to unwrap. Coral took it without flinching, which was somehow worse than flinching.

"Then we run the gorge tonight," he said. "All of us. Together."

* * *

We made ready through the afternoon. I won't catalogue it—lanterns, lines, the anchor lamp, Teo checking his dive kit with the devotion other men give to rosaries. But late in the day, with the light going long and gold on the water, Coral came and sat at the bow near me, which is my seat, and which I let him keep because I was busy pretending not to notice.

"That page they photographed," he said. "The tide tables. There was writing on the back of the sheet. In pencil. You can only see it at an angle."

"I know. I watched you turn it to the light in the pub."

"The pencil's not my father's hand." He said it quietly, to the water more than to me. "It's not anyone's hand I know. But the notation system—the way the depths are marked, the little crosses for the shoals—that's *mine*. That's how I mark charts. I've done it since I was fifteen. Somebody taught me." He was quiet a moment. "I don't remember teaching it to anyone."

The river went on being the river. A heron got up off the shallows and flapped away downstream, toward the sea, toward the fog, toward Hale and his polite men and his patient machine.

"Then either you've got a ghost," I said, "or somebody's been walking around in your handwriting, and I've known this river long enough to tell you both those things are possible, and I don't like either of them."

Coral looked at the journal in its oilcloth, and I saw his thumb move across the cover the way you'd touch a scar.

"Tonight, then," he said.

"Tonight," I said. And I put the *Kestrel*'s head upriver, into the gold light, toward the gorge, and behind us, far down the estuary, the fog was already gathering again—earlier than it should, thicker than it should, waiting for us like it had been paid to.
