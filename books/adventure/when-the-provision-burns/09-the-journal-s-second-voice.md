## 9. The Journal's Second Voice

The Salt & Anchor had a back room that smelled of lamp oil and forty years of pipe smoke soaked into the plaster, and it was there, at three in the morning, that I finally admitted the journal was lying to me.

Not lying exactly. Journals don't lie; they just tell you what their keeper wanted remembered. My father had been dead twenty years and he was still editing.

I'd claimed the corner table by the window because it faced the harbour, and because Ines had already claimed every other surface in the room with her archival kit — acid-free folders, cotton gloves, a magnifying loupe on a chain around her neck like some kind of scholarly crucifix. Teo was asleep sitting up in the armchair by the fire, boots still on, hands folded over his stomach like a man at his own wake. Maren had gone up to bed with the particular grunt she used for goodnight, which I was learning to translate as *don't be stupid*.

"You've been staring at that page for four hours," Ines said without looking up from her own work. She was cataloguing. Of course she was cataloguing. The woman had apparently spent two decades waiting for someone to bring her a primary source, and now that I had, she was going to love it to death.

"It's not adding up."

"Journals rarely do."

"The route does." I turned the journal toward her, keeping my finger on the page. "He marks the second waypoint here — the standing stone above the weir pool. But the distances are wrong. He says six miles from the chapel. It's nine. I've surveyed rivers for a living for fifteen years, Ines. I know what six miles looks like."

"Maybe your father couldn't count."

"He could count better than anyone I ever met." That came out sharper than I intended, and we both heard it. Ines set down her pen with the deliberate care of someone deciding not to take the bait.

"Then maybe," she said, "he wanted whoever read this to walk an extra three miles."

I hadn't considered that. I turned back to the page and felt the old familiar itch — the one I got when a map disagreed with the ground, when the numbers were too clean or too crooked, when something under the ink was trying to get out. It was the itch that had made me good at my job. It was also, I was beginning to suspect, the thing I'd inherited from him along with the handwriting.

Because that was the other problem. The handwriting.

I'd noticed it the first night in the cave and told myself grief was doing what grief does — making everything look like him. The journal was his, so of course the letters looked like his letters. The way the *f*s dropped below the line like they were drowning. The way he crossed his *t*s with a single impatient slash, left to right, like he was striking the word through even as he wrote it.

But tonight, with the lamp close and the paper dry, I could see there were two hands in this book.

Most of it was him. The main text, the route descriptions, the careful notes on water levels and rock types — all my father, all the man who'd taught me to read contour lines before I could read sentences. But threaded through it, in the margins, between lines, sometimes written sideways up the edge of the page like a vine climbing a wall, was a second script. Smaller. Faster. Corrections.

And the corrections were right.

That was what kept me up. Wherever the second hand appeared, the numbers changed — six miles became nine, a bearing shifted eleven degrees east, a note about a submerged passage gained a small precise annotation: *too narrow after autumn rains, go at low water only.* Every correction matched what I knew of the Vell's behaviour, matched the survey data Ines had pulled from county records, matched the landscape itself. The original hand had been wrong. The marginal hand had been there.

My father had not been working alone.

"Ines." I said it quietly enough not to wake Teo. "Look at this."

She came around the table, pulling her gloves on as she moved, and bent over the page. For a long moment she said nothing, which from Ines was its own kind of alarm.

"That's not his writing," she said finally.

"No."

She took the loupe off its chain and held it over the margin. "Different pressure. Different slant. Look how the corrections lean into the text — whoever wrote them was reading over his shoulder as he worked, or working beside him. This isn't a later annotation. These were made while the map was being drawn." She looked up at me. "Your father had a partner."

"A collaborator."

"Do you know who?"

And there it was. The question I'd been circling all night like water finding a drain.

Because I did know. I didn't know it in the part of me that makes decisions and signs invoices. I knew it lower down, in the place where you keep things you decided at fifteen never to think about again.

"Give me the book," I said.

Ines handed it over with the reluctance of a woman surrendering a rescued animal. I carried it to the lamp, tilted the page until the light raked across it sideways, and looked — properly looked — at the second hand for the first time.

Small letters, cramped, written fast because the writer was always afraid of being told to stop. A *g* made in one stroke instead of two, looped shut like a knot. An *r* that looked more like a *v*. And the numbers — the numbers were the worst. My father wrote his numerals upright, architectural, each digit standing at attention. The second hand slanted its numbers forward, eager, leaning into the future.

I used to write my numbers like that. Until I was seventeen, until the year he chose a hole in the ground over my graduation, until I trained myself out of it the way you train yourself out of any accent — deliberately, painfully, one letter at a time, until the new way became the only way and the old way became a stranger's voice.

"Coral?" Ines said. "You've gone white as the tide line."

"I drew this map."

Teo stirred in his chair. Neither of us looked at him.

"When I was fifteen. The summer of the dig." I heard my own voice come out flat, surveyor-flat, the tone I use for readings I don't like. "He used to bring me down for the school holidays. I thought I was helping. He'd sit me at the camp table with the field notes and have me plot the waypoints, correct his distances, work out the bearings. I thought—" I stopped. Started again. "I thought it meant something. That I was part of it. That he needed me."

"And then?" Ines asked gently.

"And then I went home in September and he stayed. And the letters got shorter. And then there weren't letters." I turned the page. More of my handwriting climbed the margins, fifteen years old and still alive on the paper, still leaning eagerly into a future that never happened. "He kept it. All these years, he kept the map I helped make, corrections and all. He carried my hand into that cave."

Ines sat down across from me. She didn't say anything for a moment, and I was grateful, because anything kind would have broken something I needed intact.

"Twenty years," she said at last, "and you never once wondered why the estate released a sealed item to you specifically?"

"I assumed it was legal obligation. Next of kin."

"There is no obligation. Executors can seal things forever if the will directs it. Your father directed it. He wanted this in your hands." She tapped the margin, one gloved finger resting on my own teenage handwriting. "And he wanted you to find this."

"Why? So I could feel this?" I gestured at myself, at whatever my face was doing. "So I could sit in a pub at three in the morning discovering I built half the thing that ruined my life?"

"So you could build the rest of it," Maren said from the doorway.

I hadn't heard her come down. She stood there in her coat, clearly having not gone to bed at all, holding two mugs of tea she'd presumably made for a version of this conversation she'd been expecting longer than I had.

"Maren—"

"Sit down and listen, boy. You too, Ines, since you're awake anyway." She set the mugs down, pushed one at me, and lowered herself onto the bench with the groan of a woman whose knees had opinions about stairs. "You want to know why Elias kept that map? Ask yourself why he sealed it. Not why he wrote it. Why he locked it away and made sure it reached you and no one else."

"Because he was sentimental," I said.

"Because he was frightened." Maren wrapped her hands around her mug. "We found things on that dig, Coral. Things Elias wouldn't show us. He got strange near the end — checking the locks twice, burning pages, sending Teo and me ahead on errands that didn't need doing. Whatever he'd found at the source, he didn't want it found again. Not by us. Not by anyone." Her eyes came up to mine. "Except maybe by the one person who'd understand it. Who'd helped draw the road to it. Who'd know the corrections were true because he'd been the one to make them."

"That doesn't explain why he left," I said, and my voice cracked on *left*, and I hated it. "If he wanted me involved, if he trusted me with the map, why did he choose the dig over—"

"Did he?"

The question landed harder than it should have. I stared at her.

"You were fifteen," Maren said. "What do you actually remember about that last summer? Not the story you've told yourself for twenty years. The actual days."

I opened my mouth to give her the story. The story was solid, load-bearing, twenty years of weight resting on it: he chose the river, he chose the hole in the ground, he chose the Provision or the glory or whatever it was over his son, and I had walked away first so it wouldn't matter as much when he walked away too.

But when I went looking for the actual days, they weren't where I'd left them.

I remembered arguing. I remembered shouting — me shouting, fifteen and furious about something. I remembered my mother's kitchen and a suitcase. I remembered telling him I wasn't coming back to Cornwall, ever, and I remembered—

I remembered him agreeing.

Not fighting. Not choosing. Agreeing. Saying, in that tired level voice he used for hard truths, *maybe that's best, Coral. Maybe you should stay away from this.* And me taking it as abandonment because at fifteen, agreement feels like betrayal, and it had taken me two decades to notice that a man who wanted rid of his son doesn't spend the next twenty years annotating a map in the hope the son will follow it.

"Oh," I said. It came out very small.

"Elias sent us away too," Teo said quietly. He was awake after all, had perhaps been awake the whole time, watching from his chair with those diver's eyes that saw everything underwater and most things above it. "After the source. He told us the expedition was finished, that we'd all nearly died for nothing, that it was time to go home. We thought he was ashamed. Maren didn't speak to him for three years." Teo leaned forward, elbows on knees. "He wasn't ashamed. He was protecting us. From what he'd touched down there. And I think — I think he was trying to protect you hardest of all, and it went wrong, the way protecting goes wrong."

I looked down at the journal. At my father's careful wrong numbers, and my own fifteen-year-old corrections climbing the margins like ivy, patient, persistent, waiting twenty years for someone to read them together.

He'd built the map to be solved by both of us. His errors, my fixes. A lock with two keys, separated by distance and silence, so that no one person — not Crane's men, not the executor, not even his own exhausted heart — could ever open it alone. And the key he'd mailed across twenty years of silence had arrived in my hands addressed, unmistakably, to the boy I'd been.

The rage didn't leave. I want to be honest about that. Rage doesn't dissolve just because guilt arrives; they braid, the way the journal said the river braided in flood season, two currents in one channel, and you carry both or you drown. I was furious at him for the years, furious at myself for the misreading, furious at the sheer elaborate patience of a dead man who'd rather engineer a puzzle than write a letter that said *I'm sorry, come home.*

But underneath the braid, cold and clear as spring water, something else was rising.

Because if the map needed two hands to finish — his errors, my corrections — then the route I'd deciphered in the cave was only half a route. The journal gave me the waypoints I'd verified at fifteen. But the last third of the journey, past the gorge, past the drowned village, up to the source itself — that section's margins were blank.

No. Not blank. I'd assumed blank. I flipped to the final pages now, heart going hard, and held them to the lamp.

The margins there weren't empty. They'd been scraped. Deliberately, carefully, with a blade — the paper thinned but not torn, ghost-letters pressed into the fibres, legible only when the light raked low across them. Someone had erased the final corrections and hidden them in the skin of the page itself.

I tilted the page. The lamp caught the ghosts.

Three words, in my own fifteen-year-old handwriting, pressed deep enough to survive the scraping:

*Ask Wren's father.*

Maren's mug stopped halfway to her mouth. Ines went very still. Outside, somewhere down the harbour, a boat knocked against its mooring in the pre-dawn swell, patient as a clock.

"Wren," I said. "Who in this valley is called Wren?"
