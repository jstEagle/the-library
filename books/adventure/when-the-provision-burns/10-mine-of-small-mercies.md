## 10. Mine of Small Mercies

I have catalogued eleven thousand documents in my career, and I can tell you this: the most dangerous thing in any archive is not fire or flood or the slow rot of damp. It is a gap. A missing page. An absence where something should be, and isn't. Gaps are where people hide things from themselves.

The mine was full of gaps.

We entered it at dawn through an adit half-swallowed by gorse, Maren going first with her lantern held high like a woman walking into church she didn't believe in but respected anyway. The tunnel mouth breathed cold air at us—old air, air that had been waiting down there since before the war, maybe longer—and Teo crossed himself, and Coral stood at the threshold with his surveyor's face on, all angles and arithmetic, and I thought: *here is a man who has made a discipline of gaps.*

"Bal East," Maren said, reading the rusted plate bolted beside the entrance. "My grandfather worked this level. Said you could hear the sea in the walls on a storm tide." She ducked under the lintel. "Come on, then. Standing in doorways is for ghosts."

The tunnel went in perhaps eight feet wide and then narrowed, and the lantern light turned everything the color of weak tea. Timber props every few yards, some rotted to lace, some still stubbornly holding up a hill that had been trying to lie down for two hundred years. Water somewhere below us, moving. That was the thing about these old tin workings—they were never really abandoned. They just changed employers. The water had taken the night shift.

Coral walked with his father's journal open against his forearm, the way he'd walked since Porthvell, as if the book might bolt if he set it down. He'd stopped speaking to us somewhere around mile six of yesterday's march, which by my count meant he'd been silent for approximately fourteen hours, and I had begun keeping notes on it the way I keep notes on everything: *Subject reverts to silence when observed. Subject's silence has texture. Subject's silence is load-bearing.*

"The marker should be where the levels fork," he said finally, without looking back. "According to the map."

"And according to the map," I said, "we're also supposed to trust a dead man's sense of direction."

"He was very good at directions."

"I know," I said. "I worked with him for nine years." Let him sit with that. He did—he glanced back once, something flickering across his face that wasn't quite pain and wasn't quite curiosity, and then he put his eyes forward again and we walked into the dark.

* * *

The first sign of Crane's men came forty minutes in, and it came as litter.

Teo found it—a cigarette end, crushed against a prop base, still faintly sweet when he held it under the lantern. Then another. Then a strip of orange flagging tape tied to a timber, the plastic kind surveyors use, crisp and new against wood gone black with age. Someone had been through here recently. Someone organized. Someone who marked their route because they intended to come back the same way.

"They're ahead of us," Maren said flatly.

"Weeks, maybe," Teo said, turning the tape over. "Look—the tape's bleached. Sun does that, but there's no sun down here. Time does it too, slower." He said it with the particular authority of a man whose entire profession was knowing what water and darkness do to objects over time. "Three weeks. Four."

"Four weeks ahead of us," Maren said, "and they've got lights that work properly and legs that haven't been climbing since Porthvell."

"We have the map," Coral said.

"We have half the map," I said, "and they have the other half, and both halves point at the same spring." I crouched by the flagging tape and did what I do, which is look at what everyone else walks past. The tape had been tied by someone right-handed, in a hurry, with a knot I recognized—Crane's site crews used a specific clove hitch variant; I'd seen it in the consortium's own published methodology papers, because Aldous Crane published his methodology the way other men bought yachts. And beneath the tape, scratched into the mud with a boot heel: a tally mark. One stroke. Part of a counting system.

They weren't just passing through. They were *surveying*. Measuring. Doing to this mine what Coral did to rivers—except their instrument was money and their unit of measurement was everything.

"There," Teo said suddenly, and his voice had gone strange.

His lantern was pointed at a side chamber off the main level, a drive that sloped down toward the sound of water, and in its mouth lay equipment. A coil of rope, modern, synthetic. A hard case, cracked open and empty. A helmet lamp, lens shattered, lying on its side like a dead beetle.

And boot prints. Many. Going in.

None coming out.

* * *

Maren wanted to go around it. Coral wanted to go through it fast, heads-down, no stops—"they're four weeks ahead, every hour matters"—and for about ninety seconds the two of them argued in low voices while Teo stood frozen at the chamber mouth with his lantern shaking slightly, and I looked past all three of them into the dark of that drive, and I listened.

You spend enough years in archives and you learn to listen to absences. This one was wrong. A working crew leaves noise behind them—echoes of themselves, dropped things, the residue of purpose. This chamber had none. It had the residue of *stopping*.

"Quiet," I said, and they quieted, because I'm told I have a voice for it. Nine years of shushing reading rooms will do that.

We went in single file, Maren first, and the drive descended in a series of shallow steps cut into the rock by men who'd been paid by the ton and hadn't wasted motion on elegance. The water sound grew. The air grew colder and wetter, and the lantern light found things: a discarded glove. A ration wrapper. Then a hard hat, sitting upright on a step, placed rather than dropped, and next to it—

"Oh," Teo said softly. "*Madre mía.*"

A man.

He was lying in a shallow alcove where the rock curved away from the drive, on his side, knees drawn up, and for one horrible moment I thought we'd found a body—four weeks is a long time in a cold wet hole, and I've read enough coroners' reports to know what neglect looks like even secondhand. But then the man shivered. A full-body shiver, teeth-first, and made a sound that wasn't a word but was unmistakably a complaint, and Teo was already moving toward him.

"Don't," Coral said.

It came out fast and flat, and it stopped all of us, including Teo, including me. Coral stood with the lantern light carving his face into planes, and he looked at the man on the ground the way he looked at rapids he'd decided not to run: assessing, categorizing, closing the account.

"He's one of Crane's," Coral said. "He's evidence they're ahead of us, which we already knew. He's not our problem. We don't have time, we don't have supplies to spare, and carrying a grown man through flooded workings will cost us half a day minimum. Half a day is the difference between reaching the source first and watching Crane's crew dig it out from under us." He said it cleanly, logically, each clause load-bearing, and it was all true, and I watched him say it and understood that I was watching a man recite his own architecture. *Loneliness shown as discipline.* I'd written that phrase in my notes two nights ago, in the pub, watching him refuse a third chair pulled up to our table. Some people build their isolation the way miners built these tunnels: deliberately, with good timber, to hold something up.

"Coral," I said.

"It's the correct call."

"It probably is." I crouched down by the man, because arguing standing up is for people who expect to win. Up close he was young—younger than Coral, maybe thirty, dark-haired, lips cracked, hands filthy, and there was a wrongness about his left leg, a way it lay, that told me everything I needed to know about why his crew had left him. Broken, or near enough. They'd propped him here with a hard hat for a pillow and given him their spare water, because Crane's people were professionals, and professionals abandon you *tidily*. "How long have you been here?" I asked him.

He focused on me with effort. "Dunno. Days. Three? Four?" His voice was gravel and rust. "Water ran out yesterday."

"Four days," I said, and looked up at Coral. "On a broken leg, in the dark, with four days of water. Your correct call has a corpse at the end of it. That's fine—that's a legitimate outcome, plenty of expeditions have produced worse—but let's at least be honest about what we're choosing. We're not choosing efficiency. We're choosing to be the kind of people who walk past a man dying in a hole because he's inconvenient." I kept my voice gentle, because shame delivered gently goes deeper. "Your father would have carried him."

I saw it land. I'm not proud of using it, but I'm not ashamed either—an archivist learns to use the tools at hand, and the truth was the tool closest. Coral's jaw tightened. For a long moment the only sound was the water talking to itself somewhere below, and the injured man's ragged breathing, and I thought he's going to turn and walk, he's going to choose the discipline, the architecture will hold—

Then Coral swore, quietly, in what sounded like Welsh, set down the journal, and knelt.

"Teo. Splint. Maren, what have we got for wrapping?" He was already pulling his pack off, already moving, and his voice had changed—not warmer exactly, but *occupied*, the voice of a man with a problem he could solve with his hands instead of his history. "Ines, get his name and anything he knows. If we're spending half a day on this, we're getting paid in information."

"Wren," the man croaked. "Danny Wren. Site survey, Crane consortium." He tried to laugh and it turned into a cough. "You're the Underhill lot. Boss said you'd come. Said—" another cough— "said the son always comes."

Coral's hands paused over the splint kit for exactly one second. Then they resumed, steady, and said nothing at all, and I wrote in the ledger of my mind: *gap noted.*

* * *

It took two hours to get Wren mobile, and it cost us more than time.

The splint was Teo's work—two lengths of broken prop timber and a torn shirt, bound with a skill he claimed came from a misspent youth diving wrecks off Vigo, where the salvage crews taught him that bones break in the same shapes whether they're in a body or a hull. Maren gave up her spare fleece without being asked. Coral gave up the argument, which for him was the larger currency.

Carrying him was the worst of it. The drives narrowed and the steps were cut for men with free hands and no conscience, and we rotated the burden—Teo and Maren taking the weight mostly, Coral taking turns with a grimness that suggested he'd decided to be angry at Wren personally, as a compromise between angering at himself and feeling nothing. Wren, to his credit, spent the whole ordeal apologizing in a low continuous mutter, like a man praying badly, and giving up information in installments, the way frightened men do when kindness confuses them.

"Boss's name is Halloway," he said, somewhere in the third level down, as we hauled him over a fallen timber. "Site manager. He's the one who left me. Not cruel about it—gave me the water, told me the pickup would come. It didn't." His breath caught as Maren adjusted her grip. "They found something in the east drive. Weeks back. Sealed door, old, stone. Halloway went quiet after that. Started sleeping down here. Started talking to himself—or not to himself, I couldn't tell. Then the map half arrived from London and everything sped up."

"The executor sold it," Coral said. Flat. Not a question. He'd done that math days ago, I think, and had simply refused to show his working until now.

"Paid through a shell company. Boss laughed about it. Said the old man's own lawyer sold the dead man's secrets cheap." Wren's head lolled against Teo's shoulder. "There's something else. Down past the sealed door. Boss won't say what. But the men who've been down there come back wrong. Quiet. One of them quit via the emergency fund and moved to Portugal. Another one keeps drawing spirals on everything. Napkins, his arms, the dust on the truck windscreens. Spirals within spirals." He shuddered, and it wasn't entirely the leg. "I drew one too. Before my leg. Don't remember doing it."

Nobody spoke for a while after that. The lantern light swung. Somewhere behind us, far back along the levels, water dripped with the patience of geological time, and I thought about archives again, about how the oldest documents are always the ones that change the reader. You don't consult them so much as submit to them.

We reached the fork where the marker should be as the last of the lamp batteries began to sicken, and there it was—his father's mark, chiseled into the living rock at eye height where the levels split: a river symbol, and beneath it, small, almost shy, a second line of characters that made Coral stop as if he'd walked into glass.

I looked. I'm an archivist; looking is my whole profession. The main glyph was Edmund Underhill's hand—I'd know it anywhere, that confident verticals-and-corners script I'd catalogued for nine years. But the smaller line beneath was different. Looser. Rounder. Younger.

It matched the marginalia. The corrections in the journal that Coral had gone silent over two nights ago, the second voice he'd pretended not to recognize.

"That's your handwriting," I said quietly, beside him. "From when you were fifteen."

He didn't deny it. He stood there with the lantern trembling faintly in his grip, reading his own fifteen-year-old self carved into a Cornish hillside, and whatever was happening behind his surveyor's face was happening at depth, under pressure, in the dark.

"Maren," he said at last, and his voice was rough. "Which fork?"

She checked the marks, checked her compass, checked the water sound. "Left. Down-slope. Toward the drowned chapel."

"Then we go left." He turned, and I saw him look back once—at Wren, wrapped in Maren's fleece, being carried by the family Coral hadn't asked for; at the marker with its two hands in the stone; at the dark behind us where a man named Halloway was presumably moving toward the same spring with the other half of the map and no mercy left to spend.

"Ines," Coral said.

"Yes?"

"When we catch them—" he paused, and I watched him decide to say the true thing instead of the efficient one, and it cost him visibly, like lifting timber— "don't let me be the one who decides who we leave behind. I'm not reliable on that question anymore."

I noted it in the ledger. *Gap beginning to close.*

"No promises," I said. "But I'll keep an eye on you."

We went left, down-slope, five of us now instead of four, slower by half a day and heavier by one broken surveyor who kept murmuring apologies to no one in particular—and somewhere above us, I would have sworn, the whole hill shifted its weight, listening, deciding what we were worth.
