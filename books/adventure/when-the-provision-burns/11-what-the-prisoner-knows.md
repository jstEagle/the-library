## 11. What the Prisoner Knows

Wren — that turned out to be his name, though it took him an hour and two cups of Maren's terrible camp tea to admit it — lay propped against the mine's timber shoring like a man who had been set there by someone in a hurry and forgotten. His left leg was splinted with a survey stake and what I suspected had once been somebody's spare shirt. Ines had done the splinting. She'd done it with the brisk tenderness of a woman cataloguing a damaged book: careful, unsentimental, already deciding where it belonged on the shelf.

He was maybe forty. Weathered in the way site men weather, which is to say not from weather at all but from standing around in high-visibility vests watching other people work. His hands were clean. That told me more than his face did.

"So," he said, when the tea had done its work and the fire had been fed down to a conspiratorial glow. "You're Underhill's boy."

"I'm nobody's boy."

"Sure." He shifted, winced, kept talking through it, because men like him would rather narrate their pain than sit inside it. "I helped carry your father's crates. Summer of— God, must be twenty years back. He paid us in pasties and stories. Best boss I never had."

The fire popped. Somewhere above us, water moved through the old workings with a sound like slow applause.

"You were Crane's man," I said. "In the cave. Your boots were in my father's cave."

"Our boots," he corrected mildly. "Four of us. And it wasn't a cave to us, it was a site. Crane had coordinates." He looked at me then, properly, and there was something in it that wasn't quite apology but was circling the airfield, waiting for clearance. "Your solicitor sold them to him."

"My father didn't have a solicitor."

"Everyone has a solicitor. Yours just happened to be named Pellworth, and he happened to have a daughter at university and a gambling habit, in whichever order you prefer." Wren shrugged as much as a man can shrug while splinted to a mine prop. "Crane bought the estate file. Letters, permits, half a map. The executor signed off on all of it. Clean transaction, dirty money."

I want to tell you I took this calmly. I want to report that I sat there with the dignity of a wayfinder, a professional man of rivers, absorbing betrayal the way granite absorbs rain. What actually happened is that I stood up too fast, cracked my head on a low beam, and spent a full thirty seconds swearing in three dialects while four people watched me with varying degrees of sympathy.

Maren handed me the tea again. "Sit down, love. The dead can't hear you being angry at them."

"That's rather the point of being angry at them," I said, and sat.

* * *

Here is what we knew by midnight, arranged in the order it arrived, because Ines insisted on order even in a flooded tin mine at one in the morning:

One. Aldous Crane's consortium had purchased the entire Vell valley under the fiction of a reservoir scheme. The reservoir was real enough to satisfy planners; the surveys underneath it marked not dam sites but the river's source, circled, annotated, starred.

Two. Crane possessed the western half of my father's route map, acquired through Pellworth along with everything else the estate contained. What he did not possess — what my father had apparently gone to considerable trouble to keep out of the estate file — was the eastern approach through the gorge, the markers, the key. That half existed only in the journal I'd pulled from the sea cave, wrapped in oilcloth against exactly the kind of tide that had opened the door.

Three. My father had known. That was the part I kept turning over like a stone with something alive under it. He had died twenty years ago — or rather, he had died last month, but he had *left* twenty years ago, and either way — and somewhere in those final years he had restructured his papers, split his own map in two, buried half of it in a cave behind a tide gate, and trusted no single living person with the whole. Not his expedition family. Not his executor. Not me.

"He was protecting it," Ines said quietly. She had the journal open across her knees, her reading glasses down on her nose, looking less like an archivist than like a priestess consulting scripture she didn't entirely believe in. "From everyone. Including us."

"Including especially you," Teo said. There was no bitterness in it. Teo didn't do bitterness; he did weather, and weather just moves through.

"Yes," Ines said. "Including especially me. I'd have found it in a week if he'd let me near the files, and I'd have—" She stopped. Adjusted her glasses. "I'd have wanted to use it. For the valley. For good reasons. But wanting is wanting."

Wren, from his corner, made a sound that might have been a laugh or might have been his ribs. "You lot talk about a piece of rock like it's a relative."

"It killed our friend," Maren said, without heat, the way you'd say *the sea takes men*. "That makes it family."

* * *

I sat up with Wren after the others bedded down, because I didn't trust him, and because not trusting him meant I wanted to know what else he was carrying. This is a flaw of mine. Maren says I collect information the way other men collect debts — compulsively, and always intending to settle up later.

"Tell me about the Provision," I said. "What Crane thinks it does."

Wren was quiet long enough that I thought he'd gone to sleep sitting up. Then: "You've read the journal. You know what it *is*. Lens of black glass, set in the living rock at the springhead, answers to whoever holds it. Old miners' story. Half the valley's got a version."

"What does Crane think it does?"

"He thinks it does paperwork." Wren smiled without any humor in it. "That's the thing about Aldous. He doesn't believe in magic. He believes in leverage. He's had lawyers look at the water rights for eighteen months. The Vell feeds six towns, forty farms, and a hydro scheme that keeps the lights on in two of them. All of it hangs off agreements written in the 1800s, all of them keyed to flow rates measured at the source." He tapped his temple. "You control the flow at the source — really control it, not engineer it, *control* it — and every one of those agreements becomes negotiable. Drought year? You turn it down. Farms fail. Land comes cheap. Flood year? You open it. Insurance collapses. Towns sell. He doesn't want to drown anyone, Mr. Underhill. He wants everyone to *feel* drowned, permanently, at a price they can't refuse."

"And the artifact gives him that."

"The surveys say the flow at the source doesn't obey the rain. There are years on record — the drought of '76, the flood of '89 — where the gauge readings don't match anything upstream or down. Like the river was being held back. Or let go." He looked at his clean hands. "We found the cave because the coordinates matched the journal references in the estate file. We didn't find the source because the map was half a map. So Crane sent us upriver to find the other half. Four of us went into the gorge three weeks ago." He gestured at his leg. "The gorge disagreed."

"What happened?"

"Rockfall. Old workings, rotten timber. Danny went through the floor. We got him out but he was—" Wren stopped. Started again, flatter. "He was hurt bad. Crane's protocol for injuries is a helicopter and an NDA. The helicopter came for Danny. Then it came back for the gear. It didn't come back for me. Compound fracture, fever, three days alone in the dark." He said it the way you'd read a shipping manifest. "So when your lot came through with a lantern, I'll confess I wasn't planning to be proud about it."

"Why tell us anything?" I asked. "Why not lie, stall, send us up the wrong fork?"

"Because I've seen what Crane does to people who bring him what he wants," Wren said. "They stop existing. Not dead — worse. They become *staff*. And because your archivist asked me my mother's name before she splinted my leg, and wrote it down, like it mattered." He closed his eyes. "Danny's mother's name mattered to nobody. I checked the incident report. One line. 'Subcontractor, non-fatal.' He lost two fingers."

The fire had burned to embers. Above us the mine dripped its patient count. I found I was gripping the journal hard enough to crease the cover, and made myself let go.

* * *

I didn't sleep. Instead I did what I always do when the world turns traitor: I measured things. The distance to the gorge mouth — eleven miles by the river path, seven by the ridge. The drop of the upper weir — four feet, passable at current flow. The hours of daylight remaining to us versus the hours Crane's crew had ahead. Numbers are honest. Numbers have never once pretended to be my friend in order to sell me something.

It was nearly dawn when I understood the thing that had been sitting under my ribs all night like swallowed ballast.

My father had split the map. He had hidden half of it behind a tide that opens perhaps twice a decade, in a cave the locals won't enter, wrapped in oilcloth, addressed to no one. And he had let the other half — the dangerous half, the half that leads anywhere — go into his estate, into the hands of a solicitor, into the open market. Why? A careless man's oversight? My father was many things, most of them absent, but he had never once been careless with a document. I grew up watching him index his own correspondence. The man alphabetized his regrets.

No. He'd put the western half where it could be found and bought, because a map that leads nowhere is a trap, and my father built traps the way other men build fences. Crane had spent eighteen months and a fortune chasing half a route through country designed to kill the unwary. Every mile Crane's crews walked upstream was a mile they weren't digging test bores at the source. Every dead end was a delay. My father, dead and dissolved and filed away, had been running a delaying action for twenty years.

And it had almost worked. It would have worked, except for Pellworth's gambling habit and a tide lower than any in living memory, and except for me — because I was the one who walked into the cave and carried the other half out.

"You realize," Ines said behind me, making me jump badly enough to justify whatever she'd witnessed, "that you've been doing sums aloud for an hour."

"I have not."

"'Eleven miles, minus the ford, plus the ridge, carry the gorge,'" she quoted. "It's very soothing. Like a lullaby for accountants." She settled onto the rock beside me and looked out at the grey beginning of morning filtering down the shaft. "You've worked it out. About the map."

"He baited the trap with half of it."

"He did." She was quiet a moment. "Coral. If he trusted no one with the whole of it — not me, not Maren, not Teo, twenty years of us — why leave half of it where *you* would find it? Because that's what the cave was. The tide gate, the oilcloth, the journal addressed in his hand. That was a letter. He wrote it to you."

"He wrote it to whoever found it."

"Coral." Gently, the way you'd correct a child's arithmetic. "He hid it beneath Porthvell. Twelve miles from the house where you grew up. On a coast you've mapped twice for clients who never knew you'd been here before. He knew you'd come home eventually. Men like you always come back to the water they started from." She stood, brushed off her skirts, and paused. "The question isn't whether he trusted you. It's whether you're going to trust anybody back, before Crane finishes the arithmetic you've been doing all night."

She left me with that. Archivists are dangerous people. They know exactly which page to leave open.

* * *

We broke camp at first light, which in a tin mine means when Maren said so. Wren rode on a stretcher-litter rigged from pack frames, Teo and I taking the front handles, and I will note for the record that I did not complain, because complaining in front of Maren is like complaining in front of the tide.

We were a quarter mile from the adit when Wren said, from his horizontal vantage, "There's something else. Since we're being honest with each other now."

"Mm."

"Crane's not coming up the west fork anymore. He stopped three days ago. Pulled the crews out." Wren's voice had gone carefully neutral, the tone of a man delivering news he's rehearsed. "Because he doesn't need the map anymore. He bought something else. A guide."

The litter handles went slick under my grip.

"Who?" Maren asked, flat.

"A local. Older fellow. Knows the gorge, knows the markers, knows the old chapel route. Came to Crane's office in Truro a fortnight back, name of—" Wren frowned, retrieving it. "Tregose. Josiah Tregose. Says he worked a dig on the Vell years ago. Says he owes the Underhill family a debt and intends to pay it in the worst possible currency."

I stopped walking. The litter swayed and Teo steadied it, and for a moment none of us moved, and the river below us ran on toward the sea, indifferent, carrying its cargo of light.

I knew the name. Of course I knew the name. Everyone who had ever sat at my father's table knew Josiah Tregose — the expedition's first sponsor, the man whose money had opened the valley in the first place, the man my father had cut out of the project and out of history both, and who had spent twenty years, presumably, remembering why.

"He's leading them up the east fork," Wren said. "The true fork. Crane's already at the second marker. By tomorrow night, Mr. Underhill, Aldous Crane stands at your father's spring."

Above us, far up the gorge, a sound rolled down through the mist — not thunder, though the sky was clear. Deeper. Older. Like something vast turning over in its sleep beneath the hill.

Teo crossed himself. Maren spat over the edge of the path, an old boatwoman's habit, and said what all of us were thinking:

"We've got a day. Maybe less. Someone had better start running."
