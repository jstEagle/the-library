## 12. Night on the Weir

The river turned against us an hour before dark, and it did it politely, the way bad news always arrives in Cornwall — a note under the door, a rise of six inches where there had been four.

I'd known the weir at Trelow since I was nine years old, when my father poled me over it in a flat-bottomed punt with my boots on the wrong feet. It's a low stone lip across the Vell, built by monks or smugglers or somebody who trusted stone more than God, and in a dry summer you could walk across it without wetting your laces. In a wet autumn it made a white collar across the whole river, and below it the water pooled deep and slow for a quarter mile before the gorge took it back.

We came down to it at dusk with the rain already in our collars and found the collar of white a foot higher than any of us liked.

"Sluices," Teo said, and spat over the side, which is his way of praying.

"Sluices," I agreed, because there was no point pretending otherwise. Somebody upstream had opened the weirs above us, and all that water was coming down the Vell like a debt collector. We couldn't shoot the weir in this — the stopper below it would hold a boat underwater until it got bored — and we couldn't climb out here, not with the gear, not in the dark, not with Ines's ribs the way they were.

So we did what river people have always done. We backed the *Kestrel* into the eddy under the alder bank, tied her to two trees and a boulder because one tree is a rumor and two are a promise, and we waited for the night to make its decision about us.

Coral helped with the lines. I'll say that for him. He'd been with us nine days by then and he still moved like a man in a railway station — everything packed, everything ready, one foot already on the platform — but when I threw him the second painter he caught it and made it fast without being told, and his knots were his father's knots, and I pretended not to notice that.

Ines got a fire going under the tarp, small and disciplined, the way she does everything. Teo got the kettle going. Coral got himself to the far end of the boat and sat with his father's journal open on his knees, reading it by firelight like a man reading a letter from a lawyer.

I let him have an hour of that. Then I took the kettle off him.

"You'll read it to death," I said. "It's paper, not an oar."

He looked up. He has his father's eyes, which is a cruelty I've never gotten used to — the same grey-green, the same way of looking at you slightly past your shoulder as if the interesting thing might arrive from behind. "I'm reading it properly this time. There's a second voice in the margins. I thought it was his editor."

"And?"

"And it's mine." He said it flat, no drama in it, which is how you knew it was costing him. "I helped draw this map. I was fifteen. I don't remember doing it."

The fire popped. Teo crossed himself with the poker, which is not a sanctioned use of a poker but Teo answers to a higher and stranger authority than the Church of England.

"You remember what you want to remember," I said, and sat down across from him, because it was going to be that kind of night, and a rising river is as good a confessional as any. "That's not a flaw. That's the design."

* * *

The rain kept on. The river kept on. By ten o'clock the water had come up over the shingle and was licking at the alder roots, and the weir's white collar had gone from a foot high to eighteen inches, and the sound of it had changed from a whisper to a sermon.

We ate. Ines dealt cards and lost spectacularly and accused the deck of conspiracy, which it may well have been guilty of; Teo won every hand and gave the winnings back, because he says money that comes easy leaves easy and takes something with it. Coral watched us the way he watched everything — like a surveyor, measuring the distance between himself and the rest of us and recording it in some ledger nobody else could see.

It was Teo who broke first, the way Teo always breaks first, gently and without meaning to.

"Your father," he said, "once carried me out of water colder than this."

Coral's cards stopped moving. "Did he."

"Cold enough that my hands stopped being hands. Just hooks. We were under the ice shelf at the old quarry pool — this was before your time, before everything — and I went down to cut a snagged line and the line took my ankle instead. And Emory—" Teo paused on the name, the way we all still did, "—Emory went in after me without a word. Didn't shout, didn't take his coat off, didn't do anything sensible. Just went in. And on the way up, with me half-dead on his back, he stopped. Underwater. Stopped, and looked at something in the dark, and then kept going."

"Something," Coral said.

"A light. Or what I thought was a light. I was half-frozen, I saw all sorts of things that day." Teo shrugged, and the shrug didn't fit right on him. "But I've thought about it since. About how he looked. Like a man hearing his own name called from a house he'd sworn he'd left."

Coral looked at me. I poured more tea, which is what I do when a conversation arrives that I'd been dreading for twenty years.

"Maren," he said.

"He'd want you to hear it from all of us or none of us," I said. "So we'll do it properly. Ines, you first. You were there at the beginning."

Ines set her cards down — she never sets her cards down — and pulled her coat tighter, and told it the way she tells everything, precisely, with the dates correct.

* * *

"We were twenty-two," Ines said. "The four of us and Emory's first grant and a secondhand survey boat he renamed after a mistake he'd made on it. We spent three summers on the Vell. Mapping, mostly. Emory had this theory that the river had been rerouted in the medieval period — that there'd been an older source, a spring the monks called the *provisio*, because it never failed. Drought years, flood years, it gave the same. The whole valley's agriculture was built on it. The tithe records call it the Provision because the monks were literal-minded men and bad at branding."

"And he found it," Coral said. It wasn't a question.

"He found the way to it," Ines said. "The last chamber, the one past the second marker. We got as far as the threshold. And Emory went in alone, because Emory was Emory, and he was gone for four hours, and when he came out he was—" She stopped. Ines never stops. "He was different. Not hurt. Not frightened. *Relieved*, in a way I couldn't understand then."

"Tell it straight," I said, because she was going to soften it, and Coral deserved the unsoftened version, and because saying it out loud after twenty years was going to hurt either way and I'd rather it hurt clean.

Ines nodded, and told it straight.

"He said the chamber was full of water that moved wrong. Slow spirals, all turning the same direction, like the river was thinking. And in the middle of it, set into the living rock, a lens of black glass. He said when he looked into it, it looked back, and what it showed him was everything he had ever wanted fixed. His whole life, laid out and mended. And he said — these are his words, I wrote them down that night, I still have the notebook — he said, *'It doesn't offer. It asks. It asks you to stay and hold it and be the reason the valley never thirsts, and it will pay you in exactly that, forever, and take everything else in rent.'*"

The fire had gone quiet. Even the river seemed to be listening, which rivers don't do.

"He touched it?" Coral asked. His voice was very even. Even is worse than loud, with Coral.

"He touched it," I said. "One hand, flat on the glass, the way you'd steady yourself on a doorframe. And he said the moment he did, he felt it *settle* into him. Like a weight being handed over. Like a job being offered. And he felt — this is the part I want you to hear properly, boy — he felt how much he wanted to say yes."

Coral said nothing.

"He came out of that chamber and he sat on the bank where you're sitting now — no, not where you're sitting, don't be sentimental, over there by the alder — and he told us he was leaving the expedition. That night. No discussion. Ines cried, Teo took it as a betrayal, and I told him he was a coward, because I was twenty-five and knew everything." I fed a branch to the fire. "He didn't argue. That's what I remember most. Twenty years of us calling him a coward and a quitter, and he never once argued. He just said, 'You'd have to feel it to understand, and I hope none of you ever do,' and he walked down the valley in the rain."

"And then he had me," Coral said, "and then he left me too."

The words came out flat and hard, like stones set down carefully so they don't crack the ice.

"Yes," I said, because lying to him would have been a poor return on the truth. "And that part I won't dress up. He chose the fear over the family. He thought he was protecting you from something. Whether he was right—" I spread my hands. "I've had twenty years to decide, and I still don't know."

Teo leaned forward. "But understand what he was afraid *of*, Coral. Not the glass. Not the power. He was afraid that he'd say yes. That's all. He looked at the thing that could give him everything and he saw, clear as a chart, that he wanted it — and he did not trust himself to refuse it forever. So he took himself out of the valley like a man taking himself out of a gambling house. And every year after, on the anniversary, he came back to the Salt & Anchor and sat in the corner and bought the round and never explained, and we let him, because we understood that the round *was* the explanation."

"He never told me any of this."

"He never told anyone," Ines said. "He told us the river, and he told us the route, and he told us where not to step. He kept the rest the way he kept everything — in a waterproof box, under a tide that only opens once in a generation." She looked at the journal on Coral's knees. "Until now. Until you."

* * *

The river made its decision around midnight.

It came up another foot, fast, and the eddy we were moored in began to fill with debris — branches, a dead sheep, somebody's fence rail — and Teo and I worked the lines in the rain while Ines held the lantern and Coral, without being asked twice, took the third position and kept the boat off the alder roots with a pole, exactly where a third pair of hands belongs. He didn't say anything while he did it. He didn't have to. Nine days of railway-station feet, and there he was at two in the morning, holding a boat in a flood like he'd been born to the crew, because in every way that mattered he had been.

After, soaked, we crowded back under the tarp, and he sat where he'd sat before, but differently. Closer to the fire. Closer to us.

"My mother used to say he was married to the river," Coral said, after a while. "That I came second. I built twenty years on that."

"Your mother wasn't wrong," I said. "She just didn't know what the marriage was for. He wasn't in love with the water, boy. He was keeping a vow to it. There's a difference, and it's the whole difference."

Coral turned his father's journal over in his hands. "The last pages. The ones I haven't been able to read — the ink's run where the water got in." He looked up, and for once he looked straight at me instead of past my shoulder. "If we reach the source. If it's real. What do I do?"

"Nothing," Ines said. "You look at it and you walk away. That's the whole of the inheritance he left you."

"And if walking away isn't on the menu?" Coral asked. "Crane's men are two days behind us and gaining, and the map's half theirs already. What if the only choice is who holds it?"

The rain came harder. Below us, the weir roared like a congregation that had finally been given something to shout about.

"Then you hold it the way your father held it," I said. "With one hand, flat, the way you'd steady yourself on a doorframe. And then you take yourself out of the gambling house."

He nodded slowly, and I thought that was the end of it, and I was wrong, because Teo — who had been quiet too long, which is how you know something is building in him — suddenly sat up straight and said:

"The lantern."

We all looked at him. Teo pointed downriver, toward the gorge, where the water ran black and loud.

"There's a light in the gorge," he said. "Moving against the current. Coming upstream."

I looked. He was right. A white point of light, bobbing where no boat should be, climbing the Vell in the dark and the flood — steady, patient, unhurried, like something that knew exactly where we were moored and saw no reason to rush.

Ines snuffed the fire with one practiced motion. I put my hand on the painter. Coral closed the journal.

"Well," I said, as the light came on, "let's see who's out fishing at this hour."
