## 13. The Drowned Bell Rings

I have been diving since I was eleven years old, and in all that time I have learned exactly three things worth passing on: the water is always older than you, the water does not care that you are cold, and the water keeps every promise it makes — just not always to you.

My grandmother taught me the first one. The sea taught me the other two.

So when we came down into the gorge at Lower Vell and I saw the chapel roof breaking the surface like a whale's back, black slate slick with forty years of river, I did what I always do before a dive. I said thank you. Out loud. To the water.

Maren didn't laugh. That's how I knew she was worried. Maren laughs at everything, especially the things that frighten her — it's her way of arm-wrestling the world without getting out of the boat. But she stood at the tiller with her jaw set like a cleat and watched the current curl around the bell tower, and she said nothing at all.

"It's not deep," Coral said. He was crouched at the bow with the journal open on his knees, protected from spray by his own hunched body the way a man shelters a match. "The village drowned in '79. Twelve feet over the churchyard, maybe less now with the summer drawdown."

"Twelve feet is plenty," Ines said. She had her notebook out, because Ines has a notebook for everything, including, I am fairly sure, a notebook about her notebooks. "The marker stone your father described is set into the chapel wall below the east window. Underwater."

"Underwater," Coral repeated, flat, and looked at me.

I was already pulling on my wetsuit. It's an old suit. I've patched it more times than I've patched my relationship with my sister, and both repairs hold about equally well. "The water wants something," I told them, which is what I say, and they let me say it, because by now they know it isn't superstition so much as accounting. You go into the river owing it something. Best to decide beforehand what you're willing to pay.

"And what does it want today, Teo?" Maren asked, only half mocking.

"Today?" I spat over the side, which is polite where I come from. "Today it wants to be asked nicely."

* * *

Lower Vell was a village once. You can read its whole life in the fifteen minutes it takes to row across it. There's the millstone lying in the shallows like a dropped coin. There are the garden walls, running straight down into green murk, and if the light is right you can see the roses gone to skeleton. There's the lane where children walked to school, and there is the school itself, doors still shut, as if the whole place had simply stepped outside for a moment in 1979 and the reservoir company locked up behind them.

They flooded it for water. That's the joke nobody laughs at anymore — the valley that spent two generations being promised the river would save it, drowned on purpose by men with surveying equipment and a parliamentary act. My father helped build the dam. He never once swam in the water behind it. He said it was rude to swim in a graveyard, and he was a man who thought drowning was rude generally, which is why God took him doing something else entirely, falling off a roof in Falmouth like an ordinary idiot.

The chapel is the deepest thing left standing. St. Brendan's, they called it, though Brendan never came within a hundred miles; the name was borrowed the way Cornish names borrow everything, hopefully and without apology. Its bell still hangs in the tower. They were supposed to take it out before the flooding — there are photographs, Ines found them, of workmen with ropes and a lorry — but the story goes that the night before they came for it, the bell rang by itself, three times, and the workmen refused to touch it, and the parish, which had already lost its pews and its font and its dead (they moved the dead; even the dead got better terms than the living), let the bell stay as a kind of severance.

Superstition, obviously. Bells don't ring themselves.

But I have dived that tower twice in my life, and both times, hanging in the dark under the frame with my torch beam swinging, I have felt the way you feel in a room where someone has just stopped talking.

* * *

We anchored above the churchyard, or near enough — Maren doesn't anchor over graves, and none of us argued. The gorge walls rose on either side, grey-green granite furred with moss, close enough that you could spit across the gap and hit history. The river ran slow here, deep and tea-dark, holding its breath.

Coral stripped to his shirt and started rigging a line for me without being asked. He's good with rope. He's good with everything except people, and even there I think it's less that he's bad than that he's rationed — like a man who grew up in a drought and can't stop measuring every cupful.

"I'll go down with you," he said.

"You'll do no such thing." I checked my mask strap. "You dive like a man doing arithmetic. You'll kick up the silt and I'll be blind in my own grave."

"Then tell me what to look for."

"The east window. Below the sill there should be a stone with a mark cut into it — a spiral, maybe, or a V. Your father liked spirals. Everything with him was either a spiral or a straight line, nothing in between." I held out my hand for the weight belt. "You watch the surface. You count my bubbles. If the counting stops, you shout for Maren, and Maren does whatever mad thing occurs to her, and somehow we all live. That's the system. It's worked for thirty years."

He almost smiled. Almost. With Coral it's like watching a door open a crack onto a lit room and then someone inside remembers themselves.

Ines pressed a waterproof sleeve into my hand — a plastic bag, really, sealed twice, big enough for the journal. "If the marker tells us anything," she said, "and you can read it down there, memorize it. Don't write underwater. Last man who tried wrote 'the stone says turn back' and we lost a week deciding whether that was a direction or a warning."

"What did you decide?"

"We're still deciding."

* * *

The water took me the way it always does, all at once and gently, like a hand closing over your face.

Cold first. Then the green. Then the sound — that great soft heartbeat the river makes in its chest, the one you can't hear from air, the one that means you're somewhere the world wasn't built for. I equalized my ears and dropped past the garden wall, past the rose skeletons, down the lane. A jackdaw — no, wrong depth, a perch, fat and silver-bellied — escorted me the length of the street like a bored policeman.

Twelve feet. Coral was right, it wasn't deep. Depth was never the problem. The problem with places like this is that they're shallow enough to see clearly, and what you see clearly stays with you. A child's boot, lace still tied, standing upright in a doorway of silt. A clock on a mantelpiece through a window, hands stopped at ten past four forever, because whoever owned it wound it one last time and then walked out and let the water have the time.

I reached the chapel. It stood better than anything else in the village — granite doesn't quit — but the river has been working at it for forty years the way patience works at anything, and the east wall leaned inward like a listener.

And there, below the window, exactly where a stubborn dead cartographer had promised: the stone.

It was cut with a spiral. Of course it was. And around the spiral, newer, sharper, cut with a chisel rather than a mason's tool, a second mark — a line through it, crossed out, amended. Someone had come here after the flooding. Someone had stood in the dark with tools and corrected the map.

I put my glove against it. Cold came up through the leather like a voice.

That's when the bell rang.

* * *

I want to be precise about this, because afterward everyone wanted precision from me and I kept failing to give it, so let me give it here, once, properly.

It was not a full peal. It was not the great bronze voice the bell must have had on Sundays, calling the village up the lane. It was a single stroke, deep and dull, the sound a bell makes when something heavy brushes it in the dark — a fish, a branch, a diver's tank. I know that sound. I have made that sound myself, twice, in towers I shouldn't have entered.

But the water carried it strangely. It came up through my chest before it came through my ears, and for one full second — I counted it later, one second, maybe less — every hair on my body stood up and informed me that the dead of Lower Vell had noticed me.

Then the second stroke came, and the third, and they weren't brushing-strokes. They were hard. Rhythmic. And the water around me began, very quietly, to move.

Not the way water moves. Water eddies, water breathes. This was directional. This was a shove. The whole river had turned its head and decided to go somewhere, and I was standing in the doorway.

I kicked for the surface and the current kicked back.

* * *

Here is what I understood in the next ninety seconds, in the wordless grammar of the body, while my lungs began composing their complaints: the river upstream had been opened. Somewhere above us, gates that had been closed for decades were dragging up, and all the water the valley had been saving was coming down the gorge at once, and Maren — Maren would already know, because Maren reads water the way Ines reads documents, at a glance and with rising alarm — Maren would be trying to get the boat to me, and the boat could not come to me through this.

The current had me by the ankles. I went sideways along the chapel wall, past the window, into the lee of the tower. The bell swung above me in the dark, catching my torchlight in dull bronze flashes, ringing its broken-hearted single notes each time the surge shoved it. Not haunted. Just honest. The water was telling the truth through it, the way water always does: something is coming.

The marker stone. I had one job and it was a stone.

I went back along the wall against the current, which is a sentence I never want to write again, hand over hand on the leaning masonry, boots finding footholds in forty years of mortar loss. The spiral. The amendment. I put my face close to it and made myself look, really look, the way Ines looks, taking it in not as a shape but as information.

The spiral was crossed out. Through it, cut clean and recent, ran a straight line pointing up-gorge — and beside it, small, precise, unmistakable even underwater, even to me, a letter.

A **V**.

Vell. Or a V for verification. Or — and this arrived in my head with the cold certainty of a bubble reaching the surface — a V drawn by a man who knew his son would someday follow this map, and who wanted to leave him a signature instead of instructions. Fathers do that. They leave their names where their advice should be.

I tore the plastic sleeve from my belt and — no. Nothing to put in it yet. The stone stayed. The knowledge came with me.

The current was stronger now. It had found its full voice, a long roar coming down the gorge like a train entering a tunnel, and the silt was lifting off the churchyard in clouds, and my bubbles were going sideways instead of up, which is the last thing a diver ever wants his bubbles to do.

I swam for the light.

* * *

I surfaced into chaos, which is the family business.

The gorge had become a throat. Water stood in standing waves between the walls, brown and muscular, carrying branches, a fence post, half a henhouse door. Our boat was fifty yards downstream and being held there by Maren's skill and fury in equal measure, the engine screaming against the surge, Ines hauling on a line, Coral —

Coral was in the water.

He'd tied the line around his own waist — of course he had, the arithmetic man, calculating reach versus current and disliking the answer — and he was coming toward me hand over hand along the rope, and the rope was singing, and the gap between us was closing too slowly.

I got an arm up. He caught it. For a moment we were just two idiots holding on in a flood, and I shouted the only thing that mattered:

"The stone! It's marked! A line and a V — it's him, Coral, it's his hand!"

Something went through his face that the water had no part in. And then his eyes went past me, down, to the strap at my shoulder where the plastic sleeve hung empty, and I saw him understand what I had not yet understood myself.

The journal. His father's journal. I had clipped it to my belt before the dive, because it was the one thing on that boat that could not be allowed to drown twice.

The belt strap was torn. The sleeve was gone.

"No," Coral said. It wasn't a word so much as a door slamming.

I looked downstream. The flood was carrying everything toward the weir at the gorge's mouth — branches, silt, the henhouse door spinning like a playing card. And there, low in the brown water, turning end over end, a pale rectangle catching the grey light. Sealed twice. Ines's best work. Drifting away from us at the speed of everything.

Coral's grip crushed my forearm. I heard him do the arithmetic — the rope, the current, the distance, the odds — and I heard the answer come back unacceptable, and I watched him start to untie himself anyway, because that is who he is, a man who will spend himself on a thing rather than admit a thing is spent.

So I did it first.

I'll be honest, because Teo lies about many things but never about the water: I didn't think. Thinking is for people with time. I unclipped his line, shoved him toward the boat with both hands, and dove.

* * *

The underwater world of Lower Vell, in flood, is a rumor of the world, all brown motion and roaring silence. I chased the white flicker of the sleeve through it by faith mostly, and by the fact that the journal was heavier than water and sinking as it went, settling toward the drowned lane, toward the schoolhouse doors, toward the mantelpiece clock that had stopped at ten past four in 1979 and saw no reason to start again.

I caught it six feet down, in front of a garden gate.

And then the river collected its fee.

The current took me the way it takes everything it's owed — thoroughly, and without malice, which is worse. It rolled me. It drove me down the lane, past the school, past the boot with its tied lace, and slammed me into the chapel corner hard enough that the world went white at the edges. My regulator tore loose. My mask filled. My weight belt, old friend, faithful belt, chose that moment to jam, and I hung in the flood like a bell in its frame, swinging, making my own dull note against the stone that no one living heard.

I have been drowning four times in my life. I could write you a pamphlet. The strange part is never the fear; the fear is brief and businesslike. The strange part is how calm the negotiations become. *Not yet,* I told the water. *I'm carrying something.*

Whether the water agreed, or whether my fingers finally beat the buckle, I couldn't tell you. But the belt released, the river took its toll of lead, and I rose — slowly, crookedly, like something surfacing after a long agreement — into noise and grey air and Maren's voice, which is the finest sound in Cornwall and possibly the world, swearing at me with total love.

Hands. Boat floor. Coral's shirt wrung in my fist, his face an inch away, saying my name over and over like a man testing whether a bell still rings.

The journal lay between us on the boards, sealed, sodden at one corner, intact.

"Coral," I coughed, and grinned at him with blood in my mouth, because a little drama is good for the young. "Your father writes a very legible hand. Even underwater."

* * *

We grounded at the old quarry slip below the gorge, shaking, laughing the way people laugh when the alternative is sitting very still. Maren poured brandy into all of us from a bottle she claims is medicinal, and Ines held the journal over the stove flame in turns, drying pages like a nurse with newborns.

It was Ines who found it, of course. She always finds things. She went quiet in the middle of page forty-something, and then she turned the book around and showed us the margin, where a second ink — faded, hurried, pressed hard enough to emboss — had been written over the older map notations:

*"If the sluices are opened against you, it is Crane. Do not race the river. Go high. — E.U."*

E.U. We all looked at Coral. Coral looked at the initials for a long time, and then at the flood-swollen gorge behind us, and I watched him arrive at the same shore I had touched down there in the dark, face to face with the amended stone.

"Underhill," he said. "Elias Underhill. My father knew Crane would come. He knew it twenty years ago."

Above us, far up the valley, the sluice gates groaned shut — or some of them did — and the river below began, slowly, grudgingly, to remember its manners.

But the water had shown me its teeth today, and teeth belong to a jaw, and a jaw belongs to a face, and I knew now whose face it was. Aldous Crane had put his hands on our river. The antagonist, as Ines would say, had stopped being a footnote.

"He knows we're in the gorge," Maren said quietly. "Which means he knows where we're going. Which means, gentlemen and ladies, the race is no longer ours to lose politely."

I said thank you to the water. Out loud. Nobody laughed this time either.
