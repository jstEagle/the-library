## 14. Ines Bargains

I have been captured exactly twice in my life. The first time was by a man named Bruno who ran a rare-book stall in Lisbon and had the kind of forearms that made me forget I was supposed to be cataloguing his inventory rather than cataloguing him. That captivity lasted eleven months and ended with me stealing a 1611 folio out of spite.

The second time was now, and it was considerably less fun.

They took us at the narrows below the old slate quarry—two of Crane's men stepping out from behind a spoil heap like they'd been planted there, which, knowing Crane's budget, they probably had been. Teo went for the water and Maren put the boat hard over, but there were four more upstream and a drone overhead doing lazy figure-eights like a wasp that had learned patience. Coral looked at me across the thwart with an expression I can only describe as *mathematical*, did the arithmetic of four guns against one boat, and raised his hands.

So we surrendered. All except the journal, which Coral had sewn into the lining of his coat somewhere around mile twenty-two, and all except Wren, who was still limping along the towpath half a mile back and who, as far as these gentlemen knew, did not exist.

They marched us up to a site office that had been dropped onto a farmer's field by helicopter—Crane's people didn't build things so much as *deploy* them—and sat us on plastic chairs under fluorescent light while a kettle boiled somewhere with insulting domesticity. It was all very civilized. That was the thing about Aldous Crane's operation: it never raised its voice. It simply arranged the world until raising your voice seemed unreasonable.

And then the door opened and Silas Vane walked in, and I understood why Coral's face had gone the color of old paper when he'd seen those boot prints in the cave.

* * *

Let me tell you about Silas Vane, because I've made a study of dangerous men the way other people make a study of birds, and he is a specimen worth noting.

He is not large. He is not loud. He has the mild, apologetic handsomeness of a regional newsreader, and he listens—with his whole body, head tilted, eyes soft—as though whatever you're saying is the most interesting thing anyone has said to him since the war. People mistake this for kindness. It is not kindness. It is *calibration*. He is measuring you the way Coral measures a river: depth, current, load-bearing capacity, where you'll flood first.

I knew about him before I ever met him because I read everything. Ines Okafor, archivist, professional reader of other people's paperwork, and if you think that's a small superpower then you have never watched a woman end a company with a single misfiled invoice. Three years ago I'd catalogued a box of survey documents for a land registry in Truro, and tucked inside was a preliminary report on the Vell valley with Vane's signature on the cover sheet. I remembered the signature because it was beautiful—architect's lettering, each stroke considered—and because the report itself was a lie. It called the valley "hydrologically marginal." I have stood in that valley. Marginal it is not.

So when Vane pulled up a chair opposite me, crossed his ankles, and said, "Miss Okafor. Or is it Doctor?" I was already three moves ahead of him.

"Neither," I said. "Just Ines. Like a hurricane."

The corner of his mouth moved. "You know who I am."

"You're the man whose boots were in my friend's father's cave." I let that sit. "Size eleven. Vibram sole, worn on the outside edge—you favor your left knee. You crouched at the entrance for a long time before going in; the prints at the threshold are twice as deep as the rest. And you smoked two cigarettes and stubbed both out carefully and put the ends in your pocket, because you're the sort of man who doesn't leave evidence even when the tide will erase it anyway." I smiled at him. "You're also the sort of man who reads reports marked *preliminary* and signs them anyway, which tells me you don't believe in waiting for the truth to be convenient."

For the first time, something flickered behind the mildness. Not anger. Recalculation. He turned to Coral. "Is she always like this?"

"She's worse," Coral said. "You should hear her review wine."

Vane laughed—a real laugh, brief and unguarded, and I hated that I noted it, filed it, cross-referenced it. This is my curse. I cannot meet a man without building an index of him.

"I'll come to the point," he said. "Mr. Crane would prefer not to waste your time or mine. You have something he wants. A route. We have—" he gestured vaguely at the office, the field, the valley entire, "—rather a lot of everything else. I'm authorized to offer ten thousand pounds for accurate information regarding the source of the Vell. Twenty if it saves us more than a day."

"And if we say no?"

"Then we follow you anyway, at a distance, and take it from you at the source, and you'll have spent your goodwill for nothing." He said it the way you'd discuss weather. "I'd rather write the cheque. I'm told I'm generous when I get my way."

Maren snorted from her chair like a horse being asked to respect a fence. Teo had gone very still and very Catholic, which for Teo meant he was praying in Portuguese under his breath and glaring simultaneously. But Coral—Coral was looking at me.

And here is what I want to be honest about, because honesty is the one currency I don't counterfeit: in that moment, I thought about taking the money.

Not the twenty thousand. The *principle* of it. I am fifty-one years old, I live in a flat above a chandlery with damp in the north wall, my life's work sits in boxes that smell of mouse, and the man offering me a cheque could buy the valley my friends love and turn it into a reservoir with a gift shop. I have spent thirty years preserving other people's stories for institutions that pay me in gratitude. Here was a man offering to pay me in actual currency for a single sentence.

And the truly shameful part—the part I'll take to my grave unless someone tortures it out of me, and after today I'd probably still lie—is that some cold little archivist in the back of my skull whispered: *you could sell them the true route instead. Both halves. Forty thousand. And with Crane holding the Provision and the map, what exactly do you imagine four amateurs are going to do about it?*

The whisper had a point. That's what made it a temptation and not just a sin. Temptation always shows its references.

* * *

Here is what Vane didn't know, because he hadn't done the reading—or rather, he'd done Crane's reading and not mine.

Three nights ago, while the others slept, I went through the journal properly. Not the route pages—those were Coral's obsession—but the material history. Paper stock, ink chemistry, binding thread. It's how I think; give me grief and I'll respond with provenance. And I found something odd: the east fork of the river appears in the journal twice. Once in the father's hand, dismissed in a single line—*"east fork dead end, gorge closes, no passage"*—and once more, years later, in different ink, a fuller description of the same fork as a viable ascent, written in a hand that trembled slightly and pressed too hard, the way writing goes when the writer is tired or frightened or lying.

Two entries. Same fork. Opposite conclusions. One of them was false, and I spent a long night deciding which.

I decided it was the second. Because the second entry described landmarks in perfect detail—detail that matched nothing in the first entry, detail that could only have come from having actually climbed the thing—and yet got the distances wrong. Consistently wrong. Overstated by a factor I eventually recognized: every distance in the second entry was exactly what you'd calculate if you assumed the gorge walls were climbable at their midpoint. They aren't. I checked the geological survey I keep in my head the way other women keep recipes. The east fork gorge is a slot of decomposing slate. You could no more climb its middle than you could climb a stack of wet cards.

Which meant the second entry wasn't a record. It was bait. Edmund Underhill—brilliant, insufferable, dead twenty years and still three moves ahead of everyone—had written a false route into his own journal, in case the journal was ever taken from him. He'd built a trap for exactly the man now sitting across from me offering cheques.

Edmund had known. Twenty years ago, from inside the grave, he'd known that someday someone would come asking, and he'd left a lie waiting for them like a loaded gun in a drawer.

I have loved many documents in my life. I had never before been *proud* of one.

* * *

So I did the arithmetic again, differently this time.

If I sold Vane the truth: forty thousand pounds, and Crane reaches the source perhaps two days behind us, and Coral stands alone in front of a black glass lens against a funded expedition, and the valley drowns or starves depending on which way Crane decides to squeeze it. If I sold Vane the lie: nothing in my pocket, one day bought, and a man in expensive boots walking into a gorge designed by a dead cartographer to break exactly such men.

There was a third option, of course. Refuse outright. Heroic, clean, useless—they'd tail us regardless, and we'd have gained nothing and revealed that we knew the journal held anything worth lying about.

I am many things, but I am not clean. Clean is for people who can afford it.

"One condition," I said.

Vane tilted his head. That listening posture. God, he was good.

"The cheque is made out to cash," I said, "and you tell me what's in the missing half of the map. The half your employer bought from the executor. I've seen the journal's half. I want to know what yours shows."

"Why?"

"Because I'm an archivist, Mr. Vane. I don't sell incomplete sets." I folded my hands. "And because if your half contradicts ours, I'd rather know before I send you up a mountain on my word."

It was a good lie wrapped around a better one. What I actually wanted was confirmation—proof that the executor had sold Crane genuine pages, which would mean Edmund's trap might have a matching piece on Crane's side, which would mean I needed to know what poison we were carrying too. But mostly it was theater. Negotiation is bibliography: you establish authority through citation, and I had just cited his own knee.

Vane considered me for a long moment. Then he took out his phone, tapped a few times, and turned the screen toward me.

A photograph. Half a map, drawn on the same waterproof stock as our journal, in Edmund's hand—and annotated, in the margins, in a cramped adolescent script I recognized instantly and felt in my sternum like a dropped anchor. Coral's handwriting. Fifteen-year-old Coral had annotated the missing half too. Father and son had built the trap together, each holding half of it, neither knowing the other half survived.

The east fork appeared on Crane's half as well. Marked viable. Marked in Coral's boyish hand: *mid-gorge shelf, 3 hrs, water at 40 ft.*

My heart did something complicated. I kept my face at library-quiet.

"Well?" Vane said.

"It matches," I said. "Mostly. Your copy's missing the corrections." I stood up, because standing up is half of authority and the other half is not sitting back down. "Here's what I'll give you, and I'll give it once, so attend. Forget the main channel past the weir—it's watched, it's slow, and your drone can't fly the tree cover. Take the east fork. There's a shelf at mid-gorge, three hours' climb from the quarry sluice, and it puts you above the falls and within a mile of the source cavern. Your man with the bad knee should stay behind." I smiled. "That's the route. Twenty thousand, cash, and my colleagues walk free downriver with nothing but a boat."

"Ines." Coral's voice, low, warning.

"Coral." Mine, lower. *Trust me.* I couldn't say it aloud. I hoped fifteen years of friendship could carry it, the way a good binding carries a heavy text.

Vane studied me. Then Coral. Then me again, doing his calibration, and I watched him weigh the possibility that I was lying, and I watched him set it aside—because I had cited his knee, because I had asked for the map half like a scholar and not a seller, because everything about me said *tired professional woman, mercenary, safe*. People always believe the ones who seem too tired to bother deceiving them. It is the great advantage of looking middle-aged.

He wrote the cheque. His men walked us down to the boat—Maren swearing with biblical fluency the entire way, Teo crossing himself at the drone—and Vane shook my hand at the waterline and said, quietly, only for me:

"For what it's worth, Miss Okafor, I hope you're telling the truth. I dislike waste."

"I know," I said. "You signed a preliminary report."

Something moved behind his eyes—whether recognition or respect I genuinely couldn't catalogue—and then he turned away and was giving orders in that mild voice, and we pushed off into the current.

* * *

Nobody spoke for the first mile. Maren rowed with the particular violence of a woman saving her real words for later. Teo kept glancing back at the site office until the bend took it.

Then Coral said, "You sold them the east fork."

"Yes."

"That's a death trap. My father wrote it that way on purpose—I figured it out last night, the ink, the distances—"

"I know," I said. "I found it first. I found it three days ago."

He stared at me. Behind him, Maren stopped rowing. Even the river seemed to lean in.

"You *knew*," Coral said slowly, "that there was a false route in the journal, and you let me spend two days planning around the real one, and you said *nothing*, and then you sold the false one to a man with a rifle and a chequebook, and you're only mentioning this *now*?"

"I was waiting to see if you'd earned it," I said. Which was true, and also a deflection, and also the most honest sentence I produced all day, and I'm including the ones I gave Vane.

"Earned—"

"You planned the route alone, Coral. Two nights, hunched over that journal like Gollum with a library card. You'd have done the whole expedition solo if Maren hadn't physically boarded the boat." I leaned forward. "Your father lied beautifully and died alone because nobody was allowed close enough to check his work. I am not letting you graduate from his school. So yes, I withheld a fact, to see whether you'd ask. You never ask. You *measure*."

The silence after that had texture. Maren resumed rowing. Teo murmured something that was either a prayer or a recipe.

"You enjoyed it," Coral said finally. Not accusing. Worse—curious. "Selling him the lie. You enjoyed it."

And here is where I owe the page another honesty, because this chapter of my life deserves accurate transcription: I had enjoyed it enormously. Every second. The calibration, the theatre, the cheque I intended to lose in the first convenient stream, the sensation of handing a dangerous man a beautifully bound lie and watching him thank me for it. For thirty years I have been the woman who *preserves* truths, and it turns out some dried-up aquifer in me had been waiting all that time to pour out one magnificent falsehood, and it had come up sweet.

"Yes," I said. "I hated every minute."

"Liar," said Maren, without turning around.

"Liar," I agreed, and discovered I was laughing, and that it was dangerously close to the other thing, the thing underneath, the thing that was not laughter at all.

We came around the bend below the drowned chapel and there was Wren, right where we'd left him, hopping anxiously from foot to foot on the towpath like a man whose ride was late. Maren brought the boat in. Coral hauled him aboard. And I sat in the stern with twenty thousand pounds of Crane's money in my breast pocket, thinking about the photograph on Vane's phone—about that cramped teenage handwriting on the missing half of the map, annotating the death trap in a child's careful letters.

*Mid-gorge shelf, 3 hrs, water at 40 ft.*

Fifteen-year-old Coral had helped build the trap. Which meant the trap had been built to catch whoever came asking—and the person it was calibrated against, the person whose assumptions it exploited, was the boy who'd helped draw it.

Which meant, with a certainty that arrived in my chest like a stone dropping into a well, that the east fork wouldn't stop Silas Vane at all.

It would stop Coral—if Coral ever tried to follow his own childhood map. And Vane had my face, my voice, and my lie now, and one more thing besides: he'd shown me their half of the map, and I had memorized it in four seconds, because that is what I do, and somewhere in my memory, filed and cross-referenced, was the one correction that made the false route survivable.

I looked at Coral, laughing shakily at the bow, unaware, and I decided—not yet. Some documents you release on schedule.

But I started composing the confession that night, in my head, in Edmund's beautiful architect's lettering, and I had a feeling I'd need to deliver it soon.
