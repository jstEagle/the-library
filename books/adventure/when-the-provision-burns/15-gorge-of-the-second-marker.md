## 15. Gorge of the Second Marker

The false route worked the way good lies work: completely, and then all at once.

Ines had sold Crane's site manager a route up the east fork with such conviction that I half believed it myself by the end—something about a shepherd's track above the old quarry, a marker stone shaped like a kneecap, a spring that ran sweet even in August. She'd invented the kneecap stone on the spot. That was the frightening part. She'd invented it while a man with a radio and a pistol-shaped bulge in his jacket watched her hands, and her hands hadn't so much as trembled.

By dawn we were on the west fork instead, moving fast through birch scrub while the east fork swallowed Crane's men like a bad oyster.

"They'll figure it out by noon," Maren said, setting the pace the way she set everything, like the river owed her money.

"Then we have until noon to be somewhere they can't follow," I said.

Teo, behind me, made a small sound that might have been a prayer or might have been him clearing his throat. With Teo it was usually both.

* * *

The gorge announced itself before we saw it—a cold breath rising out of the ground, the sound of water gone from a murmur to a argument. The Vell narrowed here into a slot of grey rock no wider than a road, walls climbing sheer on either side, wet enough that the stone shone like the flank of something alive. My father's journal called this stretch *the throat*. He'd underlined it twice. My father had not been a man who underlined things lightly; his marginalia were dry as survey notes, measurements and bearings, the handwriting of someone who trusted paper more than people. When he underlined something twice, he meant *this is where men die.*

We walked the riverbed where we could and climbed where we couldn't, Maren reading the water like a ledger she'd audited for forty years. Twice she stopped us at places where the current folded back on itself in ways my instruments read as flat and harmless, and twice I revised my opinion of how much of navigation is arithmetic and how much is scar tissue.

It was Ines who found the second marker.

She found it the way she found everything—not by looking up, but by looking *wrong*, tilting her head at the cliff face as though the rock had misspelled something. "Coral," she said. "Come tell me if I'm mad."

The marker was a slab of slate mortared into the gorge wall six feet above the waterline, and on it, in lines that rain had softened but not erased, was a message in two hands.

The first hand was my father's. I knew its narrow, upright letters the way you know a voice through a wall—intimately, unwillingly, from years of hearing it through the wall of a house I wasn't welcome in. It said:

*THE PATH LEAVES THE WATER HERE. CLIMB WHERE THE ROCK REMEMBERS THE OLD ROAD.*

Below it, smaller, angled, pressed hard enough into the slate to gouge:

*NO YOU DON'T — THE OLD ROAD FELL IN 88. GO BY THE LEDGE. TRUST ME. — C.U.*

My own initials. My own handwriting, aged fifteen.

I stood there with river spray hitting the back of my neck and felt the gorge tilt slightly, the way a room tilts when a memory arrives without permission. Chapter nine of this whole miserable pilgrimage—the journal's second voice, the corrections in a script I'd spent three days pretending not to recognize—had ended with me sitting alone on a cold beach telling myself I'd imagined it. Grief does that. It forges documents. It puts words in dead mouths and initials in dead stone.

But grief doesn't gouge slate. Grief doesn't know about the old quarry road collapsing in '88. Only I knew that, because only I had been there when it fell—I'd heard it go, a long tearing sound up the valley, the summer my father chose a hole in the ground over my graduation—and only fifteen-year-old me would have written *trust me* with that particular insolent underline, because fifteen-year-old me had been furious and certain and desperate to be right about one single thing in front of one single man.

He'd kept it. Twenty-some years of rain and flood, and he'd left my correction standing on the rock next to his own instruction, overruled by a teenager, preserved like a footnote he couldn't bear to cut.

"Coral," Maren said quietly. She'd come up beside me and was reading the slate. Her jaw did something complicated. "That's your hand."

"It's nothing," I said. "It's a rock."

"It's a rock with your name signed on it," Teo said, "which in my experience means something."

"We climb," I said, because if I stood there one more minute I was going to do something embarrassing, like feel things. "The ledge. Not the old road."

"The ledge," Maren agreed, and looked at me a moment longer than the conversation required, which is Maren's way of hugging you.

* * *

The ledge began a quarter mile upstream, where the gorge pinched and the west wall leaned out over the water like a listener. It was maybe eighteen inches wide at its best and considerably less at its worst, sloping outward, slick with a film of moss that pretended to be decoration and was actually an assassination attempt. Below it the river ran white and deep through a chute the colour of gunmetal. Falling in wouldn't kill you quickly. The Vell would take its time, the way it took everything in this valley—patiently, thoroughly, keeping what it caught.

Maren went first. Then Teo, who said four Hail Marys and something in Portuguese that I suspect was not a Hail Mary at all. Then Ines, who climbed like punctuation, precise and unshowy. I went last, because someone had to carry the journal wrapped against my chest, and because going last meant nobody watched me.

For a while it was almost pleasant. There's a state you get into on exposed ground—hands busy, mind empty, the world reduced to the next hold and the hold after that. People pay good money for meditation. I get it free every time I'm somewhere that could kill me, which says something about me I prefer not to examine.

Then the ledge ended.

Not gradually. It simply stopped, sheared away in some ancient frost-spall, leaving a gap of perhaps eight feet of blank wall before it resumed on the far side of a bulge in the rock. Above the gap the wall rose smooth and wet. Below it, the chute.

I got as close to the edge as I intended to and studied the problem the way I study rivers: obsessively, from a distance, hoping the numbers will change if I stare hard enough. They didn't. Eight feet of traverse on holds that might or might not exist behind a skin of moss, no protection, a landing that rhymed with *drowning*.

"Maren," I called back along the ledge. My voice came out flatter than I wanted. "There's a gap."

"I can see the gap," her voice floated back. "What's the gap look like?"

"Eight feet. Blank wall. Bad rock."

A pause. Wind moved the birches on the rim far above us, a sound like the valley turning over in its sleep.

"I'm sixty-one, love," Maren called. "I don't do eight feet of blank wall anymore. I did my last one in ninety-four and I still dream about it."

"Ines?"

"Give me a minute." A pause, then, honestly: "No. I could try it, but I'd be trying it scared, and scared traverses are how Teo and I end up in the news."

"Teo?"

"I will pray for you," Teo said, "from right here."

So that was the shape of it. The ledge resumed eight feet away, and beyond it, according to the marker and the journal and the logic of the whole route, the path climbed to the high shelf where the third marker waited—the one my father had hidden deepest, the one that supposedly needed decoding by someone who could read the hand that had corrected him. Me. And between me and it lay eight feet of nothing, and my expedition family—all three of them, the ones who'd adopted me against my explicit written objection—were strung out behind me on an eighteen-inch shelf, unable to help, waiting to see what the wayfinder did.

Here is what I want noted for the record: my first instinct was correct and professional. Go back, find another line, lose two hours, maybe lose the race entirely—Crane's men would untangle Ines's lie by noon, and noon was coming up the valley behind us like weather. Or go forward alone, make the traverse, do the thing I was actually best at in the world, which is being competent in inconvenient places by myself.

Both options had one feature in common. Neither involved admitting what I actually felt, which was that I did not want to cross that gap alone. Not because I couldn't. Because the last twenty years of crossing gaps alone had added up to precisely nothing—a life measured in miles, tidy and unshared, a grate full of burned letter-ash I'd dug back out with my bare hands because discipline is just loneliness wearing a uniform.

I looked down at the chute. The river didn't care. Rivers never do; it's their best quality.

And I heard myself say it. Out loud. To the gorge, to the water, to no one—to whoever was listening, which turned out to be everyone.

"I don't want to do this without you."

It came out rougher than I meant it. Less like a statement and more like something being extracted. I stood on an eighteen-inch ledge above a drowning machine and admitted, to the air, that the whole architecture of my self-sufficiency was load-bearing nonsense, and that if I crossed this gap alone and found my father's last secret at the top of it, I would arrive carrying it the way I carried everything—unopened, undelivered, addressed to no one.

Silence, except the river.

Then Maren's voice, closer than I expected. She'd come back along the ledge toward me, sixty-one years old and fearless in exactly one direction. "Say that again, but slower, and aimed at actual people."

"Don't make me," I said.

"Too late. You said it once. The gorge heard it, we heard it, God heard it, and Teo's already lighting a candle about it in his head." Her face appeared around the bulge of rock, weathered and absolutely unsentimental. "Now. What do you need?"

Nobody had asked me that in twenty years. Clients asked what I charged. Solicitors asked whether I'd accept delivery. The question *what do you need*, asked plainly, on a ledge, by a woman with rope calluses like tree bark, went through me sideways.

"I need—" I looked at the gap. I looked at them. "Rope. Anchored from the far side. Someone light and stupid enough to cross unprotected, fix a line, and then talk me across it like I'm a client, which I am not, because I don't hire people, I—"

"You're doing it again," Ines called.

"—and I need someone to carry the journal if my hands are occupied," I finished. "And I need you all to never mention this conversation."

"Notarized," Ines said.

Teo was already uncoiling the rope, muttering, crossing himself with one hand and flaking line with the other, which is a level of motor-catholicism I've never seen matched. The problem remained the anchor: the far side was unreachable without the very traverse we were trying to protect. Which is when Ines, who had been studying the bulge of rock with her head tilted—that wrong-headed tilt that finds everything—said:

"The old road."

"It fell in '88," I said automatically, and then heard myself, and shut up.

"It fell," she agreed. "Into the gorge. Which means parts of it are down there, and parts of it—" she pointed up and left, where a shelf of rubble fanned out of a gully thirty feet above the ledge, "—are up there. Including, if I'm any judge of Victorian masonry, an iron stanchion. They bolted the handrail posts straight into the rock. Victorians didn't do anything halfway."

It took Teo ten minutes to solo up the gully—it was easy ground, grade nothing, the kind of scramble he did in church shoes—and five more minutes of swearing in two languages before his fist shot up over the lip holding a rusted iron post, bolted into living rock, solid as sin.

"Firmirin!" he shouted, which I gathered meant *anchored*, and also possibly *blessed*, and in Teo's cosmology probably both.

Maren rigged the line with the speed of someone who'd rigged a thousand and buried several friends. The rope ran from the stanchion, down across the gap, to us. It wasn't elegant. It was a handrail over a grave. But when I took hold of it and leaned out over the chute with the full weight of my skepticism, it held like a promise.

I crossed last anyway. Old habits. But this time going last meant something different: it meant three people were on the other side watching, and if I slipped they'd see it, and one of them would do something reckless and loving about it, and somehow that made the moss-covered holds feel less like assassins and more like holds.

Halfway across, planted on a nub of rock the size of a hymnal, I made the mistake of looking down. The chute roared. Spray climbed the wall and touched my boots, cold as a hand.

"Eyes on the rock," Maren said, steady as a metronome. "You've crossed worse. You told us about the gorge in Wales, the one with the—"

"That was a bridge," I said. "There was a bridge."

"There was a *rope*," Ines said, "and you called it a bridge, which tells us everything about you, Coral Underhill."

I laughed. Actually laughed, mid-traverse, eight feet of blank wall and a gunmetal chute below me, laughing like a man at a dinner party he'd sworn he wouldn't stay for. My boot found the ledge on the far side. Hands pulled me in—not gently, none of them did anything gently—Maren gripping my forearm, Teo pounding my back hard enough to relocate something, Ines already looking past me up the slope because Ines is always looking past you at the next fact.

Above us the gorge opened into a hanging valley, green and impossible, and on the high shelf, visible now as a pale scar among the scree, waited the third marker.

* * *

We reached it an hour later, breathing hard, the sun finally clearing the western rim and pouring down into the hanging valley like something being forgiven. The marker was a boulder split by frost, faces propped apart like a book someone had abandoned mid-read. On the inner faces, protected from the rain for decades, the writing was sharp.

My father's hand covered the left face. Bearings, depths, warnings—the usual dry liturgy. But the right face was mine. Fifteen-year-old mine, in pencil, refreshed over the years in what looked like fresh graphite applied on later visits—he'd come back, again and again, and redrawn my fading letters in his own careful strokes, preserving a dead boy's handwriting the way you'd press a flower.

And beneath it, in ink, added late, the hand shakier than I remembered it:

*Coral — if you're reading this, then you came, and you brought them, or they brought you, and either way you finally understand what I took twenty years to learn: the map was never the route. The people were. Decode the rest with the key below. It only opens for the hand that drew it. — Dad*

Below, a cipher. A substitution grid built from the letters of a phrase, and the phrase was a sentence I had written on the back of a school exercise book at fifteen, a private joke between me and a father who wasn't looking, something I'd never said aloud to anyone since:

*I measure rivers because they always come home.*

I sat down on the scree with the boulder's split faces open in front of me like a mouth about to speak, and behind me I heard Maren tell Teo, quietly, "Give him a minute," and then, after a minute, "Actually, give him two. He's earned it."

I picked up the pencil stub wedged in a crack of the rock—his pencil stub, worn to a thumbnail, kept for me—and began to decode.

And that was when the sound came up the gorge from downstream: engines. More than one. And above them, thin and wrong in the clean air of the hanging valley, the buzz of a drone, already climbing toward us out of the west, where noon had arrived early and Ines's beautiful lie had just run out of road.
