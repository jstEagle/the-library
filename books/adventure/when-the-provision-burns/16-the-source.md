## 16. The Source

The last marker was a lie of the best kind.

We'd been climbing since dawn—Maren setting a pace that suggested she considered mountains a personal insult, Teo humming some diver's hymn under his breath, Ines stopping every forty yards to photograph lichen as though it might file a complaint later. The gorge narrowed until the walls pressed close enough that I could touch both sides at once, which is a thing you don't want to be able to do when the river beneath you is making noises like a crowd deciding whether to riot.

Then the gorge simply ended. Not in a wall—in a curtain. Water fell across the entire face of the rock, thin as spun glass, and behind it the light went green.

"That's not on any map," Ines said.

"Your father's maps," Maren said, "were never about where things were." She said it looking at me, because she'd been saying things like that for three days now, gentle little darts, testing whether I'd bleed.

I wiped my face, which was already wet, so the gesture was purely ceremonial. "Behind the fall, then."

"You're certain?"

"I'm certain my father never did anything the easy way. He once routed an entire survey around a hill because he said the hill had 'bad posture.'"

Teo laughed—a real laugh, startled out of him—and then crossed himself at the waterfall, because Teo crossed himself at everything: doorways, gulls, particularly aggressive puddles. "The old stories say the source hides itself," he said. "You have to walk through water to reach clean water. Baptism, but sideways."

"Baptism but sideways," Maren repeated flatly. "That's going on my headstone."

Ines was already checking her pack straps with the brisk efficiency of a woman who has decided fear is a scheduling problem. "Cold?" she asked me.

"Cold," I confirmed.

"Good. Then we won't linger."

* * *

Walking through a waterfall is one of those experiences that sounds romantic until the water hits you like a debt collector. It drove the breath out of me, drove the cold all the way to the marrow, and then—abruptly, rudely—it stopped, and I was standing in air that smelled of stone and something older than stone, and the roar of the river was suddenly behind me instead of everywhere.

I got my lantern lit with hands I pretended weren't shaking.

The others came through one at a time—Teo first, gasping and delighted; Maren like she was stepping off a bus; Ines last, emerging sleek and furious, wringing out her braid with the expression of someone composing a strongly worded letter to God.

And then nobody said anything at all.

The cavern opened above us like the inside of a held breath. The lantern light couldn't find its ceiling. The walls were granite veined with quartz that caught our small flames and threw them back doubled, tripled, until it seemed there were a dozen expeditions standing in a dozen caves, all of us equally wet and equally small. And the floor—

The floor was water. Clear, shallow, ankle-deep, warm as blood, and moving wrong.

Every wayfinder learns what water is supposed to do. It pools, it drains, it finds its level and sulks there. This water spiraled. Slow, deliberate spirals, turning around a point somewhere ahead of us in the dark, each ring wider than the last, like ripples running backward through time. They made no sound. Water that size, moving that fast, should have whispered or roared. It moved in silence, and the silence was worse.

"The Provision," Ines said quietly. Not a question. She'd read the journal more times than any of us would admit.

"It's beautiful," Teo breathed.

It was. That was the first thing—the beauty, immediate and total, the kind of beauty that makes you forget you own a survival instinct. Ahead of us the cavern narrowed into a natural causeway of dry stone, and at the end of it, set into the living rock like an eye that had been open since before eyes were invented, was the lens.

Black glass. Maybe two feet across, maybe less—it was hard to judge, because it didn't sit still in your vision. It drank the lantern light and gave nothing back, and the water spiraled toward it in perfect concentric devotion, and where the rings touched its edge they didn't stop. They bent. Curved away, curved around, curved into themselves, slow as honey, patient as geology.

My father had spent twenty years and his whole life chasing this. Standing in front of it, I understood him for the first time, and hated how much I understood.

"We don't touch it," Maren said. Her voice came out rougher than usual. "Whatever it does, whatever it wants—we look, we read, we leave. Agreed?"

Nobody agreed out loud. Nobody disagreed either. In this family, silence is a binding contract.

* * *

The final pages were waiting where the journal had always promised they'd be: in a niche beside the lens, sealed in the same oilcloth as the instrument from the sea cave, and beneath them, weighted down with a flat river stone, an envelope.

My name on it. Just that. *Coral.* In his handwriting, which I hadn't seen in twenty years and recognized in half a second, the way you recognize a voice through a wall.

The others saw me go still, and—credit where it's due—they found reasons to be elsewhere. Maren announced she wanted to check the water level at the entrance, which was absurd, since we'd just walked through it. Ines said something about documenting the spiral patterns. Teo simply looked at me, touched two fingers to his forehead in that odd half-salute of his, and walked back toward the falls to stand watch against a threat none of us could name.

I sat down on the dry stone with my back to the lens. I'm not proud of that. I'm not ashamed of it either. Some letters you need to read facing away from the thing that makes them matter.

The oilcloth crackled. Inside, the pages were dense, cramped, written fast—the hand of a man who knew his time was shorter than his sentences. No dates. He'd stopped believing in dates somewhere along the way, apparently.

*Coral.*

*If you're reading this, then the tide did what tides do, and you did what you always do, which is follow a line to its end no matter who it costs. I know exactly how you learned that. I taught you.*

*I'm not going to apologize in the way you want. You want me to say I chose the dig over you, and that I was wrong. I did, and I was, but that's not the true shape of it, and you deserve the true shape even if it arrives twenty years late and smelling of oilcloth.*

*I touched it.*

*One time. Nineteen years ago, near enough. The expedition had found the spring chamber—I'd decoded the chapel markers, Teo nearly drowned proving they were real, and I stood where you're standing now, and I put my hand on the glass.*

*It doesn't grant anything. That's the lie everyone tells about relics, because a thing that grants wishes can be taken away from a wisher. What it does is show you. Everything you've ever wanted fixed, laid out in order, most desperate first. And it shows you the fix. And the fix is always the same fix: give it your hands. Hold it, keep it, tend it, and the valley stays green, and the wells stay full, and the people stay safe—and you stay. You stay forever, Coral, because the moment you let go, everything it showed you starts dying, and it makes sure you know that, minute by minute, year by year, until holding it isn't power anymore. It's a sentence. Custody without end.*

*I held it for eleven seconds. Eleven seconds, and I saw your mother's garden alive again, and I saw you at fifteen laughing instead of slamming doors, and I saw this valley green through a hundred-year drought, and I wanted all of it so badly that my hand wouldn't come away. Teo pulled me off. Ask him sometime—he'll pretend he doesn't remember. He remembers.*

*I left the expedition the next morning. Not because I'd failed. Because I'd succeeded, and I couldn't trust myself near it again, and I couldn't ask them to spend their lives guarding a man's temptation. That's the true shape. I chose the glass over everyone, including you, because I knew—if I stayed near anything I loved, I'd go back for it. I'd tell myself I could hold it gently. Nobody holds it gently.*

*So: I'm sorry. Not for choosing the dig over you—that was cowardice wearing adventure's coat, and I've had twenty years to be ashamed of it properly. I'm sorry for the lesson I taught you instead. You think keeping people at arm's length is discipline. It's not, son. It's the glass, working in you secondhand. It got into me and I passed it on like a fever, and you've been holding the whole world at eleven seconds' distance ever since.*

*Put it down. Whatever you find here—put it down, and go home by the long way, and let someone walk part of it with you.*

*There's more. But read the rest after you've decided, not before. A man should choose with his own weight, not mine.*

*Your father,
Edmund Underhill
—who measured rivers and never once measured what it cost to stand apart from them*

I read it twice. Then a third time, because grief is a bad reader and keeps losing its place.

The lantern flame leaned. Somewhere behind me, the water turned its silent circles, and I became aware—slowly, the way you become aware of a draft, or a stare—that the spirals had changed. They were wider now. Reaching further up the causeway. As if the room had noticed me reading, and had opinions.

* * *

I don't know how long I sat there. Long enough that my clothes had gone from wet to cold to merely damp. When I finally stood, my knees reported their objections formally.

Ines was the closest, crouched at the edge of the water with her notebook, sketching the spiral pattern with the fierce concentration she brought to everything she refused to admit frightened her. She heard me coming and didn't turn around.

"Well?" she said.

"He apologized."

"And?"

"And he told me what it does." I looked past her, at the lens. Black glass, drinking light. Patient as geology. "It shows you everything you've ever wanted fixed."

Ines closed her notebook slowly. "And what did you see? When you read that?"

"Nothing. I haven't touched it."

"No," she agreed. "But you've imagined it. We all have, since the pub. Since the cave." She stood, brushed off her knees, and faced me with that terrible directness of hers, the archivist's talent for finding the exact shelf where a person keeps their worst books. "I've imagined my sister saying yes, when I asked her to come with me, thirty years ago. Maren's imagined the boat she lost with her husband on it, coming home empty but afloat. Teo—" she paused, "—Teo imagines being brave enough to stop crossing himself at everything. So don't stand there acting like the glass is a stranger to you, Coral. It's been writing to all of us for years. It's only now got our address."

The water turned. Wider. Closer.

"Where are the others?" I asked, mostly to have somewhere to put my voice.

"Checking the entrance. There's a problem." For the first time since I'd known her, Ines sounded reluctant to deliver information, which in Ines was the equivalent of another woman screaming. "The waterfall's slowed. Maren thinks—she says the flow upstream is dropping. Like something's drinking it."

I turned fully toward the lens then, and the lantern light finally did what it had been refusing to do: it reached the far wall of the chamber, high above the black glass, where the river entered through a fissure in the rock.

The fissure was dry.

Not trickling. Not reduced. Dry as a road in August, and the silence of the spirals suddenly made sense—they weren't silent because they were peaceful. They were silent because they were starving, pulling from the air itself, from the stone, from anywhere, feeding the thing that fed them.

And far downstream—forty miles, five towns, one valley full of people who'd never heard of the Provision—every well, every tap, every millrace had just begun, very quietly, to fail.

Behind me, at the mouth of the chamber, Teo's voice came back thin and strange off the wet stone:

"Coral. You need to see who's coming through the water."
