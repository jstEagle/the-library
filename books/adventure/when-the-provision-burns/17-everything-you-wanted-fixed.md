## 17. Everything You Wanted Fixed

I should tell you what the cavern looked like before I tell you what it did to me, because afterward I couldn't have described it honestly if you'd held a knife to my ribs.

The spring came up through the floor of the chamber like something breathing. Not a gush — a slow, muscular upwelling, water rising through a seam in the living rock and spreading outward in spirals so wide and so patient that at first I thought my eyes were broken. The whole room turned. Slowly. The way milk turns in tea if you never stir it. Lantern light caught the spiral edges and made them gleam like the rings of a tree, except these rings moved, and they were all turning toward the center, toward the thing set into the rock at the heart of the chamber.

The Provision.

A lens of black glass, maybe the size of a dinner plate, seated in stone as though the stone had grown around it on purpose. It wasn't reflective, exactly. It was absorptive. My lantern's beam went into it and didn't come back out, and after a while I stopped being sure where the glass ended and the dark behind it began.

My father had walked forty miles to die near this thing. He'd written his last pages here — Ines had them wrapped in oilcloth against her chest, and she'd pressed them into my hands an hour ago with a look that said *read them alone or don't read them at all*. The team was waiting above, at the lip of the gorge, where the marker stone pointed down into the earth. Maren had wanted to come. Teo had wanted to come. I'd told them the journal said one person only, which was true, and also a lie, because what it actually said was *one who is willing*, and I hadn't been willing to find out whether any of them qualified.

So: alone. Which was, if we're being honest, the state of affairs I'd spent forty-one years engineering. You get good at it. Solitude is a craft like any other. I could survey a river's course to within a hand's width over twenty miles; I could map my own isolation just as precisely, and I had, and I'd called it freedom for two decades because the alternative word was too heavy to carry uphill.

I sat down on the wet rock across from the lens, unrolled my father's final pages, and read them by lantern light while the water turned its slow circles around me.

---

*Coral.*

*If you're reading this, then either the tide gave up its secret or you're a better wayfinder than I ever taught you to be, which would be funny, since I wasn't there to teach you.*

*I'm not going to apologize in this letter. I'm going to try, but I know myself, and I know I'll dress it up in geology and hydrology and the romance of the work, because that's what I do instead of saying things. So let me say it badly and plainly: I chose the dig over you, and I told myself it was for the valley, and some nights I even believed it. Most nights I knew it was because the work asked me a question I could answer, and you asked me ones I couldn't.*

*About the glass. Listen to me. It doesn't grant power. Everyone who's ever touched it thought it granted power, and every one of them is dead or worse. What it does is show you everything you've ever wanted fixed. Your mother's illness. The argument you never finished. The town that dried out the summer you were nine. It shows it to you mended, and it lets you hold the mending in your hands, and then it asks you to be the one who keeps it mended. Forever. That's the price. Not blood. Custody. It makes you its custodian until there's nothing left of you to keep custody OF. The last keeper — the one from 1847, the one the parish records call drowned — I found his skeleton three chambers back, sitting upright, facing the glass, hands open like a man waiting to receive communion. He'd been dead a hundred and fifty years and he was still facing it.*

*I touched it once. Nineteen years ago. I saw your mother alive. I saw you at fifteen, forgiving me. I saw the Vell running green-banked all the way to the sea. And I understood that if I stayed another minute I would never leave, and if I left I would spend the rest of my life walking back, so I did the only hard thing available: I walked away downhill, and I kept walking, and I didn't stop until I was home, and by then I'd missed your birthday, and you'd stopped writing, and I deserved both.*

*Don't touch it. Don't look into it longer than you must. If Crane gets it, the valley dies slowly and politely, the way things die under contracts. If YOU take it, the valley lives, and you die by inches in a chair facing a piece of glass, and I will have failed you twice.*

*Break it. I could never make myself. That's my confession. I mapped every route to this chamber over eleven years and I could never make myself break the thing, because part of me — the part that's honest, which is smaller than I'd like — still wanted what it showed me.*

*Be better than me. You always were. You just didn't know it, because I was never there to tell you.*

*— Dad*

---

I read it twice. Then I sat there in the turning water and laughed, once, a short ugly sound that bounced off the walls and came back sounding like someone else.

He'd apologized anyway. After promising not to. Typical. The man couldn't follow his own field notes half the time.

And then I looked up, and the glass was looking back.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is the one discipline I've never abandoned. The lens did not move. It has no eyes. But looking at it was like standing at the edge of a well and feeling the well attend to you — patient, bottomless, interested. The spirals in the water tightened. Just slightly. Just enough.

*Everything you've ever wanted fixed,* he'd written.

"Right," I said aloud, to the glass, to the chamber, to nobody. "Let's see your pitch, then."

I'm telling you this so you understand I went in with my eyes open. Whatever happens next — whatever I did next — I was not deceived. I was *willing*. There's a difference, and the difference is everything, and the difference saved nothing.

* * *

It started with the smell of pipe smoke.

Not cigarette — pipe. Cherry blend, cheap, the kind that came in tins with sailing ships on the label. My father smoked it on the porch of the house in Porthvell when I was small, before the expeditions got long, before the letters got short, before the silence got total. The smell arrived in the chamber as if it had been waiting behind me the whole time, and I turned, and —

He was sitting on the rock beside mine. Older than I remembered him, because I'd frozen him at fifty-three and he'd have been seventy-four now, but recognizably, unbearably him. Reading glasses pushed up on his forehead. Field notebook on his knee. The exact posture of a man taking a break between measurements.

"You measured the approach channel wrong," he said. "It's six feet deeper than your chart says. Always was."

"You're not real," I said. My voice came out steady, which surprised me. Twenty years of practice at not needing people, apparently, included not needing them to be alive.

"No," he agreed comfortably. "But I'm accurate. That's better than real, most days."

"This is the pitch," I said. "This is how it starts."

"It's how it continues," he said. "It started the day you buried me. Everything since has been the river running uphill trying to undo that day." He tapped the notebook. "You've been surveying grief for twenty years, Coral. Very precise work. Miles, not years. I read your methodology from in here. It's elegant. It's also a wall with a door painted on it."

"Don't."

"I'm sorry about the birthday."

"Don't." The word cracked in the middle. The water spirals tightened again, and I felt the chamber lean toward me like a listener.

He stood, easy as anything, and looked down at the lens between us. "It shows you the mending," he said gently. "That's all it does. It can't give you the man. It gives you the picture of the man, and then it gives you the job of keeping the picture clean. Dust it daily. Polish it hourly. Stand guard over it until your hands forget how to do anything else." He smiled, and it was his smile, the one that meant *I've already decided and I'm telling you after*. "I lasted four minutes. The keeper in the other chamber lasted a lifetime. Both of us lost."

"Then why am I still sitting here?"

"Because you're my son," he said, "and stubbornness skips a generation, and your grandmother could out-wait a tide."

And then he was smoke, and the smoke was pipe smoke, and the pipe smoke was gone, and I was alone in a turning room with a black eye set in living rock, and my face was wet, and I hadn't noticed it getting that way.

* * *

Here is the corruption, laid out plain, because I understood it completely in that moment and understanding, as usual, changed nothing:

The Provision doesn't lie to you. That's the genius of it. A lie you can catch. A flattery you can discount. But the glass shows you true things — true longings, true losses, true mends — and lets your own heart do the selling. It's not a con artist. It's a mirror with a mortgage attached. You see what you want, you reach for it, and the reaching signs the contract. Then you're the custodian. You sit. You watch. You keep the vision polished while the actual world — the valley, the river, the people who might have loved you — goes on without you, and you tell yourself you're guarding paradise when really you're just a skeleton facing the right direction.

Wren had told us about Crane's men, weeks back, before Wren was ours: *they stopped joking around month two. Stopped eating together. Just worked, and stared at the boss, and waited.* I'd thought that was fear. It wasn't. It was rehearsal. Crane's crew had been practicing for custody.

And I sat there knowing all of it — knowing it the way I know a river's fall per mile, coldly, exactly — and I reached forward anyway.

Because here's what the journal didn't say, what it couldn't say, what no honest document can say about the person writing it: I didn't want the valley green. I didn't want the water rights secured or Crane ruined or the town saved, not first, not most. Those were the public reasons, the reasons you put in a funding proposal. What I wanted was smaller and filthier. I wanted the porch. I wanted the pipe smoke. I wanted one more argument, unfinished, finished. I wanted the version of my life where the letter that summoned me was from a living man instead of a solicitor executing a dead one's sealed instructions.

Twenty years I'd kept every human tie at arm's length, and I'd told myself it was discipline, and it was, and it was also scar tissue shaped exactly like a boy waiting on a porch for a boat that kept not coming home.

The glass offered me the porch.

I reached.

My fingers were six inches from the surface — close enough now to feel it, a cold coming off the glass like the cold off deep water in August — when the vision opened fully, and I have to describe it even though describing it feels like a betrayal of everyone upstream waiting for me.

The valley, whole. The Vell running bankfull and clear from source to sea, willows on the bends, the drowned chapel dry and whitewashed with candles inside and Teo lighting them, Maren's boat riding high on a generous estuary, Ines cataloguing a complete archive with her name and my father's side by side on the spines. Lower Vell rebuilt, children in it, the bell ringing in air instead of underwater. And my father at the center of it, older, slower, present — handing me a chart, correcting my bearing, arguing with me about the approach channel, staying for dinner, staying for the year, staying for the decade, growing old in a house I could visit, dying eventually the way ordinary fathers die: on time, forgiven, mourned properly instead of excavated.

Every detail true to longing. Every detail impossible. The chapel stays drowned. The bell stays under. Dead men don't hand over charts. The glass knew this as well as I did, and showed it anyway, because the showing was never the point. The point was my hand, six inches out, five, four —

"Coral!"

Maren's voice, distant, down the passage, distorted by rock and water. Then Ines, sharper: "Coral, the river's dropping — the whole flow's slowing — *something's happening down there!*"

The river dropping. Of course. The lens had felt me reach, and the water was answering me, gathering itself around my attention, offering its first installment. Command over the valley's water. The brochure promise. The thing Crane wanted, the thing the whole race had been about, and it was being handed to me free, gratis, right now, no contract visible, fine print available upon full possession.

Three inches.

I could hold the flow. I could starve Crane's leverage tomorrow. I could end the drought years, fill the wells, save the town, and sit here doing it, week upon week, year upon year, polishing the picture, dusting the porch, my body drying out around the wanting until some surveyor a century hence found me sitting upright facing the glass with my hands open, and wrote me up in a parish record as *drowned*, because that's what they'd always called it, and they'd be right in the way that matters. Drowned. Held under. Kept.

Two inches. The cold off the glass was on my skin now, and underneath the cold, faint as a pulse, something was *glad*.

One inch.

And what stopped me — I want this on the record, because I got no credit for it at the time and I intend to claim some retroactively — what stopped me was not courage. It was a sound. Faint, through forty feet of rock, from the gorge above: Teo, singing. Badly. Some Cornish harvest song, off-key, the kind of thing a superstitious diver sings to keep the water friendly. And under it, Maren swearing at a rope knot, and Ines saying something sharp and funny that made them both laugh, ragged, tired, afraid-for-me laughter.

They'd come down anyway. All three. Ignoring the journal, ignoring my instructions, ignoring forty miles of my very clearly communicated preference for solitude. They were climbing toward me right now, and they would find me here, and they would know me — that was the whole horror of them, the gift and the horror, they *knew* me — and if I took the glass they would stand in the entrance of this chamber for the rest of their natural lives watching me become furniture.

The vision showed my father. The singing showed me everything else.

My hand hung there, one inch from the surface of everything I had ever wanted fixed, trembling, and the water in the chamber had gone almost still, holding its breath, and somewhere above me boots were scrambling down wet rock toward a man they loved who was about to do something unforgivable or something unbearable, and I honestly did not know which.

I closed my eyes. I whispered the only prayer I owned, which was a surveyor's prayer, which was: *give me the true bearing.*

Then I opened them, and the lens was no longer alone in the dark.

There were lanterns in the passage behind me. Three of them, descending fast.

And my hand was still reaching.
