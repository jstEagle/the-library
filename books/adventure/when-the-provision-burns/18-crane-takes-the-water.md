## 18. Crane Takes the Water

### Coral

I want to be honest about what happened in those last few minutes before Crane arrived, because honesty is the only inheritance my father left me that I've ever been willing to accept.

I was still holding the Provision.

Not gripping it — holding it, the way you hold a bird you've decided not to release yet. The black glass sat warm against my palms, warmer than rock has any right to be, and the water of the spring turned its slow spirals around me like a congregation waiting for a sermon. I had seen everything I wanted fixed. My father, alive, mud on his boots, saying *well done, lad* in a voice I'd spent twenty years forgetting on purpose. The valley green through every drought. Coral Underhill, no longer a man who measured rivers because he couldn't bear to measure anything else.

And here is the shameful part: when I heard boots on the gorge path — many boots, deliberate, unhurried — my first feeling was not fear.

It was relief.

Because some part of me, the part that had watched my father drown rather than surrender this thing, understood that if I didn't put it down soon I never would, and here came someone to make the choice for me. That's what the lens does, I think. It doesn't just show you your wants. It arranges for other people to do your hardest work.

Aldous Crane came into the cavern the way a bill comes through a letterbox. He was smaller than I'd imagined from Ines's descriptions, and grayer, and he moved with the careful economy of a man who has stopped wasting motion on things that don't return profit. His site manager flanked him. Behind them, four more crew, headlamps sweeping the spiraling water with the flat professional disinterest of men who'd been told it was plumbing.

Crane looked at the lens in my hands and did not reach for it. That was the first clever thing he did.

"You're Underhill's son," he said. "The wayfinder."

"I'm nobody's son."

"Mm." He crouched, examined the water where it bent around the living rock in those slow impossible coils, and something crossed his face that I recognized, God help me, because I'd seen it in my own reflection an hour ago. Hunger wearing patience as a disguise. "My surveys say this chamber doesn't appear on any geological record. My lawyers say the mineral rights beneath this entire valley belong to the Vell Consortium. My accountants say a functioning aquifer under sole management is worth more than the town sitting on top of it." He stood. "Which of those three facts would you like to argue with?"

"The second one's wrong," said Maren, from behind me. She'd come up the path with Teo and Ines, and her voice had the particular flatness she saved for weather she intended to sail through anyway. "Water rights in Cornwall run older than your paperwork, Crane. Older than deeds. Older than your family's money, which I'd wager was made shipping things nobody should've bought."

"Everything old is negotiable," Crane said pleasantly. "That's rather the point of money."

He extended his hand then, palm up, and spoke to me directly for the first time, and his voice changed — softened, went almost gentle, the way a solicitor's voice goes gentle over a bereavement clause.

"Mr. Underhill. You've been standing there long enough to know what it costs. Your father knew. He wrote it down — I have his pages, all of them, purchased fair and square from his executor while you were measuring other people's rivers." A flicker, there, a knife slipped in among the softness. "You can carry it out of here and become its keeper, and we will take it from you eventually, because we are patient and funded and you are one man with three middle-aged accomplices. Or you can give it to me now, walk away whole, and let a professional manage the asset."

"A professional," Ines said dryly. "Like you managed the east fork?"

Crane's eyes narrowed a fraction. So the false route had cost him something after all. Good. Let him count the days.

But I wasn't listening anymore, or I was listening too well. Because he'd said *walk away whole*, and the lens in my hands pulsed warm at the word *whole*, and I felt it offer me the trade again — give up the wanting, keep the memory, go back to being the man who burned letters and dug the ashes out of the grate — and I felt how much of me wanted to say yes just to stop standing in this chamber being offered everything.

My hands were frozen. Not cold-frozen. Decision-frozen. The kind of frozen that comes when every branch of the tree leads somewhere you refuse to look.

"Take it," I heard myself say.

Maren made a sound like a gull robbed of a fish.

"Or," I said, and my voice cracked down the middle like ice on a weir pool, "don't. Take it and find out why my father drowned rather than hold it. Take it and learn what *answers to the one who holds it* actually means. I'm not giving it to you, Crane. I'm failing to destroy it, and you're stealing the failure. There's a difference, and you'll pay for it, and so will everyone downstream, and I will spend the rest of my life knowing I could have—" 

I stopped. Because I couldn't finish the sentence. I didn't know what I could have done. That was the whole rot of it.

Crane took the Provision from my hands the way a man lifts an egg he intends to sell rather than eat.

And the water screamed.

Not loudly. That was the horror of it. The spirals simply stopped — all of them, at once, the whole slow congregation of the spring going still as poured glass — and then the flow reversed, drawing backward into the rock, and the sound the cavern made was the sound of a held breath in a chest the size of a county.

Crane laughed. One short bark, surprised at himself, and I watched the hunger take off its disguise right there in front of us. It didn't bother anymore.

"There you are," he murmured to the glass.

Then he turned and walked out of the source of the River Vell carrying it like a briefcase, and the river began to die behind him.

### Maren

I have sailed the Vell since I was nine years old, and I am fifty-eight, and until that morning I would have told you I knew every mood that river had. Spate, drought, the green sulk of high summer, the brown rage of February. I'd have been wrong. I'd never seen it *obeyed*.

We came down out of the gorge ahead of Crane's crew, because Maren Vale does not walk behind armed men on a narrow path, and by the time we reached the lower reaches the water had dropped a foot. By midday, two. I put my hand flat against the hull of my boat where she lay grounded in mud that hadn't been mud since the winter of '76, and I felt her settle, and I swear on my father's grave the boat sighed.

"It'll come back," Teo said beside me. He says things like that the way other men cross themselves. "When they let it go, it comes back."

"They're not going to let it go," I said. "That's the entire business model."

Because I'd seen it already, you understand. Seen the shape of it. A valley that depends on a river, and a man who owns the river's throat. First the wells fail — and they were failing already; Ines had a call from the postmistress in Porthvell before we'd even got our boots off, the pump at the school running dry, the brewery asking questions. Then the town gets thirsty. Then a consortium arrives with a lovely offer: piped water, guaranteed supply, a reservoir, jobs — in exchange for the last of the freeholds. Drought as a sales technique. My grandfather saw the same trick done with fish quotas and never forgave the sea for letting men try it.

Coral stood at the waterline staring at the exposed mud like it was a page he'd failed to read in time.

"You gave it to him," I said. I didn't mean it cruel. All right, I meant it a little cruel. Grief makes me sharp and I've never once apologized for it.

"I froze," he said. Flat. Honest. That's the one decent thing about the man — he won't even lie to make himself comfortable. "He walked in and I handed him twenty years of my father's death because putting it down felt like mercy."

"It weren't mercy. It were surrender with better manners."

"Yes," he said. And then, quieter, looking upstream toward the source: "How long before the backing water becomes a problem? The meltwater's still coming down off the moor. If the cavern holds the flow—"

I did the arithmetic I'd been doing since the spring went silent, and I didn't like the number, and I told him the truth anyway, because lies on a boat get people drowned.

"Days," I said. "Maybe less if the rain comes. When that blockage fails — and it will fail, nothing on this earth holds forever — everything the river owes us comes down at once. And the town sits in the debt."

Coral looked at me, and for the first time since I'd known him — all of nine days, though it felt like the full tide of years — he looked afraid of the right thing.

### Ines

Let the record show — and I am the one who keeps records now, so let it show properly — that Aldous Crane held the Provision for exactly forty-one minutes before the first well in Porthvell ran dry, and exactly two hours and ten minutes before he made his first telephone call to the town council, and that the transcript of that call, which I obtained through channels I will describe only as *archival*, contains the following sentence, verbatim:

*"I'm sure we can come to an arrangement that keeps everyone's taps running. I'm a reasonable man. But reason has operating costs."*

Two hours and ten minutes. I have catalogued empires, Mr. Delacroix-style readers, I have read the correspondence of men who starved provinces from mahogany desks, and even I was impressed by the speed. Power doesn't corrupt gradually. That's the myth the powerful spread to buy themselves a grace period. Power corrupts at the speed of a phone call.

Wren came to us that evening.

He arrived at the Salt & Anchor soaked to the knees and gray-faced, favoring the leg we'd strapped in the mine, and he stood in the doorway dripping like a confession until Maren said, "Well, sit down before you fall down, you great damp liability," which is Maren for *you're one of us now, God help you*.

"I quit," Wren said.

"You were dismissed," I corrected. "There's a difference, and it matters to your references."

"He's going to do it." Wren sat, took the pint Teo pushed at him without looking at it, wrapped both hands round it like it was warm. "The reservoir plans are real but they're cover. The real document — I helped carry the survey cases, I saw the engineering brief — there's a control sluice design for the upper gorge. Permanent. He means to hold the river at the source indefinitely. Sell the water back piecemeal. He called it—" Wren's mouth twisted. "He called it *harmonizing supply with demand*. There's a village upstream of the gorge, Kestle Mill. Forty-one people. It's not in the supply plan at all. It's in the *inundation* plan."

Nobody spoke. Teo crossed himself, which for Teo is roughly equivalent to a paragraph of prose.

"And the lens," Coral said, from the corner where he'd been sitting very still for hours, a man conducting his own private court martial. "What's it doing to him?"

Wren looked at Coral for a long moment. "You want the honest answer or the useful one?"

"Honest. Useful is how I got here."

"It's eating him." Wren said it simply, the way you report weather. "I saw him this morning before I left. He hasn't slept. He's got the glass set up in the site office on a stand like a relic, and he sits in front of it, and the men say he talks to it. And he's thin, Underhill. He was heavyset in March. Whatever it showed him first, he's paying for it already, and he thinks he's winning."

"That's how it works," Coral said. "My father's journal. The keeper pays until there's no keeper left, just the glass wearing a person." He stood. "So we have days before the backed-up meltwater drowns the valley, and a man upstream who cannot be reasoned with because he is no longer entirely present, and a piece of black glass that my family has now failed to destroy twice."

"Three times," I said. "Your father failed once too. Let's be accurate in the record."

Something moved across his face — not quite a smile, but the ground where a smile might someday be built. "Three times," he agreed. "So. Options."

"Evacuate the town," Maren said immediately. "Get ahead of the flood."

"And Crane's sluice stays shut and the valley dies slowly instead of quickly," I said. "No. Someone goes back up. Someone breaks the blockage or breaks the glass, and given that the glass appears to be load-bearing for the blockage—"

"—someone breaks the glass," Coral finished.

"Someone who isn't you," Maren said, sharp as a cleat. "Because we all watched what it did to you in that cavern, lad, and it weren't nothing."

Coral looked at each of us in turn. I have spent my life among documents, and I know a draft under revision when I see one. He was rewriting himself in real time, and the edit was costing him.

"No," he said finally. "It has to be me. But not tonight, and not alone, and not before we get the low-lying streets emptied. Maren — how many boats can you raise by morning?"

Her grin was a terrible thing to behold. "On this coast? All of them."

* * *

### Coral

I write this last part by lantern in the back room of the Salt & Anchor, because Ines insists the record be kept current and because I cannot sleep, and because there is one thing I have not said aloud to any of them.

When Crane lifted the Provision out of my hands, the lens went dark for half a heartbeat — the visions cut off, my father gone mid-sentence, the green valley withered to gray stone — and in that half-heartbeat of silence I heard it, or felt it, or whatever sense it is that a thing like that uses to speak:

*You'll come back. They always come back.*

And the worst part, the part I'll deny if anyone quotes this back to me, is that some small traitorous fraction of me believed it. Not because I want the power. Because the glass keeps my father in it, somewhere, the way amber keeps an insect, and walking away from it a second time feels like burying him again.

Tomorrow we evacuate a town. Tomorrow night, or the night after, I go back up the gorge to break a thing that holds my father's voice.

The river outside the window is a brown ribbon in the mud, thinner than I've ever seen her, and I put my hand against the sill and made the wayfinder's promise, the one we say over charts and never over people: *I will find you again.*

Hold on, old girl. I'm coming upstream.

And this time I'm bringing enough of myself to finish it.
