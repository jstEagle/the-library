## 19. Downstream Flood

I've been reading water for fifty-one years, and I want it on the record that I said so out loud, standing in that spring cavern with my boots in six inches of meltwater and my ears full of the sound a mountain makes when it's holding its breath.

"Listen," I said. "The river's stopped."

Nobody wanted to hear it. Coral was still staring at his own empty hands like they'd betrayed him, and Teo was wringing out his second spare shirt of the day, and Ines was doing that thing she does where she pretends to be calm by alphabetizing something. She'd found a dead man's pencil stub and was sorting pebbles by size. That's Ines for you. The world ends, she files it.

"The river hasn't *stopped*," Teo said. "Rivers don't stop."

"This one has." I crouched and put my palm flat on the water. It should have been moving. Everything up here moves; that's the whole contract between a river and gravity. But the pool at our feet sat still as a held tongue, and the only current I could feel was coming *in* — cold water sliding past my ankles from upstream, arriving and then going nowhere. Backing up. "Crane's dammed the source. Look at the walls. See how high the old flood line sits? Now look how fast we're gaining on it."

Coral looked. He'd been looking at nothing for ten minutes, but that got him. He crossed to the wall where I pointed, ran his thumb along a band of pale stone above the waterline, and did the arithmetic the way his father used to — lips moving, eyes somewhere else.

"How long?" he asked.

"That depends," I said, "on whether that black glass keeps holding."

It was Ines who explained it, because explaining things is her religion. The Provision wasn't just sitting there being evil. It was *working*. Holding back everything the mountain sent down — snowmelt, springs, the whole upper catchment — piling it up behind itself like a man bailing the sea into his own boat. And the mountain doesn't care about glass or greed or Aldous Crane's water rights. The mountain just keeps sending.

"So it fails eventually," Teo said hopefully.

"It fails *catastrophically* eventually," I corrected him. "There's a difference, and the difference is about four hundred million gallons arriving in Porthvell all at once instead of over a week." I'd sailed through the aftermath of the Lynmouth flood when I was a girl. My father had gone down to help with the recovery boats. He never talked about what he saw, which for my father meant it was bad. A valley holds its water the way a fist holds a secret, and when the fingers open—

"Then we go back down and break it now," Coral said. "Tonight. Before it builds enough head to—"

"You can't," Ines said quietly. "You told us yourself. You reached for it and your hands froze. Crane's up there with half a dozen men and whatever hold that thing has on him, and it's had hours longer to get its hooks in. You go back tonight, you're not breaking anything. You're handing it a second volunteer."

The silence after that was the worst kind — the kind where everyone's thinking the same cowardly thought and waiting for someone else to say it.

I said it. Somebody had to, and I'm the one who's spent her life making the ugly call and living with it. "So we split."

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about leading people: it's mostly arithmetic dressed up as courage. I stood in that cavern doing sums while the water climbed my shins, and every sum came out the same way. We couldn't all go back up — too slow, too many bodies, and if we failed we'd be trapped in the gorge when the collapse came. We couldn't all go down — because if nobody broke the Provision, the town drowned anyway, just later and more politely.

Duty on one side. Love on the other. And me with a pencil stub Ines lent me, drawing the two routes on the back of a tide table: up the gorge to the cavern, down the river to Porthvell. Two arrows pointing away from each other. Geography is just love made literal, my mother used to say, though she meant something gentler by it.

"Three go down," I said. "Warn the town, start the evacuation, get people to high ground. Three go up. Break the glass or die trying, and either way the ones below need to be ready for what comes off this mountain."

"And who decides who goes where?" Teo asked.

"I decide. I'm the boat. You're all cargo."

That got a laugh out of them, which was the point. A crew that can't laugh on the eve of a split is a crew that's already sunk.

Ines went down. No argument, which surprised me until I saw her face — she'd already done her own sums, and hers included the fact that she could talk a drowning town into moving faster than any of us could shout at it. She took Wren with him, which was its own kind of mercy: give the traitor a chance to row the other direction for once. He took it without meeting anyone's eyes. Good. Guilt is fuel if you point it at something.

Teo went up. Of course he did. The man has been diving into dark holes since before I met him and has never once come up with a good reason why. And Coral —

Coral stood between the two arrows for a long time. I watched him do it. Watched him look downstream, where the town was, where Maren's boat was, where the found family he still hadn't admitted to finding was about to be waist-deep in other people's panic. Then upstream, where his father's ghost was sitting inside a piece of black glass wearing a developer's suit.

"Maren," he said. "If I go up—"

"If you go up, you do it because it's the job, not because it's the penance." I put my hand on the back of his neck, the way I used to do to his father when he got that same look, that Delacroix look, like the whole world was a debt he'd personally incurred. "Your father went up alone once. Look how that ended. You go up with Teo, you come back down, and you do it *alive*, boy, because I am not burying two Underhills. I haven't got the shelf space."

He almost smiled. "Almost" is as much as that family gives, but I've learned to bank it.

"One more thing," I said, and here's where I'll admit, privately, forever, to being afraid. Not of the water. Water and I have an understanding. I was afraid of saying the next sentence out loud, because saying it would make it true. "When that glass breaks — and it breaks tonight, I don't care what it costs — everything behind it comes down at once. Hours of backed-up meltwater, all at once. The flood wave will hit Porthvell maybe ninety minutes after you do the deed. So whatever happens up there, someone has to be on the water downstream, ringing every bell in that town, getting every soul above the churchyard line. And that someone is me."

"You can't outrun a flood wave in a launch," Coral said.

"No," I agreed. "But I can outrun it in *my* launch, in fog, at night, through water I've known since I was six years old. There's no one alive who knows the Vell's channels better, and I include the river herself in that statement, because frankly she and I are old enough to have stopped pretending we're strangers."

Teo laughed. Coral didn't. He looked at me the way his father used to look at the sea — like it was beautiful and would absolutely kill him given the chance.

"Come back too," he said. "Both of you. That's not a request."

"Wasn't offering it as one," I said, and pushed him toward the upstream arrow before I could change my mind, because changing my mind was the one thing on this mountain I couldn't afford.

* * *

The run downriver took forty minutes and cost me ten years.

I won't dress it up. I drove the Salt & Anchor's tender like a woman fleeing a crime, running reaches I'd normally take at half throttle, threading the weir gates on memory with the gauges reading numbers I didn't like. Because here's the thing about a river that's been dammed at its source: the *lower* river doesn't stop. It drains. Every mile below the blockage, the Vell was sinking, pulling back from its banks like a breath drawn in, and every fisherman's instinct I own was screaming that a drawn breath means a scream coming.

Porthvell knew it too. I came around the bend by the church and the whole town was standing on the quay looking at their river like it had told them a lie. The harbour bar was showing gravel that hadn't seen sun since the eighteenth century. Kids were paddling in the channel. An old boy was standing in the middle of the dried bed, picking up stones, turning them over, putting them back.

I ran the tender aground on the slipway and didn't tie off.

"Aldous Crane has dammed the river at its source," I shouted, before anyone could ask me anything. "The water's backing up behind it and when it comes loose it's coming down this valley all at once. I need every soul in Porthvell above the churchyard line within the hour. Houses can be rebuilt. People can't. Go."

For three seconds, nothing. A town looks at a stranger shouting about floods the way you look at a drunk singing hymns — politely, and from a distance.

Then Ines stepped up beside me on the slipway, soaked to the thighs, hair plastered to her face, and did the single most impressive thing I have ever witnessed her do, which is say nothing at all. She just held up the journal. Coral's father's journal, open to the page with the flood-line drawings, the old marks on the chapel wall, the dates of the last time the Vell lost its temper. And the old boy in the riverbed read it upside down from thirty feet away, because everyone in this valley has that page memorized in their bones even if they've never seen it written down.

He dropped his stones and started running. And Porthvell, bless its stubborn salt-caked heart, ran with him.

What followed was the finest hour of organized chaos I've ever been part of, and I include the 1989 regatta disaster. Ines took the church steps and turned them into a command post, assigning streets to volunteers like she was dealing cards. The publican from the Anchor started carrying elderly neighbours uphill two at a time and swearing cheerfully the entire way, which I maintain is the correct tone for an evacuation. The schoolteacher formed the children into a crocodile and marched them singing up to the quarry road, and I will die without knowing what song it was, but it carried all the way down to the water and it kept the little ones from crying, which is worth more than any bell.

And I took my launch back out onto a river that was barely a river anymore, and I patrolled.

That's the part I'll keep. When I'm old — older — and someone asks me what I did in the flood of the Vell, I'll tell them I patrolled. Up the emptying channels and back down, checking the side creeks, hammering on the doors of the ones who wouldn't leave, hauling a dog out of a draining oxbow because nobody evacuates a dog and leaves it, not on my water. The banks were giving off a smell I knew from my father's stories — wet mud exposed to air for the first time in decades, breathing out. The whole valley inhaling.

Waiting for the exhale.

* * *

It was near midnight when I felt it.

Not heard. Felt. A change in the ground under the keel, a tremor in the water that sailors feel in their teeth before their instruments know anything. I cut the engine and drifted in the dead channel below the church and listened to the mountain.

Somewhere far upstream, in the dark, in a chamber I'd walked out of that morning, a piece of black glass was meeting a rock floor. Or a man's hands were. Or both. I couldn't tell you what happened up there — that story belongs to Coral, and he tells it better than I ever could, and worse luck to anyone who tries to take it from him. What I can tell you is what the river did.

She sighed.

Fifty-one years on that water and I'm telling you she *sighed* — a long, low groan rolling down the valley like a door opening in the earth, and then the sound every soul in Porthvell will describe to their grandchildren slightly differently and none of them correctly. Not a roar. A *arrival*. The backed-up weight of the whole upper catchment, released all at once, coming down the gorge in a brown moving hill with trees on top of it.

I did the sums again, faster this time, and put the engine down hard.

Ninety minutes, I'd told Coral. I'd built the estimate on the old flood records and a generous channel. What came down the Vell that night was meaner than the records and took seventy. Which left me twenty minutes short and four miles from the last house I hadn't checked — the Hollis place, up the tidal creek, where old Mrs. Hollis had told Ines she'd sooner drown than abandon her kitchen, and meant it, because that generation always means it.

I ran the creek in the dark with the flood coming down behind me like God clearing his throat. Found her on her step with a suitcase packed for 1962, arguing with her cat. Got her in the boat. Got the cat, under protest, because I told you — nobody gets left, and that includes animals and stubborn women and, apparently, me.

We cleared the creek mouth as the first of it hit. I felt the wave take the tender's stern and lift it, and I'll be honest with you, I've surfed worse, but never with a passenger who gripped my arm hard enough to leave a bruise I still have. We rode the surge out into the open water below the town, where the flood spread itself thin across the flats and gave up most of its temper, and I turned the boat and looked back at Porthvell.

Water in the lower streets, yes. Through the shopfronts, over the quay wall, brown and furious and then, slowly, just water — finding its level, doing what rivers do when you finally let them. The church on its rise stood dry as a sermon. Every light in the town was burning up on the quarry road, hundreds of them, the whole population alive and lit up like a constellation somebody had bothered to arrange.

Ines found me an hour later, poling a punt through the debris with Wren hauling on the second oar like a man doing penance in public, which, fair enough.

"Coral?" I asked.

"Coming down the gorge on foot," she said. "Teo's carrying him part of the way. He's — " she paused, choosing, and Ines always chooses true over kind when it matters — "he's different, Maren. Lighter. Like something got taken out of him and he's decided to be glad about it."

I nodded and looked upstream, where the river ran free and loud and full in the dark, the way she'd run before any of us were born and the way she'd run after all of us were gone.

"Well," I said. "Somebody put the kettle on. He'll want breakfast. That family always wants feeding after they've saved the world."

And then, because the night wasn't done with us: down the valley road, headlights. A lot of them. Coming slow, in convoy, toward a drowned town — and at the front of them, a car I recognized, because I'd seen it parked outside the site office with a drone on its roof.

Aldous Crane was coming to see his purchase.

Except his purchase had just changed hands.
