## 20. The Breaking of the Glass

The water was rising faster than any of us had calculated, and I want to be honest about the calculation part: Maren had done it in her head, standing knee-deep in the outflow channel, shouting numbers over the roar like a woman haggling with God. Two hours. Maybe three. The meltwater backing up behind Crane's dam would find us eventually, and when it did, this cavern would become the inside of a bell.

I left them at the mouth of the lower passage and climbed alone.

* * *

You should understand something about the spring chamber before I tell you what happened in it. My father's journal described it as a cathedral, and my father was not a man who spent words on decoration. He'd have called a cathedral a big cold room and moved on. But he wrote *cathedral*, so.

He was right. The cavern opened above me like the inside of a held breath—black stone ribbed into vaults, water sliding down the walls in ropes and sheets, and at the center, set into a shelf of living rock that the river had been polishing for ten thousand years, the Provision.

It was smaller than I'd imagined. That's the first thing nobody tells you about relics: they're never the size of your fear. A lens of black glass, maybe the span of my two hands, seated in the stone like an eye that had grown there rather than been placed. The river came up to it from below in slow spirals, turning and turning without going anywhere, as if the water itself had forgotten which direction it was supposed to be flowing. Or as if something had told it to wait.

I stood at the edge of the shelf and felt it look at me.

I know how that sounds. I've spent twenty years being the man who measures things precisely so he never has to say *it felt like*. But there's no surveying term for what the lens did, so you'll have to take the feeling raw: it looked at me, and it knew my name, and worse—it knew my inventory. Every want I'd ever filed away under *someday*, every grief I'd catalogued and shelved, every door I'd locked and walked away from whistling. It read me like Ines reads a ledger, and then it smiled, in whatever way a piece of glass smiles.

*Come here,* it said, or the rock said, or the water said, or I said to myself in a voice that wasn't mine. *You've come so far. Come the last step.*

And that's when I heard the breathing.

* * *

Aldous Crane was already there.

He was sitting against the far wall of the chamber—not crouched, not hiding, just *seated*, like a man waiting for a train he'd been promised years ago. His site manager stood behind him with a lantern and the particular blankness of a man who has stopped being paid enough for this. Between them, on the wet stone, lay coils of Crane's engineers' cable and a portable pump housing, the machinery of the dam above us, still thrumming through the rock.

Crane himself was gaunt. I'd seen photographs—in Ines's files, in the local paper—and the man in those photos had been silver-haired and ruddy, a developer who played golf and quoted scripture about progress. The man against the wall had maybe forty percent of that man left. His skin was drawn over his skull like wet paper. His eyes were fixed on the lens with an expression I recognized, and recognizing it frightened me more than anything else in that room, because I recognized it from my own face. From the mirror. From every night since the sea cave, since the journal, since the word *Provision* had gotten its hooks into me.

He was rapt. He was owned. And he hadn't even touched it yet—or he had, once, briefly, and it had started eating him from the outside in, the way my father said it would.

"Coral Underhill." His voice was dry leaves. "You have his chin."

"I get that a lot," I lied. Nobody had ever said that to me. Nobody who'd known him had ever dared.

"He wrote about you." Crane's eyes never left the glass. "In the pages the executor sold me. Half of them are about you. Did you know that? A man buys half a map and gets a stranger's son thrown in free." A sound came out of him that might once have been a laugh. "He was sorry, you know. At the end. Sorry costs nothing when you're dead."

"Get up," I said. "The water's coming. This whole chamber goes under in two hours."

"I know." Serene. Terrible. "That's why I'm staying. It holds the flow, Mr. Underhill. While the lens sits in its bed, the river waits. Your boatwoman can do her arithmetic all she likes—the collapse doesn't happen while I hold it. I've read the old accounts. The last keeper drowned rather than give it up because giving it up meant the river remembered itself." He finally turned his head, and the movement cost him something; I watched the effort of it travel down his neck like a crack in ice. "I'm not giving it up. I'm going to sit here, and the valley is going to learn what thirst is, and by autumn every farm and every shop and every stubborn widow on that river will sign my papers. Drought or flood. Their choice. Mine, really."

Behind him, the site manager shifted his weight and looked anywhere but at his employer, and I understood that the crew had noticed too—that whatever the lens had begun doing to Aldous Crane, it was doing it fast enough that men who took his money were quietly deciding they wanted their hazard pay in advance.

"You're dying," I said. "Look at yourself. It's feeding on you already and you haven't even picked it up."

"It's *feeding*," Crane agreed, and his voice went soft and reverent, and that was the worst moment, worse than the gauntness, worse than the threat to the valley. Because I understood the tone. I'd heard it in myself for weeks, aimed at lesser things—a page of the journal, a marker stone, the shape of my father's handwriting. Wanting, unashamed, total. "Do you know what it showed me? Not power. Anyone can buy power; I have power. It showed me the reservoir finished. Full. Every field green, every tap running, my name on the plaque, and everyone who ever laughed at Aldous Crane—" his breath caught, "—drinking from it. Grateful. It shows you everything you ever wanted fixed. Your father wrote that. Did he write it to you?"

"He wrote it to me," I said.

"And yet here you are." Crane's ruined smile. "Walking toward it anyway. Like father, like—"

"Don't."

* * *

Here is the thing I have not told anyone, not Maren, not Teo, not Ines, and I am telling it now only because the water is rising and precision is the last discipline I have left:

When I climbed into that chamber, I was not coming to destroy the Provision. I was coming to take it.

I'd dressed the intention up in nobler clothes—I'd told myself I'd carry it out, sink it in deep water, put it beyond anyone's reach, including mine. But the truth, measured honestly, is that some part of me had been walking upstream for forty miles to stand exactly where I was standing, close enough to touch the thing that could give me back my father. Alive. Un-estranged. Twenty years of silence unwritten. The lens didn't need to tempt me. It only needed to be there, and to let me do the tempting.

So when I stepped onto the shelf of living rock and the spiraling water went still around my boots, I was not a hero arriving to save the valley. I was a thief arriving to steal the lie.

And the lens opened for me.

* * *

My father was mending a net.

That's the first thing it showed me—not the dig, not the discovery, but something small and domestic and unbearable: Edmund Underhill sitting on the dock at Porthvell with a torn net across his knees, working the mesh back together with those blunt careful fingers, swearing softly at a knot, the way he used to before the expeditions ate him. Evening light. Gulls. The smell of tar and salt.

"Hand me that float line," he said, without looking up. As if I'd never left. As if I'd been sitting beside him all along, fifteen years old, bored, home.

"I buried you," I said. My voice came out steady, which surprised me, because everything behind my ribs had come loose.

"You did what you had to," he said, and now he looked up, and his eyes were right, they were exactly right, the gray of them, the squint, and the lens was building him out of my own memory grain by grain and it was perfect and it was a lie and I loved it so much I couldn't breathe. "You always did what you had to. That's why I chose you for this. Not the maps. The choosing."

"The choosing of what?" I asked, though I knew. God help me, I knew, and I asked anyway, because the lie was warm and the water was cold and I wanted one more minute of the dock, one more knot, one more—

"The keeping," he said. "It needs a keeper, Coral. It always has. That's the price nobody writes down. You don't command the water. You *are* commanded. You sit in the dark and hold the flow until the holding hollows you out, and the valley drinks, and no one ever knows your name, and you forget it yourself, bit by bit, year by year, until there's nothing left to keep but the keeping." He smiled at me with my memory of his face. "Your old man got out. Barely. Do you know why I left the expedition? Why I left *you*? Because I touched it once, and I felt how easy it would be to stay, and I ran, and I ran wrong, and I ran all the way out of your life because I couldn't bear to be near you and want things." His hands kept mending the net. "Don't be me, son. Don't run. Just don't pick it up."

"That's not you," I said. "That's the glass wearing you."

"Yes," said my father, kindly. "But it's wearing you too. Look down."

I looked down. My hands were already reaching.

* * *

What happened next happened quickly, and I'll render it precisely, because precision is the only prayer I know.

Crane saw me reach. Whatever was left of the man surfaced through the rapture like a drowning swimmer, and he lunged off the wall, and his site manager shouted, and somewhere below us Maren's voice came up the passage—*Coral! Water! NOW!*—and the meltwater behind the dam found a seam in the rock and began to speak in the chamber's throat, a low rising note like the drowned bell at Lower Vell, and none of it mattered, none of it, because the lens was in front of me and my father was in the lens and the dock was in the lens and I was reaching.

And I stopped.

Not because I was strong. Because I finally did the arithmetic honestly. If I took it, I got the dock. I got the net and the gulls and the evening light, forever, on loop, hand-fed to me by a piece of black glass that would sit in my chest where my heart used to be and show me my father until there was no me left to watch him. Crane had been drinking from it for days and he was forty percent gone. I'd have lasted longer. Months, maybe. Long enough to strangle a valley. Long enough to become the thing my father ran from, except I wouldn't run, because the lens doesn't let you run, it lets you *stay*, and staying was the one thing I'd always been too cowardly to do and too proud to admit I wanted.

It feeds on whoever holds it. My father died refusing to use it. Those were the two facts I'd carried upstream like stones in each pocket, and I'd somehow believed I could arrive carrying both and set them down.

You can't set them down. You choose one.

"Mr. Underhill," Crane gasped, clawing at the shelf's edge, "don't you *dare*—"

"It shows you everything you ever wanted fixed," I said. Out loud. To the glass, to Crane, to the ghost on the dock. "And then it keeps you."

I gave it what it wanted.

That's the part I need you to understand, because it wasn't a trick and it wasn't courage. The journal implied it—my father's last marginal note, the one Ines and I had puzzled over for two days: *it cannot refuse a freely offered price*. The Provision takes. That's its nature. But it must accept what is given, and I gave it the truest thing I owned: the memory of my father. Not the lens's counterfeit, not the dock and the net and the forgiving voice—I reached past all of that, into the place where the real man lived in me, the actual Edmund Underhill, with his silences and his selfishness and his terrible arithmetic about love, the father who chose the dig over me and died before either of us could say the unsayable thing. I gathered all of it—every true memory, every grudge, every grief, the whole twenty-year weight of him—and I offered it up freely, the way you'd open your hand to let a bird go.

The lens *took* it.

I felt it leave. There's no other way to say it. One breath I was full of my father, and the next breath the space where he'd lived in me was clean and swept and empty, and the emptiness didn't hurt, which was the cruelest part—it felt like setting down a pack after forty miles, like the relief of putting down something heavy, and I understood in that instant exactly how the lens had gotten its hooks into Crane, because relief like that will make you give it anything.

Then I lifted the Provision out of its bed of living rock, and the river screamed.

Not metaphorically. The spirals stopped. The whole column of water in the chamber shuddered and turned toward me, every drop of it, and the note in the rock became a roar, and Crane was screaming too, scrambling forward on his hands and knees, and his site manager dropped the lantern and ran, and I turned and put everything I had left—all six feet of tired wayfinder, all the miles, all the years I'd measured instead of lived—into one swing, and I brought the black glass down against the bedrock.

* * *

Glass breaks the way rivers freeze: all at once, from everywhere.

The lens came apart in my hand with a sound like a struck bell heard underwater, and the shards were falling and still ringing when the river remembered itself.

It didn't burst so much as *arrive*. The meltwater that had backed up behind Crane's dam for two days came through the seam and then through the fault and then through everything, and the spring chamber filled from the bottom up in seconds, a brown-green fist closing around the shelf, around Crane, around me. I got one breath—one, off the collapsing air pocket under the vault—and then the current took me the way it takes everything, tumbling me into the lower passage, scraping me along stone, and the last thing I saw before the water closed over my eyes was the shards of the Provision spinning away into the dark, each one catching the lantern light like a small dying star, each one, I knew, still wanting.

I thought, quite calmly, as the river carried me downstream toward my family: *I can't remember his face.*

I thought: *Good.*

Then the water had all of me, and the mountain let go of its held breath, and somewhere above and behind, Aldous Crane—who had crawled to the empty bed of living rock and was scrabbling at it, weeping, calling the glass by no name because it had never given him one—was swallowed by the flood he'd built.

* * *

I woke on the gravel bar below the gorge with Maren's hands on my sternum and Teo's jacket under my head and Ines saying my name in the flat precise voice she uses for cataloguing things she refuses to feel about. The valley below us was loud in a new way—the river running free, banks bursting, the whole watershed exhaling at once—but shallow, survivable, the flood my father's arithmetic had promised once the blockage failed. Maren's calculations had assumed failure. She just hadn't known I'd be the failure.

"You broke it," she said. Not a question. Her hands were shaking and she pretended they weren't.

"Yeah."

"And?"

I sat up. The world was wet and enormous and my father was nowhere in it—not gone, exactly, but *absent*, the way a tooth is absent, the tongue going again and again to the gap. I tried to reach for his face and found smooth stone where the memory had been. I'd traded the last of him for a valley's worth of taps and wells and stubborn widows, and the trade was right, and it was done, and it cost exactly what it cost.

"And I need a drink," I said, "and someone to tell me what I forgot."

Ines crouched beside me, and for once she didn't sharpen the moment into wit. "We'll remind you," she said. "That's what archives are for."

Downstream, faint through the roar, the drowned bell at Lower Vell rang once—clear, glad, unburdened—and stopped.

Maren pulled me to my feet. "Town's flooding," she said. "Shallow. But there'll be people on rooftops by morning, and a boatwoman shouldn't work alone."

She handed me an oar. I took it.
