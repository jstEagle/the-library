## 21. Afterwater

**Coral**

The water came down the valley like an apology—loud, thorough, and impossible to argue with.

I stood on the high pasture above Porthvell with Maren's binoculars pressed to my eyes and watched the river do what rivers do when you stop lying to them. It didn't come as a wall. That was the strange mercy of it. The breaking of the lens had released the flow all at once, but the gorge below the source was a staircase of old floodplains, and each one caught the water, spread it, slowed it, passed it on gentler than it received it. The valley flooded the way a house floods when someone leaves a tap running overnight: shallow, brown, everywhere at once, and survivable if you had legs and sense.

The town had both. Sirens had been screaming since before dawn—Wren's doing; he'd driven through the night with his hazard lights on and his conscience finally in gear—and by the time the water reached the lower streets, the lower streets were empty. I watched through the glasses as a foot of Vell water slid into the fish-and-chip shop and out again under the back door, polite as a customer who'd realized they were closed.

"Anybody still down there?" Maren asked beside me. She hadn't taken her eyes off the estuary, where her boat rode at its mooring like a dog that knew something was wrong.

"Wren says no. He knocked every door himself."

"He would." She said it without warmth or coldness, just the flat accuracy she applied to everything. "Man's making up for time."

Below us, the river ran free for the first time in—I did the arithmetic and gave up, because Ines would have the real number and would enjoy telling me I'd gotten it wrong. Free meant fast, and fast meant muddy, and muddy meant that by afternoon the whole valley smelled like a wet dog's ambition. Nobody complained. People stood on the ridge roads watching their fields drown in six inches of water, and they were laughing. Actually laughing. A woman near us had her shoes off and her feet in a puddle that had been, until yesterday, Crane's future reservoir basin.

"You're smiling," Maren said, not looking at me.

"I'm cataloguing damage."

"You're smiling like a man whose father just did him a favor from beyond the grave, and you don't know what to do with your face about it." She handed me a thermos. "Drink that. You look like something the tide rejected."

I drank. It was tea boiled within an inch of its life, which is to say it was Maren's tea, which is to say it was terrible, which is to say I was starting to be fond of it, which frightened me more than the cavern had.

* * *

**Ines**

They led Aldous Crane out of the valley on a stretcher, because he could walk but wouldn't.

I watched from the churchyard wall—the high one, the dry one—with Wren standing next to me like a man attending his own trial. Below, two of Crane's own crew carried him down the lane toward the road, and he lay there with his eyes open and his hands folded on his chest, and he looked, God help me, peaceful. That was the part I couldn't stop looking at. Not the gauntness—he'd gone from boardroom-lean to cavern-hollow in what, thirty hours?—but the peace. The lens had shown him everything he wanted fixed, and then it broke, and whatever it had been feeding him was gone, and what remained was a man who had eaten a magnificent meal and woken up with nothing in the cupboard and no memory of taste.

"They'll want him assessed," Wren said. "Psychiatric. Legal. Both."

"He'll tell them the truth," I said. "That's the cruelest part. He'll describe it perfectly and no one will believe a word, because the truth sounds exactly like madness when the artifact's been smashed."

Wren shifted. He'd been shifting for two days, a man wearing a coat that no longer fit. "I keep thinking I should have told you sooner. About the map half. About what Crane paid for it."

"You told us when it mattered."

"I told you when it was convenient. There's a difference, and I've had forty miles of tunnel to learn it." He looked out over the drowned fields, the water already sliding off them, finding the channels, going home. "Your father's executor sold that page for eleven thousand pounds. I know because I filed the invoice. Eleven thousand. For half a map to a thing worth a valley."

"And your boss bought it for millions and got nothing," I said. "There's a lesson in there about valuation."

He almost smiled. Then the stretcher reached the road, and we both went quiet, and Crane turned his head—not toward us, he couldn't have seen us, but toward the sound of the river, which was running loud and insolent past the whole procession—and I saw his mouth move. I read lips well enough. I've spent my life reading dead men's marginalia; the living are easier.

He said: *let it go.*

Then the ambulance doors shut on him, and the valley went back to being a valley.

**Teo**

Three days after the flood, I dove the chapel at Lower Vell.

Not the drowned one—the real one, up on its hill, where the town kept its actual bell and its actual records and its actual grief. But the sunken village below it was mine, in the way a man owns the place that nearly killed him twice. The surge from the breaking had scoured it. Whole silt banks moved. The old bell tower, which had stood in twelve feet of green murk since before my grandmother, now stood in nine, and clear as glass, and when I went down the light came through the water in long gold bars and lit the street like evening.

I found the marker stone Coral's father had set in the chapel doorway, sixty years ago, wedged under a lintel. It had held. Everything else had moved—the mud, the eels, the roof tiles, the small tin cross off the church gable that I'd been diving toward for twenty years and never reached—but the stone had held, because a man who mapped rivers understood what water does and built accordingly.

I brought it up. Coral was waiting on the bank with a towel, which he pretended was for the stone.

"It's the second marker," he said, turning it in his hands. His thumb found the groove in it automatically, the little channel his own fifteen-year-old hand had cut as a depth gauge. He flinched slightly, the way he always did when the boy touched the man through the object. "Ines will want this."

"Ines wants everything catalogued."

"She's started on my father's papers." He said it carefully, the way you step on ice. "All of them. She's been at it three days."

"How is he?" I asked, meaning: how are you, asked sideways, because that was how we'd learned to ask him.

Coral looked at the stone a while. "She keeps finding things," he said finally. "Notes in the margins. Shopping lists on the backs of survey sheets. A drawing of a boat that looks like Maren's first one, labeled *M.'s tub, leaks.*" He paused. "He wrote about us. All of us. The whole expedition. Like we were—" He stopped, jaw working. "Like we were the find."

I took the stone back and wrapped it, because his hands had stopped working. "He was an archaeologist," I said. "We were the site."

That got a laugh out of him—short, surprised, real. And then he sat down on the bank in the sun with his father's drawings drying on the rocks around him, and I went back in the water, and neither of us said anything else, and it was fine. It was better than fine. It was the first conversation we'd had where nobody was drowning.

**Maren**

The Salt & Anchor reopened on Thursday, which surprised nobody, because Dorrie would have reopened it during the flood if the flood had bought anything.

The whole town crowded in, wet boots and all, and the first round was on the house, and the second round was on Wren, who stood at the bar buying drinks for people who'd spent a week assuming he was the enemy, and who bore it with the grim patience of a man paying off a debt in installments. By the third round they'd stopped calling him *Crane's man* and started calling him *Wren*, which in a Cornish pub is citizenship.

I sat in the corner with my back to the wall, which is the only way I sit anywhere, and watched Coral get adopted in real time.

He didn't notice it happening, which was the best part. Old Trelawney cornered him by the fire to ask about the mines, and Coral answered—actually answered, full sentences, even a joke, God preserve us—and then Mrs. Penhale wanted to know if the spring would run clean now, and he explained the hydrology of it with a napkin and a beer mat until she understood, and then the Hollis boys, who are nine and eleven and feral, demanded to know if he'd really broken a magic glass in a cave, and instead of saying *no* or *it's complicated*, he said, "Yes, and I'll tell you how, but you have to earn it," and their faces lit up like lamps.

Teo leaned against my table. "He's staying," he said quietly.

"Don't jinx it."

"He's staying. Look at him. He's telling stories to children. That's not a drifter behavior. That's a *root* behavior."

"He said last week he'd be gone by the equinox."

"People say things in tunnels," Teo said, "that the sunlight amends."

Dorrie rang the bell for quiet—which is the chapel bell's little sister, salvaged from Lower Vell generations back, and it hums underwater-sweet even dry—and raised a glass to the river. To the river! The whole room roared it. Outside the window the Vell ran silver and loud and free, doing exactly what it pleased, answerable to no lens, no consortium, no keeper. Just water, going where gravity sent it, the oldest honest worker in the valley.

Coral caught my eye across the room. Raised his glass an inch. I raised mine back, and we drank to the river, and to the man who'd refused it, and to the boy who'd broken it, and neither of us said any of that out loud, because we're not the saying-out-loud sort, either of us.

But I saw him look at the door once, around ten o'clock, the way a man checks the tide. Checking whether it's time to go.

It wasn't time. He stayed for last orders. First time ever.

**Coral**

Here is what nobody tells you about coming home to a place that was never yours: the paperwork is enormous.

Ines had commandeered the back room of the Salt & Anchor—or rather Dorrie had given it to her, having correctly identified Ines as the sort of woman you give rooms to before she takes them—and she'd filled it with my father. Forty years of expedition records, survey sheets, journals, film canisters, a biscuit tin of river stones labeled in his cramped hand like they were crown jewels. She'd built an index. Of course she'd built an index. The woman indexes her dreams.

"Sit," she said, when I put my head round the door on the fifth day. Not a request.

"I came for my bag."

"Your bag is under the table where it's been since Tuesday. Sit down. I need a decision."

I sat. She turned the ledger toward me. On the open page, in her beautiful ruthless script, was a heading: **UNDERHILL EXPEDITION ARCHIVE — CUSTODY OF MATERIALS**, and beneath it two names.

*Elias Underhill. Coral Underhill.*

Joint custody. My name in ink, in her handwriting, permanent as a carving.

"Ines—"

"Before you perform your exit," she said, in the tone she used on grad students and generals, "look at the entry above it."

Above it was the original catalogue line, dated twenty years back, written the month after the dig ended and the team scattered. *Materials retained pending return of E. Underhill. Held in trust for his son.*

Held in trust. For me. Twenty years, four custodians, three damp cellars and one pub back room, and every year on the anniversary they'd met and kept the trust alive, keeping my father's papers the way you keep a lamp lit in a window—for someone who might come home.

"You knew," I said. "When you recognized me in the pub. You knew there was a trust."

"We hoped," Ines said. "Knowing and hoping are different instruments. We're archivists; we don't confuse them." She tapped the ledger. "Sign or don't sign. But understand what you'd be refusing. Not the papers. The people who kept them."

I signed. My hand shook slightly, which I blamed on the tea, which was Maren's, which was terrible.

"There," Ines said, briskly, before the moment could become a moment. "Now. Practical matters. Your father's final journal needs a proper transcription, the route needs re-surveying now the water's changed everything above the weir, and the archive needs a second pair of hands who can read his shorthand, which is atrocious and apparently genetic, because yours is worse."

"That's not—"

"Maren also has a message for you, which I'm authorized to deliver verbatim: 'Tell the boy the *Kestrel* sails Tuesday and I've only got one pair of hands and they're arthritic.'"

I opened my mouth to explain that I had contracts, clients, a life in Wales, a system, a discipline—

And heard myself say: "Tuesday."

Ines smiled like a cat presented with an unlocked aviary, and made a note.

* * *

**Maren**

Tuesday morning came up grey and clean, the kind of light that makes even wreckage look intentional. The flood had left its calling cards along the banks—fencing in trees, a rowboat in a field of kale, Crane's survey stakes snapped off at the knee like a defeated army—and the *Kestrel* slid past all of it with me at the tiller and Coral at the bow, learning my lines, getting them wrong, getting corrected, getting them less wrong.

"You'll coil that properly or you'll swim home," I told him.

"Yes, skipper." No irony in it. That was new. That was very new.

We ran the estuary slow, because there was no hurry anymore—that was the luxury of it, the unbelievable wealth of having nowhere to be—and past the cliff at Porthvell, where the sea cave had closed itself behind the spring tides like a book shut on a finished story. Coral looked at it a long moment. I let him. A man's entitled to look at his father's grave, wherever it turns out to be.

"The kids start Saturday," he said. "The Hollis boys and the Penhale girl. Ines drew up a syllabus. There's a *syllabus*, Maren. For teaching children a river."

"Aye. God help them."

"Forty miles upstream. Flooded mines. A gorge." He shook his head, but he was smiling at the water. "My father mapped this route so no one would have to die finding it. Seems right somebody should live walking it."

We passed the weir, where the water ran easy over the stones, singing the low note it had sung since before any of us. Behind us the town woke up—smoke from chimneys, the chapel bell, Dorrie dragging her bins out—and ahead of us the river climbed away into the hills, all the way to the source, running free.

"Skipper," Coral said, hands busy on the line, eyes forward, voice carefully casual in the way of a man about to say something true. "About next year. The anniversary. The pub."

"What about it?"

"I'll be there."

I steered, and said nothing, because some things you don't answer—you just make sure the boat stays steady, and the year does the rest.

The tide was coming in behind us, gentle, unhurried, keeping its own time. We ran with it.
