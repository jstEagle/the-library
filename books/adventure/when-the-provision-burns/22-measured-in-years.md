## 22. Measured in Years

I have always measured rivers the way other people measure grudges: obsessively, and to no one's benefit. Chain and level, theodolite and patience. A river is a sentence that never ends, and I spent twenty years trying to read it without letting anyone read over my shoulder.

So it says something about how far I've fallen—or risen; Ines would insist on the distinction—that this morning I let a twelve-year-old hold the staff.

"Level," she said, which was not what you're supposed to say. You're supposed to say *level* only after you've actually checked the bubble.

"Pip."

"I checked it."

"You checked it with your eyes."

"My eyes are excellent."

"They are," I admitted, "which is exactly why you'll lie with them someday." I walked over and looked at the bubble myself. It was level. Of course it was. Pip Oates had the kind of competence that made adults feel like they were being managed, and she deployed it the way Maren deployed fog—only when it suited her, and always with an exit planned.

Behind her, her brother Tam was trying to convince the third member of our expedition, a small solemn boy named Colm who had not spoken above a whisper since Porthvell, that the water bottle he'd dropped into the Vell was not, in fact, gone forever but merely "on its way to the sea, doing a job." Tam was eleven and had the moral architecture of a used-boat salesman. He'd be running the county by forty.

"Leave the bottle," I said. "The river keeps what it takes. That's rule one."

"That's not a real rule," said Tam.

"It's the realest rule there is."

Maren would have backed me up, but Maren was half a mile downstream with the boat, arguing with the tide, which was a conversation she'd been having with the sea for fifty years and intended to win eventually. Teo was with her, checking lines twice because once is for amateurs and three times is for people who nearly drowned in a flooded chapel and have decided never to be casual about rope again. And Ines—

Ines was up ahead at the fork, sitting on the flat stone where my father's second marker used to be, cataloguing. She catalogs everything now. She has catalogued my father's papers under both our names, which took some doing legally and more doing emotionally, and when I asked her why both names she said, "Because he drew them and you kept them alive, and archives are just love with a filing system," and then pretended she hadn't said anything so sincere and made me fetch her tea.

A year. That's all it's been since the cavern, since the black glass came apart against the bedrock with a sound I still hear sometimes in the space between waking and sleep—a sound like a held breath finally let go. A year since the river burst free and took the valley's fear with it, or most of it. The town rebuilt. They always do. That's the thing about towns that I never understood until I stopped living out of a rucksack: rebuilding isn't optimism, it's just what people do instead of dying of grief.

* * *

We'd come upstream the slow way, the way my father mapped it, because the point of this trip was never the source. The source is a spring now, clean and unremarkable, a seam of water rising through ordinary granite. There's a scar in the rock where the lens sat, and the scar fills with clear water and reflects the sky, and children from the valley throw pebbles into it and make wishes, and none of the wishes come true, which is as it should be.

The point of the trip was the route. Forty miles of it: the estuary, the old mine adits, the drowned chapel at Lower Vell where the bell still hangs below the surface and rings—if you believe Teo, and Teo believes it so hard it becomes true—when the river surges. My father left markers along every mile of it, chalk arrows and brass pins and one carved stone that only decodes if you read it the way a fifteen-year-old reads, backwards and stubborn and slightly angry at the world. I know, because I was that fifteen-year-old. I helped draw this map before I erased him from my life, and the map remembered me even when I wouldn't remember it.

That's the joke I don't tell anyone. The Provision showed you everything you ever wanted fixed. But the map did something quieter and crueler and better: it waited. It kept my handwriting in its margins for twenty years like a letter I'd forgotten I sent, and when I finally read it, the letter said *come home*, in my own hand, and I couldn't argue with the sender.

"So the Provision's really gone?" Pip asked, somewhere around mile thirty. She'd been saving the question. Kids save questions the way squirrels save acorns, and then deploy them at the moment of maximum inconvenience.

"Gone."

"But it was real."

"It was real."

"Then where did it go?"

I considered several answers. The honest one was: *it went into me, or out of me, or through me—I gave it my last memory of my father and it spent the memory shattering itself, and I can't tell you whether I paid too much or got away cheap.* The honest answer would have required me to stop walking, and we were losing light.

"It went where rivers go," I said. "Downstream."

Pip frowned at that for a full quarter mile, which I counted as a victory.

Tam, naturally, wanted to know if there were more of them. "Other glasses. Other caves. My mate Denny says there's one under the quarry."

"Denny," I said, "is a liar with a bicycle."

Colm, who hadn't spoken in two hours, said quietly: "My gran says the cave under Porthvell closed itself. That the sea decided it was done being looked at."

I didn't contradict him. His gran was right, in the way that grandmothers are right about things that matter more than geology.

* * *

Here is what happened to Aldous Crane, for the record, because Ines keeps records and would want it noted. He was led down off the moor three days after the glass broke, empty-eyed and docile as a man whose thoughts had been scooped out and replaced with river-water. The lens doesn't kill you. It just finishes you—takes everything you'd have become and shows it to you until there's nothing left to be. Crane had wanted the valley's water. What he got was an eternity of wanting it, with nothing left to want with. The doctors call it a breakdown. Wren calls it what it is, and Wren should know; he carried the man's gear for six years and saw the hunger coming long before any of us did.

Wren stayed, incidentally. Works the boat with Maren now. Has the hands of a man learning gentleness late and making up for lost time. He still flinches at cigarette smoke, and nobody in Porthvell smokes near him, though nobody discusses why. That's the valley for you. It forgives without auditing.

The consortium dissolved. The reservoir died in committee. The water rights went back to the town, which promptly formed a committee to manage them, which is the most human ending imaginable, and the committee argues beautifully and the river doesn't care, and everyone is somehow happier for it.

* * *

On the last night of the expedition we camped where my father's journal said he camped, on the shelf above the spring, and I read the kids his final pages—not the ones addressed to me; those stay mine—but the field notes, the ones about water pressure and limestone and the way a river holds its shape through solid rock the way a family holds its shape through silence. Pip fell asleep mid-sentence. Tam fought sleep like it owed him money and lost. Colm listened to every word with his whole body, and when I closed the notebook he said, in his small voice:

"He sounds like he was waiting for you."

"He was," I said.

"And you came."

"A year late."

Colm considered this with the gravity of a judge. "Rivers run late," he said. "They get there anyway."

I lay awake a while after that, listening to the spring breathe. Twenty years I'd measured distance in miles because miles could be conquered—you walk enough of them and you arrive somewhere new, somewhere no one knows your name or your father's name or the shape of what you failed to say at a funeral that wasn't even a funeral because there was no body, just a solicitor's letter and a hole in the world. Miles were escape velocity. Years were the thing I refused to count, because years accumulate, and accumulation means staying, and staying means being known, and being known means being losable.

But here's what the found family teaches you, whether you like it or not—and I did not like it, and I liked it: losable goes both ways. To be known is to be losable, yes. It is also to be kept.

* * *

We came down off the moor on the fourth day, and Maren met us at the tideline with the boat riding low and proud, and Teo fed us mackerel until we surrendered, and Ines interviewed the children about their observations with the seriousness of a woman building an archive that will outlive us all. Pip gave her a full report including a critique of my teaching methods ("he makes you check things twice, which is annoying and also correct"). Tam tried to sell Ines a "genuine river stone" he'd found in his own pocket. Colm sat next to Teo and leaned against his arm and let himself be leaned upon, which for Colm is a speech.

And then, because the tide was out further than it had been in decades—the same freak low that opened the cave a year ago, the almanac promising it wouldn't again in our lifetimes—we walked the flats beneath the cliffs of Porthvell, all five of us plus three, down to where the sea cave had been.

It was there. Of course it was there. Caves don't leave. But the entrance was already taking water, the tide turning, grey-green tongues sliding across the shingle toward the dark mouth where I'd found a waterproof journal and a brass instrument wrapped in oilcloth and the beginning of everything I have now.

The kids wanted to go in. Naturally they did. Pip was already calculating the window before the water sealed it, and Tam was inventing reasons why the danger was exaggerated, and even Colm took two steps forward before stopping himself, looking at me.

"No," I said.

"There's time," said Pip.

"There isn't. And even if there were—" I looked at the cave. Somewhere inside, under the silt and the salt, were my father's boot prints, and the stranger's boot prints, and mine. Somewhere in that dark I had knelt down and touched oilcloth and felt twenty years of grief hit me all at once in a place I couldn't walk away from. It had been the worst hour of my life and the door to every good one that followed.

The tide slid another yard up the shingle. The mouth of the cave grew shallower, darker, closing like a hand folding shut.

"Your gran's right, Colm," I said. "The sea decided it was done being looked at."

"Are you sad?" Colm asked. Direct question, small voice, the kind of question adults spend decades learning to dodge and children fire like arrows.

I thought about it honestly, which is a habit I'm told I picked up recently.

"No," I said. "He's not in there anymore. He's—" I gestured vaguely upstream, at the whole forty miles, at the markers and the chapel and the spring and the scar in the rock that fills with sky. "He's everywhere else. The cave was just where he left the message. Message delivered."

The first wave crossed the threshold and curled inside, and the dark drank it, and the entrance sank by inches, and I watched it go without moving to stop it. Pip watched me watching it, and whatever she saw in my face made her put away her calculations. Tam opened his mouth to argue and thought better of it, possibly for the first time in his life. Colm took my hand, which surprised us both, and neither of us mentioned it.

The sea covered the cave the way you cover a grave you've finished grieving: gently, completely, as if it had never been open at all.

I let it.

* * *

That was four months ago. Since then: I have fixed Maren's roof, badly, and Ines's archive shelving, competently, and Teo's dive compressor, actually well, which surprised everyone including me. I have taken a permanent berth in the room above the Salt & Anchor that Ines claims I rent and Maren claims I don't, and neither of them will clarify, and I've decided not to press the issue because the ambiguity appears to be load-bearing. I have started a survey of the Vell—not for a client, not for hire, but because the river deserves accurate maps and the town deserves to own them, and because my father's route should live in more than one pair of hands.

Three pairs of hands, specifically. Pip has claimed the theodolite. Tam has claimed the boat, to Maren's theatrical despair and actual delight. Colm has claimed the margins—he draws, quietly, everything he sees, and last week I found he'd sketched the drowned chapel with the bell hanging in the green water, and beneath it, in letters small as secrets, he'd written *the river keeps what it takes, and gives back different.*

I keep the sketch pinned above my desk, next to a page of a fifteen-year-old's handwriting in a dead man's margins.

This morning I stood at the surveyor's post where the estuary meets the open sea and did the thing I have avoided for twenty years. I counted. Not miles. Years. One year since the cavern. Two decades since the letter I burned and dug out of the grate. Fifty-one years since a man and a boy walked a Cornish river and the boy memorized its bends without knowing he was memorizing his way home.

The numbers didn't hurt. That was the discovery. Counted honestly, the years weren't a debt. They were a length of line, and I was holding one end, and the other end was held—by a boatwoman, an archivist, a diver, a defected enemy turned deckhand, and three children who would carry the route upstream long after all of us were silt.

"Oi!" Maren's voice carried across the water the way it always does, cutting fog, cutting silence, cutting straight through whatever wall I'd built most recently. "You planning to stand there measuring the horizon all day, or are you going to help me with this boat?"

"In a minute!"

"You said that an hour ago!"

"I was being precise!"

She laughed, that bark like a gull with opinions, and I laughed, and the sound went out over the water and didn't come back, because it didn't need to. Some things you send downstream and trust.

I folded the survey book, tucked it under my arm, and turned my back on the sea.

Upstream, the river ran free.
