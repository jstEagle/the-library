## 1. Debt Weather

The storm came in off the coast like a debt collector—unhurried, certain of its welcome, and smiling the whole way.

Rosalind Eriksen watched it build from the roof of a fuel depot outside Saltmere, one boot hooked over the gutter's rusted lip, chewing the last of a stick of licorice root he'd bought three towns ago. The sky over the water had gone the color of a bruise going bad: green-yellow at the horizon, violet above, and threaded through with that particular trembling light that meant the air itself was holding its breath. He'd been reading weather since he was nine years old, back when his mother still had a house to be rained on, and this one had a signature he recognized. Slow rotation. Warm feeder bands. A core that hadn't decided yet whether it wanted to be vicious or merely expensive.

Either way, it was worth money.

The contract was pinned inside his jacket, a flimsy sheet of syndicated paper from the Coastal Reinsurance Consortium, third-tier underwriters, the kind of firm that insured shrimp docks and church roofs and paid out just often enough to keep their name off the fraud registries. They wanted barometric profiles from the storm's leading edge, wind vectors at three altitudes, and a full spectral lightning census from the first hour of landfall. Standard chase work. The kind of thing Rosalind had done two hundred times across four hundred miles of drowned coastline, and the kind of thing that paid exactly enough to stay one week ahead of the people who wanted to talk to him about money.

He spat the licorice root over the gutter's edge and went down the ladder two rungs at a time.

His rig sat in the depot's lee where he'd left it, tarped against the salt spray: a sensor mast taller than he was, folded like a sleeping heron, mounted on a hand-truck chassis he'd welded himself from bicycle frames and a dead generator's axle. Three anemometer cups at the crown, polished so many times the original coating had worn through to bare metal. A lightning spectrometer in a case that had once held surgical instruments—he'd bought it at auction from a hospital that had flooded, which struck him as the coast's idea of a joke. Cables coiled with the neatness of a man who had learned that tangled cable in high wind is how chasers die.

He talked to the rig while he worked. He always did. Not words, exactly—more a low humming, tuneless, something between a lullaby and an apology. The rig didn't care. But there was a comfort in it, the way there's comfort in talking to a horse before you ask it to carry you into fire.

"Last big one of the season," he told it, cinching the last strap. "After this, the dry season comes, and everything dries up. Money. Work. Excuses." He patted the mast's cold aluminum. "So let's get paid."

The first rain hit while he was wheeling the rig toward the old levee road—fat, warm drops the size of coins, then a wall of them, then nothing but water and the sound of water. Rosalind moved by feel now, muscle memory steering the hand-truck along the levee's spine while the world dissolved into gray. The delta spread out below the road on both sides, drowned country, half its fields standing in a foot of brown water that never quite drained anymore. Houses on stilts. Boats tied to second-story balconies. A church steeple poking up out of what used to be someone's orchard, its bell green with algae.

Beautiful, if you didn't know what you were looking at. If you didn't know the water wasn't supposed to be there.

He set the rig at the survey marker he'd scouted that morning—a concrete post stamped with a government seal, one of dozens strung along the levee like buttons on a coat—and began unfolding the mast. Wind came in gusts now, shoving at his shoulders, tasting of ozone and brine. His hands knew every bolt, every clamp. This was the part of the work he loved, the only part he still loved: the quiet competence of it, the small human order of torque and tension assembled against something vast and indifferent. For a few minutes, tightening a guy-wire against a forty-knot gust, Rosalind Eriksen was not a debtor. He was a man doing a thing well.

Then the sky split open.

He felt it before he saw it—the hair on his forearms lifting, a static kiss across his scalp, the taste of a coin pressed against his tongue. Old instinct dropped him flat behind the concrete marker a half-second before the world turned white and the sound arrived like the sky being torn off its frame.

When his hearing crawled back, the rig was burning.

Not all of it. The spectrometer case had taken the strike through the mast and vented it into the ground, and the anemometer cups were simply gone, sheared off and thrown somewhere into the flooded orchard. But the cabling harness—the heart of the thing, six hundred hours of soldering and salvage, irreplaceable because nobody manufactured these parts anymore for anybody who wasn't a government—was a smoking black lacework of melted insulation and fused copper. It smelled like a chapel after a funeral. Like burnt offerings.

Rosalind knelt beside it in the rain and did not move for a long time.

He ran the numbers anyway, because running numbers was what he did instead of screaming. Replacement harness: no market. Fabrication: three months, if he could find a shop, which he couldn't, not on credit, because his credit was a rumor with a body count. Contract payout: voided on equipment failure, per clause nine. Advance already spent: yes, on hull patching for a boat he no longer owned, food, fuel, and the interest payment that had come due eleven days ago.

Eleven days ago. He'd sent a letter promising payment within the fortnight. The fortnight ended tomorrow.

The rain kept falling on the wreck of his livelihood, patient as arithmetic.

* * *

Brann arrived at dusk, and he arrived smiling, because Brann always arrived smiling.

Rosalind heard the vehicle before he saw it—the deep, contented growl of a good engine being driven badly, someone who'd never had to nurse anything through hard country. It came up the levee road with its lights cutting cones through the dying rain, and it stopped twenty feet from the fuel depot office where Rosalind sat drying out over a cup of depot coffee that tasted like hot rust.

The man who climbed out was broad and soft at once, the way a man gets when strength has stopped being necessary and become ornamental. Good boots. A dry coat that had never seen weather. And the smile—wide, warm, genuinely warm, that was the horror of it—of a favorite uncle arriving at a wedding.

"Eriksen." Brann said the name like a toast. "There he is. There's the famous storm-chaser. You know, I drove two hours through that—" he gestured at the sky with genuine appreciation, "—and I have to say, I understand the appeal. It's magnificent. Terrifying and magnificent. Like you."

"Brann."

"You got my letter?"

"I got your letter."

"And?" Brann spread his hands, the picture of patience. "Here I am. In person. Because I believe in the personal touch. Numbers are cold. Ledgers are cold. But a man who shows up in the rain—that's a man who cares."

Rosalind looked at him. Brann's smile did not waver. It never wavered; that was its function. Behind the smile, Rosalind knew, was a ledger, and behind the ledger was the Meridian Syndicate, and behind the Syndicate was a chain of quiet offices in quiet cities where optimistic men lent money to desperate ones and called it opportunity, and collected in ways that never made the papers.

"The rig took a strike," Rosalind said. "Total loss on the harness. The contract's void. I'll make it right when—"

"When." Brann rolled the word around like candy. "You know, that's my favorite word in your mouth, Eriksen. *When.* When the season turns. When the next contract lands. When things improve." He settled onto the bench across from Rosalind uninvited, producing the ledger from inside his coat—a real ledger, leather-bound, absurdly old-fashioned, its pages soft as cloth from handling. "Do you know what I do all day? I collect whens. Every debtor on my route has a when. I write them down." He opened the book. "Yours takes up most of a page."

The depot's single lamp buzzed overhead. Outside, the storm's back half grumbled offshore, moving away without apology.

"Fourteen thousand, four hundred and sixty," Brann read aloud, gently, like a eulogy. "Principal, interest, penalties, and the goodwill adjustment we applied last spring when you had the trouble in Corvane Harbor—which, might I add, we never spoke of again, though we could have." He looked up, eyes bright and kind. "You've been moving for two years, Rosalind. Saltmere to Vell's Landing to Corvane to here. Always one contract ahead. Do you know why you keep moving?"

"I like the work."

"You like the work," Brann agreed warmly. "And you love the work. But you *keep* moving because stopping means settling accounts, and settling accounts means admitting you can't. Movement is a kind of hope, my friend. The cheapest kind. It costs fuel." He closed the ledger with a soft clap. "I'm not here to threaten you. Threats are for men who don't pay, and you're worse than that—you're a man who *almost* pays, which is far more profitable for us and far more corrosive for you. No, I'm here because Meridian believes in you. Genuinely. We're optimists. It's in our charter."

"That's a strange religion."

"It's a profitable one." Brann leaned forward, and for one flickering moment the warmth thinned and Rosalind saw the machinery underneath, the cold gearing of a man whose kindness was a tool with a serial number. Then the warmth returned, seamless. "We have a proposition. Not a job—a proposition. There's a difference. A job is labor. A proposition is *fortune*."

"I don't want fortune. I want the number smaller."

"Then listen." Brann stood, buttoning his dry coat, and gazed out at the darkening delta with the satisfaction of a man surveying property. "You may have heard the government lost a survey team. Nine souls, out near the delta proper. Vanished overnight. Equipment intact, meals half-eaten, not a track in the mud. Very strange. Very sad." He turned back, and the smile widened by a degree. "Very *insured*. Those instruments they carried cost more than your entire debt, Rosalind. More than ten of your debts. And the underwriters—parties with whom Meridian enjoys certain relationships—would very much like that equipment recovered. Salvage rights, retrieval fees, hazard bonuses. Enough, done properly, to zero your page in my book entirely."

Rosalind felt the trap close around him with the softness of velvet. That was the thing about Brann, about Meridian, about the whole smiling architecture of debt: they never pushed you. They opened doors and waited for your own hunger to walk you through.

"A nine-person team doesn't vanish," Rosalind said slowly. "Not out there. Not without weather."

"No," Brann agreed pleasantly. "It doesn't."

"And you don't know what happened to them."

"We know what our clients tell us, and our clients tell us the equipment is worth recovering." He tapped the ledger once against his palm. "The boat leaves when you're ready. The offer expires when the season does. Think of it, Eriksen—dry season coming, waters dropping, and you walking onto solid ground owing *nothing*. Free. Unencumbered. Able to stop moving, if you ever wanted such a strange thing."

He left the ledger's shadow hanging in the lamplight and drove away through the wet dark, engine purring, taillights shrinking down the levee road like a promise retreating.

Rosalind sat a long time with the cold coffee and the sound of the storm dying offshore.

He thought about stopping. He tried to remember what it looked like, a life with no number attached to it, a morning with no one's arithmetic waiting on the other side of the door. He couldn't picture it. Two years of motion had sanded the picture smooth.

In the end it wasn't courage or curiosity that decided him. It was simpler and uglier than that: fourteen thousand, four hundred and sixty, and a page in a soft-leathered book with his name on it, and a man with a kind smile who would come back, and come back, and come back, forever, as long as the number existed.

He went out into the drizzle and began packing what remained of his rig.

Somewhere out in the drowned delta, nine people had vanished without a track in the mud, and the only people who seemed eager to go looking were the ones who wanted their equipment back.

Rosalind told himself that was none of his business.

He almost believed it.
