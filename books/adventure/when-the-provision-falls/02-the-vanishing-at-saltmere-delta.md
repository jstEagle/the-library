## 2. The Vanishing at Saltmere Delta

The rain came in the night like a debt collector—no warning, no courtesy, just weight.

Rosalind Eriksen woke to it drumming on the tin roof of the Wayfarer's Rest, a flophouse in Corren's Landing that charged by the hour and asked no questions, which was why he could afford it. He lay in the dark listening to the water work at the world, and for a while he let himself pretend he was a man with nowhere to be. It was a game he played most mornings. He always lost.

His sensor rig was gone. That was the first thought, arriving before his eyes were fully open—the rig he'd spent three years building out of salvage and stubbornness, the array of anemometers and barometric probes and the cracked but loyal field terminal that had told him where the storms would be before the storms themselves knew. Gone, along with the mast it had been bolted to, because lightning did not distinguish between instruments and the man who loved them. It had come down the hill behind him two nights ago, a white vein splitting the sky, and when Rosalind had picked himself up out of the mud with his ears ringing like struck bells, everything above the waist of his storm-chasing life had been charcoal and copper slag.

Insurance didn't exist for men like him. Contracts did. And contracts, in this part of the coast, meant the Meridian Syndicate, who called themselves optimists because they believed every debt would eventually be paid, and who were right often enough that the belief had become a kind of theology.

He dressed in the dark. Wet clothes from the line by the window, stiff as shed skin. Downstairs, the common room smelled of kerosene and boiled fish, and the proprietor—a woman named Doss whose face suggested she had personally audited every disappointment of the last century—pushed a bowl of gray porridge across the counter without being asked.

"You look like weather," she said.

"I chase it," Rosalind said. "Sometimes it chases back."

"Radio's saying something." She jerked her chin at the set on the shelf behind her, its dial glowing a sickly amber through the gloom. "Government people. Gone missing up the delta."

He'd been lifting the spoon. He set it down.

"How many?"

"Nine, they said. Survey team. Water authority." Doss shrugged with the economy of a woman who rationed even her gestures. "Went to bed at their camp one night, weren't there in the morning. Gear all still there, they said. Generators running. Food on plates."

"No tracks?"

"That's the part they keep saying. No tracks in the mud. Like the ground swallowed them polite." She took his bowl away before he'd eaten half, which was also a kind of theology in Corren's Landing: nothing given free, not even the illusion of finishing a meal. "You know the delta."

It wasn't a question. Everyone knew Rosalind Eriksen knew the delta; he'd been running survey support and storm contracts through those drowned forests for six years, back when the water authority still pretended the flooding was an act of God rather than a matter of record. He knew which channels silted shut in a wet season and which deadfalls could gut a hull, knew the smell of the place in August—rot and salt and green things drowning slowly—and knew, better than anyone, that the delta kept what it took.

"Nine people don't vanish," he said. "Not nine. Not overnight. Not without tracks."

"Then somebody's lying," Doss said, "and it isn't the mud."

Outside, the monsoon's second wave was assembling itself over the sea, a bruise-colored wall of cloud stacked to the stratosphere, and Rosalind stood under the eave of the Wayfarer's Rest watching it build and felt the old pull in his chest—the thing that had made him a storm chaser in the first place, the conviction that weather was honest. Weather never lied about what it intended. Men did. Governments did. The rain fell on the guilty and innocent alike and never once pretended otherwise.

He was still watching it when the black car slid through the flooded street toward him, riding its wake like something that had learned to swim.

* * *

Brann got out of the car with his trousers already rolled to the knee, which was a performance, because Brann's trousers were always rolled to the knee within thirty seconds of any water deeper than a coin's width, and the rolling was always done with the same unhurried grace, as if wading through drowned streets were a pleasure he'd been looking forward to all day. He was a broad, pink-faced man with the smile of a parish priest and the hands of a butcher who'd gone soft, and he carried his ledger everywhere in a oilcloth sleeve, pressed to his chest like a hymnal.

"Eriksen." The smile widened. "You're a hard man to find when you want to be found, and easy when you don't. There's a sermon in that somewhere."

"Brann." Rosalind didn't move from under the eave. Rain ran off the tin in a beaded curtain between them. "I'm not paying today."

"Nobody said pay." Brann came up onto the porch uninvited, shook water from his sleeves with two efficient flicks, and settled against the post as though he owned it, which in a sense he did; the Syndicate held paper on half the buildings in Corren's Landing, and the other half owed them favors. "Today I'm a friend. Today I bring opportunity."

"The last time you brought opportunity, a man lost his boat."

"And gained wisdom." Brann opened the ledger anyway, more ritual than necessity—he knew the numbers better than his own children's names, if he had children, which no one had ever confirmed. "Let's review, shall we? Rig destroyed by act of lightning. Replacement cost, plus interest accrued, plus the small matter of the contract you defaulted on when your equipment stopped existing. You are, at present, a man standing in a hole of his own digging, and the rain is filling it faster than you can bail."

"I heard the news too," Rosalind said flatly. "You want me to go get their gear."

Brann's eyebrows rose, delighted. "You see? This is why I like you. You skip ahead. Most clients make me build the whole cathedral before they'll set foot in it." He tapped the ledger with one blunt finger. "A government survey team. Nine sets of instruments, generators, boats, radios, survey-grade everything, sitting unattended in the Saltmere Delta. The water authority has declared the area closed pending investigation, which means, practically speaking, that nobody official will go near it for weeks—they're frightened, and frightened institutions move at the speed of committees. Meanwhile the gear sits in rising water, degrading. A tragedy of waste."

"And the Syndicate hates waste."

"The Syndicate weeps over it." Brann closed the ledger. For a moment—and this was the thing about Brann, the thing that made men sign things they shouldn't—the smile dropped away entirely, and what looked out from behind it was patient and cold and very old in its patience. "Here is the offer, plain, because I respect you enough to be plain. Go into the delta. Salvage what can be salvaged. Bring it back to Meridian's warehouse in Port Anselm. Do this, and the ledger closes. Every number. Zeroed. You walk out owing nothing to anyone."

The rain filled the silence. Somewhere down the street a dog was barking at the water, or at the sky, or at nothing, the way dogs do when the world stops making sense to them.

Nothing to anyone. Rosalind turned the phrase over and felt its weight, the way a drowning man might turn over a plank. Six years of moving. Six years of never sleeping two nights in the same town, of contracts taken and abandoned, of keeping ahead of collectors the way he kept ahead of storm cells—because stopping meant settling, and settling meant the hole closing over him. He had told himself once, in a younger and stupider voice, that freedom was just distance maintained. And here was Brann offering to sell him the whole country of it for one trip upriver.

"It's too fair," Rosalind said.

Brann laughed, genuinely, head tipped back. "It is! It absolutely is. That's how you know it's real. Nobody cheats you with a bad offer anymore, Eriksen—it's beneath them. They cheat you with good ones." He pushed off the post and rolled his trousers down again, unhurried, the performance reversing itself. "The boat's yours to source yourself—I'm told there's a skiff at Halvard's yard, the Patience, if you can stomach the name. Fuel stipend at the fuel dock, under my name. And Eriksen—" he paused at the top step, rain streaming past him, and gave Rosalind one last look over his shoulder, mild as milk, "—the water authority is very interested in what happened to their people. So are we. If you happen to learn anything about where nine trained professionals went, you'll bring that knowledge to me before you bring it to anyone else. Consider it a bonus clause. Unwritten, of course. We're optimists, not lawyers."

"And if I find the people instead of the gear?"

"Then you'll have found something worth more than gear," Brann said, "and we'll discuss its price then."

He stepped down into the flood, waded to his car, and was gone, leaving only a wake rocking against the pilings and the smell of expensive cologne cut with river water.

* * *

Rosalind didn't sleep that night. He sat in his room at the Wayfarer's Rest with the lamp turned low and the radio murmuring, and he listened to the government spokesmen say *unexplained circumstances* and *no evidence of foul play*, phrases that in his experience meant the opposite of their words the way a mirror means the opposite of a face. Nine people. Generators still humming. Meals half-eaten. No tracks.

He laid it out in his head like a chart. Teams don't leave gear. Teams don't leave food. And teams especially don't leave without leaving traces, because human beings are messy animals and mud remembers everything. Which meant either the delta had done something deltas couldn't do, or someone had made the delta lie for them—and either way, whatever was sitting at that camp wasn't abandoned. It was *kept*. Bait keeps. Traps keep.

And he was going to row straight into it, because a man with a ledger over his head doesn't get to choose his weather.

That was the bitter arithmetic of it, and he made himself do the sums honestly, because self-deception was a luxury priced beyond even the Syndicate's reach. He could refuse. He could run again—there was always another coast, another port, another name misspelled on another manifest. But running had a cost that compounded like interest, and he was tired, bone-tired, in a way that sleep didn't touch. Six years of distance maintained, and what did he own? A duffel bag, a knife, and a talent for reading clouds. The offer on the table was a trap wearing a fair face—but it was also, possibly, the only door out of the room he'd been locked in since the day he'd signed his first Meridian paper.

Temptation, he thought, doesn't come to you with horns. It comes with a ledger and a smile and rolled-up trousers, and it calls itself opportunity.

Near dawn the rain slackened to a whisper, and through the window he watched the light come up gray and grainy over the flooded street, and he made his decision the way he did everything—against his instincts, and moving anyway.

By midmorning he stood in Halvard's boatyard, boots sinking in mud the consistency of porridge, looking down at the Patience. She was twenty feet of rot pretending to be a skiff, paint peeling in continents, gunwales soft enough to dent with a thumb, her name repainted so many times the letters had ghost-layers underneath like geological strata. Halvard, a stooped man built entirely of gristle and grievance, watched him inspect her with the expression of a merchant watching a mark.

"She floats," Halvard said.

"So does a corpse," Rosalind said. "How much?"

Halvard told him. Rosalind paid it from the fuel stipend, knowing Brann would deduct it from somewhere, knowing the ledger always balanced itself at his expense, and put his duffel aboard with the finality of a man stepping off a ledge.

The second wave of the monsoon was visible now even from the yard—a towering rampart of cloud marching up from the southeast, slow and total, swallowing the horizon the way the delta swallowed boats. By the time he reached open channel, the first outriders of wind were combing the water into long gray furrows, and the Patience wallowed under him like something arthritic but willing.

Rosalind stood at her tiller with the rain starting again on his shoulders and the drowned forest rising around him—black mangrove trunks in their thousands, standing in water the color of steeped tea, silent as a congregation—and he felt the delta close behind him with a sound he didn't hear so much as feel, like a door drawn softly shut.

Nine people, he thought. No tracks. Gear untouched.

Somewhere ahead, in all that drowned green silence, something was waiting to be found, and he already suspected he would regret finding it.
