## 3. Leaking Hull, Rising Water

The boat seller's name was Ferro, and he had the honest face of a man who had never once told the truth when a lie would fetch a better price.

"Only vessel left on the coast," he said, spreading his arms over the skiff like a priest consecrating an altar. "And she's yours for what's in your pocket."

Rosalind looked at what was in his pocket, which was not much, and then at what Ferro was calling a vessel. The skiff sat in the mud flats below the drowned town of Corren's Landing, half-sunk already, as if it had given up on the idea of floating and was simply waiting for permission to stop pretending. Her hull was the color of old teeth. Her name—*Patience*—was painted across her stern in letters that had begun to flake away, so that she read, at certain angles, *Pa tien ce*, or possibly *Pat ience*, or possibly nothing at all.

"She leaks?" Rosalind asked.

"She weeps," Ferro corrected, with dignity. "There's a difference. A leak is a flaw. Weeping is character."

"Everything about this boat is a flaw."

"Everything about this coast is a flaw," Ferro said. "You want perfection, buy a coffin. Coffins don't take on water."

It was grim humor of exactly the kind Rosalind appreciated, which is to say the kind that arrives only after you've already paid. He counted out the Syndicate's advance—the small portion of it Brann had deigned to release, folded into a waterproof sleeve that smelled of someone else's sweat—and Ferro counted it twice, licked his thumb, counted it again, and then handed over the key to nothing, because the Patience had no engine ignition worth locking, just a pull-cord and a prayer.

"Bilge pump works," Ferro said. "Mostly."

"Mostly."

"The sea is mostly water," Ferro said, shrugging. "You'll be fine."

Rosalind spent the rest of the daylight loading her. He worked with the economy of a man who had learned that everything he owned must fit inside thirty minutes' notice: the salvaged sensor housing wrapped in oilcloth, a case of ration bars gone soft at the corners, two jerry cans of fuel that Ferro swore were pure and which smelled, when Rosalind cracked the cap, faintly of turpentine and ambition. A tarp. A hand pump whose handle had been replaced with a broom shaft. A flare gun with three flares, one of which was swollen with damp and looked ready to detonate from being looked at too hard.

The monsoon's second wave was building out past the bar. You could read it in the light before you could read it in the sky—the way the horizon went the color of a bruise deciding whether to bloom, the way the gulls stopped circling and sat hunched on the pilings like old men waiting for bad news they'd already heard. The wind came off the water in long, wet shoves, pushing at Rosalind's shoulders as if the delta itself wanted him to reconsider.

He considered. That was the trouble with him; he always considered, and then did the thing anyway, because the alternative was standing still, and standing still meant settling accounts.

He pushed off from the mud at last light, with the rain still holding itself back like a debt collector who enjoyed the anticipation more than the collection.

* * *

The Saltmere Delta did not welcome visitors. It tolerated them, briefly, the way deep water tolerates a stone.

Rosalind ran the Patience up the main channel with the throttle half-open, watching the banks slide by—mangrove roots like knuckles gripping the mud, dead trees standing in the shallows with their arms raised, drowned years ago and never permitted to fall. The water here was the color of steeped tea, opaque to the first inch and bottomless after that. It gave nothing back. You could look straight down into it and see your own face looking up, wondering what you'd done.

The charts said the survey team's camp lay eleven kilometers up the eastern fork, on a rise called Tern Bank that the floods had turned into an island twice a year. Eleven kilometers. In dry weather, an hour's run. In this weather, with the second wave stacking swells against the current and the channel narrowing into a maze of half-submerged fence lines and roofless barns, it would be a negotiation.

By full dark it was raining again—not the polite drizzle of the coast but the real monsoon work, rain that fell with intent, rain that seemed personally offended by his presence. It hammered the tarp he'd rigged over the stern. It found the three places where the Patience wept, as promised, and began filling her bilge with the patience of her namesake.

Rosalind bailed by hand for the first hour, then remembered the pump, then remembered why the pump's handle was a broom shaft, and bailed by hand some more. Grim humor carried him through it. He named the leaks. The one under the port bench he called Brann, because it never stopped smiling and never gave anything back. The one near the transom he called himself, because it was smaller than people assumed and let in more than anyone expected.

Somewhere past midnight, running slow with his lamp hooded against the rain, he began to notice things.

The first thing was a light. Or rather, the absence of one. The delta holdouts kept lamps burning in their stilt houses all through the monsoon—a single bulb, a lantern, anything to prove to the water that someone was still home. But the eastern fork was dark. House after house, stilts standing in brown floodwater, roofs sagging, windows either boarded or broken—and not one light. Not one dog. Not one generator coughing through the night the way generators do when people are frightened and stubborn and staying anyway.

The second thing was a boat. Or the absence of one. Every homestead on the delta kept a skiff tied beneath its floor, the way other folk kept a rifle. Here the mooring cleats stood empty. Ropes hung down into the water, cut clean, not slipped—cut, all of them, in the same night, by hands working quickly and without argument.

The third thing was the smell. Faint, under the rain and the rot: fuel oil. Fresh fuel oil, splashed along the bank near the third abandoned homestead, soaking into the mud in a wide dark stain that the rain hadn't managed to dilute yet. Someone had burned something here recently. Someone had burned something and then left in a hurry, or been made to leave, and the fire had eaten whatever they couldn't carry.

Rosalind idled down and drifted past the stain with his engine at a whisper. On the porch of the homestead, wedged under a rocking chair that rocked gently in the wind with no one in it, sat a child's boot. One boot. Muddy, laced, upright, waiting for a foot that the mud around it suggested had walked away barefoot, fast, toward the treeline—or been carried.

He didn't stop. Stopping meant investigating, and investigating meant finding things, and finding things on this coast had a way of meaning owing things. He had come for survey equipment, not survey ghosts. He fixed his eyes on the channel ahead and told himself the story he was best at: that the delta emptied itself every monsoon, that folk fled uphill every year, that a single boot meant nothing, that cut ropes meant nothing, that the absence of dogs meant—

A dog barked. Far off, upstream, once, twice, and then cut short in a way that made the hair on his arms stand up under his slicker.

Rosalind opened the throttle.

* * *

Dawn found him at the mouth of the eastern fork, and dawn found the rain easing just enough to show him what the night had hidden: that the world ahead was wrong in ways the charts didn't record.

Fence posts stood in six feet of water, marking boundaries of fields that were now open lake. A church steeple rose from the flood a kilometer east, tilted, its bell still hanging, silent. And everywhere, riding the brown water, was debris—but organized debris, sorted debris, the kind of debris that tells a story. Clothing bundled and weighted. Furniture stacked, not scattered. An entire floating garden of potted plants, arranged in rows, drifting slowly downstream like a funeral procession that had lost its mourners.

Nobody flees a flood in rows.

Rosalind killed the engine and let the Patience drift, and in the silence—just the rain on the tarp, the bilge water sloshing its small patient applause—he heard it. Ahead, around the next bend, faint but unmistakable: the sound of a generator. Steady, diesel-throated, running rich.

The survey camp was supposed to be abandoned. Abandoned camps don't run generators. Abandoned camps don't need light.

He eased the Patience forward with the current, past the bend, and there it was: Tern Bank, a low green rise maybe four hundred meters across, ringed now by floodwater. On its crown, canvas shelters, antenna masts, equipment crates stacked under tarps—all of it intact, all of it orderly, all of it exactly what the Syndicate had sent him to salvage.

And on the near shore, drawn up on the mud with its bow grounded and its interior lights burning warm yellow through salt-hazed windows, sat a government airboat. Unmarked except for a stencil on the hull that the rain was doing its best to blur: WATER AUTHORITY — SURVEY DIVISION.

Two figures stood beside it under an umbrella, talking. Even at this distance, even through the rain, Rosalind could see they weren't surveyors. Surveyors slouch. These two stood like men waiting for a delivery.

He backed water slowly, silently, heart hammering against his ribs like the bilge against the hull, and let the current carry him back around the bend into the drowned forest, where he killed the engine and sat very still among the dead trees, listening to the rain fill his boat and his future at the same rate.

The salvage wasn't abandoned. The salvage was guarded. Which meant the Syndicate hadn't sent him to collect equipment.

They'd sent him to find out who else knew it was there.

Ahead, through the grey curtain of rain, a figure stepped out from behind a dead tree onto the nearest sandbar—a woman, alone, waving both arms over her head with the desperate rhythm of someone who has been stranded long enough to stop being choosy about rescue.

Rosalind looked at her. He looked at the bilge water rising past the second bench. He looked back at the woman, and at the jacket she wore, which even at forty meters he could tell was government issue, and far too large for her.

"Well," he said to the Patience, to the rain, to the weeping hull and the broom-shafted pump and the whole drowning world. "In for a liter, in for the flood."

He turned the wheel toward the sandbar.
