## 4. The Stranger on the Sandbar

The Patience leaked the way old men complain—steadily, without urgency, as if she had all the time in the world and intended to use it. Rosalind bailed her every twenty minutes with a dented tin pan that had once, in some earlier and more hopeful life, held engine parts. The rain came down in long gray sheets that erased the horizon entirely, so that water above and water below became a single element and he was simply adrift inside it, a man suspended in weather.

He had been running the delta's outer channels for two days. The map he'd bought from a one-eyed fuel broker in Saltmere was drawn in grease pencil on oilcloth, and half its channels had silted shut since whoever drew it last bothered to look. Twice he'd grounded on mudbars soft as bread dough, and twice he'd rocked the skiff free while the rain drummed on his shoulders like impatient fingers. His hands were pruned white past the second knuckle. His boots had given up any pretense of waterproofness somewhere around the first night, and his feet squelched with every shift of weight, a small wet applause for his own stupidity.

The Syndicate's money sat in an oilskin pouch under the thwart, and the thought of it sat heavier. Brann had counted it out into his palm with the tenderness of a man feeding coins to a slot machine, smiling all the while, and Rosalind had understood that the smile wasn't kindness. It was arithmetic. Every bill was a link in a chain, and Brann wanted him to feel each one settle.

*Salvage the gear,* Brann had said. *Generators, instruments, whatever the government left behind. Bring it to the Meridian depot at Crowsfoot Landing and your ledger goes clean.* He'd tapped the ledger then, that black book he carried everywhere like a psalter. *Simple work for a simple debt.*

Nothing about the delta was simple. The drowned forest rose around him now—cypress and gum trees standing dead to their shoulders in brown water, their bark stripped by years of flooding, pale as bone. Some of them still held the marks of the survey teams' ribbon, orange plastic flagging gone gray and brittle, tied at head height back when the ground here had been ground. The water had risen four meters since. He passed a fence post with three feet of barbed wire still strung along it, and a rusted sign whose letters had flaked away except for DELTA, which seemed redundant.

The radio crackled at intervals with nothing on it. Static, mostly, and once, faintly, what might have been a voice or might have been wind through a wire. He kept it on anyway. Silence on a boat was worse than static; silence let you hear the hull working, the little wet sighs of wood giving up.

It was near midday, though the sky made a liar of the hour, when he saw the smoke.

Not much of it—a thread of gray rising off a sandbar maybe four hundred meters ahead, where the channel bent around a stand of drowned gum trees. A cook fire, or the ghost of one, burning low against the wet. Rosalind cut the engine and drifted, hand on the tiller, listening. The rain filled his ears. The dead trees stood in their rows like witnesses who wouldn't speak.

Whoever was on that bar had either wrecked or been wrecked, and both possibilities meant trouble. But the Syndicate's route to the survey camp ran through this bend—he'd checked the grease-pencil map until his eyes ached—and a fire meant someone who knew these waters well enough to keep dry powder. Or someone desperate enough not to care.

He weighed it the way he weighed everything lately: against the ledger. Trouble avoided was interest saved. Trouble sought was principal unpaid. He put the engine in gear and went to see.

* * *

She heard him coming before he rounded the point—he could tell by the way the smoke thinned abruptly, snuffed rather than banked—and when the sandbar slid into view through the rain, she was standing at its center with her arms crossed and her weight on her back foot, watching him come with the flat patience of someone who had already decided how this would go.

She was tall, angular, dressed in river-mud to the thighs and something military above them. Her hair was dark, hacked short with a knife rather than scissors, still dripping. She had a broken radio cradled in one arm like a sick animal—the case cracked open, guts of wire and green board spilling—and she didn't set it down even as his hull ground up onto the sand.

"You're not government," she said. It wasn't a question. It was an audit, and he had failed it.

"No," Rosalind said. "Are you?"

"I'm a journalist." She said it smoothly, without a flicker, the way a card sharp lays down a winning hand. "Ilsa Vane. Coastal desk, *Meridian Free Press*." She tilted her chin at the boat. "You're storm-chasing traffic. I've seen your rig type before. Sensor masts, lightning triggers. You're the kind they hire to fly into the eye and come back with numbers."

"The kind who used to," Rosalind said. "Rig burned two weeks ago. Lightning has opinions about my profession."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth—not quite a smile, more the acknowledgment of a shared joke neither would admit to telling. "And now?"

"Now I salvage."

"Salvage." She tasted the word. Her eyes went past him, quick and professional, cataloguing the Patience: the rot in the gunwale, the bail pan, the oilskin pouch he shifted his boot over without meaning to. She missed nothing. He noted that she missed nothing, and filed it where he filed everything dangerous. "Out here? In the second wave? There's nothing out here but drowned trees and a dead survey team."

The words landed in the space between them and sat there, ringing.

"Dead?" Rosalind said carefully.

"Missing." She corrected herself with the ease of long practice, and that ease told him more than the word itself. People who were merely curious stumbled over their facts. People who were lying rehearsed. "Missing. Nine people. Gear intact, no tracks. That's the story everyone wants and nobody can get close enough to file. Which is why I'm here, standing on a sandbar, holding a radio that died on me yesterday morning."

She lifted the ruined radio slightly, as if presenting evidence. Water had gotten into it—rain, spray, immersion, impossible to say. Its face was fogged from the inside, a tiny drowned world.

"And no one's come for you," Rosalind said.

"I've been here thirty-six hours. One patrol boat went past at dawn yesterday. I signaled. They either didn't see me or didn't want to." Her voice stayed level, but he caught the thing underneath it, the thin wire of strain that thirty-six hours alone on a bar in rising water will put in anyone. The river was indeed climbing. When he'd grounded, his keel had touched sand that had been underwater the day before; he could read the tide line on her ankles, a band of dried mud a handspan above her boots. "So. You're headed up-channel."

It wasn't a question either. This woman asked very few questions and issued many statements, and Rosalind found himself admiring the technique even as he distrusted it.

"I'm headed to the survey camp," he said, because lying to her felt suddenly like bringing a knife to a gunfight. "Or what's left of it."

Something changed in her posture—nothing you could name, just a settling, like a ship taking ballast. "Then we have a destination in common."

"Do we."

"I need a ride. You need—" her eyes flicked, deliberate and slow, to the place under the thwart where the oilskin pouch sat, "—hands. Bailing, lines, whatever. Two people run a boat better than one, and you look like a man who hasn't slept since the rig burned."

He hadn't. He said nothing about it.

"Here's my offer," she went on. "I don't ask what you're salvaging or for whom. You don't ask why a journalist is forty kilometers past the last phone line with a dead radio and a jacket that doesn't fit her."

Rosalind looked at the jacket then, properly, for the first time. It was a survey-team shell—government issue, slate blue, the Water Authority crest stitched at the breast, the kind distributed by the crate to field crews. It hung on her wrong. Too broad in the shoulder, sleeves rolled three times and still covering her knuckles. Someone else's jacket. Someone smaller, or someone gone.

He looked at it for a long moment. She watched him look and did not explain it, which was its own kind of answer.

"That's a survey jacket," he said.

"It rained," she said.

"That's not an explanation."

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "It isn't."

* * *

They stood there in the rain and took each other's measure, and Rosalind understood, with the particular clarity that exhaustion brings, that he was looking at a mirror with the silvering flaked off in places. Here was another person standing in the middle of nowhere with a story stitched together from convenient parts. Here was another person whose eyes went to exits before faces, whose hands knew where things were hidden on their own body, who spoke in statements because questions could be turned back on you.

Two liars, recognizing each other across four meters of wet sand.

The strange thing—the thing he would think about later, in worse moments—was how much easier that made it. With an honest person you had to maintain the fiction constantly, guard it, feed it. With another liar you could simply... agree. Draw the borders and leave the interior unmapped.

"Conditions," Rosalind said finally. "One: you bail when I say bail. Two: if anyone official hails us, you're a journalist and I'm a contractor and we met yesterday at Saltmere. Three—" he nodded at the jacket, "—that stays on or comes off, your choice, but you don't explain it unless someone asks who'd know the difference."

"Four," she said. "If it goes wrong, we go wrong separately. Nobody drowns trying to save somebody they met on a sandbar."

"Agreed," he said, and meant it, and heard in her voice that she meant it too, and understood that they had just made the most honest contract either of them had signed in years: a mutual agreement to abandon each other.

He helped her aboard. She moved like someone who'd spent time on water—stepping where he stepped, sitting where the ballast sat best, stowing the ruined radio under the thwart without asking permission. The Patience settled two inches deeper with her weight and complained about it in the language of old boats, a creak and a shudder through the planks.

Her pack, when she swung it aboard, was heavy in a way packs aren't when they hold only clothes. Rosalind noticed. He noticed everything about her, the way you notice weather building on a horizon: not the storm itself yet, just the light going strange.

"Camp's six hours up-channel if the map's honest," he said, pushing off. The sandbar slid away behind them, taking its cold fire-pit and its thirty-six hours of solitude with it. "Map probably isn't honest."

"Maps never are," Ilsa Vane said. She settled against the gunwale opposite him, rain streaming off her borrowed hood, and watched the drowned forest slide past with an expression he couldn't read—or could, and didn't like the reading. "That's how they get you. The land tells you one story, the map tells you another, and you're standing in the difference getting wet."

"Poetic," Rosalind said. "For a journalist."

"For anyone who grew up here." Something crossed her face then, fast, gone before he could fix it—grief, or its cousin. She looked away toward the water. "The delta used to be towns. Before the floods. My grandmother sold fish at a market in a town called Vane's Landing, if you can believe the name. There's forty feet of water over the market square now."

Rosalind said nothing. The engine talked for both of them, a steady laboring churn.

"They say the water rose natural," she went on, quieter, almost to herself. "Slow disaster. Bad seasons stacking up. That's the official story, and most people believe it because the alternative is believing someone chose this." She turned and looked at him directly, and her eyes in the gray light were the color of the channel water, brown-green and unreadable. "I don't believe slow disasters. I've chased too many fast ones. Water doesn't rise like this on its own. Water rises like this when someone opens something."

The rain thickened. Ahead, the channel narrowed between walls of drowned timber, and the light began to fail hours too early, the monsoon's second wave rolling in overhead like a lid closing.

"You talk like someone with a source," Rosalind said.

"I talk like someone with questions." She smiled then, fully, and it was a good smile, warm and crooked and completely untrustworthy. "Which is what journalists have. Questions. That's all. Just questions."

He throttled up. The Patience groaned into the current, leaking her slow leak, carrying them both toward the camp where nine people had vanished from the face of the earth leaving their generators running and their meals half-eaten. Somewhere under the thwart, the oilskin pouch held the price of his freedom, and somewhere under the slate-blue jacket, this stranger carried whatever she'd really come for.

Neither of them asked. That was the deal.

But as the drowned forest closed around them and the first thunder of the second wave rolled down the channel like a door slamming in a distant room, Rosalind caught himself watching her hands—how they rested, loose and ready, on the strap of that too-heavy pack—and he understood that the questions he wasn't asking were piling up below the waterline of this small leaking boat, exactly like the water in the hull.

Quietly. Steadily.

Waiting to be bailed.
