## 5. Gear Without Ghosts

The camp announced itself by smell before sight—diesel and coffee, the twin perfumes of people who intended to stay a while. Rosalind caught it on the wind off the channel and cut the Patience's engine, letting the skiff drift through the last hundred meters of drowned cane. Beside him, Ilsa Vane went very still in a way he'd learned to read as dangerous.

"You smell that?" she said.

"Generators." He nodded toward the treeline, where a gray thread of exhaust rose through the rain-haze and bent east. "At least two of them, running rich. Somebody's burning fuel they didn't bother to shut off."

"Nine people don't forget to turn off their generators."

"No," Rosalind agreed. "Nine people don't forget anything."

He nosed the Patience along a submerged fence line until the hull kissed mud beneath a lean-to that had once been a boathouse. The water here stood waist-deep over what had plainly been a yard—a clothesline still strung between posts, a child's bicycle handlebar breaking the surface like a question nobody wanted to answer. The delta did this, drowned everything and kept the shapes of things visible just under the skin of the water, so you spent your whole life poling past the ghosts of ordinary afternoons.

Ilsa stepped over the gunwale without waiting for the boat to settle, boots finding the lean-to's platform with the certainty of someone who had walked it before. Rosalind noticed that. He noticed most things about Ilsa Vane, mostly because she worked so hard to make them unnoticeable—the way her eyes tracked left before she lied, the way she handled a boat like a woman raised on water but pretending otherwise, the way she'd slept last night with one hand closed around something in her jacket pocket that she thought he hadn't seen.

"Camp's through the rise," she said. "Survey teams always build on high ground near the channel. Standard protocol."

"Standard protocol," Rosalind repeated. "You know a lot of protocols for a journalist."

"I've covered a lot of surveys." She was already moving, and the conversation ended the way conversations with Ilsa tended to end: not concluded, merely abandoned, like a camp with its generators running.

* * *

They came up through a stand of dead melaleuca, bark hanging off the trunks in wet ribbons, and the camp opened below them in a shallow bowl: four large tents around a central mess fly, antenna mast, solar array angled uselessly at a sky that hadn't shown sun in eleven days, and the generators—two of them, big industrial units on pallets, chugging away under a tarp rigged as a rain roof. Rain drummed on canvas in a rhythm so constant Rosalind's ears had stopped hearing it as sound and started hearing it as silence.

"Nobody's home," he murmured.

"Nobody's been home," Ilsa corrected softly. "There's a difference."

There was. A camp abandoned in haste looks like a fight—knocked-over chairs, scattered gear, doors flapping. A camp abandoned in panic looks like a wound—things dropped mid-use, half-finished sentences in object form. This camp looked like neither. It looked *tidy*. Tents zipped shut. Mess tables wiped down. Boots lined up outside the sleeping tents in neat pairs, nine pairs, laces tucked in.

Rosalind counted them twice. Nine pairs of boots, standing at attention in the mud, waiting for feet that were not coming back.

"People don't leave without their boots," he said. His voice came out lower than he intended. "Not in this country. Not in this season."

"Maybe they were evacuated."

"Evacuation means you take your boots." He crouched beside the nearest pair. Dry inside. Mud dried on the soles in a pattern days old, cracked like old paint. "And evacuation leaves a record. Flight plans, radio traffic, a note nailed to a door. There's nothing here. You told me yourself—your sources heard nothing from this team for six days."

"My sources are good."

"Your sources are quiet, which is worse."

She shot him a look that might have been anger or agreement—Ilsa's expressions came in one flavor, weatherproof—and pushed past him toward the mess tent.

Inside, the horror refined itself. The long table under the mess fly held the remains of a meal: bowls of congee gone to gray skin, a pot of tea grown cold and thick, someone's mug sitting on a folded newspaper with a ring of dried tea at its base like a tide line marking where a person used to be. A card game sat mid-hand on an ammunition crate—cards face down, chips stacked, a half-eaten orange beside one player's elbow slowly collapsing into blue mold. Nobody plays cards and walks away from a hand in progress. Nobody leaves an orange to rot unless they believe they're coming back for it, or unless someone has decided for them that they aren't.

"Collected," Rosalind said quietly. He hadn't meant to say it aloud.

"What?"

"That's the word you're looking for. They weren't lost. They weren't evacuated. They were collected. Like a debt." He heard Brann's voice in his own head as he said it, cheerful and terrible: *Everything's recoverable, Eriksen. People, property, principal. It's all just a matter of who holds the paper.* He shook it off. "Somebody came up that channel with enough boats and enough authority to take nine people and leave their dinner getting cold."

Ilsa had stopped listening to him. She was moving through the camp with a new kind of purpose, and Rosalind watched her do it, because watching Ilsa work was like watching weather—you couldn't stop it, but you could learn its tells. She checked the comms tent first. Then the survey tent. Her hands moved fast and precise through equipment bags, and every time she finished with one, her shoulders dropped another degree.

"Looking for something?" he asked.

"Data drives. Survey records. That's what your Syndicate hired you to salvage, isn't it?" She didn't look up. "Hard cases, yellow labels, serial numbers starting with DA. Government inventory."

"That's the contract."

"Then help me look instead of narrating."

He helped. Or he appeared to. He worked through the sleeping tents while she tore through the survey tent, and what he found in the sleeping tents made the back of his neck prickle worse than the boots had. Personal effects everywhere—photographs pinned up, a guitar leaning in a corner with strings gone rusty, letters, a carved wooden bird on a shelf, someone's collection of river stones arranged by color. Everything a person owns when they plan to come home. And every single piece of government issue—radios, laptops, GPS units, field tablets, even the cheap digital cameras clipped to tent poles—gone. Not missing. *Gone*, with the deliberate absence of things removed by checklist.

"They took every device," he called out. "Every map, every drive, every battery that could hold a memory. But they left the photographs. Left the letters. Left—" he picked up the wooden bird, turned it over, set it down. "Whoever collected them wasn't interested in their lives. Only their data."

"Or interested in making sure the data never saw daylight," Ilsa said from the survey tent doorway. Her face had gone pale under its weathering. "Because there's nothing here. No drives. No hard copies. They stripped it clean."

"Then we report the salvage as failed and go home." He said it lightly, testing. "Contract's void. Brann can smile at somebody else."

Something crossed her face then—fast, involuntary, gone before he could name it. Disappointment? Fear? Relief? With Ilsa it was always some cocktail of all three.

"We didn't come all this way to leave empty," she said.

"*You* didn't come all this way for salvage at all." He kept his voice even. "You were on that sandbar with a broken radio and a survey jacket that fits you like it was tailored. You knew the channel markers. You knew the camp protocol. You walked into that mess tent like a woman checking on a house she grew up in. So let's try honesty for five minutes, Vane. What are we actually looking for?"

The rain filled the silence between them, drumming on canvas, on water, on nine pairs of dry boots.

"A case," she said finally. "Gray polymer, about so big—" her hands drew a rectangle the size of a bread loaf, "—reinforced corners, no markings. Dr. Anselm kept it in the survey tent under the floor panel. He was paranoid about it. Slept with it closer than his sidearm."

"Dr. Anselm being...?"

"The team lead. One of the nine." Her jaw tightened. "If the collectors took the team and stripped the obvious gear, maybe they missed the case. Maybe Anselm hid it too well."

"And what's in it that makes a paranoid man sleep next to it?"

Ilsa looked at him for a long moment, rain running off the mess fly in beaded curtains behind her, and whatever calculation she performed, she performed it fast.

"Proof," she said. "Of why the floods happen where they happen. Of why they happen *when* they happen. Proof with numbers attached."

Rosalind felt the words land somewhere below his ribs, in the place where he kept the ledger of his own debts, and he understood suddenly that whatever was in that case, it was worth more than his rig, more than his contract, more than the number Brann carried in his smiling book. Worth killing for, possibly. Worth the nine pairs of boots.

"Under the floor panel," he said. "Show me."

* * *

The survey tent's floor was aluminum decking screwed to a frame, and it took them twenty minutes of working by lantern light—rain hammering the canvas above like applause from a crowd that wanted something terrible—to find it. Third panel from the north wall, screws freshly backed out and reseated, the metal scored with pry marks where someone had been in a hurry and given up or been interrupted.

Beneath it, wrapped in a groundsheet and bound with survey tape, a gray polymer case with reinforced corners and no markings at all.

Ilsa made a sound when she lifted it out—not quite a word, low and broken, and she hugged the case to her chest the way you hold something that is either precious or explosive. Rosalind watched her and did the arithmetic he was getting too practiced at: she'd known exactly where to look, which meant she'd known Anselm's habits, which meant she'd known Anselm, which meant the jacket on the sandbar hadn't been found. It had been *given*. Or taken. Every answer she gave him opened three questions, and the questions were starting to stack up like sandbags against a rising river.

"Open it," he said.

"Not here."

"It's a plastic box, Vane, not a reliquary."

"It's proof," she snapped, and her voice cracked on the word like ice on a warming lake. "It's nine people's work and nine people's lives and it stays sealed until we're somewhere the wrong ears can't reach it. Somewhere with walls. Somewhere—" She stopped herself. Breathed. When she spoke again her voice was flat and controlled and lying. "Somewhere safe."

"There's no such thing as somewhere safe in this delta. There's only places the danger hasn't found yet." He held out his hand. "Give it here. I'll carry it. If we're swimming, you'll want your arms free."

She hesitated—weighed him, weighed the case, weighed whatever private ledger she kept—and then, surprisingly, handed it over. Their fingers touched on the handle and both of them pretended they hadn't noticed.

"Rosalind." Her voice had changed register entirely, gone quiet and serious as deep water. "When you know what's in this case—and you will know, soon, because I'm done carrying it alone—there's no going back to being the man Brann collects from. You understand that? This doesn't clear debts. This creates them. The permanent kind."

"I'm already in the permanent kind of trouble. I just haven't read the terms yet." He slung the case across his back with the survey tape. It weighed almost nothing. Things that heavy never did. "Come on. Storm's building in the west and I want the Patience tied down before—"

He stopped.

Because he'd heard it, faint under the rain and the generator drone: engines. More than one. Deep-throated, diesel-heavy, coming up the eastern channel at cruising speed, unhurried, the way boats move when they own the water they're traveling.

Ilsa heard them a heartbeat later. Her head came up like a bird dog's.

"How many?" she whispered.

"Two. Maybe three." He killed the lantern. Darkness dropped into the tent, total and soft. Through the canvas he could see the gray light flicker as something large passed beyond the treeline, throwing shadows across the drowned camp. "Collectors," he breathed. "Coming back for anything they missed."

The gray case pressed cold against his spine. In the dark, Ilsa's hand found his wrist and gripped it—hard, urgent, alive.

"Then they're about to miss us," she whispered, "or we're about to join the boots."
