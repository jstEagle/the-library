## 6. What the Floods Were For

The case was under the floor of the mess tent, and Ilsa Vane had known it would be before she ever set foot back in the camp.

She knew because she had been the one to hide it there.

Three weeks ago—three weeks that felt like three years, measured in rain instead of days—she had knelt on this same ground with a pry bar and a flashlight clenched in her teeth, lifting the aluminum panel while the others slept. She had wrapped the case in a groundsheet, wedged it into the cavity beneath the flooring where the generator's fuel line ran, and sealed it again with mud pressed flat by her own palm. Then she had gone back to her bunk and lain awake listening to nine people breathe, knowing what she was going to do, hating herself for it, doing it anyway.

Now she stood over the same spot with Rosalind Eriksen watching her from the tent flap, rain drumming the canvas above them like fingers counting down.

"You knew it was here," he said. Not a question. His voice had the flat calm of a man who has already done his arithmetic and doesn't like the total.

"I hoped," she said, which was also not an answer, and they both knew it.

* * *

The camp was wrong in the way that empty rooms are wrong—the specific wrongness of interruption rather than departure. Ilsa walked through it with her heart climbing into her throat and her hands steady, because her hands always stayed steady; it was the rest of her that shook.

Okafor's mug still sat on the folding table by the radio shack, half-full of something gone gray with rainwater, a spoon standing upright in it like a flag planted on claimed territory. The generators were still running—both of them, the big diesel and the backup, chugging away in their shelter of sandbags as if the people who fueled them might walk back in at any moment and complain about the noise. Someone had left a card game mid-hand on the chart table beneath the awning: four players' worth of tiles, a score pad with Okafor's looping handwriting, a bet unresolved. The rain had gotten to the score pad. The ink had run so the numbers bled into each other, all their debts and winnings dissolved into one gray smear.

Nobody leaves a card game. Nobody leaves a mug. Nobody leaves two generators burning fuel for three weeks unless somebody else decided when they stopped being allowed to leave.

Rosalind moved through the camp the way she imagined a scavenger bird moves—low, quick-eyed, reading everything without touching anything. He crouched at the edge of the compound where the mud began and studied the ground for a long time.

"No tracks," he said finally. "Not old ones, not new ones. Nine people, gear this heavy, vehicles coming in or out—you'd expect the mud to remember."

"The water remembers instead." Ilsa heard her own voice come out strange, scraped thin. "That's how they do it. That's how they did it."

He looked up at her then, and there was something in his face—not suspicion exactly, though suspicion lived in it too, permanent as weather. Something more like recognition. The look of a man watching another liar's mask slip and deciding, for the moment, not to reach for it.

"Did who do what?" he asked.

She should have lied. She was good at lying; she'd been lying since the night she slipped out of this camp with a dry bag and a stolen kayak paddle, since before that, maybe, if she counted the application she'd forged to join the survey in the first place. But the camp was around her, Okafor's mug and Mirela's rosary hanging on the tent pole and the card game nobody finished, and the lie wouldn't come. It sat in her throat like a stone and refused to be spoken.

"Help me get the case first," she said. "Then I'll tell you what's in it. And then you can decide whether you want to be near me when the people who took them come back."

* * *

The pry bar was still where she'd left it, wedged behind the fuel drums, rusted now along its edge. Rosalind watched her work without offering to help, which she understood—he wanted to see how well she knew this place, how practiced her hands were, how much of her story was true. Let him look. By the time the panel came up, sodden and groaning, there would be nothing left to hide except the thing itself.

The case came out heavier than she remembered, or perhaps she was weaker than she'd been. Waterlogged groundsheet, mud caked in the latches. She carried it to the chart table, swept the ruined card game aside with her forearm—an apology murmured to no one, to everyone—and popped the seals.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth: three data drives, a stack of printed schematics in waterproof sleeves, a field notebook swollen with damp, and a small hard drive labeled in her own handwriting, because she had been the communications officer and the backups had been her responsibility and her responsibility, in the end, had been to steal them.

Rosalind leaned over the table. Rain ran off the awning between them in a beaded curtain, and through it she watched him read the top schematic, and she watched the reading change him. She'd seen that happen once before—Mirela, the hydrologist, the night she'd cross-referenced the levee maintenance logs against the rainfall records and gone very quiet, very still, the way people go when the world rearranges itself around a fact. Rosalind went quiet the same way. Still the same way.

"These are breach points," he said at last. His finger hovered over the schematic without touching it, the way you hover over a wound. "Structural diagrams of the Saltmere levees. Load tolerances. And these—" he turned a page, "—these are flow projections. What happens downstream if the eastern embankment fails at high tide."

"Yes."

"This is engineering, not weather." He said it slowly, testing each word like ice on a pond. "Somebody modeled this. Somebody planned where the water would go."

"Somebody planned where the water would go, and then somebody made sure it went there." Ilsa pulled the notebook toward her, opened it to the page she had marked with a strip of survey tape. Her hands were steady. Her voice wasn't, quite. "The Water Reclamation Authority filed these as failure analyses after the spring floods. Contingency studies. What happens *if*. Except look at the dates."

He looked. She watched him look. The rain filled the silence for both of them.

"The contingency studies predate the floods," he said.

"By eleven months. All three of them. You don't model a disaster almost a year before it happens unless you're not modeling a disaster. You're rehearsing one." She turned pages for him, laying it out the way Mirela had laid it out for the team, the way it had been laid out for Ilsa in the radio shack at two in the morning with the generator humming and the rain coming down just like this. "And here—" the maintenance logs, photocopied, annotated in Mirela's small precise hand, "—the sluice gates on the eastern embankment were opened manually during the storm surge. There's a service record. Two Authority technicians signed out gate keys the night of the breach. Their names are redacted, but the signature log isn't. And here is the land registry overlay."

Rosalind turned the last sheet and stopped moving entirely.

Ilsa let him sit with it. The map showed the delta parishes, the flood extent shaded blue, and over it, in red, the parcel boundaries of every holding acquired by the Crownland Development Consortium in the eighteen months following the water. Farm by farm, wharf by wharf, churchyard by churchyard. The red parcels followed the blue water the way a shadow follows a body. Where the flood had reached, the Consortium owned. Where it hadn't, it didn't.

"They bought the drowning," Rosalind said quietly.

"They bought it at panic prices, from people standing waist-deep in their own kitchens, and then they rezoned it before the mud dried." She closed the notebook. "The government engineered the floods to depopulate the delta. The Authority breached the levees. The Consortium absorbed the land. And the survey team found the paper trail connecting all three, because finding things is what we were sent here to do—we just weren't supposed to find *this*."

* * *

The rain thickened toward evening, and they retreated into the mess tent, and Ilsa told him the rest of it with the case open between them like a body on a table.

She told him about joining the survey team under a name that was mostly true—Ilsa Vane, communications specialist, references verified by a man who owed her a favor—and about the first months of honest work, mapping channels, sampling sediment, cataloging the drowned forest. She told him about Mirela pulling the records, about the night the whole team gathered in the radio shack and the truth came apart in their hands like wet rope. About the argument that lasted until dawn. About Commander Okafor saying they had to transmit it, all of it, immediately, and someone else—she could still hear the voice but refused to attach it to a face—saying transmission meant satellite uplink meant logged traffic meant the Authority would know within the hour, and asking whether anyone truly believed the Authority would let nine witnesses file that report and board a boat home.

About the vote. About losing it.

"And so you left," Rosalind said.

"So I left." She made herself hold his eyes. He had a face built for stillness, weathered and unreadable, and she understood suddenly why he'd survived as long as he clearly had on this coast: he could wait out anything, including other people's confessions. "They voted to stay and document everything first. Build an airtight case, make copies, distribute them. Sensible. Careful. I voted for running. I lost, and then—" her jaw ached; she realized she was clenching it, "—then I did the coward's arithmetic. I figured if I stayed and they were right, fine. And if they were wrong—if the Authority came—I'd be one more body in the delta instead of one person alive who knew where the evidence was hidden. So I hid the case, and I told myself that was the brave version, and then I took a kayak up the tidal channel at three in the morning in the middle of a squall, and I didn't look back, because looking back is how you turn around."

The rain hammered the canvas. Somewhere out in the drowned trees, a branch gave way and went down with a long groan.

"Nine people," Rosalind said. Not cruelly. Just placing the number on the table between them, the way you'd set down a weight to see if the other person could lift it.

"Nine people." Her throat worked. "Okafor. Mirela. Devi and Tomas, the techs. Anselm and Bright, the surveyors. Little Ferro, who was nineteen and lied about his age to get on the crew. Ruth Osei, who kept a garden in planter boxes on the deck of the barge and made us all eat her bitter greens. And my—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "There was a junior hydrologist. Callum. He voted to stay because he believed careful work would protect us. He was twenty-six. He used to sing off-key in the radio shack while I tried to calibrate the uplink, and I told him a hundred times to shut up, and I would give—" Her voice failed on the arithmetic of it. She let the silence carry what it could.

Rosalind waited. When it was clear she wasn't going to finish the sentence, he didn't finish it for her, and she was grateful, because any comfort offered right then would have broken something she needed intact.

"There were no tracks," he said instead. "No signs of struggle. Generators still running. Whoever took them wanted the camp to look occupied. Which means they came quiet, and organized, and they knew the layout—which means someone told them the layout."

"Or they simply asked the Authority for the site plans. They're partners. Why would they need to spy?" She laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, jagged, nothing like laughter. "You want to know the worst part? The part I carry around like a stone in my boot? For three weeks I've been telling myself they might have been taken somewhere and held. Questioned. Released eventually, when the operation was secure. People survive being abducted. It happens. And then today I saw Okafor's mug sitting there full of rainwater, and the card game unfinished, and I thought—they didn't even let them pack. And I thought about what you don't leave tracks in." She looked down at her hands, steady, always steady, traitorously steady. "The delta keeps what the water takes. Everyone on this coast knows that. I grew up knowing it."

Something flickered across his face at that—*grew up*, the phrase, some door opening briefly behind his eyes—but he said nothing about it, and she was too raw to chase it.

Instead he reached across the table and turned one of the data drives over in his fingers, studying it like a man appraising salvage, which, she supposed, was exactly what he was doing. She watched him weigh it. She had watched men weigh things her whole life—her father weighing catch against debt, buyers weighing fish against coin—and she knew every stage of the expression, and she knew what came next.

"How much," he said, "do you figure something like this is worth?"

And there it was. She'd known it was coming since the sandbar, since she'd clocked the salt-corroded sensor rig on his skiff and the way he talked about money the way drowning men talk about air. She wasn't even angry. That was the terrible thing. She couldn't afford anger; anger was a luxury for people whose hands weren't already full.

"To the right buyer?" she said. "Enough to clear any debt you've got and buy a new rig and never chase a storm for hire again. To the Consortium or the Authority—it's worth whatever it costs to make it disappear, plus a margin. They've spent fortunes already. A little more to bury the receipts is nothing."

"And to everyone else?"

"To everyone else it's proof that the government drowned the delta on purpose. Proof that the floods had authors." She leaned forward. "It's the difference between a natural disaster and a crime. Everything changes if this sees daylight. Compensation claims. Land restitution. Prosecutions. Whole parishes get their history rewritten—from *acts of God* to *arrestable offenses*."

Rosalind set the drive down carefully, aligning it with the others, a small fussy precision that told her more than his face did. He was a man who handled valuable things carefully because he had learned what happened to careless men on this coast.

"You hid this case," he said slowly, "before you ran. You stole your own team's evidence and buried it under their feet, and then you left them sleeping above it."

"I told you. In case they were wrong."

"No." He shook his head once, and there was no judgment in it, only a kind of weary clarity that frightened her more than judgment would have. "In case *you* needed it later. That's not cowardice, Vane. That's the same ledger everybody on this coast keeps. I keep one too. Mine's written in somebody else's book, but it's the same math—what I'll trade, what I'll risk, what I'll walk past to stay breathing." He tapped the case once, lightly, like knocking on a door. "So here's my question, and I want the honest answer, because I'm about to decide how much of my neck to stake on you. If it comes down to this evidence and your skin—or mine—which one goes in the water?"

Outside, the monsoon answered first: a wall of wind hit the camp, snapping the awning lines, driving rain sideways through the tent flap in a cold breath that guttered the lantern. In the sudden dark and the wild light, Ilsa thought about the honest answer, and found she didn't have one yet—that the honest answer depended on a version of herself she hadn't met, standing at a moment she hadn't reached, and that all she could honestly say was:

"I came back for them." Her voice was low and it did not shake, and she held onto that. "Everything else—the case, the story, whatever you want to call it—I came back because nine people are in that water, or in a grave, or in a hole somewhere, and I was the one who left, and I am not leaving twice. Whatever that makes me worth to you, that's the price. Take it or row me back to the sandbar."

The lantern steadied. Rosalind looked at her for a long moment, rain dripping from the canvas between them, and then he nodded—slowly, once, the nod of a man signing something.

"Then we'd better move," he said. "Because that skiff of ours rides low enough already, and I've got a feeling the people who emptied this camp aren't finished checking it. Somebody's been feeding those generators, Vane. Fresh fuel cans by the shed, and the mud's churned inside the compound line. We're not the first ones back."

She froze, listening—really listening—for the first time since they'd landed. Under the rain, under the generators' chug, far off across the drowned forest where the channels braided together into darkness: the low, patient throb of an engine that was not theirs, coming upriver with the storm.
