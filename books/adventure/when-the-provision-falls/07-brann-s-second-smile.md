## 7. Brann's Second Smile

The airboat came out of the rain like a rumor made flesh.

Rosalind heard it first as a change in the water—a tremor in the Patience's hull that wasn't wind, wasn't current, but something heavier moving with intent across the drowned forest to the south. He was standing in the skiff's stern with a bail bucket in one hand and Ilsa's hidden case wedged under his boot, and for three full seconds he let himself believe it was driftwood shifting in the current, or a gator rolling, or any of the hundred small noises the delta made when it wanted to be left alone.

Then the engine note resolved into something mechanical and deliberate, and his stomach dropped through the bilge water like a stone.

"Airboat," he said.

Ilsa looked up from the bow, where she'd been rigging a tarp over the last of their dry stores. Her face did something complicated—fear, then calculation, then a flat calm she wore like a borrowed coat. "How far?"

"Close. Coming on." He set down the bucket. His hands were steady, which surprised him; inside, everything was already running, fast and cold, the way it always did before lightning struck. "That'll be Brann."

"You're sure."

"He's the only man on this coast who'd run an airboat into standing timber at full throttle." Rosalind wiped rain from his eyes and squinted south, where the drowned cypress stood hip-deep in brown water, their dead crowns like the fingers of hands reaching up from a grave. "And he's the only one who knows where I said I'd be."

The case sat under his boot. Black polymer, government stenciling half scoured away by floodwater, heavy with drives and documents that could depose a ministry or buy a man's freedom, depending on which way you turned them toward the light. They had pulled it from beneath the floor of the survey team's command tent two days ago, and in those two days Rosalind had learned exactly what it weighed—not in kilos, but in consequences.

"We could run," Ilsa said.

"Into what? The delta's a bowl. Every channel drains toward every other channel. He's got an airboat and we've got a hull that takes on water if you look at it wrong." He was already moving, though—pulling the tarp loose from the stores, dragging the case out from under his boot. "We don't run. We hide it, and we lie."

"You lie badly," Ilsa said. It wasn't cruelty. It was inventory.

"I know," Rosalind said. "Help me."

* * *

They buried the case in the mud of a small hummock island thirty meters off their anchorage, wrapped in the tarp, weighted with a coil of chain, covered with silt and a scatter of water hyacinth torn up by the roots. It took four minutes. Rosalind counted. Four minutes to hide the most dangerous object on the coast, and then they were back aboard the Patience, arranging themselves—him at the tiller, her amidships among the gear—with the studied casualness of people who had absolutely nothing to declare.

The airboat broke the tree line a minute later.

It came on like a blade through silk, fan roaring, flattening the hyacinth beds behind it into brown wake, and Rosalind watched it come and felt the old familiar dread settle over him—not the sharp terror of gunfire or flood, but the slow, cold dread of arithmetic. Of ledgers. Of a smiling man with a book in which Rosalind Eriksen's name occupied a line, and the number beside that name grew larger every month he failed to pay.

Brann stood at the airboat's controls the way other men stood at altars. Big through the shoulders, silver at the temples, dressed—absurdly, perfectly—in a dry linen suit the color of bone, as though the monsoon were a formality he'd declined to observe. He cut the engine fifty meters out and let the boat glide the rest of the way, so that the silence arrived before he did, and in that silence Rosalind could hear his own heart working and the rain ticking on the tarp and Ilsa breathing carefully beside him.

"Eriksen." Brann's voice carried across the water without effort, warm as a hearth. "You look terrible. Monsoon agrees with you the way debt agrees with most men."

"Brann." Rosalind kept his voice level. "You found me fast."

"I keep good books." The collector stepped aboard without asking, and the Patience rocked under his weight, shipping a sheet of green water across the gunwale that sloshed around all their boots. He didn't seem to notice. His eyes moved over the skiff—the bail bucket, the wet stores, the patched tarp, Ilsa—and Rosalind watched him read the boat like a page. "And where," Brann said pleasantly, "is my salvage?"

* * *

Here is what Rosalind knew about lying: he was bad at it, and he had always been bad at it, and the reason was not that he lacked imagination but that he had too much conscience for the trade. A good liar built his falsehood on a foundation of truth and mortared it smooth. Rosalind's falsehoods always showed the seams, because some part of him flinched at the mortar.

So he did what he could. He gave Brann truth, and hoped the shape of it would pass.

"The camp was stripped," he said. "Generators running, food on the tables, but every map, every drive, every instrument of value gone. Whoever took the team took the data with them. I found hulls and rust, Brann. That's all."

"Mm." Brann had picked up the bail bucket and was turning it in his hands as though appraising it. "And the nine of them? Nine trained survey personnel, vanished between one tide and the next, no tracks, no boats missing?"

"No tracks in the mud," Rosalind said. "I told your office what I told everyone. Whatever happened here, the water was involved."

"The water is always involved, Eriksen. That's rather the point of this coast." Brann set the bucket down with exaggerated care. His smile hadn't moved since he boarded—it sat on his face like weather, fixed and warm and utterly without mercy—and now it widened by a degree, which was somehow worse than any frown. "Walk me through it. You anchored where? You went ashore when? Show me."

Rosalind's mouth had gone dry. He gestured south, toward the camp site, and began to talk—channels, timings, the state of the tents, the half-eaten meal still congealing on its plates—and as he talked he watched Brann watch him, and understood with a sick lurch that the collector wasn't listening to the content at all. He was listening to the cadence. Hunting the hesitation the way a pike hunts the shadow of smaller fish.

Beside him, Ilsa spoke up, filling gaps Rosalind didn't know he'd left. She gave the story bones: the sandbar, the broken radio, how she'd hired him at Saltmere to reach the camp, the journalist's hunger for a story about the vanishing. She lied beautifully, fluently, with the easy rhythm of someone for whom falsehood was simply another dialect. Rosalind marveled at it even as sweat crawled down his spine.

Brann listened to her with his head tilted, like a man hearing music from a country he'd visited once and hadn't liked.

"A journalist," he said at last.

"Freelance," Ilsa said.

"For whom?"

"Myself. My readers. Whoever buys."

"And what do your readers make of the survey team?" Brann's tone stayed light, conversational, a man asking after mutual friends. "The government's own people, gone from the middle of a government project. There must be theories. Conspiracies. The kind of thing that sells."

"There are always theories," Ilsa said. "Most of them are weather."

"Yes," Brann agreed. "Most things are weather, until they aren't." He turned back to Rosalind, and for a moment—one long, glacial moment—the smile thinned, and underneath it Rosalind saw the actual face of the man: patient, thorough, and entirely without sentiment. "My employers sent me to collect equipment worth more than your life, Eriksen. They are... disappointed is too strong. Let us say they are curious. The Meridian Syndicate does not lend money to optimists by accident. We lend to men who find things. And when a man finds nothing where something was promised—" he spread his hands, an apology to the rain, "—we begin to wonder whether the nothing is hiding something."

"The camp was empty," Rosalind said. His voice cracked on the second word, just slightly, just enough, and he hated himself for it.

"Yes," Brann said softly. "You said."

* * *

What happened next happened slowly, the way storms build.

Brann walked the Patience. Not asked—walked it, stepping over the coiled lines, lifting the tarp, peering into the hold where their bailed water sloshed around a crate of salt pork. He examined the anchor. He examined the fuel drum. At the stern he paused over the place where, until twenty minutes ago, a black polymer case had sat under Rosalind's boot, and he crouched there, and he touched the wet planking with two fingers, and he said nothing at all.

Rosalind stood at the tiller and felt the terror arrive fully now, not as cold arithmetic but as animal fact. His mouth tasted of copper. Somewhere under thirty meters of muddy water and a foot of silt lay the only thing standing between him and whatever Brann did to men whose lies showed their seams, and the only question was whether the mud would hold its secret for one more hour.

Brann straightened. Turned. The smile was back, restored to factory settings, warm as lamplight.

"Well," he said. "Empty camps. Vanished teams. A journalist with no publication. A debtor with no salvage." He sighed, and the sigh was almost tender. "Do you know what I love about this work, Eriksen? Everyone thinks collecting is about violence. It isn't. Violence is what happens when collection has already failed. Collection is information. Knowing where a man sleeps. What he owes. What he fears." He glanced at Ilsa. "What he's protecting."

"We came for a story," Ilsa said.

"Of course you did." Brann reached into his jacket, and Rosalind's whole body clenched—but what came out was not a weapon. It was a folded paper, dry despite the deluge, kept in oilskin. Brann held it out. "Your revised terms. Since salvage has proven elusive, my employers have restructured your account. Read it."

Rosalind took the paper with fingers that didn't feel like his own. Rain spattered the oilskin; he shielded it and read.

The language was elegant. It was always elegant. The Syndicate wrote contracts like love letters. In summary: the outstanding balance—every coin of it, three years of compounding interest, the sensor rig, the boat he'd wrecked in Vell's Landing, all of it—would be forgiven. Entirely. Forgiven, in exchange for delivery of certain materials recovered from the survey site. Materials described, in the contract's careful phrasing, as *documents and data media of governmental origin, condition as found, contents unexamined.*

Unexamined. As though the Syndicate didn't already know what was in the case. As though they hadn't known before they ever sent him upriver.

The bottom of the page went cold in Rosalind's hands. Because here was the thing he'd been refusing to see since Saltmere, since the offer had worn its fair face and smiled at him: the Syndicate hadn't sent a drowning man into the delta to fish out hardware. Hardware they could have bought anywhere. They'd sent him because a debtor asks no questions, works cheap, and can be erased from the ledger afterward. They'd sent him to retrieve evidence that the government had breached its own levees—had drowned villages, had made a sea of people's lives—to seize land. Evidence the government very much wanted back. And the Meridian Syndicate, those cheerful optimists, those lenders of last resort, were not chasing the debt at all.

They were brokering. Holding the government's secret hostage, or selling it, or trading it for concessions no honest ledger would record. And Rosalind Eriksen, storm chaser, debtor, man who kept moving because stopping meant settling accounts—he was the courier they'd hired precisely because he couldn't afford to wonder what he was carrying.

He looked up. Brann was watching him with the expression of a teacher watching a slow student arrive, at last, at the answer.

"Now you see it," the collector said gently. "Yes. We knew what the survey found before you did. We knew before they found it, if we're honest—our clients tell us things. All you had to do was bring it in, and walk free. No debt. No Brann at your shoulder. Clean paper, Eriksen. A new life." He spread his hands. "Instead you give me empty camps and a story about weather. So let me ask plainly, and please, please don't insult us both: where is the case?"

* * *

The rain filled the silence. On the water, somewhere east, thunder muttered like an argument in another room.

Rosalind became aware, distantly, that Ilsa had shifted her weight. That her right hand had drifted, casually, to the boathook lying along the gunwale. That she was watching Brann the way she'd once watched a rising river gauge—with total attention and no visible feeling.

He thought about the truth. One sentence and it was over: *it's under the mud on the hummock.* And then the debt was dead, and he was free, and the documents went into Brann's oilskin and from there into whatever dark room the Syndicate kept its leverage, and the drowned villages stayed drowned, officially, forever, and the names of nine missing people stayed names and never became answers. Free. He'd spent three years dreaming of the word, and now it was being offered to him at the price of everyone the floods had taken, and the terrible thing—the thing he would carry for the rest of his life—was how long he hesitated.

Not zero seconds. He would not lie to himself about that. Long enough to taste it. Long enough to want it.

"It's gone," Rosalind said.

Brann's smile did not move. "Gone."

"Flooded out. Lost. I found the camp, I found a strongbox bolted under the command tent floor, and I brought it out, and two nights ago in the surge it went overboard. Chain, tarp, everything. It's in the channel somewhere under six feet of silt, and no diver alive will find it before the season ends." The lie came out of him rough and ugly and showing every seam, and he kept talking anyway, because the alternative was worse. "I'm sorry. I know what that costs me. Write it in the book."

For a moment Brann said nothing. The rain ticked. The Patience rocked. Then the collector laughed—a short, genuine sound, almost delighted—and shook his head.

"Oh, Eriksen. You are *terrible* at this." He stepped back to his airboat, unhurried, a man concluding a pleasant social call. "But here's the thing about terrible liars. They're terrible because they mean it. Which means somewhere in that stubborn, drowning skull of yours, you've decided that case is worth more than your freedom. And that, my friend, is information too." He settled at the controls. The fan began to turn. "So I won't search your little boat. I won't put a hook in you today. I'm going to go and consult with people who are paid to think, and when I come back—and I will come back, the dry season is coming, the water's going down, and everything this coast is hiding is going to be standing out in the open like a heron at low tide—we'll settle accounts properly. You, me, and whatever you're keeping under the mud."

The airboat swung away. Brann raised one hand in farewell, bone-colored sleeve dripping, smile intact to the last.

"Read your contract!" he called over the engine noise. "Terms remain valid! Optimism, Eriksen! It's what we're known for!"

Then the roar swallowed everything, and the drowned forest closed behind him, and he was gone.

* * *

For a long time neither of them moved.

Then Ilsa said, quietly, "You lied."

"I lied badly."

"You lied *badly*," she agreed, "and he bought it anyway, which frightens me more than if he hadn't." She turned to face him, rain streaming off her jaw, and her eyes had the flat bright quality he'd started to recognize as her version of fear. "He knew. Didn't he. About the documents. Before we ever found them."

"They're brokers," Rosalind said. The word came out tasting like bilgewater. "The government engineered the floods. The Syndicate is holding the receipts. And I'm the mule they hired to carry them, because a mule with debts doesn't ask what's in the pack." He looked down at the contract in his hand—at the elegant language, the forgiven balance, the clean paper they'd offered him in exchange for burying nine people twice. "Three years I thought they owned my future. Turns out they were renting it out the whole time."

"What do we do?"

Rosalind looked south, toward the hummock where the case slept under its blanket of silt and hyacinth. Then north, upriver, toward the fortified station where the man who had sold the rain was counting his profits. Then at Ilsa, who had lied to him about who she was and saved his life without hesitation and was now looking at him as if his answer might decide something larger than either of them.

"We stop lying to each other," he said. "Starting now. Because Brann's coming back with thinkers, and the water's going down, and when it does—" he crumpled the contract and let the river take it, "—we'd better know exactly what we're willing to drown for."

Ilsa held his gaze a moment longer. Then she nodded, once, and reached for the bail bucket, and the Patience turned upriver into the rain.

Behind them, far to the south, thunder rolled again—closer now, and getting closer, like something walking.
