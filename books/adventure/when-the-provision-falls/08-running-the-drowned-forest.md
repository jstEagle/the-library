## 8. Running the Drowned Forest

The first sign of Brann was not a sound but a silence—a hole in the delta's constant conversation of frogs and dripping canopy, as if the whole drowned forest had held its breath at once.

Rosalind cut the Patience's engine mid-stroke and let the skiff drift, his hand flat on the tiller like a man calming a dog. Ahead, the channel narrowed into a corridor of flooded gum trees, their trunks silvered with waterline rot, their crowns dead and rattling in the wind that preceded the monsoon's next wave. The light had gone the color of old pewter. Somewhere behind them—two channels back, maybe three—an airboat's fan was winding down.

"He knows we heard him," Ilsa said. She'd wedged herself low against the gunwale with the case between her boots, one hand resting on it the way other people rested hands on children or weapons. "He'll kill the engine too. Now we're two blind things listening for each other."

"Then we stop being blind." Rosalind pulled the chart from inside his jacket—Renk's stolen survey map, its margins dense with soundings—and spread it across his knees. The ink was already blooming where spray had found it. "The drowned forest runs six kilometers east before it opens onto the Sere flats. Airboats can't follow us in there. Too many standing trunks, too much deadfall under the surface."

"And if they wait for us on the far side?"

"Then we come out somewhere they aren't." He traced a line through the timber with one finger, following the ghost of an old logging road the flood had swallowed. "There's a cut here—the loggers' canal. It runs straighter than the river. If the charts are honest, we shave an hour off anything Brann can do around the outside."

"If the charts are honest," Ilsa repeated. She looked at him with that expression she wore when she was deciding how much of her contempt to spend. "You're gambling our lives on a government survey team's bathymetry."

"I'm gambling on the fact that nobody lies about where the bottom is. It's the only kind of honesty left in this delta."

She almost smiled. It was the closest thing to warmth he'd seen from her since the sandbar, and it lasted exactly as long as it took the airboat's fan to spin up again—distant, then not distant, then close enough that the sound arrived as pressure against his eardrums rather than noise.

Rosalind started the engine.

The Patience answered with a cough and a shudder, the way she answered everything, and then they were moving—low and fast down the pewter channel, the bow throwing a blade of spray that caught what little light remained. Behind them the airboat's roar swelled, flattened by distance into something enormous and featureless, a wall of sound coming up the river with Brann inside it.

* * *

The drowned forest took them the way deep water takes a stone: without ceremony.

One moment there was sky overhead; the next, canopy—dead branches interlaced forty feet up, dripping, groaning, closing over the channel until the world was green-black shadow shot through with shafts of rain. The air changed temperature by ten degrees. The smell changed too: rot and tannin and the sweet mushroom reek of wood giving up its argument with gravity after three flooded years.

Rosalind ran the throttle at half, eyes moving in the rhythm he'd learned chasing storms along this coast—scan near, scan far, scan the water, scan the sky. Standing trunks slid past on either side, some no wider than a barrel, some massive as church columns, their submerged flanks invisible. Hitting one at speed would open the Patience like a letter.

"There." Ilsa pointed left, where the water darkened in a straight seam between two giants. "The canal?"

"The canal." He swung the bow toward it. "Stay low. Branches."

The first branch came anyway. It raked the starboard gunwale with a sound like fingernails down slate and showered them with bark and beetles, and Ilsa ducked flat over the case without being told, and something in the economy of that movement—not wasted, not panicked—made Rosalind revise his estimate of her upward again. Journalists, real ones, didn't move like that. Soldiers did. People who'd spent time on boats in bad places did.

The canal was everything the chart promised: straight, deep, mercifully clear of trunks. The Patience picked up speed. For four or five minutes there was nothing but the engine's laboring note, the rain beginning again in fat sporadic drops that struck the canopy above and fell through it a second later, delayed, so that the rain seemed to be arriving from two different skies.

Then the fan-sound came again—but wrong. Muffled. Above them.

Ilsa's head snapped up. "He's not following the river."

"No." Rosalind's stomach went cold and professional. "He's running the levee road. It parallels the canal—elevated, built before the floods. We're in a ditch and he's on the highway."

Through the lattice of dead branches, maybe thirty feet up and fifty feet left, he saw it: a gray shape sliding along the top of the old embankment, fan blazing, spray sheeting off its hull. Brann's pilot was good. Better than good. He was holding the airboat at a speed no sane man would attempt on a levee in rising wind, matching their pace stroke for stroke, and every few seconds Rosalind caught the glint of glass—binoculars, or a rifle scope, no way to know which.

"He can't come down," Ilsa said. "But he doesn't have to. He's calling our position ahead. There'll be boats waiting where the canal meets the flats."

"Then we don't meet the flats." Rosalind was already studying the chart again, one eye on it, one eye on the drowning dark ahead. "There's a side channel here—" he tapped a squiggle that might have been a creek or might have been a cartographer's sneeze, "—through the worst of the deadfall. The section the survey flagged as impassable."

"Because it is."

"Because it was, in daylight, in calm water, for a crew that valued its hull." He fed the throttle. "We are none of those things."

"That's not reassuring, Rosalind."

"It's not meant to be. It's meant to be accurate."

* * *

The side channel announced itself as a black throat in the canal's eastern bank, barely wider than the Patience's beam, choked at its mouth with a raft of storm-felled limbs. Rosalind killed the engine ten meters out and let momentum carry them, and for a long terrible moment the only sounds were water kissing the hull and the airboat's fan receding up the levee, carrying their death warrant north.

"Oars," he said quietly. "Poles if you can find one. We pole through."

They poled. It was brutal, intimate work—Ilsa in the bow pushing off trunks and snags, Rosalind aft, the case lashed now beneath the thwart with the survey jacket wadded around it to muffle any metallic complaint. The channel twisted like a snake with a broken spine. Twice the Patience grounded softly on submerged deadfall and they rocked her free with their weight, breath held, listening. The forest closed overhead until the rain couldn't reach them and the darkness couldn't leave.

It was in that darkness that Rosalind began, for the first time since Saltmere, to feel something adjacent to hope—which was, he knew from long experience, precisely the moment the universe collected its fee.

The crack came from below. Not loud—a wet, fibrous snap, like a knuckle the size of a coffin lid. Then the hull lurched, listed hard to port, and the Patience began, with tremendous dignity, to sink.

"Snag," Rosalind said stupidly, uselessly, and then stopped talking because there was work to do. Water was coming through a split in the planking just forward of the keel, a black seam vomiting the river into the bilge. He grabbed the bail bucket, shoved it at Ilsa, tore open the tool locker, and got his palms flat against the split to feel its shape. A foot, maybe fourteen inches. A limb spike had gone through and torn on the way past, leaving a wound shaped like a jagged L.

"How bad?" Ilsa was bailing, fast and rhythmic, not waiting for the answer.

"We float if we keep ahead of it. Barely." He was already pulling the survey team's epoxy putty from the salvage kit—of course the kit had epoxy; government expeditions traveled prepared for everything except whatever actually happened to them. "Keep bailing. Don't stop bailing even if I scream."

"You're going to scream?"

"Probably."

He went over the side.

The water was warm as blood and opaque as tea, and the moment his head went under, the drowned forest became a cathedral of blind pillars descending into nothing. He felt his way along the keel by touch, lungs burning, and found the wound by the way the inflow tugged at his fingers like a small hungry mouth. He jammed the epoxy into it blind, working it in with his thumbs, feeling the tear's geometry with each pass, and when his chest could hold no more he surfaced gasping beside the hull, spat brown water, and went down again.

Three dives. On the third, the tug against his fingers weakened to a seep. He surfaced, hauled himself over the transom, and lay on the wet planks shaking while Ilsa bailed the last inch of water out around his legs without comment.

"You said you'd scream," she said finally.

"I reconsidered."

"Good. It would've carried." She handed him the bucket. "Your turn. I'm going under to check your patchwork, because I don't trust a man who talks about accuracy while lying to me about screaming."

Her dive was cleaner than his—no splash, barely a ripple. She was under a long time. When she came up she hung on the gunwale, breathing hard, hair plastered flat, and said: "It'll hold in calm water. In a chop, it'll fail. So we don't take a chop."

"In this delta, in this season, chop finds you whether you take it or not."

"Then we'd better be somewhere shallow when it does." She pulled herself aboard and sat dripping, and for a moment neither of them spoke, and the forest dripped around them in counterpoint, and Rosalind realized he was grinning like an idiot and made himself stop.

"What?" she said.

"Nothing. You checked my work."

"Somebody had to. You were busy lying face-down on the floorboards." But there was no edge in it. There was something else instead, something quieter, and Rosalind recognized it with a strange discomfort because he hadn't felt it aimed at him in years: regard. Plain, untransactional regard. He found he didn't know where to put it, so he picked up the oars.

* * *

They rowed the rest of the deadfall country, which was slower than poling but quieter, and the quiet was the problem, because it meant they could hear the fan again.

Not on the levee this time. Ahead. Two fans, maybe three, converging on the canal mouth where it opened onto the Sere flats—exactly where Ilsa had predicted, exactly where the chart's honest depths ended and Brann's patience began. Rosalind eased the oars and let the Patience drift among the trunks, and he and Ilsa listened together, heads tilted, like animals sharing a burrow.

"He's boxed the exit," she breathed.

"He's tried to. But Brann thinks in channels and roads. He thinks like a collector—everything moves along a ledger line, point A to point B, and you intercept at the B." Rosalind studied the chart one last time, though he barely needed it now; the drowned forest's logic was written on the water itself, in the way the current moved, in the lean of the dead trees, in the drift-lines of foam. "He's never chased anyone who was willing to go where the money isn't."

"Meaning?"

"Meaning there's a second way out. South. Through the deadfall shelf." He pointed with his chin at a stretch of forest where the standing trunks grew dense as teeth and leaned at drunken angles, their root plates exposed and rotting, entire acres of timber held upright by each other and gravity's stalemate. "When the floods killed this stand, the roots never took again. It's a wall of dead trees leaning on dead trees. The survey called it a collapse zone and marked it with a skull."

"Charming. And we go through it because—"

"Because an airboat can't. Because a hull like ours barely can. And because—" he looked up, at the leaning giants, at the wind beginning to move through their crowns with a creaking like ship's rigging, "—the monsoon's next wave is about twenty minutes out, and dead timber plus high wind equals arithmetic. Brann's men will hug the open flats and wait for us to come to them. They'll think the skull mark means we won't try it."

"And if they don't think that?"

"Then we die in interesting company."

Ilsa looked at the collapse zone for a long moment. Rain was starting again, heavier now, drumming on the canopy in earnest. Then she shipped the bail bucket, took the second set of oars, and said, "Row."

* * *

The deadfall shelf was the worst water Rosalind had ever put a boat into, and he had once run a storm surge through a wrecking yard for a bet.

The trunks stood so close that the oars touched wood on both strokes. The channel—if it deserved the word—was a suggestion, a thread of darker water threading between root plates the size of dining tables, and every few meters a fallen giant lay across it and they had to drag the Patience over, hull grinding on bark, the patched seam groaning its one-note protest. The patched seam. Rosalind thought about it constantly, the way a man thinks about a debt: always present, always accruing.

Above them the wind rose. The crowns began to talk—not creaking now but groaning, long descending notes, and once, terrifyingly, a sound like a door opening in a house with no doors. Somewhere behind them, a tree gave up entirely; the crash rolled through the forest like artillery, and a brown wave lifted the Patience and set her down again, and Ilsa bailed without being asked.

"They'll hear that," she said.

"Everyone hears that. That's the point. Every crash in here sounds like us dying. Brann's men are counting crashes and getting rich on false positives." He grinned into the rain. "Let them audit."

They were nearly through—he could see lighter water ahead, the flats' gray gleam beyond the last rank of leaning trunks—when the wind arrived for real.

It came as one blow, the monsoon's leading fist, and the whole shelf answered it. The groaning became shrieking. Timber cracked on all sides like green wood in a fire. And directly ahead, at the mouth of their thread of channel, a great trunk the width of a fishing boat began to lean—slowly, majestically, gathering speed as it fell, directly across the only water between them and open sky.

"Back!" Rosalind roared, and they backed water, and the tree hit the channel behind them with a detonation that slapped a wall of spray over the boat and drove a wave ahead of it that spun the Patience sideways into the trunks—

—and pinned her. Pinned her against a root plate, beam-on to the falling water, the patched seam downstream and straining, and Rosalind saw with horrible clarity the next tree in the fall-line: another leaning giant, twenty meters upstream, its crown already swinging toward them like a slow-motion hammer.

The wave from the first crash was still traveling. It would hit the second tree's roots, and the second tree was going to come down across the channel, and the Patience was going to be underneath it.

He grabbed the oars and pulled anyway, knowing it wouldn't matter, feeling the hull grind a half-inch and stick, and the crown overhead widened, and the wind screamed, and—

Ilsa moved.

No hesitation—that was the thing he would remember afterward, the thing that rearranged something in his chest and never quite settled back. No calculation, no negotiation, no *wait*. She was simply over the bow with the boat hook in both hands, braced on the root plate in waist-deep torrent, hauling the bow sideways with a strength that had nothing to do with her size and everything to do with whatever furnace she kept stoked behind those weather-shifted eyes. The Patience slewed. The gap opened. The second tree came down through the space where the boat had been and buried itself in the channel with a concussion he felt in his teeth, and the backwash surged, and Rosalind reached out and caught Ilsa's wrist as the water tried to take her, and hauled, and she came over the gunwale in a tangle of limbs and rain and landed against him, both of them breathing like bellows, the case somehow still lashed and dry beneath the thwart.

For a moment neither of them said anything. The forest crashed and settled around them. Ahead, through the new gap where the trees had fallen, the flats gleamed wide and empty and gray-green, and there wasn't a fan-sound anywhere on the wind.

"You didn't hesitate," Rosalind said finally. It came out rougher than he intended.

"Neither did you. You reached." She pushed her hair out of her face. Her hands were shaking now, belatedly, honestly. "That's the difference between us and them, Rosalind. When it counts, we reach."

He wanted to say something worthy of that. What he said was, "You've dented the bow."

"You're welcome."

* * *

They came out onto the Sere flats four miles south of where Brann's boats waited, in rain that had given up all pretense and committed fully to deluge. The Patience rode low but rode. The patched seam held. Somewhere behind them, faint under the storm, Rosalind could imagine collectors counting crashes in a collapsing forest and finding their ledger short one pair of corpses.

Ilsa sat in the bow with the case in her lap, watching the drowned country slide past, and when she spoke she didn't turn around.

"When we get to Renk's station," she said, "and this is over—one way or the other—you should know my name isn't Vane."

Rosalind kept his eyes on the water ahead, on the lightning walking the horizon in long white strides, and felt the flicker of trust in his chest do something complicated and new: it made room.

"I figured," he said. "Tell me on the river. We've got six hours of it left."

She turned then, and in the strobe-light of the storm he saw her decide—saw the weather shift and hold.

"All right," Ilsa said. "Here's what the floods were for."
