## 9. Confessions in the Dry Hold

The Patience leaked the way old men talk: steadily, without shame, and about everything at once.

Ilsa found the rhythm of it somewhere past midnight. Bail, listen, bail. The tin pail was dented on one side so it caught less water than it should, and the hold was barely a hold at all—four feet of bilge between the rotten deckboards and the flooded river, a space you couldn't stand in, only crouch, only kneel, only work. She knelt. She worked. The water came in through three seams she'd found and a fourth she suspected, and every fifteen minutes she filled the pail and passed it up through the hatch to where Rosalind Eriksen sat with his back against the cabin wall, tipping it over the side into the dark.

The dark was total. The monsoon's second wave had come through in the evening and broken itself against the drowned forest, and now the rain fell in that exhausted, sideways drizzle that meant the storm wasn't done, only resting. No stars. No moon. The river was a sound rather than a thing—a vast wet breathing around the hull, and somewhere out in it, the groan of dead trees shifting their feet in the mud.

"Your turn to sleep," Rosalind said, for the third time.

"I'm not tired."

"Nobody's not tired. That's a lie people tell when they don't trust the other person to stay awake."

She paused with the pail half-full of bilgewater, and in the dark she could hear him almost smiling. She tipped the pail up through the hatch anyway.

"You first, then," she said. "Sleep. I'll bail."

"I don't sleep on water."

"Everyone sleeps on water eventually."

That got a real sound out of him, low and dry, a laugh with the humor boiled out of it. Above her, the hatch lightened by a degree—clouds shifting, nothing more. She settled back into the bilge, cold water to mid-calf, and let the rhythm take her again. Bail. Listen. Bail.

It was, she thought, not the worst place she'd ever told the truth. Though she hadn't told it yet.

* * *

They had run the deadfall at dusk, and the Patience had taken a wound for it—a seam opened along the port side where a submerged trunk had kissed the hull with more affection than either of them liked—and they'd limped two miles downriver and wedged the boat into a stand of flooded cypress, bow tied to a trunk, engine cold, lantern unlit. If Brann's airboats were still hunting, light would be a confession. So they sat in the dark and bailed, and the river breathed, and neither of them slept.

Ilsa had done her counting while she worked. It was a habit from the survey—communications officers count, because everything in the field is eventually a number: batteries remaining, signal strength, days of food, people. She'd counted the team. Nine. She'd counted them every morning for eleven months, a roll call in her head, and for six days now the count had been wrong and she could not make it right.

Vasquez. Okonkwo. The Lindqvist twins. Priya. Marek. Chen. Old Halloran who'd been surveying deltas since before she was born. And Tomas.

Tomas.

The name sat in her chest like bilgewater she couldn't bail.

"Ask it," Rosalind said above her. "Whatever it is. You've been winding up to it for an hour, and the suspense is making me itchy."

She let the pail fill. "The jacket," she said. "The one I had on the sandbar. You looked at it a long time."

"I looked at everything a long time. That's how I stay alive."

"You looked at it like you knew whose it was."

The rain ticked on the deckboards. Somewhere out in the drowned forest, a branch let go and went under with a sound like a held breath released.

"Government issue," he said at last. "Survey service. I've chased enough government contracts to know the stitching." A pause, and she heard him shift, wood creaking under him. "It was too big for you. Sleeves past the wrist. You wore it anyway, in this heat."

"Keeps the sun off."

"Nobody keeps the sun off with a dead man's jacket."

She hadn't told anyone. Not in six days, not through the sandbar and the camp and the case and Brann's smile and the chase through the timber where she'd put the skiff between Rosalind and a collapsing deadfall without deciding to—her hands had decided, and she still didn't know what that meant. She hadn't told anyone, and here in the dark, in four feet of bilgewater, with a man she'd known a week and trusted exactly as far as she could throw the pail, she found the words rising like the water.

"He's my brother," she said.

The river breathed. The rain ticked.

"Tomas Vane," she said. "Survey service, hydrology team. Nine people went into the Saltmere Delta on a government contract, and eight days ago every one of them stopped existing. Gear untouched. Meals half-eaten. No tracks. The official word is relocation for safety, monsoon protocols, all of it." Her voice came out flat, and she was grateful for that; she'd rehearsed it flat. "The official word is a lie, and I'm the only person who knows where the proof is, because I'm the one who took it."

Above her, silence. Not the empty kind—the full kind, the kind where a man is deciding how much of his own truth to spend in exchange.

"You were on the team," he said.

"Communications officer. Eleven months." She bailed. The rhythm steadied her. "We found things we weren't supposed to find. Water tables that didn't match rainfall. Levee breach points that had been cut, not collapsed—clean angles, machine work, upstream of every settlement. Renk's office kept sending us to map places the water had already been told to go." She heard herself say the name—*Renk*—and realized she'd never said it aloud to anyone but herself. It sounded smaller out loud. That was the obscenity of it. Men like that should need bigger names.

"And you ran," Rosalind said. Not cruel. Just precise, the way he was precise about everything, weather and hulls and other people's lies.

"I ran." The word tasted like bilgewater. "Brann's people came to the delta edge two days before it happened. I didn't know who they were then—I thought smugglers, salvage men, the usual. I told Tomas we should pull out. He laughed at me. Eleven months in the field and he laughed at me like I was his little sister, which I am, which is the whole problem." The pail was full. She lifted it, passed it up, heard it tip and splash. "The night it happened, I was on the radio. There was a signal I didn't recognize on the survey band—encrypted, short bursts, triangulating from the east levee. I logged it. I should have woken the camp. I told myself it could wait until morning, that I'd check the direction-finding at first light, that I was tired."

"You were tired."

"I was a coward with a schedule." She said it without drama, and that was the worst way to say it. "I slipped out of camp before dawn to run a bearing from the ridge. It took me an hour. When I came back, the camp was the way you saw it. Generators running. Coffee still warm. Nine bedrolls, nine people, gone. No tracks, because the tracks went into the water. They took them out by boat, Rosalind. In the dark. While I was on a hill doing arithmetic."

The pail came back down through the hatch, empty, and she took it, and for a while there was only the rhythm again. Bail. Listen. Bail. She was shaking, she realized, and the cold wasn't all of it.

"The case," Rosalind said quietly. "At the camp. The one you were too eager to find."

"Mine. I hid it. The night before, after I logged the signal—instinct, or guilt, or whatever you want to call the thing that made me seal the data drives and the breach analysis and the survey photographs in a waterproof case and bury it under the fire pit before I went up the ridge." A breath. "I told myself it was protocol. Redundancy of records. I told myself a lot of things that week."

"And your brother."

The question she'd been waiting for, and she made herself answer it the way she'd made herself do everything else since: precisely, because precision was all she had left.

"Tomas is either alive in a hole somewhere, or he's a line in somebody's ledger. I don't know which. I do know that the people who took him are the same people whose collector found me on a sandbar with a broken radio, and that if Brann gets that case, the ledger gets balanced and my brother becomes a rounding error." She looked up through the hatch at the shape of him against the lesser dark of the sky. "So that's my confession. Communications officer, deserter, thief of her own team's evidence. Journalist was the first lie I told you and the smallest."

* * *

She climbed up into the cockpit after that, because a person can only kneel in cold water confessing for so long before her legs stage a revolt. They sat side by side against the cabin wall, shoulder to shoulder in the dark, the pail between them, and the rain went on ticking its patient, indifferent arithmetic on the deckboards.

"Your turn," she said.

"Is it?"

"You heard mine. Fair's fair, or are you the kind of man who takes confessions and gives weather reports?"

He was quiet long enough that she thought he'd refuse. Out in the flooded timber, something moved—fish, probably, or a snake, or nothing—and settled.

"Meridian Syndicate," he said at last. "You've heard the name."

"Everyone's heard the name. They lend money and collect in blood. The optimists."

"That's the joke, yes. They call themselves optimists. Brann says it with a straight face, which is either the funniest or the saddest thing I've ever seen." Rosalind tipped his head back against the cabin. "I chase storms for hire. Sensors, telemetry, ground truth for insurance companies and water authorities and once, memorably, a grain futures outfit that wanted to know which counties to bet against. The rig cost more than I'll earn in four years. I bought it on Syndicate paper, because they're the only ones who'll lend to a man with no address and no last name anyone believes."

"The debt."

"Two years of it. Interest that compounds like the river rises. I keep moving because—" he stopped, and she heard him choose the next words with the care of a man walking a rotten deck, "—because as long as I'm moving, the account is theoretical. The day I stop, it's a number, and Brann comes to collect the number. So I chase weather. Weather is always somewhere else. You can outrun a lot of things if you never stand still."

"And the salvage contract. The gear. That was the debt."

"That was the debt. Nine people's equipment, retrieved, in exchange for a clean ledger." A dry sound, almost a laugh. "I told myself it was just gear. Metal and plastic and lithium. I told myself the team had run for it, deserted, that the government would hush it up and everyone would go on breathing. I'm good at telling myself things. You'd know."

"I would."

"Then the camp wasn't a desertion. And the gear wasn't gear. And Brann didn't want equipment—he wanted whatever the team had found, and he wanted it for someone, and I watched you find that case and I watched your face and I understood that I hadn't been hired to salvage." He turned his head; in the dark she could feel his attention land on her like a hand. "I was hired to be the middleman. The optimists don't collect in blood, Vane. They collect in *deniability*. Somebody upstream wanted that evidence and wanted clean hands, and a debt-ridden storm chaser with no last name is the cleanest pair of hands money can rent."

The rain thickened for a moment, then eased. Ilsa found she wasn't surprised. That was the thing about the last week—every new horror arrived pre-fitted to a shape she'd already suspected. The world was a leaky boat, and everyone aboard had known about the seams.

"The names," she said. "Say the names."

He understood. She watched him understand—the small shift in his breathing—and then he did it, slowly, the way you pay out rope.

"Vasquez. Okonkwo. The Lindqvist twins, Anja and Brit. Priya Nair. Marek Dvořák. Chen Wei. Halloran." A pause. "And Vane. Tomas Vane."

"Nine," she said. Her voice held. She was almost proud of that.

"Nine," he said. "I read the manifest off the contract. I've been saying them to myself since the camp. It seemed—" he searched, "—like the least a man could do. Say them. So they're not just gear."

She looked at him then, really looked, as much as the dark allowed—a shape, a voice, a man who bailed water without complaint and counted other people's dead without being asked—and she felt the dangerous thing happen, the thing she'd been guarding against since the sandbar. Not trust. Nothing so clean. Something more like recognition. Two liars in a leaking hull, each holding a truth like a lit flare in a powder store.

"Here's the part you're not going to like," she said.

"I've had a bad week. I'll manage."

"If it comes to it—if it comes down to the case or you, to my brother or you—I give you to them." She said it evenly, because he deserved it even, because lying to him in this specific way would have been a second desertion. "I'll hand you to Brann with a smile and row away with the evidence and I won't lose a minute of sleep, because Tomas is my blood and you're a man I've known eight days. That's the arithmetic. I wanted you to have it before you started confusing me with an ally."

The rain ticked. The river breathed. And then Rosalind Eriksen laughed—really laughed, low and long and helpless, the first true sound she'd ever heard him make.

"God," he said, wiping his eyes with the back of his hand. "I've been sitting on the same confession since the deadfall. If it comes to the case or you, I sell you and the case both, clear my ledger, and disappear into the dry season a free man. I've already drafted the message to Brann in my head twice. I know which frequency. I know the code phrase." He shook his head. "We're a matched set, you and I. The optimists would love us."

"They'd invoice us."

"They'd invoice our *feelings*."

And she laughed too, then—cracked, exhausted, half grief and half something she didn't have a name for—and the laugh and the rain and the rhythm of the water in the hold all ran together, and for a few minutes the night was almost bearable, which was as much as the night was offering anyone.

* * *

They traded off through the small hours. She slept first, against the cabin wall, his oilskin over her shoulders because he claimed he ran hot—a lie, probably, or a kindness, and she'd stopped trying to sort his lies from his kindnesses because they were apparently the same material, like everything else on this river. She dreamed of the ridge, and the bearing she'd taken, and the coffee still warm in the cups, and woke with her brother's name in her mouth and didn't say it.

When she woke, he was bailing. The rhythm had changed—slower, heavier—and she could hear it in the hull before her eyes opened: the water coming in faster now, or the pail coming up emptier. The sky through the hatch had gone the color of wet slate with a thin wound of gray along the east, which meant either dawn or the storm's next act, and on this coast the two were hard to tell apart.

"Seam's opened wider," Rosalind said, without looking at her. "Port side. It's manageable today. It won't be manageable tomorrow."

"Then we fix it today."

"Fixing it means beaching her, and beaching her means being still, and being still—" He stopped. He didn't need to finish it. *Being still means settling accounts.* She'd learned his grammar already.

"Rosalind." She sat up. The oilskin slid from her shoulders. "The water's dropping. You've seen it—the marks on the trunks. The monsoon's turning. In three days, maybe four, the delta starts to drain, and the moment it drains, Renk's people can move on the roads, and the moment they can move on the roads, every holdout who saw anything becomes a problem to be solved." She heard her own voice go flat and precise, the survey voice, the voice she'd used to report the signal she'd failed to wake anyone for. "And somewhere in this delta there is a hole with nine people in it, or eight, or seven, and every hour the water falls is an hour they have left."

He was quiet, bailing. Then he set the pail down in the bilge, deliberately, the way a man sets down a tool he intends to pick up again.

"You're asking me to stop running," he said.

"I'm telling you the river's going to make you stop whether you choose it or not." She held his shape in the gray light, this debt-ridden, name-uncertain man who said the dead out loud so they wouldn't be gear. "The only question left is what you're holding when you do."

He looked at her a long moment. And then, out on the eastern water, through the thinning rain, came a sound that ended every argument either of them had left: the flat, carrying slap of an airboat's fan, far upstream but not far enough, running without lights, running before the dawn.

Rosalind's hand closed over the pail. Ilsa's hand closed over the case's strap where it lay against her ribs.

"Down," he said. "Both of us. Now."
