## 10. The Man Who Sold the Rain

The river changed character a mile below the station.

Rosalind felt it before he saw it—the Patience's wake flattening, the water going glassy and obedient, as if it had been told to behave. For two hundred miles the delta had been chaos: braided channels, drowned orchards, rooftops surfacing like the backs of sleeping animals. Here the current ran straight and deep between levees that were new, or new enough—concrete still pale at the seams, rebar stubble showing where the flood had chewed the old earthen walls away and someone had poured replacements with government money and government speed.

"Look at that," Ilsa said from the bow. She didn't point. She'd learned not to; they'd passed two patrol skiffs since dawn, both flying the water authority's blue pennant, both pretending not to see them in return. "Somebody's levee got fixed."

"Somebody's," Rosalind agreed. "Not everybody's."

That was the arithmetic of it. He'd been doing the sums all morning without wanting to. Back at the holdouts' shoal camp there had been forty-one people living under tarps on a strip of sand that would vanish when the dry season truly arrived, and their levee was a memory and a rumor. Here, thirty miles upriver, the concrete was fresh enough to smell of lime. The rain fell on everyone equally. The protection did not.

He eased the throttle down. Ahead, where the channel bent west, a hill rose out of the flooded plain like a knuckle—the only high ground for miles, and on it, the station.

* * *

They'd gotten the name off a dead man's paperwork.

That sounded worse than it had been, which was to say it had been bad enough. The holdout camp's elder, a woman named Sefa with hands like tree bark, had kept a waterproof satchel buried under her cookfire for three years. Inside: eviction notices dated six weeks *before* the first engineered breach. Notices printed on water authority letterhead, addressed to families by name, offering relocation compensation calculated to the peso per hectare—figures so precise they could only have come from a ledger, and so low they could only have been an insult dressed as an offer.

At the bottom of each notice, the same signature. A small, tidy hand. *A. Renk, Regional Director, Delta Water Management.*

"He signed them early," Sefa had said, watching Rosalind read. "You understand what that means? The letters went out before the water came. He knew. They all knew. And when we didn't leave—" she'd turned her palms up, a gesture that contained three years of mud and graves "—he made us leave."

Now Rosalind studied the station through the Patience's cracked spyglass and understood why Sefa had given him the satchel instead of burning it. The place had been a lockkeeper's post once, back when the delta had locks worth keeping. Renk had grown it into something else: a compound of poured-concrete blocks on the hill's crown, ringed by a chain-link fence topped with razor wire that caught the afternoon light like frost. A crane stood over a half-built extension. Diesel generators hummed behind the wall—you could hear them across the water, a fat contented drone, the sound of power that never flickered. Two patrol skiffs sat moored at a private dock. An antenna mast rose above everything, guyed with steel cable, blinking its red eye against the gray sky.

And above the gate, painted fresh on a board, a motto in official lettering: *WATER FOR THE MANY.*

"The many," Ilsa repeated softly. She'd come aft to crouch beside him, and her breath fogged the spyglass's brass rim. "I've read his speeches. He loves that phrase. *Water for the many.* He gave a address after the second breach—flood relief coordination, very moving, he cried on camera." Her voice stayed level, but Rosalind had spent enough nights listening to weather to hear the pressure building underneath it, the way you hear a storm cell before the first gust. "Three hundred people died in that one. He cried."

"People can cry about anything if the price is right."

"You'd know."

It wasn't cruel, exactly. It was accurate, which was worse. Rosalind lowered the spyglass and didn't answer, because there wasn't an answer that wouldn't be a lie, and he'd made himself a promise—recently, badly kept—that he'd stop lying to her even when lying was easier.

* * *

They took the Patience into the reeds a mile downstream and waited for dark, because there was no version of this that worked in daylight.

The waiting was the hard part. Waiting always was. Rain came and went in ragged curtains, and under the tarp Ilsa laid out the case's contents again—not the decoy case Brann believed he'd seen, but the real one, wrapped in oilcloth inside a flour sack—and went through it piece by piece the way other people prayed.

Levee engineering assessments, stamped and signed, concluding that the old barriers were sound and recommending no action—dated weeks before the breaches. Purchase orders for demolition charges billed as "channel maintenance." Internal memos from the water authority discussing "population redistribution targets" in language so bloodless it took a moment to understand that the populations being redistributed were alive at the time of the memo and mostly weren't afterward. And the crown of it, the document Ilsa handled last and slowest: a cost-benefit analysis, four pages, comparing the expense of legitimate land acquisition against the projected savings of "hydrologically accelerated resettlement."

Projected savings. That was the phrase. Somewhere a man had sat at a desk with a cup of coffee cooling beside him and calculated how much cheaper it was to drown people than to buy from them, and had found the difference satisfactory, and had signed his name.

"Renk didn't write this," Ilsa said. It wasn't the first time she'd said it. "The analysis came from above him. Ministry level. But he executed it. He's the hands. Everything north of the salt line, every breach, every notice, every compensation figure—it went through his office. He's the man who sold the rain."

"And the Syndicate brokered it." Rosalind watched the station's lights come on as dusk thickened, window by window, warm and yellow and obscene. "Meridian doesn't lend money, Ilsa. They lend leverage. Somebody ministry-side wanted this done without government fingerprints on the financing, so Meridian moved the money and took their cut, and now they're sitting on a copy of the evidence because blackmail is just lending with better interest rates." He flexed his hands. They ached in the damp; they always ached now, an old lightning injury that flared before storms, his body keeping its own ledger of debts. "Brann wasn't sent to collect my salvage. He was sent to make sure nothing left this delta that could embarrass their client. I'm not a debtor to them anymore. I'm inventory."

"You figured that out on the river," Ilsa said. "Why are you only saying it now?"

Because saying it made it real, and real things demanded decisions, and every decision he'd made in the last ten years had been small ones—the next contract, the next storm, the next town—precisely so he'd never have to make a large one. But he said none of that. What he said was: "Because now we're close enough to see his face."

* * *

They went in over the fence on the landward side, where the hill's slope let them drop from a leaning acacia onto gravel, and the gravel was the worst part—every footstep a small detonation. But the generators covered them, that fat contented drone swallowing sound whole, and the rain returned on schedule and gave them moving shadows to move inside.

Rosalind had broken into places before. Storm-chasing taught you a certain lawlessness of necessity—climbing locked transmitter towers, borrowing fuel, hotwiring vehicles in counties where nobody would come to help you. But those break-ins had been for data, for survival, for the small criminal economies of a man running from a ledger. This was different. This was walking into the house of the thing itself.

They found the offices on the second floor of the main block, behind a door with a brass plate: *A. RENK — REGIONAL DIRECTOR*. The lock was good but the frame was cheap, and Rosalind's pry bar—a boat tool repurposed—did the rest.

Inside smelled of paper and floor wax and, faintly, of coffee gone cold hours ago. A tidy room. That was the first horror of it. Rosalind had expected something else—trophies, cruelty, some visible rot—but Aldous Renk's office was the office of any competent administrator. Filing cabinets in ranks. A map of the delta on the wall, annotated in colored pins: blue for completed works, red for pending, and scattered through the southern lowlands, small black pins that Ilsa touched one by one with a fingertip, naming the settlements under her breath. Saltmere. Corren's Landing. The Fen villages. Each black pin a place that existed on the map and nowhere else.

His desk was orderly too. Ledgers in a row, spines labeled in that same small tidy hand from the notices. Rosalind opened the nearest one and the cold clarity the chapter of his life had been building toward finally arrived, complete, like a front rolling in.

It was a ledger. Of course it was a ledger. Columns ruled in pencil, figures entered nightly in a hand that never hurried: diesel consumption, patrol wages, charge counts for the demolition stores reconciled against usage reports, and then—the section that stopped Rosalind's breath—a page headed simply *DISBURSEMENTS*, listing payments made through a shell company whose name meant nothing and whose account number meant everything, because Rosalind knew that account number. He'd owed money into its parent structure for six years. Meridian Syndicate, holding company, the same routing digits that appeared at the bottom of every collection notice Brann had ever slid across a bar table toward him.

The same numbers. His debt and these drownings, flowing through the same financial plumbing, priced in the same currency, entered in the same dispassionate columns. Somewhere in a Meridian vault there was a ledger with Rosalind Eriksen's name in it, and the handwriting was probably identical to this, and the men who kept both books thought of them the same way: outstanding balances. Things owed. Costs pending recovery.

He had spent years telling himself his debt was different. Personal. Survivable. A bargain struck between consenting parties. Standing in Renk's office with the evidence of three hundred deaths arranged in neat pencil columns, Rosalind finally saw the shape of the lie. There was no different kind of ledger. There was only the ledger, and everyone in it was a number, and the only question—the only moral question in the world—was whether you were the one writing the columns or the one written down.

"Ilsa." His voice came out strange. "Come look at this."

She read fast, faster than him, and when she reached the disbursements page she made a sound he hadn't heard from her before—not grief, not fury, something drier and more terrible. Recognition. "My brother's team was funded through a grant," she said. "A water authority research grant. Which means—" her finger traced the column upward "—which means Meridian partly paid for the survey that found their own crime. They financed the people who caught them. And then collected the crew." She looked up. "Do you understand? Nobody in this whole machine ever touches anything directly. Money goes in, money comes out, and the water does all the killing. Renk never drowned anyone. Brann never drowned anyone. The ledger did."

Outside, a vehicle engine started in the compound yard.

* * *

They froze. Headlights swept the wall below the window, and voices carried up through the rain—two men, unhurried, the tone of guards changing shift rather than hunting intruders. Rosalind killed the penlight and stood in the dark with his heart slamming and the ledger open under his hand, and through the window's wet glass he watched a black sedan nose up to the main block below.

The car door opened and a man stepped out under an umbrella held by a guard, and even at this distance, even in the rain-dark, Rosalind knew him. Not from photographs—from posture. The man climbed the steps the way administrators climb steps, slightly ahead of his own escort, entitled to the ground beneath him. Medium height, silver at the temples, a raincoat that fit him properly. At the door he paused to say something to the guard, and the guard laughed, and the man smiled at the joke the way you smile at a subordinate's joke: generously, from a height.

Aldous Renk. The man who sold the rain, coming home through his own weather.

"There's your monster," Rosalind breathed.

"That's not a monster," Ilsa whispered. Her face at the window's edge was lit faintly by the compound lights, and it was the calmest he'd ever seen it, which frightened him more than shouting would have. "Monsters are excuses. That's a man with a filing system. That's what evil looks like when it's fully funded."

Below, the door closed behind Renk. Lights came on in the residence wing, one floor beneath them. Voices, faint. A kettle, perhaps. The ordinary sounds of a man ending his working day, having sold the sky.

"We should go," Rosalind said. "We have what we need. More than—the mirror documents, the disbursements, the pin map. We take this to a transmitter, we're done."

But Ilsa wasn't moving. She was looking at the black pins on the map, and her hand had gone to the case strap at her shoulder, and Rosalind watched her arrive at a decision he recognized because he'd been arriving at its opposite his entire life.

"He keeps the master copies here," she said quietly. "The originals. Paper, signed, in his own safe—I heard the survey team theorize it before they vanished. The case we have is a copy. Copies get disputed, dismissed, called forgeries. But if we take his originals—" she turned, and the storm-light moved in her eyes "—then it's not our word against his. It's his own signature against him."

"It's also his safe," Rosalind said. "Behind his desk. In his house. While he's home."

"Yes."

"And the guards?"

"Two on the gate, two on rotation, asleep by midnight if the pattern holds." She almost smiled. "You chase storms for a living, Eriksen. Tell me the difference between a storm and a fortified station full of sleeping men."

He could have told her the difference. The difference was that storms never pretended to be anything but what they were. The difference was that you could outrun a storm, and you couldn't outrun what you were becoming. Because some cold clear part of him—the part that had read the disbursements page and felt the vertigo of recognition—was already calculating angles and shift changes and load-bearing doors, already pricing the risk, already doing what the ledger did.

And another part of him, quieter, looked at the black pins and at Ilsa's face and understood that this was the stopping place. The place where moving forward and standing still finally cost the same thing, and you had to choose which price to pay with your eyes open.

Downstairs, faintly, Renk's voice rose in conversation—warm, reasonable, the voice of a man explaining water policy to a dinner guest—and Rosalind realized with a lurch that Ilsa had already picked the lock's tumblers loose in her mind, and that whatever he said next, she was going up that staircase or down through that ceiling, and the only question left was whether he went with her.

Then, below them, the front door opened again, and a voice he knew—knew from bar tables and collection notices and six years of smiling dread—said cheerfully into the rain:

"Evening, Director. Funny thing about weather. It always brings me the same man."

Brann. Umbrella folded, smile audible, standing in Renk's doorway with the rain streaming off him—and in his hand, held up to the porch light so the Director could see it, a scrap of oilcloth torn from a flour sack.

Rosalind's blood went to ice water. The real case's wrapping. Lost somewhere on the river, somewhere on the shoal, somewhere in the long night of bailing and confessions—and Brann had found it, and Brann had followed it here, and now the optimists and the architect were shaking hands under one roof while Rosalind and Ilsa stood in the dark above them with everything worth killing for in a flour sack between their feet.

"Well," Brann's voice floated up through the floorboards, warm as a loan offer. "Shall we talk about your inventory problem?"
