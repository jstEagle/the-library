## 11. Inheritance of Ruin

The rain had a sound for everything, Ilsa thought, and none of them were kind.

Against the Patience's tarp it drummed like fingers on a coffin lid. Against the river it hissed, a long sibilant whisper that never quite resolved into words but always felt as though it wanted to. And against the drowned forest that lined the channel—dead cypress and flooded gum, their crowns sticking up out of the water like the masts of ships that had gone down standing—it made a softer sound, almost tender, the way rain falls on things that can no longer feel it.

She sat in the bow with her back against the mast step, the case between her knees, and watched Rosalind sleep.

He slept badly. She'd noticed that the first night, before any of this, when he'd been nothing to her but a boat with an engine and a man attached to it. He slept the way soldiers and debtors sleep—one ear open, one hand near something sharp, ready to be moving again at the first wrong sound. She had catalogued him the way she catalogued everyone: useful, dangerous, dishonest. A storm chaser who owed money to people who collected in blood. A man who kept moving because stopping meant settling accounts.

She had thought she understood him then. She had been wrong about that, and about other things, and the wrongness sat in her chest now like swallowed river water—cold, heavy, impossible to cough up.

Because there was a name on the survey manifest she hadn't told him. There was a whole history folded up inside her like the charts in the case, and she had let him believe the simple version: journalist, chasing a story, nothing personal. Liars recognized liars; they'd agreed not to ask questions yet. But *yet* was running out. The river was carrying them toward Renk's station whether they were ready or not, and you could not walk into a man's fortress carrying a secret that big without it showing in your face like a bruise.

*Ilsa Vane.* Even the name wasn't hers—not entirely. Vane was the byline she'd built after the floods took everything else, a weathercock of a word, spinning to point wherever the wind came from. The name she'd been born with was on a deed, and the deed was under four meters of water somewhere behind them, in the country they'd been poling through all day without knowing it.

Her family's country.

* * *

She had been eight years old the first time her father took her out to measure the levee.

Not the government levee—the old one, the Eriksen-family one, raised by her great-grandfather's hands and every generation since, a long earthen spine holding the river off the fields. Her father had walked her along its crest in the blue hour before dawn, and the mist had stood over the paddies in white columns, and he'd said, "Feel that? That's dry ground breathing. As long as this wall stands, it keeps breathing."

He'd been a hydrologist the way his father had been a farmer and his grandfather a builder—by inheritance, because the land demanded it. The Vanes—Ilsa's mother's line—had come later, cartographers who married into the delta and stayed, drawing maps of a place they learned to love by walking it. Between the two families there was ninety years of surveys, of soil samples, of children raised on the levee crest learning to read water the way other children learned to read books.

Ilsa had been the last of it. The only one left to inherit.

And what she inherited was this: a drowned province, a government compensation ledger with her family's parcels listed at insultingly round numbers, and a brother who had gone looking for answers and stopped answering.

* * *

"Tomas," she said aloud, quietly, testing the name against the rain.

Rosalind stirred but didn't wake. Good. Some things needed saying to the dark first, before they could be said to a witness.

Tomas Eriksen. Two years younger than her, all elbows and certainty, the one who'd stayed on the land when she'd left it. When the second flood came—the engineered one, though no one could prove it then—he'd stood on the same levee their father had walked her along and watched the water come through a breach that no natural surge should have cut, clean-edged, deliberate, at a point where the ground was softest and the compensation maps already drawn. He'd stood there with forty neighbors and watched three generations of Eriksen work disappear in nine hours.

And then he'd gotten a job with the government survey team.

She had raged at him for it. *You're working for the people who drowned us.* He'd shrugged in that infuriating way of his and said, "Somebody has to be inside the room, Ils. Somebody has to see the ledgers." She'd thought it naivety. His kind of stubborn optimism, the family curse wearing a new coat.

Then, eleven days ago, the transmissions from the Saltmere survey camp had gone quiet. Not failed—*stopped*. Mid-sentence, mid-check-in, Tomas's voice on the last burst transmission saying something routine about barometric readings, and then nothing. Nine people. Intact gear. No tracks in the mud.

The official line was equipment malfunction and relocation. The official line always was.

Ilsa had spent two years building a journalism career out of thin air and borrowed credibility precisely so that no one would question it when she went into the delta asking questions. She'd gotten herself assigned—or had maneuvered herself into being assigned—to the civilian liaison slot on a follow-up inquiry, and then, when that stalled in committee, had simply gone ahead of it, alone, with a radio and a press jacket and a lie. She'd been following the survey team's planned route when her skiff's engine drowned in a squall and the river put her on a sandbar like a piece of driftwood it was done with.

That was where Rosalind found her. Or rather—that was where the river arranged for them to find each other, which in the delta amounted to the same thing. The delta did what it liked with people. It had certainly done what it liked with hers.

* * *

The camp had told her everything she needed to know, once she'd let herself read it.

Generators still humming. Meals half-eaten, cups still wet inside. Boots by bedrolls. Nine people had not fled that camp—fleeing people take their boots. Nine people had been *collected*, lifted out of their lives so cleanly that the only evidence left was absence itself, and absence is the hardest evidence in the world to photograph.

Except someone had missed one thing. Someone had not counted on a communications officer who knew her brother's habits better than her own—who knew that Tomas hid things the way their father had, in plain sight, under the floorboards of ordinary objects. The case had been wedged inside the housing of the camp's backup battery bank, wrapped in oilcloth, sealed against water. Inside: data drives, printed records, photographs of documents. Levee breach reports with demolition charges itemized as "hydrological remediation." Compensation disbursements dated *before* the floods they compensated for. Land transfers signed while the water was still standing on the fields. And threaded through all of it, like wire through paper, the Meridian Syndicate's name—middlemen, brokers, optimists lending money and collecting in blood, collecting this time in a whole drowned province.

Proof. Real proof, the kind that couldn't be repossessed.

And the price of it: nine people taken, somewhere, by someone, for exactly this reason.

She had not told Rosalind about Tomas. She had not told him that the jacket she carried—the survey-team jacket she'd claimed was picked up at the camp—was Tomas's, sleeves too long for her, his initials inked inside the collar in their mother's handwriting because their mother had labeled everything, even the children, even the future, as if writing a name on a thing could keep it.

Maybe it could. Maybe that was why she was here. To write the names back onto the world.

* * *

Rosalind woke at dusk, the way he seemed to do everything—abruptly, completely, as if sleep were just another current he was managing. One moment still; the next, eyes open and reading the sky.

"Wind's backing around," he said. Voice rough. "Northeast. That's the third wave building. We should make the cutoff channel before dark or we'll be spending it in the open."

"We'll make it." She passed him the thermos. He drank, handed it back, and for a while neither of them spoke, and the river spoke for both of them, that endless patient whisper against the hull.

They were running down a corridor of drowned gum trees now, the water black and mirror-still between storms, each trunk reflected so perfectly that the world seemed doubled, the living forest above and its ghost below. Here and there a roofline broke the surface—a ridgepole, a chimney, the corner of a church—and Ilsa looked at each one longer than she needed to.

"You keep doing that," Rosalind said.

"Doing what."

"Looking at the roofs like you're checking on them." He wasn't mocking. That was the trouble with him; underneath the debt-weariness and the bad lies, he noticed things, the way a man who reads weather for a living notices things. "You've been doing it since the sandbar. You look at the drowned houses the way other people look at graves."

Ilsa turned the thermos cup in her hands. The rain had thinned to a fine drifting mist, and through it the light was going the color of old pewter.

"Third house on the left, past the dead gum with the osprey nest," she said. "That one was ours."

His head came around slowly.

"The roofline's still above water. Barely. My father re-shingled it the year before—" She stopped. Started again, because she had decided, somewhere in the last hour of silence, that she would finish this, and a Vane finished what she started; it was the other family trait, the stubborn one. "My name isn't Vane. Not originally. It's Eriksen. Ilsa Eriksen. My family held land in this delta for ninety years. Farmland, a homestead, a stretch of the old levee my great-grandfather built. This whole reach of the channel we're running right now—this was Eriksen ground."

Rosalind said nothing. He had a way of receiving information like ballast—taking it aboard without comment, letting it settle where it would steady him. She was grateful for it. It let her keep talking.

"When the floods came, the government called it a natural disaster. Act of God, compensation offered, land acquired for 'delta restoration.' Except the compensation schedules were drawn up six weeks before the water arrived. Except the breaches in the levees were cut, not collapsed. Except my brother—" Her voice caught, and she hated it, and pushed through. "My brother was on that survey team, Rosalind. Tomas Eriksen. Communications. The jacket I've been wearing is his. The last transmission anyone heard from that camp was his voice, and it stopped mid-sentence, and I have spent eleven days telling myself he's alive because the alternative is a weight I can't carry and still pole a boat."

The mist drifted between them. Somewhere off in the drowned timber, a bird made one raw complaint and went silent.

"I'm not a journalist," she said. "Or I am, but it's a coat I put on over the real thing. The real thing is that this land is mine. Ours. Mine by deed and by blood and by ninety years of hands on that levee, and the men who took it wrote it down in ledgers like it was inventory. They priced my father's orchard at a number so round it could roll. They priced our neighbors' graves—there's a cemetery on the rise past the homestead, Rosalind, five generations of us—they priced the *relocation of remains* as a line item." She laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged. "So no. I didn't come out here for a story. I came out here for an inheritance. Everything the water took, I intend to take back. The land if I can. The truth regardless. And if the truth costs me everything else, then I'll stand on whatever ground is left and be the last Eriksen, owning nothing, and it will still be more than they managed to drown."

* * *

Rosalind was quiet for a long time.

When he finally spoke, his voice was different—stripped of its usual weathered irony, down to bare wood. "The case. The one you were too eager to find at the camp. That's why."

"That's why."

"And your brother built the hiding place."

"He learned it from our father. Under the floorboards of ordinary things." She looked at him directly. "You should know what you're tied to now. Because I've watched you since the sandbar, Rosalind Eriksen—yes, I know your name too, Brann's collectors shout louder than they think—and I've seen a man weighing a case against a debt. I know that arithmetic. I've done it myself these last eleven days, weighing Tomas against the mission, the mission against the truth. So understand me: I will give this evidence to the world if I have to burn every bridge on this river to do it. But I would rather not burn the ones holding us both up. So I'm asking. Not as a journalist. As the heir to a ruin. Help me carry it."

The Patience rocked gently on water gone glass-flat between storm cells. Overhead, through the torn mist, the first stars were coming out—thin, uncertain, like debts being paid in small coin.

Rosalind looked at her for a long moment, and she watched something move behind his eyes, some ledger closing and another opening, and she thought: *he's deciding whether to tell me his half.*

He didn't. Not yet. Instead he said, "There's a station upriver. Fortified. Renk's—the water authority man. If anyone knows where they took your brother's team, it's him. And if anyone's keeping ledgers worth stealing, it's him too."

"Then that's where we're going."

"That's where we're going." He paused, and his jaw worked, and for a moment she saw the cost of it on his face—the visible arithmetic of a man about to make a call he shouldn't, or hide one he already had. "First light," he said. "We'll run the cutoff channel at first light."

But his eyes went, just once, toward the case between her knees, and away again, quick as a man glancing at a purse in a crowd.

Ilsa saw it. She filed it away with everything else—the way she'd filed the breach reports, the compensation dates, the names of the missing. Inheritance, her father used to say, isn't what you're given. It's what you refuse to let go of.

She held the case a little closer, and watched the drowned roofs slide past in the dark, and refused.

* * *

She woke once in the black hours, to rain beginning again and Rosalind awake at the tiller, and heard him murmuring into the shortwave—low, clipped, careful. Numbers. An arrangement.

She lay still with her eyes closed and her heart going cold and slow, and listened to a man sell something in the dark, and could not yet tell what, or to whom, or how much of her it would cost.

The river carried them on toward Renk's station, and toward first light, and toward whatever Rosalind Eriksen had just promised the optimists in the rain.
