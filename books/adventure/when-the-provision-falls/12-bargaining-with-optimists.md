## 12. Bargaining with Optimists

The rain had a rhythm to it that night, and Rosalind hated that he'd learned it. Four days on the water and the monsoon's second wave had taught him its grammar: the long drumming on the hull when the squalls came off the sea, the softer hiss in the lulls, the way the Patience's seams ticked as the wood swelled and shrank like something breathing. He knew storms the way other men knew their mothers' voices. It was the only intimacy he'd ever kept clean.

Ilsa slept in the bow, or pretended to. The case sat between them under the tarp, wrapped in oilcloth, and every time the boat rolled he felt it shift against his boot like a sleeping animal. Like something with weight enough to drown a man.

He told himself he was checking the anchor line. That was the first lie of the night, and he made himself look at it plainly, because a man who lied to himself about small things would lie about anything. The anchor line was fine. What he was actually doing was looking at the radio.

The Syndicate frequency was still where Brann had left it in his head years ago—Channel 9, dusk and dawn, the optimists' office hours. *We lend against tomorrow,* Brann liked to say. *Tomorrow always comes.* Rosalind had spent six years proving that wrong, one storm contract at a time, running just fast enough that the ledger never caught up. But ledgers were patient things. Ledgers were the patientest things in the world.

He crouched by the radio housing, rain streaming off his shoulders into the bilge, and did the arithmetic one more time. The case held proof that nine people had been taken off the face of the delta because they'd found out the water was a weapon. It held names, dates, breach orders, the whole ugly architecture of the floods written down in a government's own hand. It was worth more than his debt. It was worth more than any debt. And the Meridian Syndicate didn't collect debts—they brokered them, traded them, sold them up the chain to whoever could pay. Which meant the case wasn't evidence to them. It was inventory.

And Ilsa—

He looked at her. She lay with her back to him, one hand curled near the broken radio she kept tinkering with even in sleep, as if her fingers couldn't stop trying to fix what had been broken. She'd saved his life in the drowned forest without a second's hesitation. She'd bailed water beside him all night and told him about her brother with a voice like a wound being cleaned. She trusted him. That was the worst part. Not that she trusted him easily—she didn't, she was too much like him for that—but that she'd decided to trust him anyway, deliberately, the way you decide to walk out onto ice that might hold.

*One call,* he thought. *In and out. Before she wakes.*

He told himself it was strategy. If the Syndicate got the case, they'd void the debt, and then no one would be hunting them, and then they could get the story out some other way—the transmitter at Renk's station, chapter and verse, all of it. There was a version of this where selling the case was the smart move, the move that kept them both alive long enough to tell the truth later.

He knew, even as he assembled it, that the argument was scaffolding around a simpler structure: he wanted to stop owing. Six years of moving, of never anchoring anywhere longer than a season, of hearing Brann's smile in every friendly voice—it had worn him down to something thin. The case was the first thing that had ever come along heavy enough to buy his way out whole. And he hated himself with a clean, cold clarity for how easy the wanting was.

His hand was on the dial before he finished deciding.

* * *

Channel 9 opened with static, then the particular silence of a line being monitored. Then Brann's voice, warm as a stove in a cold room.

"Rosalind. I wondered." A pause, comfortable, unhurried. "You know, most men run longer. You've been very diligent these past years. Almost admirable."

"Brann."

"The collector's second smile," Brann said, and Rosalind could hear it, hear the smile itself traveling down the frequency like something crawling along a wire. "That's what we called it, back when I trained. The first smile is for the handshake. The second is for the settlement. You're calling to settle."

Rosalind watched the bow. Ilsa hadn't moved. Rain filled the pauses between words.

"I have the survey team's data," he said. "All of it. The real thing, not salvage. Breach orders. Water authority correspondence. Names."

Static breathed. When Brann spoke again, the warmth had gone down half a degree—not gone, never gone, but banked, the way a lender banks enthusiasm. "Go on."

"I want full forgiveness. Every entry. The whole ledger closed. Not reduced. Closed."

"And in exchange you hand me a case containing the single most dangerous document on this coast." Brann laughed softly. "Rosalind. Do you know what happens to that document after I take it? Nothing. Nothing happens to it. It goes into a drawer in an office that doesn't officially exist, and the floods keep having happened, and nine people stay missing, and you sail free. Everyone profits. That's what we do. We're optimists. We believe every bad situation contains a transaction."

"You're brokering it for Renk. For the government."

"I'm brokering it for whoever pays," Brann said pleasantly. "The government pays promptly. That's all patriotism has ever meant to me—punctuality." A rustle of paper, audible even over the rain; somewhere behind his voice there was an office, dry and lamplit, a world away from the water rising in the Patience's bilge. "Now. Terms. Full forgiveness requires the complete archive. Every drive, every page, every copy. You'll understand I can't leave fragments floating around. Fragments become stories. Stories become investigations. Investigations are terrible for business."

"There are no copies."

"Liar," Brann said fondly. "Everyone makes copies. Fine. Bring me everything you have, and I'll bring a clean ledger. Saltmere Light, two nights from now, the ebb tide. Come alone, Rosalind. Optimists hate crowds."

Rosalind's mouth was dry. Somewhere below his ribs, self-loathing sat like swallowed seawater—heavy, salt, impossible to spit out. Two nights. He said, "Ebb tide. Alone."

"Pleasure doing business," Brann said, and meant it, which was the most obscene thing about him. "You know, I always liked you. You're the rare client who understands that a debt isn't a punishment. It's a shape. Most people spend their lives trying to escape the shape they're in. You're finally choosing one."

The channel clicked dead.

Rosalind sat with his hand still on the radio, rain hammering the tarp, and felt the transaction settle into him like a splinter working toward the heart. He'd done it. Six years of running and he'd finally stopped running by selling the one thing that mattered—and he'd dressed it up as tactics, as survival, as *later we'll tell it anyway*, and every word of the dressing had smelled like Brann's office: dry, warm, punctual.

Behind him, canvas whispered.

He turned.

Ilsa was sitting up. Her eyes were open, and they weren't wide with shock or hot with fury—they were worse than either. They were calm. She'd heard enough. Maybe all of it. Maybe just enough.

"How long?" he said. His voice came out scraped.

"Long enough." She reached past him without hurry and switched the radio off, her fingers steady, her face lit blue-white by the lightning walking the horizon. "Saltmere Light. Ebb tide. Two nights." She said it back to him flatly, like reading his sentence aloud for the record. "Come alone, she says. Optimists hate crowds."

"Ilsa—"

"Don't." One word, quiet, and it stopped him harder than a shout would have. She pulled the tarp tighter over the case between them, over the thing they'd carried together through flooded timber and sinking hulls and the whole drowning dark of the delta. "I bailed water next to you all night. I told you my brother's name. I told you I'd betray you if it came to it, and you told me the same, and I thought—we were honest about it, at least. That was the one honest thing." She shook her head slowly. "But there's honest, and there's this. You didn't betray me to save yourself. You betrayed the nine of them. My brother. All of them, so your name comes off a list."

"The Syndicate gets the case either way," Rosalind said, and heard how thin it sounded even as it left him. "They're already brokering for Renk. At least this way—"

"At least this way you're paid." She looked at him for a long moment, and the rain filled the silence, and he understood that whatever she was deciding behind those eyes was being decided right now, in real time, with him watching. "You know what the worst part is? I'm not even surprised. I planned for this. I've had a knife under my jacket since the sandbar, waiting for exactly this conversation."

She didn't draw it. That was somehow worse than if she had.

"But here's what I need you to understand, Rosalind." She leaned close, and the lightning showed him her face completely—no anger left in it, just a kind of terrible clarity. "If you make that meeting, if you hand that case across the rail of Brann's boat, then everything my brother died for becomes a line item. Everything the holdouts lost becomes a rounding error. And you don't get to be sorry afterward. Sorry is a currency too, and the optimists already cornered that market."

She settled back against the gunwale, facing him, awake now, and picked up the bail bucket without another word. The night stretched ahead of them—two nights to Saltmere Light, two nights in a leaking boat with a case between them and a knife under her jacket and a debt-shaped hole where his conscience should have been.

Rosalind took the other bucket. They bailed in silence, side by side, keeping the Patience afloat, keeping each other alive, because that was the obscene joke of it—they were still, after everything, the only two people on this coast either of them could count on.

And all the while, in the back of his skull, Brann's voice kept its warm accounting: *two nights from now, the ebb tide, come alone.*

Alone, Rosalind thought, hauling the bucket up and spilling the delta back into the delta. That was the one term he might actually honor.

The question was whether alone meant leaving Ilsa behind—or leaving himself.
