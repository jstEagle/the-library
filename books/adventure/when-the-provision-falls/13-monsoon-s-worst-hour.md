## 13. Monsoon's Worst Hour

The sky had been lying to us all morning.

It wore that flat pewter calm I'd learned to distrust in twenty years of chasing weather for money—the kind of stillness that isn't peace but a held breath. The birds had gone silent by noon. The river itself went strange, sliding backward against the wind in long oily coils, and the air tasted of iron and drowned vegetation, of everything the delta had swallowed and hadn't finished digesting.

Ilsa stood at the bow of the *Patience* with her hair whipping loose from its knot, reading the water the way she read people: too well, and without mercy.

"It's coming up the channel," she said. "Not around it. Through it."

"Then we go around."

"There is no around." She turned to look at me, rain beginning to stipple the deck between us like a warning written in dots. "That's the whole point of this one, Rosalind. It's not a wall you sail past. It's a room closing."

She was right, which was becoming an uncomfortable habit of hers. I'd spent my life reading storms as contracts—wind speed against hull strength, distance against daylight, risk against fee—and every contract has an out clause if you're willing to pay enough. But this cell had come down off the sea like something with intent, a black-bellied colossus dragging the whole horizon behind it, and the channel we were committed to ran straight through its path with drowned forest on both sides and no harbor within hours.

The first drop hit my knuckle like a thrown stone.

Then the world ended politely, all at once.

* * *

Rain didn't fall anymore; it arrived. It came sideways in sheets dense enough to erase the far gunwale, hammering the deck so hard the sound stopped being rain and became geology, became the river being beaten into submission from above. Lightning walked the clouds in long white stutters, close enough that each flash left a ghost burned across my vision, and thunder followed not as echo but as impact—a fist on the hull of the sky directly overhead.

I got the sail down with numb hands and lashed it, screaming words Ilsa couldn't hear and wouldn't have needed translated. We were running bare-poled before the wind now, the *Patience* surfing swells that shouldn't have existed on a river, climbing each gray face and dropping down the other side with a slam that shook the mast step and my teeth together.

And through it all, beneath the roar, there was the other sound. The one I'd been hearing since the second hour and pretending I hadn't.

Water where water shouldn't be. In the bilge. Rising.

The old skiff's seams had been weeping since Saltmere—I'd caulked what I could reach with tallow and prayer—but the storm was working the hull like a man wringing a rag, flexing planks that hadn't flexed in decades, and somewhere below the waterline a seam had given up arguing. The bilge pump worked in fits. The bucket worked better. Neither worked fast enough.

"We're taking water!" I shouted it into Ilsa's ear, and she nodded once—no panic in her, just that flat assessment—and dropped below to start bailing while I fought the tiller and tried to keep our bow into the seas instead of beam-on to them, because beam-on meant broach, and broach meant the river would simply pick us up and file us under lost.

An hour passed. Maybe two. Time went strange inside a storm, measured in waves survived rather than minutes. My arms burned from the tiller. Lightning struck the water close enough that the whole river flashed green-white beneath us, and I felt the charge crawl up through the wet wood and into my spine like a cold hand.

Then I heard the bucket stop.

* * *

Ilsa came up the companionway soaked to the bone, gray-faced, and stood beside me at the tiller without taking it. She had to shout even pressed close.

"Bilge is past my knees. Bucket's losing. You need to run us aground or we sink in open water."

"Aground in this—" A wave broke over the bow and nearly took us both off our feet. "There's nothing but drowned forest for miles. We ground on a submerged tree and the hull opens like a letter."

"Better a letter than a coffin." She wiped her eyes with the back of her wrist. "Which way?"

I looked at the compass, then at the black wall of the storm, then at her—and that was when I saw it in her face. Not fear. Not yet. Something colder and more deliberate, the expression of a woman who had decided something while kneeling in rising water below decks.

"You already know which way," I shouted. "You've known since the sandbar. So ask me whatever it is you've been chewing on, Vane, because I can't steer and guess at the same time."

Her eyes narrowed. Rain streamed off her chin.

"The Syndicate," she said. "Last night, at the mooring. You slipped away for twenty minutes. I watched you do it. And this morning you've been flinching every time I touch the case." She leaned closer, hair plastered across her face, voice cutting through the din like wire. "So here's how this works, Rosalind. I'm going below to bail. And you are going to tell me what you promised Brann—or I'm going to sit down there on that bench and watch the water rise, and we'll both find out exactly how much your silence weighs."

"That's insane. If we sink—"

"If we sink, we sink either way. The only difference is whether I drown knowing who sold me." She said it without heat, which made it worse. There was no accusation in it, no wounded fury—just arithmetic. She'd done the sums on me the way I did sums on storms, and she'd found the number she didn't like, and now she wanted it confirmed before she invested another hour of her life keeping us both alive.

"Ilsa—"

"Twenty minutes," she said. "Start talking."

She went below.

* * *

For a full minute I held the tiller alone and let the storm make my arguments for me. Every wave said *tell her*. Every groan of the straining hull said *tell her*. The dead don't keep secrets; the drowning shouldn't get to.

But my mouth had learned its trade in rooms like Brann's, where truth was currency you never spent unless the exchange rate favored you, and it balked now, even with the river coming for us.

I jammed the tiller and went below.

The cabin was a nightmare of swinging lantern light and sloshing dark. Water stood over the floorboards, cold as confession, and it climbed my shins as I crouched opposite her. She'd set the bucket aside. She sat with her arms wrapped around her knees, watching me, waiting, while the *Patience* bucked and the lantern swung shadows across her face like a shutter opening and closing on two different women.

"Brann caught up with us," I said. "Two nights ago. Before Renk's station."

"I know that part."

"He wasn't just collecting salvage." The words came out of me like splinters, one at a time, dragged. "He offered to void the debt. All of it. Eleven years of it, Ilsa. Everything I owe—gone. In exchange for the case. Delivered intact, delivered quiet, no broadcast, no witnesses beyond the two of us walking away clean."

The lantern swung. Her face went bright, then dark, then bright again.

"And you said?"

"I said I'd think about it."

"You contacted him again last night." Not a question. She'd counted the minutes; she'd done the math; she'd only wanted me to say it aloud so the silence couldn't hide behind plausible deniability anymore. "At the mooring. That was the exchange."

"Yes."

"What did you promise?"

"Nothing final. I told him I'd bring the case to the ferry crossing at Graywater when the water dropped. He agreed. He smiled." I heard my own voice go flat and ugly with self-knowledge. "He always smiles. That's the thing about optimists—they smile right up until they collect."

Ilsa didn't move. The water crept up another inch around us, indifferent, patient, honest in a way neither of us had managed.

"My brother is in that case," she said quietly. "Not his body—I know his body is somewhere under this flood, along with seven others. But his work. His name. The proof of what they did to him and everyone else who lived on land the government decided was worth more empty than occupied. You were going to hand my brother's grave to the men who dug it, so you could walk away owing nothing."

"I was going to hand it over so I could *live*, Ilsa. There's a difference, and it's the width of a knife blade, and I've spent eleven years on the wrong side of it." I heard my voice crack and hated it. "You don't know what debt does. It doesn't sit on you like weight—it gets *inside* you. It becomes the shape of every decision. I haven't made a free choice since I was twenty-six years old. Every job, every port, every person I let near me—all of it filtered through what I owed and who I owed it to. Brann offered me the one thing I've wanted more than anything since the ledger opened. And yes. Yes, I took the meeting. Yes, I named a crossing. Because I am a coward with a boat, and I have never once in my life chosen the expensive truth over the cheap lie until it was too late to afford anything else."

The storm slammed us sideways. The lantern went out and relit. Water sloshed up my thighs.

Ilsa uncurled slowly. She picked up the bucket.

"Keep talking," she said, and started bailing.

* * *

So I talked, and she bailed, and the storm outside tried to kill us while the truth inside did its own slower work of demolition.

I told her about the rig—my sensor array, my livelihood, the beautiful fragile instrument I'd built myself and mortgaged myself to build—and about the lightning that ate it in one white bite, and about Brann arriving the next morning with his ledger and his smile, offering to finance the replacement at terms no sane man signs. I told her about the years of running: chase contracts up and down the coast, data sold to insurers and shipping concerns, every coin of it flowing upstream into Meridian's accounts and the balance never moving, the debt a tide that rose faster than any earnings could drain it. I told her I kept moving because stopping meant settling, and settling meant they'd take the rig, the boat, the name I'd made—and a man without a name in the delta towns is just meat with debts.

"Why tell me this now?" she shouted over a thunderclap that seemed to come from inside the hull. "Why not on dry land, where honesty doesn't cost lives?"

"Because down there"—I jerked my chin at the rising black water—"the interest rate on lying just went up." I laughed, and it came out wrong, half sob. "That's the joke of it, Vane. I needed a flood to afford the truth. Same as the government. Same as Renk. Drown everything, and the books finally balance."

She stopped bailing.

For a moment, in the swinging light, she looked at me with an expression I couldn't read—something between contempt and recognition, the face of a woman finding her own reflection in a man she'd been trying not to see herself in. Then she thrust the bucket at me.

"Bail," she ordered. "Your turn. And while you do, understand this." She leaned close, and the storm seemed to lean with her, the whole cabin tilting into her voice. "I was going to betray you too. At Graywater. I was going to take the case and put you ashore and disappear, because I've trusted exactly one team in my life and the government collected them, and I swore I'd never be the fool twice." She held my eyes. "So we're even. Two liars in a leaking boat. The only question left is what we do with the last few hours of the lie."

Above us, the *Patience* shuddered, took a wave broadside, and began, unmistakably, to die.

* * *

We came up into a world of white ruin. The storm had reached some interior summit of fury—rain no longer fell so much as *happened* to us, everywhere at once, and the river had become a single heaving muscle flexing beneath the hull. And through the ragged curtains of it I saw it: dead ahead, half-glimpsed between lightning strokes, a pale smear in the black water. Sand. A shoal bar, maybe fifty meters wide, thrown up where the channel bent, its windward edge already dissolving into foam.

"Hard aport!" Ilsa screamed, seeing it the same instant I did.

I threw the tiller. The *Patience* answered with the last obedience left in her rotten frame, slewing toward the bar as a following sea lifted her stern and shoved us forward like a hand closing on the back of the neck. The keel touched. Once—a grinding shriek of wood on sand that traveled up through the deck and into my bones—then a swell picked us and drove us harder, and the hull met the bar properly, and something below the waterline gave up with a long tearing groan.

We grounded hard, heeled, held.

The storm kept hitting us, wave after wave, but the bar held too, and the *Patience* settled onto the sand at a drunken list with the river tearing at her flanks, and we scrambled off her into thigh-deep churn and dragged ourselves up the shoal's spine on hands and knees while the lightning stitched the dark above us.

Behind us, the old skiff lay on her side, taking the river into herself at last. I watched the place where my whole floating life had lived these past weeks and felt—nothing I expected. No grief. Just a strange, light emptiness, like a tooth pulled after years of ache.

Ilsa collapsed beside me in the wet sand, chest heaving, and for a long time neither of us spoke. The storm raged on, but it had begun, very slightly, to spend itself—the lightning coming farther apart now, the thunder rolling away eastward like a beast retreating to its den.

"They'll have seen the lightning strike pattern," she said finally, hoarse. "Brann's boats. Renk's patrols. A shoal bar is the first place anyone looks after a blow."

"Then we don't stay on it." I squinted through the thinning murk toward the leeward shore, where the drowned forest stood in black ranks, and beyond it—barely visible, a smudge of orange in the general darkness—a light. Small. Steady. Human. "Holdouts," I said. "Someone's burning a lamp on the high ground. People who stayed when the water came."

"People the floods were built to remove," Ilsa said quietly.

"Yes." I got my feet under me, every muscle shaking, and looked at her. "People whose stories cost them everything and were worth nothing to the men who wrote the ledger. I figure it's time I heard a few."

Ilsa rose beside me, wringing the river from her sleeve, and nodded once toward the distant lamp.

"Then let's go meet the collateral," she said.
