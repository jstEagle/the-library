## 14. What Stopping Costs

The Patience died at dawn, or close enough to dawn that the difference didn't matter. The monsoon had spent the night grinding her hull against the shoal like a whetstone working a dull blade, and when the light finally came—gray, watery, indifferent—we found her keel opened along three feet of rot the storm had been patient enough to find.

I stood in thigh-deep water inside the hold, bailing with a dented pot because there was nothing else to bail with, and watched Rosalind Eriksen do the arithmetic on his face. He was good at arithmetic. He'd been doing it since Saltmere: debt against salvage, risk against reward, his skin against everyone else's. This time the numbers came up empty, and he knew it, and for a long moment he just stood there in the rain letting it land on him like it was something he'd ordered.

"Well," he said finally. "She was honest about being rotten."

I almost laughed. Almost. My ribs were bruised from the night's work and my hands were shaking—not from cold, though the water was cold enough, but from the particular tremor that comes after you've survived something and your body insists on billing you for it later. "We need shelter," I said. "Real shelter. That cell will build again by afternoon."

He looked north across the drowned forest, where the dead trees stood in rows like a congregation that had been told to wait. Somewhere past them, maybe two kilometers as the crow flew and four as the waded man, there was high ground. We both knew it because we'd seen the smoke—a thin gray thread rising off a ridge of higher earth, too steady to be a signal fire, too domestic to be anything but someone's stove.

"Holdouts," he said.

"Holdouts," I agreed.

He didn't say what we were both thinking: that holdouts meant witnesses, and witnesses meant stories, and stories were the one cargo heavier than the case strapped under my jacket. He climbed out of the wreck, shouldered what was left of our gear, and started walking without another word. I followed him into the flooded timber, and the water closed over the Patience behind us like a page turning.

* * *

The ridge rose out of the delta like the back of some animal that had decided, after everything, to keep its head down. The houses on it were built wrong for the flat country—tall, stilted, second stories added in mismatched lumber, roofs lashed down with cable and tire rubber. A whole village that had learned, house by house and year by year, exactly how high the water could be pushed.

An old woman met us at the base of the stairs with a machete held low and easy, the way you hold a tool you've used often enough not to fear. She looked at Rosalind first—the taller figure, the stranger—and then at me, and then at the survey-team jacket I still hadn't found a way to stop wearing.

"You're from the government boat," she said.

"I was," I said. It wasn't even a lie. It was the truest thing I'd said in weeks.

Her name was Maren Oduya, and she fed us before she asked us a single question, which is a kind of hospitality I have never learned and suspect never will. The room she gave us smelled of woodsmoke and dried fish and lamp oil, and through the open shutters I could see the whole drowned valley spread out below us, brown and silver where the rain hit it, the treetops standing in it like the masts of a fleet that had sunk in place.

Rosalind ate like a man who'd forgotten food was a thing bodies wanted. I watched him do it and felt something twist in me that I refused, for the moment, to examine.

* * *

They came to us through the evening, one and two and three at a time, because Maren had sent word ahead and word travels fast in a place where everyone has lost the same thing. Not an audience—they would have hated that word—but neighbors, dropping in, settling onto benches and crates and the floor itself, drawn by the gravity of strangers. Strangers meant news. Strangers meant the outside world remembered this place existed.

A fisherman named Teodor told us about the first flood, six years back, the one they'd all called an act of God. How the river had come up overnight, clean and sudden, no storm to blame, and how his father had said afterward, standing hip-deep in his own kitchen: *rivers don't rise like that. Rivers argue first.*

A woman named Petra—she'd been a schoolteacher, and she still spoke like one, each sentence built to be written down—told us about the offers. Men in dry boots, after the third flood, going door to door with clipboards. Selling relocation. Buying land at prices that were insults dressed as generosity. And the ones who wouldn't sell—

Petra stopped there. She looked at her hands.

"The ones who wouldn't sell," Maren finished quietly, "the water came back for."

Nobody in the room said the word *levee*. They didn't have to. I sat with the case's weight under my jacket, digging into my hip like a bone that had grown there, and understood that every person in this room already knew what was inside it. Not the documents themselves. The truth underneath them. They had been living inside the proof for years, drowning in it, and the world had called it weather.

Rosalind sat very still through all of it. I know his stillness now, the way you learn a specific silence. His ordinary restlessness has a texture to it, a readiness, a man perpetually half-risen from his chair. This was different. This was the stillness of a man listening to something he cannot argue with.

Because that was the thing about Rosalind Eriksen—I'd learned it on the river, bailing side by side through the dark—he believed in ledgers. Debts and payments, costs and returns. It was how he'd survived the Syndicate, how he'd survived himself. Every cruelty could be entered in a column and balanced. But these people weren't asking to be paid. They weren't even asking to be avenged, not mostly. They were asking to be counted. To exist in somebody's book somewhere as more than a rounding error.

And there was no column for that. No exchange rate. You couldn't salvage it.

* * *

Later, when the neighbors had gone and the rain had settled into its long nighttime monotone, Maren brought us tea and stayed, sitting across from us with the lamplight carving deep lines into her face.

"My son farmed the low fields," she said, without preamble, the way old people tell things they've told so many times the telling has worn smooth. "Wouldn't sell. Said his grandfather had cleared that ground with his own hands and the water had no right to it." She sipped. "After the fourth flood, the authority men came again. Different men. Nicer clothes. They offered him triple. He still said no." Another sip. "Two months later the eastern levee failed. Just that section. Just his section, if you drew the map honestly. The engineers called it saturation failure. Saturation." She said the word like she was spitting out a seed. "My son went out in the night to check his drainage gates, because that's what he did whenever the water argued, and the water took him. They found his boat three kilometers inland, caught in a mango tree."

The lamp hissed. Rain worked the roof.

"So you'll understand," Maren said, looking at me now, directly, with those eyes that had measured floods for seventy years, "when I say I hope whatever you're carrying in that case is worth what it cost to bring it here. Because I've seen a lot of people carry things through this delta. Relief supplies. Ballots. Promises. They all weigh less than they look."

"It's proof," I said. My voice came out rougher than I intended. "Of what they did. Names. Orders. Accounting."

"Accounting," she repeated, and something moved through her face that was not quite a smile. "Then you're carrying their book. Be careful whose hands you put it in. In my experience, the people who keep books are rarely the ones who get read."

* * *

Rosalind didn't speak until we were alone, lying in the loft above Maren's kitchen with the rain drumming the tin so hard it sounded like applause for something terrible.

"She's right," he said, to the ceiling.

"About the books?"

"About all of it." He turned his head, and in the dark I could just make out his profile, the line of him I'd memorized without meaning to. "I've been telling myself a story since Saltmere. You've heard it. Everybody's heard it. *I'm just paying a debt. I'm just surviving. Get clear, stay moving, settle accounts.*" He exhaled slowly. "But sitting down there tonight, listening to that woman talk about her son—it landed on me that I've been using the debt like weather. Something that happens to you. Something you can't be blamed for."

"And now?"

"Now I'm thinking about what stopping costs." He was quiet a moment. "Moving is easy. Moving, you never have to look at anything. You can outrun a ledger. You can outrun almost everything except yourself, and I've been trying to do that for—" he huffed, almost a laugh, "—longer than I want to count. But stopping. Staying. Facing what you actually owe, not the number Brann carries around but the real thing—" He shook his head. "That costs more than running ever did. Running only costs everything you're carrying. Stopping costs who you are."

I lay there in the dark and let that sit between us, because I knew—I knew with a certainty that frightened me—that he was talking about more than the Syndicate. He was talking about the case. About the choice coming upriver like weather of its own. Sell it and walk free, or give it away and stand still in the middle of everything, exposed, accountable, done running.

And I thought about my own ledger. Nine names. Eight missing and one deserter, and the deserter was me. I'd told myself I slipped away to find help, to find proof, to come back stronger. All true, maybe. But underneath it was the simpler truth: I ran because staying meant dying with them, and dying with them meant the story died too, and I chose the story. I chose the evidence over my brother's hand reaching for mine in the dark.

We were both liars with excellent arithmetic.

"Rosalind," I said.

"Don't," he said softly. "Whatever you're about to ask me—don't ask it yet. Let me sit with it one more night. Tomorrow I'll give you an answer you can trust."

"That's not what I was going to ask."

"No?"

"I was going to say that Maren's son went out at night to check his gates. Because that's what he did when the water argued." I stared up at the roof. "I think that's the only inheritance any of us gets. What you go out to check. Who you go out for."

He was silent for a long time. Long enough that I thought he'd fallen asleep. Then, barely audible under the rain:

"Renk's station is forty kilometers upriver. Waters drop in nine days, maybe ten. After that, the government can move anyone they want, anywhere they want, and nobody in this valley will ever be counted." A breath. "There's a man in the next settlement with an airboat engine he's been trading parts for. Maren says he owes her a favor."

I turned toward him in the dark. "You're not selling the case."

It wasn't a question, but he answered it anyway, and his voice had something in it I hadn't heard before—not resolve exactly, something quieter and harder, like bedrock under floodwater.

"No," Rosalind Eriksen said. "I'm done paying debts with other people's blood. Mine's the only currency I've got left, and I'm spending all of it."

Outside, the rain kept falling on the drowned valley, steady as an auditor's pen, and somewhere upriver a man named Renk slept behind fortified walls, believing his books were balanced. By morning we'd have the engine. By noon, the river.

Nine days to make the water answer for what it had taken.
