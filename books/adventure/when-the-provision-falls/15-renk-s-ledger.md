## 15. Renk's Ledger

The station wore the night like a coat it had grown into.

From the reed bank where we grounded the Patience—half-sunk, half-hope, held together by holdout caulking and my own refusal to drown—Aldous Renk's compound looked less like a fortress than a fact. Concrete poured where the water hadn't asked for it. Floodlights burning a hard white circle into the rain, each one a small arrogant sunrise that said: *the weather does not apply here.* Generator hum under the rain's percussion, steady as a pulse. Somewhere inside, men were dry. That was the whole architecture of the place, I understood suddenly. Not walls. Dryness. Renk had built a building whose entire purpose was to never be wet while everything around it drowned.

Ilsa crouched beside me in the reeds, rain running off her in ropes. She'd braided her hair back and smeared mud across the survey jacket she still wore—her brother's jacket, though I'd only learned that two nights ago, bailing water in the dark hold while she told me his name like a coin she'd been carrying in her mouth for a year. Marek Vane. Ninth name on a list of nine.

"East service gate," she said. "The fence line dips where the levee breach cut through. They never rebuilt it because nobody official is supposed to remember the breach happened."

"And the guards?"

"Two on the gate, one on the light platform, rotating every forty minutes. I ran comms for a survey team, Rosalind, not a garrison. But I watched this station for three weeks before the team went in. Renk likes his routines. Routines are just fear wearing a schedule."

I almost smiled. Almost. My hands wouldn't have cooperated anyway; the cold had gotten into them somewhere on the river and never left. "You know what's funny," I said.

"Nothing is funny."

"The Syndicate taught me everything I know about infiltration. Brann used to say the best way into a strong room is to look like you belong to the money." I flexed my fingers, watched the ache travel through them. "Renk belongs to the money. We're going to walk in like we're a line item."

* * *

We went in the way water goes in, which is to say patiently and through the crack everyone assumed had healed. The fence dip was real, a seam of rusted mesh folded back like a page someone meant to return to. Beyond it, the compound's inner yard was a geometry of pallets, fuel drums, and a pair of airboats under tarps—the same make as the one Brann had chased us through the drowned forest in. Seeing them made my stomach do something complicated. Brann was out there. Brann was always out there, with his ledger and his smile, and somewhere in the last two days I'd sent him a message arranging an exchange that I was now, at Ilsa's side, in the rain, preparing to make meaningless. Or meaningful. I hadn't decided yet. That was the honest truth of it, and the honest truth sat in my chest like swallowed river stone.

The service door had a padlock and the padlock had a hasp and the hasp had been installed by a man who trusted concrete more than he trusted locks. Ilsa held the light; I worked the pry bar I'd taken from the holdouts' camp—an old woman named Sefa had pressed it into my hands along with a bowl of fish broth and a look that said she knew exactly what I was and was lending me the bar anyway, which was more faith than I'd earned in my whole life. The hasp screws shrieked once. Rain ate the sound.

Inside: a corridor that smelled of diesel and floor wax. The wax was the detail that got me. Someone mopped here. Someone had a job, a wage, a family maybe, and their job included keeping the floors of the drowning machine clean. I thought of Sefa's broth and kept walking.

Renk's office was on the second floor, at the end of a hall lined with framed photographs—Renk shaking hands, Renk at ribbon cuttings, Renk standing on a levee in a hard hat, pointing at the water with the expression of a man introducing his work. In every picture he looked like what he was: an accountant who had discovered that rivers could be debited.

Ilsa picked the office lock faster than I could have. I tried not to be impressed. I failed.

* * *

The room was spare. Desk, chair, filing cabinet, a wall map of the delta with the flooded zones shaded in blue—shaded, I noticed now, with the tidy confidence of a plan rather than the ragged grief of a disaster. A lamp with a green shade. A window looking out on rain.

"The cabinet," Ilsa whispered.

"Locked?"

"Everything of his will be locked. He's a man who locks his desk against his own staff." She was already at it, picks whispering in the keyway. "People like Renk think locks are morality. That's why they're easy."

The second drawer stuck, then gave. Hanging folders, tabs in Renk's blocky handwriting. *LEV. MAINT.* *DISPLACEMENT — PHASE II.* *ACQUISITIONS.* And at the back, in a folder with no tab at all, a document that made the room go very quiet even though the rain hadn't stopped.

It was an accounting. Not a memo, not a plan—those we'd already seen, in Ilsa's hidden case, the water authority's breach orders and the depopulation schedules. This was the thing after the thing. This was the bill.

Line by line, in figures as calm as tide tables: cost of the engineered levee breaches, itemized by section. Compensation paid—or rather, *allocated*, with a subcolumn showing what had actually reached the displaced, which was a fraction so small it read like a rounding error, like a joke told in numbers. Land acquisition values, pre-flood and post-flood, the delta between them—*the delta between them*, I thought, and there was no version of me that would ever say that out loud—flowing into a holding entity I didn't recognize. And then the middle of the page, where the money changed hands, where the dirty water passed through the filter:

**Disbursement & collection services rendered by Meridian Syndicate (intermediary). Fee structure per Schedule C.**

I read it three times. The rain filled the silence between readings.

"They weren't just lending against my rig," I said. My voice came out flat, which surprised me; I'd expected it to shake. "They weren't even brokering the evidence. They built this. The Syndicate was the plumbing. Every credit the government stole out of this delta moved through their hands, and every debt they hold on people like me—" I stopped, because the sentence had arrived somewhere I hadn't meant to go.

Ilsa took the pages from me and read them the way she did everything, fast and total, like weather. When she looked up her face had the stillness of deep water.

"Your debt," she said. "The one you were going to trade my brother's case for."

"I was."

"Look at it again. Look at what your debt *is*."

I looked. That was the cruelty of it—Renk had made it so legible. Somewhere in these figures, in Schedule C, in the fee structures and the disbursement schedules, was the exact arithmetic of why Rosalind Eriksen couldn't stop moving. The Syndicate had lent money into this delta the way Renk's government had lent water: generously, at first, with the flood always part of the design. My sensor rig, my boat, my years of running storms for hire to service interest that never shrank—all of it, every hounded debtor on this coast, was a line in the same ledger I was holding. Brann's smile was in here. The airboat was in here. The lightning that ate my rig in chapter one of my misfortunes was in here, amortized.

"Cold triumph," I said. "That's what this is supposed to feel like. We found it. We beat them into their own strong room and took the mirror document. I'm supposed to feel like we won."

"And instead?"

"Instead I'm reading my own obituary in double-entry." I sat down in Renk's chair without meaning to, which felt like a violation and also like the most honest thing I'd done in weeks. "If I'd gone through with the exchange—if I'd handed Brann Ilsa's case tonight for a clean ledger—do you know what I'd have been? Not paid off. *Reconciled.* A balance moved from one column to the other. An entry. They wouldn't have forgiven me, they'd have filed me."

Ilsa stood over me, rain still dripping off her onto Renk's waxed floor, and for a moment she didn't look like a journalist or a deserter or an heiress of ruin. She looked like the delta itself, deciding.

"Now you understand," she said. "Marek used to say that about the water authority. That you couldn't argue with them because they didn't think in right and wrong, only in *allocated* and *expended*. You were going to sell our dead to men who think that way."

"I know."

"You knew when you sent the message."

"I knew." The stone in my chest turned over. "Ilsa, I'm not going to stand in this room and tell you I've become a good man because I read a spreadsheet. I nearly sold you. I sat on the Patience with the radio in my hand and I meant it, right up until I didn't. What changed wasn't my heart. What changed was the arithmetic. That's the only honest thing I can offer you—that I finally ran the numbers on myself and didn't like the total."

For a long moment the only sound was the rain on the window and, below us, the generator's patient hum, the sound of men staying dry.

Then Ilsa did something I didn't expect. She put the ledger back in the folder, and the folder in her jacket—Marek's jacket—against the case, so that she carried both documents over her heart like armor.

"Then we take both," she said. "The case and the ledger. The crime and the bill. Renk's own accounting proves the Syndicate was the middleman, which means when this goes public, the Syndicate can't disown it without disowning themselves." She almost smiled, and it was a frightening thing, her smile, all edge and weather. "You wanted your debt forgiven, Rosalind. This is better. This makes your debt *toxic*. Nobody will want to collect it."

* * *

We were at the window, planning the route back along the fence line, when the floodlights died.

Not all at once. One, then the platform light, then the yard—each circle of white snuffed like candles pinched by a wet finger. The generator note faltered, caught, faltered again. In the sudden dark the rain sounded enormous, and the compound below became a place of shadows moving with purpose, and every one of those purposes was bad.

"That's not the storm," Ilsa breathed.

No. The storm didn't cut power in a neat sequence. The storm didn't put men in the yard moving in pairs with the economy of professionals. I pressed my face to the glass and made out, at the east gate, the shape I'd been dreading since the reeds: an airboat, and beside it a figure standing very still in the rain, unhurried, hands clasped behind his back like a man admiring weather he'd personally ordered.

Brann had come for the exchange early. Or Brann had come because Renk had called him. Or Brann had simply always known—had known since the drowned forest, since the sandbar, maybe since the day he'd smiled at me with his ledger open—and had let me run this far for the pleasure of closing the book himself.

The office door was three floors and a corridor away from every exit, and the corridor was filling with flashlight beams.

"Back stairwell," Ilsa whispered, already moving, already folding the ledger flat against her ribs. "Service side. There'll be a lock on it and I'll have it open before you finish being afraid."

I took one last look at the desk, at the green-shaded lamp, at the wall map with its tidy blue zones—everything priced, everything allocated—and followed her into the dark, carrying nothing of my own but the knowledge of what I now owed, which was nothing, and what I now owed anyway, which was everything.

Below us, in the yard, Brann's voice carried up through the rain, cheerful as a debt collector's dawn:

"Rosalind! I know you're in there. Come down and settle up—the terms have changed."
