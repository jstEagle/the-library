## 16. The Case Changes Hands

The exchange point was a drowned chapel.

Ilsa saw it first from the bow of the Patience as Rosalind poled them through the last stretch of flooded timber—a roofline breaking the water like the back of some sleeping animal, then the bell tower rising whole above the surface, its spire furred with moss, its bell long since stolen or sunk. The flood had come up to the eaves and stopped there, as if even the water had respected something about the place. Below the roofline, only the tops of the windows showed, arched and dark, and through the nearest one she could see the pale suggestion of pews still in their rows, submerged to their backs like congregants waiting for a service that had been postponed for years.

"You're sure this is where he said," Rosalind murmured. He didn't look at her when he said it. He'd barely looked at her since the night on the shoal, since the holdouts' fire, since everything they'd told each other with the rain coming down like judgment. He spoke the way a man walks on river ice—testing each word before putting his weight on it.

"He said the old mission at Corben's Reach. This is Corben's Reach." She checked the case on her lap for the fourth time in an hour. The latches. The weight of it. The false bottom that held nothing but air, because the real documents—the real case—rode against her ribs under her jacket, wrapped in oilcloth, warm from her own body. "He'll be punctual. Brann is always punctual. It's how he makes cruelty feel like courtesy."

Rosalind grunted and kept poling. The Patience sat low in the water even after three days of the holdouts' help—new caulking in her seams, a patched plank aft, a bilge pump salvaged from a drowned skiff—but she was still a dying woman kept walking, and they both knew it. Every league they traveled was borrowed.

Ilsa watched him work the pole and thought, not for the first time, about the shape of what she was about to do.

She had rehearsed it in the dark hours, lying in the hold while he bailed, listening to the water slap the hull and composing her lines the way she used to compose transmissions back when she was the survey team's communications officer and every word she sent could get someone killed. *If it goes wrong, you take the decoy and run east along the timberline. Don't argue. Don't look back. He'll want me alive—he wants witnesses alive until they're no longer useful, and I'm more useful than you are.* She had never said any of it aloud. Saying it aloud would have made it a plan between two people, and plans between two people required trust, and trust was a currency neither of them had ever been able to stop counterfeiting.

But she had watched him these last days. Watched him give his last dry matches to the holdout children without being asked. Watched him stand in the ruins of the Vane family's drowned orchard—*her* orchard, though he didn't know the half of it—and say quietly, "They priced all this. Somebody sat at a desk and put numbers on every tree." Watched the way his jaw set when he said it, like a man biting down on something bitter rather than spit it out.

He was going to sell the evidence. She knew that too. She'd heard him make the call on the holdouts' battered radio, heard the smoothness come into his voice, the charm he wore like weather gear. *Full forgiveness, Brann. The case, intact, and you never see my face again.* She had stood outside the lean-to in the rain and let the words wash over her and found, strangely, that she wasn't angry. She was almost relieved. Because now she knew exactly what he would do when the moment came, and knowing a man's worst choice is the beginning of loving him anyway—or the beginning of outmaneuvering him. She hadn't decided which. Perhaps both were the same thing, out here where the water had taken everything worth dividing.

The bell tower threw its shadow across the water as they came under it. Somewhere inside the drowned nave, fish moved among the pews—Ilsa could hear the small wet sounds of them, a congregation of their own.

"Mooring's on the tower side," Rosalind said. "There's an old ladder platform where the sacristy roof holds above water. We can wait dry."

"We're not waiting," Ilsa said. "We're visible. He needs to see us before we see him. That's part of the theater."

Rosalind stopped poling. For a moment the only sound was the drip of water off the blade and the far-off mutter of thunder, the season's last storms gathering somewhere north like old debts coming home.

"Ilsa." He said her name carefully, the way you handle a thing that might be wired. "When this is done—if it goes clean—you should know I meant what I said on the radio. Full forgiveness. Then we're square, both of us, and you can go do whatever it is you're really going to do with your brother's—"

"Don't." The word came out sharper than she intended. She softened it with a breath. "Don't narrate it yet. Nothing's done until it's done."

He looked at her then, fully, and she saw that he knew. Not the specifics—she'd give him credit for nothing that generous—but the *shape*. He'd spent his life reading weather; he could read a person's weather too. He knew she was carrying something he couldn't see, and he chose, visibly, deliberately, not to ask. That was the tragedy of him, she thought. He extended trust exactly once per person, like a rationed supply, and then he went back to moving.

They tied off at the sacristy platform. The wood groaned but held. Ilsa climbed up first, the decoy case in one hand, and pulled herself onto the broad dry sweep of the old roof ridge, where tiles lay scattered like fallen scales and the whole drowned valley opened out around them—water to the horizon in every direction, stitched with the black tips of trees, the sky above bruising purple with the evening's coming storm.

It was, she thought, almost beautiful. That was the government's genius, if you wanted to call it that. They hadn't just drowned the delta; they'd made it gorgeous. Grief with a view.

* * *

Brann came at dusk, exactly when he'd said he would.

The airboat announced itself long before it appeared—a flat mechanical bellow rolling across the water, scattering birds from the drowned timber in ragged clouds. Then it rounded the last stand of cypress with its running lights burning yellow, and there he was at the rail, hatless as always, silver-haired, smiling the smile that Ilsa had learned to dread more than any expression of anger. Anger you could predict. Brann's smile recalibrated itself to whatever would hurt most.

"Miss Vane!" His voice carried easily across the still water, warm as a host greeting guests. "And Mr. Eriksen. You've made excellent time. The season's turning, and here you are, punctual as creditors." He laughed at his own joke, delighted with it.

The airboat slid alongside the sacristy platform. Two men rode with Brann—Ilsa marked them automatically, the way she'd been trained: big one at the stern with a shotgun broken open over his arm, smaller one amidships with his hands empty and his eyes busy. The smaller one worried her more.

"Mr. Brann." Rosalind's voice was steady. Good. Better than she'd expected. "You brought the ledger?"

"I brought everything, Mr. Eriksen. I'm a thorough man." Brann stepped onto the platform with the ease of someone who had never in his life doubted a surface would hold him. Up close, the smile was worse. "May I?" He gestured at the decoy case in Ilsa's hands without touching it. "Ah—before we transact. A small formality. My associates will want to have a look at the boat. Standard practice. Salvage contracts specify condition of recovered goods, and the Syndicate does love its specifications."

The big man was already swinging toward the Patience.

"No," Ilsa said.

Everyone paused. Even the water seemed to pause, the last light dying off its surface.

Brann turned to her with genuine curiosity, the way a collector turns to a specimen that has done something unexpected. "No?"

"The contract is with him, not with me. The case comes off this roof into your hands, and then we walk away, and your men don't touch the boat or anything in it. Those were the terms."

"They were *his* terms, Miss Vane. You weren't party to the negotiation." Brann's tone stayed pleasant, but something shifted underneath it, tectonic. "Though I confess I'm curious why you care. A journalist's equipment? Sentiment?" He smiled wider. "Or is there something in the boat you'd rather we didn't find?"

The smaller man was already climbing down.

Ilsa's heart went very quiet and very fast at the same time, the way it did before a transmission she knew might be her last. She calculated. Three of them. One shotgun, probably a pistol on Brann, maybe a second gun on the small one. Rosalind between her and the rail, ten feet from the Patience, twenty feet from the timberline where the skiff they'd cached at dawn lay hidden under brush. If it went wrong, he could make the timber in under a minute. If it went wrong and she was still standing next to him, he wouldn't run. She knew that now. That was the whole discovery of these last weeks—that Rosalind Eriksen, who had built his entire life on never stopping, had finally found something that made stopping possible. Her. Or what she carried. He'd die on this roof before he let them take both.

So she would make sure they only got one.

"Fine," she said brightly, and lifted the decoy case. "You want to inspect everything? Inspect everything. But the deal closes tonight, Brann, because after tonight the season ends, the water drops, and your employers' little problem becomes a road problem instead of a river problem. You don't want to be negotiating salvage terms in a mudflat."

Brann's eyes flickered—she'd scored somewhere, touched the one nerve the Syndicate had, which was time. Optimists, they called themselves. But optimism, Ilsa had learned, was just arithmetic wearing perfume, and their arithmetic had deadlines.

"Inspection," Brann agreed smoothly. "Then payment."

The big man finished searching the Patience and shook his head. The small one climbed back up, dripping, and shook his head too. Ilsa let herself breathe. The oilcloth bundle against her ribs felt like a second heartbeat.

Brann took the decoy case, set it on the roof tiles, and opened it.

For a moment, nothing. The latches clicked. The lid rose. And Ilsa watched his face with the attention she'd once given radio traffic out of the capital, watching for the tell, the microsecond where comprehension arrived.

It arrived. She saw it. A stillness behind the eyes, brief as lightning, and then the smile reassembled itself, faster than she'd believed possible.

"Well," Brann said softly. "Well, well, well."

"Problem?" Rosalind asked, and his voice was good, it was nearly perfect, but Ilsa heard the hairline crack in it, and she knew Brann heard it too.

"Maps," Brann said, lifting a folded sheet from the decoy's false compartment—the blank ones, the ones she'd seeded with worthless survey charts. "Survey maps. Very nice ones." He closed the case with a click that sounded, in the gathering dark, like a door locking. "Curious, though. My information suggested data drives. Recordings. Documents of a certain... evidentiary character." He looked up, and for the first time since the airboat had arrived, he wasn't smiling. What was underneath the smile was so much worse. "My information also suggested, Mr. Eriksen, that you contacted *me*. Alone. So perhaps you can explain to me why Miss Vane has been holding the actual goods against her body since before we tied off. Oilcloth carries a particular silhouette. I've searched enough smugglers to know it."

The storm chose that moment to arrive—not the storm, just its outrider, a fist of wind that flattened across the water and set the drowned chapel groaning around them. Rain came slantwise, sudden and cold.

"Brann—" Rosalind started.

"Take him," Brann said.

The big man moved. And Ilsa moved faster, because she had rehearsed this in the dark for six nights and her body knew the choreography better than her fear did. She stepped between them, put herself squarely in front of Rosalind, and raised her hands—not in surrender, but in announcement, the way you flag a transmission.

"Wait." Her voice cracked out over the wind. "You want the real case? You want the documents Renk's office buried, the levee schedules, the requisition orders, the recordings of the water authority planning which towns to drown? I have them. All of them. And I'm the only one who knows where they are, because they're not on this roof, they're not in the boat, and they're not anywhere you'll find them if anything happens to either of us."

Brann studied her through the rain. The big man had frozen mid-reach, shotgun half-raised, awaiting the word.

"You're bluffing," Brann said.

"I'm the survey team's communications officer, Mr. Brann. I encrypted our own archives. Do you know what happens to those files if I don't enter the release key within seventy-two hours of my death or capture? They scatter. Copies go out to forty addresses across four territories—news desks, ministries, rivals of your employers, people who would pay *enormously* to watch the Meridian Syndicate burn. A dead-man's switch. I built it myself, before I ran, because I knew what was coming for my team." She let that land. Let him do the arithmetic. "So here is the new transaction. You take me. Me, alive, unharmed, and the case stays with Mr. Eriksen—as collateral, as insurance, call it what you like. Your employers get their documents and their witness. He walks away free, debt voided, and you never see either of us again. Everyone's ledgers balance."

"And if I simply take you *and* the case?" Brann asked, almost gently. "And let the switch scatter its copies? My employers are reasonable people, Miss Vane. They understand risk. Forty addresses is a mess, but a mess they could survive. Whereas two witnesses and the originals..." He spread his hands, apologetic. "You're offering me a bargain where I accept risk to avoid risk. I'm an optimist, not a fool."

"Then here's the part you haven't calculated." Ilsa stepped backward, toward the roof's edge, toward the drop into black water. Behind her the drowned nave waited, full of its silent congregation. "Every minute you spend deciding, the storm's building. That cell to the north—the big one, the last of the season—it reaches this valley by midnight. Your airboat doesn't fly in that. Neither does your extraction. So you can take us both and gamble on getting out ahead of it with a dead-man's switch ticking, or you can take the deal that's standing right in front of you." She smiled at him, and it cost her everything to make it steady. "You taught us punctuality, Mr. Brann. Consider this a deadline."

The wind screamed. Rain hammered the tiles. And Brann, dripping, hatless, looked at her for a long five seconds with an expression she would remember for the rest of her life—not anger, not respect, but the cold professional interest of a man discovering that a line item in his ledger had grown teeth.

"Done," he said.

"No—" Rosalind's voice broke out of him raw, and he grabbed her arm, and for one suspended moment they were face to face in the rain, close enough that she could see everything happening behind his eyes: the calculation, the fury, the dawning terrible understanding that she had planned this, had planned it from the start, had used his own betrayal on the radio as the stage for hers.

"East along the timberline," she said, low, fast, just for him. "The cached skiff. Don't follow. Don't be a hero. Be the man who carries it out—that's the whole point of me, Rosalind. I'm the switch. You're the signal. Get clear and stay clear, and in seventy-two hours none of this matters anymore, because everyone will already know."

"Ilsa—"

"Trust me," she said. "Once. Just once. The way I trusted you on the river."

Then she stepped back out of his reach, lifted her chin, and walked to the airboat on her own two feet, the rain streaming down her face, the true case pressed against her ribs like a second heart, betting everything—her life, her brother's memory, her family's drowned land—on a single wager:

that Rosalind Eriksen, who had spent his whole life running, had finally learned to stop—

and that what he'd stop *for* was not his freedom, but the truth.

The airboat roared away into the dark. And behind her, fading in the rain, she heard the sound of the Patience's engine catching—heading east, heading for the timberline, heading exactly where she'd told him to go.

Which meant, she thought, gripping the rail as the drowned chapel sank behind her, that either he'd obeyed her...

or he was letting her believe he had.

The storm swallowed everything, and the seventy-two hours began.
