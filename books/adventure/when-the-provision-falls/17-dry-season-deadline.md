## 17. Dry Season Deadline

The water began to leave the way a liar leaves a room—slowly at first, then all at once, and without apology.

Rosalind noticed it on the third morning after the shoal, when the Patience's anchor line went slack and hung in the current like a dead man's arm. He stood in the bow with his coffee going cold and watched the delta exhale. Mudflats surfaced where yesterday there had been brown water. A fence post rose out of the flood, then a whole fence line, then the ghost of an orchard, its drowned trees standing hip-deep now instead of neck-deep, their branches still holding last season's rotting fruit like clenched fists that had forgotten how to open.

"Three days," Ilsa said behind him. She was counting too. She'd been counting since dawn, marking the waterline against a notch she'd cut into the gunwale with her knife. "Maybe four before the channels are passable by anything heavier than this boat."

"Passable means what it always means out here," Rosalind said. "It means someone with money can get where they want to go."

"And someone without money gets found."

He didn't answer that, because there was nothing to say that wasn't already true. The receding water wasn't mercy. It was a door opening—and doors opening in the delta only swung one direction, toward whoever held the keys. The government didn't need the monsoon anymore. They needed dry ground to move men and trucks, and dry ground was exactly what the sun was baking out of the mud hour by hour, flat and glistening and patient as a ledger page waiting for names.

* * *

The holdout camp sat on a rise that had once been a hill and was now, temporarily, an island again—the first land in the delta to dry, because it had been the highest ground before the floods took everything below it. Eleven structures. Rosalind counted them his first morning: three houses with roofs, two houses without, a chapel with a bell tower leaning like a drunk against the wind, a smokehouse, a net-drying rack, and a long low building that might have been a school before the water came through its windows and wrote its own curriculum.

The people had names he learned slowly, because they gave them slowly. Old Marta, who smoked a pipe packed with something that wasn't tobacco and watched the river like it owed her an explanation. The brothers Doss, who fished the floodwater with nets woven from parachute cord and spoke to each other in half-sentences, as if the other half were understood. A woman named Corren who kept goats on the roof of her own collapsed barn and climbed up to them twice a day on a ladder she'd built from church pews.

And Tam, who was maybe nineteen, who had hands that understood engines the way Rosalind's hands understood weather—not from books but from necessity, from taking things apart in the dark because no one else would do it.

Tam found him on the second morning, crouched beside the Patience where it lay beached on the mudflat below the camp, listing to port like a man leaning on a wall to stay upright.

"She's bad," Tam said, not unkindly. He said most things not unkindly, which made them worse somehow. "Planking's sprung along the garboard. Three strakes, maybe four. Your keel bolts are weeping rust. And your transom—" He knocked on it with a knuckle, and the wood answered with a sound like a coffin lid. "Your transom is a suggestion."

"She's gotten me through worse."

"Has she?" Tam looked at him with the frank curiosity of the young. "Through what?"

Rosalind considered lying, decided the boy had earned better, or at least silence. "Through enough."

Tam nodded as if that were an answer, which perhaps it was. Then he rolled up his sleeves. "Old Marta says you can use the smokehouse roof tin for patching. And Doss has marine epoxy left from before. From when there was a before." He spat into the mud, a local punctuation mark. "But you'll need to flip her today. Water drops another hand-width tonight, and if she dries crooked she'll stay crooked."

They flipped her together, six of them by afternoon—Doss and Doss, Tam, Corren's eldest boy, a silent man called Pell whose job seemed to be being strong in places where strength was needed—and the Patience went over onto her starboard side with a groan that Rosalind felt in his teeth, like hearing an old woman cry out.

He worked until his fingers split. Scraped hull. Pried sprung planks back toward their truths. Mixed epoxy in a paint lid while Tam held the light and narrated every mistake before Rosalind made it, which was either insolence or instruction, possibly both.

"You chase storms," Tam said, as Rosalind forced a copper patch over the worst of the garboard breach. "Marta says so. Says that's how you came upriver, riding the front like it was a road."

"It is a road. If you know how to read it."

"My father chased storms." Tam handed him another clamp. "Not for hire. For warning. He'd read the sky and run the bell, ring the chapel tower when the water was coming, give people time to move their animals uphill. The government called him a fearmonger. Then the water authority called him something worse." He paused. "You know what happened to the levee at Kiln Bend?"

"I've seen the maps."

"The maps show a breach. My father showed me the breach itself, before the water covered it. It wasn't torn. It wasn't eroded." Tam's voice stayed level, the way voices go level when the feeling underneath has passed beyond any height a voice can carry. "It was cut. Clean faces on both sides, like someone took a blade of earth and sliced it. He knew what it meant. He told everyone what it meant. And when the water came anyway, and the camp here lost nine people, the ones who survived stopped listening to him. Because listening meant knowing, and knowing meant grieving, and grieving meant you couldn't get up in the morning and fish."

Rosalind drove the last clamp home and sat back on his heels in the drying mud. His hands were shaking, and he let them.

"What happened to your father?"

"He rings the bell," Tam said simply. "Every evening. Same hour. Whether anyone listens or not. You can hear it across the flats when the wind's right." He looked at the boat, at the patch, at Rosalind's ruined hands. "She'll hold. She won't hold forever, and she won't hold in a real sea. But she'll hold long enough to be a decision."

"A decision?"

"Everything out here is one," Tam said. "The water taught us that. You're either deciding or you're debris."

* * *

That night the bell rang across the darkening flats, and Rosalind stood outside the smokehouse and listened to it the way you listen to a name you haven't heard spoken aloud in years.

Isla found him there—or Ilsa; he corrected himself, though the slip felt meaningful somewhere beneath language. She came to stand beside him, arms crossed against the evening chill that the dry season was already threading through the monsoon's wet heat. Above them the sky was doing something extraordinary: clearing. Actual stars, in numbers, in constellations he could almost name, showing through gaps in the cloud deck like coins at the bottom of draining water.

"They'll move the witnesses soon," she said. No preamble. She never used preambles with him anymore; they'd spent those somewhere between the drowned forest and the shoal. "The holdouts who saw the breaches. The families who kept records. Old Marta has photographs—did you know that? Photographs of the levee cuts, taken the week after the water dropped the first time. She's been sitting on them for two years because there was no one to give them to."

"There's a case full of evidence sitting in the bottom of your pack."

"There's a case full of evidence that Brann knows exists, that Renk knows exists, and that half the Meridian Syndicate's ledgers have a line item about." Ilsa turned to face him. In the bell-light, in the starlight, her face was all angles and resolve. "Evidence isn't safe just because it's true, Rosalind. Evidence is only as safe as the person carrying it is fast."

"So we're fast."

"We're a leaking skiff named Patience with a patched hull and two days of epoxy cure time." But she was almost smiling when she said it, and he caught himself almost smiling back, and the almost between them had become, over the weeks, its own kind of country—a place they went together without ever naming it on any map.

"How many witnesses?" he asked. "If the government comes to clear the delta before word gets out. How many people are on their list?"

Ilsa was quiet a moment. "Marta keeps a count. Forty-one adults who saw the cuts or kept documents. Plus children. Plus anyone who ever sold land to the water authority under pressure and could testify to the pressure." She looked out at the dark flats, where the last sheets of standing water were catching the stars like scattered mirrors. "Two hundred souls, give or take. Enough to fill trucks. Enough to disappear."

"Where would they take them?"

"Does it matter? Somewhere with walls. Somewhere the story ends."

The bell finished its evening count. Silence came back over the delta, vast and damp and full of small sounds—goats on rooftops, water dripping off leaves, the far-off mutter of the river finding its new channel through the falling land.

Rosalind realized he was doing the thing he did, the old thing, the thing that had kept him alive for fifteen years of chasing weather for men with ledgers: he was calculating exits. How many days to the coast. What the Patience could carry. Whether the case would fit in the forward locker. Whether two could outrun whatever came upriver, and whether two was the number he wanted, and whether wanting it was itself a kind of debt he was about to incur.

He made himself stop.

It felt like setting down a heavy pack after years of carrying it—except the weight didn't leave, it just changed location, settling somewhere behind his sternum where it would live now, apparently, permanently.

"I'm not leaving," he said.

Ilsa looked at him.

"Not with the case, not without it, not ahead of the water and not ahead of the trucks. I've been running the arithmetic since the sandbar—what it costs, what it pays, which way the odds break. That's all I know how to do. It's how the Syndicate found me and it's how I found you and it's how I'd have found my way to some other coast with some other debt, if you'd let me." He heard his own voice come out rougher than intended. "But Tam asked me today what I'd been through, and I couldn't answer, because the honest answer is nothing. Nothing that cost me anything I couldn't replace. I've survived everything because I've never once stood anywhere."

The stars were fully out now. The dry season's first proper night, cold enough to see breath.

"So here's what I choose," Rosalind said. "We cure the epoxy. We finish the boat. And when the government comes upriver to move forty-one witnesses into trucks, we're between them and the trucks. Not because we'll win. Because I'm done being debris."

Ilsa studied him for a long moment, and he braced for the journalist in her, the survivor in her, the woman who had slipped away from her own crew before the abduction and had carried that arithmetic ever since—the same arithmetic, he understood now, the same cold ledger of exits and odds. He braced for her to tell him the plan was suicide dressed as virtue.

Instead she reached into her jacket and brought out the case, and set it on the rail between them, and put her hand flat on top of it.

"Marta's photographs go inside tomorrow," she said. "And the Doss brothers' net log—they recorded water levels at Kiln Bend for eighteen months before the breach. Numbers don't grieve. Numbers can't be talked out of what they saw." Her voice cracked, just once, on the next words. "My brother kept a journal. I found pages of it in the camp office. If he's alive—if any of them are alive—then every hour we spend fixing a boat is an hour they're spending in a truck."

"Then we fix it fast."

"We fix it fast." She took her hand off the case, and in the dark her fingers found his—briefly, roughly, a grip like a handshake and a promise at once, gone again before either of them could make it mean more than it could bear. "First light. Bring your broken hands."

* * *

They worked three days.

The delta dried around them like a wound closing, and with every hand-width of falling water the horizon grew roads. Tam and Rosalind re-caulked the seams with pine tar Corren rendered from the chapel's old pews. The Doss brothers braided a new mooring line from parachute cord and fishing wire. Old Marta appeared on the mudflat the final afternoon with a bundle under her arm—her photographs, forty-one of them, each in its own oilcloth sleeve—and stood watching them work without offering help or comment, as if her presence alone were a kind of caulking.

On the third evening, Tam came down the bank at a run.

Rosalind knew before the boy spoke. Knew it the way he knew weather, in the teeth, in the old scar tissue, in the animal brain that had kept him alive through fifteen seasons of other people's catastrophes. Some things arrive ahead of themselves, pushing their atmosphere before them.

"Trucks," Tam said, breathing hard. "Corren saw them from the ridge. Six of them, military green, coming up the old levee road from the south. And a barge on the river behind them, riding high and empty." He swallowed. "Empty barges ride high. They don't come back empty."

Rosalind looked at the Patience, fresh-caulked, patched, her transom still a suggestion but her heart sound. Two days of epoxy cure, done. A boat built for one storm-chaser and his rig, carrying now a case full of truth, a woman with a brother in a truck somewhere, and a choice that had finally stopped being theoretical.

"How far?" he asked.

"Twelve miles. They'll reach the camp by midmorning."

Rosalind looked at Ilsa. She was already climbing aboard, already coiling the new line, already moving with the terrible efficiency of someone who had rehearsed this moment for two years without letting herself know it.

"Twelve miles," he said, and stepped over the transom that was a suggestion, and took the tiller that was a prayer. "Then we're not going to Renk's station."

Ilsa froze. "What?"

"We're going to meet the road." He felt the old storm-reading rise in him—not dread this time, not the fleeing animal calculation, but something with edges, something like joy's grim cousin. "Six trucks move slow on a levee road built for maintenance crews. And there's a storm cell sitting off the coast—I felt it in my knee this morning, the last gasp of the season, big and wet and stupid enough to use." He grinned, and it wasn't a nice grin. "Everybody thinks the dry season means the weather's done. Weather's never done. It's just changing its mind."

"You want to chase a storm into a convoy."

"I want to be the weather," Rosalind said, and opened the throttle, and the Patience—leaking, patched, resurrected from the mud by the hands of people who had lost everything except the refusal to be moved—took the falling water at speed, heading south into the drying light, toward the road, toward the trucks, toward the last storm of the year rolling in off the sea like a debt finally come due.
