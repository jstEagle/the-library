## 18. Storm Chaser's Gambit

The last storm of the season came up from the southeast like something that had been waiting all year for permission.

Rosalind felt it before he saw it—a pressure behind his eyes, a taste of iron in the rain-wet air, the particular hush that fell over the delta when every bird went silent at once. He stood at the rail of the *Patience*, patched and ugly and floating, and watched the horizon build its black cathedral. The cloud front rose in tiers, violet at the base, bruise-dark above, lightning flickering inside it like thought behind a skull.

Every instinct he had honed over eleven years said: *run*. Run south, run shallow, run for the mangrove cut where a man with a repaired hull and a good anchor could wait out anything the sky wanted to give.

He turned the bow toward it instead.

"You're insane." Old Tam, the holdout who'd helped him recaulk the seams, spat over the side with the deliberation of a man making a formal statement. "That's not weather. That's the season closing its account."

"That's exactly what it is," Rosalind said. "And I intend to be standing there when the collector arrives."

Tam looked at him the way men look at other men who have stopped being afraid of the wrong things—with pity, mostly, and a little awe. "You know what's upriver."

"I know what's upriver." Renk's station. A fortified water-authority outpost squatting on the last high ground before the drowned forest, ringed by levee berms and floodlights and, as of three days ago, one communications officer named Ilsa Vane who had traded herself for his life and taken the real case with her. He had spent those three days doing what he did best—nothing. Moving. Waiting out the calendar while the waters dropped and the government's window for moving witnesses narrowed with them.

He was done waiting out calendars.

"The storm will hit the station around dusk," he said, more to himself than to Tam. "Renk's people will batten down. Floodlights out or useless. Radios screaming with traffic. Every guard thinking about his own roof." He tapped the barometer bolted beside the wheel, its needle already sagging like a tired shoulder. "Nobody watches the river in weather like this. Nobody can."

"And you'll be on the river."

"I'll be *in* the river, probably, some of the time." He managed half a smile. It felt strange on his face, like wearing someone else's coat. "But I'll be the only one who chose to be."

* * *

He had chased storms for hire for a decade, and in all that time he had never once asked himself what the chasing was *for*. The rig recorded wind speed, barometric drop, rainfall intensity; the Syndicate paid per verified dataset; the money went into the ledger against a debt that never shrank. The storm was a client. The data was product. He moved because stopping meant settling accounts, and settling accounts meant admitting what his life added up to.

Now he stood at the wheel of a rotting skiff with no rig, no contract, no client, and steered directly into the worst cell of the year for a woman who had every reason to believe he'd sold her out—and the difference between then and now sat in his chest like a swallowed coal.

Because this time the storm wasn't paying him.

This time he was spending everything he had left.

The first outer bands hit an hour before dusk—long gray curtains of rain marching across the water, bending the dead trees along the channel until they groaned. Rosalind ran the *Patience* hard through the rising chop, reading the water the way other men read documents. There were skills you learned when your survival depended on noticing things: how a squall line told you its direction of travel by the lean of its rain; how wind backed through the compass ahead of a cell's rotation; how the river itself changed voice—shallow water hissing, deep water moaning—as the pressure fell.

For years those skills had been armor. Tools for staying alive, staying mobile, staying unaccountable. Now he aimed them like weapons at a fortified station full of men who thought weather was something that happened to other people.

*Let them think it,* he thought, as the sky went the color of a closed fist. *I'm about to introduce them.*

* * *

Dusk never really arrived; the storm simply took the light away early, like a landlord changing the locks.

Rosalind ran the final approach with the engine at half-throttle and the spray hood down, blind navigation by memory and counting—he had sounded the channel twice in the past two days, pacing off the distances between submerged stumps, memorizing the dogleg where the drowned forest thinned before Renk's levee. Rain hammered the deck so hard it bounced. Lightning strobed the world white at intervals of two seconds, then one, then continuous, so that the whole landscape flickered like a broken film reel: black trees, white trees, black water, white water.

Somewhere in that strobing dark, the station's floodlights should have been visible.

They weren't. Either the storm had already taken the power grid, or Renk's people had killed the lights themselves—which meant they knew the storm was coming and were hunkered, which meant the timing was right.

He brought the *Patience* alongside the levee's downstream face, where the berm's shadow broke the wind just enough to work. The plan was simple in the way desperate plans are simple: the storm surge would push the river over the low eastern section of the levee within the hour—he'd watched the water levels climb all day, done the arithmetic in his head the way he used to do barometric drops. When the eastern berm failed, the station's lower level would flood. Guards would move prisoners or abandon them. Doors would open that stayed shut in fair weather.

All he had to do was be there when they did.

He tied off to a dead cedar whose roots still gripped the levee's flank, triple-hitched the line, and stood in the cockpit getting soaked through in four seconds flat, feeling the storm arrive fully now—the pressure change in his ears, the wind climbing through registers like a singer ascending scales, the first real gusts slamming the boat against its lines. Somewhere upstream, metal shrieked against metal. The river, already high, began to breathe in long irregular swells, as if something enormous below were turning over in its sleep.

"Okay," he said aloud, to nobody, to Ilsa, to whatever version of himself had once believed debts were only money. "Okay. One more contract."

There was no client to pay him. That was the whole point.

* * *

He went over the side into chest-deep water and hauled himself up the levee's muddy flank hand over hand, the wind trying to peel him off the slope like wallpaper. At the top he flattened against the berm and looked down into the station compound, and what he saw made him grin into the rain, a wild, unhinged grin he hadn't worn since he was young enough to think storms were beautiful.

Chaos. Beautiful, gift-wrapped chaos.

The compound lights were out except for one generator-fed lamp over the main building's door, swinging on its bracket so that shadows wheeled across the yard like scavengers. Men in rain gear moved between buildings at a crouch, shouting to each other in fragments the wind tore apart. A truck sat backed up to the main structure, tarp flapping, its bed half-loaded—supplies, crates, maybe people. The eastern berm, exactly as he'd calculated, was already weeping water over its crest in a dozen silver threads, each thread thickening even as he watched.

Renk's people were preparing to evacuate. And evacuation, Rosalind knew from long experience with things going wrong in bad weather, was when systems failed. When lists got lost. When someone forgot which room held the prisoner, or decided she could wait in the flooding dark while more important cargo loaded first.

He slid down the inside face of the berm, found the drainage culvert Tam had told him about—an old sluice pipe, rusted but passable, running beneath the levee into the compound—and went in on hands and knees through water that rose around him as he crawled, cold and brown and pushing, the river's fingers reaching through the earth ahead of the storm.

Halfway through the pipe, wedged in the dark with the roar of the storm muffled to a heartbeat drumming on iron, he had a moment of perfect clarity. This was what all of it had been for—not the data, not the ledger, not the years of motion. This. A man crawling through a flooded pipe toward a door he had no right to open, for reasons no ledger could price. His skills had kept him alive for eleven years. Tonight, finally, they were pointed at something worth surviving *for*.

He came out the far end into knee-deep water behind the fuel shed, pressed himself against corrugated steel, and listened.

The wind screamed. The berm, somewhere behind him, gave a long groaning sigh, and a new sound joined the storm's chorus: the steady, musical trickle of the eastern levee beginning to fail in earnest. Shouts sharpened across the compound. A whistle blew twice, urgent. The truck's engine turned over.

The lower level of the main building would be flooding now. And Ilsa Vane—who had handed herself over to buy him thirty-six hours, who had bet everything on the idea that he was the kind of man who came back—was somewhere inside it.

Rosalind crossed the compound yard in the gaps between lightning flashes, low and fast, using the swaying door-lamp's rotating shadow as cover the way he'd once used cloud shadows to hide a chase plane's approach. The main building's back door stood open, propped with a sandbag, rain blowing in across a corridor floor already slick with an inch of standing water. Inside, emergency lighting painted everything the color of old blood.

He heard footsteps, ducked through a doorway into a storeroom smelling of diesel and mildew, and waited while two men splashed past arguing about the boats. Then he moved deeper into the building, following the water.

It was rising fast. An inch became three inches became six, flowing down the corridor from the east wall in a braided current, carrying leaves and silt and one small, terrible detail: a woman's boot, mud-stained, caught against a doorframe.

Not hers. Wrong size. But it told him the lower cells were already underwater, and that whoever had been held there had been moved—or not.

His heart slammed against his ribs. He forced his breathing slow, the way he did when the barometer fell faster than the instruments could track. Panic was a luxury for people with dry ground. He checked the doors along the corridor—locked, locked, locked—then heard it, faint under the storm's percussion: knocking. Rhythmic. Deliberate. Three, pause, three. Coming from beyond a steel door at the corridor's end, half-submerged already, its lock rattling with each blow from the other side.

Three and three. Their old signal, from the nights bailing the *Patience* together. *Still here. Still here.*

Rosalind waded to the door and put his mouth close to the freezing steel.

"Ilsa."

The knocking stopped. Then, muffled, furious, unmistakably her voice: "You absolute idiot. Tell me you didn't sail *into* that."

"Wouldn't be the first stupid thing I've done this season." He was already working the lock with a pry bar from the storeroom, water climbing his thighs, the whole building groaning around them like a ship in its death throes. Through the door's seam he could see water pouring into the cell block from a ruptured grate, and beyond it, dimly, a figure standing waist-deep with her arms crossed, glaring at him through the dark as if he were late for an appointment.

Which, he supposed, he was.

"Hold on," he said, setting the bar against the hasp. "I'm getting you out."

Above them, through the ceiling, came the sound of boots and shouting and the truck pulling away—and beneath all of it, rising like a second storm, the groan of the eastern levee letting go at last.

* * *

The door gave on the fourth swing of the pry bar, hinges shrieking, and Ilsa Vane came through the gap into his arms more carried than walking, her weight wrong—one leg dragging, a bruise blooming along her jaw that hadn't been there five days ago.

"They moved the others yesterday," she said against his shoulder, no greeting, no thanks, because they were past that and both knew it. "Upstairs, then out. I heard Renk giving orders. They're taking everyone north before the roads drown." She pulled back, searched his face in the bloody emergency light, and her voice cracked on the next words despite everything. "You actually came."

"You bet on it," he said. "I don't like losing bets."

Water surged past their knees as the ruptured grate let the river in properly now, the current spinning debris down the corridor. Behind them, the building shuddered. Somewhere overhead a beam cracked like a rifle shot, and a fine rain of dust sifted down through the emergency light.

"We have maybe ten minutes before this floor's gone," Rosalind said, tightening his grip on her arm, steering them both toward the back door and the drowning compound beyond. "Can you swim?"

"In this?" She looked at the brown torrent swallowing the corridor, and for the first time since the sandbar, he saw fear move through her face like weather. "Rosalind—the case. Renk kept it in his office. Second floor. If the building goes—"

The levee outside answered her, a deep structural boom rolling through the walls like the earth clearing its throat, and the water around them jumped a foot in a single breath.

The office was upstairs. The building was coming down. And the last storm of the season, the one he'd ridden in on like a weapon, was now trying to bury the evidence—and the woman—under forty feet of collapsing concrete.

"Then we go up," Rosalind said, and pushed toward the stairs.
