## 19. The Price Collected

The water came for Renk's station the way debts come for debtors—slowly at first, then all at once.

Ilsa felt it before she saw it: a cold kiss against her bare ankle as she crouched in the corridor outside the holding room, the dark pressing close and smelling of river silt and diesel. The station had been built on pilings above the flood line, arrogant about it, but Rosalind's storm had shoved the river up its own throat, and now the delta was reclaiming what it had always owned. The water was rising through the floor grates. She could hear it gurgling beneath her like something patient.

Behind her, muffled by two steel doors and thirty meters of corridor, an alarm had begun to shriek—the storm-chaser's gambit working exactly as audaciously as he'd promised. Rosalind Eriksen had sailed *into* the last cell of the season, ridden a wall of wind straight into the station's exposed western face, and let the monsoon do what no lockpick could. Somewhere below, generators were drowning. Somewhere below, every door on the emergency circuit had swung open on failing power.

Including hers.

She flexed her fingers. They were stiff from three days of captivity, from zip ties that had bitten grooves into her wrists, from Brann's cheerful interrogations conducted with the warmth of a man discussing weather over tea. Her ribs ached where a guard's boot had found them. None of it mattered. What mattered was the weight against her spine, strapped flat beneath her jacket: the real case. The ledger. Renk's own accounting of nine lives priced and spent, with the Meridian Syndicate's name sitting fat and satisfied in the middle column.

*You bet everything,* she thought, and didn't know if she meant Rosalind or herself or both of them, standing in different corridors betting the same everything.

The door at the end of the passage opened—not burst, just opened, casual as a man stepping out for air—and Rosalind came through it sideways, soaked to the bone, a pry bar in one hand and an expression she'd never seen on him before: hope with its teeth bared.

"You're upright," he said. "Good. Walking's going to be optional in about four minutes."

"Rosalind." His name came out of her rougher than she intended. Three days of not knowing if he'd actually come, if the gambit was a story she'd invented to survive the waiting. "You absolute idiot."

"I've been called worse this week." He crossed to her fast, hands hovering near her shoulders without landing, cataloguing damage the way he'd catalogue a rig after lightning. "Ribs?"

"Cracked, maybe. I can move."

"Moving's the whole curriculum." He glanced back down the corridor toward the sound of the alarm, then up, toward the ceiling, listening. Ilsa listened too. Under the alarm, under the water's gurgle, there was another sound now—a rhythmic mechanical churn, getting louder.

Airboat. Coming up the flooded approach channel. Brann.

"He knows," she said. "He has to know the doors are open."

"He knows the station's coming apart." Rosalind took her elbow, gently, and turned her away from the holding room, away from the stairs that led down into black water, toward the eastern gallery—the long glass-walled walkway Renk had built so he could watch his drowned kingdom from above the tide line. "There's a maintenance stair off the gallery. Drops to the boat dock on the lee side. If we're quick—"

The far end of the corridor filled with light.

Not lightning. A flashlight beam, swinging wide and confident, and behind it a voice that Ilsa had heard for three days in her nightmares, always pleasant, always smiling: *"Now, let's not make this difficult."*

Brann.

* * *

They ran.

The gallery was a hundred meters of glass and groaning frame, the storm hammering the windows hard enough that Ilsa kept waiting for the whole wall to go. Through the glass, the world was chaos—rain moving sideways, the river brown and boiling, trees going down along the levee like dominoes. Below, the flooded grounds swallowed the station's legs. Somewhere in that murk, the airboat's engine note rose and fell, hunting.

Rosalind ran ahead of her, testing each section of floor with a stomp before committing his weight, and she understood why when the third section answered him with a crack. The storm had gotten inside the building's bones. Water was in the walls. Water was everywhere, doing what water did, which was find every weakness and insist upon it.

"Maintenance stair," he said, pointing left through a service door hanging off one hinge. "Thirty seconds."

Behind them, boots on tile. Not running yet. Walking. That was worse somehow—Brann walking, unhurried, because Brann believed the building was a trap they'd already stepped in.

"Ilsa." Rosalind stopped at the service door and turned, and in the strobing emergency light his face was stripped of everything clever. "If it comes down to it—if he closes the distance—you take the case and you don't look back. You hear me? You swim, you run, you crawl. You get it to people who'll use it right."

"No," she said.

"It wasn't a question."

"It wasn't an answer either." She grabbed his collar, rain-wet, cold. "Listen to me. Three days in a cell, I did the math. Every version of me escaping alone ends with the case in a river and me in a grave and nobody ever knowing. Every version with *you*—" She didn't finish it. The gallery window ten meters behind them exploded inward.

Not the glass—the frame. A whole section of wall surrendered to the wind, and the storm poured through the gap like it had been waiting for the invitation, tearing at their clothes, their breath, ripping loose papers and furniture into the dark. And through that gap, riding the surge, came the bow of the airboat.

Brann stood at the controls with rain streaming off him, and even now—even here—he was smiling. He killed the engine and the fan spun down to a whine, and his voice carried across the roar with terrible good cheer.

"There they are! Mr. Eriksen. Ms. Vane. You've cost me two boats, a season's patience, and a considerable professional embarrassment." He stepped off the bow onto the flooded gallery floor, water to his shins, hands open and empty. "I'd like those things back. Along with the case Ms. Vane is so devotedly carrying."

"The Syndicate voided you," Rosalind shouted over the wind. "I read it in your own ledger, Brann. You're not collecting anymore. You're just a man in the rain."

Something moved behind Brann's smile then—the first true thing Ilsa had ever seen on his face. It looked like hunger wearing a mask that had slipped.

"A man in the rain," Brann repeated softly. "Yes. Well. Let's see how the rain treats us all."

He lunged for the nearest support column.

Ilsa understood one heartbeat too late what he was doing. The columns holding the gallery roof were old, storm-shaken, their footings dissolved by days of floodwater—and Brann, who had built nothing in his life except other people's ruin, knew exactly where the weakness lived. He hit the column with his shoulder, once, twice, a third time, and the crack that traveled up through the structure sounded like the world clearing its throat.

"RUN!" Rosalind's hand closed on her wrist and hauled.

They went through the service door as the gallery began to die. The sound was enormous—not a crash but a *procession*, a rolling thunder of glass and steel and river water as forty meters of walkway folded into the flood. Ilsa felt the shockwave through the maintenance stair's railing, felt dust and spray hit her back like a hand. She didn't look back. Looking back was a luxury for people whose debts were paid.

The stair was narrow, rusted, lit by nothing. They descended by feel, Rosalind ahead calling the turns, the building screaming around them, and at the bottom the dock stretched out into churning black water where—miraculously, absurdly—the Patience sat tied and bobbing, half-swamped but alive, because Rosalind Eriksen did not leave his rotting skiff anywhere he couldn't reach it.

"Board. Cut the line. Go." He pushed her toward the gunwale.

That was when the second collapse came.

It wasn't the gallery this time. It was the whole eastern face of the station, surrendering at once, and the pressure wave of it rolled across the dock like a fist. Ilsa went down hard on the planking, the case grinding into her spine, and heard Rosalind shout her name through the roar—and then heard something else. A cry. Human, furious, terrified.

Brann had not gone down with his gallery. He'd ridden the collapse the way he rode everything—on top of it—and now he was on the dock twenty meters away, dragging himself up out of the wash, and the dock itself was tilting, the pilings beneath it snapping one by one like knuckles cracking.

For one suspended moment, the three of them existed in the same square meter of failing world: the collector, the debtor, the deserter. Rain fell. Pilings broke. The river waited underneath with its mouth open.

Brann came at them anyway. Of course he did. He came at them with his coat shredded and his smile finally, completely gone, and Rosalind met him—not with fists, but with the cleat line, a length of wet rope he swung wide and low, catching Brann's ankles. The collector went down on the tilting planks, slid, caught the mooring post with both hands.

And the mooring post came out of the rotting dock like a tooth.

Ilsa would remember it afterward in fragments, the way survivors remember everything: Brann's hands closing on emptiness. His body tipping backward with a strange, almost polite slowness. The moment his eyes found hers across the rain—not accusation, not fear, just a kind of astonished bookkeeping, a man watching his own account close—and then the water took him. No splash she could hear over the storm. Just the river accepting one more entry in its ledger, and the surface closing smooth as a signature.

Gone. The man who collected in blood, paid in full.

"ILSA!" Rosalind was at the gunwale, arm out, the Patience heeling away from the dying dock. "NOW!"

She threw the case first—it was the only thing in the world that mattered—then jumped, and the river caught her like a hammer.

* * *

Cold. Total, ancient cold, the kind that erased thought. The delta in flood season was not water; it was a living thing with opinions, and it wanted her. She tumbled, struck something soft—submerged brush—felt her left side detonate with pain, a white-hot line from hip to armpit that she understood distantly was metal, torn dock planking or rebar, opening her like an envelope. Then hands. Strong hands, hooking under her arms, hauling, and Rosalind's voice cutting through the roar: *"I've got you—I've got you—breathe—"*

Then the gunwale, and then the deck, and then nothing for a while.

She surfaced back into the world slowly, in pieces. Rain on her face. The sky a bruised grey-green, the storm's heart passing overhead with a sound like freight. The Patience wallowing beneath her, shipping water, Rosalind bailing with one hand and steering with the other and shouting things at the river that were probably prayers in a language neither of them spoke.

Her side was on fire. She pressed her palm there and it came away black in the darkness.

"Rosalind." Her voice was a stranger's. "How bad."

He looked at her—one glance, the glance of a man who had spent his life reading instruments in bad weather—and whatever he saw made his jaw set. "Hold this." He jammed the bail bucket into her hands. "Press it there. Hard. We're ninety minutes from the holdout camp if the river lets us be rude to it."

"Brann?"

"Gone." Something crossed his face—not satisfaction. Nothing so clean. "The river settled his accounts."

"And Renk?"

Rosalind laughed, short and bitter, without any humor in it at all. "Saw him from the gallery. Loading a case of his own into a skiff, heading upriver with his tail between his legs. He got out with his skin and nothing else. No station, no ledger, no operation. He'll spend the rest of his life being a small man somewhere far from water." He spat over the side. "Some men get blood. Some get nothing. Seems fair, doesn't it?"

Nothing about it seemed fair. Nothing about any of it had ever been fair—that was the entire point, the whole rotten architecture of it, nine people in a survey team priced at less than land value, a family's fields drowned on purpose, a brother whose name she hadn't spoken aloud in three days because speaking it in that cell felt like handing Brann one more thing to collect.

Tomas. She said it now, silently, to the rain. *We won. Hold onto that. Whoever's left to know it—we won.*

The Patience limped east. Ilsa bailed, and pressed the bucket to her side, and watched Rosalind fight the river with everything he had, and somewhere between one wave and the next she understood that the fire in her ribs was serious, that the cold wasn't leaving her, that her hands had gone the color of the water. She told him so in the flattest voice she could manage.

He didn't argue. That frightened her more than panic would have. He just put the tiller hard over and began running the boat like a man being chased by time itself, and talked to her the whole way—low, constant, nonsense mostly, stories about the worst contracts he'd ever chased, the time lightning melted his rig into modern art, the time he'd owed a fishmonger in Saltmere eleven credits and paid it back in weather reports for a year. Keeping her awake. Keeping her *here*.

"Don't you dare drift," he said, when her eyes closed longer than a blink. "You jumped off a collapsing dock for a box of paper. You don't get to quit on the boat ride home."

"It's not paper," she murmured. "It's names. Nine names. Ten, counting mine."

"Then stay awake and carry them."

* * *

The holdout camp appeared out of the rain like something remembered rather than seen: lantern light through canvas, the smell of woodsmoke fighting the wet, voices rising as the Patience scraped the shallows and hands reached out of the dark to pull them in.

After that, the night became a blur of oil lamps and pain. A woman with a surgeon's hands and a schoolteacher's voice cut away her jacket and said words like *deep* and *lucky* and *fever*, and Ilsa held someone's leather strap between her teeth and made sounds she would deny forever. There was broth she couldn't taste. There was a roof that leaked, and she found she loved it anyway, because it was a roof that belonged to no government and no syndicate and no ledger anywhere in the world.

Dawn came the way it always does after the worst night—unhurried, indifferent, beautiful. The storm had spent itself. Through the tent's open flap, Ilsa watched the light come up gold over the delta, over water that was already, imperceptibly, beginning to fall.

Rosalind sat beside her cot, soaked through, hollow-eyed, his hands still shaking faintly around a cup of tea he hadn't drunk. He had carried her from the boat to the camp through thigh-deep water, she'd learned. He had not let anyone else take her weight. He had not slept.

"We made it," she said. Her voice was gravel and gauze.

"We made it." He looked at her then, and there was something raw in his face she'd only seen once before—in the dry hold, bailing through the night, trading truths. "The case?"

"Under your jacket since the dock. Don't think I didn't notice." Almost a smile. "Still yours to help carry."

He nodded slowly. Outside, the delta gleamed, wounded and waking, and somewhere beyond the horizon the world that didn't yet know was about to learn nine names.

"One more thing," Rosalind said quietly. "The transmitter at the station's gone. But the holdouts have a shortwave rig—old, patched together, barely reaches the coast." He turned the untouched cup in his hands. "Barely is enough. Barely always was."

Ilsa looked at him—at the man who had chosen truth over freedom on a sinking dock, who carried her like she weighed nothing and the case like it weighed everything—and understood that the hardest part was still ahead.

"How soon can you broadcast?" she asked.

"As soon as you can sit up," he said. "The waters are falling, Ilsa. So are the odds."
