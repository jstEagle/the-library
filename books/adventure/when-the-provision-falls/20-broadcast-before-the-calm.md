## 20. Broadcast Before the Calm

The transmitter room smelled of ozone and old rain.

Rosalind stood in the doorway with Ilsa's arm over his shoulder, her blood drying tacky against his collar, and looked at the machine that had outlived everything around it. The station's corridors were still weeping from the storm surge he'd sailed them through—water sheeting down the stairwells, pooling in the server room below where Renk's carefully preserved records floated like drowned leaves—but up here, on the top floor beneath a roof that had mostly held, the transmitter sat dry and patient as a church organ. A Marconi-pattern longwave rig, government issue, built to broadcast flood warnings to a coast that no longer trusted the word *flood*.

"You're sure it still works," Ilsa said. It wasn't a question so much as a dare. She was gray-faced, one hand pressed flat against the wound in her side where shrapnel from Brann's collapsing airboat had opened her like a second mouth. The med kit from the holdout camp had given her pressure bandages and something for the pain that made her words come slow and deliberate, each one placed like a stone across a stream.

"The flood warnings ran every monsoon until three years ago," Rosalind said. "Nobody decommissions the thing that screams at people. They just stop listening." He crossed to the console and brushed two fingers through the dust on the main panel. Beneath it, the indicator lights were dark but intact. "The question isn't whether it works. It's whether anyone's still tuned in."

"Then make them tune in."

He almost smiled. Even half-drained of blood, Ilsa Vane could not resist issuing orders. He lowered her into the operator's chair—the only chair—and she hissed through her teeth but kept her spine straight, refusing to slump, refusing to look like a woman who'd been carried up four flights of stairs by a man who owed her more than he could count.

Outside, the last storm cell of the season was dying over the delta. Through the transmitter room's cracked window, Rosalind watched the lightning go soft and distant, thunder arriving late and apologetic, like a debt collector who knew the account was already closed. The rain had thinned to a silver drizzle. Somewhere below, water was finding its way out of the station through every crack and doorway, retreating the way an army retreats—unwillingly, leaving things behind.

* * *

The case sat between them on the console, open now, its contents laid out like relics on an altar. Data drives. Printed ledgers with the Water Authority's seal stamped in fading gold. Photographs of levee breach points taken days before the floods came, annotated in a surveyor's neat hand. And the mirror document from Renk's safe—the operation's own accounting, line by line, name by name: parcels acquired, families displaced, payments routed through the Meridian Syndicate as disbursement agents, and at the bottom, initialed in ink that had not faded because it had never been exposed to daylight, the signature of Aldous Renk himself.

Rosalind had read it twice. Once for the truth of it, and once for the shape of it—the way his own life looked when rendered in ledger form. Debts incurred. Assets collected. Losses written off. Nine names under a column headed *Personnel*, each one followed by a dash where a disposition should have been. Her brother's name was third from the top.

Ilsa hadn't cried over it. She'd gone very still instead, the way water goes still before it freezes, and then she'd said, "Broadcast all of it. Every page. Let them read their own handwriting."

Now Rosalind worked the console with hands that remembered this kind of machinery from years ago, back when he'd chased storms for science instead of money, back when sensor rigs were tools rather than collateral. He found the power feed, traced it past a tripped breaker, coaxed the auxiliary generator in the basement to cough and catch. The lights on the panel woke one by one, amber and green, and a hum rose through the floor that felt less like electricity than like a pulse.

"Frequency?" Ilsa asked.

"All of them." He pulled the frequency log from the drawer—Renk's station had been a relay hub, which meant it carried schedules for every band on the coast: emergency services, shipping channels, the fishing cooperatives' weather nets, even the pirate frequencies the holdouts used. "We've got maybe forty minutes before the authorities move to seal the zone. The waters are dropping. By morning there'll be gunboats in the channels and cordon teams on the roads. So we don't whisper this. We shout it on every channel they own, and a few they don't."

"And if they jam us?"

"They'll try. But jamming takes time to organize, and time is the one thing we finally have more of than they do." He fed the first data drive into the reader port, watching the amber light blink as it chewed through files. "Besides, you can't unhear a thing. Not really. Not once enough people have heard it."

Isla was quiet for a moment. When she spoke, her voice had lost its command edge and become something rawer. "My brother used to say that about the tide. You can't un-tide. You can only decide what the water leaves behind."

Rosalind thought about what the water had left behind. A drowned forest. A sandbar with a broken radio. A woman who'd walked away from nine people and spent months walking back toward them. A man who'd spent ten years running from a ledger and had finally, tonight, read someone else's.

"It'll leave this," he said. "If we do it right."

* * *

They began at the top of the hour, because Ilsa insisted that broadcasts should start clean, and because Rosalind understood that ritual mattered even—or especially—at the end of the world.

He keyed the mic and let the carrier tone sing out across the bands for thirty seconds, that flat, patient note that every radio operator on the coast would recognize as either a test or a warning. Then he cleared his throat, and discovered his hands were shaking, and made himself speak anyway.

"This is a transmission from Relay Station Nine, Saltmere Delta. My name doesn't matter. What follows does."

He read the ledger first. Not fast—he read it the way a priest reads the names of the dead, giving each figure its full weight, each parcel number, each displaced family, each payment routed through Meridian accounts. He described the levee breach points and the dates they'd been cut, weeks before the rains that were supposed to explain them. He read the survey team's findings verbatim, including the section where a government hydrologist had written, in language clearly meant for internal eyes only: *Depopulation via engineered inundation is proceeding ahead of schedule; land acquisition costs remain within projected parameters.*

*Within projected parameters.* Rosalind said those words twice, slowly, and heard Ilsa's breath catch beside him.

On the shipping channels, somewhere far off, a voice broke in demanding identification. He ignored it. On the emergency band, another voice, younger, frightened, asking if this was a drill. He ignored that too, though it cost him something. On the holdout frequencies—the ones the drowned people of the delta still monitored because hope, like mold, grows in any damp place—he heard static shift and settle, and knew that somewhere out there, in stilt houses and hull-shelters and the high rooms of churches, people were listening.

He read for eleven minutes without stopping. Then he switched drives and read the photographs' annotations, describing each breach point in detail sufficient that any engineer on the coast could verify it against the wreckage. Then the mirror document, top to bottom, Renk's initials included, the Syndicate's routing numbers included, the word *disbursement* included, because Rosalind wanted the optimists to hear their own vocabulary spoken aloud on every channel they owned.

When he finally paused, Ilsa leaned forward, wincing, and took the mic herself.

"For the record," she said, and her voice was steadier than his had been, "the survey team did not vanish. They were collected. Their names are—" and she read them, all nine, ending with her brother's, holding the last syllable of it a moment longer than the others, the way you hold a door for someone who can no longer walk through it. "If anyone hearing this knows where they were taken, or what was done, say their names back. Loudly. Often. Names are the one thing a ledger can't hold forever."

She set down the mic. In the window's reflection, Rosalind saw that her face was wet, and that she made no move to hide it.

* * *

The jamming started nineteen minutes in.

It came as a low growl under their signal, a brute-force wall of noise rolling up from the south, where the authorities' relay towers stood. Rosalind watched the signal meters shudder and dip. But the station's antenna array was old and broad and stubborn, built in an era when redundancy was a virtue, and the holdout frequencies—low-power, scattered, technically illegal—slipped under the jamming like minnows under a net.

"They're cutting the regional relays," Ilsa said, reading the telemetry with an officer's eye. "Give them twenty minutes and the big channels go dead."

"Twenty minutes is enough." Rosalind pulled the final drive from the case—the duplicate, the one they'd made at the holdout camp, copied onto cheap consumer drives bought from a smuggler with no questions asked. There were seven of them. "Because we're not relying on the tower alone. Every boat with a receiver has been recording since the top of the hour. Every holdout with a hand-crank unit. Every fishing crew that keeps a log. By dawn this will be copied more times than anyone can burn."

He thought of the Patience, rotting at her mooring below the station, taking water but refusing, in her contrary way, to sink. He thought of the holdouts who'd sheltered them, who'd listened to their story with faces like closed doors and then, without a word, had begun preparing food and bandages and fuel—as if the telling itself had been a key turning in a lock. One of them, an old woman named Maren whose house had been underwater for two years, had pressed a copy of the drive into Rosalind's hand and said, "Make it loud. We've been quiet long enough."

He distributed the remaining drives into oilcloth and sealed them. Six copies. Six directions. Some would be lost. Enough would not.

The jamming wall climbed higher. The big channels died one by one—shipping first, then emergency services, then the coastal weather nets, each one going to static with a sound like a held breath released. But the holdout frequencies held, thin and clear, and on them Rosalind could hear voices now, faint, repeating fragments of what they'd heard. A fisherman reading parcel numbers back. A child's voice reciting the team's names, stumbling on the fourth one, starting over. Someone singing—not a hymn exactly, but something with the same bones.

Ilsa listened with her eyes closed. "It's loose," she said quietly. "You can't repossess a thing once everyone owns a piece of it."

"No," Rosalind agreed. "That was always the plan. Debt works both ways. Now they owe the truth to everyone who heard it, and there aren't enough collectors in the world to call it in."

* * *

They shut the transmitter down at dawn's edge, gently, the way you close a book you intend to lend rather than keep.

The silence afterward was enormous. Rosalind realized he'd been speaking, in one way or another, for nearly three hours, and that his throat felt scraped and his ears rang with the ghost of the carrier tone. Outside the cracked window, the drizzle had stopped altogether. The clouds were tearing apart in long gray ribbons, and behind them, for the first time in five months, there was sky the color of nothing at all—clean, pale, waiting.

Dry season light.

Ilsa saw it too. She didn't say anything, but her hand found his on the console, and her grip was fierce despite the blood loss, and neither of them spoke about it, because some things survive better untranslated.

Below them, the station dripped and settled. Somewhere downriver, engines—gunboats, probably, coming to seal a zone that no longer contained anything worth sealing. Somewhere upriver, Aldous Renk was running with nothing but his title and his fear. Somewhere in a Syndicate office, a ledger was being amended, a liability being written off, a smile being prepared for the next optimistic investor.

And somewhere out on the water, in a hundred small boats and stilt houses and flooded chapels, people were saying the names.

Rosalind shouldered the empty case. "We should move. They'll sweep this building within the hour."

"I know." Ilsa rose, swayed, and let him take her weight without comment. At the door she stopped and looked back at the transmitter, dark now, its work done. "Do you feel different?"

He considered it honestly, the way he tried to consider everything now. "Lighter," he said finally. "Like after a storm passes and you realize you survived it. Except this one's still coming, isn't it? The consequences. Investigations, trials, whatever the Syndicate does next. All of it."

"Yes."

"Good." He opened the door onto the wet stairwell, onto the smell of ozone and old rain already fading, onto the first dry-season morning either of them had seen in half a year. "I'm done running from weather. From here on, I'd like to know what I'm walking into."

They went down together, slowly, one step at a time, while outside the light grew stronger and the water kept falling away from the land, revealing, inch by inch, everything that had been hidden beneath it.
