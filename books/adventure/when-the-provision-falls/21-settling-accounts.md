## 21. Settling Accounts

The rain stopped the way a debt collector leaves—abruptly, without apology, leaving you standing in the wreckage wondering what you'd agreed to.

Rosalind Eriksen woke on the fourth morning of the dry season to a silence so complete it felt like an injury. For eight months the coast had spoken in one voice, the endless gray percussion of water on water, and now there was nothing but the creak of the Patience's patched hull against her mooring lines and the distant complaint of gulls working the exposed mudflats. The delta had emerged from its long drowning like something exhumed: black channels running between ridges of silt, dead trees standing hip-deep in the retreating flood with their arms raised as if surrendering or blessing, depending on how you looked at it.

He lay in the bunk a while, listening to the boat settle, and waited for the dread to arrive at its usual hour. It didn't. That was new. The dread had been his traveling companion for eleven years—the low constant pressure of what he owed, of what stopping would cost—and now that the Meridian Syndicate's ledger no longer had his name in it, he kept reaching for it out of habit and finding nothing there. It was like missing a tooth. His tongue kept going to the gap.

The transmitter at Renk's station had done its work nine days ago. He still remembered the feel of the key under his palm, Ilsa bleeding through her bandages beside him, her hand flat on the console as if she could push the signal out by will alone. They had broadcast everything—the survey data, the levee breach records, the Syndicate's routing documents, Renk's own accounting of what each drowned parcel of land had been worth and who had signed for it. Not once, but on rotation, for six hours, until the first government gunboat rounded the bend and they'd walked out the back into the mangroves with the station's generators still humming behind them.

Now the world was catching up.

* * *

It caught up first in the form of a newspaper, which surprised him. He'd expected soldiers, or Syndicate knives, or at minimum another smiling man with a ledger. Instead, three days after the broadcast, a fisherman named Odell had motored out to the Patience holding a week-old edition of the *Coastal Register* wrapped in oilcloth, and refused payment for it.

"You're famous," Odell said, with the particular pleasure coastal people take in delivering news that might get someone killed.

The headline ran across six columns: **WATER AUTHORITY BREACHES CONFIRMED — PARLIAMENT ORDERS INQUIRY**. Below it, a photograph of Aldous Renk's fortified station, taken from the air, its roof collapsed where the last storm cell of the season had done what Rosalind's gambit started. There was a smaller photograph too, grainy, of a woman with a bandaged shoulder being helped into a clinic in Saltmere. Ilsa Vane, though the caption called her *Ilsa Vane-Renholm, daughter of the delta landholders*, which was a name Rosalind had never heard her use and suspected she wore the way you wear a coat that belonged to someone dead.

And there, in the fourth paragraph, was Brann.

*Meridian Syndicate representative Doss Brann, whose body was recovered from the flooded lower levels of the station, has been described by the Syndicate as "a rogue contractor acting without authorization."*

Rosalind read that sentence four times. Rogue contractor. Acting without authorization. The man who had smiled at him across two years of payments, who had tracked them through the drowned forest with airboats and hired guns, who had died in a collapse of his own making with his ledger still clutched in a waterproof case—reduced now to a rogue. A rounding error. The Syndicate was doing what large organizations do when a limb goes septic: cutting it off and burning the evidence, then issuing statements about how much they valued integrity.

He should have felt vindicated. What he felt instead was cold, because he understood exactly what the statement meant for him. If Brann was disavowed, then every contract Brann had held was suspect. Every debt he'd collected, every salvage he'd brokered—including one certain arrangement in which a storm chaser named Eriksen had offered to sell government secrets in exchange for forgiveness—was now part of the trail the Syndicate needed erased.

His freedom, when it came, arrived dressed as paperwork.

* * *

The letter came by courier on the sixth day, delivered to the Saltmere clinic where Ilsa was recovering, because everyone in the delta now knew where to find both of them and neither of them had the sense to be elsewhere. It was heavy stock, cream-colored, with the Meridian Syndicate's seal embossed in silver—a stylized meridian line crossing a globe, elegant as a scar.

Rosalind read it standing in the clinic corridor while nurses moved around him with the practiced indifference of people who had seen too much to startle.

*Mr. Eriksen:*

*It has come to our attention that services rendered to you were contracted under the authority of Mr. D. Brann, whose recent conduct has been found to fall substantially outside the standards of this organization. As such arrangements are void without valid authorization, we regret to inform you that no obligation exists between yourself and the Meridian Syndicate.*

*No further correspondence is anticipated or required.*

*We wish you fair weather.*

There was no signature. Of course there wasn't. You don't sign your name to an erasure.

He stood there turning the page over in his hands, feeling the weight of good paper, and understood with perfect clarity what had happened. Somewhere in a tall office, a committee had run the arithmetic. The broadcast had made the operation public; the inquiry had made it radioactive; and every thread connecting the Syndicate to Renk's ledger was now a liability priced higher than any asset they'd been protecting. Voiding his debt cost them nothing. Denying Brann cost them nothing. A scandal, properly managed, was cheaper than blood—and they had always been optimists, the Syndicate, men who believed every problem could be refinanced.

They hadn't forgiven him. Nothing about that letter forgave anything. They had simply decided he no longer existed on their books, the way a storm cell stops existing once it moves offshore. Freedom bought by scandal, not payment. He owed them nothing, and they owed him nothing, and somewhere in the middle of all that nothing was a man standing in a clinic corridor holding a cream-colored envelope and finding that it weighed less than air and less than nothing.

"Read it twice?" Ilsa asked from the doorway of her room.

She was upright, which was more than the doctors had promised. Her left arm was strapped across her chest, the shoulder wound bound tight, and she'd lost weight in ways the clinic food was only beginning to reverse. But her eyes were the same—level, weatherwise, the eyes of a woman who had spent her whole life reading water.

"Three times," Rosalind said. "I keep looking for the trick."

"There isn't one. That's the trick." She came down the corridor slowly, favoring the arm. "My brother used to say the Syndicate never lies. They just tell you the truth so late it does the same damage."

He looked at her. She almost never spoke of Tam, and when she did it was in the past tense worn smooth, like river stone. Nine members of the survey team. Eight bodies recovered from the station's lower levels in the days after the broadcast, identified, named in the papers, mourned in Saltmere's church with a bell that cracked on the ninth toll. The ninth body they had never found, and Ilsa had gone down to the water every morning since to look for him, though she told no one that was what she was doing.

"The inquiry committee arrives Thursday," she said. "Parliament's people. They'll want statements."

"I know. A lawyer came by yesterday. Mine, apparently. Government-assigned." He almost laughed. "Eleven years I couldn't afford a lawyer. Now they're giving me one for free, because I'm useful. Because my testimony burns the right people."

"And does it?"

He thought about it honestly, the way she always forced him to. "Some of them. Renk's already gone—fled upriver with nothing but what he could carry, according to Odell. The inquiry wants the water authority's directors. The ones who signed the breach orders." He paused. "And the Syndicate's middlemen. Which is why they voided me. I'm not their debtor anymore. I'm their exposure."

Ilsa leaned against the wall beside him, and for a moment they watched the light move on the clinic floor, white and clean, the first uncomplicated light either of them had seen in months.

"Do you feel free?" she asked.

He considered lying. It was their oldest habit together, the two liars on the sandbar, agreeing not to ask questions. But that contract had expired somewhere in the flooding corridors of Renk's station, when he'd carried her out with her blood soaking through his shirt and she'd pressed the real case against his chest with her good arm and said, *take it, take it, don't stop*—and he hadn't stopped, and neither of them had ever mentioned the bargain they'd admitted to in the dry hold, the mutual planned betrayal, because it had drowned along with everything else.

"No," he said. "I feel like a man whose debt was cancelled by the creditor's bankruptcy. The money's gone either way. It doesn't mean I earned it back."

"That's the most honest thing you've said since I met you."

"Don't get used to it."

* * *

Thursday brought the inquiry, and the inquiry brought the delta's whole broken history upriver to meet it.

They set up in the Saltmere customs house, a building that had spent the flood season half-submerged and still bore the tideline across its walls like a bruise. Rosalind gave his statement to a panel of five—three parliament appointees, a hydrologist, and a woman with a notary's face who wrote everything down twice. He told it straight, chronologically, from the lightning that killed his sensor rig to the night in the dry hold to the broadcast. He watched their faces as he described the camp with its generators still running and meals half-eaten, and saw the moment the hydrologist understood that the survey team hadn't fled the water. The water had been sent for them.

But it was the holdouts' testimony that broke the room open. Ilsa had arranged it—of course she had, she'd spent two weeks before the inquiry quietly poling through the back channels collecting names, the families who had refused evacuation and survived the engineered floods in attics and tree houses, the ones who had lost title deeds to the water and then lost the water's paper trail to Renk's shredders. They came in their best clothes, delta people, sunburnt and formal, and they laid their documents on the table: leases signed under duress, eviction notices dated *before* the levee breaches, a land registry amendment filed the same week the first dike failed. One old man, a crab fisherman named Sosthenes Pike, put a jar of floodwater on the table and said nothing at all for ten minutes until someone asked him what it was.

"That's from my front room," he said. "Third of October. The day the north levee went. Nobody breached it natural. I watched the barge come up the channel at midnight with its lights off." He looked around the room. "You can write that down or not. Water doesn't lie. People do."

Rosalind sat in the gallery and listened to his own logic come apart in other men's mouths. That was the thing nobody warned you about ledgers—that eventually you hear your own arithmetic recited by people selling lives, and you recognize the accent. *What's it worth. What can we get for it. Who signs.* He had lived by those questions for eleven years, moving fast enough that the answers never caught him. Renk had simply stopped moving, built a fort around the questions, and hired the Syndicate to collect.

The difference, he supposed, was that Renk had slept well.

* * *

The Syndicate's erasure reached even here. On the second day of hearings, the panel's chair read aloud a formal statement received by courier: the Meridian Syndicate, *a financial services organization with no operational presence in the delta region*, wished to assist the inquiry and had provided documentation regarding the activities of one D. Brann, *an independent contractor whose actions the organization deplores*. Attached were forty pages of carefully culled records—enough to hang Brann posthumously, curated enough to leave the Syndicate's own fingerprints off every page.

"They're good," the notary woman murmured, passing Rosalind in the corridor afterward. She'd recognized him; everyone had. "Whoever cleaned those files. You could eat off them."

"That's the product," Rosalind said. "That's always been the product. They don't lend money. They lend absolution, and they charge interest."

She almost smiled. "And yours? Your account?"

"Closed," he said. "By act of scandal."

He walked out of the customs house into the first truly dry afternoon of the season. The mudflats steamed. Somewhere across the channel, a crew was pulling one of the dead trees out of the shallows with horses, the animals straining belly-deep in the muck, and the sound of their effort carried flat and clear over the water. He stood there a long time, doing the strange new arithmetic of owning nothing to anyone. No rig. No debt. No boat worth the name—the Patience was held together with resin and prayer and would float maybe one more season, generously counted. No employer, no contract, no reason to be anywhere instead of somewhere else.

For eleven years, motion had been his alibi. Keep moving, and the accounts never catch up. He tried to feel the old itch, the pull toward the next coast, the next storm, the next invoice—and found instead that his feet wanted to stay planted on this specific square meter of drying mud, and he didn't know what to do with a man who wanted to stand still.

* * *

Ilsa claimed her family's land on a Sunday, in the manner of her family, which meant on foot.

The Vane-Renholm holdings lay in the upper delta, four hundred hectares of former pasture and orchard that had spent two seasons under six feet of engineered water. The registry office in Saltmere reopened that week—its own files partially restored from duplicates the holdouts had hidden in a church attic—and Ilsa walked in with her brother's death certificate in one hand and the original deed in the other, and stood at the counter while a clerk with ink-stained fingers verified, stamped, and returned what the water had taken.

"It's not much to look at," she said afterward. They stood at the boundary marker, a leaning concrete post with the old family name cast into it, half-swallowed by silt. Beyond it the land stretched flat and raw, scoured pale, dotted with the skeletons of orchard trees. A heron worked the shallows where a fence line used to run. "Two years ago this was the best stone-fruit ground on the coast. My father planted half of it. Tam and I learned to walk between these rows."

"It'll grow again," Rosalind said. "Delta soil remembers."

She was quiet a moment. "That's what I keep telling myself. That dirt doesn't hold grudges." She picked up a handful of the pale silt, let it run through her fingers. "People do, though. I've got a list. Everyone who signed the breach orders. Everyone who bought the land cheap. I'm not going to do anything with it. I'm just going to keep it. Like a seed bank."

"That's either mercy or a very slow revenge."

"With my family it was always both."

They walked the boundary together, slow, because of her arm, marking the corners where the posts still stood. At the northeast corner, where the ground rose slightly above the flood line, she stopped and pointed at a low mound of debris washed up against an old windbreak—branches, silt, a section of roofing, and beneath it, visible only as a curve of weathered wood, the hull of a small boat.

Rosalind knew it before she said anything. He'd seen the shape of it in her face for weeks, in the way she scanned every shoreline.

"Tam's skiff," she said. "He kept it here. He was supposed to meet the team at the dock the morning they vanished. He never came." She knelt, awkward with the strapped arm, and pulled a length of tarpaulin away from the hull. The boat was crushed on one side, stove in by whatever the flood had driven it against, but the nameplate survived, the letters worn but legible: *Answered*. "He named it himself. Fifteen years old, full of his own jokes." Her voice stayed level the way a wire stays level under load. "I'm going to have her rebuilt. However long it takes. And I'm going to name a mooring after every one of the eight, and the ninth spot stays empty until they find him."

Rosalind crouched beside her. The morning was bright and cool and smelled of drying mud and salt, and somewhere inland a bird sang with the unguarded enthusiasm of a creature that had survived something.

"Ilsa," he said. "About the case. About the exchange point. You bet your life that I'd choose—"

"Don't." She said it gently, which somehow made it final. "We settled accounts, you and me. In the water, in the corridors, in the hold. Everything else is just interest, and I'm done paying interest." She stood, wincing, and looked out over her ruined inheritance with the expression of a woman taking inventory of exactly what remained. "Come inside. I'll show you where the house was. And then you can help me decide what to build."

* * *

He stayed that night in the tent she'd pitched on the high corner of the property, and near dawn he woke to the silence again—the enormous, unfamiliar quiet of the dry season—and got up and walked out onto the bare ground alone.

The east was going gray, then rose, then gold, the way dawns do when the sky finally has nothing to hide behind. Mist stood in the low channels like the ghosts of the flood, thinning as the light strengthened. Off to the south he could see the Patience at her mooring, listing slightly, patient despite her name's irony. Behind him the tent, the buried orchard, the woman sleeping inside it who owned more ruined land than anyone he'd ever known and seemed, for the first time since the sandbar, unafraid.

In his pocket was the Syndicate's letter. He took it out and read it once more in the growing light—*no obligation exists, no further correspondence anticipated, we wish you fair weather*—and then, deliberately, tore it into pieces and worked them into the mud with his boot until the cream-colored paper was indistinguishable from the earth.

Fair weather. After everything, the optimists had gotten one thing right.

He stood on ground that belonged to no one yet, owing nothing to anyone, and watched the dry season's light come up over the water, and understood that the accounts were settled—not forgiven, not forgotten, just closed, the way a book closes—and that what remained was the hardest thing he'd faced in eleven years of storms:

A whole day, with nowhere he had to run.
