## 22. First Light, Dry Ground

The rain stopped in the night the way a fever breaks—not gradually, not with any courtesy, but all at once, leaving the world sweating and stunned beneath it.

Rosalind Eriksen woke on the deck of what remained of the *Patience*, beached now on ground that had not seen sky in three years, and for a long moment he could not identify the silence. It was too complete. He had spent so many months inside the monsoon's noise—the drumming on canvas, the groan of swollen timber, the hiss of water finding every seam—that its absence registered as a kind of violence. He lay still and listened to nothing, and nothing sounded like a held breath.

Then the birds started. That was how he knew.

Delta birds did not sing in the wet season; they huddled and endured like everyone else. But now they came up out of the drowned timber in ragged spirals, herons and whistling kites and small brown birds whose names he'd never learned, and their voices layered over each other until the silence broke apart into something almost unbearable. Dawn light came off the receding water in a long copper sheet, and the mudflats steamed where the sun touched them, and Rosalind sat up under his salt-stiff blanket and felt, for the first time in longer than he could measure, that there was nowhere he needed to be.

He tested the feeling the way you test ice. Put your weight on it slowly. Wait for it to give.

It held.

* * *

The delta was coming back to life around him, and it looked less like resurrection than like something waking from anesthesia—confused, tender, furious at what had been done to it while it slept. The holdouts' camp had swelled from forty souls to nearly two hundred in the week since the broadcast. They came down the old levee road on foot and by poling skiff, carrying what they had: children wrapped in tarpaulins, goats, photographs in oilcloth, an entire church bell lashed to a raft. They came to look at water that was finally going the right direction.

Rosalind walked the mudline at first light because walking was what he knew. His boots sank to the ankle with every step, sucking at him, and he let them. The reclaimed ground stretched north toward Saltmere in a vast brown plain stitched with the skeletons of things the floods had killed—fence posts standing in open water, a windmill's tower leaning like a drunk against the sky, the roofline of a farmhouse just breaking the surface two hundred meters out, shingles dark as scales. Somewhere under all that water were fields, roads, graves. The government's engineers had called it managed inundation. The people wading out now with survey stakes and weeping faces called it something else, and would for generations.

Ilsa was already out there when he reached the stake line. Of course she was. She had been out there before dawn, before the birds, because Ilsa Vane did not wait for the world to hand her anything.

She stood hip-deep in the shallows beside her brother's name staked into the mud—DANIEL VANE, SURVEY TEAM, and below it, in someone's careful charcoal, FOUND—and she wasn't crying. Rosalind had learned that about her in the dry hold of the *Patience*, bailing water through the worst night of the monsoon: grief moved through Ilsa the way floodwater moved through a house. It rose, it filled everything, and then it drained and left the structure standing. You didn't comment on it. You handed her another bail bucket, or you stood beside her in the cold shallows, which is what he did now.

"Water's dropped another hand-span," she said. "Renk's people measured it wrong on purpose, you know. The whole operation ran on bad numbers. Deliberate ones." She turned the stake a quarter-turn, squaring it to the horizon. "They thought nobody would ever check."

"Somebody checked."

"Somebody always checks eventually. That's the only arithmetic I believe in anymore."

They stood together while the sun cleared the drowned farmhouse and laid gold across the flats. Out past the stake line, holdouts were poling into the shallows to pull debris—fence wire, a door, a child's bicycle furred with weed—and stacking it on the levee in a growing rampart that was part cleanup and part monument. Nobody had organized it. That was the thing about these people, Rosalind thought. The government had priced their lives cheaply enough to enter them in a ledger, and they had answered by rebuilding without being asked, without being paid, without anyone's permission. There was a lesson in it he was still learning to read.

"I'm going into Saltmere today," Ilsa said. "The land office reopens Monday. My family's parcel is in the second registry batch." She said it flatly, but her hand had closed around the stake hard enough to blanch her knuckles. "Three years of paperwork saying this is crown water. One document saying otherwise. We'll see which one holds."

"It'll hold," Rosalind said. "You made sure of that."

"We made sure of that." She looked at him then, and the morning light caught the scar along her jaw where Brann's airboat propeller wash had thrown a shard of deadfall—three weeks ago, or a lifetime. "Don't do that thing you do. Where you fold yourself into the background of your own story."

"I don't do a thing."

"You absolutely do a thing." She let go of the stake and waded toward shore, water peeling off her in sheets. "You spent four chapters of this disaster insisting you were just the boat driver. I've read the ledger, Rosalind. You're in it. On the good page, for once."

* * *

The *Patience* was dying, and they both knew it, and neither of them said it, which was its own kind of eulogy.

He went to her after breakfast—hardtack and smoked fish and strong tea from the holdouts' communal fire, the best meal he'd eaten in a year, which told him more about the year than he wanted to know—and knelt in the mud beside her hull and put his palm flat against the planking the way you'd check a fevered child. The rot had won. The storm-chasing gambit through the last cell of the season had gotten them through Renk's outer boom and cost the boat everything she had left; the starboard strakes were soft as bread, the keelson cracked clean through amidships, and the bilge smelled of rot and river and the particular sweetness of wood giving up. She had been a bad boat when he bought her, a worse boat by the drowned forest, and somewhere in the last hundred kilometers she had become the truest friend he'd ever had, which said something about his friendships that he chose not to examine.

"You got us here," he told her. "That's more than most."

A holdout boy named Tam, maybe eleven, missing two fingers to a snare line, had been circling him for an hour pretending to fish. Now he drifted close enough to speak. "She's done, then?"

"She's done."

"My uncle wants the planks. For the new levee gate." The boy shifted, embarrassed by the transaction. "He'll trade you. We've got a skiff coming up from the south camps. Not pretty. But sound."

Rosalind looked at the *Patience* a long moment. Trade her bones for a working hull. Let her become part of a gate that kept the water off living people instead of under their floors. It was, he thought, a better ending than boats usually got. Most of them rotted where they died, unmarked, unmourned, their names fading off transoms until even the owners couldn't have sworn they'd existed.

"Tell your uncle it's a deal," he said. "And tell him the name goes on whatever he builds. Patience. Carved deep. So the water knows who's holding it back."

The boy grinned and ran, and Rosalind stayed kneeling in the mud a moment longer, hand on the hull, and let himself feel the full weight of what the season had taken. The rig, first—thirty thousand credits of sensors and telemetry, vaporized by a single bolt of lightning on a contract gone wrong, the debt that started everything. Then the money, such as it was. Then the illusion that the Meridian Syndicate was merely a lender and not a broker of other people's ruin. Then Brann, though Brann had paid for himself in the collapse of Renk's flooding corridors, buried under the weight of his own optimism. Then the boat. Then, piece by piece, every comfortable lie Rosalind had built his moving life on: that debts were just numbers, that stopping meant drowning, that a man could outrun what he owed if he simply never stood still long enough for it to find him.

The investigations had found him anyway. Standing still, it turned out, was the only way anything found you—justice included.

* * *

The Syndicate's letter arrived two days later, carried by a courier who would not come inside the camp and would not meet anyone's eyes. Rosalind read it standing in the holdouts' common tent while Ilsa watched from across the fire, arms crossed, ready to catch him if the words knocked him down.

*RE: ACCOUNT OF R. ERIKSEN.*

*In light of recent disclosures concerning the conduct of certain former associates, the Meridian Syndicate wishes to formally disassociate itself from all activities undertaken in the Saltmere Delta corridor during the current monsoon cycle. The individual known as Brann acted without authorization. All instruments bearing his signature are void.*

*Accordingly, the outstanding balance on the above-referenced account—principal, penalty, and collection fees—is hereby discharged in full. No further obligation exists between the parties.*

*The Syndicate thanks you for your discretion and wishes you fair weather.*

They had erased him to erase themselves. His debt hadn't been forgiven; it had been disappeared, like the survey team, like the flooded villages, like every inconvenient entry in every ledger the powerful kept. He should have felt cheated. Instead he folded the letter once, twice, and fed it to the cookfire, and watched the paper curl and blacken, and felt the strangest sensation in his chest, which took him several seconds to identify as laughter.

"They voided you," Ilsa said, coming to stand at his shoulder. "Like a bad check."

"Like a bad check."

"How does it feel? Owning nothing?"

Rosalind considered the question honestly, because she had earned honesty from him in a leaking hull at gunpoint of rising water, and honesty was the one currency he had left in quantity. He owned the clothes he stood in. A broken compass. Half a blanket. A name that, as of last week, appeared in government inquiry filings as a witness rather than a suspect, which might prove worth more than everything the floods had taken from him, or might prove worth nothing, depending on how far the reckoning reached before the powerful closed ranks.

"I spent six years running because I thought stopping meant settling accounts," he said. "And now the accounts are settled, and I'm standing still, and—" He stopped. Started again. "Do you know what I keep thinking about? The night in the dry hold. When we traded truths. You told me the names of your crew, all nine, and I couldn't remember the last time I'd said my own name out loud to anyone who wasn't collecting something from me."

"And now?"

"Now everybody knows it. Transmitter logs, inquiry records, the broadsheets in Saltmere are calling us 'the delta witnesses.'" He shook his head. "I chased storms for hire for six years and I was nobody. I stand still for three weeks and I'm somebody whether I like it or not."

"Do you like it?"

"That's the strange part." He looked out over the flats, where the water kept withdrawing like a tide that had finally been told the truth. "Yeah. I think I do."

* * *

On the morning the dry season truly began—the morning the papers would later mark, though no one knew it yet—Ilsa came to him at the levee with mud on her boots and a document tube under her arm and an expression he had never seen on her before, which took him a moment to recognize as peace.

"Registry confirmed," she said. "Parcel fourteen, Vane family grant, original survey 1889. The water authority's claim is annulled pending criminal proceedings." She held out the tube. "First deed issued on reclaimed delta land. Mine. Ours, if Daniel—" Her voice did the thing it did, rose and filled and drained, and the structure held. "Mine," she finished. "And there's a second line on the grant application. Co-signatory. For services rendered to the recovery."

Rosalind stared at the tube. "Ilsa."

"Don't make it strange. It's practical. Somebody has to run weather monitoring for the replanting, and you're the only fool on this coast who reads a barometer like scripture, and frankly the holdouts will riot if you leave—they've decided you're lucky, which is the stupidest thing I've ever heard, since luck is the one thing you've never had." She pushed the tube into his hands. "There's a ridge above the flood line. Good drainage. Room for a sensor mast and a house, if you ever built one, which I know sounds like a death sentence to you, so start small. A shed. A shed with a mast on it."

He looked at the deed. At her. At the drowned farmhouse breaking the surface in the distance, and the bell on its raft coming ashore, and the children running the mudflats screaming with the pure joy of people who had forgotten, briefly, catastrophically, that the world could take things from them.

Six years of moving. Six years of contracts and collectors and the sweet chemical relief of never staying anywhere long enough to owe anyone anything but money. And here was a woman who had betrayed her own crew to survive and then spent a monsoon paying it back at compound interest, offering him a piece of ground, asking nothing except that he stop.

"The shed," he said. "With a mast. And when the storms come next season—"

"When the storms come, we chase them," Ilsa said. "For free. For the warning it gives these people. And when they pass—" She smiled, and it was the first smile he'd seen from her that wasn't armor. "When they pass, we come home."

Home. The word landed in him like the first drop after a long drought—heavy, startling, soaking straight through.

They walked the levee together as the sun climbed, two people with nothing, on ground that was becoming something, and behind them the *Patience*'s planks waited for the carpenter's saw, and ahead of them the delta lay open under a sky scrubbed clean of cloud, blue from horizon to horizon, the first blue in a hundred days, and Rosalind Eriksen stood still in the middle of it—truly still, weight on both feet, no road pulling, no ledger chasing—and found that the ground held.

It held.
