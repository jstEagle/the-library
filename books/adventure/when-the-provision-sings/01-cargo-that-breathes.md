## 1. Cargo That Breathes

The fog came off the river at dawn the way it always did in the low country — not rolling, but rising, as if the water itself were exhaling after a long night of holding its breath. Oscar Lockridge had flown through it enough times to stop calling it dangerous and start calling it Tuesday. He knew its moods. He knew when it would thin by mid-morning and when it would sit on the channel like a lid on a pot, and he knew that the settlements upriver didn't care which it was, because fever didn't wait for visibility.

So he flew anyway.

The *Kestrel* was a twenty-year-old bush plane with a fabric skin patched so many times the port wing looked like a quilt, and Oscar loved her with the uncomplicated devotion of a man who had nothing else to love. She carried four crates of sulfonamide powder, a case of quinine ampoules, a bolt of gauze, and a sealed letter from a doctor in Corvane who wrote prescriptions like other men wrote sermons. Routine. The word sat comfortably in his chest. Routine was the whole architecture of his life: fly the river, drop the medicine, take payment in fish or fuel or silence, move on before anyone learned his face well enough to attach a name to it.

Seven years of routine. Seven years since the loading dock in Harrowgate, since the crane cable that snapped, since Tomas Okafor's skull against the concrete and the sound it made — a sound Oscar still heard sometimes in the space between engine noise and engine noise, a wet percussion his mind refused to file away. Manslaughter, they'd called it. Negligence. The charge had a name; the grief did not. He'd run because staying meant trial, and trial meant standing in front of strangers while they decided what he already knew: that a man was dead and Oscar's hurry had killed him.

He checked his watch against the river bend below. Eleven minutes to Kettle Landing if the fog held its current shape. It didn't. Fog never did.

It thickened instead, sudden and total, the way fog does when warm water meets cold air over a flooded plain, and the world outside the *Kestrel*'s windshield went from gray to white to nothing at all. Oscar's hands moved without consulting him — throttle back, nose down a degree, altimeter watched like a rival. This was the competence people paid him for and never thanked him for: the ability to be lost and calm about it simultaneously.

"Easy," he told the plane, or himself, or the fog. "Easy."

The river was somewhere below him, wide as a lake this season, swollen three weeks past its banks by the spring melt. Every year the flood remade the map — drowned fields, swallowed fences, turned orchards into forests of drowning trees — and every year it gave some of it back, rearranged, unrecognizable. Pilots who flew the interior learned to distrust their own memory of the ground. The river kept no promises.

Which is why, when the sandbar rose out of the white beneath him, Oscar had exactly one and a half seconds to understand that it should not exist.

His memory said open water. His eyes said land. The two facts met somewhere around his sternum and produced a decision older than thought: pull up or die. He hauled back on the stick. The *Kestrel* climbed, stalled, wallowed — and the fog, treacherous ally, chose that moment to shear away below five hundred feet, revealing the bar in full morning light: a long pale crescent of fresh silt, maybe four hundred yards of it, gleaming like a bone laid on dark water. New land. Land that hadn't been there last season, birthed by the flood from sediment the river had carried down from country no one would ever see.

There was room to land or there wasn't. There was no third option, because the engine coughed — once, twice, a dry mechanical hiccup that sounded obscenely like laughter — and then caught again, but the message had been received. Fuel starvation, probably, or water in the line, or the carburetor ice that fog loved to gift him. Whatever it was, the *Kestrel* had voted.

Oscar set her down the way he did everything: competently, alone, and slightly angry about it.

The wheels touched silt still soft as bread dough. The plane plowed a furrow, slewed left, and stopped with her tail high and her propeller bent into a shape like a question mark. Silence rushed in — the enormous, ringing silence of a place that had never heard an engine. Somewhere out on the floodwater, a bird cried once and thought better of it.

Oscar sat for a moment, hands still on the wheel, doing the arithmetic of his situation. Engine: suspect. Location: nowhere. Cargo: perishable. Schedule: ruined. Reputation: intact, because there was no one here to have one in front of. That last item mattered more than it should have, and he knew it, and he filed the knowledge where he filed all uncomfortable knowledge — deep, unexamined, load-bearing.

He climbed out into air that smelled of raw earth, that particular smell of ground seeing sun for the first time, mineral and ancient. The silt took his boots to the ankle. Around the bar, the river ran brown and muscular, carrying whole trees past him like commuters late for something. The flood was going down now — you could read it in the wrack lines, the tidemarks of debris draped across the new land like shed clothing. Grass mats. A dead catfish the size of his forearm. A fence post with three feet of wire still attached, torn from some farm that existed only downstream now, in memory.

And then, forty yards from the *Kestrel*'s bent nose, a corner of wood.

Oscar saw it the way you see anything anomalous: first as wrongness, then as object. Dark wood, squared, deliberate — not driftwood, not wreckage-scatter, but geometry. Man-made geometry, half-buried in silt that had been underwater until roughly yesterday.

He should have walked away. He understood that even then, even as his boots carried him toward it. Seven years of practice had taught him the grammar of trouble, and the first rule was simple: found things belong to the river. Salvage rights were a tangle of law he wanted no part of; salvage fleets were a species of vulture he liked even less. Anything old and valuable on this river came wrapped in claims, and claims came wrapped in men with guns and lawyers, and Oscar Lockridge's entire survival strategy consisted of being a man to whom nothing interesting ever happened.

But his boots kept walking. Curiosity is just restlessness pointed at a target, and Oscar had been restless for seven years without a target, and here one was, sticking out of the mud like an invitation.

It was a chest. Iron-banded, oak, the size of a sea chest, crusted with river mud and colonies of something white and dead — freshwater barnacles, maybe, or bryozoa, life that had colonized it during its long submersion and been stranded by the falling water. One strap had rotted; the other held. The lock was a disc of corroded brass, green as pond scum.

And stamped into the lid, repeated along every band, worn but unmistakable, was a seal: a circle bisected by a wavy line, with a star above the wave.

Oscar knew the seal. Everyone on the river knew the seal, the way everyone knows the face on a coin. Meridia. The drowned nation-state, the country that had existed for ninety years on the delta islands and then, in a single catastrophic season of engineering failures and denial, gone under — government, treasury, archives, and eleven thousand people, most of whom had gotten out and all of whom had lost everything. The Meridian Treasury, they called it now, in salvage bars and auction houses: the national reserve that went down with the state steamship *Meridian* on its final evacuation run, gold bullion and currency plates and whatever else a dying nation puts in its pockets. Never found. Forty years of sonar sweeps, fortune hunters, and drowned divers, and never found.

Until, apparently, the river got bored of keeping secrets.

Oscar stood over the chest with the water talking around him and felt the specific cold that has nothing to do with temperature. Because here was the thing he understood immediately, the way you understand a floor is rotten the instant your foot goes through: this was not cargo. Cargo could be dropped off. Cargo could be forgotten. Cargo had a consignee and a signature and then it was someone else's problem, and forgetting was the entire economy of Oscar's life.

This was not forgettable. This was the opposite of forgettable. This was the kind of thing that made names matter, that dragged pilots out of anonymity by the collar, that put faces on posters and questions in mouths. If he opened it, he would know. And if he knew, then he was no longer a man to whom nothing interesting happened, and the whole careful architecture of his seven quiet years would come down around his ears like the *Kestrel*'s bent prop.

He told himself to leave it. He told himself to dig the plane out, jury-rig the engine, and be in Kettle Landing by dark with his quinine and his silence, and let the river keep its secret until some salvage fleet dredged it up fair and square.

Then he knelt in the mud, and worked the corroded brass disc with his knife, and hated himself in a distant, academic way, the way you hate weather.

The lock surrendered with a crunch of green corrosion. The lid came up heavy, hinged, exhaling a breath of trapped river-smell — black silt and old iron and something faintly sweet, like paper rotting in the dark.

Inside, packed in wool gone gray with age, lay rows of lead-sealed document cases. Not gold. Not currency plates. Cases — dozens of them — each stamped with the same circle-and-wave-and-star, each tagged in a formal copperplate script in a language Oscar half-recognized from old banknotes. And on top of them all, one loose envelope, its wax seal broken long ago, containing a single sheet that the river had spared.

Oscar lifted it. The ink had feathered but held. It was a manifest page — he knew manifests, had lived by them, had killed a man over a rushed one — and it listed, in that same copperplate, the contents of the chest. Not bullion. Not plate. The words ran down the page in neat columns, and though he couldn't read the language, he could read the structure, and the structure said: records. Ledgers. Registers. Names, quantities, dates. An accounting.

A nation's paperwork, sunk with the nation. Or — and the thought arrived cold and complete, uninvited — a nation's evidence, hidden under the cover story of a treasury, because whoever loaded the *Meridian* had known that gold gets hunted and paper gets forgotten.

The fog was burning off. Out on the floodwater, far downstream, a shape was moving against the current — a boat, then another, small at this distance, running a search pattern. Deliberate. Grid-like. Looking for something.

Oscar looked at the chest, at the manifest in his hand, at the bent propeller that had put him on this sandbar out of all the sandbars in the world, and felt the restless thing inside him go very still, the way animals go still when they scent something larger than themselves.

Some cargo can't be dropped off and forgotten. He'd known it thirty seconds after kneeling in the mud. Now, folding the manifest page into his jacket, glancing once more at the boats working their way upstream, he admitted it aloud, to the river, to the new land, to no one:

"Well," he said. "That's done it."

He started digging out the *Kestrel*. Behind him, the chest sat open in the sun like a mouth, and the river sang on, indifferent, carrying its trees to the sea.
