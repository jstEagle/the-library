## 2. The Six-Tide Clock

Kettle Landing smelled of diesel, wet rope, and the particular sweetness of overripe bananas nobody could afford to throw away. Oscar came in low over the oxbow with his lights off, because coming in with lights off was a habit seven years deep, and set the plane down on the brown water with the gentleness of a man setting down something breakable — which, in a manner of speaking, he was. The chest rode in the cargo bay behind the second seat, lashed down with the same care he gave insulin crates, and it had not stopped weighing on him since the sandbar. Not its weight. Its weight was nothing. Forty pounds of salt-eaten oak and brass, light as luggage. It was the *knowing* that weighed.

A nation's seal does not belong to a man. That was the thought that had flown with him all sixty miles downriver, sitting in the passenger seat of his own skull like a paying customer he couldn't put out.

He tied off at the community dock, where three boys helped him moor for the price of a story about the fog, and he gave them one — edited, the sandbar and the chest sanded clean out of it — and walked up the boardwalk to Ferro's trading post to settle his fuel account and buy a tin of coffee that had gone stale in a way he'd come to find comforting.

Ferro's was the kind of store that sold everything and stocked nothing twice. Mosquito coils, outboard pistons, hymnals, shotgun shells, a freezer case humming with the effort of keeping ice cream in a climate that despised it. Oscar had been coming here for six years, long enough that Ferro trusted him with credit and short enough that Ferro still asked no questions, and Oscar had always understood that this arrangement — trust without questions — was the most expensive thing he owned. He paid for it by being forgettable. He paid for it by never staying past dark.

He was counting out bills for the coffee when the radio behind the counter, tuned as always to the settlements' shared band, said his name.

Not his name. His *description*.

*"—again, any station, the pilot who came off the north channel this morning, floatplane, dark green, scar on the left cowling. If you have seen him or his aircraft, the salvage fleet is offering fuel credit at any of their depots for reliable information. Repeat—"*

Oscar's hands did not stop counting. That was the discipline, the old discipline, the one he'd learned in a courtroom corridor with his heart going like a trapped bird: *your hands keep moving while the rest of you decides how to run.* He finished the count. He slid the bills across. Ferro took them, and Ferro's eyes — quick, brown, older than the rest of him — went from the bills to Oscar's face and back, and Ferro said nothing, which was its own kind of message.

"Rough morning on the channel?" Ferro asked, casual as a man asking about weather, which was exactly what he was asking about.

"Fog," Oscar said. "Sat down on a bar that wasn't on any chart I own."

"Mm." Ferro rang the sale. "River's been giving things back all season. High water's dropping. My grandfather used to say the river's a lender, not a giver. Everything it takes, it means to collect on."

Oscar took his coffee and his change and got as far as the door before the second voice came through the radio, and this one stopped him with his hand on the latch, because he knew it. Seven years, and he knew it the way you know a scar — not by looking, but by the way weather makes it ache.

*"All stations, all stations, this is the survey vessel Coriolis, Captain Vane commanding. We are conducting salvage operations in the north channel reaches. Vessels in the area are advised that the Meridian wreck site falls under fleet charter and any materials recovered from it are fleet property under salvage law. We will be at Kettle Landing by tomorrow's tide. Captain Vane out."*

Ilsa.

The name went through him like cold water through a wetsuit. Not Ilsa Vane the radio voice — Ilsa Vane the woman who had taught him to read currents off the surface of moving water, who had a laugh that started low and broke upward, who had stood on the dock at Corvair Bend seven years ago with his manslaughter charge hanging over him like a storm front and said, *run then, if that's what you're good at,* and he had run, because it was, because running was the one skill he'd ever perfected, and he had never once in seven years managed to stop hearing the way her voice had cracked on *good at*.

She was coming here. Tomorrow's tide. Hunting the chest that was at this moment forty feet behind him in a plane any child on this dock could describe.

Oscar walked back up the boardwalk with the unhurried gait of a man with nowhere to be, which was the hardest acting he'd ever done, and went into Ferro's back room, where Ferro kept a table, a lamp, and the settlements' only honest map — hand-drawn, corrected in pencil, annotated in three generations of handwriting with sandbars that had come and gone and come again.

"Ferro," he said. "The Meridian. What do you know?"

Ferro poured two glasses of cane spirit without being asked, which meant the answer was long. "I know what everyone knows, which by tonight will be more than everyone knows. The Meridian went down in the closing year of the war. Officially she was a mail packet. Unofficially—" he turned his glass, "—she was the last ship out of Meridia before the state dissolved, and she carried the treasury. Gold, bonds, the paper wealth of a country that had stopped existing. Forty years on the bottom, and every salvage man from here to the coast has dreamed about her. The channel she sank in was too deep, too fast, too silty. Unfindable. Until this flood."

"Until the flood."

"River gave back a sandbar where no sandbar was. River showed somebody something." Ferro looked at him steadily. "Radio says somebody did."

"Ferro—"

"Don't." The old man held up a hand. "I don't want to know. Knowing is a debt too. But I'll tell you what the map says, because the map doesn't care who's listening." He smoothed the pencil-annotated chart flat under the lamp. "The wreck lies in the Narrows, here, under what we call the closing shoals. Six months of the year there's forty feet of water over her. But when the flood drops — and it's dropping now, you can smell it — the shoals build. Sand moves down from the upper bars and packs into the channel like a cork in a bottle. Six tides, Oscar. Six tides after the flood crest passes the Narrows marker, and the channel closes to anything bigger than a canoe. Then she's sealed for another season. Maybe another forty years."

"Six tides." Oscar did the arithmetic without wanting to. Tides ran roughly twice a day on this stretch, uneven, the river fighting the sea about the schedule. "That's three days."

"Three days and change. And every salvage man, every heir, every government remnant, and every thief on this river has just heard the same clock start." Ferro knocked back his spirit. "You should hear the whole broadcast band tonight. It's going to be a choir."

It was. Oscar spent the evening at the dock listening to the settlements' radio traffic, and the choir sang exactly as predicted. The fleet, formal and cold, claiming the site under charter. A second voice, smooth and educated, claiming it under inheritance — a man identifying himself as counsel for the Reyes claim, the last surviving line of the Meridian treasury's trustees, demanding all recovered materials be held for adjudication. And a third voice, no name attached, a flat river accent offering — not asking, *offering* — to buy whatever the pilot of the dark green floatplane had found, price negotiable, no questions, and the tone of that offer had a knife in it the way a smile can have a knife.

Three parties. Three clocks ticking on the same three days. And Oscar sitting on a mooring line in the dark with forty pounds of drowned nation in his hold, understanding with total clarity that the sandbar had not been an accident of navigation. It had been a delivery. The river had handed him the one thing on earth that could not be dropped off and forgotten, and now every current, every tide, every radio wave was pulling toward him like water toward a drain.

He thought about running. God, he thought about running — the way other men think about drink. Fuel up at first light, swing south, a week of back channels and he'd be in territory where the fleet's credit meant nothing and the Reyes claim meant less. He had done it before. He was, as a certain woman had once said with terrible accuracy, good at it.

But the chest was the problem. The chest was always going to be the problem. Run with it, and he was a thief fleeing with a nation's inheritance, which was a shorter word for what he'd already been running from for seven years. Leave it, and he handed it to whichever wolf arrived first. Sink it, and the river kept it, and the river was a lender, not a giver, and Ferro's grandfather had never said what happened to the man who borrowed from it.

And under all of it, quiet as silt, was the other thing. *Tomorrow's tide.* She was coming. Seven years of careful forgettability, of being no one on any manifest, and the river had arranged it so that the one person whose questions he could not survive was sailing straight at him with a salvage fleet behind her.

Oscar sat on the dock until the moon came up over the Narrows, and the water below him breathed in and out with the tide, in and out, six more breaths and the channel would close.

He was still sitting there when the boy from the fuel shed came running down the boards, barefoot, out of breath, not from running — from the news.

"Pilot," the boy said. "There's a boat come up from the south, running dark. They're asking at every mooring for you. Not the plane. *You.* By name." The boy's eyes were wide with the pleasure of being the messenger. "They said to tell you — they said, *tell him Vane knows he's here.*"

* * *

Oscar did not sleep. He spent the night doing what he did instead of sleeping, which was working — checking the tie-downs, draining the fuel sump, running his hands over the cowling scar like a man touching a wound to be sure it was his own. The chest sat in the bay, patient as a passenger. Somewhere south of the oxbow, a boat was running dark toward him, and somewhere downriver a fleet was weighing anchor, and somewhere in the settlements a smooth-voiced counsel was drafting claims on paper that had never once stopped a man with a gunboat.

He pushed off at gray light, before the tide turned, and took the plane out onto the channel with the Narrows dead ahead and the water already running lower than yesterday, the sandbars showing their backs like a pod of surfacing whales. Three days. Six tides. He had fuel for two hundred miles, a chest that could buy a country or hang him, and a name that three separate powers now knew.

The trap of being known, he thought, easing the throttle forward as the current took the hull, is that you stop being able to choose your direction. Everyone who knows your name becomes a hand on your tiller.

The Narrows opened ahead of him, brown and glittering and full of things the river was giving back.

And on the water below, riding the last of the flood tide into the channel, came a white-hulled survey ship with a crane on her deck, flying no colors at all — and standing at her rail, a figure with binoculars raised, already looking at him.
