## 3. A Passenger Named Heir

Ilsa Vane had learned long ago that the most dangerous negotiations happened over coffee, not gun barrels. A gun gave a man something to be brave against. A cup of coffee, poured by your own hand, in your own galley, while the woman across from you smiled like she'd already read the last page of your life — that was where men folded.

So she poured the coffee.

Oscar Lockridge sat across the chart table from her with his hands wrapped around the mug like it was the only warm thing in the world, and she hated how well she remembered those hands. Seven years had weathered them, scarred the knuckles, stained the fingers with engine grease that no soap would ever fully surrender, but they were still the hands that had taught her to tie a bowline one-handed in the dark, laughing, the night before everything went wrong.

"You didn't have to send four men," he said.

"I sent four men so I wouldn't have to send twelve." She sat, mirroring him, forearms on the table, the way she used to when they'd argue about charts. "You've gotten famous, Oscar. The man who found the Meridian. Every dock from here to the headwaters is selling your name along with the diesel."

"I didn't find anything. I crash-landed."

"On a sandbar that didn't exist last spring, on top of a wreck everyone else has spent thirty years and a hundred lives looking for." She let the silence do some work. He'd never been able to sit inside her silences; he always filled them, and he always filled them with the truth, because lying to her had been the one skill he never managed. "The chest is in your plane."

"It's cargo."

"It's a nation."

His jaw tightened. Outside the galley porthole, the lead ship of the Vane salvage fleet — the *Providence*, because her father had believed in irony — rocked gently at its mooring in Kettle Landing, and beyond it the river ran brown and swollen with the flood's leavings. Somewhere under all that water lay the Meridian, and somewhere in Oscar's floatplane, under a tarp and a crate of penicillin he was supposed to be delivering to a settlement that had probably already heard the rumors, sat a chest stamped with the seal of a country that had drowned with its secrets aboard.

"I was going to open it," Oscar said finally. "Or maybe burn it. I hadn't decided."

"That's the most honest thing you've said in seven years."

"That's a low bar. You set it."

She almost smiled. God help her, she almost smiled, and she covered it by standing and taking his mug away, which was a kind of intimacy that hurt worse than touching him would have. "Here's how this goes," she said. "You're going to fly me and a passenger upriver to the shallows above the wreck. I'm paying charter rates. Generous ones. You'll be compensated for the medicine run you're abandoning, and the settlement will be compensated too, because I do my arithmetic clean."

"And if I say no?"

"Then my men will be less polite than I've been, and you'll still fly us, and you'll hate me more at the end of it. I'd rather you hated me less." She turned the mug in her hands. "I'm asking, Oscar. That's the part you should notice. I'm asking."

He looked at her for a long moment, and she watched him do the arithmetic she knew he'd do — the exits, the fuel, the odds of outrunning a fleet with a seaplane — and she watched him arrive, as she'd known he would, at the only answer that left his conscience intact. That was the thing about Oscar Lockridge. He was a coward about staying and a hero about everything else, and it had taken her years to understand those were the same wound.

"Who's the passenger?" he asked.

* * *

Tomas Reyes came up the boarding ladder like a man boarding a church.

He was younger than Ilsa had expected — thirty at most — with the careful, ink-stained hands of someone who handled old paper for a living, and he carried a leather document case the way priests carry relics: close to the body, both arms, eyes down. He shook Oscar's hand on the float, and Ilsa saw Oscar register the grip, the posture, the case, and file all of it under *problems for later*.

"Señor Lockridge. I've read your flight logs. Well — the ones that are public."

"Those are the boring ones."

"Those are the ones that prove you know every bend of this river better than any pilot alive." Tomas settled into the passenger seat and buckled the document case across his lap like a seatbelt. "I'd like to tell you what we're flying toward before we take off. Captain Vane agreed to let me. She's more generous than her reputation."

"My reputation is the accurate one," Ilsa said from the cockpit, and started the engine to end the conversation.

The Meridian rose off the water at Kettle Landing with the particular grace of an old bush plane — a wallowing, complaining grace, all of it Oscar's doing, his hands and his ears and his thirty small corrections a minute. Ilsa took the second seat and said nothing and watched him fly, because watching Oscar fly was the closest thing to reading his heart that the world still allowed. Seven years ago he'd flown like a man showing off. Now he flew like a man apologizing — to the river, to the weather, to the machine, to everyone aboard.

Tomas leaned forward between the seats and raised his voice over the engine.

"The Meridian was the treasury ship of Meridia," he said. "You know the story. The nation-state on the delta that the river took in the great flood — thirty-one years ago, in a night. The official account says the Meridian was evacuating the state treasury when she went down. Gold reserves, bonds, the national patrimony. Every salvage fleet on this river has been hunting that gold since."

"I've heard the story," Oscar said.

"You've heard the story the victors wrote. The flood didn't just take the country, señor. It took the evidence." Tomas's voice had the flat, practiced calm of a man who had said this many times to people who didn't believe him. "The Meridian wasn't fleeing with treasure. She was fleeing with *records*. The treasury was the cover story — the gold was ballast, a rumor seeded on purpose so that anyone who found the wreck would go digging for bullion and stop digging for truth. The real cargo was the state archive. Ledgers. Orders. The names of the men who sold Meridia's borderlands, who emptied the granaries the winter the flood came, who let the dikes fail because the failing dikes were worth more to them than the towns behind them."

The river unspooled beneath them, brown and patient. Ilsa watched Oscar's eyes flick to the mirror, to Tomas, back to the water.

"And you know this how?" Oscar asked.

"Because I was the archivist's apprentice, and then I was the archivist, and now I am the archive." Tomas patted the document case. "The last living claimant to the Meridian fortune is a fiction I maintain, because a fiction can charter planes and hire ships and be taken seriously. The truth is simpler. I'm the heir to a country's guilt, and I want it delivered."

"Delivered to who? Meridia's underwater."

"To the *people*, señor. The settlements along this river are Meridia's survivors and Meridia's neighbors and Meridia's debtors, all mixed together, and every one of them has been paying interest on lies for thirty years. The chest you found — the one with the seal — if it holds what I believe it holds, then it doesn't belong to the woman with the fleet, or to you with the fast plane, or even to me with the papers. It belongs to the river towns. To the descendants of everyone whose names are in those ledgers." He said it without heat, which somehow made it worse, the way a level voice always makes an accusation heavier. "Finders keepers is the law of men with planes. It is not the law of the river."

Ilsa felt Oscar look at her then, in the mirror, a long look, and she let him look. She hadn't told him why she wanted the wreck. She'd told him she wanted the salvage rights, the gold, the prize — the story the whole fleet believed, the story her own officers believed, because a captain who hunts for her father's good name is a captain whose crew starts asking what else her father was.

Let Tomas spend the truth like small change, she thought. She'd spend hers when the water was deep enough.

* * *

They put down at dusk on a wide reach above the shallows, where the floodwater had spread into a brown mirror and the drowned country's ghost lay somewhere beneath it, and Ilsa cooked because cooking was the only command she still gave with her hands and meant kindly. Rice and smoked fish and the good coffee, the kind she saved for negotiations, and the three of them ate in the plane's cabin by lamplight while rain started somewhere upriver and walked toward them through the dark.

"Six tides," Tomas said, spreading a hand-drawn chart on the chart table. "The shallows above the wreck are closing. The flood scoured the channel open, but the river is already laying it shut — sand and silt, tide by tide. Two more tides and a rowboat can cross. Four more and a man can walk. After the sixth, the Meridian is sealed for another generation, maybe forever. Whatever we take, we take inside six tides."

"Which tide are we on?" Oscar asked.

"The first closed an hour ago."

Oscar looked at the chart, then at Ilsa, and something moved behind his eyes that she recognized and dreaded — the flight instinct, the old arithmetic of exits, the man measuring the distance between himself and the horizon. She'd spent seven years learning to hate that look. She'd spent the same seven years learning that she'd never once stopped watching for it.

"Say what you're thinking," she said.

"I'm thinking I had a plan," Oscar said. "Seven years of a plan. Keep moving, keep the cargo boring, keep my name small. One chest, and the whole plan drowns." He turned the lamp down a notch, and the shadows took his face, and when he spoke again his voice was quieter, aimed at the chart rather than at her. "I'm thinking I should have burned it."

"But you didn't," Ilsa said.

"No. I didn't."

"Why not?"

He was silent so long that the rain arrived, drumming on the floats, filling the cabin with the sound of the river counting. When he finally answered, he didn't look at her, and that was how she knew it was true.

"Because the chest was stamped with a seal," he said, "and I've spent seven years running from a verdict, and I know what it is to be a thing that someone else gets to open or leave shut." His eyes came up and found hers across the lamplight. "I couldn't leave it down there. Not again."

Ilsa held his gaze and did not let herself reach across the table, because her hands had work to do tomorrow and her heart had already done enough.

Outside, the river rose a finger's width against the floats, patient as a creditor. Five tides left. And somewhere upriver, in the dark, word was already traveling faster than any plane — because on this river, rumor was the only thing that never needed refueling.
