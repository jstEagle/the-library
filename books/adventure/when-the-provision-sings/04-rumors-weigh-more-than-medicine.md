## 4. Rumors Weigh More Than Medicine

The fog had burned off by the time Oscar crossed the first bend above Kettle Landing, and the river lay open below him like a road someone had spilled. He flew low, the way he always did on the medicine run — low enough to read the water, to see where the current braided white over a snag, to catch the smell of woodsmoke drifting up through the open window and mixing with the oil-and-canvas breath of his own machine. Seven years of this route had taught him that the river was honest in a way people weren't. It showed you exactly what it intended to do.

He was thinking about the chest lashed under his cargo netting, wrapped in tarpaulin like any other freight, when he saw the boy on the bank at Ferrow's Bend pointing up at him before he'd even cut his engine for the landing.

That was wrong. Nobody pointed at Oscar Lockridge's plane. They waved, sometimes, if they wanted something carried. Mostly they just watched it come and go the way they watched weather — a fact of the landscape, unremarkable. But the boy was pointing, and shouting, and by the time Oscar brought the plane down on the mud flat and killed the prop, there were six people waiting at the waterline instead of the usual one, and none of them were holding medicine lists.

"Lockridge." Old Marta Ferrow pushed through them first, her hands still floury from the morning's baking, her eyes doing something he'd never seen them do — measuring him. Weighing him against something she'd already decided. "You found it."

"I found a sandbar," Oscar said. "I found fog. I hauled my usual freight."

"You found the Meridian chest." She said it flatly, without triumph, and that was somehow worse than if she'd crowed it. "Radio at Kettle Landing picked up the flood report two days ago. New shallows off the Serpent's Tooth. Then last night a trapper came downriver saying a pilot dragged something iron-stamped off the bar and wouldn't say what. There's only one pilot who won't say what."

Oscar kept his face still. Seven years of practice. "Rumors weigh more than medicine, Marta. You want your penicillin or not?"

"I want to know who else knows."

And there it was — the question underneath every question on the river, the one that had kept him alive this long: *who else knows*. He could lie to her. He had lied to better liars than Marta Ferrow. But she'd baked bread for him through two winters, and her granddaughter had sewn the patch on his left wing after the gull strike, and lying to her felt like pissing in the well.

"Some people know," he said.

"Vane?"

The name went through him like a fishhook. "Why would you say Vane?"

"Because everyone's saying Vane." Marta wiped her hands on her apron, unhurried, terrible. "Salvage fleet moved upriver yesterday. Three ships, running lights at night, buying fuel at every post between here and the delta. Captain Vane's offering finder's fees that would buy this whole bend twice over. And there's a man going settlement to settlement behind them — well-dressed, soft-handed, calling himself Reyes — asking after a chest with a Meridian seal. Offering more than she does." Her eyes flicked to the tarped shape under his netting, then back to his face. "You're carrying it right now, aren't you. On this plane. You absolute fool."

"It's safest with me."

"No, it's safest at the bottom of a river, which is where it was until you went digging." She turned and shouted something at the crowd in a language Oscar didn't speak, and the crowd dispersed — reluctantly, looking back at him and at the plane with the particular hunger of people who had spent their whole lives poor beside a river that occasionally gave up dead men's riches. "Load your freight and go. The longer you sit here, the longer their shadow gets."

He loaded fast. Penicillin for Ferrow's Bend, sutures and fever powder for Two Sisters, insulin — precious, cold-packed, irreplaceable — for the mission station at Candle Fork. His ordinary rounds. His ordinary life, or the shape of one he'd been wearing for seven years like a coat that almost fit. He cinched the netting down over the chest and told himself it was just another crate, just another thing to be dropped and forgotten, and did not believe himself for even the length of a heartbeat.

Marta pressed a paper-wrapped loaf into his hands as he climbed into the cockpit. "For the river," she said, which was an old blessing, and then, quieter: "Fly like they haven't seen you, Lockridge. Though I reckon it's past that."

It was past that. He knew it by the second stop.

* * *

Two Sisters sat on a bluff where the river split, and its landing strip was a mowed hayfield with a windsock that had been patched so many times it resembled a quilt. Oscar came in over the tree line at dusk, and before his wheels touched grass he could see the whole settlement gathered along the fence — forty people, maybe fifty, standing in the last light like they'd been called out of church.

They didn't point. That was the difference. They just stood there, and the standing was worse.

He taxied to the end of the field and shut down, and an old man named Corben — deacon, ferryman, the closest thing Two Sisters had to authority — walked out alone across the mown grass. He stopped ten feet from the propeller, respectful of machines the way river people were respectful of anything that could kill them, and he took off his hat.

"We heard you have it," Corben said.

"Heard from who?"

"Does it matter? The river tells everything eventually. That's what a river's for." The old man looked past Oscar at the tarped crate, and his voice gentled in a way that frightened him more than shouting would have. "Meridia. My grandmother used to sing songs about Meridia. Said it was a country that put its whole treasury on one ship and sent it away the night before the end, so the enemy couldn't have it. Whole nation's savings, gone into the water. Children grew up hearing about it. Gold enough to build every school and hospital we never got." He put his hat back on. "Is it true, what they say? That it's on your plane?"

Oscar looked at the crowd at the fence line. Faces he knew. A woman whose broken arm he'd splinted two springs ago. Kids who ran alongside the plane on takeoff for the pure joy of it. People who had never asked him a single question in seven years, because the unwritten law of the interior was that a man's past was his own cargo and nobody weighed it.

"The chest I pulled off that bar is stamped with a seal," he said finally. "I don't know what's in it. I don't know whose it is. And I've got deliveries to make before dark."

Corben nodded slowly, as if Oscar had answered honestly, which in a way he had. "Then go make them. But Lockridge — word travels faster than planes out here. By tomorrow morning, every soul on this river will know where you slept tonight. You can't outrun being known. Believe me. I tried it for thirty years before I settled here."

"What are you saying?"

"I'm saying some of us would help you, if you asked. And some of us would sell you, and we couldn't tell you which was which, and neither could you." The old man smiled, tired and kind. "That's the trouble with treasure, son. It doesn't change the water. It changes the people looking at it."

Oscar took off in the blue dark with his navigation lights off, flying by river-glow and memory, and the chest rode behind him like a passenger who kept clearing his throat.

* * *

Candle Fork was a mission station on a high cutbank: a chapel, a clinic, a schoolhouse, a garden going to seed for winter. Sister Anselm ran it — a broad, gray-braided woman who'd once stitched Oscar's forearm without asking how he got the cut, and who had never once asked him anything since. He landed there near midnight, fog rising off the water again, and found her waiting on the bank with a lantern held high.

Not for the insulin.

"Sera Voss," she said, before his wheels had stopped rolling. "Eleven years old. Rheumatic fever, now her heart's involved. I can keep her comfortable but I can't cure her, and the nearest surgeon is at Greywater, three hundred miles downriver. I radioed for a medevac a week ago. They said eight days."

"How long has she got?"

"With luck, four days. Without, two." Sister Anselm lowered the lantern, and the light fell away from her face, and her voice went flat and practical, the voice of a woman who had buried too many people to waste words on hope. "You're the only plane on this stretch of river tonight. So I'm asking. Not for the chest — I don't care about the chest, and neither does God, I suspect. For the girl."

Oscar stood in the cold fog with the lantern light swinging, and did the arithmetic he did not want to do.

Greywater was downriver. The wreck was upriver. The tide tables lived in his head the way prayers live in some men's mouths: six tides until the shallows closed over the Serpent's Tooth, and each tide was roughly half a day, and he had already burned most of the first getting here. A run to Greywater and back was half a tide, maybe more with the girl loaded slow and gentle, maybe more with fuel stops, maybe more with weather. Half a tide, spent on a child with a bad heart, out of a budget of six.

Half a tide was also the margin between reaching the wreck first and watching Ilsa Vane's cranes swing over it while he circled overhead with empty tanks. Half a tide was the difference between the clean escape he'd been planning since the night he pried that lid open — sell the contents somewhere far away, vanish deeper, start again under a name the river hadn't learned — and losing all of it.

Sister Anselm watched him do the math. She'd seen men do that math before; you could tell by her face.

"I'll fly her," Oscar heard himself say.

The words came out before the arithmetic finished, which was how the important ones always came out. He felt the loss of the half-tide like a physical thing, a door closing somewhere ahead of him, and he felt something else underneath it, unfamiliar and suspiciously like relief.

"She'll need to be warm and level," Sister Anselm said, already moving. "I'll come with her. Give me ten minutes."

While she packed the girl in blankets and heated stones, Oscar walked back to his plane and stood a long moment with his hand on the cold tarpaulin over the chest. Somewhere upriver, Ilsa was burning fuel toward the same coordinates he carried in his head. Somewhere behind him, Tomas Reyes was buying his trail from settlements like Ferrow's Bend, coin by coin. And here was a child breathing shallow in a mission clinic, weighing nothing at all against a nation's treasury, and tipping the scale anyway.

"You're a fool, Lockridge," he said aloud, to no one, and unlashed the chest long enough to shift it aft for balance, making room in the forward bay for a stretcher and a sister and eleven years of a life not yet spent.

They lifted off at 2 a.m., the girl sedated and strapped level, Sister Anselm in the jump seat with two fingers resting on the child's pulse like she was holding a door shut against the sea. Fog swallowed the cutbank behind them. Oscar flew south, lights off, into the dark half of his tide budget, and the river slid beneath him, wide and black and patient, keeping every secret it had ever been given except the ones it gave up on sandbars.

At Greywater they handed the girl to a surgical team that moved like they'd been waiting all their lives for exactly her. Sister Anselm gripped Oscar's arm on the dock, hard, through his jacket. "God keeps accounts different than pilots," she said. "Fly home safe."

Home. Upriver. Toward the wreck, with five and a half tides left and every mile of the way already talking about him.

He was refueling at dawn when the Greywater radio operator came out onto the apron, waving a flimsy message slip, his face carefully blank in the way of men carrying news they'd rather not own.

"Pilot Lockridge? Message came in an hour ago. Kettle Landing relay." The man hesitated. "It's from a Captain Vane. Salvage fleet. She says—" he glanced at the slip again, though he plainly knew it by heart— "she says she knows you have the chest, she knows where you're headed, and she says you should know she's coming. All three words underlined, sir. *She's coming.*"

Oscar folded the slip into his pocket, next to the tide tables, and looked north up the grey morning river, where the fog was lifting off the water like a curtain going up.

Five and a half tides. And Ilsa Vane had never once, in all the years he'd known her, said a thing she didn't mean.
