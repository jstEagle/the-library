## 5. What Vane Keeps in Her Hold

Tomas Reyes had spent eleven years learning to read dead men's handwriting, and so it was perhaps inevitable that he would recognize the shape of a lie when he saw one — even his own.

He saw it first in the mirror that morning, in the brass-framed glass bolted to the bulkhead of the guest cabin aboard the *Corvus*, flagship of the Vane Salvage Fleet. The man looking back at him wore a good coat and a better expression, the face of an heir, patient and entitled and faintly bored, as though the fortune were already his and merely delayed by weather. It was a face Tomas had built the way he'd once built palaeographic transcriptions: stroke by stroke, from reference material. The reference in this case had been Aurelio Reyes himself, whose letters Tomas had catalogued for six years, whose cadences he could reproduce in his sleep. The dead heir's handwriting lived in Tomas's hands now. So did his posture. So did, apparently, his capacity for quiet fraud.

"You're up early," said the deckhand who brought him coffee — a broad, sunburnt woman named Petra who treated him with the elaborate courtesy fleets reserve for passengers they've been ordered not to drown. "Captain says we make the shallows by afternoon tide."

"Then I'll want to be on deck," Tomas said, in Aurelio's voice, with Aurelio's half-smile, and hated how easily it came.

Petra left. The coffee was terrible, river-boiled and bitter as confession, and Tomas drank it standing at the porthole watching the floodwater run brown and muscular between drowned willows. Somewhere behind them, Oscar Lockridge's plane was lifting off from Kettle Landing with a chest of Meridian gold lashed in its hold, and somewhere ahead of them lay the *Meridian* herself, forty years drowned, waiting under shallows that would close like a mouth after six tides. Three parties wanted what she held. Tomas had told all of them a version of the truth, and each version had been a little more invented than the last.

The trouble with inventing yourself out of a dead man's claim, he thought, is that you begin to need the dead man's things.

Which was why, when the cabin door failed to lock properly — a fleet ship in motion, everything loose, everything provisional — and when Captain Vane's own quarters stood open across the passage while she shouted course corrections at her helmsman two decks up, Tomas did not do the honorable thing. He crossed the passage. He told himself it was curiosity, then scholarship, then self-defense: *know your captor*. All three were lies, and he catalogued them too, because that was the other thing eleven years of archives taught you. Lies keep records whether you ask them to or not.

* * *

Ilsa Vane's cabin was nothing like he'd expected, which was itself a kind of information. He had imagined opulence — salvage captains grew rich on other people's disasters, and the ones who hunted wrecks like the *Meridian* were rumored to sleep on silk salvaged from the dead. Instead: a narrow bunk made with military corners. A chart table scarred by decades of dividers. One shelf of books, spines cracked from salt. And everywhere, everywhere, paper. Logbooks. Survey charts. Bundles of correspondence tied with tarred twine, stacked with an archivist's precision that made something in Tomas's chest ache with recognition.

She was an archivist too. She just called it a hunt.

He should have taken one look and left. Instead he set down his coffee and began, helplessly, to read.

The oldest bundle was wrapped in oilcloth, and the oilcloth was stiff with age, and inside it were letters addressed to *Capt. E. Vane, s.v. Cormorant*, in a hand Tomas recognized within seconds because he had spent six years immersed in the correspondence of the Meridian maritime administration — the same bureaucratic fist, the same officious loops, the same institutional cruelty dressed as formality. Letters from the Admiralty Salvage Board. Dated thirty-one years ago.

He read the first one twice before his hands would steady.

*...it is the finding of this Board that the vessel Cormorant, while licensed to survey the Meridian wreck site, engaged in unauthorized recovery of state property. The undersigned notes with regret that Capt. Emil Vane declined to surrender materials recovered, and that in consequence his license is revoked, his bond forfeited, and his name entered upon the Register of Salvage Thieves...*

Salvage thieves. There was a register for it. Of course there was; Meridia had been a nation that believed every crime could be filed. And Emil Vane — Ilsa's father, though the letters never said so, though they didn't need to — had died, if the fleet gossip Tomas had collected in Kettle Landing was worth anything, within two years of that entry, working marginal wrecks in marginal water, a licensed man no longer licensed, until the river took him the way the river took everyone who kept working after their name went into some ledger or other.

Tomas knew about ledgers. He had spent six years in the Meridian exile archive in Corvo Bay, cataloguing exactly this kind of document, and he knew what a single line in an official register could do to a family. It could follow you for generations. It could make your daughter captain a salvage fleet and point it, with thirty-one years of patience, at one specific wreck.

Because that was what the rest of the cabin was. Not treasure maps. *Evidence.*

Survey charts of the *Meridian*'s last known position, annotated in two hands — the father's older, the daughter's younger, corrections layered over corrections like sediment. Tide tables going back decades, the six-tide window marked and remarked. A diver's logbook, water-swollen and half-legible, its final entries in Emil Vane's hand, describing — Tomas's breath caught — *the strongroom aft of the mail room*, describing a *manifest cabinet, sealed, official wax intact*, describing the last thing Emil Vane ever wrote:

*Tried to raise the manifest. Line fouled. Board will call it theft anyway. Let them come themselves if they want the truth so badly.*

And beneath the logbook, the newest document in the room, written in Ilsa Vane's own sharp slanted hand, a working note that read less like a plan than a prayer:

*Manifest proves what the Cormorant recovered was declared material, released under survey license 4-118. Manifest clears him. Get the manifest.*

Tomas stood very still in the middle of another person's grief, feeling like a burglar who has broken into a house and found it is a church.

* * *

He heard her boots on the companionway ladder and did not have time to be anywhere else, so he did the only honest thing available: he stayed where he was, with the letters in his hands, and turned to face the door as it opened.

Ilsa Vane filled it the way weather fills a horizon. She had changed since Kettle Landing — oilskins over the grey coat, hair tied back wet from spray — and for a long moment she simply looked at him, at the open oilcloth bundle, at her father's logbook lying exposed on the chart table, and Tomas watched thirty-one years of guardedness decide, in real time, whether to become violence.

"I can explain," he said.

"Can you." Her voice was flat as the shallows ahead of them. "Because from here it looks like the heir to the Meridian treasury was going through my father's disgrace."

"The heir to the Meridian treasury doesn't exist," Tomas said, and heard, with strange relief, the fraud leave his voice all at once, like a coat set down. "Aurelio Reyes died childless in Corvo Bay nine years ago. I was his archivist. I catalogued his papers. When the chest surfaced and every faction in the interior started hunting a living claimant, I — became one. His handwriting, his manner, his claim. I'm the best forgery on this river, Captain, and I've been telling myself it's in service of the truth, but mostly it's been in service of being necessary."

The silence that followed had weight and texture. Rain started against the porthole, soft as static.

"Why tell me?" she said finally.

"Because you weren't hunting treasure either." He lifted the letters slightly. "You're hunting a verdict. So am I — I just borrowed mine from a dead man. You've been carrying yours for thirty-one years." He set the bundle down carefully, aligned as he'd found it, because some courtesies mattered even mid-confession. "Your father wasn't a thief. If the manifest survives down there, it proves the Cormorant's recovery was licensed. I know the licensing formats of the Meridian administration better than any living person. I could read it for you. Authenticate it. Prove it."

"And what do you want for it?" Sharp, immediate, wounded. "Everyone wants something."

"I want to stop being someone else," Tomas said. "I've been wanting it for years. I just needed to see someone else doing it worse than me."

Something moved through her face then — not softening exactly; recalibration. The look of a woman re-sorting an enemy into a different category and finding the categories broken.

"Lockridge," she said. "The pilot. He ran from a manslaughter charge seven years ago. Did you know that?"

"I suspected. Pilots who won't land where there's a magistrate are usually running from something specific."

"He flies like a man being chased by his own name." She came into the cabin at last, took the logbook from the table, and held it the way Tomas had seen relics held — not reverently, but possessively, the way you hold the only remaining piece of a person. "I told myself he was just cargo. A means to the wreck. But I watched him take off from Kettle Landing with that chest and I thought — he's doing what my father refused to do. Running with recovered property and calling it survival." Her jaw tightened. "And I hated him for it, and I couldn't tell you why, until right now, reading your face, watching you stand next to my father's letters like you understand them."

"They're well-kept," Tomas said quietly. "Whoever kept them loved him."

"I keep them so I don't forget what they took." She looked at the rain-blurred porthole, at the brown floodwater churning past, toward the drowned ship waiting somewhere ahead under closing tides. "Four tides left, Reyes. After that the channel shuts and nobody gets the manifest — not me, not you, not whatever Lockridge thinks he's doing with that chest."

"There's something else," Tomas said, because honesty, once begun, is a tide of its own. "The manifest cabinet isn't the only sealed archive aboard the Meridian. I've read the loading orders. There's a state records compartment aft — and Captain, the treasury manifest lists crates that were never loaded. The gold story doesn't balance. Whatever that ship really carried, it wasn't only money."

Ilsa Vane went very still. Outside, the rain thickened, and far upstream — so far it might have been imagination — thunder rolled along the valley like a door being closed.

"Then we'd better hope," she said, "that Lockridge's plane is still flying."

It was at that exact moment that the lookout's bell began ringing from the crow's nest — three strokes, pause, three strokes — the signal, Tomas learned four minutes later on the rain-lashed deck, for smoke sighted downstream. Not the fleet's smoke. Not a settlement's cookfire.

An aircraft, forced down on the flooded flats below the shallows, its engine cowling hanging open like a wound — and a figure beside it waving both arms, while the river rose around his wheels.
