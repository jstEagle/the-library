## 6. The Storm That Settles Arguments

The barometer on Oscar's dash had been falling all afternoon, the needle sagging like a tired shoulder, and by the time he lifted off from Halfway Bend the western sky had gone the color of a bruise healing wrong — green at the edges, yellow in the throat of it. He'd seen that color twice before. Once over the Karst Reach, when a squall peeled the roof off a mission school. Once seven years ago, from the cockpit of a plane he was flying away from everything he loved.

He did not like seeing it a third time with a chest of Meridian state seal stamped into its iron riding in his hold.

The radio crackled through static: *All stations, all stations, line squall moving east-northeast, thirty knots and building, seek shelter immediately.* Then, underneath it, thinner, almost lost: a voice he knew. Ilsa Vane's voice, clipped and professional, calling her fleet to run for cover at Gannet Rock.

Gannet Rock. Which sat square in the middle of his route home.

He could have gone around. North past the drowned orchards, south along the salt marshes, added two hours and burned fuel he didn't have. He told himself he would do exactly that, and then his hands moved the yoke toward the rock anyway, because the chest needed shelter too, and because — he admitted it only in the privacy of his own skull, where lies couldn't follow — because some part of him wanted to see whether she'd come out of the storm the way she went into everything: first, fastest, and furious.

* * *

Gannet Rock wasn't much of an island. A whaleback of granite maybe four hundred paces long, crowned with a ruined lighthouse the Meridian government had abandoned before the nation itself went under, ringed by a shingle beach that vanished at high water into something meaner. There was a stone boathouse with its roof half caved, and that was all. When Oscar set the plane down on the flat shelf of rock above the tideline and began hauling the chest toward the boathouse, the first drops were already hitting like flung gravel.

Ilsa's flagship came out of the murk twenty minutes later, running hard before the wind, her salvage crane lashed down and her rail white with spray. Behind her, farther out and lower in the water, came a second vessel — the *Cormorant Queen*, Brack Ferro's boat, the rival crew that had been shadowing Oscar since Kettle Landing. Three parties, one rock, and nowhere on God's gray water for any of them to go until the sky finished its argument.

Ilsa came ashore in a rubber coat with her hair plastered flat and her pistol holstered but visible, which was its own kind of courtesy. She looked older than seven years should have made her. Or maybe she'd always looked like that and grief had just caught up to the rest of her face.

"Lockridge." She said his name like a diagnosis.

"Captain." He nodded at the chest between them, sitting on the boathouse floor under a tarp, dripping river water. "Before you say anything — I found it fair. Open water, no claim filed."

"Fair." She laughed once, without any humor in it at all. "You want to talk about fair to me? On this rock?"

Brack Ferro's crew came ashore last, six men with machetes and one with a shotgun wrapped in oilcloth, and the evening arranged itself the way evenings do when everyone is armed and nobody can leave: a cold truce, three fires spaced evenly across the shingle, and every man, woman, and pilot pretending not to watch the others.

* * *

The storm arrived fully after dark.

It came as sound first — a long rolling drumroll out of the west that Oscar felt in his back teeth — and then as wind that flattened the grass tufts on the rock and drove rain sideways with enough force to sting. The three parties retreated into the boathouse because there was nowhere else, and so it happened that Oscar spent the worst night of the season wedged between Ilsa Vane and Brack Ferro's bosun, with the chest between him and the door and Tomas Reyes — who had come aboard Ilsa's ship at Kettle Landing and hadn't left it — sitting quietly in the corner with his notebook, writing by candlelight as though the end of the world were merely weather.

"Tell me again," Ilsa said, somewhere past midnight, "why you get to keep it."

Oscar kept his eyes on the candle. "Because I found it."

"You found it because the flood gave it to you. That's not finding, that's being handed something you haven't earned." Her voice was low, meant for him alone, but in the boathouse nothing was meant for anyone alone; every ear was turned their way. "You've spent seven years taking things the river hands you and disappearing before anyone can ask what they cost. I know how you work, Oscar. I taught half of it."

"That's not true."

"No?" She leaned close, and he could smell salt and engine oil on her, the smell of every year he'd known her. "Then tell me why you ran from Kettle Landing the morning after we buried my father. Tell me why you never once sent word. Tell me why the first time I hear your name in seven years it's attached to a treasure chest."

"Because—" And there it was, the old charge, the manslaughter, the night on the Karst Reach when a cargo strap failed and a dockhand named Ellery Penn went into the water and didn't come up, and Oscar had stood on the bank while men shouted his name and then done the only thing he knew how to do, which was leave. "Because staying was worse."

"Staying is always worse," Ilsa said softly. "That's what cowards tell themselves so they can sleep."

Brack Ferro's bosun chose that moment to stand up, and the whole room changed shape.

He was a big man, slow-moving, with a face like a closed fist, and he crossed to the chest in four steps and put his boot on the tarp. "Enough talk," he said. "Ferro says the chest rides with us. Wind dies at dawn, we take it, and anybody who argues can swim home."

Oscar was on his feet before he decided to be. So was Ilsa. So, surprisingly, was Tomas Reyes, standing small and straight-backed with his notebook still in his hand, saying, "That chest contains records belonging to the people of Meridia, and I will stand in front of it if I have to," in the tone of a man who had absolutely no intention of surviving the night but intended to be correct while failing.

Knives came out. Not many — three, maybe four, glinting in the candlelight — and the shotgun unwrapped from its oilcloth, and for ten seconds the boathouse held perfectly still, the way water holds still just before it goes over the edge.

Then the sea came over the edge instead.

* * *

It hit the boathouse like a train.

Later, Oscar would piece it together: the storm surge, the spring tide, the wind swinging north and stacking the water against the rock. What he knew in the moment was simply that the wall of the boathouse exploded inward in a fist of black water, the candle died, the chest lurched, men screamed, and the ocean reached in and started choosing.

It chose the bosun first, took him off his feet and slammed him against the far wall. It chose the shotgun, spinning it away into the dark. It chose Tomas Reyes, sweeping the little archivist toward the broken doorway and the churning shingle beyond, and it was Tomas's cry — thin, human, nothing to do with treasure at all — that put Oscar in motion.

He caught Tomas by the wrist as the water tried to peel them both out the door. For one long, grinding moment they hung there, Oscar braced against a doorpost with his boots skidding on wet stone, the sea pulling with a patience that felt personal. Then a second wave hit, shallower, and he hauled the archivist back into the ruin and shoved him up onto the high ledge where the old lamp room stairs began.

Which was when he heard Ilsa.

She'd gone down in the first surge. The receding water was dragging her toward the breach, toward the open rock and the black heave beyond it, and her hand had found the tarp — the tarp over the chest — and she was holding on to it, holding on to *it*, because seven years of anger and one dead father had taught her that the thing in the box mattered more than her own fingers.

"Let go!" Oscar shouted.

She didn't. Of course she didn't.

So he let go of everything else. He dropped into the flood — it was waist-deep and rising, cold enough to stop thought — and fought his way to her along the wall, hand over hand, and got his arm around her chest from behind. "The chest will still be here tomorrow," he yelled into her ear. "*You* might not be!"

"I need it—"

"It'll still be here!" And then, because the next wave was already coming, because there was no time for anything true or complicated: "And so will I. Let go, Ilsa. Please."

Something in the *please* did what seven years hadn't. Her grip opened. The wave took the tarp, took the chest, dragged it grinding across the floor toward the breach — and Oscar threw himself backward with Ilsa in his arms, onto the stairs, onto the stone, as the sea filled the boathouse to the ceiling and then, slowly, grudgingly, began to give it back.

They lay on the lamp room stairs gasping, tangled together, her heart hammering against his ribs like a second pulse. Below them, in the ankle-deep wash, men were counting themselves aloud. The chest sat against the far wall, dented, intact, having traveled perhaps eight feet.

Eight feet. All that fighting, and the storm had moved it less than a boat length.

Ilsa was shaking. He held on. Neither of them said anything for a long time, and in the silence Oscar understood that he had just paid something he could never get back — that saving her had spent whatever was left of the clean escape, the quiet exit, the version of the story where he vanished upriver with a fortune and no name. You couldn't unsave a person. She was in his ledger now, permanent ink.

"Your arm's bleeding," she said finally, her voice rough.

He looked down. A gash ran from elbow to wrist where the doorpost had taken its toll, deep enough to matter, shallow enough to insult. "It'll keep."

"Let me look at it." And she tore a strip from her own shirt, there in the dark, with the storm screaming overhead and her rival's crew watching from the shadows, and she cleaned and bound the wound with the brisk tenderness of a woman who had bandaged her father's hands for years, and neither of them mentioned what it was.

Tomas Reyes appeared at the top of the stairs, soaked, notebook clutched to his chest, and said quietly, "The water took the manifest page I'd copied. But I remember it. Most of it." He hesitated. "There's something in those records, Captain Lockridge. Something I haven't told either of you. And I think — given tonight — you've earned the right to hear it before anyone else does."

Below them, Brack Ferro's men were whispering by the breach, and one of them was looking at the plane on its rock shelf, and the tide had four more hours to fall.

Oscar closed his eyes against the sting of salt. "Start talking," he said.
