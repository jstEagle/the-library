## 7. Two Tides Short

Ilsa Vane had learned to read weather the way other people read faces, and the sky over Kettle Landing that morning wore an expression she didn't trust. Low gray cloud, no wind worth naming, and a stillness on the water like held breath. The river was thinking something over.

She stood at the rail of the *Provision's Answer* — her father's ship, her father's name for it, though the man himself had been dead eleven years and the name had outlived every argument about whether she deserved to keep it — and watched Oscar Lockridge walk the dock toward his plane with the particular gait of a man who believed he was about to be free of her.

Two tides left. The shallows over the Meridian would begin their slow collapse after the next slack water, silt closing like a throat, and by the sixth tide there would be nothing left to find but rumor. Everything now depended on speed, and Oscar's plane was speed incarnate: forty minutes upriver against two days by boat.

"You're sure about this route?" he'd asked her at dawn, not looking at her while he asked, which was his way. Oscar could face down a storm front but not a direct question delivered eye to eye.

"It's the fastest channel," Ilsa said. "The floodwater opened it three weeks ago. My charts are current."

"Your charts." He'd almost smiled. "Last time I trusted your charts I spent a night in a tree."

"Last time you trusted my charts you were also running from me," she said, "and trees were the least of what caught up with you."

He hadn't laughed, but something in his shoulders had loosened a degree, and she had counted that as progress, because counting small mercies was how she'd survived seven years of loving a man built entirely of exits.

Now she watched him run the pre-flight checks, Tomas Reyes beside him hauling the last crates aboard — fuel, water, the diving gear they'd bartered half the fleet's spare chain to get. The Meridian waited under forty feet of brown water, and somewhere beneath its deck plates lay the manifest that would either redeem her father's name or prove, finally and forever, that the brand of thief the Admiralty Court had burned into it was true. She had stopped believing in one outcome or the other years ago. What she believed in now was seeing it with her own eyes.

Oscar swung into the cockpit. The propeller turned over, coughed, caught.

And died.

He tried again. The engine turned, caught, ran rough for three seconds, then quit with a sound Ilsa had heard only once before — in her father's hangar, the day a mechanic named Corben Hale had shown her what sugar in a fuel line looked like, and told her that any fool could sabotage an engine, but it took a patient fool.

"Lockridge!" She was already moving, boots ringing on the dock planks. "Shut it down. Now."

He killed the ignition and looked back at her, and she saw him arrive at the same arithmetic she had. Not weather. Not bad fuel from a careless purchase. Someone had done this in the dark hours, and there were exactly three people who'd had access to the plane since midnight: herself, Tomas, and the crewman she'd brought aboard at Kettle Landing because he came cheap and knew the river — Denny Marsh, six months with the fleet, quiet, competent, and lately too interested in the chest stamped with the seal of Meridia.

She found Denny in the galley of the *Answer*, eating eggs like a man without a care in the world, and put her knife through the table an inch from his hand before he finished swallowing.

"Talk."

"Captain—" His eyes went to the knife, then past her shoulder to where Oscar and Tomas had crowded into the doorway. "Captain, I don't know what you—"

"The fuel line, Denny. Sugar doesn't walk."

For a long moment he held her stare, and she gave him credit for the attempt. Then his gaze flicked — just once, just slightly — toward the porthole, toward the water, toward the east, where the rival fleet's smoke had been visible on the horizon since dawn, and Ilsa understood everything at once and hated how long it had taken her.

"Hale sent you."

Denny said nothing, which was answer enough. Corben Hale, second-in-command of the salvage fleet, the man who'd been whispering in ears since Port Meridian that the captain had gone soft, that the heir was a liability, that treasure shared was treasure halved. The man who wanted the wreck found fast and the witnesses lost after.

"He offered you a cut," Ilsa said. "Or he offered you your neck. Which?"

"My neck," Denny said quietly. "He has my sister's debt papers, Captain. From the Harrow Street houses. You know what happens to people who owe those."

Ilsa pulled the knife from the table. She should have thrown him off the boat at the first port; instead she heard herself say, "Then you'll work it off honestly, starting with scrubbing my bilge until your hands bleed," and saw relief break across his face like a fever breaking, and understood that mercy was a currency too, and she'd just spent some she couldn't afford.

But the larger cost sat in the dock beside them, wings folded, engine dead. Two tides. Two days by river. And Hale's ships already moving.

* * *

They took the *Answer* up the old channel by noon, the slow way, the way the river itself seemed to resent — narrow, braided, choked with flood debris that required a man on the bow with a boathook and constant, grinding attention. Oscar took the bow. It suited him, Ilsa thought, watching him work: give the man a physical problem and he was steady as bedrock; give him a human one and he started calculating exits.

Tomas Reyes sat beside her at the wheel, chart on his knees, calling depths from memory of channels that hadn't existed a month ago. He was good at it. He was good at everything, which was precisely what had begun to bother her.

Because she'd been doing sums all morning, the kind you do when trust is a ledger and someone's been writing in it. Tomas knew the Meridian's history better than any living soul claimed to. He knew the state archives' catalog numbers by heart. He spoke of the royal family with the intimacy of a relative and the precision of a scholar, and last night, half-asleep in the chartroom, he had murmured coordinates in a dead language's numbering system — the old Meridian base-sixty, used only in official documents, taught only to archivists and surveyors.

An heir would know his family's tragedy. An archivist would know his family's paperwork.

"Tomas," she said, keeping her voice level, eyes on the channel. "The claim you carry. Whose signature is on it?"

The silence lasted four full seconds. On the bow, Oscar went still over his boathook, and she knew he'd heard.

"Prince Aldous Reyes-Meridian," Tomas said at last. "My... cousin."

"Try again."

The river muttered against the hull. Somewhere ahead a heron lifted off the shallows, unhurried, indifferent to the affairs of men.

"I'm not his cousin," Tomas said. "I never met him. He died at Tarnholt Crossing eight years ago — dysentery, in a refugee column, with less to his name than I have. I was the junior archivist assigned to preserve the state records when the government fell. I was twenty-three." His voice didn't waver, but his hands closed slowly around the chart's edge until the paper creased. "When the capital burned, I saved what I could carry. Including the succession registry. Including Aldous's signed instrument of claim, which he executed legally, properly, six months before he died, designating whoever carried his documents as bearer of his claim. So yes — technically, legally, the claim is real. But the blood is not mine. The grief is not even mine, not originally. I'm a librarian carrying a dead man's inheritance, and I let you both believe otherwise because a living heir opens doors that a filing clerk cannot."

Ilsa steered. The channel bent, and she let it bend, buying time the way the river bought distance.

"Why?" she said finally. "Why lie to us? To me?"

"Because you needed a reason to help me that wasn't paper," Tomas said. "A claimant is a person. A document is a thing men argue about and then burn. I've watched documents burn, Captain. I watched a nation's entire legal existence go up in one afternoon. Paper is only as strong as the hands holding it, so I made myself into stronger hands." He turned to look at her, and his eyes were dry and terribly tired. "I won't apologize for the method. But I will tell you the truth I was protecting: the Meridian wasn't carrying gold. I've known since I catalogued the loading manifests in exile. And if we reach that wreck, what we find will not make anyone rich. It may make everyone ashamed. That's why Hale wants it — not the treasury. The proof. Proof can be sold to anyone with an interest in burying it."

On the bow, Oscar had stopped pretending to work. He came aft slowly, boathook in hand like a pilgrim's staff, and Ilsa braced for the anger — Oscar, who had run seven years from a verdict, meeting a man who had invented a verdict to survive.

"You let us risk our lives," Oscar said, "on a story."

"I let you risk your lives on a truth wearing a story's coat," Tomas said. "There's a difference, and you of all people know it. How long did you wear *pilot-for-hire* as a coat, Mr. Lockridge? Underneath it, weren't you always a man running from a manslaughter charge? Did I sail with a ferry pilot, or with Oscar Lockridge, who once—"

"That's enough." Ilsa's voice cracked across the deck like a whip.

But Tomas didn't stop, gentler now: "We are all bearers of dead men's claims. Every one of us. The only question is whether we carry them honestly."

The channel narrowed. Ahead, the water split around a fallen cottonwood, white water threading through the branches, and Ilsa put the wheel hard over and let the moment pass with the current, because the river did not wait for forgiveness and neither did the tide.

It was Oscar who broke the silence, ten minutes later, not looking at either of them, eyes on the far bank where the hills began to rise toward the high country.

"When we reach the wreck," he said, "you open the manifest first. Before me. Before her." A pause. "You carried it eight years. You get to be the one who reads it."

Tomas made a sound that might have been thanks and might have been something breaking quietly, and Ilsa kept her eyes forward and her hands on the wheel and allowed herself, for one dangerous moment, to believe that three liars might still make an honest crew.

* * *

They made the narrows below Serpent Bend by late afternoon, and that was where the smoke found them.

Not one plume. Three. Rising from behind the ridge line where the fast channel ran — Hale's ships, taking the route Denny's sabotage had denied them, except Hale had never needed sabotage to move fast. He'd needed only to not care what his hulls hit. Ilsa raised her glass and counted masts, and her stomach dropped through the bottom of the boat.

"They'll beat us to the anchorage by a tide," she said. "Maybe more."

"Then we lose," Oscar said.

"No." Tomas was unfolding his creased chart, smoothing it flat against the wheel housing with both palms, and there was something new in his face — not the archivist's caution, not the false heir's performance, but the plain stubbornness of a man who had carried paper through fire and meant to carry it further. "There's a third way. The old survey canal. My predecessor mapped it in the last year of the kingdom — it cuts through the bend, saves half a day. It's not on any chart since the Flood, because everyone believes it collapsed."

"Does it?" Ilsa asked. "Collapse?"

"I believe," Tomas said, "that it is exactly as passable as our need for it."

Oscar looked at the smoke, then at the narrowing channel ahead, then at Ilsa — and for the first time in seven years, she saw him look at a choice between escape and everything else and not flinch.

"Then we take the canal," he said. "Both tides. Whatever's left of them."

Ilsa put the wheel over toward the unmarked entrance, where the reeds grew tall and the water ran dark and unmapped, and somewhere behind them the smoke of the rival fleet thickened against the evening, and ahead — under forty feet of closing brown water — the Meridian waited with its dead men's papers, singing its low song that only two tides remained to hear.
