## 8. The Wreck Sings

Slack water came at 4:17 by Tomas's watch, and it lasted eleven minutes.

Oscar had spent the whole night learning that number — eleven minutes, twice a day, when the river stopped arguing with itself and the current over the Meridian went still as held breath. Ilsa had marked the window on her chart in red pencil, a sliver of time so thin you could miss it blinking. They had two tides left. This was the first.

He stood at the rail of the *Kestrel's Mercy* — Ilsa's lead ship, borrowed now because his own plane sat drowned to its wingtips in the mud of Two-Tides Short, sabotaged and sulking — and watched the water over the wreck go glassy. Below it, forty feet down, lay a nation.

"You don't have to dive," Ilsa said beside him. She was already in her wetsuit, half-zipped, hair tied back with a length of cord. In the gray pre-dawn light she looked like she had looked seven years ago, before he'd left her a note and a stolen boat and a debt he'd never named. "I can take Tomas down. You can work the line."

"I've been working lines my whole life," Oscar said. "It's the one job where nobody asks who you are."

"That's not true and you know it." She zipped the suit to her throat. "Everyone on this river knows exactly who you are now. That's why we're here."

He couldn't argue with that. Word had outrun them even on the slow route — every landing from here to the delta had known about the chest, about the pilot, about the six-tide clock. Somewhere behind them, Vane's rival fleet was burning fuel to catch up. Somewhere ahead, the channel over the Meridian would close like a mouth.

Tomas came up from below decks carrying three dive lamps and the expression of a man who had not slept. He was thinner than when he'd chartered aboard — they all were — but there was something lit in him now, something that hadn't been there in the beginning, when he'd been only an archivist wearing a dead man's claim like a borrowed coat.

"Eleven minutes," Tomas said. "Maybe nine, if the floodwater's still pushing through the cut."

"Then we don't talk down there," Oscar said.

They went over the side in the last of the dark.

* * *

The river at forty feet was a different country. Cold enough to ache, dark enough that their lamps carved cones out of nothing, and quiet — that enormous, pressing quiet that made Oscar's own heartbeat sound like someone knocking on a door. He'd dived wrecks before, small ones, barges and a mail plane, salvage jobs paid in cash by men who didn't want receipts. Nothing had prepared him for the scale of what rose out of the silt below.

The *Meridian* lay on her starboard side along an old channel bank, and she was vast — a state vessel, built to be seen, her hull still bearing the ghost of gilt scrollwork under a skin of river moss. Floodwater had stripped decades of mud off her in a single season, the way a dog shakes off rain. Her name ran along the stern in letters tall as a man: MERIDIAN, and beneath it, worn but legible, the motto of a country that no longer existed on any map Oscar trusted.

*The Provision Sings.*

Tomas touched the letters. Even through the gloves, even underwater, Oscar saw the gesture land in him like a blow. The archivist had spent his life cataloguing a nation's paper ghosts, and here was the body itself.

Ilsa signaled: *the hold.* They swam the length of the hull, past portholes furred white with freshwater coral, past a gunport that should not have been there — Oscar noticed it, logged it, said nothing. State vessels carried guns. Nations always had things to be afraid of.

The hold hatch had been sprung by the sinking or by the flood, hinged open like a jaw. Their lamps swept inside.

And stopped.

Oscar had imagined gold. Everyone had imagined gold — that was the whole engine of the last five days, the chest on the sandbar stamped with the seal of Meridia, the fleets, the knives on the island, the sabotage, the dead man's claim Tomas carried. Gold was simple. Gold could be divided, fought over, spent, buried again. Gold forgave everyone their reasons.

The hold of the *Meridian* contained no gold.

It contained shelves. Steel shelving, bolted to the frame, row upon row of them, and on the shelves — sealed cases, hundreds of them, each stenciled with the same crest and a classification band in a language of stripes Oscar didn't know but understood anyway. State records. Sealed state records, packed for transport, sunk with the ship rather than allowed to arrive wherever she'd been going.

Tomas found the manifest case first — smaller, mounted near the hatch, the kind of box meant to survive anything. He worked it free and got it open, and his lamp shook as he read.

When they surfaced, in the last four minutes of slack water, treading in the flat gray seam between tides, Tomas read it aloud with the river lapping at his chin.

"Manifest of the final sailing," he said. His voice cracked on *final*. "Treasury transfer — struck through. Struck through and initialed. There was never any treasury aboard. It says—" He stopped, swallowed. "It says the treasury allocation was a cover legend. The cargo is the sealed record of the Interior Directorate. Trials. Deportations. The ledger of the resettlements. Names of officers. Names of the men who signed the orders." He looked up, and his eyes were wet, and not from the river. "They sank their own crimes rather than let them be read."

Nobody spoke. Slack water ended; the current woke around them like something turning over in sleep, and they climbed aboard the *Kestrel's Mercy* in silence, hauling themselves up the ladder with the manifest case wrapped in Tomas's arms like an infant.

* * *

In the wardroom, under a swinging lamp, they opened what they'd brought up: the manifest case, and one record case Tomas had insisted on, one sample, one proof. Inside the record case, wrapped in oilcloth that had done its job across sixty years of riverbed, were documents. Photographs. A ledger with columns of names and, beside the names, entries in a clerk's careful hand — dates, places, numbers. Numbers of people.

Oscar had spent seven years running from one death, one bad night, one verdict he'd never let anyone pronounce. He knew what guilt weighed. He'd been carrying a single stone all this time and calling it a boulder.

The *Meridian* carried a nation's worth.

"This is what my father died for," Ilsa said quietly. She stood at the far end of the table, arms braced on it, not touching the papers. "Not gold. He went down on the outer bar trying to raise this ship, and the fleet court branded him a thief for it — said he'd been looting a treasury. Twenty years they've said it. My whole life, the Vane name has been *that* name." Her voice didn't rise. That was the worst of it. It stayed level, level as slack water, and underneath it Oscar could hear everything moving. "There was never anything to steal. He died diving into a country's conscience, and they called it theft."

"Now you know," Tomas said. "The manifest clears him. It's all here — the cover legend, the initials, the routing orders. Your father wasn't a thief. He was the only honest man in the story."

"And no one will ever believe it," Ilsa said, "unless these papers come up. All of them. Every case."

Oscar looked at the ledger. At the names. He thought about the settlements upriver — Kettle Landing, the ferry towns, the medevac runs, the girl they'd flown out at the cost of half a tide. He thought about how news traveled on the river: not by decree, not by newspaper, but by rumor, by radio, by the slow reliable gossip of people who traded in what was true because lies cost too much to carry.

"The interior doesn't need a tribunal," he heard himself say. "It's had sixty years of tribunals, and look where they're stored." He tapped the case. "This isn't treasure. It's an inheritance. And the inheritance is guilt."

"Then who inherits?" Ilsa asked.

Before he could answer, the deck shuddered under their boots.

* * *

Oscar felt it before he heard it — engines, more than one, big ones, coming up the channel at a speed the river didn't forgive. He was on deck in six strides, lamp doused, and there they were: three hulls rounding the bend below the wreck site, running dark except for a single signal lamp flicking a pattern he recognized from seven years of avoiding harbors with rules.

*Heave to or be boarded.*

"They were closer than we thought," Ilsa said, coming up beside him with a rifle she'd apparently kept loaded through a storm, a sabotage, and a confession. Of course she had. "That's Corvus's squadron. The *Ironbell* and two tenders. He won't parley."

"He'll want the hold," Tomas said, joining them, clutching the manifest case. "He'll want it and he'll want it quiet. If word gets out what's really down there—"

"Then we make sure it gets out loud," Oscar said.

Below them, forty feet down, the *Meridian* waited with her shelves of sealed shame, and the tide was turning, and the channel would begin to close, and somewhere in the dark the armed ships were lowering boats. Eleven minutes of slack water left in the world, and every faction on the river converging on the spot where a drowned nation kept its receipts.

Isla checked her rifle. "How many copies did you say you could broadcast?"

Oscar watched the signal lamp blink its threat across the water, and felt — for the first time in seven years — entirely still.

"Enough," he said. "If we get eleven minutes."
