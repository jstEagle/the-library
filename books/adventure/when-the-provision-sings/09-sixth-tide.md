## 9. Sixth Tide

The river had begun to close its throat.

Oscar felt it before he saw it — a change in the way the water moved around the Meridian's ribs, the current thickening, gathering itself like a man rolling down his sleeves. The shallows that had let them dive at slack were already shrinking back, sand hissing up through the brown water in long pale fingers. Two hours, maybe less, and the channel would be sealed for another season.

He stood waist-deep beside the wreck's broken stern, the sealed records case heavy against his chest, and did the arithmetic he'd been doing since dawn: one plane, three factions, six tides spent down to this last one, and every road out of the interior about to be owned by somebody with a gun.

Ilsa surfaced ten feet away, spitting river water, her hair plastered flat. She'd been down twice more, mapping the hold out of habit even though they both knew what was down there now. Not gold. Not treasury bars stamped with Meridia's drowned eagle. Paper. Sealed state records, packed in oilcloth and lead-lined cases, documenting seven years of what a nation had done to its own people before the flood took it — requisition ledgers, deportation orders, the names of villages emptied to fill someone's quota. The inheritance wasn't wealth. It was guilt, boxed and waterproofed, waiting forty years for someone foolish enough to open it.

"You're thinking," Ilsa said, treading water. "That's never good."

"The fleet's coming." He nodded upstream, where the morning fog still hung in rags. He could hear them — diesels, more than one, running without lights, which told him everything about their intentions. "Rourke's crew went armed. You saw the mounts on the *Corvina* when we passed the bend."

"I saw." She swam closer, and for a moment she only looked at him, water streaming off her jaw, and there was something in her face that wasn't about treasure or manifests or dead fathers. Something older. "Whatever you're about to do, Oscar Lockridge, say it fast. We don't have time for you to be noble in installments."

* * *

He'd worked it out in the dark hours before slack tide, lying in the bottom of the boat while Tomas read the recovered documents by lamplight, his voice going hoarse reading names aloud. The records named four hundred and eleven settlements. Some of those settlements still existed. Some of them were the places Oscar had been flying medicine to for seven years, dropping crates into fog, taking payment in silence, never asking why the people along this stretch of river spoke Meridian dialects that weren't supposed to have survived.

The nation hadn't drowned by accident. That was what the papers said. And the papers said it in official ink, with signatures, with dates — proof no faction could be trusted to hold, because any government, any salvage consortium, any heir claiming a fortune would bury it or sell it or use it as a weapon.

But rumor — rumor didn't need custody. Rumor traveled faster than planes. It was the only currency the interior had ever honored, passed settlement to settlement over radio sets cobbled from truck parts, and it couldn't be sunk, seized, or bought back.

Tomas had understood first. Of course he had; he'd spent his life carrying a dead man's claim, an archivist who knew better than anyone that a document locked in a vault protects no one. "Copy everything," he'd said, hands shaking. "Then let the river keep the originals. Let it be the vault. Nobody dives past the sixth tide anyway."

So they'd copied. All night, by lamplight, photographing page after page with the salvaged camera from Ilsa's survey kit while the tide crept back in and Rourke's diesels got louder somewhere in the fog.

Now, standing in the closing water, Oscar made the last decision alone, because it was the one nobody could make for him.

The copies went into the plane. The originals went back into the wreck.

* * *

It took twenty minutes to reseal the lead cases and lower them into the flooded hold, into the dark where the Meridian had kept its secrets for forty years, and every minute of it Oscar worked with one ear cocked upstream. Ilsa stood watch on the wreck's exposed keel, rifle across her back, watching the fog.

"They're in the narrows," she called down once. "Three boats. Rourke's leading in a skiff, which means he thinks the channel's still open enough to be worth risking his neck."

"Then he's about to learn arithmetic the hard way."

When the last case settled among the ribs, Oscar laid his palm flat on the wreck's hull one moment longer than he needed to. Forty years under the river, holding a nation's confession, and now it would hold it a while longer — until the river decided otherwise, or until some future generation came asking honest questions with honest tools. It felt less like hiding something than like entrusting it. The river had been the only neutral party in this whole affair from the beginning.

He swam for the skiff. They rowed for the shore where the plane sat beached on the gravel bar, engine cowling already open, Tomas lashing the film canisters into a waterproof bundle behind the pilot's seat.

"It's done?" Tomas asked.

"It's done. The river keeps the original. We carry the truth." Oscar swung up into the cockpit and began the preflight with hands that knew the motions so well they could do them while his mind ran elsewhere — because elsewhere was the hard part. Elsewhere was the fact that once he started the engine, once he lifted off and turned south toward Kettle Landing and its relay tower, he was not escaping. For the first time in seven years, the plan was not escape.

The broadcast would take eleven minutes. Eleven minutes on an open frequency, reading names and dates into every radio set between here and the coast, telling four hundred settlements what had been done to their grandparents in the name of a treasury that never existed. And during those eleven minutes he would be a fixed point on the map — a beacon, a target, a man broadcasting his own location to a salvage fleet with guns and a grudge.

Rumor was currency. But spending it cost.

* * *

The engine caught on the third try, coughing river damp, then steadied into its familiar ragged roar. Ilsa climbed onto the wing root, leaned in through the window, and held on to the frame with both hands so he had to look at her.

"Kettle Landing relay will rebroadcast automatically once you key it," she shouted over the engine. "Every settlement inside two hundred miles hears you by nightfall. By tomorrow the whole interior knows."

"And Rourke knows exactly where I am while I'm saying it."

"Yes." Her grip tightened on the frame. Her knuckles were white, and not from the wind. Seven years ago he'd left her without a goodbye, and two days ago he'd pulled her out of a flooding squall against his own interest, and neither of those things had been settled yet — maybe they never would be. But she looked at him now the way she used to look at charts, measuring, deciding, committing. "Eleven minutes is a long time to sit still over an open field. So don't sit still. Circle wide, come back low, and if the *Corvina*'s guns find your range—"

"I'll be somewhere else."

"That's not a plan, that's a habit."

"It's kept me alive seven years."

Something crossed her face — anger, or grief, or the place where those two things share a border. "And it's kept you alone," she said. "Fly, Oscar. Come back. Or don't. But this time, whichever you choose — mean it."

She dropped off the wing and slapped the fuselage twice, the old fleet signal for *clear prop*, and he was moving, gravel spraying, the plane wallowing up off the bar into air that smelled of rain coming down from the high country.

* * *

From eight hundred feet he could see the whole board.

The narrows churning white where the sixth tide was shutting its gate. The Meridian's keel vanishing under the rising water, going back into the river's keeping. Rourke's three boats strung out in the fog below him, running for a channel that wouldn't exist by the time they reached it — and then, as he watched, the lead skiff turning hard, the men in it pointing up. Finding him.

The *Corvina*'s deck gun wouldn't reach this high. But Rourke had a rifle man with a scoped barrel mounted at the bow, and Oscar had seen what that man could do to an engine at four hundred yards.

Eleven minutes. He keyed the radio, found the old emergency band that every settlement rig in the interior monitored because it was the only band cheap enough to build for, and began to read.

"*This is a broadcast from the wreck of the steamship Meridian. What follows are the sealed state records of the Provisional Government of Meridia, recovered this day from the ship's hold. I am reading the requisition ledgers of the Interior Resettlement Authority, years nine through fourteen. Settlement of Ash Ferry: two hundred souls removed, destination unrecorded. Settlement of Cold Larder: grain levy of ninety percent, winter following, deaths by starvation recorded as 'seasonal attrition'...*"

Names. Dates. Signatures. He read them in the flat voice he used for cargo manifests, because anything else would crack him open, and the words went out over the water and into the fog and into a thousand kitchens and trading posts and mission stations where people were setting down their cups and turning up the volume and looking at each other.

Below him, the rifleman fired. The first round went wide, slapping the river. The second kissed the tail — he felt it, a shudder through the airframe like a knuckle rapped on a door.

He kept reading. "*Settlement of Two Rivers: quarantine imposed without cause, duration eleven months, one hundred and sixty-three dead of conditions curable for the price of quinine...*"

Third round took the port-side strut. The plane yawed. He corrected with everything the old girl had, circling wide the way Ilsa told him, climbing, buying seconds with altitude, and read on. Somewhere below, other voices were breaking into the band now — Kettle Landing's relay operator, then a mission station, then a voice he recognized from a medicine run two years back, an old woman at a river post repeating his words word for word into her own transmitter so the settlements beyond relay range would hear them too. Justice by rumor. The interior passing the truth hand to hand faster than any fleet could chase it.

By minute nine, half the band was reading with him.

By minute eleven, when he signed off — "*The records remain with the river. The river keeps what nations cannot*" — the broadcast was no longer his. It belonged to everyone, which meant it could no longer be stopped, buried, or bought.

The rifleman put a fourth round through the cowling and the engine began, very calmly, to die.

* * *

Oscar glided.

Seven years of flying cargo into impossible strips had taught him one thing above all others: a plane is always landing somewhere, the only question is whether you choose where. He chose the long gravel shoal downstream of the wreck, dead-stick, wheels kissing stone, the dying engine ticking as it cooled, and he sat there in the sudden enormous quiet listening to the river run and the distant frustrated engines of Rourke's boats turning back from a channel that had closed behind them like a book.

The tide was shut. The wreck was sealed. The truth was loose in the world, multiplying in every settlement radio shack, and no one — not Rourke, not any government, not any heir — could ever gather it all back in again.

He climbed down onto the shingle on legs that shook, and looked up to see Ilsa's launch rounding the point, Tomas standing in the bow, and behind them, far off, the first answering crackle of the relay at Kettle Landing rebroadcasting his own voice back across the water.

They came ashore and stood around the wounded plane, and for a long moment nobody said anything, because there was nothing left to argue about. The treasure was gone. The manifest was public. The escape he'd planned for seven years had ended here, on a gravel bar, in front of witnesses.

"There will be questions now," Tomas said quietly. "About the records. About who recovered them. About the pilot who read four hundred names over an open band." He looked at Oscar with something like pity and something like hope. "Including old ones. About a manslaughter charge in a town called Harrow Bend, seven years ago."

Oscar closed his eyes. He'd known this was coming — had known it since the night he'd decided to stop running, though knowing and hearing were different animals entirely. The river keeps what nations cannot. But it also, eventually, gives up what men try to drown.

"I know," he said. "I'll answer them."

Ilsa stepped closer, close enough that he could see the river light moving in her eyes. "Harrow Bend," she said. "I wondered, all these years, what you were carrying. Now I know it weighs less than what you just put down." She paused. "When you go to face it — and you are going to face it, aren't you?"

"Yeah."

"Then I'll be there. Both of us will." She glanced at Tomas, who nodded once, solemn as a witness at a signing. "You spent seven years learning how to leave, Oscar. Time somebody taught you how to arrive."

Downstream, the sixth tide finished its work, and the Meridian sank back into the river's keeping, singing quiet, keeping its secret for whoever came next with honest hands.
