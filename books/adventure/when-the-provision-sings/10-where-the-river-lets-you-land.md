## 10. Where the River Lets You Land

The courthouse at Kettle Landing had been a cannery once, and it still smelled faintly of brine and old fish oil, as if the river itself had been summoned to testify. Ilsa Vane sat on the hard bench with her hands folded in her lap and her father's brass compass in her coat pocket, and she watched Oscar Lockridge do the thing no one in the interior had ever seen him do.

He stood still.

Seven years of running had taught him to move like water, always finding the low ground, the fog, the gap between tides. But here he stood in a borrowed jacket two sizes too big at the shoulders, before a magistrate with flour on his cuffs, and he did not fidget, did not glance at the door, did not calculate the distance to his plane. He had landed at the Kettle Landing strip three weeks ago, taxied to the magistrate's office, and knocked. That was the whole of it. The settlements still told the story the way they told the story of the chest — with the details polished smooth by retelling, until the knocking itself had become a kind of legend.

"The charge is manslaughter," the magistrate read, for the third time that morning, as though the words might rearrange themselves if he gave them enough air. "Arising from the sinking of the ferry *Cormorant*, seven years past. You've waived extradition. You've waived counsel. Is that correct, Mr. Lockridge?"

"It is."

"You understand the interior has no prison that would hold you and no appetite to build one."

"I understand," Oscar said, "that the river keeps its own records."

Ilsa's hands tightened around the compass. She had testified an hour ago — about the *Cormorant*, about the night Oscar had come to her, half-drowned and incoherent, and told her a cargo net had failed, that the ferry had gone down with three souls aboard because a load master had stacked it wrong and the company had looked away. About how she had believed him and then, when the warrant came, had watched him choose the fog over her. The magistrate had listened the way river people listen — patiently, weighing everything, believing nothing that couldn't be drowned.

Tomas Reyes had testified before her. He had stood at the cannery's old gutting table, now draped in the flag of a nation that no longer existed, and he had read aloud from the Meridian's manifest for forty minutes without stopping. Not the treasury columns — the other pages, the sealed state records, the ledgers of a government that had shipped its own people downriver as cargo and called it provisioning. He had published all of it in the weeks since, mimeographed on the trading post's ancient press, and the copies had moved upriver faster than any salvage fleet could ever have moved. Every settlement radio had read them aloud. Every settlement had already known. That was the justice of the interior: not a verdict handed down but a verdict handed around, until it weighed so much no one could carry it alone.

The magistrate set down his papers.

"Mr. Lockridge. The court finds that the sinking of the *Cormorant* was caused by the negligence of the Meridian Shipping Concern, a corporation now dissolved and beyond the reach of any court. The court further finds that you, at nineteen years of age, reported that negligence, were ignored, and have spent seven years paying a debt that was never yours." He paused. "The court also finds that you fled. That is not nothing. A man who runs from a charge he did not commit still leaves the family of the drowned without an answer for seven years. Do you understand that?"

Oscar's voice came out low. "Yes, sir. I've been carrying it the whole way."

"Then the court sentences you to two years' service, served where you stand: medevac flights out of Kettle Landing, provision runs to the settlements, at the discretion of the river council. You'll fly when they tell you to fly and land where they tell you to land." The magistrate almost smiled. "Which, I'm given to understand, is what you were doing anyway."

There was laughter in the cannery — the dry, surprised laughter of people who had come expecting a hanging and gotten a homecoming instead. Ilsa did not laugh. She watched Oscar's shoulders drop half an inch, watched him close his eyes for one breath, two, and when he opened them he looked — she searched for the word and found it and was almost embarrassed by it — *landed*. Like a plane that had finally found ground that would hold it.

* * *

The council gave him the old mail plane, the one with the fabric patches and the engine that sang a half-tone flat, and they gave him a schedule, and the schedule was the punishment and the gift both. Every Tuesday he flew the upper route: Salt Lick, Ferrow's Bend, the mission at Cold Harbor. Every Friday the lower: Kettle Landing's own clinic, the oyster farms, the leper light at the river's mouth.

Ilsa went with him on the first Friday. She told herself it was to inspect the route, fleet business, salvage charts that needed updating now that the flood had redrawn half the map. She told herself a lot of things in those weeks. Her fleet sat at anchor off Kettle Landing, half her crews dismissed, the salvage contracts void now that the Meridian's secret belonged to everyone and therefore to no one. There was nothing left to hunt. She had not yet decided whether that was a grief or a relief, and she suspected it was both, and she suspected it would be both for a long time.

Oscar flew the way he had always flown, which was to say the plane became an extension of his hands and the river a language he read without translation. But something in the flying had changed. He did not cut the corners. He did not skim the fog looking for the gap that would let him through ahead of everyone. When the wind came up the wrong way at Ferrow's Bend, he circled twice and landed long on the second approach, and Ilsa realized she was watching a man who had stopped treating every landing as an escape.

At Cold Harbor they took on a girl with a fever — the same village, Ilsa realized, that had once offered to hide him if he'd fly their sick out first. The girl's grandmother pressed a sack of dried fish into Oscar's hands and would not take no. "You stopped for her," the old woman said, in the flat way of people who state facts rather than offer gratitude. "The river remembers who stops."

On the flight back, with the girl sleeping in the cargo sling and the sun going down the color of hammered copper, Oscar spoke for the first time in an hour.

"I used to think the provision was the cargo," he said. "Medicine, mail, flour. Things that keep people alive." He glanced over at her, then back at the river. "Now I think it's the stopping. The landing. Anybody can move cargo. The hard part is being somewhere when the somewhere needs you."

"That's the most words you've strung together since the courthouse."

"I'm practicing. The council says I have to attend a settlement meeting once a month. Speaking in front of people." He shuddered theatrically, and she laughed, and the laugh surprised them both, hanging there in the cockpit like something they'd have to decide what to do with.

Below them, the Meridian's sandbar had gone under again with the spring water. The river had taken back its chest, its records, its dead. Tomas had dived the site one last time in September, alone, and come up with nothing but a hull plate, which he'd had cut into small squares and mailed to every settlement that had lost someone to the state ships. Ilsa kept hers in the same pocket as her father's compass — the two pieces of metal that stood, between them, for everything she'd spent ten years getting wrong and right.

"Ilsa." Oscar's voice had changed. Quieter. "The council asked me yesterday if I'd take the upper route permanent. Year-round. It means a hangar at Kettle Landing. It means—" He stopped, restarted. "It means I'd be a fixture. You know what I used to say about fixtures."

"You said they were things the river eventually swallowed."

"I've revised my opinion of the river."

She looked at him — at the profile she had memorized at nineteen and mourned at twenty-four and was only now, at last, seeing whole. There was no fog in him anymore. That was the strange thing. She had spent years angry at the man who vanished into weather, and the man sitting beside her was clear all the way down, and it turned out that was harder to be angry at, and easier to love, and she was tired of pretending those were separate calculations.

"Take the route," she said. "Take the hangar. Take the whole damn river."

"And you?"

She pulled her father's compass from her pocket and set it on the console between them, its needle swinging, settling, pointing the way compasses do when they've finally stopped being argued with.

"The fleet's becoming a survey company. Charts, medevac support, ferry service. Somebody has to keep the salvage captains honest, and I'm told I'm very intimidating." She let the corner of her mouth lift. "I'll be at Kettle Landing on Fridays."

The plane droned on into the copper light. Below, the river ran wide and unhurried, carrying its silt and its secrets and its small boats home, asking nothing of them, forgiving everything, keeping its own records in its own time. Somewhere under that water lay a ship that had sung once, terribly, and now sang quiet. Somewhere behind them lay a courthouse that smelled of brine. And ahead — ahead there was only the strip at Kettle Landing, the lamps already lit, the whole settlement turned out to watch, because in the interior a landing was never just a landing. It was a promise, kept in public, that a man had decided where he belonged.

Oscar brought the plane down soft as a held breath. He taxied to the tie-downs, killed the engine, and sat for a moment in the sudden silence with his hands still on the yoke, as if listening for something.

"What is it?" Ilsa asked.

He shook his head slowly. "Nothing. That's just it. For the first time in seven years, there's nowhere I need to be, and no one I need to hide from, and nothing—" He stopped, and she watched him find the word the way he'd find a landing strip in fog: carefully, and all the way. "Nothing singing at me to move."

They climbed down onto the gravel together, into the smell of woodsmoke and the sound of the settlement gathering, and the river went on singing its quiet provision — not the song of the wreck, not the song of the running, but the small, patient song of water that lets you land, and lets you stay, and remembers, always, who stopped.
