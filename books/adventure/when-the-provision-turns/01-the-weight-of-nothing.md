## 1. The Weight of Nothing

The thief left Marrowgate an hour before dawn, and Kestrel Thornbury picked up his trail a half hour after that, which meant the man had a head start and Kestrel had a temper. Neither would matter by dusk.

He crouched where the road gave out into open salt and read the ground the way other men read letters. Two sets of prints — one old, crusted white with mineral bloom, one fresh, the edges still sharp as cut leather. The fresh set was narrow, shallow-heeled, weighted oddly toward the outside of each foot. A man favoring his ankles. A man not used to walking far.

*Good,* Kestrel thought, and felt nothing else about it at all.

That was the trick of tracking that no one taught you in the guild: you had to be empty. Not patient — patience was a virtue for people who could afford it — but *empty*, hollowed out like a gourd, so that the land could pour itself into you. Every rock, every crack in the salt crust, every wind-scoured ripple of sand had to be allowed to speak without your own noise drowning it out. Worry made trackers deaf. Debt made them deaf too, and Kestrel carried enough debt to fill a grain silo, so he did what he always did when he needed to work: he folded it up small and put it away in the back room of himself, behind a door he pretended wasn't there.

The Saltreach flats opened before him, pale as a dead man's palm, going on until the horizon bent them into a shimmering line where sky and salt became the same argument. To the north, the faint blue suggestion of the Verrin Ridge. To the south, the drowned country, where the old canyons held seawater and secrets in equal measure. And straight ahead, small and stubborn, a figure moving with the particular hunched determination of a man who believed distance was safety.

Kestrel straightened, rolled his shoulders until something cracked, and began to walk.

Not run. Running on the flats was a way to die tired. He set a pace he could hold from now until the sun went down and let the arithmetic do its quiet work. The thief was making maybe three miles an hour over broken crust, stopping to rest because men who weren't used to the salt always stopped to rest. Kestrel could make four without breaking sweat before noon. By midday the gap would be closed to a mile. By late afternoon, close enough to see the color of the man's eyes.

The guild called this *walking a man down*, and there were rangers famous for it, rangers whose names got spoken around wellheads with a kind of superstitious respect. Kestrel had never been famous. Seven years ago he'd been on his way — young, licensed, with a reputation for finding things in country where finding things got people killed — and then Alden had gone missing with the Provision expedition, and Kestrel's reputation had gone missing with him, because grief is a kind of incompetence and everyone knows it. After that came the bad contracts, the good-faith loans that turned out to be neither, and Doss.

He put the thought back behind the door. The door was getting heavy. He noticed that, filed it away, kept walking.

* * *

By noon the salt had begun to cook. Heat stood up off the crust in visible columns, bending the world, so that the thief ahead of him seemed to waver like a reflection in disturbed water. Kestrel chewed a strip of dried cactus fruit — sour, fibrous, the only food the flats gave away free — and drank two measured swallows from his canteen. Two swallows, no more. The water in that canteen was the most expensive thing he owned, bought on credit at four times its worth, and he rationed it the way misers rationed coin, except that water was worth more than coin out here and everyone knew that too.

The tracks told him stories as he followed them. The thief had stopped here, sat down — the print of his seat in the crust, already crumbling. Rested longer than he should have. Here he'd cut left toward a low ridge of crusted dune, looking for shade that didn't exist, then cut back, having learned the lesson. Here — Kestrel knelt, touched the ground — he'd dropped something and picked it up again. A small scatter of crumbs, sweetish. Bread. So he had food. That narrowed things. Men who stole bread were a different animal than men who stole water, and Kestrel dealt with them differently.

Except the crumbs were old. Days old, baked hard, blown half clear. The bread was gone. Which meant the man was running on an empty stomach across forty miles of salt, and either he didn't know what that cost or he knew exactly what it cost and had decided being caught was worse.

Kestrel had opinions about that. He kept them to himself and walked.

The hours stacked up like stones. His shadow shrank to a puddle under his boots and then began to stretch east again, thinning into evening. The light went from white to brass to a deep bruised amber, and the flats changed character with it — softer, almost kind, the heat lifting off them like a hand withdrawn. This was the hour Kestrel loved, had always loved, even now, even broke. The desert forgave nothing but it apologized beautifully, twice a day, in color.

The gap had closed the way arithmetic promised. He could see the man clearly now: medium height, dark clothes gone grey with dust, a pack riding high and light on his shoulders. Light pack. Whatever he'd stolen, he hadn't taken much of it. Or he'd cached the bulk somewhere and was carrying only what he needed, which would make him smarter than most thieves and harder to recover from.

Kestrel stopped, unslung his own pack, and ate the rest of the cactus fruit sitting right there in the open, in full view, making no effort to hide. Let the man look back and see him. Let him do the same arithmetic. A tracker who hid was a tracker who feared the outcome; a tracker who sat and ate told you everything you needed to know about how the day would end.

The figure ahead stopped. Turned. Stood still for a long moment — Kestrel imagined him squinting through the heat-shimmer, doing his own sums — and then, instead of running, did the last thing Kestrel expected.

He sat down. Right there on the salt, cross-legged, and waited.

* * *

"Well," Kestrel said, twenty minutes later, standing over him. "You're either brave or finished."

"Both," the man said. He was younger than the walk had made him look — late twenties, maybe, with a face the sun had been working on for years and a beard grown in patches, the beard of a man who'd stopped caring in stages. His lips were cracked white. His hands, resting open on his knees, trembled slightly, and not from fear. "You're the ranger."

"I'm a ranger."

"They said you'd come. At the well camp, they said if anyone tracked me, it'd be Thornbury." He looked up, and his eyes were fever-bright and steady at the same time, a combination Kestrel had seen before and never learned to be comfortable with. "I'm not going to run. I couldn't if I wanted to. I've got maybe a mile of running left in me and I'd rather spend it on something useful."

"That's the smartest thing anyone's said to me all week." Kestrel lowered himself onto a slab of crusted salt across from the man, keeping his knees loose, his weight forward. Habit. "Name."

"Tolliver. Just Tolliver." He swallowed. His throat clicked. "You're going to want to know what I took."

"It crossed my mind."

"Then ask."

Kestrel studied him. The pack sat between them, small and shapeless. Around them the flats ran out to the edge of the world, empty as a swept floor, and the sun was going down in bands of copper and violet, and somewhere behind them both, thirty miles back, Marrowgate sat in its shallow bowl of mudbrick and debt with its lamps coming on.

"Here's how this goes," Kestrel said. "You tell me what you took and from whom. If it's coin, you give it back and I take you in and you work it off in the salt quarries, which is ugly but survivable. If it's goods, same. If it's—" he paused, because he'd seen it before, more than once, and it never got easier— "if it's water, that's different law. Water theft goes to the magistrate, and the magistrate hangs it. You know that."

"I know that." Tolliver's voice didn't waver. His hands did. "Ask me anyway."

"What did you take?"

Tolliver reached over, slow, deliberate, and pulled the pack around. He loosened the drawstring and tipped it. Out slid a waterskin. One waterskin, scuffed, patched at the seam with tar and thread, holding — Kestrel judged by the sag and slosh — perhaps three quarts.

Three quarts. Enough to keep a man alive eleven days in the cool season, six in the hot. Not enough to sell. Not enough to be worth the walk, let alone the risk. Three quarts was not cargo. Three quarts was a life, measured out in sips.

"Whose?" Kestrel asked quietly.

"The well camp. The Corvane survey camp, up past the north rim." Tolliver's voice had gone flat, recitation-flat, the voice of a man who'd rehearsed this in his head for forty miles. "They struck a seep two weeks back. Good flow, clean water. They fenced it. Put a guard on it. Their own people drink free, and the surrounding camps — the diggers, the widows' camp, the kids — they pay. Four bits a quart. Four bits." He laughed, and there was nothing in it. "I worked their trench line for six days for a quart and a promise. Then I filled this at night and I walked."

"You stole from a company camp," Kestrel said. "That's not petty theft. That's theft from a chartered interest. The guild contract says—"

"I know what the guild contract says." For the first time the man's voice rose, cracked, came back down. "I've heard what the guild contract says my whole life. I'm asking you what *you* say. They said you were fair. They said—" he stopped, and his fever-bright eyes did something complicated, "—they said you were the one who let Marta Senn go. Two years back. When she took the goat."

Kestrel said nothing. The wind moved across the flats, carrying salt dust in long low ropes that hissed against the crust.

Marta Senn. A widow with two children and a dying mother-in-law, who'd taken a neighbor's goat in the night and butchered it, and Kestrel had caught her at dawn with blood on her skirts and her whole future hanging off her shoulders like wet wool. She'd offered him everything she had, which was nothing. And he'd stood there in the pink early light with the law in one hand and the look on her children's faces in the other, and he'd written in his report that the goat had been taken by drift scavengers, and he'd hated himself for a month, and he'd do it again tomorrow. Some debts you paid in coin. Some you paid in lies. He'd never figured out which kind cost more.

"Marta had children," he said finally.

"So does the widows' camp." Tolliver lifted the waterskin and held it out, an offering, a surrender, a dare. "There's a girl there, eight years old, carrying water four miles a day from the deep well because her mother's lungs are shot. Four bits a quart, ranger. Do the sums. The Corvane men bathe in what she can't afford to drink." His arm shook with the weight of three quarts. "So. What do you say?"

The sun touched the horizon. The flats went the color of an old wound, then the color of a new one, then dark.

* * *

They sat in the blue afterlight while Kestrel thought, and the thinking was not easy, and he did not pretend otherwise.

Here was the shape of it, laid out plain: he owed Doss one hundred and forty marks, due in ten days, secured against his ranger's license. No license, no legal standing. No legal standing, no contracts. No contracts, no income. It was a machine with one output and it ground fine. He had spent seven years feeding it — honest work, hard work, work that paid a third of what it cost — and the debt had not shrunk so much as a single mark, because Doss's interest grew faster than any honest labor could harvest, and Kestrel had understood this for years the way you understand a tide: completely, and without being able to do anything about it.

A man in that position could not afford mercy. Mercy was expensive. Mercy was the luxury of men whose accounts balanced.

And here was the other shape of it: a hundred and forty marks was nothing. Less than nothing. It was a number in a ledger in a counting house in a town that would be dust in fifty years, and out here, under the first stars, on a salt flat that had been killing and sparing men since before anyone invented ledgers, the number weighed exactly what Kestrel decided it weighed. Nothing. Everything. Both. The flats didn't care. The flats had watched a thousand men choose wrong and a thousand men choose right and had buried them all in the same white silence.

"You're not going to take me in," Tolliver said. It wasn't a question. He'd read something in the silence.

"Don't tell me my business."

"You sat down when I offered you the skin. A man planning to arrest me takes the skin. Evidence." Tolliver almost smiled. Almost. "You just looked at it like it was the saddest thing you ever saw."

Kestrel looked at the waterskin lying in the salt between them, catching the starlight. Three quarts. Somewhere behind them, a girl of eight was asleep or not asleep, dreaming of water or of nothing, and tomorrow she'd shoulder her yoke and walk her four miles, and some chartered interest would stand between her thirst and the seep her grandfather might have drunk from free.

"Here's what happens," Kestrel said. "You don't go back to the widows' camp. Corvane will have hired trackers by now, and they hire the kind who don't ask questions, and if they find you near those people, those people pay for it. You understand?"

Tolliver nodded slowly.

"You go south. There's a well crew working the old Verrin cut, two days' walk. Decent people. They take strays if you'll haul rope. Tell them Thornbury sent you — that name's still worth something there, God knows why." He paused. "And you leave the skin."

Tolliver's head came up. "That's—"

"That's mine to carry back. That's the part that keeps this honest. I return the water, I report a recovered theft, nobody gets hung, nobody gets searched." He held out his hand. "It's not charity. It's bookkeeping. I hate sloppy books."

For a moment the younger man just looked at him, and Kestrel watched him try to find the trap in it, because men who'd been kicked enough times went looking for traps in kindness the way other men went looking for water. Then Tolliver set the skin in his hand, and his fingers stayed on it a second longer than they needed to, and he said, "Why? Why are you like this? Everyone says you're fair, but fair costs you. I can see it costs you. Why do you pay it?"

Kestrel stood. His knees ached. Forty miles of salt in a day would do that, and tomorrow he'd have to walk forty back, and the day after that he'd sit across a table from Doss with ten days on the clock and one hundred and forty marks of nothing in his pocket, and the door at the back of himself where he kept the debt was groaning on its hinges.

"Because somebody paid it for me once," he said, "and I never got the chance to pay it back. So I pay it sideways. It doesn't balance. Nothing balances." He slung the waterskin from his belt, next to his own canteen, and found that its small weight bothered him more than any burden he'd carried all day. "Go on, now. South. Walk at night, sleep in the day. And Tolliver—"

The man had already risen, shouldering his empty pack. He stopped.

"When you get to the Verrin cut, and someone asks what happened out here — and someone will — you tell them the ranger took the water back and let you go because you begged." Kestrel met his eyes. "Tell them I was hard about it. Tell them I made you cry. Pride is cheap and it keeps better than water. Don't spend my reputation buying yours."

Tolliver looked at him for a long moment in the dark. Then he nodded once, turned south, and walked, and inside a hundred yards the night had him, and it was as if the flats had opened and closed around him like water.

* * *

Kestrel stood alone under the stars for a while, listening to the salt tick and settle as it cooled. Then he turned north, toward Marrowgate and its lamps and its ledgers, and began the long walk home with two skins at his belt — one nearly empty, one full of stolen water that was going to cost him more than it was worth, the way everything did.

He was three miles along, the lights of Marrowgate just beginning to glimmer on the horizon like embers raked flat, when he admitted the thing he'd been refusing to admit since noon:

Ten days. One hundred and forty marks. And the only thing he owned that Doss wanted was his father's compass, and the only thing he owned that *he* wanted was the same compass, and only one of them could keep it.

Behind him, far to the south, the flats lay silent and white under the moon, holding nothing, giving nothing, forgiving nothing.

Ahead, the lamps of Marrowgate burned like a creditor's smile.

Kestrel walked on.
