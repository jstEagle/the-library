## 2. A Compass and a Creditor

Marrowgate smelled of brine and bad decisions, the way it always did at the end of a dry season. The town sat where the Saltreach flats gave up their pretense of emptiness and became something worse: civilization. Kestrel rode in with the last light behind him, his shadow stretched long across the packed-salt road like a man trying to leave himself behind.

He was tired down to the marrow. Three days tracking a thief who'd turned out to be nothing but a thirsty boy with an empty canteen, and now he had nothing to show for it but dust in his teeth and a debt that hadn't shrunk by so much as a single coin. His ranger's pay for the season — what little the guild had scraped together — was already spoken for. It had been spoken for before he'd earned it. That was the arithmetic of Doss Merrow: every coin Kestrel made arrived already dead, assigned to some past mistake before it could warm his palm.

The town gate stood open, as it always did. Marrowgate didn't believe in closing itself off; it believed in letting people in so they could be fleeced at leisure. Lamplighters were working the main street with long poles, and the smell of frying onions drifted from the Gullet, the tavern where Kestrel's room waited — if you could call a cot behind a curtain a room. He passed the well at the crossroads, its rope worn smooth by ten thousand desperate hands, and he thought again of the boy on the flats, drinking water that wasn't even his own theft. There was a lesson in that somewhere. Kestrel didn't have the energy to find it.

The counting house of Doss Merrow occupied the finest building in Marrowgate, which told you everything about the town's priorities. It had been a granary once, back when Marrowgate had grain to store, and its thick stone walls and iron-banded door now served a different kind of hunger. Above the entrance, someone had carved a set of scales into the lintel — old work, older than Doss — and beneath them a motto in a language nobody spoke anymore. Kestrel had asked a guild scholar about it once. *Weigh all things,* the scholar had translated. *Forgive none.*

Kestrel tied his horse — a swaybacked roan named Cinder who deserved better than him — outside the counting house and stood a moment in the street, working up the particular courage that this building required. It wasn't the courage of rangers or fighters. It was the courage of a man walking into a room knowing exactly how much he was worth, and having to hear it said aloud.

Inside, the air was cool and dry and smelled of ink and lamp oil. Ledgers lined the walls from floor to ceiling, thousands of them, each one a life bound in calfskin. A clerk looked up from a high desk, took in Kestrel's salt-crusted coat and road-worn face, and made a small sound of recognition that might have been pity. He disappeared through a back door without a word.

Kestrel waited. That was part of it too. Doss never kept a man waiting long enough for anger to build; he kept him waiting just long enough for the anger to curdle into something more useful — dread, mostly, and the dull shame of being known as a debtor. On the walls hung framed documents: deeds to wells, bills of sale for water rights, a charter signed by the rangers' guild authorizing Doss Merrow as an official creditor to guild members. Even the institution meant to protect men like Kestrel had sold them a rope and called it a lifeline.

The back door opened. Doss Merrow came through it wiping his hands on a linen cloth, as though he'd just finished eating something delicate.

He was a small man, which always surprised people who'd only heard of him. Neat, gray-bearded, with spectacles perched on a nose like a knuckle. He dressed plainly, expensively. His eyes were pale and quick and entirely without malice, which was somehow worse than cruelty. Cruelty wanted something from you. Doss only wanted what was owed, and he wanted it the way rain wanted the ground.

"Ranger Thornbury." He said the name the way a man reads a line item. "You're back early. I had you on the flats until week's end."

"The trail went cold."

"Trails do." He gestured toward his office, a small room dominated by a desk of black ironwood and a single window looking out on the scales carved above his own door. He liked that view. Kestrel had watched him enjoy it before. "Sit."

Kestrel sat. The chair was deliberately uncomfortable — he'd figured that out years ago, the way the seat pitched a man slightly forward, toward the desk, toward supplication. He refused to lean forward. He sat back, arms crossed, and let the discomfort be his.

Doss opened a ledger without hurry. His finger traveled down a column of figures, and Kestrel watched him read his life in numbers: the seed loan after the drought year, the gear replacement when the sandstorm ate his kit, the interest compounding quietly through seasons when the guild paid late. Every entry was true. That was the trap of Doss Merrow — he never lied about a debt. Lies could be argued with. Arithmetic couldn't.

"Two hundred and forty marks," Doss said. "Plus the seasonal service charge, which brings us to two hundred sixty-one and change. Say two hundred sixty-two, and we'll call the change my gift." He looked up over his spectacles. "Your guild stipend this quarter is forty marks."

"I know what my stipend is."

"Then you know the gap." Doss closed the ledger gently, the way a man closes a coffin lid. "Here's where we've arrived, Ranger. I've carried you for three years. I've been patient because you're reliable — you pay when you can, you don't run, you don't drink your debts away like half the guild. In this town, that makes you nearly virtuous." He smiled faintly. "But patience is also an investment, and investments are expected to return. The guild charter gives me certain rights when a member defaults. You know what they are."

"Forfeit the license," Kestrel said flatly. "Sell it to cover the balance."

"Sell it," Doss agreed, "to whoever bids highest. And there's no shortage of men who want a ranger's license, Thornbury. Men with capital and no scruples about how they use it. A license in the wrong hands is worth more than in the right ones — that's not cynicism, that's just the market." He folded his hands. "So. Ten days. Bring me two hundred sixty-two marks by the tenth day, or the license comes to me, and you go back to whatever you were before the guild took you in."

Kestrel stared at him. "Ten days."

"You'll notice I'm not asking for it today. That's generosity. Some of my colleagues would have taken the license while you were still on the flats."

*Some of your colleagues would at least let a man keep his dignity.* But dignity wasn't legal tender in Marrowgate, and Kestrel knew it. He stood, slow, keeping his voice level because level was all he had left. "I'll get the money."

"How?" Doss asked, genuinely curious, the way a naturalist might wonder how a fish breathes. "You have no salvage claims pending. No bounty contracts. Your gear — what remains of it — wouldn't fetch fifty marks, and you need it to earn the rest. Which leaves..." He tilted his head, almost kindly. "What does that leave, Ranger?"

Kestrel didn't answer. They both knew what it left. It left the thing in his coat pocket, the weight he'd carried against his ribs for eleven years, through every storm and dry camp and lonely mile of the range. He walked out without another word, because walking out was the only move on the board that felt like his own.

* * *

His room at the Gullet was four paces by three, divided from the hallway by a curtain that had once been white. He lit the stub of candle on the sill and sat on the cot, and for a long time he did nothing but listen to the tavern noise below — dice, laughter, someone badly mangling a ballad about the drowned coast. Sounds from a world where money moved hand to hand in small, honest amounts.

Finally he took out the compass.

It was a heavy thing, brass-cased, dented along one edge where it had taken a bullet — his father's story, not his; the old man had told it a hundred times, and the telling grew grander with each retelling until the bullet was the size of a fist and the distance it had been fired from grew to a hundred yards. The lid bore scratches that might have been initials under all the wear. Inside, the needle floated on its pin, steady and sure, pointing north with the calm certainty of something that had never owed anyone anything.

His father had been a ranger too, back when the guild was younger and the range was wilder. He'd died on it — not heroically, just thoroughly, caught in a flash flood in the drowned canyons south of the Reach. They'd brought Kestrel his effects in a canvas bag: a knife worn to a sliver, a hat gone stiff with sweat and sun, and the compass. Eleven years old, holding a dead man's instrument, Kestrel had decided the compass was the whole of his inheritance, and in a way he'd been right. Everything else his father had left him — the name, the reputation, the stubbornness — had been spent down over the years, the way a family estate gets sold room by room. Only the compass remained, and now a man with pale eyes and clean fingernails was asking, politely, whether Kestrel intended to keep it.

He opened the case and watched the needle tremble, settle, point. North. Always north, no matter where the rest of the world had gotten to. He thought about what the compass would bring at market. Fine brass, old make, the works still true. Maybe thirty marks. Forty if some collector fell in love with it. Against two hundred sixty-two, it was a cup of water thrown at a house fire.

And yet.

It wasn't about the marks. Kestrel understood that with a clarity that felt like cold water. Selling the compass wouldn't save the license — the numbers didn't work, and Doss knew they didn't, which meant Doss wasn't really after the compass at all. Doss was after the surrender. He wanted Kestrel to come to him stripped of the last thing that mattered, so that the next loan — the big one, the ruinous one, the kind that bought a man's future outright — would feel like rescue instead of what it was. That was how small tyrants worked. They didn't take everything at once. They took things in order, saving the soul for last, because the soul fetched the best price.

Kestrel closed the compass and held it in his fist, feeling the dents and the worn brass, all the miles his father's thumb had rubbed into it. *Not yet,* he told it. *Not while I'm breathing.*

Below, the ballad ended and someone demanded another round. Kestrel blew out the candle and lay back on the cot with the compass on his chest, rising and falling with his breath, and tried to think of a way to raise two hundred sixty-two marks in ten days that didn't end with him owned.

There wasn't one. He fell asleep knowing it, and slept anyway, because exhaustion doesn't negotiate.

* * *

Morning came gray and salt-hazed. Kestrel spent it doing what desperate men do: arithmetic in public. He walked the market row and priced his gear honestly — the good boots, forty marks if the buyer was drunk; the long rifle, sixty, and then he'd be a ranger with no rifle, which was a joke with a punchline everyone in Marrowgate had heard before. The guild advance office offered loans against future stipends at rates that made Doss look like a charity. The bounty board held nothing worth more than twenty marks inside ten days unless a man wanted to hunt the deep flats in storm season, and the flats collected those debts permanently.

By noon he was back at the Gullet, nursing a cup of chicory he couldn't afford, when the tavern door swung open and let in the heat and a figure wrapped in a dust-colored cloak.

Kestrel knew the walk before he saw the face. He'd know it anywhere — that rolling, hip-loose stride that treated every street like a stage it had personally hired. Seven years since he'd seen her, and Sable Vane still entered a room like she was collecting it.

She spotted him instantly. Of course she did. She crossed the tavern floor through the smoke and the dice-players, pulled out the chair across from him, and sat down uninvited, pushing back her hood to reveal a face older than he remembered and no softer for it. Her hair was cropped short now, silvering at the temples, and there was a new scar along her jaw. But the eyes were the same: green and quick and always three moves ahead, and always enjoying the game more than any decent person should.

"Hello, Kestrel," she said. "You look terrible."

"You look expensive."

"That's the idea." She signaled the barkeep with two fingers, ordered something amber, and turned back to him with a smile that had edges. "I hear you're in trouble. Small town. Word travels."

"Word travels fast when the creditor talks."

"Doss." She wrinkled her nose. "Ugh. He's got you on the clock, doesn't he? Ten days?"

Kestrel said nothing, which was answer enough.

Sable leaned forward, lowering her voice, and for a moment — just a moment — the performance dropped and something almost human looked out at him. Almost. "I have a job," she said. "A real one. Big enough to clear your books twice over and buy you a new life besides. And I came here first. Before anyone else. Before Corvane, before the guild brokers, before all of them. Because despite everything—" she paused on the word, letting it carry seven years of wreckage between them— "you're the best guide on the Reach, and I don't trust anyone else to get my crew there alive."

"What's the target?"

She told him one word, and the word landed in Kestrel's chest like a stone dropped down a well.

*Provision.*

The tavern noise seemed to recede. The chicory went cold in his cup. Somewhere far away, dice rattled and a woman laughed, and Kestrel Thornbury sat very still, staring at the woman who had ruined him, hearing the name of the ship his brother had died chasing.

"No," he said.

"You haven't heard the terms."

"It doesn't matter what the terms are." He stood, chair scraping, and the compass pressed its familiar dent against his ribs like a warning. "Find another guide."

"Kestrel." Sable stayed seated, unhurried, watching him with those green eyes. "Ten days. Two hundred sixty-two marks. What exactly is your alternative?"

He had no answer, and she knew it, and the worst part — the part that would keep him awake that night, staring at the ceiling while the compass rose and fell on his chest — was that she'd known it before she ever walked through the door.
