## 3. Sable Vane Returns

The letter came at dawn, delivered by a boy on a mule who wouldn't come past the gate.

Kestrel read it standing in the doorway of his rented room above the farrier's shop in Marrowgate, the paper thin as moth wing, the ink pressed hard enough to score through from the other side. He knew the hand before he read a word — that sharp, slanted script that cut its letters like it was angry at them.

*Thornbury. I'm at the Salt Cellar until the second bell. Come hear me out or spend the rest of your life pretending you didn't get this. — S.V.*

He read it twice. Then he held it over the little iron stove until the corner caught, watched the flame crawl up toward her name, and thought better of it at the last moment, pinching the fire out between thumb and forefinger. The scorched edge curled like a dead leaf. He set what remained on the table, next to the compass, and looked at both for a long time.

Seven years. Seven years since the Dry Fork inquiry, since the guild tribunal where Sable Vane had stood before the assessors with her chin high and her evidence neatly bound and told them, in that clear carrying voice of hers, exactly how Ranger Kestrel Thornbury had misread the water signs and led three head of stock and one man into a dying sinkhole. She hadn't lied, exactly. That was the thing about Sable that had always made her dangerous. She never lied. She just arranged the truth the way a jeweler arranges stones — to catch the light she wanted and hide every flaw behind a setting.

The man had lived. Kestrel had pulled him out himself, half-drowned, coughing brine. But the tribunal hadn't cared about the rescue. They cared about the error, and the error had a name attached to it, and the name was his. His license had been suspended for two years. His contracts had dried up after that — rangers were like wells; once one went bad, nobody trusted the water again no matter how sweet it came up after.

And now she was sitting in the Salt Cellar drinking Marrowgate's worst beer, waiting for him with an apology she'd taken seven years to write and probably hadn't written at all.

Kestrel dressed slowly. He put on the good coat, the one without the burn holes, and then took it off again and put on the work coat, because if this was going to be what he suspected it was, she could look at the frayed cuffs and know exactly what her testimony had cost him. Petty? Maybe. But pride was the only currency he had left in any quantity, and he wasn't spending it on Sable Vane's comfort.

* * *

The Salt Cellar at midmorning was mostly empty — dockhands sleeping off night shifts in the corners, a card game going soft and bitter by the window. The room smelled of spilled beer and old salt, and the light through the glass-block windows came down greenish, like being underwater. Fitting, Kestrel thought, for whatever she was about to propose.

She'd taken the back table, the one with sight lines to both doors. Of course she had. Some habits you don't grow out of; you just grow around them, the way a tree grows around a fence wire.

Sable Vane looked older. Not worn — sharpened. Her hair was cut shorter than he remembered, iron-gray coming in at the temples where it used to be all black, and there were new lines around her mouth that might have been carved by weather or by laughter, though knowing Sable he'd have bet on weather. She wore a salvage captain's longcoat, good leather, Corvane-made buckles, and a signet ring he didn't recognize on her right hand. She'd done well for herself. She'd done well for herself the way a knife does well in a drawer — kept sharp, kept oiled, kept out of sight until needed.

"Kestrel." She said his name like she was testing the weight of it. "You look terrible."

"You look rich." He sat down across from her without being asked. "That's usually worse."

A ghost of a smile crossed her face and was gone. She pushed a cup toward him. "Beer here's undrinkable."

"I know. I've been drinking it for seven years."

Something flickered behind her eyes at that — not guilt, he decided. Recognition. Guilt would have been too much to hope for.

"Straight to it, then," she said, and produced a folded document from inside her coat, laying it on the scarred wood between them but keeping two fingers on it, the way gamblers keep fingers on their stake. "I need a guide. The best one I can buy. And everyone I ask says the same name, and they say it the way people say the names of dead men."

"Who's everyone?"

"People who matter." She leaned forward slightly. "Six tides out, northeast across the Reach, down through the drowned canyon country to the Shallows. There's a wreck out there, Kestrel. A big one. Supply ship, pre-war hull, went down with everything aboard."

The words landed in his chest like stones dropped down a well. He heard them fall a long way before they stopped.

"The Provision," he said.

Her eyebrows rose a fraction. "You know it."

"Everyone knows it. It's a story we tell children so they'll stop asking why the flat is empty." He kept his voice level, which cost him something. "Ship full of water-rites and minted coin, enough to buy a city. And every crew that's gone looking has come back short a few men or hasn't come back at all. That's not treasure, Sable. That's a grave with rumors."

"It's a grave with coordinates." She tapped the document under her fingers. "New ones. Verified twice."

"Verified by whom?"

"By people who are no longer available for comment," she said dryly, "but whose instruments were very good."

Kestrel sat back. Outside, a cart rattled past, and somewhere a well-chain clanked its slow bucket-music. Ordinary sounds. The sounds of a town that did not know what she was about to do to him.

"No," he said.

"You haven't heard the number yet."

"I don't need to. The answer's the same at any price." He stood, pushing the chair back. "Find another guide."

"Sit down, Kestrel."

There it was — the old tone, the tribunal voice, the one that had made grown assessors sit straighter in their chairs. He hated that part of him that still responded to it, the part that straightened like a dog hearing its old master's whistle. He stayed standing.

"Seven years ago you took my name into a tribunal room and broke it over your knee," he said quietly. "You did it with documents. You did it politely. You did it *correctly*, which is the part that still keeps me up some nights — because if you'd lied, I could hate you clean. Instead you told the truth in the shape most useful to you, and I lost two years of license and five years of reputation, and you got promoted off my ruin. So no, Sable. Whatever's in that envelope, whatever number you're carrying, it doesn't buy my morning, let alone six tides of my life."

She didn't flinch. He'd forgotten how completely she refused to flinch. Instead she turned the document around and slid it across the table toward him, removing her fingers from it like a dealer revealing the card.

"That's not the offer," she said. "That's the apology."

He looked down. It was a formal instrument, drawn up in guild-legal format, stamped with the seal of the Eastern Assessors' Office. His eyes found the operative line and stopped there as if the words were a wall.

*...formal retraction of testimony given on the fourteenth of Thaw-Month, with acknowledgment of material omission... petition filed for restoration of record...*

"A retraction," he said. His voice sounded strange to him, distant, like someone else speaking through a wall of water.

"With the omissions named. Specifically: that the sinkhole showed drainage patterns consistent with seasonal recharge, which I observed and did not report, because reporting them would have complicated the narrative I'd already committed to." She said it evenly, each word placed like a stone in a cairn. "I've filed it. It's in process. When it clears, your record reads clean."

Kestrel lowered himself back into the chair. His legs made the decision without consulting him.

"Why," he said.

"Because I need you, and I don't hire men who hate me. Men who hate you make mistakes in the field, and mistakes out there—" she nodded toward the east, toward the flats, "—out there, mistakes are measured in crews."

"That's not an apology. That's procurement."

"Yes," she said simply. "It is. But it happens to also be true, and true things are rare enough in my line of work that when I find one, I file it properly." She took a breath, and for just a moment — a half-second he'd have missed if he hadn't spent seven years learning to read her — something moved under the competence, something tired and human. "It should have been filed seven years ago. I knew that seven years ago. I told myself the tribunal outcome wouldn't change, that the man still fell in the hole, that retraction would only muddy my own record. All true. None sufficient. There. Is that enough contrition for you, or do you want me to cry?"

"I want you to tell me why a woman with a Corvane-buckled coat and a salvage fleet at her back needs a disgraced desert ranger badly enough to dig up a seven-year-old tribunal."

For the first time, she hesitated.

It was small. A pause the length of a heartbeat, her thumb moving once along the edge of the table. But Kestrel had hunted men across open salt for a living, and he knew what hesitation looked like on a person who never permitted herself any. It looked like a crack in a dam.

"Because the Provision isn't a story," she said finally. "Or it is, but it's a recent one. The ship went down eleven years ago. Officially it was a grain hauler out of Corvane, lost with all hands in the Shallows during a storm surge. Unofficially—" she glanced around the emptying room, and lowered her voice, "—it was a supply vessel for the Water Compact. Rites, coin, survey equipment. Enough documented water-law in its hold to settle three border disputes and enough coin to make the disputes irrelevant. And eight years ago, a crew went after it. Nine people. Guild-sanctioned expedition, private funded. They had coordinates. Good ones."

Kestrel's hands had gone still on the table. He was aware of his own pulse, slow and heavy, like footsteps in another room.

"They never came back," he said.

"One came back." Sable's voice had dropped almost to nothing. "Half of one. A diver named Corrick, three weeks later, walking out of the Glass Reach with no gear, no boots, and nothing in his head but the same sentence over and over. *The Provision turns. Trust no one who counts.* He died that winter. Fever. Never said another coherent word."

The Salt Cellar seemed very quiet. The card game by the window had ended; the players were gone; the green light lay on the tables like silt settling.

"Eight years ago," Kestrel said. His mouth was dry. "What month?"

"What?"

"What *month*. Eight years ago. When did they go out?"

She frowned, reaching for the answer. "High summer. First tide of—"

"Thaw-Month," he said. "Late Thaw-Month."

"...Yes. Why does that—"

"Because my brother went missing in late Thaw-Month, eight years ago." The words came out of him flat and strange, each one a stone he'd carried so long he'd forgotten it had weight until he set it down. "Alden Thornbury. Salvage rigger, second-class. He signed onto a funded expedition and told our father it was routine contract work. Then he was gone, and the guild records showed the expedition charter sealed, and nobody — *nobody* — would tell me where it had gone or what it was called. Sealed. I spent two years pulling at that seal, Sable. You know what happened when I pulled too hard?"

He watched her face change. Watched her understand, all at once and too late, what she had walked into this morning carrying.

"You were cited for unauthorized records access," she said quietly. "Third strike on your file. Six months before the Dry Fork inquiry."

"And you sat on that tribunal," Kestrel said, "knowing exactly why my file looked the way it did. Knowing I'd been tearing at the edges of a sealed expedition trying to find my brother. And you let them treat me like a ranger with a habit of bad judgment." He laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "You didn't break my name over your knee, Sable. You finished a job someone else started."

"Kestrel—"

"Get out of my way."

She didn't move. Of course she didn't. She leaned forward instead, and now her voice had lost every ounce of the tribunal in it, gone low and fast and honest in the way she only ever got when the stakes finally exceeded her composure.

"He's dead, Kestrel. If he was on that crew, he's been dead eight years, and nothing you do changes that. But the Provision is real, and it's sitting out there in the Shallows holding the only complete account of what that expedition found and why none of them walked out — because Corrick's ravings weren't fever-talk, they were a warning, and I have spent two years and most of my fortune getting verified coordinates to that wreck. I can offer you a share that would clear every debt you have and buy you a range of your own. Or I can offer you the truth about Alden, which is worth more and pays worse. Either way, I need you on my flank when I go get it, because the Corvane fleet is fitting out for the same coordinates as we speak, and their captain is Rell Maddox, and Maddox does not leave witnesses."

The name Maddox meant nothing to him. The rest of it was a roar in his ears, tide-sound, blood-sound.

"Six tides," she said. "One share. One truth. Your choice, Thornbury. But choose today, because the second tide waits for no one, and neither do I."

Kestrel stood. The chair scraped. Somewhere far away, the second bell began to ring, and the sound of it went through him like cold water.

"No," he said.

Sable Vane closed her eyes briefly. When she opened them, the composure was back, seamless as ever, and he understood that he had just seen the last honest thing she planned to show him today.

"I filed the retraction this morning," she said. "It stands whether you sign on or not. Consider it... interest paid on an old debt." She gathered the document, folded it away inside her coat, and rose. At her full height she was nearly as tall as he was, and for a moment they stood close enough that he could smell salt and engine oil on her, the smell of the deep flats, the smell of the place where Alden had gone and not come back.

"For what it's worth," she said, "I'm sorry about your brother. I didn't know."

"You knew the expedition was sealed. Everyone who mattered knew. You just never connected it to the ranger whose ruin you were arranging." He stepped back. "Enjoy Marrowgate, Captain. The beer's terrible and the company's worse."

He walked out through the green underwater light, past the sleeping dockhands, out into the white glare of the street, and the door swung shut behind him with a sound like a verdict.

* * *

He made it four blocks before his legs betrayed him and steered him into the shade of the aqueduct arches, where the town's water sang past overhead in its great iron throat, and he stood with his palms flat against the cool stone pillar like a man steadying himself on a ship's rail.

*Alden.*

Eight years of not-knowing, compressed into ten minutes of knowing-worse. Dead. Eight years dead, out in the Shallows, in the dark, in a drowned ship full of other men's treasure. And Sable Vane — Sable, of all the people in the world — had walked into Marrowgate carrying the coordinates like a knife wrapped in money.

No. No, he'd said no, and he'd meant it, and the no was correct. The job was sworn off. Every ranger on the rolls knew the Provision was a forbidden heading; the guild had struck it from sanctioned charts after the vanished expedition, and men who went anyway came home short or didn't come home. He had a license hanging by a thread, a debt with teeth, and a father's compass that pointed nowhere worth going. Walking into that wreck's shadow would be the stupidest—

*Trust no one who counts,* Corrick had said, over and over, until fever took him.

Kestrel pressed his forehead against the stone. Above him the water sang on, indifferent, going wherever the town directed it, the way everything in his life went where someone else directed it.

He was still standing there when the shadow fell across the archway — a broad shadow, patient, wearing the particular silhouette of a man who had been looking for him. Kestrel knew that silhouette too. Everybody in Marrowgate knew it.

Doss's enforcer didn't say anything. He just stood at the edge of the shade, thumbs in his belt, and waited, and smiled the small smile of a man delivering a message he was going to enjoy delivering.

"Your creditor wants a word," he said. "About payment. About *deadlines*."

Kestrel closed his eyes. Ten days, Doss had told him last week. Ten days, or the license goes, and the license was the last thing between Kestrel Thornbury and nothing at all.

Ten days. One share. One truth.

He opened his eyes and looked east, past the rooftops, toward the flats, where the horizon shimmered like the surface of a sea that wasn't there anymore.

"All right," he said, to the enforcer, to the arches, to no one. "Let's go hear it."
