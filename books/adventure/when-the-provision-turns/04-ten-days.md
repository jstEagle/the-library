## 4. Ten Days

They came for his gear at first light.

Kestrel heard the wagon before he saw it — the particular complaint of axles that had never once been greased out of kindness — and by the time he'd pulled on his boots and stepped into the gray morning of Marrowgate's back lanes, three of Doss's men were already at his door. Not knocking. Standing. There was a difference, and the difference was the whole message.

The one in front was called Ferrow, a broad man with a ledger-keeper's face and a bruiser's hands, and he held a paper the way a priest holds scripture.

"Morning, Thornbury."

"It isn't," Kestrel said.

Ferrow smiled without warmth, which was the only kind of smile Doss's men were issued. "Mr. Doss extends you courtesy. Ten days remain on your note. In the meantime, he's decided to hold certain assets in trust." He unfolded the paper with two fingers. "Ranger's kit. Longarm. Water rig, filtration cloths, the camel-hide satchel. The compass is itemized separately."

"The compass isn't collateral."

"The compass is itemized," Ferrow repeated, and there it was — the flatness in his voice that meant Doss had said this exact thing to him that morning, probably twice, probably while eating. "You'll get everything back when the note clears. Mr. Doss wants you motivated, not destitute. He's a fair man that way."

Kestrel looked past him at the wagon. His gear was already loaded — they'd gone through the window at the back of the room he rented, the one with the broken latch he'd been meaning to fix since spring. His longarm lay across the top like something dead. Beside it, wrapped in oilcloth because even creditors' thugs had some instinct for what mattered, sat his father's compass.

His father had carried it thirty years across the Saltreach. Had found water with it, or claimed to — though Kestrel had grown old enough to suspect the compass found nothing but north, and the finding of water had been his father all along, reading sand and wind and the color of the horizon at dusk. Still. It was the only thing left of the man that wasn't buried or burned, and now it rode in a debt-wagon between a bedroll and a box of cartridges.

"Ten days," Ferrow said again, as if Kestrel might have forgotten overnight. "Then the license forfeits, and the guild sells your bond to whoever bids. You know how that works. Rangers who lose their notes end up working guard duty for men like me." He tapped the wagon's rail. "Or worse. Men like Doss."

"I'll have it," Kestrel said.

Ferrow studied him with something almost like pity, which was worse than contempt. "Everyone says that on day four." He climbed onto the bench, gathered the reins, and the wagon rolled away down the lane, leaving Kestrel standing bare-handed in his own doorway, watching ten days of his life drive off toward the counting house.

He went inside and sat on the edge of the cot and did the arithmetic he'd already done a hundred times, because arithmetic doesn't change just because you hate it. He had eleven marks and some copper. A ranger's note ran to four hundred. In ten days, tracking work might bring him thirty, forty if the season was kind, and the season hadn't been kind since before his brother left.

Alden.

The name arrived the way it always did — uninvited, from below, like a fish rising under still water. Seven years since the Provision expedition walked out of Marrowgate with forty men and a promise, and none of them came back. Seven years since Kestrel had stood at the gate and watched his brother's back disappear down the salt road, and had said nothing, because saying *don't go* would have meant admitting he wanted to go himself.

Now Sable Vane wanted him to follow that same road, and pride said no, and pride was about to cost him everything his father had built.

* * *

He spent the middle of the day doing what poor men do instead of solving problems: walking. Marrowgate in the heat of noon was a town holding its breath — shutters cracked an inch, dogs flattened in whatever shade they could find, the whole place smelling of hot stone and the brine vats down by the rendering yards. He walked past the guild hall without going in. He walked past Vane's convoy, camped on the flats side of the wall, her wagons gleaming with new canvas, her crew moving with the crisp economy of people being paid well. He did not walk close enough to be seen.

It was coming back through the tannery quarter, where the lanes narrowed and the shadows went deep even at midday, that he felt the eyes.

Not hostile. Familiar, if anything. He let his stride lengthen naturally, turned down the alley behind the salt stores, and waited in the dark beside a stack of hides until the footsteps hurried after him — light, quick, trying too hard to be quiet.

"You followed me from the market," Kestrel said, without turning. "You breathe through your mouth when you're nervous. I told you that yesterday."

The thief stepped out of the glare into the alley's brown half-light, and up close he was younger than Kestrel had thought the night before — nineteen, maybe twenty, all elbows and hollow cheeks, with the sun-scoured look of someone raised on the open flats rather than in any town's alleys. He clutched a bundle against his chest the way children clutch bread.

"I didn't steal anything today," he said quickly. "I want you to know that first."

"That's a strange thing to lead with."

"You're the only person in this town who ever let me go." The boy's eyes flicked to the alley mouth, then back. "I've been thinking about that. About why."

"Because you stole water," Kestrel said. "And a man dying of thirst isn't a thief, he's just a man dying."

"And because I told you where I got it." The boy shifted his weight. "You remember? The cistern under the ruined chapel, east quarter. Nobody's supposed to know it's there. It's not on any guild map."

Kestrel remembered. He also remembered deciding, somewhere between the boy's cracked lips and the full skin he carried, that the information was worth more than the water. "What's your name?"

"Wick."

"Wick." He tasted it. "All right, Wick. You've had a day and a night to think about why I let you go. Give me the answer, and then tell me what you actually want, because nobody hunts a stranger through a tannery district out of gratitude."

Wick's jaw worked. When he spoke again, his voice dropped, and the nervousness in it changed quality — became something older and heavier than a boy's fear.

"I've heard things," he said. "Working the caravans, the wells, the smuggler camps out past the Reach. Things about the Provision. About the wreck, and what's really on it, and about the crew that went out seven years back." He swallowed. "About your brother."

The alley went very quiet. Somewhere beyond the walls, a tannery wheel creaked its slow circle, and the smell of lime and hide hung thick enough to chew.

"Careful," Kestrel said softly.

"I know. I know how it sounds." Wick's hands tightened on his bundle. "But here's the thing — I'm not the only one who's heard these things. There are men in the Corvane fleet's camp who've paid good coin for scraps of it. And there's a woman named Vane sitting outside your walls with wagons full of gear, and she didn't come all this way for salvage, not really. Everybody out on the flats is talking, Kestrel Thornbury. Everybody except you. And you're the one with the most reason to listen."

Kestrel took a slow breath. The heat pressed down; sweat traced his spine. "Say it, then. What have you heard?"

"Not here." Wick glanced at the alley mouth again. "And not for free — no. That's wrong. Not for coin." He held out the bundle. "For protection. You walk me out of Marrowgate alive, past Doss's watchers, past the Corvane scouts working the gate, and put me somewhere safe with your crew, and every word I know is yours. Because the men I'm hiding from aren't thieves, ranger. They're the ones who made sure the last expedition never came home. And I think—" his voice caught, steadied— "I think they'll want to make sure the next one doesn't either."

Kestrel stared at him. The boy's hands were shaking, but his eyes weren't. Whatever Wick was, he wasn't lying — Kestrel had spent fifteen years reading liars on the salt, and liars always performed their fear. This boy's fear was real, worn smooth like a stone carried too long in a pocket.

"What's in the bundle?" Kestrel asked.

Wick unwrapped it carefully. Inside, wrapped in waxed leather gone stiff with age, was a scrap of chart — brittle, salt-stained, torn along one edge as if ripped from something larger. Kestrel took it with fingers that had suddenly gone careful. The ink had faded to ghost-brown, but the lines were legible: coastline, soundings, depth marks in an old surveyor's hand. And near the center, circled twice, a set of coordinates with a single word beneath them in a different hand — a hasty, slanting hand that Kestrel knew better than his own.

*Alden.*

The alley tilted. Kestrel put his palm flat against the warm hide-stack and breathed until the ground steadied.

"Where," he said, and his voice came out rough, "did you get this."

"A dead man gave it to me. Two years ago. Out past the Glass Reach." Wick's voice was barely above a whisper now. "He was dying. He'd been dying a long time. He said — he said the ship turns, and the ones who sent them out knew it would turn, and—" He stopped. Shook his head. "That part I keep until we're safe. All of it. Protection first, ranger. That's the price."

For a long moment Kestrel said nothing. He looked at the chart, at his brother's handwriting, at the coordinates that matched — he was almost certain they matched — the numbers Vane had shown him two nights ago with her apology that wasn't one.

Pride said: walk away. Pride said: you don't take charity from thieves, don't sign on with the woman who broke you, don't chase ghosts into drowned canyons because a boy with shaking hands offers you a torn map. Pride had kept him upright for seven years, had been the only thing holding him together besides debt.

But pride didn't pay four hundred marks in ten days. Pride wouldn't stop Doss's wagon from taking the compass tomorrow, or the license the day after, or the name after that. Pride was a luxury, and he had finally, finally run out of the means to afford it.

"If I do this," Kestrel said slowly, "you tell me everything. Every word, every rumor, every name. No more holding pieces back for leverage. Protection buys the whole truth, or it buys nothing."

"The whole truth," Wick agreed. Then, quieter: "Even the parts you won't want."

"Especially those." Kestrel folded the chart and slid it inside his shirt, against his chest, where it seemed to burn cold. "Vane's convoy leaves at first tide tomorrow. If you're at the east water-gate an hour before dawn, I'll walk you through. After that, you're under my word — and my word is the only thing I have left that Doss hasn't itemized."

Wick nodded, rewrapped nothing — the chart stayed with Kestrel now — and backed toward the alley mouth, pausing at the edge of the light.

"Thornbury." He hesitated. "The man who gave me the chart. Before he died, he laughed. I never understood why. But when I saw your face just now, I think maybe I do."

Then he was gone, quick footsteps fading into the tannery noise, and Kestrel stood alone in the dark with his brother's handwriting against his heart and the shape of his ruin suddenly, terribly clear.

Refusing Vane meant losing the gear, the license, the name. Refusing Vane meant Doss owned him outright, body and future, until there was nothing left to repossess. Refusing Vane meant Alden's chart hanging around his neck like a stone over a grave he'd never visit.

There was no refusing anymore. There was only the tide, and the road, and whatever waited where the Provision turned.

Kestrel pressed his hand once against his chest, feeling the chart's edges through the cloth, and went to find Sable Vane — before his pride could wake up and talk him out of the only door he had left.
