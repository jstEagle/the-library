## 5. The Sworn-Off Job

Kestrel didn't sleep that night. He sat in the loft above the livery with his father's compass open in his palm and the needle trembling toward a north that had never once pointed him anywhere good, and he listened to the wind come off the Saltreach carrying grit against the shutters like fingers asking to be let in.

Ten days. Doss's voice was still in his ears, patient as sand filling a boot. *Ten days, Thornbury, and then I take the license, and then I take everything else, and you can go be nothing somewhere else where it costs less.*

He turned the compass over. The brass case was worn smooth at one corner from years of his father's thumb worrying it on long patrols, back when the rangers were respected instead of mortgaged. There was no engraving inside — he'd checked a hundred times as a boy, hoping for some secret, some inheritance beyond the object itself. Just the old paper dial gone amber, the needle, the glass cracked in one corner like a river seen from very high up.

Alden would have known what to do. Alden always knew what to do, right up until the day he walked out of Marrowgate with thirty-one other souls and never walked back.

The thought arrived the way it always did — sideways, uninvited, wearing the face of an ordinary thing. Kestrel set the compass down before his hand could shake.

* * *

Sable Vane found him at dawn, which told him she'd been looking since dark.

She stood at the bottom of the livery stairs in a dust-grey riding coat cut finer than anything Marrowgate deserved, her hair bound back tight, her boots clean despite the salt. Seven years hadn't softened her; if anything they'd honed her, the way wind hones stone — same shape, less of what was soft. She held two cups of tea from the vendor on the corner, and she offered one up to him like a peace treaty neither of them believed in.

"You look terrible," she said.

"You look expensive."

"I'm doing well." She said it without apology, which was the most honest thing about her. "Can we talk?"

"We're talking."

"Somewhere without an audience." Her eyes flicked past him to the stablehand pretending very hard to curry a mule that had been dead asleep for ten minutes.

Kestrel came down the stairs anyway. Not because she asked — because the tea smelled like cardamom, and cardamom cost more than he'd spent on food in a week, and pride was a luxury his creditor had already repossessed. He took the cup. It burned his palms pleasantly.

They walked. Out past the grain exchange, past the counting houses with their shuttered windows and their soft men inside, out to the low wall at the edge of town where the cobbles gave up and the saltflat began its long pale argument with the horizon. The morning was coming up red behind the eastern mesas, the way it did when there was dust in the air, and dust meant wind, and wind meant the flats would be moving today.

"Say your piece," Kestrel said.

Vane looked out at the flat for a while. He remembered that about her — she never rushed a hard sentence. She'd been the guild's best negotiator at twenty-three, and the reason wasn't charm. It was patience. She could stand in silence until the silence did her work for her.

"The Provision," she said finally. "I have coordinates. New ones. Survey-grade, off a deep-tide sounding taken last season by a Corvane hydrographer who didn't know what he had. The wreck sits in the Shallows, forty leagues past the Silt Gate. At low water the mast breaks the surface. At high water there's nothing but green sea and a story."

"And you want me to guide a salvage crew across six tides of the worst country in the territory to get to it." He sipped the tea. It was good. He hated that it was good. "You know my answer."

"I know the answer you gave me last night."

"It hasn't improved."

"Then hear the rest." She turned to face him fully, and he saw something in her face he'd never seen there before, or maybe had refused to see: care, or its expensive counterfeit. "Kestrel. Do you know why the job is sworn off? Not the tavern version. Not the ghost-story version the divers tell each other."

"The last crew went out after it and never came back. That's not a tavern version. That's the guild record."

"That's the summary." Her voice dropped. "The full record is sealed. I've read it. All of it. Every manifest, every deposition, every letter the families wrote begging the guild to send someone after them. Thirty-two people, Kestrel. Rangers, salvagers, a guild cartographer, two hydrographers from the Corvane survey. They went out seven years ago this month with the best equipment money could buy, and the guild sent exactly one search party, and the search party came back after four days and declared the Shallows unworkable, and everyone agreed to stop saying the name of that ship out loud because it was easier than admitting what actually happened."

"What actually happened?"

"That's the part that isn't in any record." She looked away, out at the red morning. "Because nobody knows. No wreckage washed ashore but flotsam. No bodies. One lifeboat, found empty, forty miles north of where it should have drifted. And one message capsule, recovered from a relay buoy six weeks later, containing a single line in the hand of the expedition's second officer."

The wind moved across the flat below them, dragging a veil of salt dust along the ground like something walking. Kestrel heard his own pulse.

"Who was second officer?" he asked, though the question was already dying in his throat, already knowing its own answer, because there was only one reason Sable Vane had crossed half the territory to stand at the foot of his stairs at dawn holding tea she knew he couldn't afford.

"Alden Thornbury," Vane said quietly. "Your brother was second officer on the Provision expedition. And the message in that capsule was addressed to the guild commandant — and it was intercepted. By me."

* * *

The tea went cold in his hands. Or his hands went cold around the tea. He couldn't have said which.

"You're lying," he said.

"I've lied to you before. You know what it looks like when I do it. This isn't it."

Seven years ago. Seven years ago Sable Vane had been rising through the guild administration on a reputation for results, and Alden had been — Alden had been Alden, laughing, certain, packing his kit for a contract so lucrative it made their father's pension look like pocket lint. *One run, Kes. One run and I buy Mother's water rights back outright.* And Kestrel had stayed behind with the border patrols and the small debts and the slow erosion of everything, and then the Provision hadn't come home, and the guild had closed the file, and Kestrel had learned to stop saying his brother's name in rooms where people might look at him with pity.

And Sable Vane had been in the guild administration seven years ago.

"You intercepted it," he said. His voice came out flat and strange, like someone else's. "A distress message. From my brother. And you buried it."

"I filed it," she said. "There's a difference, and the difference is the whole reason I'm standing here humiliating myself."

"Then say the difference."

"The message didn't ask for rescue." She met his eyes and held them, and to her credit she didn't flinch. "It was a warning. One line. I've read it perhaps a thousand times in seven years and I still don't understand it, and I am not a woman who fails to understand documents. But it wasn't a plea, Kestrel. If it had been a plea, I'd have moved heaven and the guild both. What it was—" she stopped herself, jaw tightening, and he watched her decide how much truth she could afford. "What it was frightened me. And I was twenty-six years old and ambitious and I told myself that filing it was prudence. That acting on it would be panic. And then the search party failed, and the file closed, and telling myself things became a habit."

"What did it say?"

"No." She shook her head once, sharp. "Not here. Not standing in the wind. If you come with me — if you sign — you'll read it yourself, tonight, in my strongbox, with your own eyes. I won't have you hearing it secondhand and deciding I poisoned it."

"You think I'd sign on to a sworn-off job because you'll show me a piece of paper?"

"I think you'd sign on because your brother wrote it." The words landed between them like a dropped blade. Vane didn't step back from it. "And I think you hate me enough that you'd rather find the truth yourself than take it from my mouth, and I have learned, at considerable cost, that being hated usefully is better than being trusted falsely. So yes. I think you'll sign."

Kestrel looked out at the flat. The red light was climbing the mesas now, and the salt below the wall glowed like the inside of a shell, beautiful and barren and utterly indifferent to what he owed, whom he'd lost, what he was about to do. Somewhere out there — forty leagues past the Silt Gate, under green water that rose and fell twice a day like something breathing — there was a ship, and in the ship there might be nothing, or there might be bones, or there might be answers, and every one of those possibilities was more than the guild record had ever given him.

Thirty-two people. One empty lifeboat. One line in his brother's hand.

And Doss waiting in Marrowgate with his ledgers and his enforcers and his ten days, ready to strip Kestrel of the license that was the last thing standing between him and being no one at all.

He could refuse. He could keep refusing, the way he'd refused for seven years, and stay clean. Stay poor. Stay licensed for nine more days. And then stand in the street and watch Doss's men carry his gear out of the livery, and spend the rest of his life knowing that pride had cost him the only chance he'd ever get to stand where Alden had stood and see what Alden had seen.

Or he could sign.

"Terms," he said.

Vane exhaled — just barely, just enough that he caught it, and understood how much the answer had mattered to her, and hated her a little more for making him see it.

"Standard salvage shares. Ten percent to guide, paid on return regardless of yield. Full authority over route and pace once we're past the Silt Gate — the crew answers to you on the ground, me on the coin. You read the message tonight. And Kestrel—" she paused, choosing, "—you tell me, honestly, when you've decided whether you're doing this for the money or for him. Because a crew can survive either motive. It's the man who doesn't know which one he's carrying who gets people killed."

"And if I'm carrying both?"

"Then we're even," she said. "So am I."

He should have asked her then what she meant. He'd have years afterward to wish he had. But the wind was rising off the flat and the red morning was burning down to white and somewhere behind him in Marrowgate a creditor was sharpening ten days into nine, and Kestrel Thornbury was tired — tired in the marrow, tired of standing at walls watching horizons he'd sworn off.

"Where do I sign?" he said.

Vane produced the contract from inside her coat — she'd carried it with her, he realized; she'd known before she climbed his stairs — and a pen, and held both out. He took the pen. His signature came out smaller than usual, cramped against the margin, as if the hand itself were trying to take up less room in what it had just done.

She rolled the contract and stowed it. For a moment neither of them moved. The flats breathed their dust at the edge of the world.

"Crew musters at first tide, day after tomorrow, east gate," Vane said. "Engineer named Brammel, a diver called Osha who's the best in the territory and knows it, navigator twins who finish each other's sentences, and a quartermaster named Hesk." Something flickered across her face at the last name, quick as a fish under ice, gone before he could name it. "Come meet them. Decide for yourself what you're leading."

"And the message?"

"Tonight. My office. Come alone." She turned to go, then stopped, her back to him, the fine grey coat bright against the burning salt. "Kestrel. Whatever you find in that capsule — whatever it says — remember that I was young, and that I've carried it longer than you've carried the anger, and that neither fact excuses me. I only ask that both facts sit in the room with us when you read it."

She walked. He watched her go all the way to the east gate, small and straight-backed against the glare, and then he looked down at the cold cardamom tea in his hand and drank it anyway, all of it, bitter and sweet at once, like every bargain he'd ever made.

* * *

That night he sat in the loft again with the compass open and the lamp turned low, and he did not sleep, and he did not pretend anymore that this was about the debt.

It was about a line in his brother's handwriting, locked in a strongbox across town. It was about thirty-two people the territory had agreed to forget because forgetting was cheaper than searching. It was about the empty lifeboat, forty miles north of where it should have drifted — north, always north, as if even the drowned were trying to choose a direction.

Toward midnight he opened the compass case all the way and pressed his thumb to the worn corner of the brass, his father's gesture, inherited without permission, and spoke to the empty room in a voice too low to wake anyone, though there was no one to wake.

"Hold on, Alden," he said. "I'm coming. Late — but I'm coming."

Outside, the wind changed, swinging hard out of the southeast, off the drowned country, off the Shallows, and the compass needle shivered in its glass and settled, pointing the way he would ride at first tide — toward the wreck of the Provision, and whatever his brother had died trying to warn the world about.

He just didn't know yet that the warning had a traitor's name hidden inside it.
