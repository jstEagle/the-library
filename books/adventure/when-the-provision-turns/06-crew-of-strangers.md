## 6. Crew of Strangers

The convoy waited at the east gate of Marrowgate like a wound that hadn't decided whether to close.

Kestrel counted the wagons from the top of the gate stairs, where the morning wind came off the flats smelling of salt and old rust. Six wagons, two water-tenders, a drill-rig mounted on the lead wagon like some iron insect impaled on a spear. Canvas covers lashed down. Lanterns still burning against the last of the dark. And beneath it all, the particular hush of a crew gathered before dawn that didn't trust one another yet — the hush of strangers doing arithmetic about each other.

Seven years since he'd stood at a gate like this. Seven years since he'd sworn off salvage runs, and here was the arithmetic of his own life come due: ten days, a debt with Doss's name on it, and a brother's ghost riding shotgun in a contract he'd signed with a hand that hadn't quite trembled. He'd told himself it was for the answers. He was still telling himself that.

Below, Sable Vane stood at the head of the convoy in a dust-grey riding coat, checking a ledger with the same brisk efficiency she'd once used to gut his reputation in front of the guild tribunal. She didn't look up. She didn't need to. She'd known he'd come. That was the thing about Sable — she never gambled on people; she calculated them.

Kestrel came down the stairs with his bedroll over one shoulder and his father's compass in his coat pocket, riding against his hip like a second heartbeat. The compass was the only thing of value he owned, and he'd spent a bad hour last night deciding whether to pawn it. He hadn't. Some debts you paid in coin. Some you paid in pride. He was running low on both.

"You're late," Vane said, without looking up.

"Sun's not up."

"The crew's been here an hour."

"Then they're more frightened than I am."

That got her eyes. Something moved behind them — not warmth, nothing so generous, but a flicker of the old recognition, the way two blades remember each other. "They're professionals," she said.

"Professionals are the most frightened people I know. That's why they're professionals."

She closed the ledger. "Introductions, then. Try not to insult anyone before the first tide."

* * *

The engineer was called Brammel, and Kestrel found him exactly where he'd expected to find him: headfirst in the guts of the lead wagon, boots sticking out over the drill-mount, swearing at a fuel line in the low, conversational tone of a man who'd been swearing at machines his whole life.

"Brammel," Vane said. "This is Kestrel Thornbury. He's our ranger."

The boots withdrew. Brammel unfolded from the wagon's undercarriage like a folding rule — long, angular, all elbows and grease — and stood blinking at Kestrel through goggles pushed up onto a forehead scarred by old burns. He was perhaps fifty, built like the wagons he loved, and his handshake was a careful thing, calibrated, as if he were testing Kestrel's load-bearing capacity.

"Thornbury." He said the name like he was reading it off a part label. "The Thornbury. Saltreach ranger. The one from the tribunal."

"Seven years ago," Kestrel said. "You've got a good memory."

"I've got a good memory for disasters. It's how I keep my wagons alive." Brammel wiped his hands on a rag that was dirtier than his hands. "Vane says you know the drowned canyon route."

"I know a route through it. Whether it's the drowned canyon's route is another question."

Brammel grinned — a sudden, mechanical flash of teeth — and Kestrel felt something in his chest loosen half a notch. He hadn't expected to like anyone on this crew. Liking people on a salvage run was how you got them killed, or got yourself killed for them. But Brammel's grin was the grin of a man who'd already made peace with the odds and found them acceptable, and that was rarer than water out here.

"She's a temperamental girl," Brammel said, patting the wagon's flank. "The drill-rig. She doesn't like salt dust, doesn't like cold starts, and doesn't like being rushed. Otherwise we'll get along fine."

"What's she for?"

Brammel and Vane exchanged a glance — a quick, closed-door glance that lasted half a second and told Kestrel more than either of them intended.

"Salvage," Vane said.

"Salvage," Kestrel repeated. "With a drill-rig."

"The Provision's been down nine years. Salt crusts over everything. You don't cut through salt crust with good intentions." Vane turned away before he could press it, and Kestrel let her go. He filed the drill-rig away in the growing drawer of things this job didn't add up around. You didn't bring a drill-rig to a wreck unless you expected to go deep, or expected the deep to be sealed.

* * *

The diver was at the second water-tender, and she was nothing Kestrel expected.

He'd built a picture of her in the night, the way he built pictures of everything he dreaded: a big, scarred man, quiet, with hands like anchors. Instead the diver was a woman not much older than himself, sitting cross-legged on the tender's rim with her gear laid out before her in an arrangement so precise it looked ceremonial — mask, air-lines, weights, a knife, all of it cleaned and ordered like an altar. She had dark hair cut brutally short and eyes the flat grey-green of deep water, and she was sharpening the knife with long, unhurried strokes, watching him come.

"Osha," Vane said. "Best diver on the coast. She's pulled cargo out of the Silt Gate wrecks at forty feet."

"Thirty-eight," Osha said. Her voice was low and even, like water finding its level. "Vane rounds up."

"Thirty-eight," Vane conceded, and moved on.

Kestrel stood there a moment, feeling assessed. Osha's gaze wasn't hostile — it was worse than that. It was thorough. She looked at him the way he looked at a stretch of flats before crossing it, reading the ground for what would give way.

"You've dived before," she said. Not a question.

"Never. Desert ranger. I've pulled men out of flash floods, if that counts."

"It counts." She resumed sharpening. "It counts for nothing, but it counts."

"Fair enough."

"You'll be on the surface when we dive. Your job's the weather and the water and the route. My job's what's under it." She tested the blade against her thumb, satisfied. "One piece of advice, ranger, and then we're done talking until I've had tea."

"Go on."

"Everyone on this convoy wants something from the Provision. Vane wants her name back. Brammel wants the rig paid off. The twins want adventure, which is the most dangerous want there is. And the quartermaster—" she paused, just long enough, "—wants whatever he's being paid for. So when you're figuring out who to watch, ranger, start with the man who counts everything."

She went back to her knife. The conversation, as promised, was over.

* * *

The twins were impossible to tell apart for exactly four seconds, and then it became the easiest thing in the world.

Pell was the one leaning against the third wagon with his arms crossed and a grin already loaded, a lean young man with sun-bleached hair and the restless energy of someone who'd never once been bored in his life. Pim was the one sitting on the wagon's bench with a chart spread across her knees, marking it in fine pencil strokes, not looking up, not needing to. Same face, same build, same faded expedition jackets. Different weather entirely.

"Pell," said Pell, offering a hand.

"Pim," said Pim, offering nothing but a nod and a glance that took his measure and moved on.

"Thornbury." Pell's grin widened. "The Thornbury. Osha said you were the one from the tribunal. That's brilliant. A crew with a legend on it."

"I'm not a legend. I'm a ranger with debts."

"All the best legends are." Pell dropped into step beside him as Kestrel walked the line of wagons, and Kestrel understood immediately that the boy attached himself to people the way burrs attached to wool — without malice, without effort, and very hard to remove. "Pim and I have navigated the Reach twice. Never past the Glass Reach, mind. Nobody navigates past the Glass Reach. That's where you come in, isn't it? The flats past the Reach, the drowned canyon, the Shallows. Vane says you're the only one who's read the old survey charts."

"Vane says a lot of things."

"She said you refused the contract first." Pell said it lightly, but his eyes weren't light at all — they were quick and curious and missing nothing. "What changed your mind?"

Kestrel looked at him. Young, eager, alive in the way only people who hadn't lost anyone yet were alive. "The same thing that changes everyone's mind," he said. "Money."

Pell laughed. Pim, from her chart, said without looking up, "He's lying, Pell."

"Pim reads people like she reads charts," Pell said cheerfully. "She's usually right. It's insufferable."

"She's usually right," Kestrel agreed, and moved on, and felt the boy's curiosity settle onto his back like a second bedroll. He'd have to be careful with Pell. Careful with both of them. On a convoy, the ones who watched you closest were the ones most likely to see what you were hiding, and Kestrel was carrying more than a compass in his coat. He was carrying a brother's name and seven years of silence, and he did not yet know which of those would prove heavier.

* * *

The quartermaster found him, which Kestrel noted, and did not like.

Hesk was a compact man, grey at the temples, with the neat, economical movements of someone who had spent his life handling other people's property and accounting for every grain of it. He wore his supply-ledger slung across his chest in an oilcloth case, and his hands were clean — conspicuously, deliberately clean, in a way no honest quartermaster's hands were by the first hour of a loading morning.

"Master Thornbury." His voice was smooth as sanded wood. "Hesk. Quartermaster. I'll be handling rations, water shares, and the general book of the expedition. You'll want to register your personal kit with me before we cross the flats. Standard practice."

"Standard," Kestrel said.

"Water shares especially. On a run this long, water is currency. I keep the count." He smiled. It was a well-made smile, fitted to his face like a good joint, and it did not reach anywhere near his eyes. "Vane speaks highly of your desert knowledge."

"Vane speaks highly of whatever's useful at the moment."

Hesk's smile didn't flicker, but something behind it did — a small recalculation, a figure carried to a different column. "Of course. Well. When you're ready to register your kit, I'm at the third wagon." He turned to go, then paused, as if remembering something incidental. "One question, if you don't mind. Purely for the book. You signed on late. After the contract was posted, after the crew was hired. Vane doesn't add crew late unless there's a reason." His grey eyes were pleasant and patient and utterly without warmth. "What's your reason, Master Thornbury?"

"My reason's my own. Put that in the book."

Hesk inclined his head, gracious as a banker, and walked away, and Kestrel stood watching him go with the cold, familiar certainty of a man who has tracked enough thieves to know the walk. Hesk moved like a man with two masters — smooth on the surface, and underneath, always underneath, a small hurried rhythm, like a heart counting someone else's money.

Osha had told him where to start looking. She hadn't needed to bother. Hesk had told him himself, just by standing there.

* * *

They rolled out at first tide, when the flats turned from grey to gold and the wind came up out of the east carrying the smell of the drowned country.

Kestrel rode point, out ahead of the lead wagon, because point was where a ranger belonged and because he wanted the crew at his back where he could count them. Behind him the convoy strung out across the salt in a line half a mile long: the drill-rig wagon grinding and groaning, the water-tenders swaying, the crew wagons trailing dust. The sun climbed. The salt stretched. Somewhere out past the horizon's shimmer, nine years of silence waited at the bottom of the Shallows, and under that silence, a brother.

He made himself stop thinking about Alden and think about wagons instead. Wagons were manageable. Wagons had problems you could fix.

By mid-morning he'd learned the crew the way you learn any country: by its faults. Brammel's rig was running hot on the left axle and he knew it and was nursing it, muttering to it, loving it. Pell talked. Pim watched. Osha said nothing at all and saw everything, and twice Kestrel caught her eyes on him when he turned — not hostile, not friendly, just reading, the way she'd read him at the water-tender, patient as deep water. And Hesk — Hesk moved up and down the line all morning with his ledger, counting water barrels, counting rations, counting, counting, and three times Kestrel saw him stop at the convoy's rear and stand facing back the way they'd come, toward Marrowgate, toward whatever or whoever was behind them, with his hand resting on his oilcloth case like a man resting his hand on a door he expected to open.

At noon they halted in the shadow of a salt ridge to rest the teams, and Vane climbed up onto the ridge beside him, a canteen in one hand and the convoy spread out below.

"Well?" she said.

"They'll hold together."

"That's not what I asked."

"It's the answer you're getting." He drank and handed the canteen back. "Your quartermaster's counting more than water."

"Everyone counts something."

"Your diver thinks he's reporting to someone. She didn't say who. She didn't have to — I've seen the walk." He watched Hesk below, moving among the wagons, clean-handed and smooth-voiced. "You hired a man with two masters, Vane. On a run this deep, that's not a crew member. That's a leak with a ledger."

Vane was quiet a long moment. The wind moved her dust-grey coat, and for a second she looked older than seven years had made her, older than the tribunal, older than the girl he'd once — he cut the thought off at the root. That country was burned. Nothing grew there.

"I know who he reports to," she said at last, quietly. "I've always known. It's the price of the financing."

"Whose price?"

She looked at him then, and whatever was in her face, she folded it away before he could read it, the way she'd folded away everything for seven years. "Ask me again when we're past the Glass Reach," she said. "If we're both still speaking."

She climbed down from the ridge. Below, the crew was moving again, harnesses jingling, Brammel's rig grinding back to life, Pell waving up at him with that loaded grin. Kestrel stayed on the ridge one moment longer, looking back the way they'd come — back toward the flats, back toward Marrowgate, back toward the horizon they'd crossed that morning.

And there, at the very edge of seeing, where the salt shimmer dissolved into sky, he caught it: a smudge of dust. Thin. Distant. Moving.

Not following their line. Running parallel. Fast.

Kestrel stood very still, the way he'd learned to stand on the flats when the land was telling him something, and watched the dust smudge hold its parallel course, patient as a shadow, and he thought of Osha's flat grey-green eyes and her quiet voice: *start with the man who counts everything.*

He thought of Hesk, facing rearward, hand on his oilcloth case.

He turned and swung down off the ridge to tell Vane that they weren't alone on the salt — that whatever this convoy was, it had already begun to be followed.
