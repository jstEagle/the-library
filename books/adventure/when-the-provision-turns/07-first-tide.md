## 7. First Tide

The salt began at the edge of Marrowgate and did not end anywhere Osha could imagine.

She had grown up with water the way other people grew up with walls — always around her, always pressing, always the first and last fact of any day. The drowned canyons south of the Verrell coast had taught her that water was not a thing but a place, a country with its own laws, its own weather, its own patience. Salt was different. Salt was water's ghost, water's corpse, water after everything living had been wrung out of it. The flats stretched white to every horizon, cracked into polygons the size of wagon wheels, and the light came off them so hard and so flat that Osha's eyes watered for the first hour and ached for the three after that.

She rode the second wagon, the one with the dive gear lashed under oilcloth, and she watched Kestrel Thornbury ride point and tried to decide what was wrong with him.

Nothing you could name. That was the trouble. He did his job the way a good guide does it — reading the flats like a diver reads current, calling the pace, calling the rest, calling the line they'd hold when the mirage hours came and the world turned to liquid glass. He was good. Better than good. She'd watched enough hired hands to know the difference between a man following a map and a man who *was* the map, and Thornbury was the second kind. His father's compass hung at his neck on a leather cord, and he never once looked at it, which told her he didn't need to.

But there was a tightness in him. A held breath. He rode like a man in a room where someone was sleeping — careful, quiet, listening past the world for something that might wake up.

Osha knew what it was to carry a drowned thing. She carried her own. Her brother had gone down off the Verrell shelf eight years ago, a salvage diver on a wreck called the *Cormorant's Grace*, and the sea had given back his weight belt and one boot and nothing else. So she recognized the posture. Thornbury was carrying someone. And whatever job had brought him onto this convoy, it wasn't the coin.

The convoy itself was eleven wagons and forty-one souls, and Osha had spent the first tide learning all of them, because a diver who doesn't know her crew is already drowning.

Brammel rode the lead engine wagon, a mountain of a woman with grease worked so deep into her knuckles it looked like tattooing, and she talked to the engine the way other people talked to horses — low, coaxing, occasionally profane. The twins, Pell and Pim, ran the navigation wagon and finished each other's sentences so completely that Osha suspected they didn't actually need each other for it; they just liked the sound. Pell was the careful one, Pim the quick one, and both of them had laughed at her the night before when she'd told them the flats were just another kind of sea. "Flat sea," Pell had said. "No waves." And Pim: "No bottom, either." She'd decided she liked them, in the way you like people you might have to pull out of a hole.

Hesk she didn't like at all.

The quartermaster was a neat, dry man with ink-stained fingers and a ledger he touched more often than he touched people. He counted everything — water, salt-pork, axle grease, hours — and he counted it aloud, in that soft carrying voice of his, so that everyone on the convoy knew exactly how much of everything remained and exactly how much of everything was owed. Osha had met his kind on every crew she'd ever sailed with. They were the ones who survived. They were also, in her experience, the ones who made it necessary for others not to.

And then there was Vane.

Sable Vane rode at the head of the convoy beside Thornbury, and Osha watched the two of them the way she'd watch two currents meeting — no collision, no mixing, just a long seam of tension where the water changed color. Vane was all edges. She wore her salvage license in a brass frame on her chest like a warrant, and she gave her orders in a voice pitched so it carried exactly as far as it needed to and no farther. Seven years ago, Osha had heard, Vane had done something to Thornbury's name that couldn't be patched. Nobody would say what. But Osha had watched Thornbury take orders from her for a full day now, and she had never once seen him look at her face.

* * *

The mirage hours came at midday, as the guide had promised.

The world dissolved. The saltflats turned to a sheet of molten silver, and the horizon lifted and wavered, and distant rocks became towers, and towers became ships, and the convoy moved through a landscape that was all reflection and no substance. Osha had seen heat-shimmer over open water, but this was different — this was a sea that lied. The twins navigated by dead reckoning and a brass theodolite and a long chain of chalk marks left by caravans past, and Pell called out bearings in a flat, uninflected voice while Pim marked them in a ledger that was the twin of Hesk's and twice as honest.

"Two hours to the mirage line," Pell said. "After that the ground's real again."

"Define real," Pim said.

"Hard enough to break an axle."

"Comforting."

Osha sat with her back against the dive wagon's rail and drank her ration — a measured swallow, warm as bathwater, tasting of the canvas bag — and let her eyes rest on the horizon because there was nothing else to rest them on. That was how she saw it first.

She thought it was a mirage. Everything out here was a mirage. But mirages shimmer, and this didn't. It sat on the edge of the world like a thing that had decided to be there: a long dark smudge, broken into segments, moving. Not drifting the way heat moves things. Moving *with purpose*, and moving fast.

She counted. It was a habit from the water — count what's coming before it arrives, because by the time it arrives you won't have time. Eleven shapes. No — twelve, one trailing. Wagons, or something wagon-shaped, strung out in a line that was too straight for a caravan and too disciplined for drifters. And above them, catching the white glare and throwing it back in hard little knives, points of light that could only be one thing.

Metal. Polished, deliberate, meant to be seen.

"Twins." Her voice came out low and flat, the voice she used under the water. "Pell. Pim. Look north by east. Tell me what you see."

Pell raised the theodolite. Osha watched his shoulders change — a small settling, the way a man's shoulders settle when the world confirms something he hoped it wouldn't.

"Convoy," Pell said. "Heavy wagons, eight, ten, twelve — hard to count at this distance. Escorted. Those flashes are—"

"Armor plate," Pim said. He'd taken up his own glass. "Or gun shields. Somebody spent money."

"Corvane," Osha said.

The name went down the convoy like cold water down a spine. She hadn't meant to say it aloud, but it was the only word that fit, and everyone within earshot heard it and passed it back, wagon to wagon, in murmurs, the way sailors pass a warning. *Corvane. Corvane fleet. Corvane on the horizon.*

The Corvane Salvage Combine didn't race. It *arrived*. It had the capital to buy whole wrecks before smaller crews ever found them, the lawyers to make salvage law a weapon, and the guns to make the law a suggestion. Everyone in the trade knew the Corvane fleet when they saw it: the gun shields, the matched gray wagons, the way it moved like a thing that had never once doubted it would get where it was going.

And now it was behind them — or beside them, or ahead of them, depending on what the flats did — and it was faster. Osha could see that even at this distance. The Corvane wagons ran bigger engines, and they weren't hauling dive gear and ration water the way Vane's convoy was. They were hauling *for* the race. Everything about that dark line on the horizon said speed, and everything about Vane's convoy said endurance, and endurance was a fine quality in a long grave.

Vane came back down the line at a canter.

Osha had to give her this: she didn't panic. She reined in beside the navigation wagon, took Pell's glass without asking, and studied the horizon for a long, silent minute while the whole convoy watched her back and pretended not to. Then she lowered the glass and handed it back, and her voice, when it came, was the same level, carrying voice as always — but quieter, pitched now to the wagon and not the line.

"Corvane fleet. Twelve wagons, armed, running the same bearing we are." A pause, letting that settle. "They've got our heading, which means they've got coordinates, which means somebody talked, which we can discuss when we're not standing in the open. What matters now: they're faster and we're not going to outrun them, so we're going to make them *not know where we are*."

"How?" Pim asked.

"Silence," Vane said. "Effective immediately. No lamps after dark. No smoke at rest stops — cold food, covered fires, and if I see so much as a glint off a cookpot I'll melt the cookpot. No engine noise above half-throttle. No signals of any kind, to anyone, for any reason. The twins will run us dark on dead reckoning and chalk lines." She turned in the saddle, and her eyes went down the whole convoy, wagon by wagon, face by face, and Osha felt them pass over her like a hand checking a seal. "And no one speaks of what we carry or where we're going. Not to each other, not to yourselves, not in your sleep. As of this moment, this convoy is a closed mouth. Anyone who opens it answers to me."

She wheeled her horse and cantered forward, toward the point, where Thornbury rode.

Osha watched them talk. Or rather, she watched them *not* talk — Vane reining in beside him, a few words, Thornbury's head turning north by east toward the dark smudge, and then a longer stillness. From this distance she couldn't hear anything, but she could see the shape of it: Vane speaking, Thornbury listening, and then Thornbury saying something that made Vane's back go straight as a pike. Whatever it was, it wasn't agreement. Then he turned his horse north, away from the Corvane line, and pointed at something on the ground that Osha couldn't see, and Vane nodded once, curt, and rode back down the line giving new orders.

The convoy turned. Not much — ten degrees, maybe fifteen — but on the saltflats a turn was a sentence, and everyone read it. They were leaving the marked caravan route. They were going out onto the unmarked white, where the chalk lines ended and the ground kept its own counsel, and the only person in forty-one souls who knew what was out there was the man riding point.

* * *

They made cold camp that night in the lee of a salt ridge, wagons drawn into a rough crescent, fires unlit, the whole convoy eating salt-pork and hardtack in a silence that had weight to it. The stars came out in the terrible abundance they only have over dead ground — no smoke, no light, nothing between the convoy and the whole burning floor of the sky — and Osha sat her watch on the ridge with a rifle across her knees she only half knew how to use, watching the north by east where the Corvane fleet had been.

It was still there. Dimmer now — they'd doused their lights too, which told her something. A fleet that douses its lights isn't a fleet that's given up the chase. It's a fleet that's hunting.

She heard boots on the salt behind her and didn't startle. She'd learned to place people by their walk. This one was long-strided, deliberate, weight on the heels: Thornbury.

He came up the ridge and stood beside her, not quite close, and for a while neither of them said anything. That was all right. Divers understood silence. Most people used it to fill space; divers used it to listen.

"They're holding pace," he said finally. "Not closing. They don't need to close. They know where we're going."

"You know that how?"

"Because we're going to the only place out here worth going." He said it without drama, the way you'd say the sun comes up. "The Provision's wreck is the only mark on six tides of salt. Corvane didn't follow us. Corvane was always coming."

Osha turned that over. "Then this was never a race. It's a procession."

"Until the Shallows. Then it's a race again." He crouched, picked up a chunk of crusted salt, and turned it in his fingers. "Vane's counting on the flats to slow them. Heavy wagons, unmarked ground, mirage hours. She thinks endurance beats speed." He set the salt chunk down. "She's wrong. Endurance just loses slower."

Osha studied him in the starlight. His face was all planes and shadow, and there was that tightness again, that held-breath quality, and it struck her that it had gotten worse since the Corvane fleet appeared — no. Since *before*. Since Marrowgate. Since before Marrowgate, maybe. She thought about the way he'd signed on — late, reluctant, at the last tide, after everyone said he'd sworn off this exact job years ago. She thought about the way he never looked at Vane. She thought about the word that followed him around the Marrowgate docks like a stray dog: *brother*.

"My brother drowned eight years ago," she said.

He didn't move, but she felt him listen. It was a real listening, the kind most people don't know how to do.

"Off the Verrell shelf. Wreck called the *Cormorant's Grace*. The sea gave back a weight belt and a boot." She kept her eyes on the north. "After that I stopped diving for pay and started diving for wrecks. There's a difference. Pay is a reason. Wrecks are a question. You don't stop asking until you get an answer, and the answer's never the one you wanted, and you ask the next one anyway."

The salt chunk crumbled in Thornbury's fingers. He brushed the dust on his knee and didn't say anything for a long moment.

"What's your question, ranger?" Osha asked. "Because it's not the coin. I've watched you look at Hesk's ledger. A man who wants coin looks at a ledger like it's food. You look at it like it's a cage."

For a moment she thought he wouldn't answer. The wind came off the flats, dry and faintly bitter, carrying the smell of the cold camp behind them — pork fat, engine oil, horse. Somewhere down in the crescent of wagons, one of the twins murmured figures in his sleep.

"My brother went after the Provision seven years ago," Thornbury said at last. His voice was level, and the levelness was the loudest thing about it. "He didn't come back. Nobody came back. The job got sworn off, the guild banned it, and everyone agreed to let the salt keep its secret." He stood. "Vane came to me with new coordinates and a contract, and I told her no. And then I signed on anyway."

"Why?"

"Because the salt's had seven years," he said, "and I'm done letting it keep things."

He walked back down the ridge toward the wagons, and Osha sat her watch alone and understood, finally, what was wrong with Kestrel Thornbury. Nothing was wrong with him. Something was *unfinished*. He was a diver in a dry country, and the wreck he was diving was a man, and he wouldn't come up until he'd brought something back.

She looked north by east. The Corvane fleet's lights were dark, but the fleet was there — she could feel it the way she felt current through a hull, a pressure on the edge of things, patient and enormous. Twelve wagons full of guns and money, all pointed at the same drowned ship she was pointed at, and somewhere between here and the Shallows the two lines were going to converge, and when they did, the flats would drink what they drank.

Below her, a light flickered — once, twice, three times — down near the quartermaster's wagon. Small. Shielded. Deliberately shaped, not a lantern's accident.

Osha went very still.

By the time she leaned forward to be sure, it was dark again, and Hesk's wagon sat quiet under the stars, and the salt wind blew across the flats carrying nothing but salt.

But she had counted. Counting was the first thing the water taught her.

She settled back against the ridge, rifle across her knees, and watched the quartermaster's wagon, and the dark line of the Corvane fleet, and the point rider's silhouette where Thornbury sat his horse in the cold, and she began — quietly, in the diver's ledger she kept behind her eyes — to count everything else.
