## 8. The Drowned Canyon

The saltflats ended the way all things in the Saltreach ended — not with a border but with a slow surrender, the white crust giving way to cracked clay, the clay to gravel, the gravel to a darkness ahead that Kestrel felt in his teeth before he saw it.

He rode point on the lead wagon, half a mile out from Vane's convoy, and watched the land fold downward like a page being turned. The horizon simply stopped. Beyond it lay a country of broken stone and shadow, terraced down and down into the earth as if some ancient hand had pressed its thumb into the world and left the print behind. The drowned canyons. Every ranger's map of the region marked them with the same notation: *road lost below second tide line.*

"Thornbury." Vane's voice carried up from behind him, dry as the flats they were leaving. She had ridden forward without him noticing, which was unlike her, or perhaps exactly like her. "You've gone quiet."

"I'm always quiet."

"You're *calculating* quiet now. There's a difference." She drew alongside, her dappled courser picking its way through the first of the gravel fans. "Talk to me."

Kestrel nodded at the dark seam in the earth ahead. "The old trade road runs along the canyon floor for forty miles. When I was licensed, it was dry nine months of the year. You crossed at dawn, you were out by dusk, and you thanked whatever gods you keep that nobody tried to follow you." He paused. "That was fifteen years ago. The water's been rising since. Some seasons the floor doesn't drain at all."

"And your desert knowledge tells us what?"

"That we're about to find out whether Brammel's engines can swim."

She almost smiled. Almost. It was the closest thing to humor she'd offered since Marrowgate, and he understood why she rationed it. A captain who laughed too easily led crews who stopped watching the horizon.

Behind them, the convoy stretched out in a dust-colored line: six wagons, two water-sledges, twenty-three souls counting the twins' dog. Osha rode the third wagon with her diving rig lashed beside her like a sleeping animal. Hesk brought up the rear, as always, where he could see everyone and no one could see what he wrote in his little book.

*Everyone reports to someone,* Kestrel thought, and put it away.

* * *

The descent began at midday, and by mid-afternoon Kestrel understood why the maps had given up.

The old road revealed itself in fragments — a stretch of flagstone here, emerging from the clay like a bone through skin; there, a cut embankment where engineers dead a century gone had carved the grade with fire and patience. But between the fragments, water. Not the bright violence of a river but something stranger: still, glass-green pools lying in the canyon's throat, deep enough that the road vanished beneath them and reappeared fifty yards on, patient and drowned, waiting.

Brammel stood on the lead wagon's engine housing, goggles pushed up into his wild gray hair, reading the water the way other men read scripture. He was a broad man built like something assembled rather than born, and when he spoke it came out in a low rumble that seemed to come up through the wagon itself.

"Three feet here," he called down. "Maybe four over the stones. The boiler'll take it if we go slow. Slow as a funeral."

"Then we go slow as a funeral," Vane said.

They went slow as a funeral. The convoy waded into the green dark, wheels turning through water the color of bottle glass, and the canyon walls rose on either side until the sky was a ribbon of copper overhead. Sound changed down there. The wagons' creaking echoed off stone and came back altered, so that it seemed the canyon itself was groaning, complaining about the weight of them. Kestrel kept his eyes moving — walls, waterline, walls again — because a ranger learns early that still water is not the same as safe water.

It was near dusk, with the ribbon of sky going violet, that Brammel's engine died.

There was no drama to it. One moment the lead wagon was churning forward with its patient mechanical heartbeat; the next, a sound like a struck bell rang out from the boiler, a single high note of metal giving an opinion, and then nothing. Steam sighed from somewhere underneath. The wagon settled in the water with the weary finality of a tired animal lying down.

The convoy halted in a chain behind it, engines idling, their exhaust plumes rising pale against the darkening stone.

Brammel was under the wagon before anyone could stop him, flat on his back in eight inches of green water, arms buried to the elbow in the engine's guts. Kestrel crouched at the edge of the road and listened to him work: the clink of tools, muttered diagnostics, one long creative curse that echoed off three canyon walls.

"Well?" Vane demanded.

"It's the intake," Brammel rumbled from beneath. "Silt. The drowned road silted over back there and I sucked up a bellyful of it. Filter's choked solid. I can clear it, but I need the engine cold first, and I need it *dry*, or I'm just packing mud deeper while I dig." A pause. "Two hours cold. Maybe three."

Vane looked at the water rising gently around the wagon's axles. "And if the water rises faster than that?"

"Then we camp on the roof and discuss our options in the morning."

Kestrel walked the road ahead while they argued. His lantern threw a small circle of amber against the gathering dark, and beyond it the canyon went on swallowing itself — pool after pool, each one deeper than the last, the drowned road glimmering beneath the surface like a promise someone else had made. At the fourth pool he stopped and held the lantern low.

The water there was moving.

Not flowing — seeping. A slow, silent creep, sliding across the flagstones toward the convoy, coming from everywhere and nowhere. He knelt and touched it. Cold. Colder than the air, colder than the pools behind them. And it was higher on the stone than it should have been; he could see the wet line on the canyon wall a handspan above the current surface, then another above that, fainter, older — tide marks, written in mineral ghost-script, climbing the rock like a ladder.

He'd seen marks like those once before, as a young ranger, on a canyon wall where a salvage crew's last camp had been found. Empty bedrolls. A cold fire. No bodies.

He walked back fast.

* * *

"The whole floor floods," Kestrel said, dropping Alden's old survey notes onto Vane's chart table — the folding desk in the second wagon where she planned her tides. "Not from rain. From below. These canyons sit on top of the aquifer system, and twice a day the pressure pushes water up through every crack in the floor. My brother mapped the timing on his last run. High water comes with the night tide. We have maybe four hours before this road is ten feet under."

Vane studied the notes. Her face did what it always did — gave away nothing, admitted nothing — but her finger tapped once against the table, which for Sable Vane was the equivalent of another woman screaming.

"Brammel needs three hours cold," she said.

"You don't have three hours. You have maybe two before the water starts climbing past the axles, and when it does, this whole convoy is sitting in a bathtub with no drain."

"Then give me an alternative, Thornbury, because I don't see one. The road is the road. It's the only way through to the Reach."

"There's another way." He said it carefully, because saying it meant committing to it. "My brother found it. It's in these notes — he called it the Pilgrim's Causeway. A raised shelf of natural stone that runs along the north wall, maybe sixty feet above the floor. Old pilgrim route, pre-roads. It bypasses five miles of flooded canyon and comes back down to the road past the deep section." He met her eyes. "But it's narrow. Single-file wagons, and one stretch where the outer edge crumbled years ago. If a wheel goes over, the wagon goes into the canyon, and there's no retrieving it. No retrieving anyone."

Silence, except for the distant idling of engines and the soft, obscene whisper of rising water.

"Your brother's notes," Vane said at last. "How accurate?"

"Alden didn't write anything inaccurate in his life. It got him killed."

Something moved behind her eyes at that — too quick to name — and then she turned and raised her voice. "Hesk! Get the crew leaders. We're moving in ten minutes."

* * *

Finding the causeway's mouth in the dark was the hardest part, and Kestrel did it the way his father had taught him: not by looking for the path but by looking for what the path avoided. The pilgrims who'd carved the route had been desert people, and desert people built shelter from the wind, not views. Where the canyon wall formed a deep re-entrant, a hollow like a cupped hand, Kestrel stopped the lead wagon and climbed.

The trail was there. Narrow, rubble-strewn, hugging the wall — but there, switchbacking up through the dark, marked here and there by stacked cairns that centuries of wind had worn into slumped, headstone shapes. He stood at the base of it and felt something twist in his chest that had nothing to do with fear. Alden had walked this. Alden had stood in this exact spot, probably at this exact hour, reading the same stone.

*Keep up, little brother,* he thought, and started climbing.

Getting six wagons up a pilgrim trail built for foot traffic was two hours of the worst work Kestrel had ever supervised, and he had once spent a summer dragging a stuck well-bore out of a sand sea. Brammel, engine cleared and grudgingly restarted, refused to let anyone else drive his machine up the shelf. "She listens to me," he growled, and drove her up switchbacks so tight the rear wheels nearly kissed the front, his hands moving on the controls with a tenderness he showed to nothing else alive.

Osha walked the outside edge of every wagon as it passed the exposed stretches, checking the ground with a long steel probe, calling out holds and soft spots. She worked in silence, economical, her eyes catching the lantern light like an animal's. Twice she stopped a wagon cold with a raised fist, probed, moved a stone, waved it on. Kestrel found himself watching her more than the trail.

The bad stretch came at midnight.

The shelf narrowed to barely wider than a wagon bed, and on the left — the outer edge — a chunk of causeway the size of a chapel had fallen away sometime in the last decade, leaving a gap spanned only by rubble and prayer. Below it, the canyon dropped away black and breathing. Kestrel went out alone with lanterns and flagstones and laid a crossing: wedging flat stones into the gap, testing each with his full weight, building a road wide enough for one wheel at a time.

"Thornbury." Vane stood at the near edge, lantern high. "If it won't hold, we turn back."

"If we turn back, we drown on the road instead of falling off it. Same ending, worse view." He set another stone. "It'll hold. Stone laid on stone with the weight spread — my father built bridges in the Ashfall country. First thing he taught me: the stone wants to hold. You just have to ask it properly."

"And did your father teach you everything?"

"He taught me everything except how to put down a debt." He heard her almost-smile in the dark.

The lead wagon crossed at ten minutes past midnight. Kestrel walked beside the front wheel the entire way, guiding Brammel by voice through the gap, close enough that his boot heel hung over the void, close enough to see the green-black nothing breathing below. The flagstones held. The second wagon followed, then the sledges, then the rest, each driver sweating through the crossing while the canyon wind pulled at their sleeves like beggars.

When the last wagon cleared, Osha appeared at his elbow. She looked out at the gap, then at him.

"Your brother's notes," she said. Her voice was low and rough, like water over gravel. "You read them like scripture too."

"They're all I have of him."

"No." She shook her head slowly. "You have his eyes. You look at country the same way. Like it owes you an answer." She turned back toward the wagons. "Get some sleep, ranger. Tomorrow I want to know what else he wrote."

* * *

They descended to the road past dawn, where the canyon opened and the water lay flat and silver in the early light, ten feet deep over the place where they would have been sleeping. Nobody said anything about it. Nobody needed to. But when Kestrel rode point again, he noticed the drivers of the second and third wagons lifting two fingers off their reins as he passed — a salute, small and unspoken, the kind working people give when words would embarrass everyone.

Even Hesk lifted his fingers. Though with Hesk, Kestrel thought, it might just be counting.

At noon they halted in a side gorge to water the teams, and Vane found him sitting on a ledge above the camp, eating hardtack and watching the canyon mouth ahead, where the walls fell away toward the Glass Reach shimmering in the heat-haze.

"Thornbury." She sat down beside him without asking, which was also unlike her, or exactly like her. "That causeway. Your brother's route. You saved this convoy tonight."

"Stone wanted to hold. I asked it properly."

"Don't do that."

"Do what?"

"Deflect. I've known you eleven years, Kestrel. You were deflecting the day we met." She looked out at the heat-shimmer, and for a moment her voice lost its captain's edge. "I owe you thanks. I'm not good at owing. So consider it said."

"Noted," Kestrel said.

She stood, brushed grit from her coat, and paused. "One more thing. Tonight, on the shelf — Osha said you stopped at the third cairn and touched it. Like you knew it."

Kestrel looked at her. "Alden built that cairn. I'd know his stacking anywhere. Three stones, flat sides out. He learned it from me." He turned back to the shimmering Reach. "He was here, Vane. Whatever happened to him, he stood on that exact trail. Which means the Provision isn't a story to me anymore. It's a destination."

Vane was quiet a long moment. Then: "Eat your hardtack, ranger. We make the Reach by tomorrow's tide."

She walked back down toward the wagons. Kestrel stayed on his ledge, chewing the stale bread, watching the heat-devils dance over the drowned country behind them — and that was when he saw it, far off across the flats they'd crossed yesterday, at the very edge of vision where heat made the world liquid:

Dust. A long, thin line of it, moving parallel to their route, keeping pace.

Too organized for weather. Too steady for mirage.

Someone was running the same race they were, and they were not slowing down.
