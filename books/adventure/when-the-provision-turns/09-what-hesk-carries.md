## 9. What Hesk Carries

Osha had learned to read water before she could read words, and she had learned to read people the same way — by what moved beneath the surface.

Hesk's surface was smooth. That was the problem.

She watched him now from the shadow of the second wagon's canvas awning, a tin cup of brackish tea going cold in her hands, as the quartermaster made his evening rounds along the convoy. He counted everything. That was his trade and his armor: sacks of salt-preserved meat, coils of rope, the water barrels lashed under tarps, the ration tallies chalked on slate. He counted with a little brass clicker he kept in his left palm, and his lips moved with each number, and there was nothing in his face at all. A man counting could look at anything without seeming to look at it.

The convoy was three days out of Marrowgate, camped in the lee of a ridge the maps called the Knuckle because it rose from the flats like four bent fingers of stone. The first tide had gone well — too well, Osha thought. They'd made better distance than Vane's schedule promised, and Kestrel Thornbury had found water where the charts said none was, a seep beneath a shelf of gypsum that tasted of iron but kept them from dipping into reserves. The crew had eaten well and slept hard, and this morning they'd crossed the last of the open pan and begun the long descent toward the drowned canyons, where the old road sank beneath green water and the journey became something else entirely.

And somewhere behind them, over the curve of the world, the Corvane fleet was moving too.

Osha hadn't seen it herself. She'd only heard Kestrel describe it at the evening fire two nights back — dust on the southern horizon, a column of it, moving with the discipline of machines rather than men. Vane had ordered silence about it. Not secrecy exactly; the crew knew. But silence in the sense that no one spoke of it, the way you don't speak of a tumor while the surgeon's knife is still clean.

Now, watching Hesk, Osha thought about silence differently.

* * *

It began, as most rot begins, small.

On the second night out, Osha had come off her dive-gear check late — Brammel liked the compressors bled and the tanks racked in a particular order, and she obliged him because a diver who annoyed her engineer dived on short air — and she'd crossed the camp in the dark toward her bedroll. The ridge threw its shadow early here, and the stars came down close and hard, and in that hard starlight she saw Hesk standing apart from the fires, up on the first knuckle of stone, with something in his hand that caught the light.

A flare stick. Unlit. He was holding it up against the sky, sighting along it southward, the way a man sights a rifle.

He didn't light it. He stood there maybe a minute, turning it, checking it, and then he put it away inside his coat — inside the coat, not his kit, which was where a quartermaster kept inventory — and climbed down and went to his bedroll and slept, or lay still enough to pass for sleeping.

Osha had told herself a dozen explanations by morning. Flares were part of any convoy's stores; a quartermaster checked his stores. Maybe he was checking the burn time. Maybe he was counting them. The man counted everything; maybe flares were just one more number.

But he'd sighted it south. South was Corvane.

So tonight she watched, and she waited, and she told Brammel she'd take the middle watch alone, which suited her because Brammel snored like a pump with a cracked gasket and because nobody questions the diver when she says she wants quiet. Divers were allowed their strangeness. It came with the trade — you spend your working life in a green dark where sound travels wrong and your breath is the loudest thing in the world, and people learn not to press you.

At moonrise — a thin one, barely enough to cast a shadow — Hesk got up.

He moved well for a soft-bellied man. No fumbling with his boots, no rustle of canvas. He'd prepared for this the way he prepared for everything: precisely, quietly, in advance. He took a bundle from under his bedroll and went up onto the rocks, and this time he did not merely sight along the flare. This time he struck it.

The flare went up with a hiss and a bloom of red, climbing high above the Knuckle, and for three full seconds the whole camp might have seen it — but the camp was asleep, and the fires were banked low, and Osha was the only pair of eyes in the world watching red light climb into black sky and hang there, bleeding color across the stars, before it guttered and died.

Southward, past the edge of the ridge, past the dark shoulder of the flats, something answered.

Not a flare. A lamp. One flash, then two, then a long third — far off, so far it might have been a star falling wrong, but Osha had spent years reading lights off wrecks at night, distinguishing lantern from phosphorescence from the running lights of salvage barges, and she knew a signal when she saw one. Three flashes. Acknowledgment. Receipt of message.

Hesk stood a moment longer, looking south. Then he turned, and Osha pressed herself flat into the shadow between a water barrel and the wagon wheel, heart going slow and deep the way she'd trained it to go, and listened to his footsteps cross the sand and fade toward his bedroll.

* * *

She didn't wake Vane.

That surprised her later, when she had time to think about it. It would have been the sensible thing — go to the captain, report what she'd seen, let command handle it. But something stopped her, some instinct older than sense, and it took her half the night's remaining dark to name it.

If Hesk reported to someone aboard, reporting to Vane would tell that someone that Osha had seen. And if Hesk reported to someone outside — to Corvane, to whoever paid him — then Vane already knew, or worse, Vane was the someone, and Osha's report would be a drop of water vanishing into a deeper well than she could see the bottom of.

Paranoia, she thought, is just pattern-reading with bad data. Every diver knows the feeling: the shape in the murk that is either a wreck rib or a shark, and until you're close enough to touch it, both are equally real.

She wanted more data.

She got it the next evening, and it was almost too easy, which itself was information. Hesk ran his inventory at dusk as always, clicker clicking, and when he finished the flare case — a steel box lashed under the lead wagon's bench — Osha, who had positioned herself refilling canteens ten feet away with nothing on her mind but water, watched his hands. Quartermasters' hands tell stories. She'd learned that from her father, who'd run stores on a dredge fleet and could weigh a sack of grain by the sound of it landing.

Hesk's hands paused on the flare case a half-second longer than they paused on anything else. And when he closed it, he did not snap the hasp home. He left it resting, loose, the way a man leaves a door he intends to come back through.

Then he walked to the camp's edge, north side, and stood looking out at the darkening flats for a long time, and Osha saw his shoulders do something they never did during inventory. They rose, and fell. He sighed.

A man who sighs, she thought, is a man carrying something heavier than his kit.

* * *

She confronted him on the fourth night, at the edge of the drowned canyon country, where the land began its long broken descent toward water and the wind came up out of the depths smelling of mineral and old stone.

She chose the place deliberately. The convoy was pitched in a fold of rock with sentries posted — Pell and Pim, the twins, taking first watch together the way they always did, finishing each other's silences. Beyond the sentries, the ground dropped away into the first of the flooded cuts, black water visible below like a vein of obsidian. There was nowhere to run that wasn't down, and Hesk did not strike her as a man built for down.

He was at the flare case again. Of course he was. She'd timed it — third night, same hour, the habit of a careful man becoming the trap of a careless one. He had the case open and two flare sticks in his hands when Osha stepped out of the dark and said his name.

He didn't jump. Credit to him for that. He closed the case slowly, set the flares on top of it, and turned around with his face arranged into mild inquiry, though she could see the arrangement cost him something — a tightening at the corners of the eyes, a swallow that moved his whole throat.

"Osha." Her name in his mouth sounded like an item being checked off. "You're off watch."

"So are you."

"I don't sleep well on new ground." He gestured at the canyon dark beyond the ridge. "Bad air comes up out of those cuts at night. Old water breathing. A man gets restless."

"That why you're up here?" She nodded at the flares. "Restless?"

"They're inventory."

"You checked them an hour ago. I watched you count them. Twelve sticks, burn time marked on each casing in your own hand." She'd gone through the case herself that afternoon, while the wagons were being rigged, and memorized the tally. "You count everything twice, Hesk. I've watched you do it all week. But you don't take things out twice."

The wind came up the cut and moved between them, cold and wet-smelling, and somewhere below in the black water something shifted — a rock settling, or a fish, or nothing.

Hesk looked at her for a long moment. Then he did the thing she had not expected, the thing that reorganized everything she thought she knew about him: he sat down. Lowered himself onto the bench beside the flare case with the heaviness of a man setting down a load he'd carried so long his shoulders had grown around it.

"You're the diver," he said. "You go down into places where the truth is at the bottom and bring it up. So I'll say this once, plain, and you can decide what to do with it." He rubbed his face with both hands. "Yes. I've been signaling. Two nights, and tonight would have been the third. Red flare, south bearing. And yes, someone answers."

"Corvane."

"I don't know that it's Corvane." But his voice said he knew exactly what it was. "I know the bearing. I know the interval. I know what I'm paid, and I know it's more than Vane pays me, and I know that whoever pays it knew my sister's debts in Marrowgate down to the copper, which is a kind of knowledge that buys obedience cheaper than coin does."

Osha crouched down to his level, forearms on her knees, close enough to see the sweat on his upper lip despite the cold. "Paid to do what, exactly?"

"Report position. Bearing and distance, once a night. Nothing else." He held up a hand as if warding off the accusation she hadn't yet made. "I swear on whatever you want sworn on — I have not sold the route, the schedule, the crew roster, or the woman's coordinates. Position only. That was the term. Position only, and my sister walks out from under her creditors whole."

"And you believed them."

"I believed the part I needed to believe." He laughed, short and airless. "You're a practical woman, Osha. You've buried divers. You know how a man makes peace with a bad contract — he reads only the clauses that keep him alive."

"Who pays you?"

"No."

"Hesk—"

"No." And there, for the first time, was steel under the softness, and she understood suddenly how this man had survived years aboard ships full of harder people. "You can put me off this convoy, and whoever it is will simply find another pair of eyes, ones I haven't talked to, ones I haven't warned. You can put me in the water, and my sister drowns in debt within the month — they showed me the ledger, Osha, they showed me every line of it. Or you can leave me be, and I keep signaling position and nothing else, and I keep my ears open, and anything I hear that isn't position — anything at all — comes to you. Not to Vane. To you."

"Why me?"

"Because you caught me." He said it simply. "Whoever else is watching this crew — and there is someone else, I'd bet my clicker on it, because I'm the distraction, and distractions are only bought when there's something worth hiding behind — whoever else it is, they haven't been caught. You have eyes that work in the dark. I'd rather they work for me than on me."

Osha sat back on her heels. Below them the black water breathed its cold breath upward, and she thought about the green dark, and shapes in murk, and how the only way through fear was forward, slowly, with your air metered and your hands steady.

"If you're lying," she said, "if there's one flare more than position in what you're selling—"

"There isn't."

"—then I will put you in that water myself, and I won't weight you. The canyon will decide how deep you go."

Something flickered across his face — not fear exactly. Recognition. The look of a man meeting, unexpectedly, another professional.

"Fair terms," he said.

"One more thing." She stood, and he looked up at her, and she let the pause stretch until it hurt. "The person who answers your flares. The lamp code — one, two, long three. Where did you learn it?"

His brow creased. "It was given to me. With the contract. Why?"

"Because it's a dredge-fleet acknowledgment." Her voice came out flat. "Old pattern. My father used it. It hasn't been standard in fifteen years." She watched the blood leave his face by degrees. "Somebody taught that code to your paymaster, Hesk. Somebody who worked the dredges. Somebody old."

She left him sitting there among the flares and walked back down into the camp, past the banked fires, past the wagons with their loads of rope and tanked air, and stood a while at the edge of the firelight watching Kestrel Thornbury sleep — or lie still enough to pass for sleeping — with his father's compass in one open hand, the way other men hold a talisman or a wound.

Position only, Hesk had said. But positions were how ambushes were drawn. Positions were how fleets arrived at basins ahead of you, at gates, at shallows, at exactly the wrong hour. Whoever held Hesk's reports wasn't collecting data. They were drawing a line on a map, and this convoy was walking down the length of it.

And the lamp code — the old dredge code, dead fifteen years, alive again in the dark south of the Knuckle — meant the paymaster wasn't Corvane born. It meant someone had gone over. Someone who had once, maybe, worn the same salt-stiff canvas as the rest of them.

Osha looked south, past the ridge, past the reach of firelight, to where the desert ran black to the edge of the world.

Tomorrow they descended into the drowned canyons, where the road went under and the wagons would have to pick their way along causeways older than any living memory, single file, exposed, slow. Perfect ground for a fleet that already knew where its prey would be.

She went to find Vane.

And found, instead, that Vane's tent was empty, her bedroll cold, and Kestrel Thornbury standing at the camp's edge in the starlight, watching the southern dark, holding a scrap of paper that had not been in his possession at sundown — his face lit faintly red, as if by a flare only he could see.
