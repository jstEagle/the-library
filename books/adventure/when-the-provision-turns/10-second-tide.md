## 10. Second Tide

The storm came out of the west like a wall being pushed over.

Kestrel saw it first — a brown smear on the horizon that thickened as he watched, climbing into the sky until it blotted out the sun and turned the afternoon the color of old brass. He had grown up reading storms the way other men read letters, and this one said nothing good. It was wide, it was fast, and it was aimed straight down the throat of the Glass Reach.

"Vane!" he shouted, cupping his hands against the wind that was already arriving in advance parties — hot gusts that stung with salt and grit. "Storm's coming! Big one!"

She was up on the lead wagon before he finished, one hand shading her eyes, her coat snapping like a flag in distress. For a long moment she didn't move, just read the horizon the way he did, and when she turned back her face had gone flat and hard.

"How long?"

"Hour. Maybe less if the wind holds." Kestrel pointed north-northeast, where a line of low black hills broke the otherwise featureless plain. "There's a basin behind those rises. Old volcanic caldera, half-collapsed. The Saltreach charts call it the Cup. Rangers used to shelter there during the dry-season blows."

"You've been there?"

"Twice. It'll hold both fleets."

The word was out of his mouth before he could weigh it, and he felt Vane's eyes sharpen on him like a blade finding the gap in armor.

*Both fleets.*

Because of course they weren't alone anymore. The Corvane convoy had been visible since midmorning — a smudge of dust paralleling them three miles south, close enough now that Kestrel could make out individual wagons through his spyglass, the glint of mounted outriders, the tall signal mast flying Corvane's black-and-silver pennant. They were faster. They had been faster for two days, gaining ground by inches, and everyone in Vane's crew knew what it meant: sooner or later the two convoys would arrive at the Shallows together, and then the race would become something else entirely.

Now the storm was going to force the *something else* early.

"Get us into that basin," Vane said. "All wagons, full water, hobbled teams. If Maddox wants shelter too, he can have the far side of it. Nobody crosses the middle. Understood?"

"Understood," Kestrel said, and meant it, and wondered how long a promise like that would survive contact with Rell Maddox.

* * *

They made the Cup with perhaps twenty minutes to spare.

It was exactly as Kestrel remembered — a great broken bowl of dark stone, half a mile across, its walls stepped in terraces where ancient lava had cooled in folds. Wind-scoured sand lay in the bottom in long rippled sheets, and a spring-fed seep trickled from a crack in the northern wall, barely enough to wet the stones but enough to matter. The eastern rim had collapsed at some point in the deep past, leaving a sloped ramp that let wagons down in single file.

Kestrel stood at the top of that ramp and directed the convoy in, calling angles to Brammel, who wrestled the engine wagon down with a stream of curses so inventive that Pell, watching from below, actually applauded. Osha came last, walking beside the water wagon with her hand resting on its flank as though it were a horse she trusted more than people.

By the time the last wagon reached the floor, the sky had gone the color of a bruise and the wind arrived for real.

It hit the basin like a living thing. The storm's leading edge screamed over the western rim and filled the hollow with a howling brown chaos, sand moving not in gusts but in rivers, in flood, the whole air become a grinding current that scoured paint off wood and skin off knuckles. The crews lashed canvas down, doubled the wagon ties, and retreated into the lee of the terraces, where the stone broke the wind into eddies and let men breathe.

Kestrel found a shelf of rock on the southern side, out of sight of the eastern ramp, and sat with his back against stone and his father's compass in his palm. He wasn't checking it. There was nowhere to check it against. He just needed the weight of it, the small cold circle of brass, while the world outside tried to erase itself.

That was how Osha found him, an hour into the blow. She dropped onto the rock beside him without asking, close enough that their shoulders touched, and handed him a strip of dried fish without looking at him.

"Eat," she said. "You gave your ration to the twins' team yesterday. I count things. It's a diver's habit."

He ate. The fish was salt-rough and tasted like nothing, but his body took it gratefully.

"They're coming in," Osha said after a while, nodding toward the eastern ramp through the murk. "Corvane. I counted eight wagons and maybe thirty riders. Hesk is already down there talking to their outriders like they're old friends at a market."

Kestrel's jaw tightened. "Vane know?"

"Vane told Hesk to stay in camp. Hesk heard her perfectly well and went anyway." Osha's voice was dry as the sand streaming overhead. "I'm starting to think our quartermaster hears only what pays him."

Through the brown curtain, shapes moved on the far side of the basin — lanterns kindling against the gloom, wagons drawn into a defensive ring, riders dismounting and leading their animals to the seep. Even muffled by the storm, the sounds carried strangely in the stone bowl: the creak of Corvane's rigging, a snatch of laughter, the high whine of a much finer engine than Brammel's.

Then a voice, cutting through everything, pitched to carry across open ground:

"Thornbury! Dominic Thornbury's boy! Get over here — I won't bite, and neither will my crew while the sky's falling!"

Osha looked at him. "You know that voice?"

"No," Kestrel lied.

He knew it. He'd know it anywhere. Seven years older than Alden, seven years louder, Rell Maddox had been the third member of every trouble his brother ever found — the big laughing boy from the ranger academy who could drink anyone under a table and swim any channel, who had stood at Alden's shoulder in a hundred of Kestrel's own memories. *Rell and Alden and me against the whole Saltreach,* Alden used to say. Except somewhere along the way Rell had gone one direction and Alden another, and the last Kestrel had heard, Maddox had sold his ranger's commission to Corvane Salvage and never looked back.

Kestrel stood. His legs felt strange, like they belonged to someone younger.

"I should go," he said.

"Should you?" Osha asked quietly. "Or do you want to?"

He didn't answer. She didn't expect him to. That was the thing about Osha — she asked questions she already knew the shape of, and then let you carry them yourself.

* * *

Crossing the basin floor was like wading upstream through a river of sand. The storm had settled into its long middle hours now, the wind steady and enormous, visibility down to forty feet of churning brown. Kestrel kept the eastern wall on his left and walked, head down, goggles up, until the Corvane camp materialized around him out of the murk — and it was a different order of camp entirely.

Where Vane's crew traveled lean and mean, Corvane traveled *armed*. Wagons bristled with harpoon mounts. Riders' rifles hung in oiled slings across their backs. A cook tent stood rigid against the wind, guyed with steel cable, and beneath it sat men eating hot food off real plates. Everything gleamed. Everything was maintained. Money, Kestrel thought, you could smell it even through the grit.

And in the center of it, seated on an overturned crate with a tin cup in his hand, was a mountain of a man wrapped in a white duster gone grey with dust.

Rell Maddox had been handsome once, in the blunt way of prizefighters. Time and weather had worked him over — a scar split one eyebrow, his nose had been broken at least twice, and his hair had gone iron at the temples — but none of it diminished him. If anything the damage improved him, the way weather improves stone. He saw Kestrel coming and rose, and rose, and kept rising, until he stood a full head taller, and then he smiled, and the smile was Alden's smile, worn by someone else's face.

"There he is," Maddox said. "Seven years I've wondered what happened to you, and here you are walking out of a sandstorm like a story. Sit down. Eat something that isn't jerky for once."

"I'm not staying."

"You're staying until the storm breaks or I'll have my boys tie you to a post, and we'll both pretend it's hospitality." He said it lightly, but two of the nearby riders shifted, and Kestrel understood the offer came with infrastructure. Fine. He sat. A plate appeared — rice and some kind of spiced fowl, actual heat in it, actual care — and Kestrel realized with a small humiliation how hungry he was, and how long it had been since anyone fed him like this.

Maddox watched him eat with something that wasn't quite pity and wasn't quite hunger. Then he waved his crew back, and for a few minutes there was only the storm roaring over the rim and the two of them sitting in its shadow like men in the eye of something larger than weather.

"Alden wrote to me," Maddox said at last. "Before the Provision run. Did you know that?"

Kestrel's spoon stopped. "No."

"Course you didn't. He wouldn't have. Alden kept his letters like he kept his debts — private, paid, no witnesses." Maddox turned his cup slowly in his huge hands. "He wrote that he'd found something. Something worth more than the cargo manifest said. He wrote that when he came back, things were going to change — for the range, for the water, for all of it. And he wrote—" Maddox paused, and for the first time the big man's voice caught on something, "—he wrote that he wished you'd been there to see it, Kestrel. That of everyone in the world, you were the one he wanted standing next to him when it came true."

The storm howled. Kestrel stared at his plate.

"He never came back," Kestrel said.

"No. He didn't." Maddox set the cup down. "And I've spent seven years trying to find out why, same as you. Difference is, I've got the fleet to do it properly. Corvane doesn't send four wagons and a prayer. We send what it takes." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and his eyes — pale grey, almost colorless — fixed on Kestrel with uncomfortable directness. "So here's what I want to say to you, and I want you to hear all of it before you decide I'm the enemy.

"Vane hired you because you're cheap and you know the flats. Fine. But Vane isn't looking for answers about Alden. Vane is looking for salvage tonnage and a bonus clause. When we reach the Provision — and we will reach it, Kestrel, my engines are a day ahead of hers and my divers are better — Vane will strip that wreck for anything that weighs anything, and whatever your brother actually found down there will get logged, auctioned, and forgotten inside a season. That's not villainy. That's just what salvage *is*."

"And Corvane's different?" Kestrel asked. His voice came out flatter than he intended.

"Corvane is bigger, so its mistakes are bigger. But I'll tell you what I intend, and I'll put it in writing if you like." Maddox's voice dropped, went rough. "Alden was my brother too. Not by blood. By choice, which is the kind that counts. Whatever he found in that hold, I mean to finish what he started — bring it home, see it used right, and put his name on it. Not Vane's name. Not Corvane's board of directors. *His.*" He spread his hands. "You want to be there for that? Walk away from her contract. Come with me. You'll have a wage that clears your debts in a month, a berth for as long as you want it, and a crew that'll stand with you instead of counting your rations behind your back."

The offer hung in the storm-dark like a lantern.

And God help him, Kestrel felt it pull. Not the money — though the money pulled too, hard, ten days of Doss's leash yanking at his throat — but the rest of it. The *brotherhood* of it. Sitting here in the warm light of a real fire with real food in his belly, hearing Alden's name spoken by the last man alive who had loved him besides Kestrel himself — it would have been easier to refuse a drink of water in the deep desert. Every lonely year since the news came, some part of Kestrel had been walking toward this exact moment: someone who would say *your brother mattered*, and mean it, and have the power to prove it.

"You knew him," Kestrel said quietly. "Really knew him."

"Better than anyone breathing," Maddox said. "Which is why I'm asking, not ordering. He talked about you the way men talk about saints, Kestrel. Stubborn, honest, too proud to drown. I figure some of that rubbed off on me." He smiled again, tired this time. "Come with me. Finish it together. For Alden."

For a long moment Kestrel didn't speak. The storm thundered over the rim of the Cup, and somewhere across the basin Vane's camp was surely watching the glow of Corvane's fires and drawing its own conclusions, and Hesk was surely filing reports, and the tide of everything was still rolling toward the Shallows whether Kestrel sat here or stood up now.

"I need to know one thing first," Kestrel said.

"Ask."

"The message Alden sent back. From the wreck. Did it reach Corvane?"

Something flickered across Maddox's face — quick, quickly mastered, but not quickly enough. Not for a man who had spent his life reading weather and liars.

"What message?" Maddox said.

"That's what I thought," Kestrel said softly, and stood up.

Maddox rose with him, and for a heartbeat the two of them faced each other across the crate, the storm screaming, the firelight jumping, and Kestrel saw the calculation move behind those pale eyes — the offer recalculating, the brotherhood re-weighting itself against utility. It lasted less than a second. Then Maddox laughed, big and easy, and clapped Kestrel on the shoulder hard enough to stagger him.

"Stubborn," Maddox said fondly. "Just like he said. Go on back to your side of the bowl, Thornbury. Storm'll break by morning, and then we'll see whose engines are better." He picked up his cup, and his voice followed Kestrel into the dark, cheerful and carrying: "But think about my offer! It stands as long as I do!"

Kestrel walked back into the brown roar with the taste of good food turning sour in his mouth and one thought repeating like a footfall:

*Maddox lied. About the message. About something.*

And if Maddox was lying — then either Alden's message had never reached anyone, or it had reached everyone, and every soul in this basin was lying about it.

Across the basin, through a gap in the storm, he saw a single red flare climb above Vane's camp, hang burning against the murk, and fall.

Hesk's work. Signaling again.

Kestrel lengthened his stride. The storm could keep the sky. He had a traitor to catch before morning.
