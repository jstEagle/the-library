## 11. Maddox's Story

The sandstorm had passed in the night, leaving the basin scoured and pale as a dead man's palm. Kestrel woke with grit between his teeth and Maddox's voice already in his ears — not because the man was talking, but because everything he'd said by the fire kept replaying whether Kestrel wanted it to or not.

*Alden reached the Provision alive.*

He sat up on his bedroll beneath the wagon's shadow and watched the Corvane camp across the basin. Two hundred yards of hardpan separated them, and in the gray light before dawn the enemy looked almost peaceful: cookfires banked to coals, wagons huddled like beasts at rest, sentries walking slow circles with their lanterns hooded. Rell Maddox had shared salt-bread and bitter tea with them last night under a flag of storm-truce, and now that truce hung over the basin like weather neither fleet could afford to break.

Kestrel found Vane at the lead wagon, already awake, already armored in her competence — gloves on, charts out, hair bound back so tight it looked like discipline made visible.

"You knew," he said.

She didn't look up from the chart. "Good morning to you too."

"Maddox says Alden reached the Provision. Says he sent word back. Word that *you* intercepted." He kept his voice low, flat, the way he'd learned to speak when anger wanted to make him loud. "Seven years I thought my brother died out there with no one the wiser. And you're telling me there's a letter."

Vane rolled the chart closed with deliberate slowness, as if the motion itself were an answer she could hide behind. Then she looked up, and whatever he expected to find in her face — guilt, defiance, the old sharp amusement — none of it was there. What was there was worse. It was tiredness.

"Walk with me," she said.

* * *

They walked the rim of the basin while the sky went from ash to copper. The storm had carved new shapes into the flats overnight; whole dunes had migrated, and the causeway they'd used coming in now ended in a shelf of soft silt that would swallow a wheel to its hub. The land rearranged itself while you slept. That was the desert's oldest lesson, and Kestrel had never decided whether it was cruelty or mercy.

"I intercepted a message," Vane said at last. "That part is true. I'll give Maddox that much — he always did know how to cut with the truth instead of the lie. Lies heal over. Truth keeps bleeding."

"When?"

"Fourteen months after your brother's expedition went dark. A courier bird came into Marrowgate carrying a tube sealed with Provision registry wax. It should have gone to the guild. It came to me because I was running the salvage desk that season, and because—" she paused, choosing, "—because someone paid for it to come to me. I never learned who. The bird's leg band had been re-stamped. Someone in the guild rookery redirected it."

Kestrel stopped walking. "You've known this for seven years and you're telling me now. On day nine of a race where every hour costs us water."

"If you'll let me finish," Vane said, "you'll understand why telling you sooner would have been the cruelest thing I could have done."

"That's not your call to make."

"No," she agreed quietly. "It wasn't. That's rather the point of what I'm about to say."

The sun cleared the eastern rim and laid a long blade of light across the basin floor. Down below, the Corvane camp was stirring. Figures moved between the wagons. Somewhere a harness bell rang once and went silent.

"The message was short," Vane said. "Alden wrote it — I recognized the hand, we'd served together two years before he took the Provision contract, before you and I—" She stopped herself. Kestrel let her stop. "It was three lines. No coordinates. No request for rescue. Three lines, and the last line was a warning."

"What did it say?"

Vane turned to face him fully, and he saw her hands were not quite steady, which frightened him more than any lie would have.

"It said: *We reached her. She's more than cargo. One of our own sold us, and I know the name.*"

The words went into Kestrel like cold water into a crack in stone — painless going in, and then the pressure of everything around them changed. He heard himself ask, "What name?"

"There was no name. That was all of it. *I know the name.* And then nothing. Seven years of nothing." Vane's jaw tightened. "I reported the interception to the guild board within the hour. Do you know what they told me? They told me the message was a forgery. They told me the Provision registry wax had been counterfeited twice that same year by slaver crews working the coast. They told me that if a genuine survivor signal existed, the guild's own search protocol would have surfaced it, and since their protocol had already concluded the expedition lost, the message must be false. Case closed. File sealed. My career continued."

"And you believed them."

"I *wanted* to believe them." For the first time since Marrowgate, since the contract, since the apology she hadn't meant — Vane's voice cracked, just slightly, like ice bearing weight. "Kestrel, if that message was real, then I sat behind a desk and filed away the only proof your brother ever lived long enough to send word home. Do you understand what that does to a person? I told myself the guild was right. I told myself for seven years. And then Maddox shows up at the edge of the Glass Reach with your brother's name in his mouth and a story about a letter, and every wall I built comes down in an afternoon."

Kestrel looked out over the basin. His hands had gone numb without his noticing, the way they did in deep cold or deep shock. *One of our own sold us.* Alden's crew had been eleven people. Rangers, divers, a navigator, two engineers. People whose faces Kestrel still carried, some of them. People who had eaten at his table when the expeditions came through Marrowgate.

"You said it named a traitor," he said slowly. "Maddox said it named a survivor. You're both holding pieces of the same letter and telling me different things."

"Maddox is telling you what serves him." Vane's composure was returning, layer by layer, like sediment settling after a flood. "Think about it. If Alden survived to reach the wreck, then the ledger might have been recovered, catalogued, cached — recoverable. That's worth a war. But if Alden died sealing something away, if the last thing he did was bury the aquifer map to keep it from the man who sold him—"

"Then the ledger's a grave good," Kestrel finished.

"Then the ledger is worthless except to whoever wants to dig up a grave." She held his eyes. "Maddox needs Alden to have survived. So that's the story Maddox tells. I need you to hear the uglier version, because you're the only one aboard who'll believe it."

"Why would I believe you? You lied to me for seven years by omission. You signed me onto this run knowing what the Provision meant to me and never saying a word about any message."

"Yes."

"Just — yes? That's all you have?"

"It's all that's true." She said it simply, without flinching, and somehow that was harder to forgive than a defense would have been. "I could give you reasons. I have them. They'd even be good ones. But you'd smell the varnish on them, and you'd be right to. I withheld it. I was wrong. I'm sorry. None of those sentences undoes anything, and I'm not going to pretend otherwise to buy back your trust, because I can't afford the price and neither can you."

* * *

He left her at the rim and walked down into the basin alone, past the picket lines, past Osha's dive gear drying on a rack Brammel had welded from wagon scrap. Osha watched him pass but didn't call out — she'd developed that instinct around him, the sense of when a man needed the silence more than the company. He was grateful and resented it in equal measure.

At the neutral ground between the camps, Maddox was waiting, as if he'd known Kestrel would come. The Corvane captain sat on an overturned crate with a tin cup in his hands, and up close, in honest light, he looked older than his reputation. Gray at the temples. A scar through one eyebrow that Kestrel didn't remember from the guild rolls.

"She told you," Maddox said. Not a question.

"She told me her version."

"Mm." Maddox gestured at the hardpan across from him. Kestrel didn't sit. Maddox shrugged and stayed seated, which was its own kind of message: *I'm not a threat unless you make me one.*

"Let me guess the shape of it," Maddox went on. "She admits the letter existed. She admits she buried it. And then she gives you a version where Alden's last act was suspicion — where he died pointing at a traitor, so that the ledger becomes poison, so that nobody goes looking. Am I close?"

"She said the letter warned of a traitor. She said there was no name in it."

"There was no name in *her copy*." Maddox set down the cup. "Because there was more than one bird, Thornbury. Your brother wasn't a fool. He split the message — half to the guild, half to a private channel. The second bird came south, to the coastal relay at Brackwater. I was running Corvane's Brackwater office that year. I bought the tube off a relay clerk before the guild's people got within a hundred miles of it."

Kestrel felt the ground shift again, that sensation of standing on the salt crust over open water. "And your half said?"

"Said he was alive. Said the hold was sealed but sound. Said he'd found something under the Provision that made the water wars look like children fighting over a puddle — his words. Said he was staying until the tides gave him a window to bring it out himself." Maddox's voice had gone quiet, and quiet made it heavier. "Said if he didn't come out by the spring tide, it meant the traitor found him first. And asked — this is the part Vane will hate — asked that if anyone came looking, it be you. By name. *Send Kestrel. He won't count wrong.*"

The basin seemed very still. Kestrel became aware of his own heartbeat, slow and enormous, like something turning over underwater.

"He wouldn't write that," Kestrel said. "Not to you. He didn't trust Corvane. He called your fleet pirates with letterhead."

"He did." Maddox smiled, and there was real grief in it. "To his face, at the Marrowgate docks, spring of the drought year. And then eighteen months later he trusted me with his life in a letter, because out there, past the Reach, the flags stop mattering. Out there there's just who answers when you knock." He stood, unhurried, brushing grit from his trousers. "Ask yourself the question that matters, ranger. If Vane received a genuine distress-and-survival signal and sat on it — why? Guild pressure explains a lot, but it doesn't explain why she never mentioned it to you in seven years of crossing paths. Unless the letter said something she couldn't afford for you to hear."

"She told me what it said."

"She told you what she could survive telling you." Maddox picked up his cup. "There's a difference. There's always a difference with Sable. Ask her sometime what she traded to get the Provision contract approved after the guild banned the route. Ask her who signed the waiver. Ask her why a banned job suddenly has guild paper on it." He raised the cup slightly, a toast to nothing. "I liked your brother. I'm sorry he's dead. But if he is dead, then somebody in that convoy of yours helped kill him, and they're probably still eating at your fire."

He walked back toward the Corvane lines, unhurried, and Kestrel stood alone on the neutral ground with both versions of his brother turning in him like the two sides of a tide.

* * *

He found Vane at the water wagon, checking seals. Her hands stilled when his shadow fell across the barrels, but she didn't turn around.

"Maddox says there were two birds," Kestrel said.

"I know what Maddox says. Maddox has been saying things since the day I met him."

"He says the second bird came south. To Brackwater. To him."

Vane's shoulders dropped a fraction — not surrender, he thought, but the settling of weight onto a structure built to bear it. "Then either he's lying," she said, "or we are both holding halves of a letter that was cut in two by a drowning man who didn't know which post was still standing." She turned. Her eyes were red-rimmed and dry. "You want to know which of us is telling the truth. I can't prove mine. He can't prove his. And I notice that both stories end the same way — with you going into that wreck. Isn't that convenient for everyone but you?"

"Nothing about this is convenient for me."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't." She looked past him, toward the eastern rim, where the sixth tide waited somewhere beyond the horizon like a debt coming due. "But here's what I can give you that Maddox can't. The guild file on the Provision expedition. Sealed, but I broke it open the night after Maddox first spoke your brother's name. There's a crew manifest addendum. A late substitution — one man swapped aboard two days before departure, paid through a third-party chit, identity flagged *unverified*. Eleven names went out. Ten bodies' worth of gear came back on the drift current over the next two years, logged by coastal patrols. Kestrel. Ten."

The arithmetic landed in him like a stone down a well. "One man never accounted for."

"One man," Vane said, "whose name was flagged unverified. Whose pay chit traces back through three shells to a counting house in Marrowgate." She let that sit exactly as long as it needed to. "Doss's counting house, Kestrel. Your creditor financed the man who may have sold your brother's crew. And now Doss holds your license, your gear, and your ten days."

The wind moved across the basin, lifting fine salt off the hardpan so that the whole world shimmered faintly, mirage-thin, as if reality itself had been sanded down to something you couldn't quite stand on. Kestrel thought of Doss's soft voice, his clean ledger-room, the way he'd said *ten days* like a man setting a clock rather than making a threat. He thought of Hesk, quiet at the edges of every conversation, always near the strongbox. He thought of Alden's handwriting, which he'd know anywhere, saying *trust no one who counts* — though he hadn't read those words yet, wouldn't for another night and a stolen key, and so for now they were only a shape moving under the surface of him, a thing he could feel but not name.

"Which story do I believe?" he asked, and it wasn't really a question for Vane.

"Neither," Vane said. "Believe the wreck. The wreck doesn't negotiate, doesn't intercept, doesn't edit. Whatever Alden found, it's still down there holding its shape, and it's the only witness left." She picked up the seal-wax and began working the barrel lids again, methodical, done with him. "Fourth tide tomorrow. Get some sleep."

He walked back toward the fires, and behind him the basin lay in its bowl of morning light, and above the far rim the dust of the Corvane column was already rising — they'd broken camp first, they always broke camp first — and Kestrel understood, with the calm of a man watching water rise past his knees, that every person in this convoy might be lying to him, and that the only road left ran straight down into the green dark where his brother had made his last choice.

He just had to reach it before anyone else decided what the truth was worth.
