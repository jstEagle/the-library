## 12. Night Watch

The basin had gone quiet the way deserts go quiet after a storm — not peacefully, but with the held breath of something that hadn't finished deciding what it wanted to do next.

Kestrel took the middle watch because no one else would take it. The sandstorm had spent itself against the basin's western rim around dusk, and now the air hung thick with grit that hadn't yet found its way back to earth, so that every lantern flame burned muddy and low and the stars came through like coins seen through dirty water. The convoy sat in a rough crescent at the basin's floor: Vane's lead wagon, the water wagon, Brammel's engine-wagon wheezing as its metal cooled, the two outriders lashed down under canvas. Beyond them, two hundred yards across the flat, the Corvane fleet sat in its own neat formation, lanterns burning brighter, horses stabled in picket lines that looked drilled even in the dark.

Two fleets sleeping in the same bowl of sand, close enough to hear each other's animals shifting. It was, Kestrel thought, the kind of arrangement that ended badly nine times out of ten, and the tenth time it ended worse.

He walked the perimeter slow, boots crunching on storm-polished sand, the rifle slung loose across his back where he could reach it without thinking. The watch rotation was his own design — he'd offered it to Vane an hour after Maddox rode off with his offer still hanging in the air between them, and she'd accepted with a single nod, which from Sable Vane was practically a speech of gratitude. Two hours each, four watchers, overlapping arcs. Simple. The kind of thing you did when you didn't trust the people camped beside you any more than the ones camped across from you.

Which was accurate. He trusted none of them — not Vane, not Hesk with his too-careful ledger entries and his eyes that went to Corvane's lanterns a half-second too often, not even Osha, though she'd earned more of his trust than anyone aboard since Marrowgate. Trust was a currency out here, and Kestrel had learned long ago that everyone was spending from someone else's purse.

The thought of purses turned his mind, as it had been turning all evening, to Vane's strongbox.

* * *

It sat in her quarters at the rear of the lead wagon — an iron-banded chest no bigger than a bread loaf, locked with a mechanism Kestrel had watched her open twice: once at Marrowgate to pay Hesk's advance, once on the second day to check their water scrip. She kept the key on a cord around her neck. She slept with it there. He'd seen the shape of it under her shirt when she emerged from her quarters at dawn, rumpled and sharp-eyed, already armored again in competence.

He had no plan to steal from it. That was what he told himself, anyway, as he made his first circuit and came back past the lead wagon and heard, faintly, Vane's voice inside — low, clipped, talking to Pell or Pim about the morning's route. And then the voice stopped, and boots crossed the deck, and Vane came down the wagon's steps with her coat on and her hair tied back, carrying a signal lamp.

"Can't sleep either?" she said.

"I sleep fine."

"You pace fine, anyway." She lifted the lamp toward the dark eastern rim of the basin. "I'm going to walk the line. Maddox's sentries will be doing the same, and I want them to see us doing it. Visible discipline. It keeps honest men honest and tells the dishonest ones we're watching."

"And you're telling me this why?"

"Because you're on watch, and if you see a lamp moving along our perimeter you should know it's mine and not shoot it." A ghost of the old smile, the one that had made him hate her seven years ago and distrust her ever since. "Come or don't."

She walked east. Kestrel stood a moment, watching the lamp shrink, and then — telling himself it was watch discipline, nothing more, checking the wagons was his job — he climbed the lead wagon's steps and let himself into the corridor behind her quarters.

Her door was shut but not locked. She never locked it; she said locks invited questions about what needed locking. Inside, the strongbox sat under the fold-down desk, exactly where it always sat, and the key cord hung over the peg by her bunk because she'd put her coat on over her head and left the cord behind.

Kestrel stood in the dark of her quarters for a long time. Long enough for the engine-wagon's cooling ticks to count out thirty heartbeats. Long enough to turn around twice.

Then he took the key off the peg, knelt, and opened the strongbox.

* * *

Inside: coin in a leather roll, maybe forty crowns — petty cash, the float for bribes and tolls. Water scrip stamped with the Marrowgate syndicate's seal. A folded chart of the Glass Reach with pencil corrections in Vane's hand. And beneath all of it, wrapped in oilcloth gone soft with age, a square of heavy paper that did not belong with the rest.

He knew before he unwrapped it. Some part of him had known since Maddox said the words *Alden reached the Provision alive and sent back word*, since Vane's face had done the small terrible thing it did — a tightening at the corners of the mouth, a flicker downward of the eyes — before she admitted, *I received a message.* Since she'd said it named a traitor, not a survivor, and refused to say more.

He unwrapped it anyway.

The paper was expedition stock, the heavy waxed kind the guild issued for field correspondence, water-resistant and meant to survive being carried in a coat through weather. The ink had faded to brown at the folds. The hand was cramped, hurried, written — Kestrel realized with a lurch that started in his stomach and climbed into his throat — with the paper braced on a knee, probably, or a crate-top, in bad light, fast, because fast mattered.

He knew the hand. He'd known it his whole life. Alden had written letters home from every posting, and his letters always slanted hard to the right when he rushed, and his *t*s always lost their crossbars when he pressed too hard, and here they were, seven years old and brown as autumn, missing their crossbars.

*Brother,* the message began, and Kestrel had to set the paper down on his knee for a moment because his hands were not steady and he would not smudge it.

*If this reaches anyone, reach Kestrel Thornbury, ranger, Saltreach district. Tell him —*

And then a smear, water or sweat or blood, and beneath the smear the writing resumed, tighter, smaller:

*— the Provision is not cargo. It never was. What it carries is worth more than the whole manifest and worth killing for, and someone on this crew has already decided to kill for it. We are three days out. The wreck sits over living water. The Provision turns. Trust no one who counts.*

No signature. No date beyond the guild stamp in the corner, dated — Kestrel checked it twice, made himself read the numerals slowly — six years, four months ago. Sixteen months after the expedition was declared lost. Sixteen months after the world had stopped looking for Alden Thornbury and started using his name as a cautionary tale.

Sixteen months after Vane received this, read it, and folded it into oilcloth and put it at the bottom of an iron box.

*Trust no one who counts.*

Kestrel read the line until the words stopped being words. The lamp outside had come back around; he could see its glow through the shutter seam, moving along the wagon's flank, pausing. He refolded the message along its original creases, rewrapped it in the oilcloth, and — this was the part he examined least closely, later, because examining it meant admitting what he'd become somewhere between Marrowgate and the drowned canyon — he did not put it back in the strongbox.

He put it inside his coat, against his chest, where the journal pages would someday rest, though he didn't know that yet. Then he locked the box, hung the key cord back on its peg, and stepped out into the corridor just as Vane's boots hit the bottom of the stairs.

* * *

"You're in my wagon," she said. Not loudly. Her lamp was raised, and the light caught the dust on his sleeves and the shape of the paper under his coat, or she pretended it did.

"Checking your seals," Kestrel said. "Storm damage. Standard watch procedure."

"There was no storm damage."

"No," he agreed. "There wasn't."

They stood in the corridor with the lamp between them, and outside, far off, one of Corvane's sentries coughed, and the sound was enormous in the silence. Vane's face in the lamplight was older than he remembered it being at dinner, older than it had been on the flats, and tired in a way that had nothing to do with the hour.

"You took it," she said.

"You knew what it was," he said. "You've known for six years. You let me sign onto this contract believing my brother was a question with no answer, and you had part of the answer folded up under your coin."

"It named a traitor, Kestrel. Not a survivor. I told you—"

"You told me what was convenient." His voice stayed level, which surprised him; he'd expected it to crack, and its steadiness felt like someone else's voice borrowed for the occasion. "'The Provision turns.' What does that mean, Sable? Turns how? Turns toward what? 'Trust no one who counts' — counts what? Coin? Rations? Days?" He stepped closer, and the lamp threw both their shadows huge against the wagon wall. "He was alive sixteen months after you gave up on him. Someone on his crew sold them out, and he knew it, and he wrote to me, and you sat on it. So don't stand there and tell me what the message named. Tell me what you named, when you decided my brother's last words were worth less than your career."

Vane didn't flinch. He'd forgotten that about her — or maybe he'd never known it, maybe it was new, this woman who had replaced the rival of seven years ago the way a river replaces itself, same name, different water. She set the lamp down on the desk, deliberately, and when she spoke her voice was quiet.

"If I had given it to you then, what would you have done?"

"What does it matter?"

"It matters because I'm asking honestly, and I have asked myself the same question for six years, and I have never once liked the answer." She looked at the strongbox, not at him. "You'd have gone after him alone. No crew, no contract, no support. Into the Reach, into the Shallows, chasing a message that says *trust no one* — and you'd have trusted the first friendly face you met, because that's who you were at twenty-six. You'd have died out there, and the guild would have two Thornbury names on the lost rolls instead of one, and the message would have changed nothing except the size of the grave."

"So you decided that for me."

"Yes." She said it plainly, no flinch in it either. "I decided it for you, and I was wrong to decide it, and I have known I was wrong for approximately six years, and I brought you on this contract partly for the coordinates and partly because I thought — I *hoped* — that facing it with a crew and a purpose might be the closest thing to an amends I could make." She finally looked up. "I'm not asking you to forgive me tonight. I'm asking you to keep reading it. Every night, if you have to. Because that message isn't finished with us, and neither is whoever he was warning about."

"The traitor," Kestrel said. "You think they're still alive."

"I think people who sell crews don't retire. I think 'no one who counts' was specific, and I think we should find out what it counts before the sixth tide." She picked the lamp back up. "Keep the message. It was always yours. But Kestrel — whatever you think of me after tonight, don't let it make you careless. Maddox is two hundred yards away, and Hesk hasn't slept well in three days, and men who don't sleep well are men getting ready to do something."

She went up the stairs and out into the dark, and Kestrel stood alone in her quarters with his brother's letter against his heart, feeling the dread settle into him the way silt settles in still water — silently, gradually, and all the way down.

* * *

The rest of the watch passed in circuits and arithmetic.

That was the thing about a message like Alden's: it got into your head and started counting things whether you wanted it to or not. *Trust no one who counts.* The crew counted rations — Hesk counted them, twice daily, aloud, making sure everyone heard the numbers shrinking. Hesk counted shares, wages, distances, tide-tables. Hesk counted everything, out loud, in that dry quartermaster's voice, and Kestrel had thought nothing of it because counting was the man's entire trade.

But so did Vane. So did Brammel, counting pressure gauges and valve turns. So did the twins, counting bearings. Counting was what expeditions *did*. Either Alden's phrase meant something narrower than it sounded, or it meant nothing at all — a dying man's paranoia, salt-poisoned, addressed to a brother who'd spent seven years learning to stop hoping.

Except the message wasn't dying-man scrawl. It was urgent-man scrawl. There was a difference, and Kestrel had spent enough nights reading tracks to know it. Alden had written fast because fast mattered, and clear because clarity mattered more. *The wreck sits over living water.* He'd understood something about the Provision that the guild charts didn't show, something worth sealing a hold over, something that had gotten — what? Fourteen people killed? Fifteen? The rolls listed twelve lost with the expedition; Alden's message implied a thirteenth walking free somewhere, uncounted.

*Trust no one who counts.*

On his third circuit, Kestrel paused at the edge of the firelight and looked, really looked, at the camp. Osha slept sitting up against the water wagon's rear wheel, her diving knife on her thigh, her breathing even — a woman used to sleeping in places where waking up fast was survival. Brammel snored in the engine wagon with the door open to catch any breeze. The twins had taken opposite watches, as always, so that one of them was always awake and neither would say why. And Hesk —

Hesk's bedroll was empty.

Kestrel went very still. Then he moved, low and quiet, along the wagon line, and found the quartermaster fifty yards out, past the picket line, standing on a low rise of storm-heaped sand with his back to the camp. He wasn't signaling. He wasn't doing anything so obvious. He was simply standing, facing east, toward the Corvane lanterns, his hands folded behind his back, his head tilted slightly as if listening to something across the dark.

Or waiting for something.

Kestrel sank down behind the water wagon and watched. Five minutes. Ten. The grit in the air had mostly settled now, and the stars were coming out clean and cold, and the basin lay between the two fleets like a held coin that both hands wanted. Eventually Hesk turned, descended the rise, and walked back to his bedroll with the unhurried gait of a man who had gotten what he came for — whatever that was — and lay down and was, within minutes, breathing the slow breath of a sleeper.

Maybe he'd been sleepless. Maybe he'd been clearing his head. Maybe Kestrel was seeing traitors in every shadow because his dead brother had told him to, from six years away, in four words that wouldn't stop turning over.

*The Provision turns.*

He said it to himself as the sky in the east went from black to iron, testing it like a strange coin bitten between the teeth. A ship doesn't turn. A wreck certainly doesn't. But a *thing* turns — a wheel, a lock, a tide, a vent, a valve. Something at the wreck site moved, cycled, opened and closed. Living water, Alden had written. Water that behaved like a mechanism.

And *no one who counts* — Kestrel turned that coin over too, and it came up the same way every time: Hesk's voice reciting ration numbers, Hesk's ledger, Hesk's flares on the third night, Hesk's employer unnamed. Doss counted. Doss counted everything Kestrel owed down to the copper, and Doss had sent him onto this convoy like a coin into another man's purse.

The seed of it planted itself then, in the grey hour before dawn, and Kestrel felt it go in — not understanding, not yet, but knowing, the way a ranger knows weather three days off by the taste of the wind. Something at the end of this route was going to turn, and when it did, the person standing nearest the numbers would be standing nearest the truth.

The relief watch came — Pim, rubbing sleep from her eyes, yawning. Kestrel handed off the rifle and the perimeter report and walked back past the lead wagon, and Vane was awake, sitting on the steps with tea she wasn't drinking, watching him with an expression he couldn't read and didn't try to.

"Well?" she said.

"He was on the rise again," Kestrel said quietly. "Facing Corvane. Just standing."

Vane set down the untouched tea. "Then tomorrow we give him something to count," she said, "and see where the number goes."

Out past the basin's rim, the sun came up the color of old brass, and somewhere under two hundred miles of saltflat and drowned canyon, the Provision waited, turning.
