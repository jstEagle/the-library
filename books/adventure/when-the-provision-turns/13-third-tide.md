## 13. Third Tide

Pell had learned to count in her mother's belly, or so Pim liked to claim. He said it every time she beat him to a sum, which was always: *You had a head start, twin. Nine months of arithmetic before you ever saw the sun.* She never told him the truth — that numbers weren't something you learned but something you heard, a low hum under the world, and that some people were simply born with their ears open.

She was hearing it now, and the hum was wrong.

The Glass Reach began without ceremony. One moment the convoy was crossing hardpan the color of old bone, the wagons throwing up dust that hung in the windless air like smoke from a battle nobody had fought; the next, the ground changed under them, going glassy and pale, fused sand that rang faintly beneath the wheels. The heat came up off it in sheets. Pell watched the horizon bend. Not shimmer — *bend*, the way light bent through water, except there was no water for sixty miles in any direction. The land ahead folded over on itself and showed her things that could not be there: mountains floating upside down, a second convoy traveling on the sky, her own face looking back at her from a mile away, enormous and wavering.

"Stop staring at it," Kestrel Thornbury said, reining in beside her wagon. "Mirages eat landmarks. You look too long, your eyes start believing."

"I'm not believing anything," Pell said. "I'm counting it."

He gave her one of his looks — the flat, patient look of a man who had spent years alone in country that wanted him dead and had made peace with the fact that most people talked too much. "Counting what?"

"The bends." She tapped her temple. "The Reach folds light in patterns. Regular ones. If I count the intervals between distortions, I can tell where the real horizon is underneath. It's like hearing a song through a wall. The wall doesn't change the song."

Kestrel looked at her for a long moment, and something shifted behind his eyes — not warmth exactly, but recognition. The look of a man meeting another person who listened to the hum.

"And what's the song saying?"

"That we're being followed." She nodded west, where the air rippled like the surface of a struck drum. "Corvane. Two miles back, maybe less. They've stopped matching our pace. They're closing."

He turned his head, studied the empty horizon as if he could see through it, and Pell watched him decide whether to believe her. She'd seen people make that decision about her whole life — the flicker of doubt, the polite nod, the slow retreat into condescension. But Kestrel had grown up with a brother who read water out of stone, Pim had told her that much around the night fires, and maybe grief made a man willing to trust strange instruments.

"All right," he said. "I'll tell Vane. Keep counting, Pell. And keep your eyes on the ground, not the sky. The Reach has teeth under all that glass."

"What kind of teeth?"

His jaw tightened. He looked ahead at the pale plain unrolling toward the drowned canyons, and for a moment he wasn't seeing the present at all. "Sinkholes," he said. "Salt crust over hollows. Out here they call them widow-makers, because the crust holds until it doesn't, and it always picks the person walking last." He met her eyes. "Nobody walks last in the Reach. Nobody wanders. You stay between the wagon tracks, you step where I step, and if the ground sings — a high note, like wet glass — you stop moving entirely. Do you understand me?"

It was the most words he'd strung together in two days, and every one of them was aimed at her like an arrow. Pell felt the hum shift under her ribs. "I understand," she said. "Ground sings, I freeze."

"Good." He wheeled his mount toward Vane's lead wagon, then paused. "Pell. Your brother — does he hear it too? The counting?"

"He hears music," she said. "I hear math. Between us we almost make one whole person."

Something that was almost a smile crossed Kestrel's face and was gone. "Almost is more than most people get out here."

* * *

The Reach swallowed the afternoon.

By the fourth hour the mirages had stopped being curiosities and become a siege. Landmarks dissolved. The butte they'd been steering toward since morning — Vane called it the Anvil, a black thumb of basalt rising off the glass — appeared, vanished, reappeared in three places at once, drifting across the horizon like a boat at anchor dragging its mooring. Pim navigated from the second wagon with his sextant and his star tables and his stubborn faith in arithmetic, while Pell counted the folds of light and fed him corrections, and between the two of them they kept the convoy pointed true when everything else lied.

Hesk did not help. Hesk sat his wagon with his ledger-book and his long face and made comments designed to land like splinters.

"Anvil's gone," he announced at the top of the hour, pointing east, where nothing was. "Vane's lost us our heading. Back home we'd have turned around by now."

"Back home you'd have been sold twice over by now," Osha called from the dive wagon, not looking up from the harness she was mending. Her voice was mild. Her hands were not; Pell noticed how the needle moved faster when Hesk spoke, small furious stitches.

"I'm only saying what everyone's thinking—"

"Then stop saying it," Brammel rumbled from under the third wagon, where he lay half-buried in engine guts, a wrench in one fist like a scepter. "Some of us are working."

Pell smiled to herself and kept counting. The intervals between light-folds were shortening — Corvane still coming, patient as tide — and the ground-note under the wheels had developed a new texture, a hollowness, like tapping a wall and finding a room behind it. She filed both facts away. Twin habit. Everything went into the ledger behind her eyes; nothing came out until it was needed.

It was near dusk when the Anvil finally resolved into something solid and real, close enough now that its shadow stretched long and violet across the glass, and Vane called the halt. Camp went up in the practiced scramble of six tides' habit — wagons in a crescent, pickets set, the water barrels chained because water was the only currency left that mattered. Rations had been cut again that morning. Half-measures. Everyone had begun to move a little slower, speak a little shorter, guard their canteens with their elbows. Thirst makes animals of people, Pell's mother used to say. It just takes longer for some than others.

Pell took first watch with Kestrel. They walked the perimeter together in the blue hour, boots ringing softly on the fused sand, and he taught her the Reach the way her mother had taught her numbers: patiently, relentlessly, as though her life depended on every lesson, because it did.

"See that patch?" He stopped, pointed with his chin at a place where the glassy crust darkened, going matte and grey. "That's not shadow. That's where the crust thins. Salt crust over a void — the wind scours it, the heat bakes it, and it looks solid right up until it isn't. Crust will hold a man's weight for years and then drop him forty feet into a brine chamber the width of a cellar." He crouched, touched the grey patch with two fingers, rubbed them together. "Feel that? Soft. Like candle wax. Solid ground out here should feel like a church floor. Anything soft, you go around."

"How do you know all this?" Pell asked. "Really."

He was quiet long enough that she thought he wouldn't answer. The first stars were coming out over the Anvil, sharp and cold, and somewhere behind them Pim was arguing with Brammel about fuel ratios, and the hum of the world ran low and steady under everything.

"My brother mapped this Reach," Kestrel said at last. "Alden. Seven years ago, with the Provision expedition. He sent notes back — survey work, mostly. Ground conditions, safe lines through the canyons. I've read those notes so many times I could recite them drunk." His thumb moved along his belt, touching the worn spot where a compass hung. "He knew this ground better than anyone alive. And it took him anyway." He stood. "So no, I don't *know*. I memorized. There's a difference, and the difference is that memorizing keeps you humble."

"Alden," Pell said. "That's why you came. Not the coin."

Kestrel looked at her, and for a heartbeat the flat ranger mask slipped and she saw down into him — into a room flooded to the ceiling with seven years of not-knowing. Then the mask came back up.

"Watch the perimeter," he said. "Sing if you see anything."

And he walked the line, and Pell stood her watch, and the stars wheeled, and the hum under the world stayed steady until nearly midnight, when it didn't.

* * *

It happened at dawn, and it happened fast, and afterward Pell would replay it ten thousand times and it would never once play differently.

They broke camp in the grey half-light, wheels turning before the sun cleared the Anvil, because Vane wanted the Reach crossed before full heat turned the mirages from treacherous to blinding. The order of march was fixed: Vane's wagon, the twins' wagon, the dive wagon, Brammel's engine-wagon, Hesk's supply wagon last. Kestrel rode point on his lean desert horse, a quarter mile ahead, reading the ground the way other men read scripture.

Pell rode the wagon bench beside Pim, counting folds, calling corrections. The morning light came up gold and then white, and the Reach ignited — the whole plain blazing like a sheet of hammered tin, mirage towers rising on every side, a city of glass ghosts that existed nowhere and everywhere. It was beautiful. That was the trap of it. It was beautiful the way deep water is beautiful, and Pell understood suddenly why the divers spoke of the sea with such respect and such fear in the same breath.

"Fold interval's stretching," she told Pim. "We're gaining on Corvane. Or they're letting us think we are."

"Noted, sister-mine." Pim marked his chart. His hands were steady. His eyes kept flicking west. "You feel that, though? Under the wheels?"

She did. She'd been feeling it for an hour: the hollow note, deeper now, wider. The room behind the wall, growing.

"Kestrel flagged a soft patch a ways back," she said. "Grey crust. We're swinging wide of—"

That was when Hesk's voice rose from the rear wagon, cracked and carrying: "Water! Water barrel's loose — strap's parted — somebody stop, it'll be gone in a minute!"

Pell turned in her seat. Behind them, Hesk's supply wagon had shed a barrel — whether by accident or design she would wonder forever — and it was rolling, bouncing, gouting precious water in silver arcs across the glass, careening off toward a stretch of ground that lay outside the wagon tracks, outside the safe line, out where the crust went grey and thin and soft as candle wax.

"Leave it!" Vane shouted from the front. "No one leaves the line! Hesk, let it go!"

But Pell was already moving. Because it was water, and water was life, and because she had done the arithmetic without meaning to — a barrel that size, at the rate they drank, was three days of life for four people, and three days was the margin between reaching the Shallows and dying in the Reach, and the numbers didn't lie, the numbers never lied, and someone had to—

"Pell!" Pim's hand closed on her sleeve. "Pell, the line, stay on the—"

"I hear it," she said. "The ground hasn't sung yet."

She dropped from the moving wagon. The impact rang up through her boots. She sprinted, angling after the barrel, and behind her she heard Kestrel's voice tear across the plain — raw, amplified by the glass, carrying impossibly far:

"*PELL! STOP! THE GROUND — THE GROUND SINGS—*"

And she heard it. God help her, she heard it — a high clear note rising out of the earth beneath her feet, pure as a wet finger on a wineglass rim, the sound of salt crust beginning to fail — and her body knew what to do, Kestrel had taught her, *you stop moving entirely,* and she stopped, one foot planted, one foot raised, frozen mid-stride thirty feet from the barrel, which rolled on without her, wobbling, shedding its silver cargo, and came to rest on a patch of grey crust at the edge of a shadow that was not a shadow.

For three heartbeats, nothing. The note faded. The Reach held its breath. Pell stood balanced on one foot like a heron, laughing shakily at herself, already composing the story she'd tell Pim tonight —

The crust let go.

There was no dramatic collapse, no roar. The ground simply opened, quietly, the way a mouth opens, and the world tilted, and Pell's planted foot sank to the ankle, to the knee, and the grey crust around her flowered outward in cracks like frost on a window, and she understood with perfect, terrible clarity that the note hadn't been a warning. It had been a countdown.

"*KESTREL!*" Pim's scream, from the wagon. "*PELL'S DOWN—*"

She got her arms out. Spread her weight. The crust groaned beneath her chest. Below her boots — below the place where her boots had been — was darkness, and a smell like rotten eggs and old sea, and a sound of brine lapping somewhere far down, forty feet, fifty, in a chamber the width of a cellar. Her fingers found the lip of the hole, found solid crust beyond it, and she dug in and held, breath rasping, legs dangling over the void, the whole Reach watching her with its thousand mirror-eyes.

"Hold!" Kestrel was off his horse and running, running flat-out across ground he would never have allowed anyone else to cross, rope streaming from his saddle as he dragged it behind him. "Hold, Pell, don't pull, spread your weight — PIM, THE ROPE, ANCHOR IT TO THE WAGON —"

They came fast — Pim white-faced and screaming her name, Osha with a coil of line, Vane shouting orders that Pell couldn't parse over the blood in her ears. The rope snaked out to her. She caught it on the second throw. Kestrel bellied down at the hole's edge, distributing himself flat as a starfish, and got his fists in her collar, and for a moment — one long suspended moment — Pell hung between the sky and the brine-dark, held by a man who had already lost one sibling to this country and was not, was *not*, going to lose another.

"Got you," he said through his teeth. "I've got you. Pull."

They pulled. The crust shrieked. More of it went, a whole shelf of it calving away into the dark with a sound like ice breaking, and Pell felt her legs swing free over emptiness, felt the cold breath of the deep rise past her face — and then hands, many hands, hauling, and she came up over the lip and onto solid ground and lay there gasping, alive, whole, saved.

The barrel was gone. It had gone down during the rescue, silently, without even a splash they could hear. Three days of life for four people, swallowed by the Reach, and the Reach hadn't even burped.

* * *

They stood in a ring around the hole afterward, because that was what people did, the way they stood around graves.

The sinkhole's mouth was eight feet across, its edges crumbling inward even as they watched, the crust surrendering inch by inch to the void. Pell sat wrapped in a blanket twenty yards back, Pim's arm iron around her shoulders, Osha pressing a cup of water — *water, from their own shortened ration* — into her shaking hands. Brammel had rigged a lantern pole and lowered it down the shaft. The light showed smooth salt walls descending into green-black murk, and then nothing, and the nothing went down farther than the lantern could reach.

"No bottom," Brammel said quietly. "Or none we'll find. Brine chamber, like Kestrel said. She'd have—" He stopped. Looked at Pell, sitting there breathing. Didn't finish.

Vane stood at the hole's edge with her fists on her hips, staring down into the dark as if she could interrogate it. "We lose the barrel, we lose three days of margin," she said, and her voice was hard, but Pell heard the crack under it. Vane had ordered no one to leave the line. Vane's orders had been right. Vane knew that the next time, the orders might not be enough. "We cut rations again. Quarter measures. We make the Shallows in two days instead of three, and we pray the mast shows at low tide."

"Quarter measures," Hesk repeated, oily with sympathy. "On top of half. People break, Captain. People do desperate things over water. Maybe we should discuss alternatives. Maybe Corvane—"

"Finish that sentence," Kestrel said, "and I'll finish you."

He hadn't moved from where he knelt — still at the hole's edge, still staring down, one hand pressed flat against the crust as though he could hold the whole Reach shut through sheer refusal. Pell watched him and understood that he wasn't looking at the void. He was looking at the space where she had almost been. Seven years ago the Reach had taken Alden Thornbury down into a dark like this one, and no one had stood at the edge with a rope, and Kestrel had carried that — carried it across six tides of saltflat, carried it into every silence, into the way he checked each crew member's position a dozen times a day without seeming to, into the lessons he'd pressed on her at midnight like a man paying down a debt.

*I've got you,* he'd said. And he had. And Pell knew — with the same certainty she knew numbers — that the saving of her had cost him something worse than losing her would have. Because now he knew exactly what he'd failed to do seven years ago. Now he had the missing measurement.

"Kestrel." She said it gently. He didn't turn. "Kestrel. I'm here. I'm fine. You pulled me out."

"The ground sang," he said, to the hole, to the dark, to a brother seven years dead. "I told her the ground sings. She heard it. She *stopped.*" His hand curled against the crust. "And it took her anyway. Right on the line. Right where I swore it couldn't." He looked up at last, and his eyes were dry and terrible. "It doesn't matter what you know out here. It doesn't matter what you teach. The Reach just decides."

"No," Pell said. She stood, blanket and all, and walked to him on legs that barely obeyed, and put her hand over his where it pressed the broken crust. "The Reach decided, and you overruled it. That's the count that matters. Ask my brother. He's been doing the sums since I went down — haven't you, Pim?"

Pim's voice came rough from twenty yards back. "Every number says she lives. Every single one."

Kestrel looked at their hands on the crust, at the hole that had almost been a grave, at the convoy waiting beyond with its shortened rations and its fraying trust and its traitor quartermaster already whispering to whoever paid him. Something in his face closed — not healed, never healed, but *shelved*, the way a man shelves a weight he must carry farther.

"Two days to the Shallows," he said, standing, taking up the rope, coiling it with hands that shook only a little. "Quarter rations. Nobody leaves the line. Nobody walks last." He swung up into the saddle, and his gaze went north, past the Anvil's shadow, toward the drowned canyons and the wreck that waited under the sixth tide with its sealed chambers and its drowned secrets. "And when this is over — when we know what happened to Alden — I'm coming back to this hole with a hundred feet of line and a winch, and I'm mapping every widow-maker in the Reach. So the next crew gets a warning that isn't a voice screaming across the glass."

He turned his horse north. The convoy groaned into motion behind him, wheel after wheel, smaller and wearier than yesterday.

Pell climbed back onto the bench beside Pim and picked up her counting. But her hands wouldn't quite steady, and the hum under the world had changed key — lower now, sadder, the way a song changes when it passes a graveyard — and far to the west, at the very edge of the folding light, she counted the fold-intervals shorten once more.

Corvane was coming. And behind Corvane, riding the mirages like a rumor, something else was moving that she couldn't name — a distortion in the pattern that didn't belong to any fleet, any wagon, any living thing.

She didn't tell anyone. Not yet. Some numbers you sit with a while first, turning them over, checking your work.

But she started keeping a separate tally. Just in case.
