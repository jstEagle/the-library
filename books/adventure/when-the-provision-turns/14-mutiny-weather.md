## 14. Mutiny Weather

Osha felt the change in the air before anyone said a word about it — the way she always did, the way the sea had taught her when she was small enough to be thrown from a skiff and told to swim home. Pressure dropping. A dryness in the wind that tasted of ground glass. The saltflats ahead had gone the color of old bone, and the horizon had begun to shimmer in long horizontal bands, like something trying to crawl out of the world.

"Storm weather," she said to Brammel, who was walking the lead wagon's axles with a grease-stick in his teeth.

Brammel spat, looked up, squinted at the banded sky. "That's not storm weather. That's *before* storm weather." He took the grease-stick out of his mouth and pointed it east, where the light had turned a sickly brass. "What comes before is worse. What comes before decides whether you get to have an opinion about what comes after."

She left him to his axles. There were only so many hours of warning in a sky like that, and Vane would want to know they had them.

But when Osha reached the command wagon, she found Hesk already there, standing too close to Vane's chart table with his hands folded behind his back in that way he had — the posture of a man who wanted you to believe he had nothing in his hands.

"—simply arithmetic," Hesk was saying. His voice was smooth as silt. "We've burned through a third of the water since the canyon. The Corvane fleet carries double cisterns. If we signal them now, before this weather closes, we could trade the coordinates for passage and provisions both. Nobody drowns. Nobody dies of thirst in a hole in the Glass Reach."

Vane didn't look up from her charts. "Nobody sells my route either."

"It isn't selling. It's—"

"It's selling." Vane's finger came down on the map, on the smudged ink line that marked their course into the Reach. "You want me to hand Maddox everything we've bled for because your arithmetic says we might get thirsty. Go do your sums somewhere else, quartermaster."

Hesk's face did something very small and very controlled — a tightening around the eyes, a settling of the jaw — and then he bowed, shallow and correct, and turned, and saw Osha standing in the wagon doorway.

For half a heartbeat, something moved behind his expression. Not guilt exactly. Osha knew guilt; she'd seen it on drowning men. This was more like recalculation. Like a merchant finding one coin short and deciding which pocket to steal it from.

"Diver," he said pleasantly. "Hot day."

"Hottest yet," Osha said, and stepped aside to let him pass, and watched him go, and did not like the way his boots sounded on the planking. Even. Unhurried. A man with nowhere to be except everywhere at once.

* * *

The water rationing began at dusk, announced by Vane herself, standing on the lead wagon's bench with a lantern held high so the whole convoy could see her face.

"Half measures until we clear the Reach," she called out. "One liter at morning bell, one at evening. No exceptions. Not for rank, not for seniority, not for bleeding hearts."

A low murmur went through the camp — forty-odd people sprawled among six wagons and the salvage skiff lashed atop Brammel's rig, all of them tired, all of them salt-cracked, all of them doing the same silent math Hesk had done in the command wagon. Half measures meant four days of margin instead of eight. Four days to cross country where the mirages ate landmarks and the ground opened without warning.

Kestrel Thornbury sat apart from the others, as he usually did, cleaning a rifle he rarely fired, and Osha watched him watch Vane. There was something in his stillness that bothered her. Not hostility. Something more like a man listening to a song he half remembered and couldn't decide if he liked.

Pell's death was three days behind them now, and nobody had stopped carrying it. Pim hadn't spoken above a whisper since. The twins' wagon, which used to ring with their bickering, was silent as a sealed tomb, and everyone gave it room.

It was into that silence that Hesk chose to work.

Osha saw the shape of it over the next two days, the way you see the shape of a current under flat water — not the thing itself but the drift of everything floating on it. Hesk moving from fire to fire with his canteen and his easy voice. Hesk sitting with the drovers, laughing at their jokes a beat too readily. Hesk by the evening ration line, loudly refusing his own measure: "Give mine to the twins. They've lost enough." Small coin, spent generously, buying something larger.

By the second night, the crew had split like a drying riverbed — not into open channels yet, but into cracks, deepening.

Osha found Kestrel at the perimeter, on watch, facing east into the dark where the wind had begun to carry a fine mineral grit that ticked against the wagon boards like slow rain.

"You hear it?" she asked him.

"I hear Hesk."

"That's what I mean."

Kestrel worked the bolt of the rifle, open, shut, open. In the lantern light his face was all planes and shadow, older than his years, the desert written on him in fine lines. "He's not wrong about the water," he said. "That's what makes him dangerous. A liar lies clean. Hesk tells the truth and lets you draw the knife yourself."

"And Vane?"

"What about her?"

"She's holding back something." Osha sat down on the wheel hub beside him, close enough to speak low. "I dive wrecks, Thornbury. I know what concealment looks like. She's got a hold full of it and she's riding high on top and pretending the ship floats level. You've known her longer than any of us. What's she hiding?"

The bolt clicked shut. For a long moment Kestrel said nothing, and the wind gusted, and somewhere out in the flats a sound rolled across the salt — a low groan, vast and mineral, the sound the Glass Reach made at night when the heat left the ground and the salt crust settled, like the earth turning over in its sleep.

"She's hiding Alden," he said finally.

Osha waited.

"My brother was on the last expedition to the Provision. Seven years ago. Vane was on it too — or connected to it, she's never been straight with me about which. And whatever happened out here, she knows more than she's said, and I signed onto this run anyway, because I'd rather walk into her lie than live outside the truth another year." He looked at Osha then, and his eyes in the lantern light were flat and clear and terribly tired. "That's what I know. Now you know it too. Congratulations. It doesn't help."

"No," Osha agreed. "But it explains why you back her in front of the crew while looking at her like that."

"Like what?"

"Like a man playing cards who's pretty sure the deck is stacked and can't prove whose hand it stacks."

Kestrel almost smiled. It never quite landed. "Go sleep, diver. Storm's coming. We'll need our water more than our opinions."

* * *

The storm hit at dawn, or rather its herald did — a wall of ochre dust rising off the southern flats, miles wide, rolling toward them with the slow certainty of a tide. Vane made the call fast and good: hard turn northwest, into a basin she'd marked on her charts, a bowl of sheltered stone where the convoy could lash down and wait out the worst of it.

They made the basin with an hour to spare. Wagons circled, wheels chocked, canvas doubled and staked. Then the world outside ceased to exist. The sand came not in gusts but in sheets, a solid brown roar, and inside the basin the air went strange and thick, and the light turned the color of weak tea, and everyone's voice dropped without being asked to.

It was in that pressed-in hush, that afternoon, that Hesk made his move.

Osha heard it start at the far wagon — Hesk's voice pitched just below shouting, the register of a man who wants to be overheard but never caught at it. She drifted closer, as she drifted through water, quiet, unhurried, hands loose.

"—not saying mutiny. I'm saying *arithmetic*." A cluster of drovers around him, five or six faces, Pim among them, hollow-eyed and listening. "Vane's got us committed to a route only she understands, through country that's already killed one of us, chasing a wreck that killed seven people seven years ago. And for what? Shares in cargo that may not exist, paid by a woman who won't tell us what happened to the last crew who tried. Meanwhile Maddox is sitting out there with full cisterns and a fleet that's offered terms twice."

"Maddox tried to kill us at the Silt Gate," one of the drovers said.

"Maddox *tested* us at the Silt Gate," Hesk corrected gently. "There's a difference. A man testing a door isn't trying to break it. He's checking whether it opens. And I'm telling you, friends, it opens. Terms. Provisions. An escort through the Reach. All we give up is a set of numbers Vane refuses to share with the very people risking their lives on them." He spread his hands. "Ask yourselves why she won't share them. Ask what a woman hides when she hides her own route."

Osha stepped into the circle. "Maybe because every time she's shared something, someone's sold it."

The group turned. Hesk's smile arrived a half-second late, like a man stepping onto a deck that had already shifted. "Diver. Come to join the discussion?"

"Come to count heads." She let her gaze move slowly around the circle, unhurried, the way she'd learned to read a surface before diving it. "Six of you. Yesterday it was four. Tomorrow it'll be nine, and the day after that Hesk will have enough votes to call it consensus, and consensus will call it mutiny, and nobody will have done anything wrong at any point. That's how it works. That's how it always works. I've seen crews come apart and I'll tell you what kills them — it's never the storm. It's the man with the arithmetic."

"Careful," Hesk said softly. The pleasantness was gone now, or mostly gone, a mask slipping to show the thing beneath, which was colder and much more patient. "You're accusing me of something. Say it plainly or don't say it."

"I'm saying you talk like a man who's already decided where he's jumping, and you're trying to convince the rest of the crew the ship is sinking so they'll jump with you." She held his eyes. "I'm also saying that when the truth comes out — and it always comes out, Hesk, water finds its level — I intend to be standing on the side of it that isn't you."

Silence. The sand roared against the canvas overhead. Somewhere a stake groaned.

Then Kestrel was there — Osha didn't see him arrive, he simply was, materializing at the edge of the circle with his rifle slung and his thumbs hooked in his belt, wearing the particular blankness she'd come to recognize as his most dangerous expression.

"Problem here?"

"No problem," Hesk said smoothly. "Crew morale. Healthy debate."

"Healthy." Kestrel looked at the drovers, at Pim, at each face in turn, and Osha watched something pass between him and them — not words, just weight. "Here's my contribution to the debate. We're four days from the Shallows. Everything Hesk just promised you — Maddox's terms, his cisterns, his escort — requires you to trust the man who put a ball through our lead wagon's driver at the Gate. Pell trusted somebody once. Look how the twins are sleeping." Pim flinched. Kestrel didn't soften it. "Vane's secretive. Fine. Secretive isn't treacherous. I've worked with both, and I can tell you the difference from the inside. When we make camp tonight, ask yourselves which one Hesk is. Because I've been asking since Marrowgate, and I'm getting close to an answer."

He let that sit. Then he turned and walked away, and one by one the drovers drifted after him, back to their wagons, back to their chores, the circle dissolving like foam. Only Pim lingered a moment, looking at nothing, and then went too.

Hesk remained, alone in the tea-colored light, adjusting his cuffs.

"You're better at this than I expected," Osha said quietly.

"At what?"

"Whatever it is you actually do. Quartermaster is the coat. I've been watching you wear it since first tide. It fits in the shoulders and hangs wrong everywhere else."

Hesk smiled at her, genuinely this time, and that was somehow worse. "Diver," he said, "when this is over — however it ends — I hope you remember that I never lied to you. Not once. Everything I said about the water, about Vane, about the odds. All true. Check my figures anytime." He buttoned his cuff, precise as a ritual. "Truth's the cheapest commodity on this convoy. Everybody trades in it. Most of them just don't know they're spending."

He walked away into the brown roaring light, even-footed, unhurried, and Osha stood there a long time with the sand ticking on the canvas and the taste of grit in her teeth, and admitted to herself the thing she'd been refusing to admit since the canyon:

She believed him. About the water, about the odds, about all of it. Every word true.

And none of it mattered, because the question had never been whether Hesk told the truth. The question was who was paying him to spend it — and that evening, when she climbed the lead wagon to take her watch and found Vane's strongbox lid hanging open and Vane herself staring into it with a face gone white as the salt outside, Osha understood that the answer had just gotten out ahead of them.

"Vane," she said slowly. "What's missing?"

Vane didn't answer. But her hand, resting on the empty box, was shaking — and through the canvas walls, faint under the storm's roar, Osha could hear the even, unhurried sound of boots crossing the sand toward the eastern edge of camp, where the basin's stones rose highest, and where a man with a flare pistol could put a signal over the wind to a fleet that was waiting for exactly this weather to close the distance.
