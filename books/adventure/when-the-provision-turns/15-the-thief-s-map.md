## 15. The Thief's Map

The thief's name was Wick. He'd told them that much on the second night out of Marrowgate, spitting the word like a seed he'd been holding under his tongue for years, and Kestrel had almost laughed — a man named for the smallest light in the dark, selling the way through the biggest dark of all.

Now, at the mouth of the Glass Reach, with the third tide pressing at their backs like a hand between the shoulder blades, Kestrel stood before a wall of salt-crusted stone that looked exactly like every other wall of stone in the Reach, and waited for Wick to prove himself honest or dead.

"You're certain it's here." Vane's voice was flat, stripped of performance. She'd stopped performing somewhere around Pell's death, and what remained underneath was harder and older than anything she'd ever shown Kestrel in Marrowgate. "Because if you've walked us two days into the Reach to die of heat, I'll—"

"I'm certain." Wick crouched, brushing grit from the base of the cliff with both hands, quick and furtive as a rat working a grain sack. He was maybe thirty but looked fifty; the desert had eaten him early and often. "My uncle ran this route. Smuggler's road, back when the water barons taxed everything that moved. They called it the Hollow Step." His fingers found something — a seam, a hairline crack running vertical through the rock face, so fine Kestrel would have taken it for natural fracture. "You have to know where the salt's been broken and reset. See? The mortar's different. Newer. Somebody patched this and wanted nobody to know."

Kestrel knelt beside him. Up close he could see it: the seam was real, and the mineral crust over it had a faintly different sheen, like skin grown over an old scar. His father had taught him to read country this way — not by landmarks, which lied, but by texture, by the small dishonesties of a landscape trying to keep a secret.

"Open it," Vane said.

Wick worked the seam with a pry-bar and a patience that surprised Kestrel. Grit hissed down. Then a section of rock groaned inward on hidden hinges gone stiff with years, exhaling a breath of air from the tunnel beyond — cool, mineral, and wrong somehow, like the breath off a grave.

Everyone felt it. Osha shifted her weight. Brammel muttered something about bad air and lit a lamp anyway. Hesk stood at the rear with his arms crossed, wearing the expression of a man calculating distances to exits.

"Bad air first," Kestrel said, catching Brammel's wrist before the lamp went in. "We test it. Nobody walks in on faith."

"Since when do rangers give the orders?" Hesk said.

"Since the last navigator went into a hole in the ground," Kestrel said, and didn't look at him, and heard the silence behind him go very still. He hadn't meant to say it. It came out anyway, the way grief does when you stop watching the door.

Vane cleared her throat. "Test the air."

They burned a taper at the threshold, then sent it in on a pole. The flame held steady, yellow and calm. Brammel pronounced it breathable if stale. Pim — quieter since losing his twin, moving through the days like a man carrying something breakable — volunteered to go first, and Kestrel put a hand on his shoulder and went instead, because there were some weights he wouldn't let a boy carry twice.

* * *

The Hollow Step ran straight into the heart of the Reach.

That was its genius, Kestrel realized within the first hundred yards. Above ground, the Glass Reach was a killer precisely because it offered nothing — no shade, no landmark, mirages swallowing the horizon until even a ranger's eye drank lies. But beneath it, the smugglers had cut a corridor through the salt bed itself, following some ancient fault line, and the walls glittered faintly in lamplight like the inside of a geode. Cool air moved through it, barely, a whisper of draft that meant it connected to the surface somewhere ahead. Enough to breathe. Not enough to feel safe.

"How long?" Vane asked.

"Uncle said half a day's walk," Wick answered. "Saves three days above ground. And the Corvane fleet doesn't know it exists." He paused. "Didn't. I only ever sold it once before."

The words dropped into the tunnel like a stone into a well.

Kestrel turned slowly. "Sold it to who?"

Wick's lamp trembled. In the shaking light his face looked hollowed, carved out of old wood. "Seven years back. A salvage outfit came through Marrowgate asking after the Provision. Big crew, good gear. I was younger. Hungrier. They paid me silver for the route and I never thought—" He swallowed. "I never thought about it again until your brother's name started coming up in the taverns. Until I understood whose crew it was."

The silence that followed had texture. Kestrel could feel the whole crew looking at him, could feel their eyes like heat on the back of his neck, waiting to see what he'd do — whether he'd break, whether he'd kill the man, whether the ranger code held or snapped.

He made himself breathe. Made himself think of Alden alive, walking this same corridor, laughing probably, because Alden had laughed at everything, even the things that deserved weeping. Seven years ago his brother had come down this passage with twenty good people, guided by a thief's map, toward a ship full of treasure.

And none of them had walked back out.

"Keep moving," Kestrel said. His voice sounded like someone else's.

* * *

Two hours in, they found the first sign, and Kestrel knew — before his mind caught up, before any conscious thought formed — that something was wrong. It was the way the others went quiet. Sound carried strangely in the tunnel; a boot scuff traveled thirty yards. So when the whole column stopped breathing at once, he felt it through the floor.

Ahead, wedged against the tunnel wall, lay a wagon wheel.

Not a fragment. A whole wheel, iron-rimmed, salt-eaten but whole, tipped on its side as though the wagon had simply been set down and abandoned. The wood had gone grey and soft as cheese. Brammel touched the rim and pulled his fingers back coated in white powder.

"Salt gets into everything down here," he murmured. "Preserves it, too. In a way." He didn't say *like a mummy*. Nobody said it. Everybody thought it.

They pressed on, and the signs multiplied. A crate stenciled with a shipping mark Kestrel recognized from Marrowgate chandleries. A coil of rope fused into a single solid mass. A lantern, glass fogged white, oil long since evaporated to a crust of varnish in the bowl. All of it arranged with a strange, terrible orderliness — nothing scattered, nothing looted, as though the owners had set their belongings down neatly and walked away.

Or been made to.

"Kestrel." Osha's voice, low, from the point position. She'd taken point without being asked, ever since Pell. She stood now with her lamp raised, and the light fell across something at the tunnel's edge that made Kestrel's chest tighten before he could see what it was.

A boot. Expedition-grade, high-shafted, the kind issued by salvage outfits out of Marrowgate. Salt had preserved the leather perfectly, down to the laces, down to the maker's stamp on the heel.

Inside the boot was a foot.

* * *

They found them over the next quarter mile, and Kestrel counted because counting was the only thing keeping him upright.

Eleven. Eleven bodies, or what the salt had left of them — desiccated, light as driftwood, dressed in the rotted finery of expedition gear. Some sat against the walls as though resting. One lay curled in the center of the tunnel like a child asleep. Two were arm in arm, and Kestrel had to turn away from that one, had to press his palm flat against the cold salt wall and breathe until the burning in his throat subsided.

Twenty had gone in. Eleven lay here. The arithmetic of it opened a door in his mind he refused to walk through yet, because nine unaccounted-for meant nine possibilities, and one of those possibilities wore his brother's face.

"Ranger gear," Osha said quietly beside him. She'd been reading the dead the way she read wrecks, methodically, giving them that much dignity. "Look at the stitching. That's guild-issue harness. Your brother's crew had rangers in it?"

"Alden hired the best." Kestrel's voice came out sanded flat. "Three guild men, he told Mother. Three."

One of the seated figures wore a ranger's badge, corroded green, still pinned to the breast of a coat that had once been blue. Kestrel knelt in front of him. The body was small, fine-boned. There was a wedding band fused to one finger by mineral deposit, and on the ground beside the hand, placed deliberately, was a compass — cracked, dead, its needle frozen pointing back the way they'd come.

Toward home. Toward the direction they'd never reached.

"They didn't die fighting," Osha said. "No wounds I can find. No weapons drawn. Look how they're sitting. They just... stopped."

"Poison air?" Brammel suggested from further back, hating the theory even as he offered it. "A pocket of bad gas. The vent systems down here—"

"The air's moving," Kestrel said. "It's been moving the whole way. A gas pocket kills fast and stays foul. This air is clean enough to burn a lamp steady." He looked down the tunnel, into the dark ahead, where the lamplight gave up and the blackness took over. "They stopped because something made them stop. Or because they'd already given up. There's a difference, and I don't like either one."

Hesk spoke from the rear, and for once there was no calculation in his voice, only a raw unease he didn't bother hiding. "How far does this tunnel go?"

"To the far side of the Reach," Wick said. His voice had gone thin. He kept his eyes on his boots. "It comes up in a drowned canyon system. The Shallows. Right where—" He stopped.

Right where the Provision went down. Everyone heard the rest of the sentence anyway.

Vane stood among the dead with her arms wrapped around herself, and Kestrel watched her, and saw something he had never seen on Sable Vane's face: not guilt exactly, not yet, but its shadow — the expression of a woman doing arithmetic of her own. Twenty minus eleven. She was doing it too.

"We camp here," she said finally. "Nobody goes further tonight. We're tired, we're grieving, and whatever's at the end of this tunnel deserves better than exhausted eyes." She met Kestrel's gaze, and held it. "Tomorrow we walk out the other side. Together."

Kestrel nodded. But sleep wouldn't take him, and around middle-night he rose from his bedroll and walked back along the tunnel with a lamp, past the sleeping forms of the crew, to the seated ranger with the frozen compass.

He crouched there a long time.

Then, gently, he turned the dead man's coat collar aside, looking for a name tape, an initials stamp, anything. And there, inside the collar, in faded ink gone brown with age, he found a name that was not Alden's — but a name he knew, a name from seven years ago, from the taverns of Marrowgate, from the roster of the lost expedition that had been read aloud at every memorial service the guild had ever held:

Corporal Edda Marsh. Guild ranger. Second expedition, Provision salvage. *Lost with all hands.*

All hands. Except the arithmetic said otherwise. Eleven here. Nine missing. And somewhere past the end of this tunnel, in the drowned canyons of the Shallows, on a wreck that broke the surface only at low tide, the answer was waiting — sealed inside a hold that someone, once, had barricaded from within.

Kestrel closed the corporal's collar with careful fingers, and sat back on his heels in the cold glittering dark, surrounded by the neat, patient dead, and understood that he was no longer guiding a salvage crew toward treasure.

He was walking the same road his brother had walked, one day behind him, seven years late — and the tunnel ahead did not lead to coin or water-rites or bounty.

It led to whatever had made twenty people set down their belongings, sit down against the walls, and wait.

Behind him, far back in the dark, a sound drifted through the still air of the Hollow Step: faint, rhythmic, unmistakable.

Water. Moving. Coming from the direction they were going.
