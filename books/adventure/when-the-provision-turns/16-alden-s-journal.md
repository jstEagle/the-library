## 16. Alden's Journal

The tunnel breathed cold.

That was the first thing Kestrel noticed after the horror settled into something he could carry — the air down here moved, slow and mineral-sweet, like the inside of a cave that had never seen sun. It should have been dead air, sealed air, seven years of rot and stillness. Instead it stirred against his face with a patience that felt almost deliberate, as though the mountain itself were exhaling through the bones.

He knelt where he had been kneeling for a long time — long enough that his knees had gone numb against the salt-crusted stone — and did not touch anything else.

The bones lay in a rough crescent, eleven skeletons by Osha's count, arranged not scattered but *settled*, the way men lie when they stop moving rather than the way they fall. They wore expedition gear: harnesses rotted to lace, water-rite satchels fused to ribcages, boots gone to leather lace and buckle. One still had a guild ranger's badge pinned at the collar, green enamel gone black. Kestrel knew the pattern. He'd have known it anywhere. It was the issue stamp from the year Alden made senior.

He didn't reach for it. He kept his hands flat on his thighs and breathed the cold sweet air and let the tunnel hold him the way it held them.

Behind him, the smuggler's passage ran back up toward the Glass Reach, a throat of darkness the thief's map had promised and delivered. Ahead, the tunnel narrowed toward a collapsed section choked with rubble and old timber — the remains, maybe, of a brace, or a door someone had tried to build and failed. Lamplight from Osha's lantern threw their shadows long across the crescent of the dead.

"You don't have to do this now," Osha said quietly. She had been saying versions of it for an hour. She was good at saying things quietly. Divers were, he supposed. Everything worth saying underwater had to be said soft or not at all.

"I do." His voice came out rusted. "It's why I'm here."

"It's why you *think* you're here."

He looked back at her. In the lantern light her face was all planes and shadow, diver's face, a face built for reading pressure. She held his gaze without flinching, and he understood again why Vane had hired her, why Vane trusted her with the parts of the job that required a person rather than a tool.

"His name was Alden," Kestrel said. "My brother. If there's anything of him down here, I'm the one who reads it. That's not grief talking. That's just fact."

Osha considered this the way she seemed to consider everything — fully, without hurry, as if time were something she carried in surplus. Then she set the lantern on a flat stone between them, turned its wick low, and sat down cross-legged on the opposite side of the bones, making herself witness rather than intruder.

"All right," she said. "Read."

* * *

The journal was wedged beneath the third skeleton from the wall — no, not beneath. *Under the folded arms.* Someone had placed it there deliberately, tucked into the crook of crossed forearms the way a sleeping man holds a book he means to finish. The leather cover had gone stiff as bark, salt-bloomed white along the spine, but the cord binding it held, and when Kestrel worked it free the pages inside were dry. Dry, in a flooded canyon system, in a tunnel that breathed. He would think about that later. He would think about a great many things later.

Half a journal. That was the other thing. The cord had been cut — not frayed, cut, clean, with a blade — and the back half of the book was simply gone. Whoever took it took it recently enough that the remaining pages hadn't shifted. Kestrel turned the cover with hands that shook more than he wanted them to.

Alden's handwriting. Of course it was. Seven years, and the sight of it went through Kestrel like a hook through soft flesh — the same forward-leaning script, the same habit of crossing his t's too hard so they tore the page. Their father had beaten penmanship into both of them at the same kitchen table; only Alden had ever made it look like he enjoyed the beating.

*Day one out of Marrowgate,* the first surviving page began. *Crew of fourteen. Vane's contract, Vane's coin, my name on the manifest as route-master because nobody else can read the flats.*

Kestrel closed his eyes.

*Route-master.* Alden had been proud of that title. He'd written home about it — one letter, brief, insufferably pleased with himself, arriving two weeks before the news arrived that the Provision expedition had gone silent. Kestrel remembered the letter better than he remembered most of that year. He remembered being angry about it, actually, angry that his little brother was taking Vane money to walk into a story everyone knew ended badly. He remembered composing a reply full of older-brother contempt and never sending it, because pride was cheaper than postage and he'd always assumed there would be time.

There had not been time.

He opened his eyes and read.

*The Provision is not what the charts say. Charts call her a supply hauler — grain, tools, water-rites, the usual drowned-cargo rumor. She's not. I got a look at the manifest fragment in the captain's strongbox before we ever left Marrowgate, and I'll say it plain so there's no mistaking it later: the Provision was carrying a ledger. Sealed case, wax-stamped, guild-escorted. A survey ledger of the deep aquifer.*

Kestrel's breath stopped.

The deep aquifer. Every child in the Saltreach grew up on the word the way children elsewhere grow up on ghosts — the fabled mother-water, the vein beneath the veins, the source that fed the springs nobody could explain and drained the wells nobody could save. The water wars had started over less than a rumor of it. Whole towns had burned over less than a chart. And here was his brother's handwriting, matter-of-fact, forward-leaning, tearing the page with every crossed t:

*If it's real, it ends the wars. Not by giving everyone water — nothing gives everyone water — but by showing whose land sits over the vein. That's power you can't dig up or drink. Whoever holds that ledger holds the map of everything under us.*

*Vane knows. I think Vane has known since before she signed us. I don't know yet if that makes her a fool or something worse.*

Kestrel read the line three times. Around him the tunnel breathed its cold sweet breath, and Osha watched him without speaking, and somewhere far above, muffled through stone and salt, the wind off the Glass Reach made a sound like a fleet changing course.

* * *

He read on, and the journal became a log of the journey, and the journey became familiar in the way of a nightmare retold.

*Fourth day. Crossed the Silt Gate ahead of schedule. Maddox's convoy shadowing us — Corvane colors, running dark. Vane says let them follow; she says the Provision rewards the patient. I don't like how much she smiles when she says it.*

*Maddox.* Kestrel filed the name beside the memory of a sandstorm basin and a captain with Alden's laugh in his mouth. So Maddox had chased the Provision even then. Had followed it all the way to the end, whatever the end had been, and walked away knowing something. Walking away while these eleven — twelve, thirteen, fourteen — stayed.

*Sixth day. Water discipline holding. Hesk counts the rations twice daily and reports the count to Vane in private. I've started counting them myself. Our numbers don't match. His are lower.*

Kestrel looked up sharply. "Hesk," he said aloud, and the name fell into the tunnel like a stone into a well.

Osha's eyes narrowed. "The quartermaster? Our Hesk?"

"Our Hesk counts our rations twice daily." Kestrel heard his own voice go flat and careful, the voice he used when the ground under him started shifting. "And reports to Vane in private."

"That was seven years ago. Different crew."

"Hesk isn't young, Osha. Hesk wasn't young then." He turned back to the pages, and the dread that had been planted in him at Vane's campfire — *trust no one who counts* — put out another root, deeper.

*Alden counted,* he thought. *Alden counted, and Alden wrote it down, and Alden is dead in a hole in the earth with a cut journal under his arms.*

* * *

The middle pages were route notes, mostly — soundings, landmarks, the small meticulous record of a route-master doing his work well even as the work curdled around him. Kestrel read them fast, hungry and grieving at once, because every entry was Alden's voice and none of them were meant for him. Then, two-thirds through the surviving half, the handwriting changed. Tighter. Faster. Written, Kestrel guessed, by lamplight in a hurry, or by a man who had stopped trusting his own crew enough to write slowly.

*Ninth day. Found who sold us.*

The words stood alone on the page for the length of a full line, underlined twice, the second stroke tearing through the paper.

*I'll write it plain. One of ours has been passing our position to Corvane since Marrowgate. Signal work — flares, mirror code, once a relay marker left at the cairns. I caught the pattern because the counts never matched and the delays always did. Every time we lost half a day, Corvane gained exactly half a day. That's not weather. That's arithmetic.*

*I won't put the name in this book. Names get read by the wrong eyes, and I've learned this week that books travel worse than people. But I told Vane. I told her at the last camp before the Shallows, and I watched her face while I said it, and brothers, I want to be wrong about what I saw there.*

*She wasn't surprised.*

Kestrel's hands had gone very still. Stillness was a desert skill; it lived in the body below thought. He was aware of Osha rising quietly, of her taking the lantern and lifting it higher, of her checking the tunnel mouth behind them without being asked — because she had read his face, and his face had told her what kind of information this was, and divers knew when to watch the exits.

*Vane wasn't surprised,* the page repeated, in ink seven years dry. *Either she already knew and let it run, or she's part of it, or — worst thought, the one I keep putting down and picking back up — she doesn't care who wins the race as long as she's paid either way. I don't know which it is. I know she intercepted a message meant for the guild two days ago and told no one. I saw the courier bird come in. I saw her burn the slip.*

*Burned slips leave edges. I pulled enough of it out of the fire ring to read four words. Four words, and I've been turning them over ever since like stones looking for what's under them:*

*THE PROVISION TURNS.*

*Trust no one who counts.*

Kestrel did not realize he had stopped breathing until Osha's hand closed on his shoulder.

"You've seen this," she said. Not a question. "The words. You've seen them before."

"Last night." His voice sounded like someone else's. "In a message I stole out of her strongbox. Same words. Same hand." He looked down at the page, at the torn t's, at the underline that had ripped clean through the paper seven years ago in a lamplit tent beside a drowned sea. "It's his writing, Osha. It was always his writing. She's been carrying my brother's warning around in an iron box like it was evidence of a debt."

"Maybe it was."

"No." He was standing now; he didn't remember standing. "Maybe it was *proof*. Maybe it was the only proof anyone ever had, and she sat on it, and she sat on it, and then she came to me with new coordinates and an apology she didn't mean and asked me to walk the same road he died on." The words came out level, which frightened him more than shouting would have. Level was what happened when there was too much feeling to waste any of it on volume. "She didn't hire a guide. She hired a bloodhound. She wanted the Thornbury nose on this trail because if anyone could find what Alden found—"

He stopped. Because the thought underneath the thought had finally surfaced, and it was worse than all the others, and it fit.

"—because if anyone could find what Alden found," he finished quietly, "it's the man who shares his blood and his training and his father's compass. She didn't want a guide. She wanted *him*, and he was dead, so she took the next best thing."

The tunnel breathed. The lantern flame leaned and righted itself.

* * *

There were three pages left.

Kestrel knew it the way you know a stairwell is ending — by the feel of the pages under his thumb, thinning, the final leaf lighter than the rest. He made himself sit back down. He made himself read slowly, because these were the last words Alden Thornbury had ever set down, and a man owed the dead that much: attention. Full attention. No skimming toward the ending.

*Last page, and I'll be quick because the light's going and so is the paper.*

*We reached the Shallows at slack tide. The mast showed, just like the stories say — a black finger above the water for the length of a bell, then gone. Vane ordered the dive for morning tide. I argued for diving now, at slack, while the wreck stands open. She overruled me. She said the hold floods and drains on a rhythm and we should take it at the drain. She said it like a woman reciting someone else's lesson.*

*But here's what I know, and I'm writing it so it survives me: the wreck doesn't flood and drain. It turns. There's a vent under it — I felt the warm current come up past the hull at slack, warm water in cold country, pushing up from below like breath. Twice a day the Provision fills from underneath, and twice a day it empties, and the timing isn't any tide table's business. It's the aquifer breathing. The ledger isn't guarded. It's *kept*. Kept by the one thing in this desert that never runs dry.*

*So: trust no one who counts. Counting is how they track you, counting is how they sell you, counting is how a quartermaster's tally becomes a knife. The ledger doesn't belong to whoever dives fastest. It belongs to whoever understands what it's sitting on.*

*If you're reading this, I didn't come back up. That's not a mystery, so don't make it one. We sealed ourselves in — my choice, my order, and the crew agreed, every one, after we saw who was waiting on the surface with Corvane's boats. Better the green dark than their hands. Better the water keeps it than the war spends it.*

*The case is sealed and the chamber is braced and the vent will do the rest. Tell Kes—*

The sentence stopped. Mid-word. No ink smear, no hesitation mark — just a stop, as if the light had gone, or the water, or the man. The rest of the page was blank except for one line added lower down, smaller, pressed hard, written — Kestrel was sure of it — in the dark, by feel, the letters leaning drunk against each other:

*tell him north is a choice*

Kestrel sat with the journal open in his lap for a long time.

Above them, faint through the stone, the wind changed its note. Osha said something — his name, probably, twice — but it reached him from a long way off, across seven years and a kitchen table and an unsent letter full of older-brother contempt. *Tell him north is a choice.* Their father's phrase. Their father's compass, worn smooth by two boys' hands, riding against Kestrel's ribs at this moment in its oilcloth wrap. Alden had known. Down here, in the dark, sealing himself into the green with a ledger and a crew who chose to stay — Alden had spent his last strength pressing their father's own words into wet paper, addressed to no one, addressed to him.

"He knew you'd come," Osha said softly. "Didn't he. He wrote it like a letter."

"He wrote it like a will." Kestrel closed the journal and held it a moment against his chest, against the compass, leather to oilcloth, brother to brother. Then he wrapped it, corded it, and stowed it inside his coat, over the heart, where the desert couldn't take it and neither could Vane.

When he stood, his legs answered him steadily, and his mind had done the thing desert minds do: it had stopped drowning and started mapping. Because the journal had given him more than grief. It had given him arithmetic.

Hesk counted rations then. Hesk counted rations now. *Trust no one who counts* — and aboard their convoy, right now, a quartermaster tallied their stores twice daily and reported privately to a woman who had burned a message seven years ago and smiled her way through a race she might have been losing on purpose.

"Osha," Kestrel said. "Where's Hesk right now?"

Her face changed. "On watch. Top of the tunnel. Why?"

"Because Alden caught a traitor with a tally sheet, and I just found fourteen graves that say what happens next." He was already moving, lamp high, boots finding the old smuggler's footing without his attention. "And because Vane ordered us into the Shallows on a 'drain tide' — and my brother proved there's no such thing."

Behind them the crescent of bones settled back into the cold sweet dark, keeping what they had chosen to keep.

Ahead, up through the breathing stone, the sixth tide was waiting — and so, Kestrel was suddenly certain, was a man with a ledger's price already memorized.
