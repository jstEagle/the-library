## 17. Fourth Tide

Sable Vane had commanded salvage crews through two wars, three mutinies, and one marriage, and she had learned long ago that the sea of salt told you everything if you knew how to listen. The flats had a grammar. The crunch of crust under a wagon wheel meant dry going. A dull wet slap meant brine seeping close beneath the surface. And when the horizon went soft and gray at its edges — the way it did now, ahead and to the east, where the Silt Gate cut through the drowned hills like a wound that never healed — the salt was telling her they were out of time.

She stood on the lead wagon's bench beside Pell's replacement, a silent Marrowgate teamster who drove with his elbows and watched nothing but the road, and she looked back down the line of her convoy: six wagons now instead of seven, the water drays riding low, the crew scattered across them like survivors of something rather than members of an expedition. Which, she supposed, was what they were.

Kestrel Thornbury rode the third wagon, and he was watching her.

He'd been watching her since the Glass Reach, since Pell went down into the sinkhole with a sound Vane still heard at night — not a scream, worse, a kind of surprised quiet, the crust closing over him like a book shutting. Kestrel had shouted the warning a half-second before the ground gave. He'd been right. She'd been wrong to push the pace through mirage country, and he knew it, and he hadn't said a word about it, which was somehow more unbearable than if he'd crowed about it for a week.

That was the trouble with Kestrel Thornbury. Seven years ago he'd been easy to hate — all straight-backed certainty and ranger's righteousness, the kind of man who thought a clean conscience was a form of wealth. She had taken that certainty apart in front of half the guild with a forged survey report and a well-timed lie, and she had enjoyed it, because he'd beaten her fair at the licensing trials and fairness was something she'd never trusted. But the man who came back from ruin was harder to hate. Ruin had sanded him down to something quieter. Something that pulled drowning strangers out of skirmish fire without asking whose side they were on.

*Don't,* she told herself, turning back to face the Gate. *You need him for six more days. Don't start needing him.*

The Silt Gate announced itself long before they reached it. First came the smell — sulfur and old rot, the breath of drowned country. Then the ground changed under the wagons, crust giving way to a gray paste that sucked at the wheels, and the drowned hills rose around them on either side, their flanks striped with tide-marks like the rings of some vast felled tree. Between the hills ran the old causeway road, built by engineers dead two hundred years for a sea that had since walked away, and the road funneled down toward a single gap between two bluffs — the Gate itself, maybe forty yards wide, choked with silt bars and the ribs of ships that had tried to force it in the wrong season.

It was the only way through to the Shallows. And everyone knew it.

"Vane." Osha came up the line at a jog, rope coiled over one shoulder, her diver's calm stretched thin. "Brammel says the Corvane wagons are behind us. Two miles. Maybe less."

"Two miles is a day, in this muck."

"Not the way Maddox drives. Hesk's flares have been painting us for him since Second Tide. He knows exactly where we are. He's not following our trail — he's following *us*."

Vane felt the old cold settle into her stomach, the one that arrived whenever a plan met its arithmetic. Six wagons, twenty-two souls, half her water gone to the storm basin and half her patience gone to Hesk. Behind them, a fleet that outgunned her three to one. Ahead, a bottleneck every soul on the flats would fight through at the same hour, because the tide governed the Gate and the tide did not negotiate.

"How long until low water?" she asked.

Osha spat over the wheel-rail, reading the sun, the stripes on the bluff, the smell of the air, the whole litany of a diver's instincts. "Four hours. Maybe five. The Gate runs shallow at low water — you could take wagons across the sill itself if you didn't mind scraping your axles down to the iron."

"And at high water?"

"At high water the Gate is a throat," Osha said. "And we're standing in it."

* * *

They made the decision the only way decisions got made anymore, which was badly, quickly, and with everyone shouting. Vane called the crew to a halt in the lee of the first bluff, and the argument happened in a ring of faces while the wind carried the faint, unmistakable sound of engines from the east.

"We run for the Gate now," Brammel said. The engineer had grease to his elbows and despair in his voice. "We'll hit the sill at mid-fall. Risky, but we cross ahead of Corvane and we're done with this country."

"We run and they run faster," Osha said. "Maddox's wagons are light. Ours are carrying his prize for him."

"Then we ditch weight—"

"Ditch weight and we die of thirst in the Reach, brilliant—"

"Enough." Vane let the word carry the way she'd learned to, flat and heavy as a dropped anchor. The ring went quiet. She looked at Kestrel, because she hated herself a little for looking at Kestrel, and asked the question she didn't want answered. "Ranger. You've crossed the Silt Gate."

"Twice," Kestrel said. "Both times at low water, both times on foot." He crouched, drew in the silt with one finger: the two bluffs, the gap, the sill, and beyond it the spreading shallows marked with a rough crescent. "The sill runs diagonal. Deeper channel on the north side, hardpan shelf on the south. If we cross hugging the south bluff, we can take wagons over at low water. But we'll be exposed on the sill for the better part of an hour. Anyone on those bluffs with a rifle owns us."

"So Corvane doesn't need to catch us," Vane said slowly. "They just need to get here first and climb."

Kestrel's jaw tightened. She watched him do the same arithmetic she'd already done, arrive at the same ugly sum. "Then we don't run," he said. "We hold the bluffs ourselves. Put riflemen up on both sides tonight, rest the crews, cross at first light on the falling tide."

"And give Maddox a night to surround us."

"And give Maddox a night to surround us," Kestrel agreed. "Or—" and here he paused, and Vane saw the desert ranger surface through the salvager, the man who read country the way she read ledgers, "—we split the difference. Send the two fastest wagons ahead empty, running lights and noise, straight for the Gate like we're making a break for it. Corvane takes the bait, commits to the chase, and bunches up in the funnel. Meanwhile the real convoy circles wide along the north rim and crosses the sill at dawn from above them."

"That's your lead wagon," Brammel said. "The one with the water."

"No," Kestrel said. "The bait carries no water. It carries me, Osha, and enough lanterns to look like a treasury on wheels."

"It also carries the man who knows the crossing," Vane said, and heard her own voice come out sharper than she intended. "If Corvane takes the bait wagon, we lose our guide."

"If Corvane takes the bait wagon," Kestrel said, meeting her eyes, "you lose a debt you were going to have to pay anyway."

The silence after that lasted three heartbeats, and Vane spent all of them furious — at him for offering his life like loose change, at herself for caring, at the flats for making everything cost. "No one is spending anyone," she said finally. "We run the decoy, we run the crossing, and every man comes off that sill alive or I want to know why. Break camp. We move at dusk."

As the crew scattered, Kestrel lingered by her wagon, and for a moment neither of them spoke. The wind off the Reach carried sulfur and engine-smoke.

"You could have taken Maddox's offer," Vane said. "At the basin. He offered you a place, a share, a fleet. Instead you're riding a lantern-wagon into a gun funnel for a woman who ruined you."

"You haven't ruined me yet this time," Kestrel said. There was no smile in it, but there was something adjacent to one, dry as the crust they stood on. "I'm saving that for later."

He walked away before she could decide whether that was forgiveness or a threat.

* * *

Dusk came down purple and cold, and the decoy went out like a promise.

Vane watched from the north rim as the bait wagon broke away below, lanterns blazing fore and aft, Kestrel standing tall on the bench with his coat open so the light caught the brass buttons of his old ranger's uniform — a costume, a lure, a man making himself visible to men with rifles. Osha rode beside him. The wagon thundered down the funnel toward the Gate, loud and bright and gloriously stupid, and somewhere in the dark behind it, engines changed pitch. Corvane took the bait. Vane heard the pursuit build — the growl of light wagons pushing hard, a whistle blown twice, the disciplined chaos of a fleet committing to a chase.

"Now," she breathed. "Now, now, *now*."

The real convoy moved along the north rim in darkness, wheels wrapped in sacking, drivers steering by starlight and Kestrel's chalk marks. For an hour it worked. For an hour, Vane allowed herself the dangerous luxury of believing the plan was good.

Then the shooting started.

It began as flashes at the mouth of the Gate — muzzle-light stuttering against the bluff shadows — and Vane understood in one sick drop of the stomach that Maddox hadn't committed to the chase. He'd split his force too. Half the fleet chased the lanterns, and half had climbed the south bluff hours ago and sat there in the dark, waiting for exactly the crossing she'd planned, because Rell Maddox had known Alden Thornbury and Alden Thornbury had taught his brother to think, and brothers, apparently, thought alike.

"Rifles on the south bluff!" The cry went down the line, and then the night tore open.

The first volley swept the head of the column and found the lead wagon. Vane saw it happen in fragments: the draft-team screaming, a wheel bursting, the wagon slewing sideways on the rim track with its driver pitched clear and its cargo of tools and spare iron cascading down the bluff-face in a ringing avalanche. Return fire crackled from their own flankers. Someone was shouting for Brammel. The lead wagon, their last reserve of repair gear, groaned once at the rim's edge and went over, tumbling end over end into the dark, and landed on the silt below with a sound like a struck bell that kept ringing.

"Push through!" Vane screamed, standing upright on her own wagon bench with bullets singing past her like hornets with opinions. "The sill! Get to the sill! They can't hold the bluff and the Gate both!"

It was nearly true. It was true enough. The convoy lurched forward through the smoke and cordite stink, down the switchback toward the Gate floor, and Vane was standing to wave the second wagon onward when the rifleman on the bluff found his range.

She never heard the shot. She heard Kestrel.

He came off the third wagon like something thrown, crossed the space between wagons in two strides nobody should have managed on a lurching deck, and hit her at the waist, and the world went over sideways. The bullet cracked past where her chest had been and sparked off the wagon's iron rail an inch from her ear. They landed together in the silt between the wheel-rails, his body over hers, heavy and breathing hard, close enough that she could see the salt dust on his eyelashes and the old scar she'd given him a reputation over, just above his brow.

"Stay down," he said. Not gently. Not unkindly. Just a fact, like weather.

"I had it handled."

"You didn't."

"I *didn't*," she admitted, because lying under a man who'd just caught a bullet meant for her seemed suddenly obscene, "but I would have."

Something moved through his eyes then — not the hatred she'd earned, not the cold professional distance he'd maintained since Marrowgate. Something older and wearier and more dangerous than either. He rolled off her, came up on one knee, and put three rounds up the bluff-face at the muzzle-flash with the unhurried economy of a man who'd shot at things in worse places, and the flash didn't come again.

"Crossing," he called up the line. "Everyone on the sill. South bluff's spent — they'll shift to the far side. Go!"

They went. The convoy poured onto the causeway sill as the tide fell, wagons grinding over the hardpan shelf in a strung-out line, the drowned hills black on either side, the water sheeting off the mudflats in silver threads under the stars. Behind them the Gate mouth burned with the lantern-wagon's abandoned lights, and Corvane's wagons milled in the funnel like bees whose hive had been moved in the night. By the time Maddox understood the geometry of what had happened, the last of Vane's wagons was off the sill and onto the eastern flats, and the tide was coming back across the causeway like a door closing.

* * *

They made camp at midnight in the lee of a drowned watchtower, its top rising from the shallows like a broken tooth. No fires. Cold rations. The crew collapsed where they stood, and Vane walked the perimeter alone because sleeping was for people whose convoys hadn't lost their lead wagon and half their tools.

She found Kestrel at the tower's base, sitting on a fallen block of masonry, cleaning his rifle with the methodical patience of a man performing a ritual. She stood over him for a while. He didn't look up.

"The buttons were a nice touch," she said. "The ranger's uniform. Very theatrical."

"You hired a guide, not a monk."

"You could have died on that bluff. Both bluffs. You could have died twice today and saved me the trouble of deciding whether to trust you."

Kestrel set down the rifle and looked up at her then, and the moonlight did something inconvenient to his face, stripping away the years and the ruin and leaving, briefly, the man who'd beaten her at the licensing trials — young, certain, infuriatingly decent.

"You counted my rounds on the bluff," he said. "Three shots, one target, no wasted powder. You used to count my rounds at the trials too. You told the guild board I panicked and fired wild."

"You did fire wild."

"I fired *first*. There's a difference." He rose, slinging the rifle, and stood closer than he needed to, close enough that she could see he was exhausted down to the marrow and still standing straight, because that was simply what he was made of. "Seven years, Vane. I've had seven years to decide what I'd say to you if I ever stood this close again. Turns out there's nothing to say. You did what you did. I am what I am. We both want to reach the Provision, and tomorrow the tide gives us one more chance to do it."

"That's not an alliance," Vane said. "That's a truce of convenience."

"All the good ones are." He picked up his bedroll and turned away, then paused, and without looking back said, quietly, in the voice of a man setting down something heavy: "For what it's worth — you counted right, back then. I did panic. Just not about the shooting."

He walked off toward the picket line, and Sable Vane stood alone under the broken watchtower with the tide whispering against the sill miles behind them, and found that the words were in her mouth — *thank you*, two syllables, the easiest currency in the world — and found that she could not spend them. Not yet. Her pride was the last thing she owned outright, and she guarded it the way other people guarded gold.

But she stood there a long time, listening to him move in the dark, and she thought: *when this is over — when the Provision turns and the ledger is ours and the debt is paid — I will say it. I will say it plainly, and he will not forgive me, and it will be the first honest transaction of my life.*

She did not know, then, that the ledger would ask a price no bounty could cover. She did not know that Hesk was awake at the picket line, counting wagons and memorizing tides, his cipher-key warm in his pocket. And she did not know that the Provision, patient beneath the green water of the Shallows, had already begun to turn.

She only knew that tomorrow was the fifth tide, and that for the first time in seven years, she was glad Kestrel Thornbury was on her side of the field.
