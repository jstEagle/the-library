## 19. Hesk's Price

Kestrel did not sleep with the journal under his coat.

That was the first thing a thief would expect — that the man who carried something precious would clutch it to his chest like a child's doll, and so Kestrel had learned long ago, on nights colder than this one, that the safest place for a treasure was somewhere ordinary. He had sewn the half of Alden's journal into the lining of his bedroll weeks ago, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp, and what he slept with against his ribs was nothing but a folded square of canvas cut to the same size, stiffened with salt so it had the right weight, the right crackle when a man turned over in his sleep.

He had not done it because he suspected Hesk. He had done it because he suspected everyone, and because seven years of grief had taught him that suspicion was cheaper than trust. But in the last two days, watching the quartermaster across the cookfire — the way Hesk's eyes went to Kestrel's coat whenever Kestrel reached inside it, the way he'd begun taking the middle watch instead of the first, the way he chewed his dried meat slower than any man who wasn't thinking about something else — the suspicion had sharpened into certainty, and certainty into a plan.

Tonight they camped in the lee of a basalt shelf at the edge of the smuggler's tunnel country, where the bones of Alden's crew still lay three miles back along the passage, wrapped now in Vane's spare tarp because Kestrel could not leave them naked to the wind. The convoy was small and tired: one wagon fewer than they'd started with, the lead wagon burned at the Silt Gate, its ash still under Brammel's fingernails. Osha slept near the water barrels with her diving knife loose in its sheath. The twins Pell-and-Pim — only Pim now, though he answered to both names some nights, half-asleep, as if waiting for his brother to finish a sentence — had taken the far watch without being asked. Vane sat apart, as she always did since her confession, mending a strap no one had asked her to mend, and Kestrel had not spoken to her since he walked away from her fire. Some things needed more silence than an apology could fill.

And Hesk lay by the wagon, snoring.

The snoring was very good. That was the thing Kestrel noticed most. It was rhythmic, professional, unbroken — the snoring of a man who had practiced it, or of a man whose body knew how to perform sleep while his mind ran numbers. Real sleep stuttered. Real sleep swallowed itself and restarted. Hesk's snoring ran like a well-oiled pump, and Kestrel lay in the dark listening to it and thought: *you are counting my breaths, you son of a bitch.*

The moon was a thin nail-paring, low over the eastern rim, which meant the dark would be deepest in the hour before it rose enough to matter. Kestrel closed his eyes and breathed slow and even, the way he'd taught himself to breathe when tracking men through the night — the breath of a sleeper, four counts in, six out — and let the hours come down around him like silt settling in still water.

* * *

It was past midnight when the snoring stopped.

Kestrel kept his breathing steady. In the black behind his eyelids he built the camp from sound alone: the tick of the wagon's iron fittings cooling, the mutter of wind off the shelf, Osha's soft exhale, the desert's own silence pressing in like a hand. Then, threaded through all of it, a sound almost too small to be a sound at all — the whisper of leather sole lifting from grit, held, set down again. A man moving the way trackers move, or thieves. A man who had learned it somewhere and learned it well.

Hesk crossed the camp in maybe forty heartbeats. Kestrel tracked him by ear — past the cold fire ring, past the water barrels, a pause beside Osha that made Kestrel's jaw tighten until he heard Osha's breathing continue unchanged, deep and trusting, and felt something in his chest go hard and cold. Then the pause at his own bedroll. The hesitation there was longer. A thief's whole career lived in that hesitation — the moment before the hand went where the hand should not go.

Kestrel let him commit.

The fingers touched the bedroll's edge, found the fold where the false packet lay tucked beneath the flap, and began to draw it free — and Kestrel moved. Not fast; speed made noise. He came up out of his blankets the way water comes up through sand, silent and total, one knee landing outside Hesk's reach, one hand closing on the quartermaster's wrist and the other clamping flat over his mouth before the man's lungs could load a scream.

They froze together like that, two statues in the dark, while the camp breathed on around them. Hesk's pulse hammered against Kestrel's palm — fast, then faster, then, interestingly, steadying, as if the man were already negotiating terms with himself. His eyes in the dark were wet and wide and, above Kestrel's hand, calculating.

"Don't," Kestrel said, barely a breath. "There are three people in this camp who'd gut you for less than this, and I'm the kindest of them."

He lifted his hand a finger's width. Hesk didn't scream. He exhaled instead, long and shaking, and whispered back with the flat pragmatism Kestrel had come to know as his native tongue:

"You weren't asleep."

"No."

"The packet's wrong." Hesk's voice was thin but level. "Too light. I've hauled cargo twenty years, Thornbury. I know what paper weighs."

"Then you also know I've been waiting nine days for you to try this." Kestrel took the wrist higher, turned it, felt the man's resistance gutter out. "Get up. Quietly. We're going to have a conversation away from sleeping ears, and you're going to decide how honest it is."

* * *

They walked fifty paces out from camp, to the base of the basalt shelf where the rock threw a wall of blackness deeper than the night around it. Kestrel put Hesk's back to the stone and stood close enough that the man could feel the ranger's knife through his coat, though Kestrel hadn't drawn it. The suggestion was the weapon. The blade was just paperwork.

"Start with who," Kestrel said.

"I don't—"

"Nine days ago you were signaling Corvane with flares. Osha caught you, and you said you were paid to report our position but not by whom. I believed you then, because a bought man lies cheap and you looked like a man carrying a lie too heavy to run with." Kestrel leaned in. The smell of him — salt, sweat, engine grease borrowed from too many hours helping Brammel — filled the space between them. "But flares are wages. This is investment. Nobody invests in a salvage convoy unless they know what's at the end of it. So somebody told you what we're really hunting. Somebody told you about the ledger. Who?"

The silence stretched. Out on the flats, wind combed through the saltbush with a sound like whispering crowds. Hesk's face worked through several expressions — fear, calculation, a brief flicker of genuine misery — and settled on something older and wearier than any of them.

"You'll kill me anyway," he said. "Once I say the name."

"If I wanted you dead I'd have let you take the packet and put a bolt in your back at fifty yards. I want the name, Hesk. Give me something true for once in this convoy's miserable life."

Hesk laughed — a dry, cracked sound with no humor in it, the laugh of a man hearing the shape of his own coffin. He rubbed his freed wrist. When he spoke again, his voice had dropped to almost nothing, as if the name itself might be listening.

"Doss," he said. "Marrowgate. Doss of the counting house."

The world did not tilt. Kestrel had expected, in some chamber of himself he hadn't known was braced, that it would — that the name would land like a blow and stagger him. It didn't. It landed like confirmation, like the final stone clicking into a lock he'd been turning blind for weeks, and what he felt instead of shock was a terrible, clarifying calm.

"Say it again."

"Doss. Your Doss. The man holding your paper." Hesk's eyes flicked up, found Kestrel's, held them with the defiance of the damned. "You think it's chance your license got called due ten days before Vane rode into town? You think it's chance the only guide fool enough to sign on happened to be the one drowning? He picked you, Thornbury. He's been picking you since the day you signed the note. Every payment you missed, every extension he granted — that wasn't mercy. That was reeling line."

"He wants the ledger." Kestrel heard his own voice go flat, distant, a voice reporting weather. "The aquifer map. Enough water-rites to end the wars — or to own them."

"He wants to *sell* it," Hesk said. "Corvane's just the buyer. Or one of them. Maddox pays for position reports; Doss pays for product. I'm a simple man, ranger. I move goods. Information's just goods that don't spoil." He paused, and something almost like shame crossed his face, quickly buried. "He has my family's debt too. My brother's. Fourteen years of it. You're not the only man in Marrowgate on a leash. You're just the only one who didn't know he was wearing a collar."

The words hung between them. Kestrel turned them over and found, to his disgust, that none of them surprised him. Ten days. Doss had given him ten days — exactly long enough for pride to starve, exactly long enough for Sable Vane's contract to look like salvation instead of ruin. And the Provision, the sworn-off job, the wreck that had eaten his brother — dangled in front of the one man in Marrowgate with the grief and the desperation to say yes. It wasn't luck. It had never been luck. The debt had been the leash, and the leash had been leading him here, across six tides of saltflat, toward a drowned ship full of paper that could buy a city — for Doss.

"How long?" Kestrel asked. "How long have you been his?"

"Since Marrowgate. Before we loaded the first wagon." Hesk shrugged, a small defeated motion. "He approached me at the counting house. Knew my brother's name. Knew what he owed to the copper. Men like Doss always know. That's their whole trade — knowing what a man can't afford to refuse." He looked at the ground. "He said find out what the wreck holds. Said if it's coin, take inventory. Said if it's anything else—" Hesk's throat worked. "Said bring him the cipher. The rest doesn't matter without the cipher, he told me. Ledger's just locked paper. Cipher's the key to everything."

The cipher. Alden's journal pages, dense with the expedition's field code, the key to reading whatever the ledger contained — and Kestrel had been carrying them against his skin for nine days, sewn into oilcloth, sleeping beside them like a brother's ghost. He thought of the bones in the tunnel wearing expedition gear. He thought of Alden's warning, inked years ago in a dead man's hand: *one of our own sold us out.* One of their own. And now one of Kestrel's own, and the pattern repeating itself across seven years like a tide coming back for what it left unfinished.

"Vane," Kestrel said suddenly. "Does Doss know about Vane's confession? About the message she intercepted?"

Hesk blinked. "I report positions and cargo. Her sins are her own business."

"But Doss knows why you're really here. He knows it's the ledger, not the bounty."

"He wrote the question," Hesk said simply. "I just carry the answer back."

Kestrel stepped back. The knife stayed sheathed; the suggestion dissolved into the dark. For a long moment he only looked at the quartermaster — this gray, competent, tired man who had counted rations fairly and signaled their deaths impartially, who had sold them all for the same reason Kestrel had signed Vane's contract, because the alternative was drowning slowly in someone else's arithmetic.

"Here's what happens now," Kestrel said. "You go back to your bedroll. You snore. Tomorrow you do your job exactly as you've done it, and you send Doss nothing — no cipher, no word, nothing. Because if a single page leaves this convoy through your hands, I will tell Vane what you are, and she won't be gentle, and neither will Osha, and Pim will help them because he needs something to be angry at that isn't the world. Do you understand me?"

"And after?" Hesk's voice cracked on it. "After the wreck. After the ledger. What happens to a man like me when the job's done and I've brought his employer nothing?"

"That depends," Kestrel said, "on whether you'd rather owe Doss or owe me. One of those debts gets forgiven."

Hesk stared at him. Somewhere behind his eyes, Kestrel could see the arithmetic running — the same cold ledger-work the man applied to everything, weighing loyalty against survival, coin against throat. And then, slowly, the quartermaster nodded, once, like a man signing a contract he already regretted.

"One condition," Hesk said hoarsely. "When this is over — when we're back in Marrowgate — you tell Doss yourself. To his face. That his leash broke. I've wanted to see a man's face fall off for fourteen years."

"Get in your bedroll, Hesk."

The quartermaster went. Kestrel stood a moment longer in the shadow of the shelf, looking east, where the nail-paring moon was climbing and the saltflats beyond camp ran silver and endless toward the Shallows — toward the wreck, toward the vent, toward whatever waited in the green dark under the sixth tide. Two tides left, maybe three if the weather turned. Doss had spent fourteen years and a stranger's brother buying his way to this stretch of desert, and he thought the hardest part was done.

Kestrel pressed his hand flat against his ribs, against the oilcloth and the pages sewn there, against the last true words of a dead man.

*The Provision turns,* Alden had written. *Trust no one who counts.*

He'd thought it was a riddle. Standing in the dark with the taste of Hesk's confession still in the air, Kestrel finally understood it was just good advice, given too late — and that the counting had never stopped, not in Marrowgate, not on the flats, not even here at the edge of the world. Everyone was counting. Everyone was owed. And somewhere behind them, riding the wind off Marrowgate like a smell of smoke, the creditor was already reaching for what he believed was his.

Kestrel walked back to his bedroll, lay down, and did not sleep at all.

* * *

Dawn came up the color of old brass. Kestrel was already awake when Osha stirred, and he crossed to her quietly, crouching by the water barrels where the others couldn't hear.

"We need to talk," he said. "Before Vane wakes. Before anyone wakes."

Osha read his face the way she read water — quickly, completely, without flinching. She glanced once toward the wagon, where Hesk lay snoring his perfect, practiced snore, and then back at Kestrel, and her hand drifted, casual as scratching an itch, to the knife at her hip.

"It's worse than Corvane, isn't it," she said. It wasn't a question.

"Worse," Kestrel said. "And closer to home than either of us guessed."

Behind them, the sun cleared the rim of the world and struck the saltflats ablaze, and out on that burning white distance, small and patient as a debt, the road to the Shallows waited — two tides of it, and every mile of it watched.
