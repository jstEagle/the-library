## 20. Fifth Tide

Osha felt the change in the water before anyone saw it.

She was riding the lead wagon's running board, boots braced against the slats, when the air came off the flats different — heavier, wetter, carrying that mineral tang she knew from a hundred dives in flooded quarry pits. Salt and iron and something older underneath, something like the breath of a cellar that had been closed for years. The others smelled it too. She watched Brammel lift his head from the engine housing like a hound catching scent, and Hesk go still mid-count of the water barrels, his lips moving around numbers he'd forgotten.

"Shallows," Kestrel said from the driver's bench. He said it quietly, the way a man says the name of a grave.

They had been crossing since before dawn, the fifth tide of their passage, and the saltflat had been changing under them all morning — hard pan giving way to crust, crust to mud, mud to a gray spongy ground that sucked at the wagon wheels and made the convoy groan like a ship in weather. The drowned canyon country was behind them now. Ahead, the land simply stopped pretending to be land. The horizon had gone soft and silver, and where the sky met it there was no clean line but a trembling, as if the world were seen through heat-shimmered glass.

Osha had grown up on water. Real water — rivers, lakes, the cold green reservoirs of the northern ranges where her people had dived for salvage three generations before the wars made divers worth more than soldiers. She knew every mood water could wear. And what lay ahead of them now was not a lake or a sea but something between: the Shallows, where an old arm of the ocean had crept inland over centuries and then been betrayed by the drying climate, leaving behind a vast, shallow, brackish sheet — knee-deep for miles in places, forty feet down in the old channels, and hiding, somewhere beneath its dull green skin, the thing they had crossed half a desert to find.

"Depth soundings," Vane called from the second wagon.

Pell would have taken the soundings. Osha's chest tightened at the thought, the way it did every time the twins' absence opened up again — one twin gone into a sinkhole, the other walking silent at the rear of the convoy with his eyes fixed on the ground, as if he might find his brother in the cracks of the earth. Pim hadn't spoken more than ten words in two days. He took the sounding pole now without being asked and stood at the bow of the lead wagon as they rolled out onto the first true water, driving the pole down through the murk again and again, calling depths in a flat voice that didn't sound like him at all.

"Two feet. Three. Four. Six." A pause, the pole sliding deeper. "Nine."

Nine feet. Osha looked over the side. The water was the color of old bottle glass, opaque near the surface, and she could see nothing below the first handspan except the ghost of her own reflection, warped by ripples. But she could feel it — that was the diver's curse and gift both — feel the weight of what lay under them, the way you feel a body under a blanket in a dark room. Nine feet wasn't deep. Nine feet was nothing. She had worked eighty-foot black water by candle-lamp. And yet her hands were cold on the wagon rail, and she couldn't have said why, exactly, except that everything about this place felt *watched*.

The convoy moved out onto the Shallows proper along a spine of hardened ground the old charts called the Causeway Road — a raised bed of packed shell and stone, barely wider than the wagons, running straight as a spear toward the heart of the flood. On either side the water stretched away, broken here and there by dead trees standing to their shoulders in the flood, white and branchless, like a congregation of the drowned waiting for a sermon. The sky above was high and pale and empty. Their engines, muffled per Vane's order, ticked and clanked in a silence so complete Osha could hear the water lapping against the wheels.

It was Hesk who broke it, because Hesk couldn't bear quiet; it gave him nothing to sell.

"Charts say the wreck sits four miles out," he said, coming up beside her on the running board. He kept his voice low, conversational, the tone of a man discussing freight rates. "Low tide's past noon. If your captain's coordinates are right, we'll see her mast about then."

*Your captain.* Osha noted the phrasing. Hesk had stopped saying *our* anything days ago. "If they're right," she said.

"You doubt her?"

"I doubt everyone." She said it without heat, and meant it without malice, and Hesk smiled his thin smile as if she'd paid him a compliment.

He drifted off toward the rear, and Osha found herself watching him go, watching the set of his shoulders, the way his eyes flicked constantly to the horizon behind them — south, always south, where Corvane would come from. Twice since the Silt Gate skirmish she'd caught him with his signal kit half-unpacked. Twice he'd had an explanation ready before she asked. She had reported it to Vane, and Vane had done nothing visible, which frightened Osha more than punishment would have. It meant Vane was keeping Hesk close the way you keep a snake you haven't yet decided how to kill.

Ahead, Kestrel stood up on the bench, one hand shading his eyes.

Osha followed his gaze and forgot, for a moment, how to breathe.

* * *

The mast rose out of the Shallows like a finger pointing at heaven from a grave.

It stood perhaps half a mile ahead, dead center on the Causeway Road's line, and at first Osha took it for another dead tree — the Shallows were full of them. But no tree grew that straight, or that tall, or with that particular lean of engineered things, the deliberate rake of a spar stepped by human hands. As the convoy drew closer, details resolved out of the haze: the crossyard still lashed to it, trailing a few feet of rot-blackened rigging that stirred in the breeze like weed; the crow's platform collapsed on one side; and running up the length of the mast itself, faint but unmistakable even through the water-stain of years, a painted band of faded blue.

Provision blue. Every child in Marrowgate knew the color. The supply ships that had once run the water routes wore it on hull and spar alike, and half the songs about the good years mentioned it — *the blue sails coming home* — though the Provision had never worn sails, only the band, tradition older than function.

Nobody spoke. The convoy slowed, engines dropping to an idle, and the only sound was water and wind and Pim's voice, very soft, counting under his breath the way Pell used to count soundings, and then stopping, as if he'd heard himself and couldn't bear it.

Vane came forward along the causeway to the lead wagon. She had her spyglass out but didn't raise it. She just stood looking at the mast with an expression Osha had never seen on her — stripped, unarmored, almost young. Whatever Sable Vane had buried seven years ago, whatever confession she carried behind her teeth like a stone, it surfaced now in her face, and Osha looked away from it because it was not hers to see.

"There she is," Brammel said at last, and his voice cracked oddly on the last word, engineer's reverence and fear in equal measure. "Fourteen years under. She'll be soup inside. Frames gone soft, planking—" He shook his head. "Anything we want will be in the strongrooms. Iron-lined. Those hold."

"If she holds them," Hesk said.

"She holds them," Kestrel said.

Osha glanced at him. He hadn't taken his eyes off the mast, and there was something in his stillness that went beyond focus — a man looking at a door he'd sworn never to open, knowing what waited behind it. His brother was down there. Or his brother's bones were. Or worse than bones, if Alden Thornbury had lived long enough to leave messages, choices, accusations sealed in iron. Osha had read enough of the recovered journal pages to know that Alden had left *something*, and that Kestrel carried it against his ribs like a second heartbeat.

"How long to low water?" Vane asked.

Kestrel checked the sun without looking at any instrument — ranger's habit, reading the sky like a page. "Three hours. Maybe less. Tide's already falling; see the weed line on those stumps?" He pointed to the dead trees. "Water's dropped a foot since morning. By full low, the wreck's upper works should break clear. Hull, deck, maybe the cargo hatch." He paused. "And the vent will be at its quietest. That's when we dive."

"The vent," Osha said.

All of them turned to look at her, and she realized she'd spoken the thought aloud — the thought that had been sitting in her chest since the Glass Reach, growing heavier with every mile. She climbed up onto the wagon bed so they could all hear her, because some things deserved to be said plainly.

"I've been thinking about the name," she said. "*When the Provision turns.* Everyone says it like a curse, like a story to scare children. But wrecks don't turn. Wrecks don't do anything. They sit where they sit until the sea eats them." She looked at the mast, at the water lapping its base a hundred yards out. "So either the name means nothing, or it means the wreck moves. Sits on something alive. A spring, a vent, a channel that floods and drains — twice a day, like a tide, because tides are just the sea breathing, and anything connected to the sea breathes with it."

Silence. Somewhere out on the water, something large turned over — a fish, a turtle, something — and the ripple rings spread wide and slow.

"Alden's journal," Kestrel said quietly. "The pages I have. They mention the hold flooding. Regular flooding. I thought it was damage." His jaw worked. "It wasn't damage. It was schedule."

"Then we dive at slack low and we're out before it turns back," Brammel said. "How long is slack?"

"On a vent-fed basin? Could be an hour. Could be twenty minutes." Osha shrugged. "Could be the vent doesn't follow the tide at all. Springs keep their own time. That's the terror of them — you can't chart a thing that breathes when it wants to."

"Then we don't waste a minute of slack," Vane said. Her armor was back on now, the captain's voice level and hard. "One dive team. Osha leads. Kestrel goes with her — he knows what the ledger looks like, and he has the right to see what's down there. Brammel rigs the lift lines and the air hoses. Everyone else stays on the wagons with weapons ready, because Corvane is behind us and Doss's maps put them half a day back, which means they're closer than that."

"And me?" Hesk asked.

"You'll count barrels," Vane said, and walked away.

* * *

They made camp on the causeway itself, wedging the wagons wheel-to-wheel so the convoy became a small fortress in the middle of the flood. Osha spent the waiting hours the way she always did before a dive: checking gear until checking became prayer. Her mask, her weight belt, the knife at her calf, the spare lamp in oilskin. Brammel had built her a wonder over the course of the journey — a surface hose rigged to a hand-pumped bellows, crude but honest, feeding air down a canvas line that Kestrel would tend from above while she worked. Two hundred feet of hose. Enough for the channels. Not enough for mistakes.

Kestrel sat apart, on the tailgate of the lead wagon, cleaning a rifle he clearly had no intention of using underwater. Osha went and sat beside him. For a while neither spoke. The light was going long and gold, and the mast threw its shadow across the water toward them like a sundial marking the hour of their arrival.

"My brother dove," Kestrel said finally. "Did you know that? Not professionally. He just — loved it. Lakes, mostly. He'd stay down till his lips went blue and come up laughing." He ran the cloth along the barrel without seeing it. "Our father forbade it. Said the water takes what the desert spares. Alden laughed at that too."

"He sounds like he was easy to love," Osha said.

"He was impossible to live with and easy to love. The exact opposite of me." Kestrel set the rifle down across his knees. "Seven years I told myself he died fast. Storm, or raiders, or the sinkhole country. Quick, clean, no time to be afraid." He looked out at the mast. "Now I know he had time. Time to write. Time to choose. And I'm about to swim down into the room where he made his choices, and I don't know if I'm going to find peace down there or proof that I've spent seven years being wrong about everything."

"What could you have been wrong about?"

"That staying away was loyalty." His voice dropped. "He asked me to come, once. Before the expedition. I said the job was cursed and I had a license to protect. He said, 'Then protect it from somewhere else, because I'm going.'" Kestrel exhaled slowly. "I chose my pride over my brother, and then I spent seven years calling the choice principle."

Osha reached over and took the rifle out of his hands, set it aside, and held his hands instead — diver's hands, rope-scarred, steady. "You're going down there tomorrow to bring him home," she said. "Whatever form home takes. That's not pride. That's the other thing."

He looked at her then, really looked, and something in his face eased by a degree — not healed, nothing would heal that, but eased, the way a splint eases a break.

Above them, the first stars were coming out over the Shallows, and the water had gone dark and mirror-still, and the mast stood black against the last light like a candle nobody had lit.

* * *

Osha woke before dawn to a sound she couldn't name.

Not a noise, exactly. A pressure. A deep, sub-audible thrum she felt in her sternum and the roots of her teeth, rising up through the causeway stones, through the wagon frame, through the bedroll beneath her. She lay still and listened with her whole body, the way she'd learned to listen in black water, and after a moment she identified it: the vent. Or the aquifer beneath it. The ground itself breathing — a slow, vast inhalation, felt rather than heard, and then a long silence, and then, fainter, the exhale.

Around her the camp slept on. Hesk snored. The sentry — Pim, poor hollow-eyed Pim, who volunteered for every watch now — stood at the wagon rail staring south. Osha rose quietly, pulled on her gear, and walked to the edge of the causeway.

The pre-dawn water was gray silk. And the mast—

The mast was taller.

She counted before she trusted her eyes, marked the waterline against the barnacled base of the spar, compared it to yesterday. The tide had fallen through the night, yes, but not like this. This was the vent drawing breath, sucking the basin down, pulling the water off the wreck the way a lung pulls air. Already the dark shape of the crossyard stood clear, and below it, emerging inch by inch from the green murk, came the upper deck — railings furred with weed, a cargo hatch hanging open like a jaw, the great blue-painted letters of the ship's name appearing across the stern transom one stroke at a time as the water released its grip:

**PROVISION**

Full and whole, in letters tall as a man, rising out of the Shallows into the first pink light of dawn.

Osha stood at the causeway's edge with her heart hammering and her skin crawling, awed and terrified in equal measure, because she understood suddenly and completely what the old stories had been trying to say. The Provision wasn't hidden. It had never been hidden. It was *kept* — lowered and raised, drowned and revealed, twice a day, by the living water beneath it, shown to whoever arrived at the right hour and concealed from everyone else. The wreck turned like a lock, and the key was time, and they had arrived with almost none of it left.

Behind her, footsteps. Kestrel, rifle forgotten, staring past her shoulder at the emerging hull. Then Vane, then Brammel, then the whole camp waking in a slow ripple, every face turning toward the water, every voice gone silent.

"One tide," Kestrel said softly. "We've got one tide left."

"No," Osha said. She pointed south, where the horizon trembled — not with heat-shimmer this time, but with dust. A long, low, advancing wall of it, moving fast across the flats, and beneath it, catching the new sun, the glint of glass and gunmetal.

"We've got less than that," she said. "Corvane's here."
