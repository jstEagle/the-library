## 21. Into the Green Dark

Osha had been diving since she was nine years old, when her mother first lowered her into the drowned quarries south of Vell with a rope tied around her waist and a lantern in a glass bell. She had felt wrecks shift under her. She had felt currents try to peel her off hulls like old paint. She had once spent four hours in a flooded cistern with a dead man's hand resting on her shoulder because the exit had collapsed behind her and panic was a luxury she could not afford.

She knew every kind of dark the water could offer.

The dark inside the Provision was a new one.

It was not the dark of depth, which is really just distance from light. It was the dark of a throat. The wreck lay canted on its port side in the Shallows, half-buried in silt, its starboard rail barely a fathom beneath the surface at low tide. From above, through the churned green water, it had looked like a ribcage picked clean. From inside, swimming the flooded corridor that had once been its main deck passage, it felt like being swallowed by something that had died mid-meal and never quite finished the work.

Kestrel went first. He had insisted on that, and Osha had let him, because she had watched him all the way from Marrowgate and understood by now that Kestrel Thornbury did not ask permission so much as announce intentions. He moved through the water the way he moved across the saltflats — deliberate, economical, a man who had learned that every wasted motion was a debt collected later. His lantern swung from a hook on his harness, throwing long amber blades across the corridor walls, and where the light touched, Osha saw the wreck's interior for what it was.

Not a salvage site. A tomb that had been tidied.

That was the thing she noticed first, before the dread could properly take root. The corridor was not choked with the debris of a foundering. Cargo netting had been cut loose and dragged aside — dragged, not drifted. A hatch cover stood propped against the bulkhead at a neat angle. Someone had organized this space. Someone had lived in it, or tried to, and living people impose order on chaos the way water imposes level.

She touched Kestrel's shoulder twice — *stop* — and pointed.

He saw it. Even in the lantern's shivering reach, the difference was plain. The silt on the deck plating was undisturbed in a wide swath ahead of them, but along the starboard wall, a channel had been worn through it, smooth as a riverbed. Knees. Someone had crawled this corridor on their knees, over and over, for a long time.

Kestrel's hand drifted to the journal pages sewn inside his coat, and Osha watched him do it, and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that the water would let them keep.

* * *

They had gone down on the ebb, when the Shallows drained and the wreck's upper works stood exposed like the bones of a leviathan left on a table. Vane had wanted to wait for the survey crew's pumps, but there were no pumps, and no survey crew, and the sixth tide was coming whether they were ready or not. So it was the two of them, and Brammel working the winch line from the rail above, and a length of good cordage, and two lanterns in pressure bells that Brammel had sworn — with the specific, unconvincing confidence of an engineer lying about his own work — were rated for twice this depth.

"Fifty breaths past my signal," Osha had told Kestrel on the rail, tapping her own wrist where she wore a leather band with notches cut into it. "Fifty, and we go up whether you've found what you're looking for or not. I don't dive for dead men twice."

"You've dove for dead men before, then," Kestrel had said. Not a question. He had a way of saying things that weren't questions that made them worse than questions.

"Once," she said. "He was my teacher. I brought up his tools. They were the only part of him the water hadn't kept."

Kestrel had looked at her for a long moment in the grey light before dawn, and whatever he saw in her face, he'd nodded and said, "Fifty," like a man agreeing to terms he intended to break.

Now, in the corridor, she counted.

The water inside the Provision was colder than the sea above it. That was wrong, and she knew it was wrong, and knowing didn't help. The Shallows sat over the aquifer vent — Alden's journal had been clear enough about that, and the wreck's daily flooding confirmed it — and cold water rising from the deep rock should have been a gift, a sign of the vent's location, a map written in temperature. Instead it felt like the wreck was breathing out. Like the whole drowned structure was exhaling something old and patient from its lowest rooms, and the cold was just the breath's edge.

She swam closer to Kestrel and put her lantern forward. The corridor ended at a junction: port toward what had been the galley, starboard toward a stairwell descending into black, and straight ahead, the double doors of the crew compartment.

The doors were shut.

That was not remarkable. What was remarkable was how they were shut. A cargo spar — an ironwood length thicker than her thigh — had been wedged between the two door handles and braced against a deck cleat. Someone had crossed it with a second brace, and a third, a lattice of scavenged timber lashed with what looked like stripped cable. It was not the work of a minute. It was not even the work of an hour. It was the work of days, of a person with time on their hands and terror in their chest, building a wall against a world that had already ended.

"From the inside," Osha breathed, and the words came out as a silver ribbon of bubbles that climbed the lantern light and vanished.

Kestrel hung in the water beside her, very still. She had learned to read him in stillness — the stillness of a man tracking, the stillness of a man deciding — and this was neither. This was the stillness of a man standing at a door he was afraid to open because of what it would confirm. His gloved hand rose and hovered an inch from the ironwood spar without touching it, the way you'd hover a hand over a sleeping animal you didn't want to wake.

Osha understood. She had grown up on the drowned coast; she had heard every story the salvage crews told to scare the new divers, and most of them were lies, and the ones that weren't lies were worse. Sealed hatches meant one of two things. Either the people inside had kept something out —

— or they had kept something in.

She looked at the lattice again. At the direction of the braces. Every wedge pointed inward. Every brace was set to resist pressure from the corridor side. Whatever the last survivor of the Provision had feared, they had feared it *out here*.

* * *

They went through the galley first, because Kestrel signed it — a flat chopping motion, *that way* — and because Osha trusted his instinct for terrain even underwater, especially underwater. Desert men read ground the way divers read current, and a wreck was ground, however drowned.

The galley told them things the corridor had only hinted at.

Copper pots hung from hooks, green with age, arranged by size. A knife rack held its full set. On the long prep table, silt had settled in the shapes of objects long since taken away — a rectangle here, a circle there — and beside one of the shapes, pressed into the grime by a hand long dissolved, a palm print. Small. Left hand. Fingers spread, as if someone had braced themselves against the table and stood there a long time, looking at nothing, in a room that was slowly filling.

Kestrel found the tally.

It was scratched into the galley's inner doorframe, low, at kneeling height, in a hand that started confident and deteriorated into gouges. Osha counted by lantern light while Kestrel held the bell steady. Groups of four with a fifth struck across — prison tallies, the oldest counting system in the world. She counted the groups. Counted them again.

"Two hundred and six days," she said. Her voice was a bubble-ribbon, and the number hung in the water between them like something with weight.

Two hundred and six days. The Provision had gone down — she did the arithmetic against the tide tables Vane kept — nearly three years ago. Which meant the last survivor had lived in this wreck for the better part of seven months. Had eaten something, drunk something, kept a tally on a doorframe in a galley that flooded twice a day with water from the deep aquifer. Had crawled that corridor on their knees until the silt wore smooth. Had built their barricade one brace at a time.

And then, at some point, had stopped.

Osha had seen drowned rooms before. She had never seen one that felt *inhabited*, and this one did — the way a den feels inhabited after the wolf has left, the way a house feels when the family is only out at market. The Provision was not an empty wreck. It was an abandoned home, and homes hold the shape of their people the way water holds the shape of nothing at all, and somehow that was worse.

Kestrel's hand was on the doorframe, his fingers resting in the tally marks, not tracing them, just resting, the way you'd rest a hand on a gravestone. Through his glove she could not see his face well — the lantern lit him from below, hollowing his cheeks — but she saw his shoulders, and his shoulders were doing something she had never seen them do in three tides of hard travel. They were shaking. Slightly. The water amplified it, made it terrible, a tremor translated into current.

She put her hand on his arm. Not gently. Firmly, the way you grip a diver who's about to bolt for the surface, because bolting for the surface kills. He turned his head. Under the mask's edge his eyes were wet, and the water made it impossible to tell whether that was grief or just the sea finding its level, and she suspected he was grateful for the ambiguity.

*Fifty,* she signed against his wrist, tapping the notches on her band. They had maybe thirty left.

Kestrel nodded. Then he turned to the stairwell.

* * *

It went down into the hold, and the hold was where the wreck kept its heart.

The stairwell was a throat of black, and the lantern light only reached the first six steps before the water swallowed it. Osha felt the change before she saw it — a pressure shift, a slow lateral pull, the vent's breath cycling. The wreck flooded on a schedule, Alden's pages had said. *The Provision turns.* They were somewhere in the turn now, and the water in the lower hold was moving, and moving water in a drowned structure is a hand looking for things to take.

Kestrel went down. She went down after him, counting notches.

The hold opened around them like a cathedral built by something that hated people. The lantern found cargo cradles the size of wagons, most of them empty, their contents long since looted or dissolved or carried off by the tide. The Provision had been a supply ship — water-rites, coin, sealed stores — and its hold had been built to keep things safe from the world. It had done its job too well. It had kept everything, and let the world's people go.

And there, in the center of the hold, half-sunk in silt, stood the strongroom.

Osha saw it and felt her breath catch in the regulator — a small mechanical hitch, the body's animal brain recognizing a threshold. The strongroom was a cube of riveted plate perhaps three fathoms to a side, set into the hold's floor like a safe dropped into a grave. Its door was shut. Its locking wheel was thrown. And around its base, packed into the silt with the same desperate industry as the barricade above, someone had built a ring of ballast — scrap iron, chain, a broken capstan arm — as if to weigh the whole chamber down, to anchor it, to make sure that whatever was inside stayed inside even if the sea had other plans.

The cold poured out of the seam under the strongroom door. Osha felt it on her bare knuckles like a current from the bottom of the world. The vent was down there. Under the strongroom, or beneath it, or threaded through the rock it sat on — the aquifer's throat, the thing the whole war-torn coast would burn cities to own.

And Alden had known. Alden had been down here, in this hold, in this dark, and had written *the Provision turns* in a hand that Kestrel could not read without shaking, and had sealed a chamber rather than let what was inside it be taken.

Kestrel hung before the strongroom door, lantern raised, and the light trembled with the current and with his hands, and Osha watched him and understood something she had been circling since Marrowgate. He had not come for the ledger. He had come to stand where his brother had stood. Everything else — the debt, the fleet, the race, the coin — was scaffolding around that single need, and now the scaffolding was underwater and the need was all that was left, and it was looking at a locked door.

He reached for the wheel.

She caught his wrist. Hard. He turned, and she pointed up — *air* — and held up her band. Twelve notches left. The lateral pull was strengthening; she could feel the wreck beginning to drink, the vent's cycle turning toward flood. The lower hold would fill first. It was already filling. A ribbon of silt was creeping along the deck plates toward the stairwell like something living, patient, unhurried.

Kestrel looked at the wheel. Looked at her. And Osha saw him make a decision — saw it happen in his face, the same way she'd seen it happen on the causeway at the drowned canyon, the same way she'd seen it when he'd backed Vane against Hesk's mutiny. Kestrel Thornbury deciding looked like a man setting down a weight and picking up a different one.

He didn't touch the wheel. Instead, he leaned close to the seam beneath the door, lantern angled low, and looked.

And went rigid.

Osha crowded in beside him, and the lantern's edge found the gap under the strongroom door, and in the gap — in the thin black slit where the cold poured out — there was light. Faint. Green. Not lantern-green, not phosphor-scum green, but a deep, living, submarine green, pulsing with a slow rhythm that had nothing to do with the tide and everything to do with the rock below. The vent. The aquifer's own light, breathing under the door like a banked coal.

And on the inside of the door, scratched into the plate where only someone kneeling at the seam could ever see it — scratched from the *inside* — a single line of letters, shallow and hurried, the metal around them bright where the silt had never reached:

**IT TURNS FROM BELOW. DO NOT OPEN AT THE TURN.**

Osha read it twice. The second reading changed nothing. The letters did not rearrange themselves into mercy. Someone had been on the other side of that door — or someone had known what was on the other side of that door — and had left a warning calibrated to be found by exactly one kind of person: a diver, at low water, kneeling, looking.

Alden.

The green light pulsed. The silt-ribbon at the stairwell thickened. The wreck groaned — a long, structural complaint, iron and timber settling into the vent's rhythm, the Provision beginning its turn — and Osha felt the first true push of rising water against her fins, cold as the bottom of the world, coming up from below.

She gripped Kestrel's harness and pointed hard at the stairwell. *Up. Now.*

For one terrible half-breath he didn't move. His hand was flat against the strongroom door, over the scratched warning, the way you'd press your palm to a wall to feel a heartbeat through it. The green light under the seam washed his face, and in that light he looked young, and wrecked, and certain — all at once, all three, in a combination Osha had never seen on a living man.

Then the wreck groaned again, louder, and the cold current became a shove, and he moved.

They went up the stairwell two abreast in the narrowing dark, the lantern swinging wild, the water rising past their knees, their waists, their shoulders, the tally marks and the barricade and the palm print on the galley table sliding past below them like the contents of a dream being put away. Osha counted her last notches with her lips moving against the regulator's mouthpiece, and above them the corridor's hatchway showed a small grey coin of surface light, and she hauled Kestrel toward it by the harness because his arms had gone slow, and the green light followed them up from below, fainter and fainter, patient as arithmetic, until the sea took them back.

They broke the surface inside the wreck's upper hull, gasping, in the pocket of trapped air beneath the overturned deck, and Brammel's face appeared at the hatch above, grey with relief, hauling on the line.

Osha got one arm over the rail's shadow and hung there, breathing, before she looked back down through the hatch at the water below — already a hand's depth higher than when they'd gone in, already turning, already green at the edges where the vent's light bled up through the flooded hold.

Kestrel surfaced beside her, tore the regulator free, and coughed for a long time. When he could speak, he didn't say *I found it* or *it's real* or any of the things a salvager says. He looked down at the green water rising in the hatchway, and his voice came out scraped raw.

"She sealed it from the inside, Osha. The strongroom. Someone sealed it from the *inside*."

And below them, deep in the wreck, the green light pulsed once — bright, deliberate, like a knock — and the Provision began, ahead of any tide table Brammel had ever drawn, to turn.
