## 22. The Turning

The water in the hold came up to their waists now, and it was warm.

That was the first wrong thing. Kestrel had been in drowned places before — wells, cisterns, the flash-flooded arroyos of the Saltreach where a man could lose his boots and his toes in the same afternoon — and every one of them had been cold, cold as a dead man's handshake. This water was blood-warm. It moved against his legs with a slow, deliberate pressure, like something breathing.

"Kestrel." Osha's voice came thin through her diving mask, her lamp beam cutting a yellow cone through water gone murky with stirred silt. "You feel that?"

"I feel it."

"Water doesn't sit warm in a hull this deep. Not unless it's coming from somewhere."

Below them, the flooded hold of the *Provision* opened into darkness that their lamps could not finish. The wreck lay canted on its starboard side at an angle that had seemed random from outside — just the way old ships die — but now, standing chest-deep on what had been the port bulkhead, Kestrel felt the geometry of it differently. The ship hadn't simply settled. It had been *placed*, the way a hand places a lid over something it means to keep.

Alden's journal was inside his coat, wrapped in oilcloth against his ribs. He'd read those half-pages so many times in the last two days that he no longer needed lamplight to see them. *The Provision turns.* His brother's handwriting, cramped and hurried, written by a man who had understood something too large for the paper to hold.

*Turns.* Kestrel had thought it meant betrayal. A wheel of treachery, someone selling the crew. Then he'd thought it meant tide — the wreck surfacing and sinking with the moon's pull, which they had already seen. But standing here in warm water that rose while the sea outside stood at slack, he felt the word rearrange itself again, the way a lock's tumblers shift before they give.

"It's rising," Osha said. "Outside tide's slack. I checked the line before we dropped. Slack water, Kestrel. Nothing should be moving."

"How fast?"

She held her lamp low, found the waterline stain on the bulkhead — an old tidemark, dark as a bruise, running along the steel at shoulder height. She looked at it. Looked at the water around their chests. Did the arithmetic without saying any of it out loud, because neither of them wanted the number spoken.

"We find the chamber," she said instead. "Fast."

They swam. Or something between swimming and wading — the hold was full of wreckage, collapsed shelving, cargo cradles torn loose and hanging in nets of rust, and every few strokes Kestrel's lamp caught shapes that his heart insisted on reading as bodies before his eyes corrected them: coiled rope, a swollen crate, the pale curve of a broken spar. The crew compartment they'd found sealed from the inside was aft; Alden's journal had said the ledger went forward, into the strongroom below the original chart house, and the journal's map — drawn from memory, in the dark, by a dying man or a desperate one — showed a corridor running beneath the cargo deck.

Kestrel led. He told himself it was because he knew the layout from the pages better than Osha did, but some quieter part of him knew the truth: he wanted to be first when they found it. Whatever Alden had left down here, whatever his brother had chosen in the last hours of his life, Kestrel wanted to meet it face-on, not over someone else's shoulder.

The corridor mouth appeared out of the murk like a throat — a hatchway, dogged open, its wheel still turned. Beyond it the passage sloped down, and the water in it was darker, warmer still, and moving. Not sloshing with their passage. Moving on its own. A current, slow and steady, flowing *up* the corridor from somewhere below.

"Kestrel." Osha had gone very still, her lamp fixed on the water sliding past them into the main hold. "Currents don't run uphill."

"No," Kestrel said. "Springs do."

He thought of the desert then — not the flats they'd crossed, but the deep desert, the rock country where he'd ranged as a young man with his father, before the compass, before everything. There were places out there where the earth breathed water. Vents, the old rangers called them, though his father had used an older word: *breaths*. Fissures where the deep aquifer pressed up through cracked stone, sometimes seeping, sometimes gushing, sometimes — rarely, terribly — blowing. Whole springs had been known to turn in a season, shifting their channels underground like a sleeper rolling over. The water wars had started over three of them.

His father's compass sat in his pocket, and he thought absurdly of its needle, faithful north, and how it would spin uselessly down here in all this iron.

"The aquifer," he said aloud, and heard his own voice change. "It's under us. Right under us. The ship didn't sink onto rocks, Osha. It sank onto a breath."

Osha's lamp swung to him. Even through the mask he could see her eyes go wide, the diver's mind doing what divers' minds did — mapping pressures, volumes, failure points. "Then the hold—"

"Floods twice a day. When the vent runs." His mouth had gone dry despite the water around him. "*The Provision turns.* It wasn't a warning about people. It was a description. The whole wreck turns — drowns and drains, drowns and drains — twice a day, like a lung."

"Then the men who barricaded themselves in the crew compartment—" She stopped. She didn't need to finish it. They had both seen the sealed door, the furniture dragged against it, the scratches. Men who had figured out the rhythm too late, or not quite late enough, sealing themselves against a flood that came from below and would not be argued with.

"And Alden knew," Kestrel said quietly. "He knew the schedule. That's how he wrote the journal. That's why the pages stop where they stop." Because his brother had been counting minutes too.

The water was at their collarbones now.

* * *

They dove the corridor together, lamps forward, kicking down the sloping passage with the warm current pushing against them like a living thing objecting to their presence. Kestrel counted doors as he passed — two sealed cabins, a galley whose copper pots still hung in a row, swaying gently in the current like a wind chime waiting for wind — until the corridor ended at a door unlike the others.

It was steel, not wood. Vault steel, the kind used for paymasters' rooms and munitions locks, and it stood closed but not locked — the handle turned freely when Kestrel tried it, opening inward with a groan of hinges that had waited seven years for the touch.

Beyond it, the strongroom.

Their lamps found it in pieces: a chart table bolted to the floor, its papers long dissolved into pulp; a wall of small drawers, brass fittings green with age; and against the far bulkhead, exactly where Alden's map had promised, a case.

Not a chest. A case — a flat, reinforced box the size of a coffin lid, strapped shut with seals of lead stamped with the fleet's mark, sitting upright in a bracket welded to the wall. Someone had welded that bracket. Someone aboard had made sure the case would not float, would not drift, would not leave. The welds were neat, professional, done by lamplight in a flooding ship.

Kestrel put his hand on the lead seal and stopped.

Through his glove he could feel it — faint, rhythmic, unmistakable. A vibration. Deep below the hull, below the seabed, something vast was pulsing, and the pulse was traveling up through the wreck's bones into the case, into his palm, into his chest, until he couldn't tell whether the beating he felt was the vent or his own heart.

"She's live," Osha said behind him. Her voice had the flat calm she wore when the water was worst, the calm of a woman who had learned that panic spent air faster than work did. "Kestrel. The whole ship is sitting on a live vent. We need to see the straps, confirm the ledger, and get out. In that order. How long has it been since we dropped?"

"Twenty minutes. Maybe twenty-five."

"Then we have—" She didn't finish. Outside, slack water lasted perhaps forty minutes either side of the turn. If the vent ran on the tide's clock, the hold would begin filling in earnest soon, and it would fill from below, faster than any sea could.

Kestrel worked the straps. The lead seals cracked like old bread. Inside the case, packed in tarred wool that had kept the water out across seven years of tides, lay the ledger.

It was smaller than he had imagined. A book, bound in oiled leather, metal-capped at the corners, its pages dense with columns — and even without opening it he knew what the columns were. Depths. Pressures. Channels. The deep aquifer, mapped vein by vein, the kind of knowledge that ended wars or started them, depending on who held the pen. Enough water-rites and coin to buy a city, Sable Vane had said. She'd been thinking too small. This could buy the desert.

He lifted it free of the wool, and the wool shifted, and beneath it — wedged into the corner of the case, placed there deliberately, its cover facing up so that anyone opening the case would see it — lay a second book.

Smaller. Thinner. Familiar.

Kestrel's hands stopped working.

He knew that cover. He had grown up watching that cover sit on his brother's knee by the cookfire while Alden filled it with sketches of birds and lists of stars and, later, in the ranger years, with route notes in a hand so precise the guild had once asked permission to copy it for training. Half of that journal was wrapped against Kestrel's own ribs right now, recovered from the bones in the smuggler's tunnel.

This was the rest of it.

"He brought it down," Osha whispered. "He came back for it. Or—" She shook her head slowly, water dripping from the edge of her mask. "Or he never left. Kestrel, the crew compartment was sealed from the inside. What if he didn't die in the tunnel? What if he got back here?"

Kestrel took the journal out of the case with both hands, the way you lift a sleeping child, and the ledger sat in the wool beside it, heavy and silent and worth more than every debt in Marrowgate.

And then the ship spoke.

* * *

It began as a sound below hearing — not a noise but a pressure, a change in the water's weight, the way the desert changes its mind before a storm. Kestrel felt it in his teeth first, then in the soles of his boots where they gripped the tilted deck. The vibration from the vent, steady as a heartbeat since they'd entered, stuttered.

Quickened.

"Out," Osha said. No flatness left in it now. "Kestrel. *Now.*"

But he was already opening the journal, because the pages were dry — dry, after seven years, sealed in the case with the ledger — and because Alden's last words were right there under his lamp, and because some part of him had crossed six tides of saltflat and drowned canyon precisely for these seconds and would not surrender one of them, even to drowning.

The handwriting was different from the earlier pages. Slower. Written in the dark, probably, by feel, the letters leaning like tired men. The last entry was short.

*Alden, final day. We understand it now, too late to matter. The Provision sits on a breath — the old rangers' word is true, the deep water lives under this whole reach and moves when the moon tells it to. Twice a day the vent opens and the hold floods. Twice a day it drains. We were never wrecked. We were swallowed.*

*I am sealing the chamber. Maddox will say the ledger should arm a fleet. Hesk — if it was Hesk, and I think it was — will say it should be sold. Both are wrong. Water isn't a weapon and it isn't coin. It's the only covenant we have with each other. So I am giving it back to the ground. Let the Provision keep it. Let it turn.*

*If you're reading this, you came looking for me. I'm sorry for that, and proud of it, in the same breath. Don't dig me up. Dig a well.*

The water hit them.

Not a rise — a shove. The current reversed all at once, the warm upflow becoming a torrent, and the strongroom door slammed against its frame hard enough to ring like a bell. Silt exploded upward from the deck, erasing their lamps' reach to arm's length. Through the murk Kestrel saw the ledger slide in its case, saw Osha lunge and pin it, saw the water climbing the walls with a speed no tide had ever managed.

"The chamber floods from below!" Osha's shout was half-swallowed. "The whole hold fills in minutes — we go up the corridor or we don't go!"

Kestrel stuffed the journal inside his coat beside its other half, lashing the oilcloth with numb fingers. The ledger — the ledger sat in its case, in its wool, in its welded bracket, exactly where a dead man had decided it belonged.

For one heartbeat he held it. One heartbeat of arithmetic: Doss's ten days, the license, the debt, the city this book could buy, the wars it could end or feed. His hands actually closed on it. The leather was cool and real and heavier than lead.

Then he felt the vibration through the case again — that vast, patient pulse — and heard his brother's voice underneath it, plain as a man standing at his shoulder: *Let it turn.*

He let go.

"Go!" he shouted, and pushed Osha ahead of him into the corridor.

They fought uphill through water that was no longer water but weather — a churning column of silt and debris, the galley pots tearing loose above them and spinning away into the dark like startled birds. The current sucked at their fins, dragged at the journal against Kestrel's chest as if the ship itself wanted it back. Twice Kestrel lost the corridor wall and found it again only because Osha's lamp beam swept past and he threw himself toward the light.

The main hold, when they reached it, was unrecognizable. The water had climbed fifteen feet in the time it took them to cross a hundred. Their exit line — the guide rope they'd rigged to the breach in the hull — hung slack and swinging, its upper end no longer reachable from inside, the breach itself now a pale glow far overhead, narrowing as the ship rolled by fractions around them. The whole wreck was shifting on its perch, settling deeper onto the breath beneath it, a lung expanding.

Osha grabbed the line and looked at him, and even now, even here, she pointed — not up, but at his coat, at the shape of the journal under the oilcloth, and nodded once. *Worth it,* the nod said. *Now let's not die for it.*

They climbed. Hand over hand up the guide rope through water that pressed harder with every fathom, the light from the breach shrinking to a coin, a fingernail, a spark — and then, as Kestrel's lungs began their final argument, the spark widened, and cold sea air touched his face, and they broke the surface inside the air pocket at the top of the hull, gasping, coughing, alive.

Below them, through the breach, they could hear it: the sound of the *Provision* filling, deep and resonant, a ship-sized chest drawing the largest breath of its long death.

Osha spat water and laughed — the ragged, disbelieving laugh of the nearly-drowned. "Twice a day," she panted. "Seven years. And we picked the one window."

Kestrel didn't answer. He was looking back down through the breach, at the green-dark water closing over the strongroom, over the case, over the ledger and its welded bracket and the patient pulse beneath it all. Somewhere down there, in the dark, his brother's choice was holding — sealed, turning, kept.

*Dig a well.*

Then the light changed.

Not the vent. Not the tide. Daylight — hard, angled, wrong — sweeping across the Shallows from the east, and with it a sound carrying flat and clear over the water: the grind of hulls grounding, the bark of orders, many voices.

Corvane's fleet had arrived at the wreck.

And on the nearest deck, glass raised against the sun, a figure was watching the air pocket where they floated — watching, Kestrel understood with a cold that had nothing to do with the sea, the exact spot where two heads had just broken the surface of a ship everyone believed empty.

Maddox had seen them come up.

And he had seen that they came up empty-handed.
