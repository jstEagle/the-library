## 23. Sixth Tide Begins

The tide had turned against them before the Corvane sails ever broke the horizon.

Kestrel felt it first in the water — a change in the pressure beneath the Shallows, a deep, arterial pulse that made the flooded wreck below hum like a struck bell. Osha surfaced beside him a moment later, treading the green-grey swell, and he saw the answer written on her face before she spoke.

"They're here," she said. "Three hulls. Coming around the Silt Gate at speed."

He looked up. The sky over the Reach was the color of old pewter, and against it, three dark shapes were sliding down the last channel of deep water toward the Shallows — Corvane's fleet, running with every scrap of canvas they could bear, their hulls cutting white scars through the flat sea. At this distance they were almost beautiful. Almost silent. But Kestrel had spent seven years learning to read distance like a language, and what it said was: *twenty minutes.*

"Get everyone out of the water," he said. "Now."

Osha didn't argue. She went under once, twice, signaling to Brammel and the twins on the wreck's exposed ribs, and Kestrel swam for the mast himself — the great broken spar that leaned out of the sea at an angle like a man leaning on a wall to catch his breath. He hauled himself up its barnacled length hand over hand, salt stinging the raw skin of his palms, until he stood on the tilting deck where Vane was already shouting orders.

They had been diving since dawn. Six hours in the green dark, hauling themselves through corridors of drowned timber and silt, following Alden's journal page by page deeper into the hold. They had found the crew compartment sealed from the inside — Kestrel still carried the weight of that discovery in his chest like swallowed stone — and they had found the ledger chamber beyond it, its iron case bolted to the bulkhead as if the ship itself had been built around it. They had not yet opened it. The vent's rhythm had driven them up before they could; the water in the hold had begun to rise with a suddenness that felt less like tide and more like intention.

And now Corvane had arrived to finish what the sea had started.

"Vane." He crossed the listing deck to where she stood at the rail, spyglass raised, her knuckles white around the brass. "How many?"

"Two longboats minimum per hull. Armed." She lowered the glass. Her face was pale under the salt and sunburn, but her voice was level, and Kestrel gave her credit for that. Whatever else Sable Vane was — liar, betrayer, the woman who had buried his brother's last message to protect her own name — she did not break in front of her crew. "Maddox isn't coming to talk."

"He came all this way to talk."

"He came all this way to *take*." She turned to face him, and for one unguarded moment Kestrel saw something in her eyes he had never seen there before: fear. Not for herself. For all of them. "Kestrel. If Maddox gets that ledger—"

"He doesn't get it."

"You don't understand what's in it. I've read enough of Alden's pages to know. Aquifer charts, flow rates, the locations of every deep vein between here and the Glass Reach. With that, Corvane could end the water wars in a season — by winning them. Every town, every well, every ranger patrol from Marrowgate to the coast would answer to whoever held those pages." She gripped his arm, hard. "You'd be handing your brother's killer a kingdom."

"Alden wasn't killed by Maddox."

"No?" Her laugh was short and bitter. "Then who sealed him in that hold?"

Kestrel had no answer. That was the worst of it. Somewhere in the drowned dark below them lay the proof of what his brother had chosen, and he would not know what that choice had been until he opened the case — or until someone opened it for him.

The first longboat grounded on the sandbar thirty minutes later.

* * *

They came ashore in disciplined silence, twelve men in Corvane grey, rifles slung across their backs, moving across the exposed flats with the unhurried confidence of people who owned everything they walked on. Rell Maddox came last, stepping from the boat into ankle-deep water without waiting for help, and even at two hundred paces Kestrel recognized the set of his shoulders, the deliberate economy of his movement. Seven years hadn't changed him. Seven years ago, Maddox had been the only man at Alden's farewell dinner who drank to his brother's success instead of his brother's luck.

Now he was walking toward Kestrel with a rifleman on either side and the sixth tide rising behind him.

"Form a line at the mast," Vane said quietly. "Nobody fires unless I say."

"And if they fire first?" Hesk asked. His voice had a new smoothness to it today, Kestrel noticed. A rehearsed calm. Since the Silt Gate skirmish, the quartermaster had been watching Kestrel with the patient attention of a man counting down to something.

"Then we die on a wreck in the middle of nowhere," Vane said, "and nobody ever finds our bones. So let's not."

Kestrel took his place at the rail. Below and behind him, the sea was doing what the sea always did here — pulling back, gathering itself, preparing to turn. The wreck groaned beneath them, a long wooden complaint that ran up through the soles of his boots. He thought of Alden's words, ink-blurred and desperate: *the Provision turns.* He had understood it in the chamber, finally, with the water climbing his thighs — the wreck sat over a living vent, and twice a day the aquifer breathed out, flooding the hold in minutes. The next turning was coming. He could feel it in the deck's tremor, faint as a sleeping animal's heartbeat.

Which meant whatever happened in the next twenty minutes had to happen fast.

Maddox stopped ten paces from the rail. Up close, the years had been kinder to him than to most men — silver at the temples now, a new scar splitting one eyebrow, but the same steady grey eyes that had once toasted his brother across a table in Marrowgate. He looked at Kestrel for a long moment, and something almost like grief moved behind the steadiness.

"Kestrel Thornbury," he said. "I hoped it would be you."

"Wish I could say the same."

"No, you don't." Maddox smiled faintly. "You never could lie well. Alden used to say you blushed when you tried." He stepped closer, boots crunching on the shell-encrusted planking, and his voice dropped to something meant only for Kestrel. "I'm not here for a fight, ranger. I'm here for what's owed. My crew found that wreck six years ago — my divers, my charts, my coin. Everything below this deck belongs to Corvane by right of blood and money both. All I want is the ledger."

"The ledger's in an iron case bolted to a bulkhead forty feet underwater," Kestrel said. "In a hold that floods twice a day. You're welcome to it."

"I have divers too."

"Not like ours."

"No," Maddox agreed pleasantly. "Not like yours. Which is why I'd rather trade than drown mine." He straightened, and his voice lifted to carry across the whole deck, warm and reasonable and utterly without mercy. "Captain Vane! Your crew is tired, under-provisioned, and standing on a grave that's about to fill with water. I'm offering employment. Full shares, Corvane contract, safe passage home. All I require is that your diver and your ranger open the case and hand me what's inside."

Silence, except for the sea. Kestrel watched his crewmates' faces — Brammel's stubborn scowl, the twins trading a glance, Osha's jaw set hard, Hesk's careful blankness. Maddox had done his arithmetic. Everyone aboard knew the numbers: one wreck, one vent, one enemy fleet, and a hold full of rising water. Refusal was arithmetic too.

"We haven't opened the case," Vane said.

"Then open it."

"It may be sealed. It may be empty. It may be trapped — Alden Thornbury wasn't a careless man."

Something flickered across Maddox's face at the name. "No," he said softly. "He wasn't." And then, louder, with terrible patience: "Captain. I will make this simple. Open the case and give me the ledger, or my riflemen will take your crew one at a time and question them on the sandbar, where the tide can watch. I have until the sixth tide completes. You have less."

Vane's hand moved — a small motion, half-gesture, half-spasm — and Kestrel caught it. *Wait,* it said. Or maybe *forgive me.* With Sable Vane it was impossible to know which.

"Give us an hour," she said.

"Half of one."

She turned to her crew, and the negotiation began — low, urgent, conducted in the shadow of the mast while Maddox's men spread along the rail with the loose discipline of wolves who had already eaten. Kestrel heard Hesk's voice first, silk-smooth: *captain, we can't dive again, the vent—* and then Osha, sharp: *we go down together or not at all—* and underneath it all, the wreck groaning its slow complaint, and beneath even that, faint but unmistakable now, the pulse of the vent quickening like a heart before a race.

Kestrel slipped away from the argument. Nobody stopped him; everybody was talking. He moved along the rail to the shadowed side of the deckhouse, and there he did the thing he had been avoiding since the Silt Gate: he took out Alden's journal.

Half of it. The rest lay forty feet down, wedged in a bulkhead of the flooding hold, waiting. These pages he had carried against his chest since the smuggler's tunnel, wrapped in oilcloth, and he unwrapped them now with hands that were steadier than he expected. His brother's handwriting marched across them — cramped, slanting, increasingly hurried as the pages turned. Charts. Depth soundings. And warnings, over and over, in words that had become litany:

*The Provision turns. Trust no one who counts.*

He had thought he understood. Standing in the ledger chamber with the water at his waist, he had thought the words meant the vent, the flood, the mechanical truth of the wreck. But reading them now, with Maddox's riflemen lining the rail and Hesk's silence stretching thin as wire, another meaning surfaced — cold, obvious, unbearable.

*Alden hadn't been writing about the sea.*

He had been writing about the crew. About someone aboard who counted — counted shares, counted coin, counted the value of a man's life against the price of his silence. Someone who had sold the expedition once already. Someone who was aboard again now, wearing a quartermaster's badge and reporting to a creditor in Marrowgate.

Kestrel looked up. Across the deck, Hesk stood at the rail speaking quietly with one of Maddox's riflemen — not arguing, not negotiating. *Confirming.* The rifleman nodded once and moved away, and Hesk turned back to the crew's huddle wearing his smooth, reasonable face, and began, gently, persuasibly, to argue that opening the case was the only sane choice.

The journal pages went inside Kestrel's coat. He folded them small, pressed them flat against his chest beneath his shirt, and buttoned his jacket over them, and the oilcloth crackled once, softly, like a held breath released. Then he rolled his shoulders, put on the face of a man beaten and bargaining, and walked back into the light.

* * *

The negotiation lasted eleven minutes, and Kestrel remembered almost none of it clearly afterward — only fragments, the way drowning men remember weather.

He remembered Vane's voice, stripped of all its old silk, saying: *Rell, I'll give you the coordinates of the case itself. I'll draw you the route through the hold. But my crew walks off this wreck free, or I burn the charts in front of you.*

He remembered Maddox answering, gentle as a surgeon: *Sable. We've known each other fifteen years. Don't insult us both.*

He remembered Hesk stepping forward, hands raised, saying the word *compromise* as if it were a lifeboat, and the way Osha looked at him — the way she had been looking at him since the tunnel, since the flares, since the beginning — and how Hesk met her gaze without flinching, which was its own kind of confession.

Mostly, though, he remembered the water.

Because while they talked, the sea was making its own argument. The tide had begun its turn — he could see it now in the whirlpools forming off the wreck's northern quarter, slow brown spirals sucking downward, and hear it in the vent's pulse, which had climbed from a tremor to a thrum that shivered the deck planks and rattled the loose rigging. The crew felt it. Maddox's men felt it. Even the riflemen had begun glancing at the water with the particular unease of soldiers whose war had suddenly acquired a third army.

"The hold floods in minutes when it turns," Kestrel said, and his voice came out rougher than he intended. "Your divers go down now, they might reach the case. They won't come back up. I've seen it. Ask me how I know."

Maddox studied him. "You've been down."

"I've been down."

"And your brother?"

The question landed like a blade laid flat against skin — not cutting yet, just resting there, waiting. Around them the whole standoff went quiet. Vane had stopped mid-sentence. Osha had gone very still at the rail. Even the sea seemed to lean in.

"My brother," Kestrel said carefully, "sealed the compartment from the inside."

It was the first time he had said it aloud. Saying it changed nothing about the facts and everything about their weight; he felt the sentence pass through the assembled company like current through water. Maddox's face did something complicated — grief and fury fighting for the same ground — and for a moment the captain of the Corvane fleet looked less like a conqueror than a man standing at a graveside trying to remember what he'd come to bury.

"He wouldn't do that," Maddox said finally. "Not unless—"

"Unless what? Say it."

"Unless he was keeping something from someone." Maddox's eyes swept the deck — Vane, Hesk, the riflemen, back to Kestrel — and when they returned they had hardened into something Kestrel recognized from mirrors and bad years: the look of a man deciding that sentiment was a luxury. "Enough. Here is what happens now. My divers go down at the next slack. Your diver guides them — willingly or otherwise. If the case opens, everyone shares. If it doesn't—" he shrugged, and the shrug contained executions, "—then the tide does my work for me, and I'll salvage what floats."

"And if we refuse?" Vane asked.

"Then I take your crew off this hull and hold them on my flagship until you change your mind. The sea will keep. It always keeps."

The vent pulsed. Harder now. A shudder ran the length of the wreck, and somewhere below, deep in the flooded dark, something vast shifted its weight. Kestrel felt the journal pages against his chest, warm from his body, and understood with sudden clarity that the choice had already been made — had been made seven years ago by a man sealing a door in the dark, and made again in a smuggler's tunnel, and made again this morning when Kestrel buttoned his coat over his brother's last words.

All that remained was the timing.

"Slack water's in twenty minutes," Osha said quietly, at his shoulder. Her eyes were on Maddox, but her voice was for Kestrel alone. "Whatever you're going to do — do it before then."

"How do you know I'm going to do anything?"

"Because you've got your father's compass-face on." She almost smiled. "The one you wear when north is the last thing anyone expects."

Below them, the first whirlpool spun wide and black, and the Provision began, slowly, to turn.
