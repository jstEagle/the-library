## 24. Counting Down

Rell Maddox had counted men's last breaths before. He had stood at the rail of the *Corvane's Pride* and watched a mutineer go over the side, had knelt beside a dying scout in the Glass Reach and held his hand until the counting stopped. A man's final hour had a sound to it, Maddox thought — a thinning, like wind through a cracked reed — and he was hearing it now in the groan of the Provision's hull beneath his boots.

The wreck lay half-sunk at the Shallows, her black mast leaning against a sky gone the color of old pewter. The sixth tide was coming. Everyone on that hull could feel it in their teeth.

Maddox stood at the center of it all with a pistol in each hand and eleven hostages kneeling on the barnacled deck planking, and he felt nothing so much as impatience. Not cruelty — he had never been a cruel man, whatever the stories said — but the specific irritation of a man watching sand run through a pinched hourglass while fools argued about the glass itself.

Vane was talking again. She was always talking. That had been true seven years ago and it was true now, her voice carrying that dry Marrowgate polish across the wet deck like a blade laid flat on a table. *We can split it. Corvane takes the ledger's contents, my crew takes salvage rights, everyone walks away rich.* As if the aquifer were a cargo hold to be divided by weight. As if water could be owned the way coin was owned.

She didn't understand. None of them understood, except perhaps the Thornbury brother standing at the edge of the kneeling line with his jaw set like a man chewing on gravel.

Maddox shifted his weight and the hull answered him — a deep, structural complaint from somewhere below the waterline, the sound of a dead ship remembering she had once been alive. He glanced down through a broken hatch at the green-black water churning in the flooded hold, and for one unsteady moment he saw not water but a face. Alden Thornbury's face, younger than Maddox wanted to remember, grinning through a dust storm outside Marrowgate with a canteen held up like a trophy.

*To the man who finds what's worth finding,* Alden had said, and drunk.

That had been nine years ago. Maddox had been twenty-six and hungry in the way that made young men dangerous, running survey contracts out of Corvane's eastern yards, and Alden Thornbury had been the only ranger on the border who'd ever shared his fire without asking what it cost. They had mapped three seasons together before the Provision contract came down and Alden signed on with an expedition crew and walked out of Maddox's life with a wave and a promise: *When I come back, I'll have found something worth more than salt.*

He never came back. And for nine years Rell Maddox had built a fleet and a reputation and a slow-burning hatred of the word *vanished*, because vanished meant unanswered, and unanswered meant unfinished, and unfinished business was the only kind of debt Maddox had ever refused to carry.

Now here was the answer, rising with the tide.

"Captain." Hesk, the fat quartermaster, was looking at him sideways from where Vane's crew knelt, his small eyes doing arithmetic. "The tide—"

"I know about the tide."

"The ledger chamber floods when the vent opens. If we're standing on this hull when—"

"*I know about the tide.*" Maddox said it quietly, which was worse than shouting, and Hesk shut his mouth with a click of teeth.

Kestrel Thornbury lifted his head. He'd been silent since the standoff began — since Maddox's divers had come up the wreck's flank and taken the crew at gunpoint while they were still dripping from the hold — but silence had always been the Thornbury way. The older brother had talked enough for both of them.

"You knew him," Kestrel said. It wasn't a question. He'd seen it, then — whatever had crossed Maddox's face when they'd dragged him up from below. Brothers recognized brothers' ghosts.

"I knew Alden," Maddox said.

The name landed on the deck like a dropped anchor. Vane stopped talking. Osha, the diver, went very still, seawater still sheeting off her shoulders. Even the twins Pell-and-Pim's surviving brother quit his quiet sobbing into the salt-crusted collar of his coat.

"Three seasons of survey work, east of the Reach," Maddox went on, and heard his own voice go strange and far away. "He pulled me out of a sinkhole once. My leg was broke and he carried me four miles on a litter made of wagon canvas and regret. Never let me pay him back. Said debts between friends were just another way of keeping score, and he wasn't interested in scoring." Maddox laughed, short and ugly. "First honest man I ever met who meant it."

"He went down with the Provision," Kestrel said. "You've known that for how long?"

"Known it? No." Maddox turned, boots grinding on shell and weed, and looked the ranger full in the face. "Suspected it. Feared it. Chased the rumor of it across six years and two fleets. I bought coordinates from smugglers and burned captains who sold me false ones. I spent everything Corvane gave me and most of what they didn't." He gestured with the pistol toward the drowned hold, the green dark below, the thing they had all come across six tides of hell to find. "And now I'm standing on his grave, and the only thing left of him is a sealed case full of paper that tells the whole border where the water lives."

"Then you understand," Kestrel said, rising slowly to his feet — Maddox let him; there was no threat in the man, only gravity — "what that paper costs."

"I understand what it's *worth.*"

"It's not the same thing."

"No," Maddox agreed. "It never is."

* * *

The argument, when it finally came, did not begin with shouting. It began with the water.

Osha felt it first — she would always feel it first, that was her gift and her curse — a change in the pressure under the hull, a shudder in the green dark below like something enormous turning over in its sleep. She was kneeling ten feet from Maddox's second-in-command and she let her eyes flick downward, just once, and Maddox caught it.

He caught everything. That was why Corvane had given him a fleet.

"The vent," Osha said. Her voice was rough from the dive, from the drowning-cold of the hold, from the hostage-taking. "It's early. Or we're late. Either way, captain, you're standing on a kettle with the lid rattling."

"How long?"

"Minutes. Maybe less. When it goes, this whole section floods to the mast step. You've got divers of your own — you know what a vent surge does to a man in a sealed space."

Maddox did know. He'd lost two men to a vent surge off the Silt Gate three years back, good divers, and he remembered the sound their gear made when it came up empty.

So he made the calculation every commander makes, and he hated the answer, and he gave the order anyway. "Fall back to the wagons on the shoal line. Keep the hostages. We talk on high ground."

They retreated across the wreck's spine as the water below began, very softly, to sing — a low harmonic moan through the ship's bones, the sound of pressure finding its voice. By the time they reached the shoal, a bare spine of packed silt and dead coral fifty yards off the wreck's flank, the Provision's deck was already taking water in sheets, green tongues licking up through hatches and seams.

And there, knee-deep in the rising shallows with guns on eleven exhausted people, Rell Maddox finally said what he had crossed the border to say.

"That ledger names every aquifer vein under the Saltreach. Every depth, every flow rate, every recharge point. Enough water to end the wars — or to win them." His voice carried flat and clear across the water, the voice of a man who had rehearsed this speech in the dark for years without knowing it. "Corvane wants it. Doss wants it. Half the creditors in Marrowgate want it. And every one of them wants to sell it in pieces, lease by lease, well by well, until the desert owns the people drinking from it instead of the other way around." He shook his head. "Not me. I say we take it whole. Fund an expedition — no, fund an *army*. Seize the vents, hold the recharge points, and declare the deep water free to anyone who swears to defend it. One war, short and clean, and after it, no more water lords. No more Dosses. No more children dying in the flats because some counting house put a price on a cup."

"You'd start a war to end thirst," Vane said.

"I'd start a war to end *this.*" He swept the pistol toward the kneeling crew, toward the wreck, toward the whole horizon of salt and debt. "Every year we wait, someone else files a claim. You think the ledger can stay secret? Hesk already copied half the cipher — don't look surprised, quartermaster, I read your flares. The secret is already dead. All that's left is deciding who holds the water when it comes up. I'm offering to hold it for *everyone.*"

"And who guards the guardian?" Kestrel asked.

Maddox turned to him. Of everyone on that shoal, only the Thornbury brother hadn't flinched, hadn't bargained, hadn't begged. He stood in the shallows with the water climbing his thighs and looked at Maddox the way Alden used to look at bad survey data — patiently, sadly, waiting for the man to see the error himself.

"My brother wrote something," Kestrel said. "In a journal I pulled out of a tunnel full of his crew's bones. He said the Provision carried a ledger of the deep aquifer, and he said one of their own sold them out for it. Nine people died on that ship, Maddox. Not from the wreck. From the *wanting*. And the man who betrayed them believed exactly what you believe — that the water was too important to leave to chance, that he was the right man to hold it." Kestrel's voice didn't rise. It dropped, quiet as the tide. "Alden chose to seal that chamber rather than let the ledger be taken. He died behind a barricade he built with his own hands, in the dark, writing by feel, and the last thing he trusted the world with was a warning: *the Provision turns, trust no one who counts.* You've been counting your whole life, Captain. Fleet tonnage, bounty shares, bodies. What makes you different from the traitor in his own crew?"

For a long moment there was only the water, and its singing.

"Because I loved him," Maddox said finally, and was surprised to find it was true. "Because I've buried more men than I can number and I am *tired*, Thornbury. Tired of ruling the desert by rationing it. Someone has to take the water out of the hands of the counting houses, and if it isn't a soldier, it'll be a merchant, and if it isn't a merchant it'll be a king, and none of them will love anything but the ledger itself."

"Alden didn't trust the ledger either," Kestrel said. "He trusted the *water*. There's a difference. The water doesn't need defending, Maddox. It needs *finding*. Every ranger on the border could be drilling wells off that map inside five years — no army, no claims, no throne. Just water, everywhere, for whoever digs."

"And the wars in between?" Maddox spat. "You think the Dosses of the world will stand aside while peasants dig free wells? They'll burn them. They'll salt the ground. Freedom needs a wall around it or it's just a target."

"Then build the wall out of rangers, not soldiers. Out of people who—" 

The hull spoke.

It was not a groan this time. It was a *crack* — a report like a cannon fired underwater, rolling up through the shoal and into their bones, and every head turned toward the Provision. Along the wreck's waterline, seams were weeping in silver ropes. The singing below had risen to a whine. And out on the green surface of the flooded hold, directly above the sealed chamber, the water had begun to turn — slowly, gently, a vast rotation like milk stirred in tea, catching the grey light and giving back something else.

Something green. Something lit from beneath.

Osha was on her feet, pointing, her voice cracking with awe and terror in equal measure. "The vent. The vent's opening — that glow is the aquifer light, I've seen it in the deep caves, that's *living water*—"

Maddox raised his pistols. "Nobody moves toward that wreck."

"Captain—" his second started.

"NOBODY MOVES." But even as he shouted it, Maddox was doing his own arithmetic, fast and cold, and the numbers were terrible. The chamber would flood within minutes. Whatever was in that sealed case would either drown in place forever — or be taken now, in the next few minutes, by whoever reached it first. His entire fleet, nine years, one dead friend's grave, all of it narrowing to a gap of minutes and forty yards of rising water.

And Kestrel Thornbury was already moving.

Not toward the wreck — toward *him*. Straight at Maddox through the waist-deep shallows, hands open, unarmed, walking with the unhurried certainty of a man who had already decided how this ended. Behind him, Vane surged up shouting, and Osha grabbed for the ranger's coat, and Maddox's divers leveled their pieces, and none of it mattered, because the water was rising and the wreck was turning and somewhere below it all, in a sealed room built by a dead man's hands, the last of Alden Thornbury waited in the green dark.

"Give me the journal pages," Kestrel said, close enough now that Maddox could see the salt dried white in his beard, the exhaustion carved under his eyes. "Or shoot me. Those are your choices, Captain. Because I am going into that hold, and I am going to sit with my brother's choice, and I am going to honor it — and no fleet on this border is going to stop me."

The water sang. The wreck turned. And Rell Maddox, who had counted everything his whole life, felt the count finally slip his grasp —

— as the vent opened early, and the sea came up to claim them all.
