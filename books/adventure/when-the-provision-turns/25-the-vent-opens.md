## 25. The Vent Opens

Osha felt the water change before she heard it.

She was forty feet down the drowned hold's main corridor, running her palm along a bulkhead rib furred with white bacterial mat, when the temperature shifted. Not the slow cooling of depth — something else. A pulse. Warm water moving against warm water, like a heartbeat under the skin of the sea.

She had dived the flooded canyons for eleven years. She knew what living water felt like. This was living water, and it was waking up.

Above her, through the green murk, Kestrel's lamp swung in the sealed crew compartment, its beam cutting thin gold blades through the silt they'd disturbed. He had found something back there — she'd seen his silhouette go rigid against the light, shoulders locking the way a man's do when grief arrives wearing an old face. She'd been about to swim to him when the pulse came again, stronger, and this time it carried sound with it: a low groan that traveled through the hull and into her bones, up through her chest, into her teeth.

The Provision was groaning.

* * *

Everything Osha knew about wrecks she had learned from her mother, who had learned it from the salvage divers of the old coast, before the wars took the coast away. Her mother's first rule had never left her: *the sea does not hate you, girl. It simply doesn't know you're there.* The second rule came harder-won: *but a wreck knows. A wreck remembers every weight it ever carried, and when it decides to shift that weight, you want to be outside the hull, not inside it.*

The third rule she was learning now, in real time, with her lungs full of air from a tank Brammel had patched twice on the crossing: *when the water tells you to leave, leave.*

The pulse became a current. It came from below — from somewhere beneath the keel, beneath even the mud the Provision had settled into two hundred years ago — and it moved through the hold like breath drawn through a throat. The bacterial mat rippled off the bulkheads in pale streamers. Silt that had lain undisturbed since Alden Thornbury's era lifted in slow brown ghosts. And the temperature kept climbing, degree by degree, until Osha's bare forearms felt bathwater-warm in water that should have been cold as a well at midnight.

She kicked for the corridor junction where Kestrel had gone. The green dark swallowed her lamplight; she could see maybe eight feet now instead of twenty. Somewhere behind her, Pell's rope — no. Not Pell. Pell was dead, buried at the sinkhole's edge under a cairn of salt stone. That was Vane's line now, the safety line, anchored to the hull breach above. Vane herself was topside, holding the Corvane captain's attention with words while Kestrel and Osha did the work words couldn't do. Maddox's divers were in the water too — she'd counted their fins against the surface light, four shadows circling the mast like sharks that had learned patience.

The groan came again, longer this time, and the hull answered it with a sound Osha had only heard once before, in a flooded mine shaft near Glass Reach: metal singing under stress, a high thin note that meant the structure was past arguing with.

*Kestrel.* She found him at the mouth of the crew compartment, turned toward her, lamp raised. Even through the murk she could read his face: he'd found what he came for. His hand pressed flat against his coat, over his chest, where the journal pages rode. He pointed up — *go* — and she shook her head and grabbed his wrist and pointed up harder. They were leaving together or not at all, and she told him so with a look that had ended arguments with men twice her size.

He almost smiled. Then the vent opened.

* * *

It did not erupt the way the books described eruptions — no single explosion, no wall of force. It opened the way a wound opens: suddenly, and then all at once.

Beneath them, below the keel, below the mud, the aquifer vent — the thing Alden's journal had called the turning heart, the reason the hold flooded twice daily on the tide's rhythm, the reason the Provision *turned* — decided it was done waiting for the tide. Later, on the surface, Brammel would swear the pressure gauge on his pump rig had spiked a full hour ahead of any tidal table. Later still, Kestrel would say quietly that Alden had known it might, that the last pages of the journal were written fast, written scared, written by a man watching the schedule slip. But in the moment there was no later. There was only the sound.

It was not loud. That was the horror of it. It was a deep subsonic push, felt rather than heard, and the entire wreck lurched upward six inches and settled with a grinding shudder that Osha felt in her fillings. Mud clouds erupted from below in boiling columns. The warm current became a river, then a geyser's preamble, and through the floor grating of the hold — a grating that had been rusted solid for two centuries — water began to rise. Not seep. *Rise.* Fast. Visible. A column of silver-flecked water climbing the interior of the hull like mercury in a thermometer.

Kestrel's hand closed on her arm. Through the roar of bubbles and shifting ballast, he shouted — she read it more than heard it — *the tide table lied.*

They swam.

* * *

The corridor they'd entered through was already wrong. On the way down it had been a generous passage, ten feet of headroom, silted but navigable. Now the rising water had compressed their world: the air pocket that had filled the upper hold was being eaten from below, and the ceiling gap between waterline and deck plating narrowed with every stroke. Osha's shoulder scraped a stanchion. Her tank caught on a dangling cargo strap and she tore free with a lurch that spun her half around, and for one bad second her lamp showed her nothing but brown churn and her own hands.

Then Kestrel's hand found her ankle and steadied her, and his lamp found the breach — the jagged hull tear they'd entered by, glowing faintly with surface light — and they went for it together, two divers swimming uphill through a ship that was filling faster than any tide had a right to fill it.

Behind them, the grating let go.

She heard it happen — a shriek of corroded iron, then a boom she felt in her chest — and the column of vent-water came up through the hole like something released from a long imprisonment. It wasn't clear water. It was mineral-heavy, silver-bright, hot enough that she felt it as a distinct layer against her legs where it pooled and climbed, and it carried the smell of the deep earth even through her regulator's seal: sulfur and iron and something older, something that tasted the way the saltflats looked at noon, endless and indifferent.

Nature sides with no one. Her mother had said that too, once, after a dive that killed two friends. *The sea doesn't take sides, girl. That's the mercy of it and the cruelty both.*

The Provision didn't care whose brother had died in it. It didn't care about debts, or ledgers, or the war Maddox wanted to fund with the water beneath it. It was a hull with a wound, and the wound was opening, and everything inside it — treasure, truth, traitors — would be treated exactly alike.

* * *

They broke the surface inside the air pocket beneath the main deck, gasping, and for three seconds Osha just hung in the black water and breathed, letting her lamp swing across the space.

The pocket was shrinking. She could see it — the waterline crawling up riveted plating that had been dry minutes ago, the air above it thick with spray and the smell of hot stone. Somewhere aft, the groaning continued, rhythmic now, like labor pains. The whole wreck flexed with each pulse.

"Kestrel." Her voice came out cracked. "The breach. We go now."

He was already moving — but not toward the breach. Toward the ladder well, where a shape hung tangled in line, thrashing.

Hesk.

The quartermaster had gone into the water after them — of course he had; Osha had watched him fake a regulator fault at the entry point and slip in on the second wave, and she'd said nothing because Vane had ordered silence and because part of her had wanted to see what Hesk would do unsupervised. What he'd done was follow them down, alone, on a borrowed rig, hunting the ledger chamber while everyone else played hostage politics on the hull. And now the rising water had caught him. His guide line had fouled in a collapsed shelf bracket, and he was wrapped in it, and his lamp had gone out, and he was making sounds through his regulator that needed no translation.

Kestrel hung in the water between the breach and the drowning man. Osha saw the arithmetic run behind his eyes — she'd spent enough weeks beside him on the convoy to watch him think, and he always thought in costs, in weights, in what a choice would buy and what it would burn. Ten seconds to the breach. Twenty to Hesk. The water rising all the while.

He went to Hesk.

Of course he did. She cursed him with real feeling, all the words her mother would have washed her mouth out for, and followed.

* * *

Cutting Hesk free took eleven seconds, and Osha counted every one of them, because counting was how she kept fear in its cage. Kestrel held the man's harness steady while she sawed through the fouled line with her dive knife, the blade skipping off a brass fitting once, twice, biting on the third pass. Hesk fought her the whole time — grabbing at her mask, at her hose, blind panic with hands — and she drove her knee into his chest hard enough to knock the wind from what little air he had left, and shouted through the water, muffled and furious: *"Hold still or drown, you idiot, those are your options!"*

He held still.

They got him to the breach and shoved him through it into open water, and the moment his fins cleared the hull Osha felt the difference — the sea outside was cold and clean and moving in long honest swells, nothing like the hot churning trap behind them. She and Kestrel came out after, kicking hard for the surface, and the last thing her lamp showed her of the Provision's interior was the air pocket collapsing, the water sealing shut over the place where the ledger chamber lay locked in its case, where Alden Thornbury had made his choice in the dark, where the true map of the deep aquifer waited under two hundred feet of green water for whoever was fool enough to come back for it.

Then they surfaced, and the world above was worse.

* * *

The Shallows had turned into a battlefield with no army in it.

The Provision's broken mast still broke the surface at low tide, but low tide was ending, and the vent had changed the schedule of everything. Around the wreck site the sea steamed — actually steamed, wisps of vapor rising off patches of silver-hot water as if the flats themselves had a fever. The convoy wagons sat beached on the shoal spit two hundred yards east, and the Corvane skiffs ringed the wreck closer in, and between them, on the hull itself, the hostages: Vane's people, roped at the wrists to the old gun mounts, Maddox's divers standing guard among them with spearguns. Osha counted heads as she swam — Brammel, Pim, two wagon-hands, Vane herself, kneeling at the highest point of the exposed hull with her hands free, which meant she'd been talking, which meant the negotiation was either going very well or very badly.

Maddox stood facing her, and even at distance Osha could see the set of his shoulders had changed. He was looking past Vane. Past everyone. He was looking at the water they'd just come out of, at the steam, at the silver bloom spreading across the surface like ink dropped in milk.

And Hesk was already there.

The quartermaster had reached the Corvane skiff nearest the hull — Osha watched him haul himself over the gunwale with the desperate strength of a man who has just understood how close he came to dying for nothing — and he was talking fast, gesturing down at the water, at the wreck, at the ledger. Even without hearing him, Osha could read the shape of it. *I know where it is. I've seen the chamber. I can get you the cipher, the case, the key — I'm worth more wet than dead.*

Maddox listened. Then he looked at the steam rising off the sea, and whatever Hesk was selling, the price had just gone up beyond anyone's purse.

Because the wreck groaned again — louder now, audible across the open water, a sound like a cathedral door swinging on rusted hinges — and the whole hull shifted under the hostages' knees, and Vane grabbed a gun mount to keep her seat, and one of the Corvane guards stumbled and nearly went into the silver water and snatched himself back with a yell. The sea around the mast began to turn. Slowly. A vast lazy spiral, drawing debris inward, drawing the steam into a rotating veil, and beneath the spiral — faint, deep, like a lamp behind stained glass — a green light was gathering.

Osha had seen bioluminescence before. Every diver had. This was not that. This was the vent itself showing through, the deep aquifer's chemistry burning bright in the churn, and it lit the underside of every cloud and the faces of every person in the Shallows with a sickly submarine glow, and it was getting brighter.

"The tide," Brammel shouted from the shoal spit, waving his pressure gauge over his head like a madman. "It's not the tide! The table's wrong — the vent's running early — we have minutes, not hours!"

On the hull, Vane rose to her feet. Osha couldn't hear what she said to Maddox over the groaning of the wreck and the rush of the turning water, but she saw the woman's mouth move with the flat deliberate clarity of someone spending her last coin: whatever terms had been on the table a minute ago were gone, and Vane was telling him so.

And Kestrel, treading water beside Osha, chest heaving, coat heavy with his brother's stolen pages, went very still.

"He'll pull them off the hull," Osha said. "Before it goes. Even Maddox isn't—"

"No." Kestrel's voice was strange. Flat and far away. "Look at Hesk. Look at his hands."

Osha looked. Hesk was pointing — not at the hostages, not at the skiffs. Down. At the water. At the green light. And he was holding up, where only Maddox could see them, a folded scrap of paper, grease-stained and familiar.

The cipher page. Copied. When? In the strongbox? In the hold? It didn't matter. What mattered was that Hesk had stopped selling information and started selling the door itself, and the green light beneath the wreck was brightening with every heartbeat, and the Provision groaned a third time — deeper, longer, final-sounding — and began, slowly, terribly, to turn.
