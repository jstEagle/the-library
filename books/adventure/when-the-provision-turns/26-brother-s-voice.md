## 26. Brother's Voice

The water took the hold in stages, and Kestrel learned its stages by feel.

First it came as a whisper along the deck plates, a cold tongue licking at his boots while he pressed himself flat against the bulkhead where Osha had last seen him. Then a murmur, rising through the seams of the old ship like breath through teeth. Then a voice — not a whisper, not a murmur, but a low, grinding assertion that the *Provision* was waking, that the vent below was opening its throat, and that everything sealed inside her hull would soon belong to the green dark again.

He had maybe minutes. Maybe fewer. The lamplight Osha had left burning on the ladder platform threw a trembling coin of gold across the water, and beyond that circle the hold went black in every direction, a cathedral of drowned iron with him kneeling at its altar.

"Kestrel!" Osha's voice came down the companionway, distorted, bouncing off wet steel. "The tide's turning early — the whole wreck is groaning — you have to come *up*—"

"I found something!" he shouted back, and his own voice sounded foreign to him, cracked and small. "Give me two minutes!"

"You don't *have* two minutes!"

He had one. He knew it the way a man knows the weight of a falling rock — not by calculation but by the animal certainty in his gut. Above him, through three decks of corroded plating, he could hear the muffled argument of voices, Maddox's demands, Vane's cold arithmetic, the creak of the hull as Corvane's divers swarmed the superstructure. Up there, men were deciding who owned the dead. Down here, the dead were deciding something else.

The bulkhead seam had caught his eye because nothing else in the hold made sense. Every crate had been broken open years ago — salvage crews, tide action, time itself had scattered the Provision's cargo like a gutted fish. But this one section of wall, just aft of the crew compartment, was different. Plates bolted from the *inside*, their edges sealed with tar and canvas, and wedged into the gap between two ribs, a shape that might have been driftwood or might have been leather.

Kestrel waded to it, water now at his thighs, and worked the shape free with fingers gone numb from cold.

It was a journal. He knew before he saw the cover. He knew by the way it sat in his hands, by the oilcloth wrapping tied with the particular double knot Alden had used on everything — saddle rolls, ration bundles, the laces of his boots when they shared a tent on the long survey seasons, back when they were boys together under the same stars and neither of them owed anyone anything.

His hands shook so badly he could barely work the knot.

*Not yet,* he told himself. *Read it later. Climb first.*

But the water was rising past his waist, and the lamp above was guttering, and some part of him — the part that had spent seven years telling himself Alden died fast, clean, somewhere out in the salt without pain — understood that if he climbed now, he would never read these pages. Because up there waited Maddox and Hesk and Doss's long arm, and any paper that surfaced into that light would be taken, copied, burned, or used. This was the last private room in the world. His brother had built it. And his brother had built it for someone to find.

Kestrel sat down in the flooding hold of the Provision, in the dark, with the water climbing his chest, and opened his brother's journal by the light of a dying lamp.

* * *

The early pages were familiar. Alden wrote the way he talked: quick, certain, a little too fond of his own jokes. Entries about the convoy out of Marrowgate, about Vane — *she counts everything twice and trusts herself once, which is one more than she should* — about the saltflats crossing and Pell's father, who had been navigator on that run too, a family trade ending badly. Kestrel read fast, skimming, hunting forward through dates, through the Glass Reach, through the Silt Gate, until the handwriting changed.

It changed after the tunnel. After the bones Kestrel had found — no. After the tunnel *before* the bones. The dates confused him for a moment until he understood: what Kestrel had found in the smuggler's passage was the expedition's outward march. What he held now was written inside the Provision herself. Alden had reached the wreck alive. Had lived here. Had *written here*, in the dark, while the tides turned over him twice a day.

*Day 19 inside. The hold floods at high water and drains at low. We've learned her rhythm. Brammel would have loved her — she's an engine, really, breathing on the moon's schedule. We moved everything to the upper compartments. Rell says we can cut the ledger case free and swim it out on the neap tide. I keep saying wait.*

*I keep saying wait because I counted.*

Kestrel's breath stopped. The lamp flame bent sideways in a draft from the vent far below, and the shadows swung across the page like a curtain half-drawn.

*Someone is counting wrong on purpose. The rations. The air. The tide tables. Small errors, always small, always in the direction that keeps us here longer than we need to be. I've checked my figures four times. I am not wrong. One of us wants the Provision to stay found — or wants us to stay lost.*

*Trust no one who counts.*

The words from Vane's strongbox, the words that had sat in Kestrel's chest like a swallowed stone for days, suddenly turned over and showed their other face. Not paranoia. Arithmetic. Alden hadn't been losing his mind in the dark; he'd been doing ranger's math, the same math Kestrel did on every patrol — counting water, counting hours, counting the distance between what people said and what they did. And the count had come out wrong, and the wrongness had a direction, and the direction pointed inward.

The water was at his ribs now. Cold as a verdict. He kept reading.

*Day 23. It was Hesk.*

The name landed like a blade between the ribs. Kestrel actually laughed — one short, ugly bark that echoed off the flooded steel — because of course. Of course it had always been Hesk. Seven years ago and now, the same man, the same trade, selling positions and selling people, and the only thing that had changed was the price.

*He signaled Corvane's scouts from the canyon rim on the ninth night. I saw the flare pattern myself — three-and-one, the old courier code. He told them we'd found the ledger chamber but couldn't crack the case. They came down on us at the fourth tide. Maddox wasn't leading them then — he was a lieutenant, and a decent one, which is worse. Decent men follow bad orders beautifully.*

*We ran for the wreck. Six of us made it inside. Four didn't.*

*Day 25. The case won't open without the cipher key, and Hesk has the cipher key memorized, which means opening the case anywhere in the world is the same as handing it to Corvane. Rell wanted to smash it. I said no. You can't smash what's written inside a man's head.*

*So I've been thinking about what the Provision actually is. She's not a ship. She's a lock. The vent below turns her into a lock twice a day, and locks have one purpose: they decide what passes and what doesn't.*

*Day 26. I've made my choice. I'm sealing the lower compartment with the case inside it. The tar will hold against everything except a determined crew with cutting torches and months of time, and no one will have months, because the moment Corvane finds this wreck they'll also find the vent, and the vent does not negotiate.*

*I'm not doing it to hide the aquifer. I'm doing it so that whoever comes next has to understand it first. A map of the deep water in the hands of a fleet is a war. In the hands of no one, it's a grave. Let them dig for it. Let them learn the tides. Let them sit in the dark and count, the way I counted, and find out whether they're honest.*

*If you're reading this, you're either very good or very desperate, and probably both. I'm sorry about the dark. I'm sorry about the water. I counted it all out carefully — you'll have enough time if you don't waste any being angry at me.*

*Kestrel. If it's you — and I think it's you, because who else would be fool enough to come —*

The handwriting broke here. Not faded — broke, the letters staggering as though the hand holding the pen had been struck, or had stopped to listen to something in the dark. When it resumed, the characters were larger, scratched, the pen pressed hard enough to tear the paper in two places.

*— I want you to know I wasn't afraid. That's a lie. I was afraid the whole time. But I want you to know the fear didn't win. I sat in this hold with the water coming in and I thought about the well at Ash Hollow, and how you taught me to find water by watching which way the birds fly at dusk, and I thought: the water was never the enemy. The water was always just trying to get where it was going. It's the counting that kills. Men count until the numbers are worth more than the people.*

*Don't pay my debt with theirs.*

*Seal it. Walk away. Be nobody. It's the only rank worth having.*

*Your brother,*
*Alden*

*P.S. The compass. Check the compass.*

The lamp died.

Darkness took the hold entire, total, absolute — the kind of dark that has weight, that presses on the eyes like hands. The water was at Kestrel's shoulders. Somewhere above, muffled through iron and fathom, the negotiation on the hull continued, voices like insects in another world. Below him, faintly, he felt it through the soles of his boots: a deep, rhythmic pulse, the vent breathing, the Provision turning.

And in his coat pocket, against his heart, his father's compass.

*Check the compass.*

Kestrel stood in the flooding dark of his brother's grave and did the hardest thing he had done in seven years, harder than the sinking sand at the sinkhole, harder than facing Maddox across the basin: he let go. Not of the journal — that he clutched like a bone, oilcloth-wrapped, pressed inside his shirt where no search would find it easily. He let go of the other thing. The seven-year weight. The image of Alden dying alone and screaming in the salt. The story he had told himself every night since, the story that had made grief into a wound instead of what it evidently was:

A choice. Made calmly. Made *kindly*. Made so that a map of living water would not become a knife in the world's throat.

His brother had not been lost. His brother had been the lock.

And the last entry — *don't pay my debt with theirs* — was aimed straight through seven years of dark at the exact bargain Kestrel had ridden out here to make. Vane's contract. Doss's leash. The bounty that would clear his name and buy back his license. All of it priced in the same currency: the aquifer, the ledger, the deep water that could end the wars or win them. Alden had seen the whole shape of it from inside this iron belly, and had chosen the grave over the gold, and had left his little brother a message that was not comfort but instruction.

*Be nobody. It's the only rank worth having.*

The water touched his chin. The pulse below grew stronger, a drumbeat in the keel, and Kestrel felt the wreck shudder around him — the vent opening wide, the sixth tide arriving ahead of schedule, the Provision turning toward her next transformation. Air bubbles streamed up past his face from some ruptured seam, silver threads in the blackness, and he followed them the way Alden had once taught him to follow birds at dusk: not because they knew the way, but because they were going where he needed to go.

His head broke the surface of the upper compartment a moment later, and there — God, there — the narrow column of grey light from the open hatch, and Osha's silhouette against it, diving down toward him with a rope in her fists and terror written across her whole body.

She caught his collar. Hauled. Their heads came together in the chop and she shouted over the roar of water filling the decks below: "The ledger — tell me you got the ledger—"

"The chamber's sealed," Kestrel gasped. "It stays sealed."

"That's not what Maddox is going to—"

"I know what Maddox is going to do." He got a hand on the rope. Through the hatch above, the sky was churning — not with cloud but with boats, hulls, ropes, the silhouettes of Corvane's boarders dropping onto the Provision's spine as the green water rose around her. And on the highest point of the wreck, already planting his banner in the rusted rail, stood Hesk, free of his bonds, pointing down into the flooding hold and screaming something Kestrel couldn't hear but didn't need to.

Hesk had copied the cipher. Hesk knew the case was still down there. And the tide that Alden had trusted to guard his grave was rising faster than any of them had planned for — carrying all of them, guilty and grieving alike, toward the surface of a decision that could no longer wait.

Kestrel spat salt water, gripped the rope, and started climbing — his brother's last words sealed against his chest, and the fire he would need to build already burning behind his eyes.
