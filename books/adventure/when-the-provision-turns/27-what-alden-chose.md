## 27. What Alden Chose

The water was rising, and Kestrel Thornbury let it.

That was the strange arithmetic of the moment — he stood in a flooded hold with green light climbing his chest and did not run. Above him, through the shattered skylight of the Provision's sterncastle, the sixth tide poured in like something alive, silver ropes of it unspooling down into the dark. The wreck groaned around him, a long low note that lived in the bones rather than the ears, and somewhere above, muffled by timber and sea, he could hear Osha screaming his name.

He wasn't listening. He was reading.

Alden's journal lay open in his hands, its final pages swollen with salt and years, the ink gone brown but stubborn. His brother had written in the dark — Kestrel could feel it in the lines, how they wandered off the ruled margins, how they doubled back over themselves where the pen had lost its way and found it again. The last entry was short. It was not a confession. Alden had never been one for confessions; he'd been one for decisions, clean and final as a blade laid down on a table.

*The chamber seals from inside. I have the bar. Whoever comes for the ledger will have to take the whole ship apart to get it, and by then they'll understand what it's worth, which is nothing, which is everything.*

*If you're reading this, Kes, then you came anyway. I'm sorry for that. I'm sorry for all of it. But I'm not sorry for this.*

*Some things should stay buried so deep that even greed drowns on the way down.*

*Tell Father the compass points true.*

Kestrel read it twice. Then a third time, because the water was at his ribs now and his hands were shaking, and he wanted the words fixed in him somewhere deeper than paper before the paper dissolved.

*Tell Father the compass points true.*

Their father had been dead eleven years. Alden had known that when he wrote it. Which meant the line wasn't a message at all. It was a key — or a joke, or a prayer, the kind of thing a man says to himself in the dark to keep his hand steady while he bars a door he means to die behind. Kestrel felt something crack open in his chest, quiet and enormous, like ice shifting on a spring river.

His brother had chosen this. Not the drowning — no one chooses that — but the meaning of it. Alden had found the ledger of the deep aquifer, the map that could end the water wars or win them, and he had looked at it the way Kestrel had once watched him look at a rattlesnake coiled between him and a well: without hate, without fear, just the flat clear assessment of a man deciding what must be done. And then he had sealed himself in with it.

*Trust no one who counts.* That was the message Vane had intercepted, seven years ago. Kestrel finally understood it whole. Not *count no one* — *trust no one who counts*. No one who tallies. No one who weighs men against coin. Doss counted. Maddox counted. Hesk counted, always, in someone else's ledger. Even Vane counted, though she'd spent seven years pretending her arithmetic was guilt.

And Alden — Alden had stopped counting. He'd walked into the green dark with a bar of iron and a journal and made himself the lock.

"I'm here," Kestrel said aloud. His voice came out ruined, half-water. "You stubborn bastard. I'm here."

The vent answered. Below him, down in the flooded bilge where the aquifer breathed, there came a sound like the world clearing its throat — a deep tectonic groan, and then light. Green light, rising through the water column in slow luminous columns, the vent's glow brightening as the tide turned and pressure built. The Provision *turned*, Alden had written, and now Kestrel felt it turn beneath his boots: the whole wreck shuddering as the sea began to reclaim what the low tide had briefly lent back.

The chamber door was ahead of him, ten feet through darkening water, still barred from the inside. Through its iron grate he could see the ledger case bolted to the deck, brass-bound, sealed, untouched. Seven years of salvage fleets and blood and debt, all of it pointed at that box, and it sat there gleaming in the vent-light like an idol that had outlasted every worshipper.

Kestrel tucked the journal inside his coat, against his skin, and put both hands on the bar.

For one moment — one long, green-lit, lying moment — he considered it. He could lift the bar. He could drag the case out. The ledger would buy Marrowgate ten times over. It would buy his license, his name, his future. It would buy every debt he owed and every debt anyone had ever held over him, burned to ash in a single transaction. Maddox was up there on the hull with hostages and guns, and even Maddox had loved Alden once, in whatever broken way men like Maddox loved anything. Maybe some part of the ledger could be bargained. Maybe the wars could be ended instead of won. Maybe—

The vent pulsed. The green light flared, and in it Kestrel saw, absurdly, clearly, a memory: Alden at fourteen, crouched at a desert seep, cupping the last inch of water in his hands and giving it to a stranger's dying horse while Kestrel screamed at him that they needed it too. Alden hadn't argued. He'd just looked up with that same flat calm and said, *It's not ours, Kes. Nothing's ours. We're just the ones standing nearest.*

Nothing's ours. We're just the ones standing nearest.

Kestrel lifted the bar free of its brackets — and drove it home again, sideways, jamming it deeper than any hand could reach, wedging it into the warped frame where rust and seven years of tide had already fused half its length. Then he took the deck pry-bar hanging beside the bulkhead and bent the bracket bolts until they shrieked. The grate screamed. The water rose past his shoulders.

"Sorry," he whispered — to Maddox, maybe, or to the dead, or to himself. "No. Not sorry."

He turned and swam.

* * *

The surface hit him like a slap, and then Osha's hands hit him like two more.

She had him by the collar, hauling him across the tilted deck toward the hull breach where the others clung to the rigging stumps, and she was shouting something furious and terrified that the wind tore apart before it reached him whole. He caught fragments — *the tide, the tide, it's early, it's coming NOW* — and then he was being dragged up the last of the slope and over onto the barnacled spine of the wreck's exposed keel, where the sixth tide stood revealed in all its terrible appetite.

The sea was coming back. Not creeping — *coming*, a gray-green wall of it marching up the Shallows with the vent's eruption churning the water behind it into foam and glow. The mast, which had stood proud of the surface all morning, was already knee-deep and sinking. In minutes the Provision would be forty feet under again, and in an hour it would be a rumor, and by next week it would be a story sailors told each other about a light that turned green over the Shallows twice a day.

Maddox stood at the far end of the keel with six Corvane divers and a cocked harpoon gun, and he was looking at Kestrel with an expression Kestrel had never seen on a man's face before: hope. Raw, naked, boyish hope, absurd on a man that scarred, that armed, that old.

"The chamber," Maddox said. "Where is it. Where's the case."

"It's there," Kestrel said. He got his feet under him, dripping, the journal a hard rectangle against his heart. "Sealed. Barred. From the inside."

Something crossed Maddox's face — confusion first, then the beginning of understanding, arriving the way grief arrives, in stages he couldn't refuse. "Barred." He said it like a foreign word. "By who?"

"You know who."

"No." Maddox shook his head slowly. His harpoon gun drifted downward, forgotten. "No, he wouldn't. He sent word. He was going to bring it back. He told me—" and there it was, the crack running through the man's voice, seven years of a story collapsing all at once. "He told me we'd split it. He told me—"

"He told you what you needed to hear," Kestrel said. "Or you heard what you needed. One of those. Alden found the ledger and he understood it, Rell. Really understood it. And he chose." Kestrel gestured at the water swallowing the deck below them, at the green glow pulsing up through it like a heartbeat. "He's been choosing this for seven years. You just arrived late."

Maddox stared at him. Behind him, his divers shifted, uncertain, their weapons drooping as the math of the situation became undeniable: there was no ledger to take, no bargain to make, no war to fund. There was only a box at the bottom of a flooding tomb, locked by a dead man's decision, and a tide coming in that did not negotiate.

"You could open it," Maddox said quietly. "You've had time. You were down there long enough. Don't tell me you didn't touch it."

"I touched it," Kestrel said. "I left it."

"Why?"

And Kestrel, who had spent thirty-odd years and one entire ruinous month learning to answer exactly that question about everything — why the ranger's badge, why the debt, why the job, why the fire — found that the answer came easily, at last, at the bottom of everything.

"Because my brother asked me to," he said. "And because he was right. Some things should stay buried so deep that even greed drowns on the way down."

The wall of water reached them.

It didn't crash — the Shallows were too shallow for a true wave — but it *arrived*, a rising roar that lifted the whole wreck on its shoulder and rolled it, and then everyone was moving, diving, swimming, the world reduced to green churn and the scream of timbers. Kestrel got one arm around Osha, who had refused to leave the keel until he surfaced, and together they were swept clear of the hull as the Provision rolled once, majestically, like a whale sounding, and slid beneath the white water.

The last thing Kestrel saw of her was the mast going down — straight, unhurried, a soldier laying down arms — and the green light of the vent glowing up through the deepening water like a lamp left burning in a window for someone who was never coming home.

Then the sea closed over it, and the sixth tide owned everything.

* * *

They regrouped on the convoy's surviving wagons, beached on the sandbar a quarter mile out, where the water ran waist-deep and warm and mercifully shallow. Vane had gotten everyone off the hull during the chaos — Kestrel counted heads twice before he believed it: Brammel, soaked and swearing; Pim, hollow-eyed where Pell should have been answering; Osha, wrung out and silent beside him; Hesk, wet, sullen, and conspicuously empty-handed.

Hesk. Kestrel's eyes narrowed. During the eruption, in the chaos — Hesk had gone into the water. Kestrel had seen him dive. And come up with nothing.

"Looking for something, quartermaster?" Kestrel asked pleasantly.

Hesk's face did the small arithmetic it always did. "My kit," he said. "Lost my kit."

"Mm." Kestrel let it go. There would be time. There was always time for Hesk, and Hesk knew it, and that knowledge sat between them like a knife on a table.

Vane came and stood beside him at the wagon rail, watching the place where the wreck had gone down. The green glow was already fading as the tide deepened, dimming to a suggestion, a rumor. By tomorrow it would be invisible. By next month, even they might doubt it.

"You left it," she said. Not a question.

"You knew I would."

"I hoped." She was quiet a moment. "I didn't dare know."

Kestrel glanced at her. Her face was stripped bare in a way he'd never seen it — seven years of rivalry and apology-that-wasn't and apology-that-was, all of it scoured down to the woman underneath, who was tired, and old in the ways the desert makes people old, and grieving a man she'd helped kill with a buried message.

"Alden trusted you with that message," Kestrel said. "And you failed him. And then you spent seven years failing him louder." He watched her flinch and didn't soften it. "But you brought me here. That's the only entry in your ledger that matters tonight. So we're square, Sable. We're not friends. But we're square."

"That's more than I hoped for," she said, "and less than I deserve," and she turned away to give the order to make for shore, and Kestrel let her have the last word, because it cost her enough to say it.

Behind them, far out in the Shallows, the sea turned green one last time as the vent exhaled — slow, luminous, patient — and went dark.

Maddox's boats were pulling away northward, small and black against the sunset. Before they went, one of them had paused alongside the sandbar, and Maddox had stood in the bow and looked at Kestrel for a long moment across the water.

"Doss won't stop," Maddox had said. "Men like him don't grieve. They invoice." He'd almost smiled. "Your brother was smarter than both of us, Thornbury. Try to be smarter than him too." And then the boat had turned, and the Corvane fleet had become a smudge, and then a memory.

Now Kestrel sat on the wagon bench with Osha's shoulder warm against his and the journal a hard, damp weight against his chest, and he thought about the walk home. About Doss waiting in Marrowgate with his enforcers and his arithmetic. About a cipher copied into a thief's memory, riding north right now inside Hesk's skull.

The journal was safe. The cipher was not. And a debt collector did not need the whole book — only the key to it.

Osha stirred against his shoulder. "What now?" she asked.

Kestrel watched the sun go down over the Saltreach, red as an open wound, and felt — for the first time in longer than he could measure — light. Unburdened. Free in the specific, terrifying way of a man who has finally decided what things are worth.

"Now," he said, "we go home and settle accounts."

Out in the darkening Shallows, deep beneath the turning tide, a green light pulsed once — like an answer, like a warning, like a brother keeping watch — and went out.
