## 28. Betrayal at Low Water

Rell Maddox had commanded the Corvane fleet for eleven years, and in all that time he had never once watched a prize slip beneath the water on purpose.

He stood on the hull of the Provision now, boots planted on the barnacled curve of her keel where she listed half-sunk in the Shallows, and he watched the green light of the vent fade as the sixth tide closed over her like a lid. The sea above the wreck turned slowly, luminously green — a color he had no name for, a color that belonged to whatever lived down there in the dark, breathing through the drowned ship. Then even that dimmed, and there was only gray water, and the flat horizon, and the taste of salt and failure on his tongue.

Behind him, his crew worked in silence. That was the worst part. Not cursing, not complaining. Silence. Eleven years of discipline held together by a man who had just lost everything they'd crossed six tides to take.

He heard footsteps behind him — the particular gait of Sarn, his first mate, who had followed him since the border wars and who had earned the right to say what others wouldn't.

"The divers are back aboard," Sarn said. "Two of ours didn't surface."

"I know."

"And Hesk is gone."

Maddox turned. "Gone how?"

"Skiff. Took one of the skiffs at the height of the chaos, when the vent opened. Rowed hard for the eastern channel before anyone thought to stop him. He had something wrapped in oilcloth under his arm." Sarn paused. "You saw what was in that chamber, Captain. The case was empty. The ledger's gone — or it was never there. But Hesk went into that hold before any of us did. Before the water took the stern."

Maddox looked east, toward the low smudge of the Corvane fleet anchored beyond the Silt Gate, their signal lamps already blinking questions across three miles of open water.

"He copied something," Maddox said. "In the chamber, while we were arguing. I saw him crouched over the case with a charcoal stick and a strip of sailcloth. I thought he was praying." He almost laughed; it came out like a cough. "A man like Hesk doesn't pray. He transcribes."

"So we go after him?"

"No."

Sarn stared at him. "Captain—"

"We go after him, we chase a cipher we can't read onto a fleet that outguns us two to one, and Doss wins twice — once with the copy, once with our hulls broken. No." Maddox flexed his hand, feeling the ache of the dive still in his knuckles, the cold of the green dark still in his bones. "Hesk will run to Doss because Hesk always runs to whoever pays. And Doss will discover that what he bought is half a key. A cipher without its text. Let him chew on that."

He started aft, toward the rail where the other crew waited — Vane's people, Thornbury's people, the ragged survivors of the salvage convoy that had raced him across the flats and beaten him here by half a tide. He owed them a conversation he did not want to have. He owed them more than that, if he was honest, and honesty had been sitting poorly on him ever since he'd seen the empty chamber and understood, all at once and too late, what Alden Thornbury had done down there in the dark seven years ago.

* * *

They gathered on the Provision's exposed hull because there was nowhere else big enough — two crews, forty-odd souls, standing on the grave of the thing they'd all come to plunder. The tide was coming back in around them, slow and patient, licking at their boot soles. Maddox stood facing them, and beside him, arms folded, face unreadable as slate, stood Kestrel Thornbury.

Maddox had known Alden. Had drunk with him, argued with him, trusted him enough to follow him halfway to the Shallows once, years ago, before the guild pulled them both back. Seeing Kestrel was like looking at a fire through smoked glass — everything familiar about it, everything wrong.

"You came for a ledger," Maddox said, and his voice carried flat across the water without effort; he'd learned to speak over storms and he spoke now the same way. "Enough water-rites and coin to buy a city. Enough to end the water wars, some of you believed. Some of you hoped. I won't insult you by pretending I came for anything nobler than the same."

No one moved. Osha stood near the rail of the listing wreck with her diving knife still on her belt, watching him the way she'd watched him underwater — measuring, unafraid. Brammel leaned against a capstan. The twin, Pim, had an arm around nothing; Pell wasn't there to stand beside. Vane stood slightly apart from her own crew, which told Maddox something had broken between her and them, or perhaps between her and herself.

"The chamber is empty," Maddox went on. "The ledger is either drowned past recovery or it was never in the case at all. What I know is this: Alden Thornbury sealed that compartment from the inside. There are barricades down there — bulkhead timber, wedged and nailed, done by hand, done in the dark, done by a man who knew the vent would flood the hold twice a day and chose to be inside it anyway." He let that sit. He watched faces change. "He had the ledger. He could have taken it out. He could have sold it, spent it, ruled with it. Instead he built himself a tomb and locked the treasure inside it."

Kestrel shifted. Just slightly. Maddox caught it — the small motion of a man adjusting something heavy inside his coat — and said nothing, because saying something would have been a cruelty and also a mistake, and Maddox had made enough mistakes for one lifetime.

"So," Maddox said. "Here is what happens now. My fleet stands down. We don't board you, we don't press you, we don't take your wagons or your water. You'll find that generous, coming from me, and you should ask yourselves why." He looked at Kestrel. "It's because your brother beat me. Seven years ago, in the dark, without firing a shot, he beat every one of us — Doss, Corvane, the guild, the whole hungry machinery of this coast. He looked at the most valuable thing ever sunk in these waters and decided it was worth more as a grave. And I can't argue with a dead man who was righter than everyone living."

The tide crept higher. Somewhere below their feet, faint through the hull, the vent breathed — a sound like a vast throat clearing, like the sea swallowing.

"Hesk ran," Maddox continued. "Some of you know it, and those who don't will hear it now. He took a copy of the ledger's key cipher. It's worthless without the journal text it maps to — and I'd wager my fleet that the journal text is not long for this world. But Doss paid Hesk, and Doss believes Hesk, and Doss does not accept disappointment gracefully. When he learns the ledger itself is gone — and he will learn it, because Hesk's copy will tell him nothing and Doss will squeeze until Hesk confesses that there was nothing to confess — he will send more men. Better ones. Hungrier ones. He'll come for the wreck, for the coordinates, for anyone he thinks might have seen the chamber." His eyes moved across them, deliberate, naming each crew in turn. "That includes every soul standing on this hull."

Vane spoke for the first time. "You're warning us."

"I'm telling you the weather," Maddox said. "What you do about it is yours."

"And why?" Her voice was hoarse, stripped down. She had been crying, or close to it, though her face gave away nothing else. "Why let us walk? You had us at the standoff. You had hostages and guns and the high ground of the hull. One order and—"

"One order and I'd be Doss," Maddox said. "I've been his instrument for eleven years. I've seized ships for him, burned claims for him, buried men for him whose names I never learned. I told myself it was survival. Out here, on this wreck, over your brother's grave—" he nodded at Kestrel, who did not flinch, "—I found the line I'd been telling myself didn't exist. So no, Vane. No hostages. No war over an empty room. Take your crews and go home."

He turned to leave, then stopped, because there was one more thing, and it sat in his chest like a swallowed stone.

"Thornbury."

Kestrel looked at him. Up close, the resemblance to Alden was unbearable — the same set of the jaw, the same way of listening with the whole body, as though the answer might come from anywhere and deserved attention wherever it arrived.

"Maddox," Kestrel said.

"Alden and I argued for three days outside Siltgate once, about whether the deep aquifer should belong to whoever could pump it or to everyone who needed it. I said pumpers. He said everyone. I called him a sentimentalist." Maddox's voice roughened. "He called me a landlord. We laughed until we couldn't breathe. Best argument I ever lost." He held out his hand. "I'm sorry I got here too late to lose another one."

Kestrel looked at the hand for a long moment. Whatever was hidden inside his coat seemed to weigh on him visibly, dragging at his shoulders like wet canvas. Then he took it — a brief, hard grip, diver's grip, the grip of a man who had recently held another man's life underwater and knew exactly how much force that took.

"He'd have said you were half right," Kestrel said. "The aquifer belongs to everyone. But somebody still has to do the pumping."

Maddox huffed — almost a laugh. "Yeah. That sounds like him."

The tide reached their ankles. Below, the green light kindled again, faintly, through fathoms of darkening water — the vent turning, the Provision keeping its secret, the dead man's choice still holding fast against everything the living wanted from it.

* * *

They parted company at slack water, when the Shallows went glass-still and the two fleets could pass each other in the narrow channel without grinding hulls. It was an old courtesy of the flats: enemies sharing water, sharing silence, sharing the road home. Maddox stood at his flagship's rail and watched Vane's convoy pull away — the wounded lead wagon replaced with a salvaged Corvane spare, which he'd ordered given without being asked, because Brammel had looked at the broken axle the way a surgeon looks at a wound and Maddox had learned, sometime in the last hour, that he was tired of being the kind of man who let things break.

Sarn joined him at the rail. "Signal from the picket boats. They've tracked Hesk's skiff to the eastern shore. He made landfall and headed inland on foot, toward the Marrowgate road."

"Doss's road."

"Doss's road."

Maddox nodded slowly. In his mind he laid the pieces out the way he'd once laid out battle charts: Hesk running inland with half a key. Doss waiting in Marrowgate with his counting house and his enforcers and his leash around Kestrel Thornbury's neck — because Maddox had done his research, before the race, and knew exactly what kind of debt the ranger carried and what it was secured against. Doss would expect the ledger to arrive in Thornbury's pack, because Doss had arranged the whole expedition to make sure it did. Hire the desperate man. Leash him through his debt. Let the rival fleets fight each other down to the bone, then collect from whichever survivor crawled home.

And instead Thornbury would come home with nothing but a story, and stories were the one currency Doss had never learned to spend.

"He'll send more," Sarn said quietly. "You said it yourself. Enforcers. Claim-jumpers. Maybe worse."

"He will." Maddox watched the convoy shrink against the western glare, dust rising off the saltflat behind the wagons like smoke from a doused fire. "And when he does, he'll send them past the Silt Gate. Past my pickets. Because Doss thinks I work for him." He smiled, and there was no warmth in it, only a kind of grim relief, like setting down a weight carried too long. "Let him think it a little longer. First honest thing I can do for Alden Thornbury is lie to his creditor."

"You're changing sides, Captain?"

Maddox considered it. The sun was dropping toward the flats, and the water around the Provision had gone fully green now, glowing from below like a lamp behind jade, and he understood that he would see that color in his sleep for the rest of his life — the color of a choice, holding.

"No," he said. "I'm finally picking one."

He turned from the rail and gave the order to come about, and the Corvane fleet swung east toward home, leaving the Shallows, the wreck, and the green turning light behind them — while far to the west, on the Marrowgate road, a lone figure walked with something burning a hole in his coat, and a counting house waited with its doors open and its enforcers patient, and the last tide of the story began to rise.
