## 29. The Long Walk Back

The flats did not forgive, and they did not forget. Sable Vane had learned that thirty years ago, on her first crossing, when the salt had taken her instructor's mule and half their water and taught her that the desert kept its own ledger — one that no contract could dissolve.

Now she walked at the head of a convoy that moved like a wounded animal.

Six wagons had left Marrowgate at first tide. Four returned. The lead wagon was gone, taken at the Silt Gate in a hail of Corvane shot, along with two draft lizards and a young hand named Ferrow who'd signed on for shares and gotten a shallow grave instead. Pell was gone too, swallowed by the Glass Reach, and though they'd marked the sinkhole's edge with iron stakes and a cairn of salt-crusted stone, everyone knew the flats would take the markers within a season. The desert erased debts of that kind. It only remembered what it wanted to remember.

Vane counted heads twice an hour. She couldn't help it. Brammel limped beside the second wagon, his hands bandaged where the engine housing had scalded him, muttering calculations under his breath the way other men prayed. Osha walked with her dive gear strapped to her back even now, as if the Shallows might rise up out of the salt behind them and demand she go back down. Pim hadn't spoken since the wreck. He walked beside his dead twin's empty saddle, one hand resting on the cantle, and when the wind gusted he flinched as though Pell had called his name.

And Kestrel Thornbury rode drag, ten lengths behind the last wagon, alone, the way he'd ridden out and the way, Vane suspected, he would ride until something in him broke or healed. His coat hung heavy on his shoulders. She knew what was sewn into the lining. She'd watched him take the pages from Alden's journal before the sixth tide came down, watched him fold them away while Maddox raged over an empty chamber, and she had said nothing then and said nothing now. Some things a woman carried without carrying them aloud.

The sun stood high and white over the Saltreach, and the convoy limped home through it, leaving a line of tracks that the wind was already smoothing away behind them.

* * *

They stopped at dusk in the lee of a shattered mesa, and Vane made herself walk the wagons before she let anyone sleep.

It was quartermaster's habit, drilled into her from her salvage days: count the water, count the food, count the living. She found the water barrels at just under half. Enough. Barely. She found the ration tins dented and short — Hesk's doing, she realized, the man had been skimming from the start, padding his reports to whoever paid him, and the thought sat in her chest like a stone. She'd hired him. She'd checked his references herself, seven years after she'd stopped trusting anyone's references, because the Provision contract had needed a quartermaster with Corvane connections and she'd told herself she could manage him.

She had managed nothing. Hesk was gone — fled to Maddox at low water with whatever cipher he'd copied — but his fingerprints were all over the convoy still. In the short rations. In the flares missing from the signal locker. In the way Pim watched every new face like a man counting exits.

"You should eat."

Vane turned. Osha stood behind her, holding out a tin of salted fish and a strip of dried fruit, her dark hair still stiff with mineral crust from the dive. Up close, Vane could see the fine tremor in the diver's hands that hadn't stopped since they'd surfaced. She'd held Kestrel's rope in the green dark. She'd felt the hold shudder around them when the vent opened early. Some things stayed in the hands long after the mind let them go.

"I've eaten," Vane said.

"You haven't. I watched you give your share to Pim." Osha didn't lower the tin. "You're no good to anyone starving, Captain."

*Captain.* The word landed strangely. Vane took the tin because refusing it twice would cost more than eating it. "I'm not captain anymore. Not since the hull."

"The contract's not dissolved yet."

"It will be tomorrow." Vane looked out past the wagons, to where the last light was draining off the flats, turning the salt from white to violet to a gray that matched the sky exactly, so that the horizon simply ceased and the world became a bowl of fading color with nine people huddled at the bottom of it. "There's nothing left to hold anyone to. No bounty. No shares. Just a long walk and a shorter story when we get home."

Osha settled onto a supply crate beside her, unhurried, and ate her own portion in small deliberate bites. For a while neither spoke. The convoy's evening sounds filled the space — Brammel coaxing the damaged engine into a low idle so it wouldn't seize overnight, the lizards chewing their mash, Pim's silence sitting at the fire like a tenth member of the crew.

"Kestrel told me what you said to him," Osha said at last. "At your fire. After the fourth tide."

Vane's jaw tightened. "He had no business repeating that."

"He had every business. You confessed to burying a man's brother's last message for seven years. That's not a private thing between two proud people. That's a weight the whole crew carried without knowing it." Osha turned to look at her directly, and there was no cruelty in her face, which somehow made it worse. "I'm not here to punish you. I'm telling you what I saw down there, in the hold. When the vent opened and Kestrel went back for the journal — he wasn't thinking about you. He wasn't thinking about Maddox or the ledger or Doss. He was thinking about Alden. Whatever you carry from this crossing, don't make the mistake of thinking you're the center of it. None of us are. That's the mercy of it."

Vane stared at her. Thirty years in the salvage trade had taught her to read people fast — to find the lever, the angle, the want — and she found nothing in Osha's face to leverage. The diver meant exactly what she'd said, no more, no less, and expected nothing back.

"You're a strange woman, Osha," Vane said quietly.

"I'm a diver. We see things plain, down where it's dark." Osha stood, brushed salt from her trousers, and paused. "For what it's worth — the apology was real the second time. I heard it. So did he. Whether he keeps it is his business, not yours. But you gave it honest, and honest counts for more out here than coin does."

She walked back toward the fire, and Vane sat alone with her cold tin of fish and the enormous violet dusk, and found, to her surprise, that she was crying — silently, uselessly, the way she hadn't cried since she was nineteen years old and had buried her own ambitions under a promotion she'd bought with another person's silence.

Seven years ago, a message had come to her office in Marrowgate bearing Alden Thornbury's seal. She had read it standing up. She had burned it the same night, and reported the expedition lost with all hands, and slept soundly, because the message had named a traitor aboard the Provision and Vane had understood — instantly, coldly — that the traitor's name touching her own channels would end her career before it began. So she had buried it. And a family had spent seven years not knowing. And a brother had spent seven years hating a rival who deserved, it turned out, exactly the hatred she'd earned.

Honest counts for more than coin. She wiped her face with the heel of her hand and went to check the water barrels again, because counting was the only penance she trusted.

* * *

They buried Pell properly on the third day out, at the sinkhole's edge.

It was a detour — four hours across hardpan that the wagons hated — and Vane made it without putting it to a vote, because some decisions a captain made alone or she wasn't worth her pay. They reached the Glass Reach margin near midday, and there it was: the sinkhole, a ragged mouth in the salt perhaps forty feet across, its edges already slumped and softened by wind, the iron stakes they'd planted leaning at drunk angles. The mirages were working the horizon as always, throwing up towers of water and cities of glass that dissolved when you walked toward them. Pell had died chasing certainty through country that offered none. Vane thought there was probably a lesson in that, and decided she was too tired to learn it.

Pim knelt at the edge first. He'd carried his brother's effects himself — would let no one else touch them — and now he laid them out on a spread tarpaulin with the care of a man defusing something: Pell's navigation instruments, wrapped in oilcloth. His logbook, water-swollen beyond reading. The twin's pendant they'd both worn since childhood, split between them, each half a broken circle. Pim placed his own half in with the rest, and Vane saw his hand shake, and saw him close it into a fist until the shaking stopped.

"We should say words," Brammel murmured. "Somebody ought to—"

"Kestrel will," Osha said.

And he did. Vane watched him come forward from the drag position, dismounting slowly, favoring ribs that Maddox's skirmish had cracked. He stood at the sinkhole's rim for a long moment, looking down into the blue-shadowed depths where the salt crust gave way to darkness, and Vane wondered — not for the first time — how deep that hole went, and whether Pell had known, in those last seconds, anything at all.

"Pell Ashgrave," Kestrel said. His voice carried flat and clear across the salt, the voice of a ranger accustomed to open distances. "Navigator. Twin. He read mirage better than any man I've crossed with, and I ignored his warning, and he died for it." He paused. Vane saw Osha glance at her — quickly, meaningfully — and understood that Kestrel was not merely speaking; he was settling accounts aloud, in front of witnesses, because that was how men like him did penance. Not with tears. With precision. "I owe him a debt I can't repay. So I'll do the next thing. I'll carry his name to the guild rolls, and I'll navigate this route again someday, and when I do, I'll trust the navigator's word over my own pride. That's the burial. That's what I've got."

He looked at Pim. "Your brother saved us twice in the Reach. The tunnel entrance — that was his fix, not mine. Say it however you need to say it, but say it true: he was good."

Pim's face crumpled and reassembled itself, twice, before he managed: "He was better than me. Everyone thought we were the same. We weren't. He was better." He laid the tarpaulin's bundle at the edge, weighted it with stones, and built the cairn over it with the methodical patience of grief — stone upon stone upon stone, each one chosen, each one placed, until a marker stood waist-high against the endless white.

They stood around it in a loose crescent, hats off, the wind pulling at their collars. Vane found herself speaking before she'd decided to.

"I signed Pell to this contract," she said. "I chose the route through the Reach on a schedule I set, to beat a fleet I was racing out of vanity. He's dead because of my choices, and I won't pretend otherwise, and I won't ask forgiveness, because forgiveness isn't mine to ask for." She looked at the cairn a moment longer. "But I'll see his name entered in the guild rolls too. Both names. That I swear on my license, and my license is the only thing I've got left that means anything."

Nobody answered. Nobody needed to. Kestrel met her eyes across the cairn — one long look, unreadable, the ghost of Alden somewhere behind it — and then he nodded, once, and put his hat back on, and the ceremony was over.

They rode out of the Glass Reach as the afternoon light went long and gold, and Vane rode at the rear now, watching the cairn shrink behind them until it was a speck, then a suggestion, then nothing at all. The flats closed over it like water.

* * *

On the fifth day, with Marrowgate's smoke visible on the horizon like a smudge of ash, Vane called the crew together and dissolved the contract.

She did it formally, because formality was all she had left to give. She unrolled the original charter on a wagon tailgate — the heavy paper, the wax seals, the column of names with share percentages written beside them in her own handwriting, back when the Provision had been a fortune waiting under the Shallows and every signature had been an act of faith. The paper looked absurd now. It looked like a map to a country that had never existed.

"Crew of the Vane expedition," she read, "by authority vested in me as contracting principal, I hereby declare this charter void. All obligations of share, wage, and claim are released effective immediately. No member owes the venture further service, and the venture owes no member further payment." She looked up. "That includes hazard pay clauses. I know what the charter promised for a successful recovery. There was no recovery. But you bled for it anyway, and I won't have it said that Sable Vane's crews work for promises. What remains of my operating funds goes to the families — Pell's, and Ferrow's, and the two hands we lost at the Gate. Equal portions. I'll deliver them myself."

Brammel cleared his throat. "And your cut? Your investors—"

"My investors can file their grievances with my estate, if I have one when they're done." A few tired laughs moved through the group. "Gentlemen. Osha. Pim. You signed on to a lie I half-believed myself — that the Provision was treasure, that the race was winnable, that the past stays buried if you drive fast enough. None of that was true. What was true is that you held together when everything came apart, and I've commanded crews twenty years and never seen better. Go home. Drink something. Forget me if you can."

"Can't promise the forgetting," Brammel said, and shook her hand with his bandaged one, gently, as if she were the injured party.

One by one they came to her. Pim, who gripped her wrist hard and said nothing and meant everything. Osha, who held the handshake a moment longer and said, "Marrowgate, then. If you're ever sober enough to talk, I'm usually awake." Even the lizards seemed to understand something was ending, standing hipshot and patient in the traces of wagons that belonged, now, to no venture at all.

Last came Kestrel.

He stood apart, as always, and Vane walked to him rather than making him cross the distance, because she owed him that much geometry at least. Up close she could see the salt had gotten into everything — the seams of his coat, the creases around his eyes, the gray coming into his beard. Seven years ago she had ruined this man's reputation with a forged survey report, and he had never once come to collect the debt. He'd simply carried it, the way he carried everything, and she was beginning to understand that this was the whole of him: a man who carried.

"The charter's void," she said. "You're free of it. Free of me."

"I was free of you the day I signed," Kestrel said. "That was the point of signing."

"Yes." She almost smiled. "Well. We're none of us free of what we sign. I learned that from you, actually — you and your brother both. Alden signed on to my father's last expedition, you know. Did you know that? Twenty-two years old, first contract, bright as a struck match. I've been signing Thornburys onto doomed ventures my entire career. It's practically a family tradition." She stopped herself. "I'm sorry. That's not absolution talking. That's just an old woman cataloguing her sins."

"It's not your job to absolve yourself, Vane." Kestrel's voice was quiet, and for once there was no edge in it — just the flat dry tone of a man stating weather. "That's not how it works out here. You do the harm, and then you do the next right thing, and then the next, and you keep doing them until the ledger balances or you die trying. My brother believed that. I used to think it was naive." He glanced toward the southeast, toward the Shallows, invisible beyond sixty miles of salt. "I don't anymore."

"The guild confession is filed," Vane said. "Alden's message, verbatim, what I did with it, all of it. They can strike my license if they want. Probably they'll want."

"They won't. Guilds forgive confessors and hang deniers — it's cheaper." He swung up into the saddle, and the movement cost him; Vane saw him breathe through the pain of his ribs. "Marrowgate by nightfall. Doss will be waiting."

The name dropped between them like a stone into still water. Vane felt her stomach tighten. "Thornbury. The ledger's drowned. The cipher's worthless without the pages. There's nothing Doss can take from you."

"There's always something a creditor can take." Kestrel gathered his reins. "That's what makes them creditors." He looked down at her, and for a moment — just a moment — something moved behind his eyes that might have been the ghost of respect, or the ghost of farewell, or simply exhaustion wearing a kinder mask. "You gave Pell's family your funds. You filed the confession. You dissolved the charter when you could have held us to it and squeezed blood from the wreckage. That's three right things in a row, Vane. Keep the streak going. It suits you better than the armor did."

He turned his horse and rode ahead toward the smoke of Marrowgate, and Vane stood in the failing light watching him go, and understood that she had just received the only absolution she would ever get from that quarter: not forgiveness, but acknowledgment. A nod across a grave. Three right things in a row.

It would have to be enough. Out here, it always was.

She turned toward the wagons, toward the crew she no longer commanded and the town she no longer controlled, and followed him home across the darkening flats — toward Doss, toward the reckoning she could see coming like weather on the horizon, toward whatever the next tide brought.
