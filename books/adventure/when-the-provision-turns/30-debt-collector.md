## 30. Debt Collector

Marrowgate smelled the same as it always had — brine and lamp oil and the sweet rot of the fish market two streets over — but to Kestrel it smelled like a trap he'd walked into willingly.

He had known, of course. From the moment Hesk named his employer on the saltflat, from the moment Vane dissolved the contract and put nothing in anyone's hands but grief, Kestrel had understood that this reckoning was waiting for him at the end of the road like a debt collector at a funeral. Doss did not chase men across six tides of salt and drowned canyon. He didn't have to. He simply owned the door they would eventually come home through.

And so Kestrel came home through it.

The counting house stood where it had always stood, at the crook of Marrowgate's one paved street, a squat stone building that had once been a customs office back when the town had been worth taxing. Its windows were narrow as gun slits. Its door was iron-banded oak, and standing on either side of it, in the last gold light of evening, were two of Doss's enforcers — big men in dust-colored coats with the flat, patient faces of dogs that had been trained never to bark first.

They knew him. That was the worst part. They nodded, almost courteously, and stepped aside, and Kestrel felt the full weight of what that courtesy meant: *you were always coming here; we only wondered when.*

The interior was dim and warm, lit by a single brass lamp on the counter and the orange glow of a stove in the back room. Ledgers lined the walls in pigeonholes, hundreds of them, each spine labeled in Doss's small precise hand. The smell was paper and ink and old sweat — the smell of every promise ever made in Marrowgate, filed and cross-referenced and held against the people who made them.

Doss sat behind the counter, though *sat* was too generous a word for the way he occupied the chair. He was a small man, dry as a seed pod, with a long face and eyes the color of weak tea, and he wore his wealth the way other men wore poverty — invisibly, in the fit of his coat and the softness of his boots and the absolute stillness of his hands. Those hands rested on the counter now, folded, patient, and beside them lay an open ledger with a fresh page ruled and waiting.

"Thornbury." He said the name the way a man reads a figure off a bill of lading. "You're three days past my estimate. I had you arriving tomorrow."

"The flats were slow going home. We buried a woman on the way."

"I heard." Something flickered behind the tea-colored eyes — not sympathy, but accounting. Losses recorded. "Pell. One of the navigator twins. A shame. She owed me four crowns." He turned the ledger slightly, as if inviting Kestrel to admire the neatness of it. "You owe me considerably more."

Kestrel stopped in front of the counter. His legs ached from six tides of riding; his shoulders carried the particular exhaustion of a man who has been underwater and come back up changed. He set his hat on the counter, deliberately, the way a man sets down a weapon before a parley.

"I imagine you want to talk about the Provision," he said.

"I imagine we do." Doss unfolded his hands and laced the fingers together again, a small ritual of composure. "Sit, if you like. There's water."

There was. A clay jug and a single cup, set out on the side table as if Doss had expected him thirsty. Kestrel looked at it for a long moment. Water, freely offered, in Marrowgate, by a man who sold everything — it was either a courtesy or a demonstration, and with Doss it was always both. He poured a cup and drank it slow, letting the man watch him do it, and then sat down on the hard chair across the counter.

"Hesk told you about the ledger," Kestrel said.

"Hesk told me many things. That the wreck sits over a living vent. That the hold floods twice daily. That your brother sealed the chamber rather than let it be taken." Doss's voice never rose or fell; it moved along its level like water finding its plane. "That the ledger itself remains locked in its case, at the bottom of the Shallows, under thirty feet of green water that turns twice a day." He paused. "And that Hesk copied the key cipher before he ran. Which means the ledger is recoverable. By anyone with the cipher and the nerve."

"That's what he told you."

"It's what he sold me, yes. At considerable discount, I might add, since he arrived half-drowned and hysterical." Doss tilted his head. "You're going to tell me something different. I can see it in your face. You've been rehearsing it since Ash Hollow. So let's not waste the lamp oil. Tell me."

Kestrel leaned forward. Up close, in the lamplight, he could see the fine cracks in Doss's composure — the faint tremor in the folded fingers, the sheen of sweat at the hairline. The man had staked something enormous on this. More than the debt. Perhaps everything.

"The Provision is gone," Kestrel said. "The sixth tide took it. The whole hull went down into the vent channel — you could stand on the shore and watch the mast go under like a man sinking. There is no ledger to recover. There is no wreck to dive. There is a hole in the world where both of them used to be, and it fills and empties twice a day, and anything you sent down there would come back up as driftwood."

Silence. The stove ticked in the back room. Somewhere outside, a cart rattled over the paving stones and faded away toward the market.

"You're lying," Doss said, without heat.

"I'm telling you the truth, which looks the same from where you're sitting."

"They're not the same thing at all." Doss opened the ledger in front of him and turned it around so Kestrel could read it. His own name ran down the page in entries — some years old, some recent, each one dated and initialed. The gear seized in chapter ten. The license bond. The interest, compounding, patient as salt. "Here is what I know, Thornbury. I know a convoy left Marrowgate with forty-one souls and came back with thirty-six. I know the Corvane fleet turned around at the Silt Gate without pressing their advantage, which Maddox does not do unless there was nothing left to press it for. And I know that you — a man drowning in debt, a man who signed on for coin — came back owning nothing but a compass and a dead man's journal." He tapped the page. "Men don't walk away from fortunes. Men walk away from graves. So either the fortune is real and you're hiding it, or the fortune is gone and you're telling the truth. And I have spent twenty years learning that the second story is almost never the one men tell."

"And yet."

"And yet." Doss closed the ledger. "So let us test it. Your debt stands at two hundred and sixty crowns. Your license forfeits at dawn. Unless—" he raised one finger, "—unless you give me the cipher. Hesk's copy is unreliable; the man copies numbers the way a drunk copies letters. If you have seen the true cipher, if Alden's journal holds it, then I can send my own divers with proper equipment and proper patience, and whatever is down there, down in that turning water, will come up into my hands. And your debt burns with it. Every entry. Clean page."

Kestrel sat very still.

It would be so easy. That was the thing no one warned you about temptation — how reasonable it sounded in a warm room after a long road. Two hundred and sixty crowns erased. His license restored. All he had to do was hand over a string of characters he hadn't even written down, characters living behind his eyes where Alden's handwriting lived, where the last line of a dead man's dark-written pages lived. *The Provision turns. Trust no one who counts.* He could give Doss the cipher and keep the words. He could pay his debt and keep his soul. Everyone would win.

Everyone except whoever dove next. Except the next crew that went down into the green dark chasing a dead man's arithmetic. Except the aquifer itself, mapped and claimed and fought over, the water wars fed another decade, another generation — all of it purchased with the price of a debt Kestrel Thornbury found inconvenient.

His father's compass pressed against his ribs inside his coat. *North is a choice.* He hadn't opened it yet. He wasn't ready. But he knew what it said. Some part of him had always known.

"No," Kestrel said.

Doss exhaled slowly through his nose. "No?"

"There is no cipher to give. Even if there were, I wouldn't. And even if I would—" Kestrel stood, and the chair scraped back, and both enforcers shifted in the doorway behind him, "—it wouldn't matter. Because you're right about one thing, Doss. I walked away from a grave. My brother built that grave with his own hands, from the inside, and the only payment he ever asked was that nobody dig it up. I'm here to honor that payment. Not yours."

For the first time, something moved in Doss's face — not anger, but the tighter thing underneath anger: fear, dressed quickly in contempt. "You think sentiment frightens me? I have enforcers at the door and a magistrate on retainer. I can take whatever you're carrying. I can take the coat off your back and shake out every seam."

"You can," Kestrel agreed. "And you'll find a compass, a canteen, and a change of socks. No journal. No pages. No cipher." He met the tea-colored eyes and held them. "I burned it."

The lie landed in the room like a dropped lantern.

Because it was a lie — the pages were still against his chest, folded flat, warm from his body, Alden's cramped handwriting sleeping against his heartbeat. He had decided it on the ride in, somewhere past the sinkhole where they'd buried Pell, watching the sun come up over the flats: that if Doss pushed, if it came to steel or search, the pages would burn before they'd be taken. He had carried the thought the whole way home like a coal cupped in both hands. Now he set it down between himself and the creditor and let it glow.

"You burned it," Doss repeated.

"Every page. On the flats, the night before we made Marrowgate. I read what mattered and I fed the rest to the fire." Kestrel shrugged, slow and easy, a man with nothing left to lose performing the part. "You should understand what you bought when you bought Hesk. You bought a coward who copies things he doesn't understand. The cipher isn't numbers, Doss. It's keyed to the journal itself — to the wording, the phrasing, pages only I've read. His copy is a key to a door that's ash. Send your divers. Spend your crowns. They'll find a hole in the world and a dead man's lock, and they'll die trying, because that water turns and it doesn't care whose money sent them."

He watched the fear move behind Doss's eyes now, undisguised. The man's empire was built on certainty — on knowing exactly what everyone in Marrowgate owed and exactly how to collect it. Kestrel had just handed him a debt that couldn't be collected, an asset that existed nowhere, and the look on Doss's face was the look of a man discovering that the ground floor of his house was water.

"You're bluffing," Doss said quietly. "A man doesn't burn his brother's last words."

"A man doesn't," Kestrel agreed. "But a brother might. If the words asked him to." He picked up his hat. "You wanted aquifer secrets. Here's the only secret there is: the deep water belongs to no one, because no one can hold it. Not Corvane. Not you. It turns, and it takes, and it keeps. My brother learned that at the bottom of the Shallows, and it cost him everything he had, and he paid it gladly so that people like you would never get to spend it."

He turned for the door. Behind him, Doss's voice cracked its level for the first time in twenty years:

"Your license forfeits at dawn, Thornbury. You'll leave this town with nothing. Nothing! Do you hear me? I will see to it that every guild, every yard, every well in Marrowgate knows your name and your balance, and you will beg on the flats before winter!"

Kestrel stopped with his hand on the iron-banded door. He didn't turn all the way — just enough to speak over his shoulder, and he found, to his own surprise, that he was almost smiling.

"Doss. For seven years I've been walking around this town carrying your ledger on my back like a pack frame. Two hundred sixty crowns. Interest. Terms. I've dreamed about being free of it more times than I've dreamed about water." He pushed the door open; the evening air came in cool and smelling of the sea. "But I never once imagined it would feel like this. Like setting something down. You didn't own my debt, old man. You owned my *yes*. And I'm done saying it."

He stepped out into the street. The enforcers parted — not orders, just instinct, the way men step aside for weather — and the door boomed shut behind him.

The lamps of Marrowgate were coming on, one by one, down the crooked street. Somewhere a gull argued with the tide. Kestrel stood on the paving stones with his hat in his hand and his heart going hard against the folded pages hidden in his coat, and he understood that he had perhaps a minute, maybe less, before Doss recovered, before the fear curdled into action, before the enforcers came back out that door with orders to shake every seam he owned.

One minute to decide whether the lie became the truth.

He looked down the street toward the harbor, where Osha's diving outfit kept its lamps burning late, where a future had been offered plainly and not yet answered. Then he looked at the brazier outside the chandlery across the way, its coals banked and glowing, unattended while the chandler argued prices with a fishwife.

Kestrel crossed the street.

* * *

The coals were perfect — low orange, patient, the kind of heat that takes a thing slowly instead of all at once. Kestrel crouched beside the brazier with his back to the counting house and drew the pages out of his coat.

Alden's handwriting climbed the sheets in the lamplight spilling from the chandlery window: figures, headings, the careful columns of a man who had trusted arithmetic the way other men trusted God. And beneath the cipher, beneath the map references, the last lines written blind in the dark of a flooding hold, the ones Kestrel had already carried in his memory for weeks and would carry until he died: *The Provision turns. Trust no one who counts.*

Behind him, the counting house door banged open. Boots on paving stones. Doss's voice, high and thin, calling his name, calling to the enforcers, calling to anyone.

Kestrel touched the first page to the coals.

The paper caught with a soft breath of flame, curling, browning, the cipher blooming into light and then into nothing. He fed it the second page. The third. The heat kissed his knuckles and he didn't pull back. Page by page, Alden's ledger of the deep aquifer — the map that could end the water wars or fund the next one, depending on whose hands held it — went up in small orange flowers, and the ash lifted on the harbor wind and drifted out over the roofs of Marrowgate toward the sea.

*Trust no one who counts,* Kestrel thought, watching the last sheet blacken. *Count me out.*

By the time the enforcers reached him, breathing hard, Doss's voice cracking behind them, there was nothing left in Kestrel's hands but warmth and a few flakes of grey already dissolving in the wind. One of the enforcers grabbed his shoulder and spun him around, and Kestrel let himself be spun, calm as a fence post, holding up empty palms.

"Too late," he said. "Tell him it's done."

The enforcer searched him anyway — rough hands, quick and thorough, coat seams, boot tops, hatband — and came up with a compass, a canteen, and a change of socks, exactly as promised. He stared at the little pile of nothing in his big hands as if it had personally insulted him.

From across the street, Doss stood in his doorway in the lamplight, small and dry and suddenly very old, watching the ash blow seaward. His mouth worked once, twice, around words that wouldn't come. Twenty years of ledgers, and no column for this.

Kestrel retrieved his compass from the enforcer's palm, settled his hat on his head, and walked north up the crooked street, away from the harbor lights, owing nothing, carrying nothing but a dead man's final sentence and the strange, weightless terror of a free man's first steps.

Behind him, in the counting house window, he saw Doss reach for a fresh page — because men like Doss always reached for a fresh page — and Kestrel smiled grimly in the dark and kept walking, because whatever the old man wrote on it next, it would be the first entry in a book Kestrel Thornbury no longer appeared in.
