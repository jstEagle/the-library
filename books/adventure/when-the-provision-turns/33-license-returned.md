## 33. License Returned

The summons came on a Tuesday, which Kestrel found fitting. Tuesdays were the guild's day for paperwork — renewals, grievances, the slow grinding of bureaucracy that kept the rangers' hall smelling of ink and old leather and the particular staleness of men who had traded the open range for a chair by a window. The summons itself was a heavy card of pressed vellum, sealed with the guild's wax: a stylized cactus bloom inside a circle of rope. It named him. It named the date. It named the ceremony.

*Ceremony.* The word sat in his stomach like a stone swallowed wrong.

He read the card three times on the porch of Osha's outfit, standing in the early light while the well crew worked behind him and the smell of wet sand and rope oil drifted up from the yard. The card said the Guild of Range Wardens, by unanimous vote of the Bench of Masters, did hereby restore to Kestrel Thornbury the full rights and standing of a licensed ranger, in recognition of services rendered — and here followed a paragraph of language so formal it took him a moment to parse it — *in recognition of his leadership and courage in the preservation of life during the Provision expedition, wherein four souls were brought living out of the Shallows against all expectation.*

Four souls. He counted them in his head the way he counted everything now, an old habit that had become a kind of prayer. Osha. Brammel. Pim, who had lost his brother Pell and come home quieter than anyone. Vane, who had signed the dissolution and left before the ink dried. Four. And one name that would never come home at all, no matter how the guild dressed it in vellum.

"You're going to make that face all morning, or are you going to tell me what it says?" Osha stood in the doorway with two cups of chicory, steam rising off them into the cool air. She had a way of appearing at the exact moment a silence became unbearable, as though she kept a ledger of silences the way Doss had kept a ledger of debts.

Kestrel handed her the card.

She read it standing, one thumb hooked in her belt, and when she finished she didn't hand it back. She turned it over, turned it over again, and then looked at him with the flat, patient expression she used on divers who lied about how much air they had left.

"They're giving you back your name," she said.

"They're giving me back a piece of paper."

"A piece of paper that says you can work. That says you're a ranger again instead of a man who used to be one." She tapped the card against her palm. "Seven years, Kestrel. Seven years you've been walking the flats on borrowed standing, doing the guild's work for free because nobody would license you after the Provision rumors started. And now they want to hand it back in front of a crowd, with trumpets."

"There are no trumpets at a guild ceremony."

"There's *pomp.*" She said the word like it was a disease. "There's the Bench of Masters in their formal collars, and there's you standing on that floor in front of every ranger in the district, and every one of them will be thinking the same thing: *that's the man whose brother sold the expedition.* Or worse — *that's the man whose brother died for nothing.* And you'll stand there and let them think it, because that's what ceremonies are for. Letting people think things at you."

Kestrel took the cup of chicory she offered. It was too hot to drink and he drank it anyway, scalding his tongue, a small punishment for a morning that hadn't earned one.

"They're not wrong about Alden," he said.

"They're wrong about everything. That's the whole trouble with towns." She set the card on the porch rail and weighted it with a stone, a small domestic gesture that made something in his chest ache unexpectedly. "You don't have to go."

"I have to go."

"Why?"

He didn't have an answer ready, which was rare for him, and Osha knew it was rare, and she waited with the patience of someone who had spent her professional life at the bottom of dark water, where answers came up slowly or not at all. Behind her, the well crew's morning sounds filled the yard — the creak of the windlass, the slap of wet canvas, Brammel swearing cheerfully at a pump gasket that had never once in its life done what he asked.

"Because they're going to read the citation," Kestrel said finally. "Out loud. In the rolls. And there's a name they're not going to say."

* * *

The rangers' hall in Marrowgate had been built in the town's first decade, back when Marrowgate was less a town than an argument between a well and a wagon road. It was stone at the base and salt-bleached timber above, and the great room where ceremonies were held had a ceiling so high it collected its own weather — dust motes drifting in the light from the clerestory windows like slow, directionless birds. The rolls of the guild were kept there, in a case of iron-banded oak against the eastern wall: forty years of names, entered in a hand that had changed with each guild clerk but never in its care. Every ranger who had ever held a license in the Saltreach district was in that book. Every ranger who had died holding one had a line beneath their name in red.

Kestrel had stood in that room exactly twice in the last seven years. Once to surrender his license — Doss's creditors had forced the suspension after the first Provision rumor, and Kestrel had walked out before the clerk finished reading the motion — and once, two years later, to ask them to reopen the file on his brother. They had refused. *No body, no evidence, no standing,* the Bench had said, and the man who said it had been Master Corliss, who was standing at the front of the room now in his formal collar, adjusting his spectacles and pretending not to notice Kestrel at the back.

The hall was fuller than Kestrel expected. Not packed — Marrowgate was too tired a town for packed — but full. Rangers in their range coats, a scattering of townsfolk, the well crew from Osha's outfit who had apparently closed the yard for the morning without asking anyone's permission. Brammel had put on a coat that fit him like a tarpaulin on a fence post. Pim sat alone near the window, hands folded, watching the dust motes. And near the front, in a chair that had clearly been set out for her and clearly would not be filled, a place had been left with a ribbon on it — the guild's mark of honor for a member who could not attend. Kestrel didn't have to ask whose. Word traveled. Vane's confession had been filed with the guild before she left Marrowgate, all of it: the intercepted message, the seven years of silence, the career she had built on the bones of a dead man's expedition. The Bench had read it in closed session. Whatever they had decided to do about it, they had decided not to do it today.

The ceremony was short, as guild ceremonies went. Master Corliss read the citation in a voice like dry paper — the services rendered, the lives preserved, the tides survived — and Kestrel stood where he was told to stand and felt the words pass over him like weather. When Corliss came to the part about the license, an old clerk named Fenwick brought the book itself out of the iron-banded case, and the room went quiet in the particular way rooms go quiet around old books, and Kestrel watched Fenwick's pen move across a fresh line with his own name on it and felt — nothing. Or not nothing. Something colder and stranger than nothing. The feeling of a door being unlocked from the outside, by someone else, when you had already climbed out the window.

Then Corliss folded his notes and said, "Ranger Thornbury, the guild extends its — "

"I'd like to say something," Kestrel said.

The room shifted. Corliss's spectacles caught the light. Kestrel had planned this in the way he planned everything — not with words, but with a direction, a bearing, the way you planned a crossing of the flats: know where the water is, know where the sinkholes are, walk.

"Alden Thornbury held a guild license for eleven years," he said. His voice carried better than he expected; the high ceiling did that, gave small voices room to grow. "He was entered in these rolls in the spring of his nineteenth year, and he served without a single mark against his name. When the Provision went down, he was first over the rail helping the passengers, and when the expedition went into the Shallows, he was its guide. He didn't come home. For seven years this guild recorded him as *missing, presumed lost,* and then as *lost,* and then stopped recording him at all, because the story got tangled up with rumors and debts and the ambitions of people who weren't there." He did not look at the chair with the ribbon. He made himself not look at it. "I'm not here to argue the rumors. I'm not here to settle anyone's ambitions. I'm asking the guild to do one thing, and it's the only thing I want from this room."

He turned to Fenwick and the book.

"Enter his name again. In the rolls. With the citation the guild just read for me — the services rendered, the lives preserved. Because he found the Provision. He went into the water. He did the guild's work at the bottom of the Shallows, and he did it alone, and nobody has ever said so out loud." Kestrel's voice held. He had crossed six tides of saltflat and drowned canyon to keep it holding, and it would hold now. "I don't want the ceremony. I never wanted the ceremony. I want his name back in the book where it belongs, in red if that's the rule, in black if it isn't. I want the guild to write down that Alden Thornbury was a ranger, and that what he did out there was ranger's work, and that the guild knows it."

The silence afterward had weight, like the pressure at depth. Corliss looked at the Bench. The Bench looked at Fenwick. And Fenwick — old, half-deaf, the keeper of the rolls for longer than anyone could remember — closed his pen, set it down, and did something no one in the hall had ever seen him do.

He opened the book backward.

He turned the pages back through seven years, past the suspensions and the forfeitures and the quiet removals, back to the page where the names of the lost were kept, and he found the line. *Thornbury, Alden.* The red ink had faded to the color of old rust. And then Fenwick turned forward again, to the fresh page, to the line he had just written in Kestrel's name, and beneath it, in his careful clerk's hand, he wrote: *Thornbury, Alden. Ranger. Restored to the rolls by petition of his brother, in recognition of services rendered at the Shallows of the Provision. The guild records that he did not fail. The guild records that it failed him.*

He read it aloud. All of it. His cracked old voice filled the high room, and the dust motes drifted, and Pim put his face in his hands at the window, and Brammel blew his nose like a foghorn, and Kestrel stood very still and let the words land where the stone had been sitting in his chest all morning, and found that the stone had moved.

* * *

Afterward, the hall emptied the way halls do — in eddies, in clusters, in the slow drain of people who wanted to say things and people who wanted to avoid saying things. Corliss caught Kestrel at the door with a handshake that was almost an apology. "The Bench will want to talk about Vane's filing," the old man said. "There will be a hearing. Her license, the Corvane contracts, all of it. You'd have standing to speak."

"I know."

"You don't have to."

"I know that too." Kestrel looked out through the doors at the white glare of the street. "When the hearing comes, I'll say what's true. That's all I've ever had to offer anyone."

Corliss nodded slowly, and then, in the manner of old men who have spent a lifetime being careful, added the one thing that wasn't in any citation: "Your brother's petition — the one you filed two years ago. I argued against it. I want you to know that I was wrong, and that the guild was wrong, and that some of us knew it at the time and were cowards about it." He straightened his collar. "The flats are short on water and shorter on forgiveness, Ranger. Spend both carefully."

Kestrel walked out into the heat.

Osha was waiting by the rail, and she didn't say anything for the length of a street, which for her was a speech. When they reached the corner where the road forked — one way down to the wells, the other up toward the flats — she finally said, "Fenwick's line. *The guild records that it failed him.* I've never heard a guild admit anything. I didn't think they could spell it."

"They can spell it," Kestrel said. "They just don't write it often. Costs them something."

"What does it cost you?"

He considered the question honestly, the way she always made him do. The license sat in his coat pocket, a folded paper where a folded paper had been before, and the difference was that now the paper was his again. Seven years of walking the range on borrowed standing, of taking work under the table, of being *the Thornbury boy* in every room — and it was over, and it had cost a brother, a sinkhole, a wreck at the bottom of the Shallows, and a fire he hadn't lit yet but could feel coming, somewhere ahead, the way you feel weather.

"Nothing," he said. "That's the strange part. It cost me nothing, and I'd have paid anything for it a month ago." He turned up the fork that led toward the flats, and Osha fell in beside him without asking why, because she had stopped asking why sometime around the fourth tide. "I used to think the license was the thing. The name on the paper. Get the paper back and you're a ranger again, whole, the way you were." He shook his head. "But you don't come back the way you were. You come back different, and the paper's just paper, and what you actually got back out there —" he tapped his own chest, once, "— that doesn't come from a Bench of Masters."

"Where does it come from?"

"From doing the work when there's no paper at all. From carrying people out of the water because they're in the water." He looked at her. "You knew that already. You've been doing it for years without anybody's book."

Osha smiled — the small, crooked one, the one she spent like currency because it was rare. "Careful, Thornbury. Talk like that and I'll think you're about to say yes to something."

He didn't answer. Not yet. The question she had asked him in the yard a hundred mornings ago was still open between them, patient as water — *come dive with us, come work, come have a future that isn't a debt* — and he was closer to an answer than he had been, but not there yet, because there was one more thing he had to do first, and it wasn't in Marrowgate.

That night he sat at the table in his room with his father's compass in front of him and the guild's citation beside it, and he read the citation once more, and then he set it aside, because it wasn't the paper he was thinking about. He was thinking about a cairn of stones at a place called Ash Hollow, at the coordinates of a camp that had been waiting seven years for someone to come and stand beside it. Vane's letter was in his coat, unopened since the day it came. He hadn't been ready. He thought maybe he was ready now — ready to ride out alone, no contract, no convoy, no tide to race, just a man and a horse and a bearing.

He opened the letter, and read it through once, and folded it away, and in the morning, before the town was fully awake, he saddled up and took the north road out of Marrowgate, past the wells, past the last fence, out onto the flats where the salt caught the dawn and burned it white.

Ash Hollow was two days' ride. He meant to arrive alone.
