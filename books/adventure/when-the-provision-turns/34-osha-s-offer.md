## 34. Osha's Offer

The rain came to Marrowgate three days after they buried Pell, and the whole town stood out in it like fools, faces upturned, letting the sky spend itself on them. Osha watched from the doorway of Brammel's workshop, arms crossed against the chill, and thought that this was what water was supposed to feel like — a gift falling out of nowhere, asking nothing back. Not something you bled for. Not something you drowned in.

Kestrel Thornbury stood in the middle of the street with his hat off.

He wasn't the only one. Half of Marrowgate had taken their hats off, an old flatland courtesy for the dead, though there was no funeral today, no cairn, no name spoken aloud. Just rain, and people standing in it, and somewhere in the crowd Kestrel with his face turned up like a man listening to a voice only he could hear.

She'd been watching him for six days now, in the way she watched everything — sideways, patient, from below the surface where nobody thought to look. She had learned that habit in the flooded canyons south of the Glass Reach, where the divers who survived were the ones who stopped trying to see through the water and started feeling for it instead. You didn't look at a man to know him. You felt the current he made.

Kestrel's current was strange these days. He moved through Marrowgate like a man walking through a house he no longer lived in — touching nothing, sitting nowhere long, answering when spoken to but a half-beat late, as if translating from some other language. The guild had reinstated his license; she'd heard the criers read it from the steps of the assay office. *Restored for valor in the rescue of the crew of the salvage convoy Vane.* And he had declined the ceremony, asked them to enter a dead man's name in the rolls instead, and walked out before the ink dried. That was the story told in every tavern on Salt Row by the second night, and each telling made him larger and more alone.

Osha knew better than most how much of a legend was just exhaustion wearing good posture.

* * *

She found him the next morning at the drying racks behind the tannery, of all places, mending gear. Her gear, she realized as she came closer — the dive rig she'd hauled out of the Provision's hold, salt-stiff and half-ruined, its bladder patched in three places with waxed canvas cut in neat, careful squares. Someone had worked on it through the night. The someone was sitting cross-legged on the packed earth with a bone needle in his teeth, squinting at a seam like it had insulted his family.

"You don't mend diving rigs," she said.

He took the needle out of his mouth. "I don't do it well."

"That's not what I said."

"No." He looked at the patchwork, at the neat squares of canvas, and something moved behind his eyes — the ghost of the man who'd once laughed at her across a card table in Marrowgate, seven years and one ruined reputation ago. "It's what I said to myself around the third hour."

She sat down across from him without being invited. The tannery smell was thick and ammoniac, the kind of stink that got into your clothes and stayed for weeks, and neither of them minded. People who had spent days inside a drowned shipwreck holding their breath against rot and old death were hard to offend with a little ammonia.

"Your stitching is too tight," she said, taking the rig from him. "Canvas needs slack or it tears along the seam the first time you flex it. Here — watch." She showed him the loose, even pull her mother had taught her, back before her mother taught her depth tables and pressure math instead. "You're treating the cloth like it's skin you're closing over a wound. It's not. It's alive. It moves."

Kestrel watched her hands for a long moment. "Everything I've touched lately," he said quietly, "I've treated like a wound."

The words sat between them. Osha let them. She was good at letting things sit — silence, bodies, grief. In the green dark under the Shallows, panic killed faster than drowning did, and the cure for panic was always the same: stop, breathe, let the thing be what it was.

"I'm starting an outfit," she said.

He glanced up.

"A diving outfit. Proper. Not salvage-on-spec, not contract work for whoever's fleet is biggest that season. A crew that goes down for what's lost and brings it back — people first, cargo second. There are forty wrecks between here and the Silt Gate that nobody's touched because nobody could pay the upfront costs, and families on both shores who've spent years not knowing whether their dead are lying in a hold or washed ashore. I have the skills. Brammel says he'll build me a bell if I can find him investors. What I don't have—" she turned the rig over in her hands, "—is a ranger."

"I'm a ranger again. Officially."

"You're a ranger who almost died twice in one week and walks like a man auditioning for a ghost story." She kept her voice level, unhurried. Diver's patience. "I'm not asking for your license, Kestrel. I'm asking for you. Desert reading, route sense, the way you spotted that causeway under four feet of floodwater when our navigator twins couldn't find north with both hands. And—" she hesitated, then decided plainness was worth the cost, "—and because I dove with you into that hold, and I'd rather dive with you than with anyone I've ever gone down with. That's the whole pitch. No coin in it yet. Probably no coin in it for a year. Brammel's bell will leak and the investors will be vultures and the guild will want a cut of everything. But it would be ours."

She heard herself say *ours* and didn't take it back.

Kestrel was quiet for so long that a tannery worker crossed the yard, saw the two of them sitting among the racks, and quietly went back the way he came, the way people did around open grief.

"My brother chose the fire," Kestrel finally said. "Sealed the chamber rather than let Maddox take the ledger. I keep thinking about that. About what it costs a man to choose like that, and what it gives him." His thumb moved over the compass at his belt — the father's compass, the one thing he'd carried through all of it. "I owe nothing now. Do you understand what that means? Six days free. I haven't been free since I was nineteen years old. And I don't—"

He stopped. Osha waited.

"I don't know how to be owed nothing," he said. "Every road I've ever walked, I walked because someone held the paper on it. I don't know what I choose when nobody's choosing for me. Give me time."

"Time," Osha said, "is the one thing I never argue with. Down there, arguing with time gets you dead." She finished the last seam, bit the thread clean, and tossed the rig into his lap. "Keep that. You mend it, you learn it. A man should know the shape of the thing he might be asked to wear."

He caught it against his chest and looked at her — really looked, the way he hadn't since the Shallows, since the sixth tide swallowed the wreck and they'd stood on the hull watching the water turn slowly green above a grave that would keep its secret.

"Osha," he said. "If I say yes, I'd be saying it to get away from something. If I say no, I'd be saying it to punish myself. Neither one of those is fair to you."

"I know," she said, and stood, and brushed the tannery dust off her knees. "That's why I'm not asking for an answer today. Or this week. I'm asking you to come look at the water tomorrow morning. First light, the east dock. Brammel's going to float the bell frame for the first time, and it's going to list badly to port and we're all going to pretend not to notice. Come watch it with us. That's all. Watch the water, and tell me later what you choose when nobody's choosing for you."

She walked away before he could answer, because diver's discipline also meant knowing when to surface — slow, controlled, leaving the deep thing undisturbed behind you.

* * *

But she did not sleep well, and near dawn she gave up trying and went down to the east dock early, alone, to check the bell frame before the others arrived. The sky was doing that grey-pink thing it did over the flats, the salt haze burning off, the water lying flat as poured glass. The bell frame sat on its mooring lines like a sleeping animal, listing to port exactly as predicted, and Osha stood at the rail looking down at her own reflection and let herself, for one unguarded minute, hope.

Hope was a dangerous thing in her trade. She'd buried two partners who hoped too far past their air. But there it was anyway, rising in her chest like a bubble from deep water, unstoppable, wobbling toward the light: *he'll come.*

She heard his boots on the planks before she saw him. Slow, even, unhurried — the walk of a man who owed nothing and hadn't yet learned what to do with the extra weightlessness. He came and stood beside her at the rail without a word, hat in his hands, and together they watched the sun come up over the flats and the water catch fire with it.

"Brammel's going to be furious about that list," Kestrel said at last.

"Brammel's going to blame the mooring lines. The mooring lines will be fine. It's the frame."

"It's the frame," he agreed.

They stood there while the light spread. Then, from the landward end of the dock, footsteps again — quick ones, several sets — and a runner in guild colors came pounding down the planks with a sealed letter held high, calling Kestrel Thornbury's name, saying there was word from the south, from Ash Hollow way, urgent, marked with Vane's seal.

Osha watched Kestrel take the letter, watched his thumb break the wax, watched the color leave his face as he read whatever Vane had written — and beside her, the bell frame groaned softly on its lines, settling deeper into the water, patient as anything that has learned to wait.
