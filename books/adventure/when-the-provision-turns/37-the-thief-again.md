## 37. The Thief Again

The well crew came to Marrowgate on the back end of the dry season, when the town's cisterns had dropped so low you could hear them echoing, and every conversation in the counting houses turned to the price of a barrel. Kestrel noticed them because he noticed everything now — an old habit sharpened by months of walking the range with nothing owed to anyone. Six wagons, drilling rigs lashed down under canvas, a string of mules that looked better fed than most of Marrowgate's children. A legitimate outfit, guild-stamped, working the eastern aquifer line where the deep water ran close enough to the surface to reach with patience instead of blood.

He was in the Dusty Lantern, or what passed for it — a shaded porch behind Ferro's trading post where the beer was warm and the shade was honest — when the man walked up and stopped three paces away, hat in his hands like a man approaching a grave.

Kestrel knew him before he knew him. The body remembered first: the narrow shoulders, the way the man stood with his weight rolled onto his toes as if always about to run. Then the face caught up — thinner now, sun-scoured, a new scar splitting one eyebrow, but the same face that had bolted across the Saltreach flats at dusk two years ago with a stolen skin of water bouncing against his spine.

"Renn," Kestrel said.

The man flinched at his own name, then managed something that was half smile, half wince. "You remember."

"You stole from me." Kestrel set his cup down. "I don't forget debts. Mine or other people's."

"I know." Renn's hands worked against each other. "That's why I'm here."

* * *

It had been dusk, that evening on the flats. Kestrel had tracked the thief for six hours across ground that gave up nothing — no tracks in the crusted salt, no shadow long enough to read — until instinct did what skill couldn't and told him to stop walking and simply wait by the only shade within a mile, a leaning slab of sandstone that threw a wedge of darkness like a door left open. The thief had come to it the way all desperate things come to water. Kestrel had been sitting inside the shadow when the man arrived, and the look on his face when he found the shade already occupied was something Kestrel had never quite managed to forget: not fear exactly, but the particular exhaustion of a creature that has run out of country.

Kestrel had taken the waterskin from him and weighed it in his hand. Half full. Stolen, by the smell of it, from a caravan cistern — the seal wax still crumbling under the cap.

"You robbed a caravan for half a skin?"

"It was leaking," Renn had said. "I figured — I figured if it was going to waste anyway—"

"And your family? The story you'd have told me if I'd asked?"

The man had gone quiet. There had been no family. There never was. Just a man alone on the flats with a leaky skin and a thirst, doing the arithmetic that desperate men do, where the numbers are always small and always ugly.

Kestrel had handed the skin back. "Drink it slow. And don't steal from caravans — they shoot. Steal from me, I'll just chase you until you're too tired to feel bad about yourself."

The thief had stared at him. "Why?"

Because, Kestrel had thought, I know what it is to owe more than you carry. Because a debt of half a skin of water isn't worth a man's life, and because some part of me — the part my brother would have recognized — needed to believe that was true.

"Go on," he'd said aloud. "Before I change my mind."

Now, two years later, on the porch of the Dusty Lantern, Renn stood in front of him holding a canteen.

It was a good canteen — hammered tin, dented but sound, the strap replaced with new leather. It hung from Renn's fist the way a man holds something he's rehearsed presenting. He thrust it out.

"For you. Filled this morning. From our own well — the one we drilled past Sorrow Bend, forty feet down before we hit sweet water. Cleanest draw in the eastern range." His voice cracked slightly on the last word, and he steadied it. "I've owed you since the flats. Half a skin. I figured — I figured interest."

Kestrel looked at the canteen and didn't take it. Not yet. Something in him wanted to make the moment hold, the way you hold a match to see how long the flame will stand before the wind takes it.

"You joined a well crew," he said.

"Guild-licensed. Brammel trained half our drillers — you'd know him, maybe, the engineer—"

"I know him."

"Well. He says a man who's stolen water knows its weight better than any surveyor. Says thieves make the best well-men, if you can turn them." Renn almost smiled. "Turned out he was right about me. Two years clean. Two years of honest holes in honest ground." He looked down at the canteen. "I keep thinking about that night. You could've taken everything. My boots, my pack, the skin. Nobody would've blamed you. Instead you gave me back the only thing I had and told me to drink it slow."

"That wasn't charity," Kestrel said. "I was tired of carrying it."

"No." Renn shook his head slowly. "Maybe it started that way. But I've thought about it every day since. Every day I lower a bucket into ground that used to be dry, I think — somebody let me keep my water once. Somebody decided I was worth more than what I'd taken." He held the canteen out again, arm straight, steady now. "So this is me deciding you were right."

Kestrel took it.

It was heavier than it should have been, the way filled things are heavier than empty things — not just the water but the meaning of it. He unscrewed the cap and smelled it: cold, mineral, faintly sweet. Deep water. Aquifer water, drawn up through forty feet of stone by honest labor, carried sixty miles to be given away free to a man who hadn't asked for it.

He drank. Slowly. The way you're supposed to.

* * *

They sat on the porch while the light went long and amber over Marrowgate's rooftops. Renn talked — about the well crew, about the eastern line, about the strange quiet that had settled over the water trade since Doss's empire cracked. Word had traveled, the way word does. The Provision was gone. The ledger drowned. Hesk's cipher worthless. Whatever map of the deep aquifer might once have existed now lived only in the memory of a dead man and the conscience of a living one, and neither was for sale.

"They say Corvane pulled out of the eastern range entirely," Renn said. "Maddox himself signed the withdrawal order. Some say he changed after the Shallows. Some say he just got smart." He glanced sideways. "Some say a ranger burned a journal in a counting house rather than let it be searched. That true?"

Kestrel drank again instead of answering.

Renn nodded as if the silence had confirmed everything. "My crew chief heard the whole story from a caravan man who heard it from a diver who was there. Osha, her name. She tells it different every time, but the ending's always the same — the wreck goes down, the ledger stays down, and the ranger walks out owing nothing." He paused. "Is that true? Owing nothing?"

Kestrel turned the canteen in his hands. The tin caught the last of the light.

"I used to think debt was a number," he said. "What you owe, what you're owed. Doss taught me that math. Every favor a lien, every kindness a loan. I spent years believing I'd die on somebody's ledger." He screwed the cap back on. "Then I learned there's another kind of accounting. The kind where you give a thirsty man his water back and never expect to see him again — and then you do see him again, two years later, and he's handing you a well."

"Is that still debt, though?" Renn asked. "If I repaid it?"

Kestrel considered. "No. That's something else. That's just... water moving downhill. Finding its level. Nobody keeps score."

Renn laughed — a short, surprised sound, as if he hadn't expected to laugh on this porch. "Brammel says the same thing about aquifers. Water doesn't keep accounts. It just goes where it can."

"And comes up where it's needed."

"Yeah." Renn stood, brushing off his trousers, suddenly awkward again — the old thief's discomfort with having done a thing openly and well. "Well. I should get back. Wagons move at dawn." He hesitated. "The crew's working the eastern line for the next season, maybe longer. If you ever come through Sorrow Bend—"

"I might."

"There's always water there. For you specifically." He put his hat back on, and for a second Kestrel saw the flat-runner again, the desperate man in the dying light — and saw, underneath, the well-man he'd become. "Thank you, Ranger."

"Not a ranger anymore."

"Thank you, Kestrel, then."

He went. Kestrel watched him cross the street toward the wagon yard, narrow shoulders squared now, weight settled on his heels like a man who had stopped running. At the corner Renn paused, touched his hat brim without turning around, and disappeared into the blue dusk of Marrowgate.

* * *

Kestrel sat a while longer with the canteen between his boots.

The porch emptied around him. Ferro brought another beer without being asked and left it without a word, which was Ferro's way. Somewhere down the street a well crew's mule complained about the night. The first stars came out over the Saltreach, hard and white, the same stars that had watched him track a thief across the flats two years ago and decide, for reasons he couldn't fully explain even then, that mercy was cheaper than cruelty and cost less in the end.

He thought about Alden. He thought about the cairn at Ash Hollow, the stone he'd added, the way grief settles eventually into something you can carry — not lighter, exactly, but shaped to fit. He thought about the compass in his pocket, and the engraving inside it, and the choice he made every morning now when he opened his eyes: north, or wherever else, freely chosen, owing nothing.

Half a skin of water. That was where this whole road had started — not with Vane's contract, not with Doss's leash, not even with Alden's ghost. With a tired man letting a thief keep what he'd stolen, because the alternative was becoming the kind of man who wouldn't.

And look what the water had done in two years. It had moved downhill. It had found its level. It had come back up through forty feet of honest stone and six hundred miles of distance and landed in his hands, cold and sweet, wearing a new leather strap.

Kestrel lifted the canteen to the darkening east — to Ash Hollow, to the Shallows, to whatever graves and wells lay out there in the blue — and drank the rest of it slow.

He was screwing the cap back on when he noticed the paper folded under the strap. Small, grease-spotted, tucked there deliberately. He worked it loose and held it up to the porch lantern.

A hand-drawn sketch. A stretch of the eastern range he knew well — Sorrow Bend, the dry wash below it — except the wash wasn't marked dry. Someone had inked in a vein of blue running beneath it, and beside it, in careful letters:

*Thought you'd want to know the ground remembers where the water is. So do I. — R.*

Below that, a second line, smaller, added as if after hesitation:

*There's a man asking after you in town. Guild coat, but the guild doesn't send men who travel alone. Thought you'd want to know that too.*

Kestrel read it twice. Then he folded the paper along its creases, slid it into his pocket next to the compass, and looked out at the street — at the lamplight, the shadows between buildings, the long empty road north where the flats began.

Somewhere out there, someone was asking after him. And the guild, he knew, didn't send men who traveled alone.

He picked up the canteen, now empty and somehow still heavy, and went to find out why.
