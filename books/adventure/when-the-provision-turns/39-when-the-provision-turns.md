## 39. When the Provision Turns

The patrol left Marrowgate on a morning so clear it felt like an apology.

Kestrel rode point out of habit more than duty. Behind him came six guild rangers in dust-gray leathers, two supply wagons, and a young corporal named Wren who kept glancing at him sideways the way people did when they'd heard stories and hadn't yet decided which ones to believe. He let them look. Seven months of guiding patrols had taught him that silence answered most questions better than words.

The Saltreach opened ahead of them the way it always did — flat to the horizon, white as a scoured bone, shimmering where the heat lifted off the crust. Somewhere under that glare lay the old road, the drowned canyon, the Glass Reach with its sinkhole that had taken Pell and never given him back. Kestrel knew every mile of it now the way a man knows the inside of his own hand: by touch, by memory, by the small scars of it.

"Ranger Thornbury." Corporal Wren drew up beside him, reining her mule wide of his horse's heels. "The Shallows — is it true what they say? That you can see the mast at low tide?"

"You can see it if the tide's low enough and the light's mean enough," Kestrel said. "Most days it's just a rumor the water tells."

"And the wreck itself? The *Provision*?" She lowered her voice, though there was no one within half a mile to hear. "They say it's cursed."

"They say a lot of things." He adjusted his hat brim against the sun. "It's not cursed. It's just honest. It keeps what it was given."

Wren frowned at that, chewing on it, and fell back to the wagons. Kestrel rode on alone at the head of the line, and beneath him the saltflat ticked and settled in the morning heat like something old turning over in its sleep.

He had not meant to take this route. When the guild posted the patrol — a survey run along the southern flats, mapping water claims before the dry season brought the claim-jumpers out — he could have swung east through the Ash Hollow road and spared himself the sight entirely. He had told himself he would. Then he'd stood over the route map in the guild hall with a charcoal pencil in his hand, and his hand had drawn the line south without asking him first.

North is a choice. His father's compass sat in his coat pocket, and some mornings he still took it out and read the engraving like a man reading a letter that arrived late. But south, he was learning, was also a choice. You didn't get to stop choosing just because the choosing hurt.

* * *

They made camp that night at the edge of the Silt Gate, in the lee of a wind-carved bluff that smelled of brine and old stone. The rangers built a low fire, and Kestrel walked the perimeter out of habit, checking the picket lines, listening to the flats breathe.

It was Osha who found him there, or maybe he found her — the distinction had stopped mattering somewhere around the fourth month of her letters arriving irregularly from the coast, each one longer than the last, each one ending with the same plain sentence: *The offer stands.*

She wasn't supposed to be here. That was the first thing he understood, seeing her come up out of the dark with her diving knife at her belt and her hair tied back for work she wasn't doing. She moved like the water had never fully left her, even on dry land — a rolling gait, weight forward, eyes doing the work of ears.

"You're a long way from your outfit," he said.

"I'm a long way from everything. That's the general condition." She stopped an arm's length away, close enough that the firelight caught the salt-scars on her forearms. "I heard a guild patrol was running the southern route. I heard who was leading it."

"People talk too much."

"People do." She glanced back toward the fire, where Wren was laughing at something one of the others said. "Your corporal asked me three questions before I got two words out. She wanted to know if the stories about the Provision were true. I told her the only true story I know about it."

Kestrel waited. He was good at waiting; the desert had taught him that before anything else did.

Osha looked at him for a long moment, and whatever she saw in his face made her voice go quieter. "I told her that a man went down into the green dark and came back up carrying nothing but paper and grief, and that he chose right. That's the whole story. Everything else is decoration."

"She'll be disappointed. Decorations are the part people want."

"People want treasure." Osha shrugged. "You and I both know what's actually down there."

He did know. Some nights he knew it too well. He would wake in his rented room above the farrier's shop with the taste of brine in his mouth and the memory of that flooded hold pressing on his chest like a hand — the sealed door, the barricade built from the inside, the scratch-marks beside it where someone had counted the tides. Alden had counted forty-one before the marks stopped. Forty-one turnings of the vent, twice a day, twenty-some days alive in the dark with a ledger he refused to let anyone carry into the light.

Kestrel had added those numbers up more times than he could count, and they never came out gentler.

"Walk with me," Osha said.

They walked. Past the pickets, past the last of the firelight, out onto the hard-packed salt where the stars came all the way down to the ground and a person could believe the world was bigger than its debts. Her shoulder brushed his once, and neither of them made anything of it, and both of them noticed.

"The outfit took a contract in the Verren shallows," she said after a while. "Salvage, mostly. Honest water work. We're short-handed, and I'm tired of hiring men who flinch at deep dark." A pause. "The offer stands, Kestrel. It's been standing seven months. I'm not going to keep saying it like a debt collector. I'm saying it once more and then I'm going to let it be a door instead of a demand."

"That's the trouble with doors," Kestrel said. "They only work from the side you're standing on."

"Then walk around to my side. It's not complicated." But she smiled when she said it, a small crooked thing, and bumped her shoulder against his deliberately this time. "You don't have to answer tonight. You don't have to answer this year. I just wanted you to hear it out here, under open sky, instead of in ink."

They stood together at the edge of the firelight until the watch called the hour, and then she went to her borrowed bedroll near the rangers' fire — she'd talked her way into the patrol's company two days back with a diver's credentials and a smile that no quartermaster in Marrowgate had ever successfully argued with — and Kestrel stayed out a while longer, looking south.

Tomorrow they'd pass the Shallows. He could feel it coming the way you feel weather in an old wound.

* * *

The tide was wrong for it, and then it wasn't.

They struck camp at gray dawn and made good time across the hardpan, the wagons rattling, the rangers spread in a loose skirmish line because the southern flats still bred claim-jumpers the way stagnant water bred flies. By midmorning the ground had begun its long shallow descent toward the coast, the salt giving way to silt, the silt to mudflats stitched with tidal channels that gleamed like spilled mercury. And there, far off across the water, the Shallows opened before them — a vast drowned basin where the sea came in shallow and warm over the bones of an old harbor road, so clear in places you could see the sand rippling thirty feet down.

Kestrel reined in at the crest of a low rise and looked, because he had promised himself he wouldn't and because he had known all along that he would.

At low tide the Provision's broken mast stood out of the water a mile offshore, black and leaning, a splinter of the ship pointing at nothing. But the tide was climbing now, mid-flood, and the mast had gone under, and there was nothing to see — or should have been nothing.

Except that the sea above the wreck was turning green.

It started as a stain, a discoloration in the blue-green water, the kind a man might blame on weed or shadow. But Kestrel had seen it before, from the hull of Corvane's ship, from the inside of the hold itself, and he knew what it was. The aquifer vent. The living heart of the thing. Twice a day the sea above the wreck turned slowly, luminously green as the vent breathed its mineral light up through the fathoms — the same light that had filled the flooded hold like a held breath while he and Osha swam through the green dark with the rising water at their heels.

"Ranger." Wren had come up beside him, glass raised. "What is that? The water — it's glowing."

"It does that," Kestrel said.

"What causes it?"

He considered the question. There were answers a survey report could hold — mineral seeps, thermal vents, chemical luminescence, all the dry vocabulary the guild loved — and then there was the truth, which fit in no ledger.

"There's a spring under the seabed," he said finally. "Deep one. Comes up through a wreck that went down over it years ago. The water turns when the tide pushes against it. Twice a day, every day, whether anyone's watching or not."

Wren lowered the glass. She was quiet a moment, watching the green spread and deepen across the water like something waking. "Is that where your brother died?"

The question landed clean and unarmored, the way only young people's questions could. Kestrel didn't flinch from it. He had spent seven months learning not to.

"Yes," he said.

"And the ledger? The aquifer map everyone talks about?"

"Still down there. Sealed in its case, in a chamber that floods twice a day." He turned in the saddle to look at her. "His name was Alden. If the guild ever asks you what's in the Shallows, you tell them that. Not the treasure. The name."

Wren held his gaze, and something passed between them that had nothing to do with rank — the look of one person deciding to trust another's grief enough to carry a piece of it. "Alden," she repeated. "I'll remember."

Below them, the patrol had halted at the tideline, watering the stock, and the rangers stood in a loose cluster staring out at the green water. Even from here Kestrel could see the change come over them — the same hush that had fallen over the convoy the day the mast first broke the surface. Awe and terror, Osha had called it, and she'd been right. Some places you didn't need to understand to feel the weight of.

Osha came up the rise and stood at his stirrup. Neither of them spoke for a while. The tide climbed. The green brightened, reached its full strange bloom — the whole basin lit from below like a lamp behind jade — and then, slowly, as the flood peaked and began its long retreat, it started to fade.

"It's turning," Osha said softly. "Every day. All these months, and it never stopped."

"No. It won't stop." Kestrel watched the light dim by degrees, patient as breathing. "That's the thing about it. The Provision doesn't rest. It just turns."

"Like everything else."

"Like everything else."

She was quiet a moment longer. Then: "When I dove with you, in the hold — I never told you what I thought about, down there. While the water was coming." She looked out at the fading green. "I thought about how he must have heard it. Every turning. Twice a day, the sound of it filling up. And he stayed anyway. Long enough to seal the chamber. Long enough to make sure nobody could take what he'd found and turn it into a war." Her jaw tightened. "People talk about courage like it's loud. It isn't. It's a man counting tides in the dark and choosing, forty-one times, not to open the door."

Kestrel's throat worked. He looked at the water until he trusted his voice. "Forty-one," he said. "You counted the marks too."

"Somebody had to remember the number."

"I remember it."

"I know." She put her hand briefly on his boot, a diver's grip, brief and sure, and let go. "That's why the offer stands. Not because I need a diver. Because the world needs more people who remember numbers."

Down at the tideline, the rangers were mounting up. Wren blew a whistle, two notes, and the wagons creaked into motion along the coastal track that skirted the Shallows' western edge. The patrol was moving on. Patrols always moved on; that was the whole shape of the work.

Kestrel sat his horse a moment longer.

The green was nearly gone now, sunk back into ordinary sea-color, and the wreck beneath it had become again what it would be for the rest of his life: a grave, a secret, a choice still holding. Somewhere down in the flooded chamber, Alden's ledger slept in its case, worth a city and worth nothing, guarded by water and by a dead man's stubbornness and by the simple fact that Kestrel had chosen fire over coin and memory over wealth, and would choose it again, and again, every time the question was asked. That was the price of survival. Not paid once. Paid daily, like the tide. And he had learned — walking out of Doss's counting house owing nothing, standing at his brother's cairn with a stone in his hand, opening his father's compass to find *north is a choice* waiting inside like a blessing — that some prices you pay gladly, forever, and call it freedom.

He touched his hat brim to the water. An old ranger's gesture, half salute, half farewell. Nobody saw it except Osha, and she was the only one who needed to.

Then he turned his horse down the slope and rode to catch the patrol, and the Shallows slid away behind them, green and turning, keeping its own counsel.

* * *

They camped that night within sound of the surf, and Kestrel took the last watch.

The others slept. The fire burned down to a red eye. Out past the dunes the sea worked at the shore in its slow, endless arithmetic, adding and subtracting, adding and subtracting, and Kestrel sat wrapped in his coat with his father's compass open in his palm, not reading the needle — the needle was a formality; he knew where north was the way he knew his own heartbeat — but reading the engraving, running his thumb over the worn letters.

*North is a choice.*

Seven months ago he'd walked out of Marrowgate owing nothing, nameless in the way that mattered, free for the first time since he was a boy. He'd thought freedom would feel like arrival. It didn't. It felt like this: a cold watch, an open sky, a compass in the hand, and the whole unmarked distance of a life still to be chosen, one day at a time, with no debt collector and no contract and no ghost holding the leash. Freedom, it turned out, wasn't the absence of weight. It was choosing which weights were yours to carry. Alden's name. Pell's grave at the sinkhole's edge. The green light on the water, twice a day, forever. He carried them all, and they were heavy, and they were his, and no one could take them from him or sell them out from under him, and that was the whole difference between a debt and a vow.

Behind him, a bedroll rustled. Osha came and sat beside him at the fire's edge without a word, shoulder to shoulder, the way she'd stood beside him in the green dark when the water was rising and there was nothing to do but rise with it.

"Verren shallows are three weeks' ride northwest," she said, watching the coals. "Good water work. Poor pay. Excellent company, if I do say so."

"You do say so."

"I do." She nudged him. "No pressure, ranger. I'm just telling you where north is."

Kestrel looked at the compass in his hand, then closed it, and put it away, and looked instead at the dark horizon where the night met the sea — and past it, further than the sea, further than the maps, at all the country he hadn't walked yet.

"All right," he said.

Osha turned her head. "All right?"

"All right. I'll come. On my terms. Work that pays little and owes less." He almost smiled. "And somebody has to keep you honest about the deep dark."

She laughed — a real laugh, low and surprised, the kind the desert rarely earned — and knocked her shoulder into his and left it there, and the fire burned down, and the tide kept its count out in the darkness, and somewhere far to the south the sea above a drowned ship turned slowly green in the dark, a grave, a secret, a choice still holding, whether anyone watched or not.

Kestrel watched until his relief came, and then he slept, and in the morning they broke camp and rode north.
