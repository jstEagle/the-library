## 40. North Is a Choice

The morning Kestrel signed on with Osha's outfit, the contract was written on the back of a supply manifest, in pencil, and it fit in four lines.

*One diver's tender and surface watch. Pay: shares of salvage, split even when there is any. Board and water included. No term. Either party may walk.*

Osha slid it across the table of the Salt Lantern with a stub of pencil worn down to the wood. "That's it," she said. "Read it anyway."

He read it. He read it twice, because for seven years he had not signed anything that short. Contracts in Marrowgate came with riders and clauses, with forfeiture schedules and interest compounded by the tide, with language designed by men like Doss to make sure that whatever you thought you were agreeing to, you were actually agreeing to less. This was four lines and a signature line, and the last clause — *either party may walk* — sat at the bottom like a door left open.

"You could have asked for more," Osha said. "After what you did on the Provision run. After Maddox. The guild would back you now. You know that."

"The guild backed me once already." He set the pencil down. "I don't want backing. I want work."

"That's a strange thing for a man to want."

"It's the only thing I've ever wanted straight." He signed. *K. Thornbury*, in his father's cramped hand, because he had never learned to write any other way. "No debt. No lien. No man holding paper over my name. That's the whole price, Osha. You're getting me cheap and clean."

She looked at the signature for a long moment, then folded the manifest into quarters and put it inside her coat, over her heart, which was where she kept things that mattered. "Cheap," she said. "We'll see about clean."

* * *

The outfit worked out of a converted barge called the *Lantern's Sister*, moored in the shallows off the drowned road east of Marrowgate, where the old highway sank beneath green water and the pilings of some pre-war bridge still stood like the ribs of something patient. There were six of them. Osha ran the dives. Two sisters named Ferro and Quill handled the winches and the air lines. A taciturn old salt called Marnie cooked and kept the books and had opinions about both. And Kestrel did what Kestrel had always done best: he watched the horizon, read the weather, and told people when the land — or the water over it — was about to turn against them.

It paid little. Some weeks it paid nothing at all, when the tides were wrong or the sites were picked clean or the guild fees ate the margin whole. On those weeks they ate Marnie's saltfish stew and complained about it in the comfortable way of people who are complaining about nothing, and Kestrel lay on deck at night under a sky so crowded with stars it looked like spilled coin, and he counted what he owed.

Nothing.

He counted it again most nights, the way a man who has been hungry keeps touching his belt after the meal, checking that it holds. Nothing owed to Doss, whose empire had cracked open that winter like river ice and whose counting house stood shuttered now behind guild seals. Nothing owed to Vane, who had sailed south with her confession filed and her name half-ruined and her head, by all reports, held high. Nothing owed to Corvane, to Hesk, to the memory of Pell, to the ghost of Alden — except the stone he had added to the cairn at Ash Hollow, and that was a debt he intended to carry all his life, gladly, the way you carry a scar.

What he had instead was small and strange and took getting used to: wages. Four coppers a day when the diving was good. A bunk that was his. A canteen — the thief's canteen, returned full, its dented flank polished bright — hanging by his hammock, filled every morning out of habit, though he was never thirsty anymore. Not the way he had been.

He found, in those first months, that poverty without debt was a different animal entirely from poverty with it. Poor and owing, he had been a hunted thing, running a track laid down by other men. Poor and free, he was just a man with empty pockets and a full morning, and the emptiness didn't gnaw. It echoed. It had room in it. He was still learning to live in that room.

* * *

In the spring the outfit took a contract salvaging a grain hauler off the Silt Gate — the same waters where Corvane had once overtaken them, where the lead wagon had gone down in a skirmish that felt, now, like it had happened to someone else. The wreck was shallow and mean, full of silt and swollen grain and one unlucky cat that had apparently lived aboard until the very end; they raised it mewing and furious and Osha kept it, naming it Ledger, because she said every outfit needed one thing aboard that couldn't be reasoned with.

Kestrel stood surface watch while Osha dove, tending her line, watching the bubbles rise silver through the green light. Below him, forty feet down, the world went quiet and slow, and he imagined her down there in it, moving through the drowned road's broken arches, and he felt something he did not have a name for yet but suspected might be peace.

"Thornbury." Marnie, at the rail, squinting north. "You see that?"

He saw it. Dust, far off across the flats — a single traveler's plume, thin and unhurried, the kind of dust that meant one rider and no escort. Another figure coming out of the deep interior, where the maps gave up and the salt took everything. Another story walking toward them out of nowhere, the way stories always did.

"Rider," he said. "Alone. Coming easy."

"Guild?"

"Guild rides in pairs." He watched a moment longer. "Trader, maybe. Or a pilgrim. Or somebody running from something slower than we are."

Marnie grunted and went back to her stew. But Kestrel stayed at the rail, and the dust grew, and by late afternoon he could make out the shape of a lean desert horse and a rider in a patched traveling cloak, and something about the set of the rider's shoulders — the particular economy of a person who has learned to carry everything they own close to their body — made him think of a younger version of himself, ten years ago, riding into Marrowgate with a ranger's license and a father's compass and no idea what either would cost him.

The rider drew rein at the shore opposite the barge and cupped their hands and called across the water. "Is this Osha's outfit? The divers?"

Osha surfaced at the sound of voices, treading water beside the hull, pushing back her hood. "Depends who's asking!"

"A woman named Vane said I'd find work here." The voice was young. Tired. Honest in the way of someone too exhausted to lie. "She said — she said you take people who need a start."

Kestrel looked at Osha. Osha looked at Kestrel, water streaming from her jaw, and grinned.

"Well?" she called back to the shore. "Can you cook? Because Marnie's stew is a crime and we've suffered long enough."

* * *

They took the new hand aboard that evening. Her name was Sera; she was nineteen, and she had walked out of the Glass Reach alone, which by itself should have been recommendation enough, and she had lost a brother to the water wars, which she mentioned only once, quietly, the way people mention things that are load-bearing. Kestrel showed her the tender's ropes and the watch rotations and where the safe water was kept, and when she asked him how long he'd been with the outfit, he had to stop and count.

"Little over a year," he said, and was surprised at how easily it came out. How little it weighed.

"And before that?"

"Before that I owed a lot of people a lot of things." He coiled a line, loop over loop, the motion as familiar as breathing. "Now I don't."

Sera watched him coil the rope with the hungry attention of someone learning a trade from the ground up. "Vane said you were the one who burned the Provision ledger. She said the aquifer map is at the bottom of the Shallows because of you. Half the border towns think you're a fool. The other half are building shrines."

"Both halves are wrong." He hung the coiled line on its peg. "There wasn't anything to burn but paper. What mattered was already at the bottom, and it stays there, and that's the whole story."

"And your brother?"

Kestrel paused. Out past the breakwater, the sun was going down over the flats in the enormous unhurried way it had, laying gold across the salt until the whole world looked gilded and temporary. Somewhere out there was Ash Hollow, and the cairn, and the stone he had added. Somewhere out there was the Shallows, where at high tide the sea turned slowly green above a wreck that held a grave, a secret, and a choice, all three still holding, all three his.

"My brother chose what he chose," Kestrel said. "I'm choosing mine."

Sera nodded slowly, as if that answered something, and maybe it did.

* * *

Summer came, and with it the long working days, and Kestrel learned the rhythms of a life that belonged to no one's ledger. He learned that Osha hummed when she was nervous underwater and that Ferro cheated at cards and that Quill let her. He learned that Marnie's stew improved dramatically if you complimented it first, which was blackmail, but survivable blackmail, the kind you could live with. He learned the tide tables of the drowned road the way he had once learned the trails of the Saltreach — not from charts but from the smell of the wind and the color of the water and the behavior of birds — and when the guild patrol came through in autumn asking for a guide to the Shallows, the outfit's vote was unanimous before he'd even finished saying he'd do it.

He guided them out and back. At high tide, standing on the patrol skiff, he pointed to where the sea above the wreck turned slowly green with the vent's light, and the young rangers stared at it in silence, and none of them asked him to explain, because some things explain themselves if you're willing to look.

On the ride home, the youngest ranger — a boy with a new license and an old family name, some cousin of a cousin of the Thornburys' old neighbors — rode up alongside him.

"They say the guild's going to reopen the deep survey," the boy said. "South of the Reach. Big contract. Big pay. They'd want a man like you leading it."

"Would they."

"Wouldn't you want that? A job like that could set you up for life."

Kestrel looked at the boy for a moment — at the hunger in him, the good clean hunger of someone who hadn't yet learned what big contracts cost — and he found he wasn't bitter about it. That surprised him most of all. He had expected bitterness to be permanent, the way debt had seemed permanent. Neither was.

"I wanted it once," he said. "Then I got it, in a manner of speaking. Now I've got a bunk and a boat and a cat I can't reason with, and I owe nobody nothing." He tipped his hat brim down against the low sun. "You'll learn the difference someday. Or you won't, and you'll be rich. Either way, kid, keep your own compass."

* * *

Winter. The *Lantern's Sister* rode at anchor through a week of storms, and on the third night, with rain hammering the deck and Ledger the cat refusing all shelter on principle, Kestrel sat in the cabin with the lamp turned low and opened his father's compass.

He did this sometimes now. It had taken him more than a year to be able to do it at all — for seven years the compass had been collateral, a thing valued rather than loved, and even after Doss fell he had kept it shut, the way you keep a letter unopened when you're afraid of what it says. But one night in the spring he had pried the brass case open with a knife tip, and inside, scratched into the underside of the lid in his father's small hard hand, he had found the engraving:

*NORTH IS A CHOICE.*

He had sat with it a long time that first night. His father had been a ranger too, and a hard one, and Kestrel had spent his whole life assuming the compass was about certainty — about knowing where true north lay and holding to it. But his father had chosen his words carefully, the way careful men do, and the words weren't *north is true*. They were *north is a choice*. As if the old man had known, somewhere in his flinty heart, that direction was never something you found. It was something you decided. Every morning. Over and over. Against the pull of every debt and fear and easy road that wanted you going some other way.

Kestrel closed the compass now and held it in his palm and listened to the rain.

Through the cabin's fogged glass he could see the deck lamp swaying, and Osha's silhouette moving along the rail, checking lines in the storm because she never trusted anyone else to check them right. Tomorrow the weather would clear. Next week there was a small contract, a sunken chapel two miles up the coast, poor pickings probably, a few relics and a lot of mud. The month after that, nothing scheduled at all, which Marnie fretted over and Kestrel did not, because he had learned that the unscheduled part was not the gap in a life. It was the life.

A knock. Osha came in shaking off rain, hair plastered flat, grinning like the storm was a personal gift to her.

"Line's holding. Cat's an idiot. Stew's on." She dropped onto the bench across from him, dripping, and nodded at the compass in his hand. "You always look at that thing like it's going to argue with you."

"Sometimes it does."

"What's it say tonight?"

Kestrel turned it over once in his palm. Brass, worn smooth, older than both of them, holding steady through every storm it had ever been carried into — not because it was strong, but because it knew what it pointed at.

"Same thing it always says," he said. "Same thing I finally do."

* * *

Spring again. A year and a season since the signing on the back of a manifest.

They were beached at Marrowgate for reprovisioning, and Kestrel walked the town in the early morning the way he liked to, before the heat, when the streets smelled of bread and brine and the market stalls were just waking. He passed the old counting house, still sealed, its windows blank. He passed the Salt Lantern, where a different creditor's signs now hung, and kept walking. He passed the well where the water-thief — Jem, his name was Jem, and he worked a legitimate crew now and stood straighter than he used to — gave him a nod across the square, man to man, debt long closed.

At the outfitter's he bought nothing he needed and one thing he didn't: a new hat, wide-brimmed, salt-white, to replace the old one that the winter storms had finally claimed. The outfitter wrapped it in brown paper and Kestrel carried it under his arm like a parcel, feeling faintly ridiculous and entirely content.

He was halfway back to the docks when he saw the dust.

It rose off the northern flats beyond the town walls — a single plume, thin and vertical in the still air, the signature of one traveler walking, not riding. Coming south, toward Marrowgate, toward the water, toward whatever waited for them here. Kestrel stopped at the wall gate and watched it for a while, the way he had learned to watch everything out here: patiently, without needing it to be anything yet.

Another traveler. Another story, walking toward them out of the wide empty world, carrying whatever they carried — debts, maybe, or griefs, or a compass of their own still shut. Maybe they'd need work. Maybe they'd need a guide. Maybe they'd need what Kestrel had needed, once, and gotten from strangers on a barge: four lines on a manifest, a bunk, and a door left open.

Behind him, from the direction of the docks, Osha's voice carried up the street, calling his name — breakfast, and a tide to catch, and a day of small honest work waiting on the water.

Ahead of him, the dust rose against the pale northern sky.

Kestrel Thornbury settled the paper-wrapped hat under his arm, touched two fingers to its brim all the same — a greeting to the stranger, to the road, to whatever came next — and turned, and walked north toward the harbor, owing nothing, owing no one, free.
