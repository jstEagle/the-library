## 1. The Debt Collector Smiles

The morning the debt collector came to the *Provision*, Sebastian Villalobos was frying eggs in a pan that had seen better decades, and he would spend a long time afterward wondering whether things might have gone differently if he'd had a better breakfast. Not for himself — for the visitor. There was something about a man who ate well that made him harder to lie to, and Sebastian intended to do a great deal of lying before noon.

The shack sat on stilts at the end of Pier Nine, leaning into the harbor wind like an old sailor telling a story he didn't quite believe anymore. It smelled of diesel, tar, and Bo's engine degreaser, which Bo insisted was coffee. Through the open door Sebastian could see the *Provision* herself riding at her mooring, forty feet of stubborn steel hull and mismatched rigging, storm-chaser by trade, debtor by circumstance. She looked good in the early light. She always did. It was one of her more expensive talents.

He heard the car before he saw it — too clean an engine for this pier, where everything idled with a cough. Then the footsteps on the planking: unhurried, polished shoes, each step placed like a man who had never once worried about a loose board.

Sebastian flipped the eggs. He did not turn around.

"Villalobos." The voice arrived warm as bathwater. "You look terrible."

"I look like a man who works for a living, Marisol," Sebastian said, still facing the stove. "You wouldn't recognize it."

Marisol Delgado — no relation to the syndicate that everyone assumed, which she enjoyed correcting and never denied — stepped into the doorway and filled it the way weather fills a window. She was a broad, cheerful woman in a linen suit the color of fresh money, carrying a leather folder under one arm and a paper cup of coffee she had clearly purchased somewhere civilized. Behind her, at a respectful distance, stood two of her people: identical men with identical expressions, built like breakwaters, wearing matching gray jackets despite the heat.

"Four eggs?" Marisol said, peering past him at the pan. "For you alone? No wonder you're broke."

"It's called morale, Marisol. Look it up."

She laughed — a real laugh, big and unguarded, which was the worst thing about her. A man could hate a cold enforcer. Nobody knew what to do with one who seemed genuinely delighted to see them.

Sebastian plated the eggs, set four forks on the table out of pure spite, and gestured at the only chair worth sitting in. "Sit. You're letting the flies in."

"Don't mind if I do." She settled into the chair, crossed her ankles, and laid the leather folder on the table between them with the care of a woman setting down a sleeping cat. "Is the crew around? I'd hate to say anything twice."

And there it was. Sebastian kept his face pleasant and his heart doing something slow and heavy against his ribs, like an anchor dragging bottom.

Because they were all there, of course. They were always there. Priya was at the chart table by the window, ostensibly correcting tide tables, actually listening with the total stillness she reserved for weather systems and threats. Kip was on the porch steps outside, supposedly re-splicing a line that had been perfectly spliced since Tuesday, close enough to hear every word through the screen. And Bo was underneath the shack itself, working on the bilge pump through the hatch, which meant only the soles of his boots were visible from the porch, sticking out into the morning like a man who had given up on dignity entirely.

Nobody in this family had ever successfully pretended not to be eavesdropping. It was their single greatest tradition.

"They're around," Sebastian said. "Say what you came to say."

"All business." Marisol sighed happily and opened the folder. "I love that about you. Most people want to chat first. Tell me about their kids. Show me photographs of their boat." She glanced out the door at the *Provision*. "Lovely boat, by the way. Old girl. Good bones. You can tell she's been loved."

"She has been."

"That's going to make this awkward, then."

Sebastian sat down across from her, folded his hands, and gave her the smile he used on charter clients who wanted refunds. "Marisol. It's a beautiful morning. The fish are biting. Say your piece and let me finish my breakfast while it's hot."

"Thirty days."

The word landed in the room and just — stayed there. Thirty days. Sebastian heard it the way you hear a hull crack: not loud, but final.

"The principal," Marisol continued, tapping the folder with one manicured finger, "stands at eighty-two thousand, four hundred and six dollars. The six dollars bothers Mr. Delgado personally; he's asked me to mention it. Interest accrues weekly. In thirty days — that's the thirtieth, a Friday, he checked — either the full amount is paid, or ownership of the vessel transfers to satisfy the debt." She smiled wider. "And if the vessel isn't worth enough — and I'm told she isn't, no offense, the survey was brutal — then we discuss alternative arrangements."

"Alternative arrangements," Sebastian repeated.

"Fingers are traditional," Marisol said cheerfully. "But Mr. Delgado is flexible. He's a modern man. He'd take teeth."

From beneath the shack, Bo's boots twitched. From the porch steps came the small, distinctive sound of Kip snapping a splice he'd already finished, which meant he'd stopped pretending to work, which meant in about ninety seconds someone was going to have to physically restrain him.

Sebastian leaned back and laughed. He made it a good laugh — big, easy, the laugh of a man who found the whole thing mildly amusing. Inside, behind the laugh, he was doing arithmetic he'd been doing for eleven months, and the numbers hadn't improved once.

Eighty-two thousand. The *Provision*'s last three charters had paid forty-one combined, and thirty-eight of that had gone straight back out — fuel, parts, Priya's new charts, Bo's compressor, Kip's mother's roof. Storm-chasing paid in adrenaline and reputation, and reputation, it turned out, was not legal tender.

"You know what my problem is, Marisol?" he said, pouring coffee into the chipped mug he kept for guests, because hospitality was a weapon too. "Delgado lends money to people who chase hurricanes for a living, and then acts surprised when the returns are weather-dependent."

"He doesn't act surprised," Marisol said, accepting the mug. "He acts disappointed. It's worse. Thank you." She sipped, made a face at the dockside coffee, and drank it anyway, which Sebastian chose to interpret as respect. "Here's the thing, Sebastian. I like you. Genuinely. You're the only client who's ever offered me breakfast. So I'm going to tell you something off the record."

"Off the record. From the syndicate's collections department."

"I'm the syndicate's *relationships* department," she corrected, wounded. "Collections is a different van. Uglier men. Anyway. Off the record: Mr. Delgado doesn't actually want your boat. Your boat is a rounding error to him. What he wants—" she tapped the folder again, twice, "—is for other people to watch what happens when he doesn't get paid. You understand? You're not a debt, Sebastian. You're a lesson. Which means the deadline is real, and the fingers are real, but also—" she leaned forward, and for one moment the cheerfulness thinned and he saw the actual machinery underneath, "—it means if you found eighty-two thousand dollars in the next month, from anywhere, he'd take it and be delighted, and nobody would ever speak of this again. He's not sentimental. But he's not stupid either. Money is money."

"And if the money comes from somewhere he doesn't like?"

"Then it isn't money," Marisol said pleasantly, "it's a problem."

The wind shifted outside. Down at the mooring, the *Provision* swung gently on her line, and Sebastian watched her through the window and felt the old, stupid, magnificent ache he always felt looking at her — pride first, and terror right behind it, holding its coat.

Thirty days. Eighty-two thousand dollars. He had a crew of four, a boat held together with argument and epoxy, and a phone book full of people who owed him favors, none of whom owed him that much.

"So," Marisol said, draining the coffee and standing, brushing nothing off her immaculate suit. "I'll leave the paperwork. Read it or don't. Everyone reads it eventually." She paused at the door and turned back, and her expression softened into something almost kind. "One more thing, off the record, free of charge. You've got people sniffing around the harbor asking after you. Salvage interests. Big outfit, Corvus flag on their tenders. They're asking about your crew, your range, your insurance history." Her eyes flicked to the *Provision*, then back. "People don't ask those questions unless they're planning to need you, or planning to replace you. Either way — thirty days, Sebastian. Don't make me come back with the uglier van."

She let herself out. The breakwater twins followed. The too-clean car pulled away down the pier, and the harbor went back to being a harbor — gulls, halyards clinking, somebody two slips down swearing at an outboard motor.

Silence held the shack for exactly four seconds.

Then Bo's voice floated up through the floorboards, muffled and calm as ever: "So are we selling my surgery fund, or Kip's, or should we just draw straws?"

"We're not selling anybody's anything," Sebastian said.

"Eighty-two thousand," Priya said from the chart table, without looking up. She'd heard every word, obviously, and had apparently spent the entire conversation running figures, because Priya ran figures the way other people breathed. "After fuel, moorage, and the interest curve, we'd need a job netting ninety-one minimum inside twenty-six days, accounting for a weather margin."

"Thank you, Priya."

"There's no weather margin in that number, Sebastian. There's barely a *number* in that number."

The porch steps creaked, and Kip came through the door with the spliced coil over his shoulder and murder in his young face. Twenty-three years old, built like a spring, Tomás's shadow in more ways than he knew. "I say we go find this Delgado and explain to him, personally, with visuals, why his math is wrong."

"Sit down, Kip."

"He threatened your *fingers*, Skipper."

"He threatened everybody's fingers. It's a package deal. Sit." Kip sat, vibrating. Sebastian looked around the shack — at Priya's steady dark eyes, at Bo emerging from the hatch below wiping grease on a rag, at Kip coiled on the bench — and felt the weight settle onto his shoulders where it lived, familiar as a harness. "Nobody's losing fingers. Nobody's losing the boat. We've got thirty days and the best storm crew on this coast. We've survived worse than arithmetic."

"Name one time," said Priya.

"The hurricane off Cabo."

"We lost the mast."

"We survived the hurricane off Cabo," Sebastian repeated firmly. "The mast is a detail."

Bo snorted. Even Kip's jaw loosened half a degree. That was the trick of it, the only trick Sebastian had ever really known: keep them laughing, keep them rowing in the same direction, and the fear stays in the hold where it belongs. He wasn't sure it was leadership. Some nights he suspected it was just another kind of drowning, done loudly enough that nobody noticed.

Priya turned back to her charts, and her pen stopped moving. That was how Sebastian knew something was coming — Priya's pen never stopped moving unless it was making room for something bigger.

"There is one number," she said slowly, "that solves this. One job. If anyone were foolish enough to offer it."

The shack went quiet in a different way.

"No," Sebastian said.

"You don't even know what—"

"There's one wreck on this coast that pays like that, and we swore off it, and the answer is no."

Kip had gone very still. "The Meridian Star," he said quietly.

Nobody answered him. Six years sat down at the table with them, uninvited, taking up the whole bench. Outside, the gulls wheeled over Pier Nine, and the *Provision* tugged at her mooring like she could smell the deep water from here.

And that was when Bo, wiping his hands forever on the same rag, said the thing that started everything:

"Somebody's offering it. That's why the Corvus boats are asking questions. Somebody's already shopping for a crew crazy enough to dive the Verge." He looked up at Sebastian, mild and terrible. "Question is whether they hire us or hire *over* us."

* * *

They voted at sunset, which was not yet, though Sebastian could feel the vote coming the way Priya felt weather — pressure dropping, air going strange. For now there was dinner: rice and beans and the last of the good sausage, eaten on the porch with feet up on the rail, watching the light go gold and then copper over the breakwater. Marisol's folder lay closed on the table between the salt shaker and the beer, radiating quiet menace like a sleeping cat after all.

Kip ate fast and stared at the water. Bo ate slowly and stared at nothing. Priya ate precisely and stared at her charts, which she had brought onto the porch, which was against all porch law.

Sebastian ate and stared at the folder, and thought about Tomás, and did not read Tomás's logbook, which was in the drawer under the chart table where it had been for six years, and which he had never once opened, and which everyone knew was there, and which no one had ever mentioned aloud.

"You know what I keep thinking," Kip said finally, to the water. "Tomás would've laughed at that woman. He'd have made her eat with us. Fed her the good sausage. By the time she left she'd have forgotten why she came."

"He would have," Bo agreed softly.

"He'd have hated Delgado's lot worse than any of us," Kip went on, and his voice had an edge now, the grief-edge, the one that cut whoever held it. "Six years they've been buying up this harbor. Buying wrecks. Buying *people*. And we just sit here and pay them rent on our own lives."

"Kip," Sebastian said.

"No, Skipper, I'm serious. I'm serious for once in my life. We swore off the Verge because of him, fine. But we've been swearing off everything since. Every deep-water job, every salvage contract worth the name — 'too risky,' 'bad water,' 'not after what happened.' We've been hiding in plain sight for six years and calling it loyalty, and meanwhile the world got bought out from under us, and now a smiling woman comes to take our boat and our *hands*, and we're going to sit here eating sausage?"

The porch was silent. Far out past the breakwater, the evening fishing fleet was coming home, lights coming on one by one, little constellations returning to harbor.

"What would you have us do?" Priya asked, not unkindly.

"I don't know," Kip said. "Something honest. Something *his*. Something that costs."

Sebastian looked at the boy — at Tomás's ghost wearing a young man's shoulders — and then at the folder, and then at the water, where the last light was going out of the Verge's direction, west and south, past the reef, past the grave marker, out to where a dead ship held a dead man's last hour in a black box at the bottom of a drowned labyrinth.

Tomorrow, he suspected, someone was going to knock on this door with a contract. Someone flush, and smiling, and carrying old history in a leather folder of her own.

He intended to say no.

He gave himself until tomorrow to mean it.
