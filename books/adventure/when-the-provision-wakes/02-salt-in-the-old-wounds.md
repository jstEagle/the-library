## 2. Salt in the Old Wounds

Imogen Reyes had rehearsed this conversation eleven times on the flight over, and she had abandoned every version of it by the time her heels hit the boards of Pier Nine.

The rehearsal problem was simple: Sebastian Villalobos did not respond to scripts. He responded to weather. You had to arrive at him the way a front arrives at a coastline — you read the pressure, you adjusted, and if he turned ugly, you got wet and kept talking. Six years of racing against him across three oceans had taught her that much. Six years of losing to him, mostly, which was its own kind of education.

The *Provision* sat at the end of the pier like an old dog that had been left out in the rain too long and refused to admit it. Imogen remembered her in better days — the year Sebastian had repainted her hull a blue so loud it could be seen from orbit, and Priya had named the color "Regret" before the paint was dry. The Regret had faded now to something closer to apology. Her rails needed sanding. Her name was peeling at the V. But her engine cover was open, and Bo was elbow-deep in it, humming, and there was still light coming off the boat the way light comes off a thing that is loved badly but honestly.

Imogen stood at the top of the gangway for a moment longer than pride allowed, then walked down anyway.

Bo saw her first. His head came up out of the engine like a gopher checking for hawks, and his face did something complicated — surprise, then arithmetic, then a kind of weary amusement, as if she were a bill he'd been expecting but had hoped would arrive after payday.

"Well," he said. "Hell's gotten nicer shoes."

"Hello, Bo."

"Don't 'hello, Bo' me." He wiped his hands on a rag that had given up being a rag sometime last season. "You've got syndicate stitching on that jacket. I can see it from here. That's Delgado money walking down my pier."

"Your pier?"

"The pier I'm standing on. Everything's mine when I'm holding a wrench." He jerked his chin toward the cabin. "He's below. Counting what we don't have, probably. He does it Tuesdays."

"It's Thursday."

"He's behind schedule."

She found Sebastian in the galley, exactly where grief and debt had always kept him: sitting at the little bolted-down table with a ledger open in front of him and a cup of coffee gone cold beside it, staring at numbers as though they might, under sufficient scrutiny, confess to being smaller.

He looked up. And here was the thing Imogen had not rehearsed for — not the anger. She'd budgeted generously for anger. It was the half-second before the anger, the flicker of something almost like gladness, the way a man looks up at an old scar and finds it aching in familiar weather. Then the shutters came down, board by board, and he was the Sebastian everyone else got: arms crossed, jaw set, eyes doing their lighthouse impression.

"No," he said.

"I haven't said anything yet."

"You're standing in my galley wearing forty thousand dollars of jacket, and you came alone, which means you wanted privacy, which means it's either an apology or a job, and you've never once apologized in your life. So it's a job. No."

Imogen pulled out the opposite bench and sat down uninvited, because sitting down uninvited was the only way anyone had ever gotten anywhere with Sebastian Villalobos. "The coffee's cold."

"The coffee's been cold since Tuesday. We're between paydays."

"That's why I'm here."

"That's why *you're* here," Sebastian said. "Why you're *here* is a longer story, and I already told you how it ends."

She set the envelope on the table between them. Heavy paper. Syndicate watermark, subtle as a slap. She watched him look at it the way a man looks at a snake he knows personally.

"Meridian Star," she said. "Salvage contract. Black box recovery, full wreck survey, hazard pay, completion bonus. The bonus alone clears your books twice over."

The galley went very quiet. Somewhere above them, Bo dropped a wrench, which was Bo's way of eavesdropping loudly.

Sebastian didn't touch the envelope. He looked at it the way you look at a grave marker — briefly, and then away, and then back, because you can't help it. "You have some nerve."

"I have some money," Imogen said. "The nerve came free."

"Get out of my boat."

"It's not your boat. It's the bank's boat, and the bank sold your paper to Delgado six weeks ago, and Delgado doesn't do patience, Sebastian. They do fingers." She kept her voice level, almost gentle, and hated herself for how well she knew how to do gentle-as-a-weapon. "I've seen their ledger. Your crew is on page nine. There are four names and one boat, and the interest is eating the boat faster than the rust is."

"You've seen our ledger?" He laughed, and it was an ugly sound, one she'd never heard him make before, and she catalogued it with the rest of her regrets. "Six years you spend trying to beat us on the water, and now you're reading our mail for the people who own us. That's quite a career arc, Imogen."

"Yes," she said. "It is."

That stopped him. She'd meant it to. Honesty was the one blade she had left that hadn't dulled, and she was going to use it even though it cut her hand worse than his.

"I'm not proud of who signs my checks," she said. "But I am the only person in this whole rotten arrangement who's telling you the truth, so let me finish telling it. The Meridian Star went down in the Coral Verge six years ago with a black box that somebody wants very badly never to be read. Delgado wants it recovered quietly. The Corvus fleet is already underway with a syndicate cutter escort and enough dive gear to outfit a navy. If they get it first, you don't exist. If you get it first, you eat for a decade. Those are the only two options on the menu, and you weren't consulted about either, because poor men rarely are."

"And Tomás?" Sebastian said.

There it was. She had known it was coming the way you know a wave is coming — you see the trough first, the water pulling back, and then it takes the beach out from under you.

"What about him?" she asked, and made her voice flat, because flat was armor.

"You know what about him." Sebastian leaned forward, and now the anger was fully arrived, and underneath it, worse, the grief, the enormous patient grief of a man who had spent six years carrying a body that wouldn't stay buried. "We swore off those waters. We swore it over his skiff, on that reef, with his mother standing right there. You were *there*, Imogen. You sent flowers. Plastic ones, because you were broke then, and somehow that was the more honest gesture. And now you walk down my gangway with syndicate stitching on your sleeves and ask me to sail back to the exact spot where—"

"I know where he died."

"Do you?" Sebastian's voice cracked on it, just slightly, and he covered it by standing up, which put the little galley lamp behind him and turned him into a silhouette with a temper. "Because the woman I raced against — the woman who once tacked through a squall line so tight it should have killed us both just to prove a point — that woman would never take syndicate money to go dig around a wreck she helped mourn. So either she's dead, or I never knew her, or—" he stopped. Recalibrated. Sat back down slowly, like a man remembering his knees. "Or something happened to you too."

Imogen looked at the table. At the ledger with its columns of red. At the cold coffee with its skin of oil, because good coffee was also between paydays.

"A lot of things happened to me," she said. "None of them are in the contract."

"Everything's in the contract. That's what contracts are." He pushed the envelope back across the table, an inch, two inches, until it touched her knuckles. "No."

"Sebastian—"

"*No.* Take your envelope, take your syndicate, take your Corvus fleet, and go find some other broken crew to dig up other people's dead. We're not for sale."

"You're already sold," Imogen said quietly. "That's the whole point. I'm the only one offering to buy you *back*."

Above them, deliberately loud, Bo began hammering something that had not previously required hammering.

Sebastian rubbed his face with both hands, and for a moment — just a moment — she saw the boy he'd been before any of it, the one who'd once traded her his last orange for her last dry sock halfway through a race neither of them finished, and they'd laughed about it for a year afterward. She held onto that image the way you hold onto a rail in rough water.

"Here's what I remember," she said, softer now. "I remember a crew that took a contract nobody else would touch because an old fisherman's granddaughter needed passage to a hospital. I remember Kip diving into oil-slick water to pull a stranger's dog off a sinking barge. I remember you giving away your prize money — your *prize money*, Sebastian, the only money you ever had — to Tomás's mother, and telling her it was insurance payout, and letting her believe it." She swallowed. "I remember who you are. And I am asking you to be that person one more time, in the worst place in the ocean, for the worst client imaginable, because the alternative is watching Delgado take this boat apart bolt by bolt and leave four of the best sailors I've ever raced against with nothing but their hands and their pride, and pride doesn't float."

"You don't get to use them against me."

"I don't get to use anything against you. That's the trick of it. They're already against you. Debt doesn't need my help." She stood, tucked the envelope under her arm — not leaving it, not yet, because Sebastian Villalobos respected nothing that came free — and paused at the foot of the ladder. "Six tides. That's when the monsoon makes landfall, and that's when the Corvus stops racing you and starts burying you. You've got until the tide turns tomorrow night. I'll be at the Harbormaster's office. Even if the answer's no, come tell me no in person. You owe me that much."

"I owe you nothing."

"No," Imogen agreed. "You owe Tomás everything. That's rather the problem, isn't it? For both of us."

She climbed the ladder into the grey afternoon light, nodded to Bo — who saluted her with the hammer, which was as close to warmth as Bo extended to anyone carrying syndicate stitching — and walked back up the pier without looking at the boat, because looking back was a weakness and she had spent six years learning to hide hers.

She was nearly to the Harbormaster's office when she heard footsteps behind her, quick and young, and turned to find Kip jogging down the planks after her, rain jacket flapping, out of breath, furious.

"You," Kip said, stopping three feet away, chest heaving. "You've got a hell of a nerve."

"So I'm told."

"Tomás was my— " Kip stopped. Started again. "He taught me to splice. He taught me to read swells. He used to say I had saltwater where my sense should be, and he *meant it nice*, and you want to drag us back to the reef where—" The voice failed. Kip stood there in the wind with tears starting and jaw clenched against them, twenty-two years old and carrying a grief that had no business being that heavy, and Imogen thought: *this is what I did. This is one of the things I did.*

"I know," she said.

"You don't know anything."

"You're right," Imogen said. "I know less than you think and more than you'd believe, and neither of those facts helps anybody today." She held out the envelope. Kip stared at it like it was on fire. "Take this to your captain. Read the terms. All of them, including the clause on page four about hazard contingencies — yes, it's generous, yes, that should worry you, worry is free. Then make your crew vote. That's what you do, isn't it? Vote. Even in the rain. Especially in the rain."

Kip took the envelope finally, reluctantly, the way you take a live thing. "And if we vote no?"

"Then I'll have lost to Sebastian Villalobos one more time," Imogen said, "and I'll deserve it."

She turned and walked on toward the office, and did not look back, and heard Kip's footsteps retreat toward the boat, and let herself hope — carefully, the way you carry water in your hands — that the argument she'd planted below decks would grow in the dark the way arguments do, fed by debt and grief and the terrible arithmetic of love.

Behind her, the *Provision* creaked at her moorings, holding her old paint and her older ghosts, waiting — as everything in the Coral Verge waited — for the tide to turn.

* * *

From the office window, Imogen watched the lights come on aboard the little blue boat as evening came down. She counted them. Four. Then, a long while later, a fifth — the galley, where the ledger lived.

They were talking. She couldn't hear the words across the water, and she wasn't sure she wanted to. Some votes you shouldn't witness. Some oaths you shouldn't hear breaking.

She poured herself a drink she didn't taste and opened her own ledger — the real one, the one with names in it that would keep her awake — and looked, as she did every night, at the entry dated six years ago, in her own younger handwriting, her own younger signature, the scuttling order that had started all of it.

*I'm sorry, Tomás,* she thought, the way she thought it every night, the prayer that had never once been answered and never once stopped being prayed. *One way or another, I'm bringing you home.*

Out past the breakwater, far to the east, the sky had begun to organize itself into something with intentions.
