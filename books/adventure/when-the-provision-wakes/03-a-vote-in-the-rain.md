## 3. A Vote in the Rain

The rain came the way it always did in the harbor — sideways, personal, and full of opinions. It hammered the tin roof of our shack like a drummer who'd been told to hurry up and hadn't taken it well.

Priya Chandrasekaran here, navigator of the *Provision*, keeper of charts, holder of grudges (mine are small and alphabetized), and — as of that evening — the only member of this crew who could still do arithmetic without flinching.

I want to be clear about something before I tell you how we voted to break an oath we'd sworn over a grave: nobody in that room was stupid. Desperate, yes. Grieving, certainly. But not stupid. Stupidity would have been easier to forgive.

* * *

It started, as most bad decisions aboard the *Provision* did, with Bo trying to fix something that wasn't broken.

Bo Okonkwo was our engineer, a woman built like a friendly filing cabinet, with hands so large she could palm a wrench the way other people palmed coins. She sat at the galley table with her left arm stretched out flat, and on that arm, just below the elbow, was a lump the size of a plum that had no business being there.

"That's new," said Kip.

"It's not new," Bo said. "It's been there since March."

"It's July."

"Time is a construct." Bo prodded the lump with one finger, the way you might poke a sleeping dog you hoped was dead. "Anyway. It's fine."

"It's not fine," I said. "I've seen that thing grow for four months. It's got its own postal code now."

Kip Ardoin was our deckhand, twenty-six years old, all elbows and fury, with a face that looked permanently like it had just heard an insult on someone else's behalf. He leaned across the table and squinted at the lump with the professional interest of a man who'd once watched his grandmother's tumor go from grapefruit to basketball because nobody could afford the doctor in Belize.

"You need surgery," Kip said.

"I need a boat," Bo said. "And a crew. And approximately eleven thousand dollars of engine parts, three thousand of which are due Friday. The arm can wait."

"The arm cannot wait," I said. "Arms don't wait. That's the whole problem with arms."

Sebastian Villalobos, our captain, said nothing. He stood at the window with his back to us, watching the rain come off the harbor, which was what Sebastian did instead of talking when talking was about to become expensive. He had the particular stillness of a man doing math he didn't want anyone else to see. I recognized it because I did the same math every night: the debt to the Delgado syndicate stood at forty-two thousand dollars, accruing interest at a rate I refused to calculate sober, against a boat worth maybe nineteen if you found a buyer who didn't look too hard at the hull.

Sebastian turned around. He had the smile he used at customs offices — warm, easy, entirely load-bearing.

"So," he said. "Imogen Reyes came by the office today."

The rain filled the silence for a moment. Even the roof seemed to lean in.

"And you're only telling us now?" Kip said.

"I was going to tell you after dinner."

"We haven't eaten."

"I was optimistic."

He laid the contract folder on the table between Bo's arm and Kip's elbows, and I felt the temperature of the room change the way you feel weather change before you can name it. Imogen Reyes. If our crew were a family — and we were, in the way that families who share a bathroom and a debt are families — then Imogen was the aunt nobody invited but who showed up anyway, smelling of money and other people's mistakes.

"The Meridian Star," Sebastian said. "She wants the black box. Salvage contract, syndicate-backed, paid on delivery. Enough to clear everything."

Nobody moved. Outside, a mooring line creaked somewhere, the harbor's version of a nervous cough.

"No," I said.

"Priya—"

"No." I said it again, firmer, because the first one hadn't stuck. "You're not serious. You cannot be serious. We swore."

"We swore," Sebastian agreed quietly.

We had sworn. Six years ago, on the dock at Puerto Cielo, in weather almost exactly like this, the four of us plus Tomás Vidal had poured rum into the sea and promised each other we would never work the Coral Verge again. Not because the water was bad. The water was beautiful; that was the obscenity of it. We swore because the Verge was where Tomás's skiff had washed up, half-swallowed by the reef, and where the search boats had spent nine days finding nothing but his cooler, his hat, and one boot. We swore because some seas keep what they take, and you don't go back to negotiate.

"He died out there," I said. "And now the woman who—" I stopped myself, because what I was about to say wasn't fair, and I try, as a rule, to save my unfairness for private. "Now she wants us to go back. For her. With her money."

"With Delgado's money," Sebastian corrected. "Which is worse, or better, depending on how you look at it."

"How am I supposed to look at it?"

"Honestly? I've been looking at it for six hours and I still don't know."

Bo cleared her throat. It was a big throat; the clearing of it rearranged the air in the room. "Can I say something?"

"You're going to anyway," Kip said.

"I'm going to anyway." Bo flexed the fingers of her bad arm, and I saw her wince — actually wince, which Bo never did, not when the engine block fell on her foot two years ago, not when she cauterized a fuel line with a road flare in a squall off Sable Point. "I need the surgery. And I need the parts. And I can't have both, and if I have to choose, I'll choose the boat, because the boat keeps all of us fed, and an arm is — statistically speaking — replaceable at a discount."

"That is the bleakest sentence ever spoken at this table," Kip said, "and I once heard Sebastian describe love as 'a recurring cost.'"

"It has overhead," Sebastian said mildly.

* * *

Here is what I knew that night, spread across my charts and my memory like cards I couldn't put down:

I knew that Imogen Reyes had money now, real money, syndicate money, and that Imogen Reyes had never in her life spent a dollar without expecting three back. I knew the Meridian Star went down in the Coral Verge six years ago with a black box that somebody, apparently, still wanted badly enough to hire the one crew emotionally incapable of saying yes. I knew that when a woman like Imogen offers you exactly the sum that makes your problems disappear, the sum is not the point. The sum is the hook. The point is whatever's on the other end of the line.

I smelled a trap. I want that on the record. I smelled it the way sailors smell squalls — not with the nose, exactly, but with some older instrument behind the sternum that modern science hasn't located yet.

But here is the other thing I knew, the thing I didn't say out loud, because saying it would have made it true in a way I wasn't ready for: I knew that Bo's arm was getting worse, and that Kip hadn't slept a full night since March, and that Sebastian had started answering questions about the future with jokes, which is what Sebastian does when the future stops answering back. I knew that desperation doesn't make people stupid. It makes them brave in directions they'd never have chosen.

* * *

Kip spoke last, which was unusual. Kip usually spoke first, loudest, and with props.

He sat turning his beer bottle in slow circles, watching the label spin, and when he finally looked up his eyes were wet and furious about being wet.

"Tomás radioed me," he said. "The night he died."

The rain got louder. Or I did. Hard to say.

"What?" Sebastian said.

"Nine days before the skiff washed up. He raised me on the long-band, voice all busted up with static, and he said — he said, 'Kip, if anything happens, check the box.' And I said, 'What box?' And he said, 'The ship's box, you idiot,' and then the signal went, and I figured — I figured he was drunk, or the storm was scrambling him, or both. I never told anyone. Because telling anyone meant admitting I'd talked to him that night and let him go out alone anyway."

"Kip," Bo said softly.

"Don't." He held up a hand. "Six years I've carried that. Six years I've told myself the sea took him, random, senseless, the way the sea does. And now Imogen Reyes walks into our office smelling of syndicate money asking for the Meridian Star's black box, and every hair on my body stands up and says: *the box, you idiot.*"

I looked at Sebastian. Sebastian was looking at Kip with an expression I'd never seen on him before — not the customs smile, not the captain face, but something rawer, younger, like a man hearing a knock at a door he'd bricked over years ago.

"You're saying Tomás knew something about the Meridian Star," I said.

"I'm saying I don't know what I'm saying. I'm saying I need to know. There's a difference between grief and not-knowing, and I've done the second one long enough." He set the bottle down with exaggerated care, the way angry men handle fragile things. "I'm voting yes. Whatever the rest of you do."

* * *

We voted the way we always voted: no ceremony, no raised hands, just Sebastian going around the table like a teacher collecting homework.

"Bo?"

Bo looked at her arm. Then at the window, where the *Provision* rode her mooring in the dark, our whole livelihood and our whole world bobbing on water that wanted, in a patient way, to kill us. "Yes," she said. "Sorry, Priya."

"Don't apologize to me. Apologize to Tomás."

"I will," Bo said, "when I see him. First item on the agenda."

"Kip already voted. Sebastian?"

Sebastian was quiet a moment. Rain ran down the window behind him and made moving shadows on his face, so he seemed to flicker, present and absent at once.

"I brought the contract," he said, "because I already know my answer. I'm tired of owing Delgado money. I'm tired of flinching at the name Reyes. And I'm tired — God, I'm so tired — of pretending that staying away from the Verge has kept Tomás safe. He's not safe anywhere. He's gone everywhere. So yes."

Three yeses. All eyes came to me, and I hated them a little for it, and loved them more, which is the general operating condition of this crew.

I looked down at the contract. Syndicate letterhead. Imogen's signature, quick and slanted, like a person signing while walking away. A number with enough zeroes to buy Bo a new arm and the *Provision* a new everything, and freedom on top, and freedom, I have learned, is the most expensive thing anyone ever offers you for free.

"If this is a trap," I said, "and it is a trap — I want it noted that I said so. In the log. In ink. When we're all hanging from syndicate hooks, I want someone to remember that Priya said *no* first, loudest, and with the most dignity."

"Noted," Sebastian said. "Dignified. Very you."

"Fourth yes?" he asked.

I pushed the contract back to the center of the table, toward all of them, away from me, which was as close to a no as I was going to get to give.

"Yes," I said. "God help us. Yes."

Four to one, except the one was also a yes, which made it five, which is how oaths die — not with a dissent, but with a dissent that loves you too much to leave.

* * *

Later, when the others had gone to their bunks and the rain had settled into its overnight rhythm, I sat at the chart table with the lamp turned low and did the thing I always do before a passage: I laid out the route in my head. Puerto Cielo to the Coral Verge, three days' run in decent weather, four in bad. And the monsoon season opening like a mouth on the horizon, right on schedule, right on top of us.

And I thought about Tomás Vidal, who had taught me celestial navigation because I was nineteen and cocky and thought GPS was for cowards. *The sky doesn't lie to you,* he used to say. *People lie. Instruments fail. The sky just hangs there, being honest.* 

I wondered what the sky had hung over him that night. I wondered what he'd seen from the deck of whatever he was on, and whether he'd thought of us, and whether — this was the thought I couldn't put down, the one that followed me into my bunk and lay there breathing — whether *check the box* had been a warning meant for all of us, six years early, waiting patiently for the day we'd be desperate enough to hear it.

Outside, far out past the harbor lights, thunder rolled across the water like something enormous clearing its throat.

In the morning, Imogen Reyes would send the advance payment. In the morning, we'd start provisioning for the Verge.

Tonight, the rain kept falling, and the oath lay broken on the galley table, and none of us — not one of us — had any idea yet what we'd actually agreed to carry home.
