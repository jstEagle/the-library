## 4. Six Tides

The chart table in the *Provision*'s cabin had not been this crowded since the night Bo tried to teach everyone poker and Priya had cleaned them all out in forty minutes using nothing but arithmetic and a face like a closed door.

Now the same four bodies leaned over the same scarred wood, but nobody was laughing. The chart of the Coral Verge lay flat under a coffee mug, a tin of biscuits, and Kip's elbow, which he refused to move on principle.

"Elbow," said Priya.

"This is my thinking elbow."

"Your thinking elbow is covering the Serrano Passage."

"That's because I'm thinking about it."

Sebastian let them bicker. It was better than the silence that had followed the vote — the particular quiet of five people who had just agreed to do something they'd promised each other, six years and one funeral ago, never to do again. Bickering was the sound of a crew still working. Silence was the sound of one coming apart.

He uncapped the marker and drew the first line.

"Here's how I see it." The line ran from their dock at Puerto Cielo, southeast along the shelf, then out past the last of the shipping lanes into open water. "Three days' run if the weather holds. Two and a half if Bo stops treating the engine like a museum piece."

"I treat her like a lady," Bo said, without looking up from the disassembled fuel pump she was cleaning with a rag and what smelled like half the rum ration. "Unlike some people, who treat everything like it owes them money."

"It does owe us money," Sebastian said. "That's rather the point of the exercise."

The joke landed softer than he'd hoped. He watched it land — saw Kip's mouth twitch and then flatten, saw Priya's eyes stay on the chart, tracking his line the way she tracked weather: patiently, suspiciously, waiting for it to lie to her.

Bo set down the pump housing. "Say the number again."

"Six tides."

"Not the tides. The other number."

Sebastian capped the marker. He'd been hoping to get through the whole briefing without saying it twice. "Two hundred and ten thousand. Delgado's figure. Interest included."

"And if we don't—"

"We will."

"But if we don't," Bo persisted, because Bo had never once in eleven years let him finish a sentence that ended in comfort, "what does the man with the cheerful voice take?"

"The boat. And—" Sebastian flexed his right hand, looked at the fingers, spread them on the table like a man showing off a card trick. "These, apparently. He was very specific about which ones. Said something about how a captain needs the thumb for signing things."

Nobody laughed. Even Kip didn't laugh, and Kip laughed at funerals.

"So we make the Verge, we find the Star, we're down and up before tide six," Sebastian went on, drawing the second line, longer, rougher, skirting the drowned reef country where the water went from teal to ink between one sounding and the next. "Imogen's contract pays on delivery of the black box. Enough to clear Delgado, enough to fix the *Provision* properly, enough left over that Bo gets her hospital in a real city with real doctors instead of a vet with a boat shed and good intentions."

"My surgeon has excellent intentions too," Bo said. "He also has a bottle of tequila as an anesthetic plan."

"He's a professional."

"He's your cousin."

"A professional cousin."

Priya finally spoke. "The monsoon makes landfall in six tides. That's not a deadline, Sebastian, that's a wall. The leading edge alone will be running seas we haven't seen since—" She stopped herself. Everyone knew what she wasn't going to say. Since Tomás. Since the last time they'd been out here chasing something into weather that was already chasing them back.

"Since the *Marisol* run," Sebastian finished for her, gently. "I know. I was there."

"You were there," Priya said, "and you came back, and you swore on a dock in front of witnesses that you'd never take this crew into the Verge again. I remember the witnesses. I was one of them."

The cabin got very small around them. Rain ticked against the porthole — the first of it, the scouts, the soft rain that always came ahead of the big weather like a landlord checking if anyone was home.

"I know what I swore," Sebastian said. "And I know what I'm asking. And I'm not going to pretend those are the same thing." He put both hands flat on the chart, over the empty blue southeast of everything. "So here's the honest version. We go fast, we go shallow, we dive the wreck, and we are out of the Verge before the monsoon's leading edge crosses the hundred-fathom line. If the box isn't up by tide four, we leave it. Contract or no contract, debt or no debt. Tide four, we turn for home, and Imogen Reyes can bill me for her disappointment. That's the plan. That's the whole plan. There is no clever part after that."

"Tide four," Priya repeated.

"Tide four."

She nodded once, slowly, and pulled the chart toward her, and began — as she always did — turning his plan into a route, which was the difference between a wish and a way home.

* * *

They spent the afternoon provisioning, and if you'd watched them from the dock you might have mistaken it for any other departure: crates of diesel and drinking water going aboard, Kip arguing with the chandler about the price of rope with the passion of a man defending his homeland, Bo lashing spare parts to the deck rails with more care than most people show their children.

But there were differences, and Sebastian catalogued every one of them the way he used to catalogue cloud shapes.

Kip bought twice the medical kit he used to. Nobody commented.

Priya stood at the end of the dock for a full minute before boarding, looking southeast, where the horizon had gone the color of a bruise deciding whether to bloom. Then she came aboard without a word and wrote something in the logbook that she covered with her arm when Sebastian walked past.

And Bo — Bo climbed down into the engine room and didn't come up for two hours, and when she did, her hands were shaking, and it wasn't the rum.

It was dusk when the radio crackled and the harbor office relayed the bulletin they'd all been waiting for and dreading: *Monsoon system confirmed. Landfall projected six tides. All small craft advised against open-water transit.*

"Advised against," Kip repeated, reading the printout by the galley lamp. "Well. Good thing we've never once taken advice."

"The Corvus has taken it," Priya said quietly.

Everyone turned. She was standing by the radio with a sheet of traffic reports in her hand, and her face had gone still in the way it only went still when she was frightened and refusing to spend energy on showing it.

"Three sightings logged in the last day. The *Corvus Prime*, the *Corvus Minor*, and a tender running their flag. Heading southeast. Same heading as us." She laid the paper on the chart table, next to Sebastian's lines. "They left yesterday. They'll beat us to the Verge by a full tide."

Sebastian read the report twice. The names meant nothing new — the Corvus fleet had been circling salvage jobs for years, vultures with better financing — but the heading meant everything. Nobody ran for the Coral Verge in monsoon season unless someone with deep pockets had told them to.

"With whose backing?" he asked, though he already knew.

"Whose do you think?" Priya said. "Delgado doesn't just want their money back, Sebastian. They want the box first. They want to hand it to whoever pays best, or sink it, or bury it. And they've given the Corvus a head start, a fleet, and six tides of weather cover to do it in."

The rain picked up outside, drumming on the cabin roof, and the lamp swung gently on its hook, and for a moment Sebastian felt the old vertigo — the sense of standing at the top of a slide he'd sworn off, hearing the countdown start.

Then Kip, of all people, broke it.

"So we go faster."

They all looked at him. Kip was leaning in the galley doorway with a mug of coffee he hadn't drunk, and there was something in his face Sebastian hadn't seen in six years — not grief exactly, but grief with a direction. A compass needle where the wound used to be.

"They've got a head start," Kip said. "Fine. They've also got syndicate men aboard, and syndicate men dive like they file paperwork — slow, careful, and only as deep as the rules allow. We've got Bo. We've got Priya. We've got—" he gestured at Sebastian with the mug, slopping coffee, "—whatever you are. And we've got a reason they don't."

"Which is?"

"Tomás." The name came out of him like a splinter leaving skin. "Whatever's down there, whatever's in that box — he died near it. I want to know why. And I want to be the one holding it when the answer comes up."

The lamp swung. The rain drummed. Somewhere out in the dark, southeast of everything, a rival fleet was steaming toward the same wreck with better engines and worse reasons.

Sebastian looked at his crew — Bo with her shaking hands and steady eyes, Priya with her covered logbook, Kip with his compass-needle grief — and felt the thing he always felt at the start of a chase, the terrible gift of it: dread and adrenaline braided so tight he couldn't have told you which strand was which.

"First light," he said. "We run the outside of the shelf, we burn the clean diesel, and we don't stop for anything that isn't sinking." He picked up the marker and drew one final line on the chart — short, steep, straight into the heart of the Verge. "Tide four, we're out. Whatever happens."

"And if the Corvus is already sitting on the wreck?" Bo asked.

Sebastian capped the marker. Outside, far off across the harbor, thunder cleared its throat like a man about to make an announcement.

"Then," he said, "we'd better hope they're as careful as their paperwork."

* * *

He didn't sleep. Around midnight he found himself in the wheelhouse with the chart folded open on his knees, tracing the line he'd drawn, and it was there that Priya found him, bringing two cups of tea the way she always did when she'd decided a conversation was necessary and wanted to arrive armed.

"You didn't tell them the rest," she said.

"There isn't a rest."

"Sebastian." She handed him a cup. Steam rose between them. "You drew the route through the Serrano Passage. Nobody runs the Serrano in pre-monsoon swell. Not even us. Especially not us." She looked at him over her own cup. "Why that passage?"

Because it saved nine hours. Because the Corvus wouldn't risk it. Because the shortcut through the graveyard was the only way to make tide four mean anything.

"Because it saves nine hours," he said.

Priya studied him for a long moment, the way she studied weather fronts, and he could see her deciding whether to push. She didn't. She just leaned against the wheelhouse rail beside him and looked out at the harbor, where the rain had softened to a mist and the lights of Puerto Cielo wobbled on the black water like coins at the bottom of a well.

"Six tides," she said.

"Six tides."

"My grandmother used to say the sea gives you exactly enough time, and never a minute more." She sipped her tea. "She also said never trust a shortcut named after a shipwreck, but I'm choosing to ignore that part."

Sebastian laughed — really laughed, for the first time since the enforcer's smile — and clinked his cup against hers.

Below them, the *Provision* rocked gently at her moorings, loaded and fueled and ready, a small stubborn animal waiting for the gate to open. And away to the southeast, past the edge of the lamplight, past the edge of the map almost, the storm was gathering itself over the Coral Verge like a debt coming due — patient, enormous, and absolutely certain of its schedule.

At dawn, they would point the bow at it.

At dawn, the clock would start.
