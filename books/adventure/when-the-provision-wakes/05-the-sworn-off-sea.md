## 5. The Sworn-Off Sea

Priya felt the line before she saw it.

Not a line on a chart — though it was on the chart too, a faint pencil stroke in Sebastian's hand from six years ago, drawn across the passage at the edge of the Coral Verge and never erased. *Here be grief*, it might as well have said. No. What she felt was subtler: the boat itself changed. The *Provision* had been humming all morning — Bo's engine running sweet after two days of coaxing, Kip whistling off-key in the galley, the whole vessel vibrating with the particular energy of people who had made a bad decision and were determined to enjoy it.

Then the pencil line slid under the hull, and everything went quiet.

Kip stopped whistling mid-note. Bo's hammering from the engine room — he'd been "tuning" something that did not need tuning, which was how Bo prayed — ceased. Even the wind seemed to lower its voice.

Priya stood at the nav station with her hands flat on the table and let the silence settle over them like ballast. She'd been a navigator long enough to know that water held memory. Fishermen knew it. Old sailors swore to it. And this stretch of sea, blue and harmless as a postcard, held more of it than anywhere they'd ever sailed.

Six years ago, Tomás Ferreira had gone into these waters in a skiff with a radio that worked and a plan nobody questioned, and had not come back.

"Passing the line," she said, because someone had to say it out loud. That was her job, really, beyond the charts and the tides. Someone had to say things out loud or this crew would carry every feeling to its grave unopened, like cargo nobody would claim.

Nobody answered. Sebastian stood at the wheel with his jaw set in the way that meant he was fine, absolutely fine, don't ask. Kip dried the same clean mug with the same rag for the fourth time. From below came the sound of Bo deciding, very deliberately, to start hammering again.

*Well,* Priya thought. *This will go beautifully.*

* * *

It went beautifully for exactly four hours.

The trouble started, as trouble often did aboard the *Provision*, with lunch. Kip had made rice and beans — his one dish, executed with the confidence of a man who had once watched a cooking show through a shop window — and they ate on deck because the heat below was ferocious. For a while it was almost normal. Kip told a story about a parrot he'd known in Port Amaya that could swear in three languages, and even Sebastian's mouth twitched at the corners.

Then Bo, mouth full, said the fatal thing:

"You know what I keep thinking? Tomás would've hated this job."

The words landed on deck like a dropped wrench.

"He would," Bo pressed on, because Bo had never in his life recognized a landmine before stepping on it. "He hated salvage. Said it was fishing for other people's mistakes. He only ever went out that night because—"

"Don't." Kip set down his bowl.

"—because somebody had to check the beacon, and we all drew straws like cowards, and—"

"I said *don't*."

"Or what?" Bo stood up. He wasn't a big man, but he was built like the engines he loved, all torque and no patience. "You going to swing at me again? Go ahead. Maybe it'll knock loose whatever's rattling around in there. We're all thinking it, Kip. He died because of us. Because we let him go alone."

"We didn't *let* him—" Kip was up now too, rice forgotten, fists half-curled. "You want to do the math again, Bo? You want to count who voted what? Because I remember. I remember every single—"

"Gentlemen." Sebastian's voice, quiet, from the wheel. It carried the way quiet does when it comes from a man who can also be loud. "Sit down. Both of you. You're blocking my view of nothing, and I'd hate for you to waste it."

Bo sat. Kip stayed standing a moment longer, breathing hard, staring at the middle distance where six years ago apparently lived. Then he sat too, and picked up his mug, and said, to no one, "I'm not swinging at anybody."

"No," Priya agreed mildly. "You're drying dishes with your eyes."

That got a broken half-laugh out of him, which was the point. She'd learned long ago that this crew's fights were like squalls — loud, fast, and survivable if you knew where the wind was coming from. The wind, today, was guilt. Everyone's. Hers included, though hers was the quiet kind, the kind that lived in arithmetic: *one skiff, four crew, zero votes against sending him.* She'd done the calculation a thousand times and it always came out the same.

Sebastian killed the engine to drift while tempers cooled, and the boat rocked in the long swell, and for a while the only sound was water doing what water does.

Then Sebastian said, "I never read his log."

Priya looked up. So did the others.

"His final log," Sebastian said. He was still facing forward, hands on the wheel, talking to the horizon because it was easier than talking to them. "After the accident. The coast guard returned his effects. There was a logbook in the bag — the little green one, waterproof paper, he was so proud of that stupid book. I put it in the chart drawer under the spare dividers and I never opened it. Six years. Not once."

"Why not?" Kip asked. It wasn't an accusation. It was almost a whisper.

"Because I knew what would be in it." Sebastian's knuckles whitened on the wheel. "Either 'everything was fine' — and then I'd have to live with a fine that wasn't fine — or it wasn't fine, and I'd have to live with that instead. So I chose the drawer." He laughed, short and joyless. "Man who'll argue with a hurricane won't open forty pages of a dead friend's handwriting. That's courage for you."

The silence that followed was different from the earlier one. Heavier, but warmer somehow — the silence of people recognizing their own cowardice in someone else's, which is one of the few silences that actually comforts.

"The drawer's still there," Priya said quietly. "Under the dividers. I've seen it."

"I know you've seen it." Sebastian finally turned. His eyes were red-rimmed but steady. "Everyone's seen it. It's the most famous unread document on this coast. When we get back — if we get back — I'll read it. That's my price for this job. Not Imogen's money. That."

Nobody said anything clever. There wasn't anything clever to say. But Kip reached over and squeezed Sebastian's shoulder once, hard, the way men on boats do when hugging would sink the ship, and Bo muttered something about checking the bilge, which everyone understood to mean *I need a minute,* and Priya looked at her chart and thought: *grief is ballast.* It weighed them down, yes. But ballast was also the only reason a small boat stayed upright in big weather. The trick wasn't throwing it overboard. The trick was trimming it — learning where each person kept theirs, and never, ever shifting it all to one side at once.

She marked their position. They were well inside the sworn-off water now. No turning back that wouldn't cost more than going on.

* * *

Evening came down violet and gold, and the sea did its evening trick of looking forgiving. Priya took first watch alone at the nav station, running numbers she already trusted, mostly for the pleasure of the ritual. The monsoon clock ticked in her head: five tides left after this one. The Corvus fleet was somewhere ahead, a smudge of smoke on yesterday's horizon, syndicate money burning fuel to beat them to a wreck that had already beaten everyone once.

Footsteps behind her. Kip, holding two mugs of tea that smelled strongly of being mostly sugar.

"For the watch," he said, handing her one. He leaned on the doorframe, looking out at the darkening water. "Hey, Priya. Can I say a thing without it becoming a whole thing?"

"That's my specialty."

"Tomás radioed me that night." He said it fast, like pulling a splinter. "Before he went dark. Nineteen hundred hours, give or take. And it was strange, Priya. It was — he sounded scared, but not storm-scared. Storm-scared has a shape. This was different. He said, 'Something's wrong with the Star.' Not the weather. *The Star.* And then the transmission cut and I figured — interference, reef shadow, whatever. I figured it for six years." He stared into his mug. "I never told anyone. Not even Seb. Especially not Seb."

Priya turned that over carefully, the way you turn over something that might be explosive. "Why tell me now?"

"Because tomorrow we pass the reef where they found his skiff." His voice cracked, just barely, and he pushed through it. "And because we're about to pull a black box out of the same wreck he was trying to warn us about, and if there's even a chance those two things connect, somebody should know before we're all too busy drowning to talk."

Outside, the first stars were coming out over the Coral Verge, indifferent and bright. Somewhere ahead, wedged in a trench beneath a hundred feet of beautiful lethal water, the *Meridian Star* waited with whatever it was Tomás had heard.

"Thank you," Priya said. "For saying it out loud."

"Yeah, well." Kip drained his tea and headed for the hatch, then paused. "Don't let me near Bo at breakfast. I'm not promising anything."

"Noted. I'll seat him downwind."

She logged the position, the time, the tide. Then, in the margin of the chart, next to Sebastian's old pencil line, she wrote in her own small precise hand: *19:00 — something's wrong with the Star.*

Some lines, once crossed, couldn't be uncrossed. Fine. She'd chart what lay on the other side.
