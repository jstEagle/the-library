## 6. First Tide: The Corvus on the Horizon

Bo Okonkwo had a theory about engines, and it was this: an engine never lies to you. A person could smile at you over breakfast and be planning to sell your boat out from under you. A weather report could promise fair skies and deliver a squall that peeled your awning off like a banana skin. But an engine? An engine told you exactly what was wrong with it, in its own language, if you were willing to listen. The knock that meant a bearing was going. The hiccup in the idle that meant water in the fuel. The high, thin whine that meant something was about to become very expensive.

The *Provision* had been talking to Bo all morning, and what she was saying was: *I am tired, I am old, and I do not appreciate being asked to outrun a syndicate fleet on a fuel line I wouldn't trust to water a garden.*

"Tell me about it," Bo muttered, elbow-deep in the engine bay, a rag over one shoulder and grease up both forearms to the elbow. "Nobody appreciates us."

The engine bay was Bo's favorite room in the world, which said something sad about Bo's life that Bo preferred not to examine. It was hot, it smelled of diesel and salt and the particular burnt-sugar scent of old wiring, and nothing in it had ever once pretended to be anything other than what it was. Above, through the deck plates, Bo could hear the muffled percussion of the crew's morning: Kip's boots clomping across the foredeck, Priya's voice running numbers, Sebastian doing his captain thing, which mostly consisted of standing at the wheel looking like a man who had made peace with his own bad decisions.

Six tides. That was the clock now. Monsoon landfall confirmed by three separate weather services, which meant it was real, because weather services only agreed when the news was bad. Six tides to cross two hundred miles of open water, slip into the Coral Verge, pull a black box off a wrecked ship, and get out again before the sky came down on their heads. And somewhere behind them — or ahead of them, or beside them, hidden inside all that glittering empty ocean — the *Corvus* fleet was doing the same math with better boats and worse intentions.

Bo wiped down the secondary fuel filter for the fourth time that hour. Not because it needed it. Because hands that were busy couldn't wring themselves.

* * *

They'd raised the Corvus at midmorning, though "raised" was generous. Priya had spotted it first — she always spotted things first, being the sort of person who counted the lifeboats on other people's ships out of professional habit — a smudge of smoke on the southern horizon, hanging there like a question nobody wanted to answer.

"Three vessels," she'd said, not looking up from her binoculars. "Lead ship's got the profile of a converted trawler. Deep keel, heavy superstructure. The other two are running screen." She'd lowered the binoculars and looked at Sebastian. "They're not following us. They're pacing us."

There was a difference, and everyone aboard knew it. Following meant they hadn't seen you yet. Pacing meant they'd seen you, measured you, and decided you weren't worth overtaking yet. It was the maritime equivalent of a cat watching a mouse cross the kitchen floor without hurrying.

Sebastian had adjusted their heading five degrees north, casual as anything, and the smudge of smoke had adjusted with them, five degrees north, casual as anything, and nobody had said a word about it for the rest of the morning, which was somehow louder than saying a word about it would have been.

Now, past noon, Bo climbed up into the daylight to check the cooling intake and found Kip at the rail with a pair of binoculars, staring south with the expression of a man watching his own funeral procession go by.

"They're still there," Kip said, without turning around.

"I figured. The engine's been sulking since dawn. She knows we're being hunted. Engines are sensitive."

Kip grunted. Through the glasses, the lead Corvus vessel resolved into gray steel and a bridge too clean for honest work. Painted on her bow, small but legible even at this distance: a black bird with its wings folded. Waiting.

"You know what I hate about it?" Kip said. "It's polite. They're just *there*. Like they've got all the time in the world."

"That's because they think they do."

"And do they?"

Bo considered the horizon, the smoke, the folded wings. Considered the *Provision* underneath them, twenty years old, held together by welds and stubbornness and one very opinionated engineer. "Not if I have anything to say about it," Bo said, and went back below, and neither of them mentioned that this was not, strictly speaking, an answer.

* * *

The cutter came at the change of tide.

Bo heard it before anyone announced it — a different engine note threading in under the *Provision*'s own rhythm, big and bored and official, the sound of a machine that had never once worried about its fuel bill. By the time Bo got up on deck, wiping hands on the rag out of pure reflex, the thing was already alongside: a syndicate patrol cutter, white hull, navy-blue stripe, the Delgado crest stenciled on the bow like a signature on a threat.

Two men stood on her deck. One held a megaphone he wasn't using. The other held a clipboard, which was worse.

"Vessel *Provision*," the megaphone man called anyway, his voice carrying flat across the water. "This is a routine documentation inspection. Prepare to receive a boarding party."

"We're a salvage vessel in transit," Sebastian called back, arms crossed, every inch the aggrieved small-boat captain, and God love him, he was good at it. He sounded less like a man drowning in debt than a man mildly inconvenienced by bureaucracy. "Our papers are current. We filed our transit plan out of Port Meridian four days ago."

"Routine," the man repeated, in the tone of someone who had made an entire career out of that word and everything it excused.

The boarding party was one man. He came over the rail with the easy, practiced grace of somebody who'd done this a hundred times, and he was smiling, and the smile was the problem. It was a warm smile. A friendly smile. It was the smile of the Delgado enforcer who had visited their dockside shack, transplanted onto a younger face, and Bo felt the hair on their arms stand up under the sun.

"Papers," the boarder said pleasantly, and Sebastian handed them over, and the man read them slowly, thoroughly, the way you read a menu when you already know what you're ordering.

He walked the deck while he did it. That was the part that sat wrong. An inspection was a glance at registration, a look at the wheelhouse, done. This man took a *tour*. He paused at the winch housing. He glanced down the hatch at the hold. He let his eyes rest on the dive gear racked by the mast — on the spare tanks, the lines, the lift bags — longer than any paperwork required, and Bo watched him count the tanks, lips moving slightly, and understood that the papers had never been the point.

"Salvage, huh," the man said, handing the documents back to Sebastian. "Out here? Nothing to salvage out here but weather." He smiled again. "Good luck with your fishing."

"We're not fishing," Sebastian said evenly.

"No," the man agreed, stepping back to the rail. "You're really not." He swung over the side, dropped to the cutter's deck, and turned for one last word, delivered through cupped hands, cheerful as a neighbor borrowing sugar:

"The Corvus says hello. They say you should think hard about whether whatever's down there is worth what it'll cost you to reach it. They say—" and here the smile widened into something almost kind, "—accidents happen in monsoon season. Take care of your boat. Take care of each other."

The cutter peeled away in a wide, unhurried arc, and the sea closed behind it as if it had never been, except that everyone aboard stood very still for a long moment, listening to the engine note fade, each of them privately rehearsing the words *accidents happen* and finding they didn't fit anywhere comfortable.

"Well," said Kip finally. "That was friendly."

"Friendly like a knife is friendly," said Priya.

Sebastian stared south, where the Corvus smoke still hung. "They know exactly what we're after," he said quietly. "And they wanted us to know they know. That wasn't an inspection. That was a message delivered by hand."

* * *

Bo found the radio problem that evening, and it rearranged everything.

It started as a nagging itch. The long-range set had been pulling in weather bulletins all day — garbled at the edges, but serviceable — and then, shortly after sundown, it hadn't. Static where there should have been voices. Not dead static, either; Bo had heard dead radios, and this wasn't that. This was *shaped* static, a wall of noise sitting precisely on the frequencies that mattered, while the bands above and below came through clear as church bells.

Bo pulled the access panel and put a hand inside, and stopped.

The wiring harness had been disturbed. Not broken — *tidied*. Cable ties replaced with newer ones. A connector reseated with fingers rather than tools. Someone had been inside the radio, recently, carefully, and whoever it was had known exactly what they were doing, because the only trace they'd left was a small ceramic component soldered across the transmitter line, no bigger than a thumbnail, that did not belong on any schematic Bo had ever memorized.

Bo sat down on the deck plates and looked at it for a long time.

A limiter. Or a tracker. Or a beacon. Bo didn't know — that was the poison of it, the not-knowing. It could be a cheap jammer that crippled their transmit range so no distress call would ever reach help. It could be a homing tag singing their position to the Corvus right now, explaining the smoke on the horizon, explaining the polite, patient pacing, explaining how a rival fleet always seemed to be exactly where the *Provision* was going to be.

Or it could have been installed back in port. Before they'd ever left. Before Imogen Reyes had come walking down the dock with her contract and her syndicate money and her eyes full of old grief.

Bo thought about the crew above: Sebastian at the wheel, jaw set; Priya charting tides by lamplight; Kip sharpening a knife that didn't need sharpening. Thought about the oath they'd all sworn six years ago, and the vote they'd taken in the rain, four to one, and how a crew was only as strong as the secrets it could survive.

An engine never lies. But a radio, apparently, could be taught to.

Bo wrapped the component in a rag, tucked it into a pocket, and restored the panel, leaving the wiring exactly as found — because whoever had put it there might be aboard right now, might be listening for exactly the kind of noise an angry engineer makes, and until Bo knew whose fingerprints were on it, silence was the only safe frequency.

Up top, the wind had begun to shift, coming around out of the southwest with a new weight in it, a wet, far-traveled smell that hadn't been there yesterday. The first breath of the monsoon, arriving ahead of schedule, like everything else in this story.

Bo climbed the ladder and stood in the dark at the rail, holding the little ceramic stranger in a fist that shook slightly, and looked south, where the Corvus burned their patient lights.

The enemy was inside the weather. Bo just didn't know yet if the weather was also inside the crew.
