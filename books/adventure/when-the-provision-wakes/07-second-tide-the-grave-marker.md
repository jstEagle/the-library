## 7. Second Tide: The Grave Marker

The reef showed up on the horizon at mid-morning, a low brown smear like a bruise on the edge of the world, and Kip Okafor knew it before Priya said its name.

He'd been working the winch for an hour without really working it — hands moving, mind somewhere six years back and forty fathoms down — and when Priya's voice came over the deck speaker, flat and careful the way she got when she was handling something fragile, he felt his stomach drop through the deck plates.

"Grave Marker reef, off the port bow," she said. "We'll pass it at half a mile."

Nobody answered. That was the thing about this crew. They could fill a galley with noise — Bo's singing, which was a crime against music; Sebastian's endless, elaborate complaining about the price of diesel; the whole rolling argument about whether Kip's coffee or Priya's coffee was the real coffee — but when it mattered, they could go quiet as a held breath.

Kip kept his eyes on the reef. The *Provision* rolled under him, patient as a dog that knows it's carrying something heavy.

Six years ago, they'd come for Tomás in the dark. Six years ago, the searchlight had swept this same water and found the skiff — what was left of it — wedged in the coral like a bone caught in a throat. Half the hull. One oar. The emergency kit, unopened, which was the detail that had haunted Kip ever since, because Tomás had drilled them all on the emergency kit, Tomás had *invented* the emergency kit, Tomás had once made Kip repack it three times because he'd put the flare after the whistle instead of before, and a man like that did not die with his emergency kit unopened unless something had happened very fast, or very strange, or both.

The official story — the one the maritime board had written in its tidy, bloodless language — was engine failure and weather. A rogue chop. A tragedy of the sea. The sea takes, the sea keeps, sign here, condolences, next case.

Kip had signed. They all had. It was the last thing they'd ever all agreed on, and it had been a lie, and he'd known it was a lie, and he'd signed it anyway because grief is a tide and it doesn't care what you think you'd do.

He became aware that Bo had drifted over to the winch, standing beside him with the studied casualness of a person who has decided to say something and is waiting for a weather window.

"You're doing the thing," Bo said.

"What thing."

"The thing where you polish a winch that's already polished. You've been at that drum for an hour. It's shinier than the day we bought her."

"Maybe I just take pride in my work."

"Maybe." Bo leaned on the rail beside him, and for a while neither of them said anything, and the reef got closer, brown and patient, wearing its white crown of breaking water like something dressed for a funeral it had been attending for six years.

"You were close with him," Bo said finally. Not a question. Bo didn't really do questions; Bo did observations delivered like a man setting down tools.

"Yeah."

"Closer than any of us."

Kip's hands stopped on the winch. "He taught me to read weather. Not off instruments — off the *water*. The way it flattens before a squall. The way fish go quiet. He said the sea talks all the time, most people just don't listen." His voice did something he didn't authorize. He cleared his throat. "I listen now. Every day. Because of him."

Bo nodded slowly, looking at the reef. "Sebastian's been quiet since the marker came up on the chart."

"Sebastian's always quiet about Tomás."

"Quieter."

The reef was close enough now that Kip could make out individual coral heads, dark shapes in the clear green water, and the white sand channels between them like veins. Somewhere under that water, or near it, or carried off by currents to who knew where, was the rest of the skiff. And the rest of Tomás.

The *Provision* rolled. Kip gripped the rail.

"Hey." Bo's voice was gentle, which was rare enough that it startled him. "You okay?"

"I'm—" Kip started, and that was as far as the sentence got, because the reef chose that moment to pass abeam, and the current shifted, and the hull gave a long groan as the water beneath them shallowed, and every sound of it — the groan, the water, the white crown of the reef sliding past like a slow wave goodbye — hit Kip all at once, six years of it, all of it, the flare after the whistle, the unopened kit, the tidy bloodless report, and his knees decided independently of his brain that they were done for the day.

He sat down hard on the deck. Not collapsed — sat, the way a man sits when his body has outvoted him.

"Kip—" Bo crouched.

"I'm fine."

"You're sitting on the deck."

"I'm fine *sitting on the deck*."

Bo, to his enormous credit, sat down too. Just folded himself onto the salt-crusted planks like it was a planned activity, like they were two men enjoying the view, and said nothing, which was somehow worse than saying something, and Kip felt the thing in his chest — the thing he'd been carrying since the funeral, since the report, since the night he'd stood at the rail of a different boat and watched the searchlight sweep empty water — begin to crack.

Not break. Crack. There was a difference. A break you could sweep up. A crack just let things *out*.

"He radioed me," Kip said.

Bo went very still.

"That night. The night he died." Kip's voice came out low and rough, like it was being dragged over coral. "The official time of the incident — the time in the report — he was already dead, or dying, or gone. That's what the report says. Engine failure, weather, no distress call received, no time to call. But he radioed me, Bo. At 2317. I've got the log. I never gave it to the board. I never told anyone."

The wind moved through the rigging. Somewhere below, Sebastian's voice, muffled, saying something to Priya about the current. The world going on, indifferent.

"What did he say?" Bo asked quietly.

"That's the thing." Kip laughed, and it came out wrong, wet and jagged. "That's the *thing*, Bo. It wasn't a distress call. He wasn't scared. He wasn't even — his voice was steady, you know how he got, that low calm voice, like he was reading you a recipe. And he said—" Kip stopped. Swallowed. Six years of swallowing, and it wouldn't go down this time. "He said, 'Kip, if anything happens, don't let them tell you it was the weather.'"

The deck seemed to tilt, though the sea was flat as poured glass inside the reef's lee.

"And I said, 'Tomás, what's happening, where are you,' and he said, 'They're going to say the weather. It wasn't the weather. Don't sign it, Kip. Whatever they put in front of you — don't sign it.'" Kip's hands had closed into fists without his permission, knuckles white against the deck. "And then there was a sound. In the background. Not the sea. A *voice*. Another voice, on his boat or near it, and Tomás covered the mic — I heard the click — and then he came back and his voice was different, faster, and he said, 'I have to go,' and the transmission ended and that was it. That was the last time I ever heard my brother's voice, and he spent it telling me not to sign the report, and I *signed the report*, Bo. Three weeks later. Because they told me if I made noise, the insurance payout would stall, and the crew needed the money, and—" His voice gave out entirely. He sat there on the deck of the *Provision*, in the lee of the reef where Tomás's skiff had washed up, and cried the way he hadn't let himself cry in six years — silently, horribly, with his whole body, the way you cry when the dam goes all at once.

Bo didn't touch him. Bo just sat there, shoulder an inch from his shoulder, and let the reef slide by.

* * *

Sebastian found them twenty minutes later. Kip had gotten most of himself back together — the way you do, the way you always do, the cracks papered over with work — and was coiling line with hands that only shook a little. Sebastian came up the companionway, took one look at the two of them sitting on the deck like a pair of shipwreck survivors, and didn't ask what happened, because Sebastian had a genius for knowing which questions would break things.

Instead he sat down on the other side of Kip, uninvited, and looked at the reef falling away behind them.

"I never read his final log," Sebastian said.

Kip's hands went still on the line.

"His boat log. The paper one. The board had it for the inquiry and then they gave it back and it's been in the chart drawer under the spare fenders for six years because I couldn't—" Sebastian exhaled. "I told the crew I read it. I told everyone I read it cover to cover, looking for answers. I never opened it. I couldn't stand the idea of his handwriting stopping."

The line hung slack in Kip's hands. "You *lied*?"

"For six years. To everyone. Including myself, mostly." Sebastian's jaw worked. "And now we're sailing back into the water where he died, on a job from a woman who I'd bet my last tooth knows more about that night than the board did, and I keep thinking — what if the log says something? What if it's been sitting under my spare fenders this whole time, saying something, and I was too much of a coward to listen?"

The wind shifted. Priya's voice came down from the wheelhouse, and Kip would remember afterward how carefully she said it, like she'd been listening at the door of their grief and had decided it was time:

"Sebastian. You should come up here. The Corvus just changed course."

Sebastian stood. Kip stood. And below them, in the chart drawer under the spare fenders, a logbook with a dead man's handwriting sat waiting, six years patient, for someone to open it.
