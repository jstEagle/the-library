## 8. Third Tide: Into the Verge

The charts lied.

Not in the way charts usually lie — the small honest lies of scale and drift that every navigator forgives — but in the grand, malicious way of a chart drawn by someone who had never actually been here. The Coral Verge appeared on Priya's table as a scattering of friendly dots, a reef field you could thread like needles through cloth. What lay beneath them instead was a drowned cathedral, spires and buttresses of coral rising two hundred feet from black water, and between them channels that narrowed, forked, and closed again like the thing was thinking about it.

"Whoever surveyed this," she said, "was drunk."

"Or dead," Bo offered from the engine hatch, where he was performing his morning liturgy of tapping things with a wrench until they agreed to work. "Surveyors who get it right tend to stay employed."

Priya marked their position with a grease pencil and did not look up at the horizon, because looking at the horizon meant seeing the Corvus fleet's smoke, three miles back and holding station like a debt collector. Two days now they'd shadowed her. Never closing. Never falling behind. Just *there*, patient as interest compounding.

"You could tell me we're lost," Kip said from the bow, where he'd appointed himself lookout on the grounds that the bow was furthest from Bo's wrench-tapping. "I feel like that would be comforting. Familiar."

"We are not lost." She traced the channel ahead. "We are precisely where I intended to be, which is a narrow place where their big hulls can't follow."

"And is there a reason we're going to a narrow place?"

"Yes. It's called the only way through."

Kip considered this. "So we're not lost, we're just committed."

"That's what lost means when you do it on purpose," Sebastian said, coming up the companionway with four mugs of coffee he'd made strong enough to strip varnish. He handed them out like communion. "Priya. Talk to me."

She turned the chart toward him and put her finger on the labyrinth's heart. "Here. The trench. If the Meridian Star went down in the Verge — and everything Imogen paid us says she did — she's here. The deep water collects in these three basins like rain in a gutter. Everything else is reef too shallow to hide a dinghy."

Sebastian studied it the way he studied all navigation problems, which was to say he nodded along convincingly while understanding perhaps forty percent of it and trusting her for the rest. Six years of that arrangement. It worked because she never once gave him reason to doubt it, and because he never once pretended otherwise.

"How long?"

"Two hours to the first basin. Then slow going. We'll want the tide with us — slack water in the channels, or the current'll fold us against coral like laundry."

"Then we go with the third tide." He said it lightly, but she heard the weight under it. They'd started counting tides the way condemned men count days, and everyone aboard knew it. "Anything else?"

"The Corvus stopped following an hour ago."

That got everyone's attention. Even Bo looked up from his wrench.

"Held station off the eastern spur," Priya continued. "Right where the deep water starts. Which tells me they know exactly where they're going, and they don't need us to lead them there."

"So they've been here before," Kip said quietly.

"Or they have better charts than ours." She met Sebastian's eyes. "Either way, we're not racing them anymore. We're racing whatever they already know."

* * *

The Verge took them at the turn of the tide, and it was beautiful, and Priya hated how much she loved it.

The water changed color first — from the honest blue-green of open sea to something layered, turquoise over teal over a darkness that wasn't color so much as depth announcing itself. Coral towers rose on either side of the channel, some of them taller than the boat's mast, crowned with fans and branches that moved in the swell like slow applause. Fish came off the walls in silver sheets when their wake touched them. A turtle the size of a dinner table regarded them with ancient disapproval and declined to move.

"It's showing off," Kip said.

"Everything out here is showing off," Priya said. "That's how it kills you. You're busy admiring the scenery when the channel closes."

And the channels did close. Twice in the next hour she brought them to a full stop, engine idling, while she read the water ahead — the dark seams where reef ran just under the surface, the pale threads of sand between them, the way the swell stood up or flattened depending on what lay beneath. Bo hung over the rail calling depths off the sounder in a voice gone flat and serious, all the humor wrung out of him by numbers. Forty feet. Twenty-five. Eleven.

"Eleven," Sebastian repeated, from the helm.

"Keel's eight," Bo said. "It's fine. It's fine. Probably fine."

"I love how you say probably," Sebastian said. "Very reassuring. Very nautical."

"Port ten," Priya said. "Follow my finger, not your instincts."

His instincts, to his credit, he surrendered without argument, which was more than most captains would do, and more than he'd have done six years ago. That was the thing people missed about Sebastian Villalobos, she thought, watching him ease the boat through a gap barely twice his beam, sweat standing on his forehead despite the breeze. Everyone saw the bravado, the grin, the debts and the swagger. Nobody saw that underneath it was a man who had learned, slowly and at cost, to hand over the wheel of his own judgment to other people and trust them with it. Tomás had taught him that. The hard way.

She didn't let herself think about Tomás beyond that. Not yet. Out here, thinking about Tomás was ballast you couldn't jettison, and she needed the boat light.

* * *

They found the first basin at midday, and it was empty.

A great round amphitheater of coral, three hundred yards across, plunging away into blue-black. Priya circled its rim twice while Bo ran the sounder and came up with nothing but walls and deeper walls. No wreck. No debris. Nothing but a silence so complete it felt staged.

"Second basin, then," Sebastian said.

"Second basin," she agreed, and turned them south, and tried not to notice that the Corvus's smoke had reappeared on the northern horizon, closer than before, as if they'd known the first basin would be empty too.

* * *

The second basin held the Meridian Star.

Priya saw it before anyone told her — saw it as a wrongness in the light, a shape in the blue below that her eyes kept trying to file as reef and failing. Then the sun shifted and the whole of her came clear at once, and Priya heard Kip swear softly at the bow, and Bo stop tapping things entirely.

She lay wedged in the trench at the basin's edge, a hundred and ninety feet down, and even at that distance she was enormous. A coastal freighter, maybe two hundred feet of her, driven into the trench at speed and at an angle that had folded her bow under an overhang of coral like a letter tucked into an envelope. Her superstructure still stood. Her masts still rose, draped in soft coral the color of fire. The Verge had spent six years upholstering her, claiming her, turning steel into reef — and had not managed, even slightly, to make her anything but a shipwreck. She looked less like a dead thing than a sleeping one. Like something waiting.

"God," Kip whispered. "She's *intact*."

"She's intact because she was sunk, not wrecked," Priya said, and then heard herself, and wished she could take it back.

But it was true, and everyone on deck could see it. Wrecks broke. Storms tore ships apart, scattered them across acres of bottom, left them as fields of ribbing and plate. This ship sat in one piece, settled neat as a coffin lid, driven deliberately into the one deep pocket in fifty square miles of reef. Ships didn't do that by accident. Ships didn't do that at all.

Nobody said anything else for a long moment. The swell slapped the hull. Somewhere below, the Meridian Star held her silence.

"Okay," Sebastian said finally, and his voice was carefully, deliberately level. "Bo. Water clarity? Can we dive her today?"

"Clear as glass down to maybe a hundred feet. Below that it goes dark fast. We'd be diving the last sixty on lights, in a trench, in a tide window." Bo paused. "It's doable. It's not *fun* doable."

"Nothing on this contract has been fun doable." Sebastian turned. "Kip, rig the tender. We go down together, first pair, before the light goes."

"Captain." Priya kept her voice mild. "Before anyone ties on a tank. Look at the trench mouth. Starboard side, where the coral overhangs."

He looked. So did the others. And it took a moment — the reef was good at hiding things, that was its whole profession — but then Kip saw it and pointed, and there was no unseeing it after.

A mooring line. Old, algae-furred, frayed at the end, anchored to a coral head at the trench lip and running down into the dark. And beside it, snagged in the coral thirty feet down, a dropped dive light, its housing cracked, its beam long dead. And further along the rim, half-buried in sand: a weight belt. Commercial make. Syndicate gray.

"They've been down," Bo said.

"They've been down *and come back up fast*," Priya said. She pointed at the mooring line again. "Look at the fray. That's a cut line, not a wear break. Somebody bailed off that line in a hurry. And the light—" she gestured at the dead torch hanging in the coral, "—you don't drop a light unless you're dropping everything. Something down there sent them up."

"Sharks?" Kip offered.

"Sharks don't make commercial divers drop their belts and cut their lines. Sharks don't make them leave gear behind worth more than our boat." She looked at Sebastian. "The Corvus found her, dove her, and failed. Failed badly enough to run. And then instead of reporting success or failure to whoever's paying them, they sat outside the reef for two days waiting for us."

"So they were told to wait for us," Sebastian said slowly. "Not to beat us to it. To *wait*."

"Which means whoever's paying them doesn't primarily want the box." Priya felt the cold arrive in her stomach, the navigator's cold, the one that came when the chart and the water disagreed. "If they wanted the box, they'd have taken it when they had divers on it. They want *us* to go down and get it. They want us to do the dangerous part."

The wind moved through the rigging with a sound almost like speech. Below them, patient as six years, the Meridian Star waited in her trench with whatever had frightened professional divers still folded somewhere inside her.

"Imogen said salvage," Kip said. His voice had gone quiet and hard. "Recover the box, collect the fee, pay the debt. That was the pitch."

"That was the pitch," Sebastian agreed.

"Then why does it smell like bait?"

Nobody answered him. That was answer enough, and they all knew it, and Priya watched the knowledge move around the cockpit from face to face like a lamp being passed — Bo's jaw tightening, Kip's knuckles whitening on the rail, Sebastian staring down at the drowned ship with an expression she'd seen on him exactly once before, six years ago, at a memorial with no body to bury.

"We could turn around," Bo said at last. "Right now. Run for it. Corvus can't follow us into the shallows."

"And spend the rest of our lives owing Delgado our fingers," Kip said. "And never knowing why Tomás—"

He stopped. But the name was out, hanging in the salt air, and no one reached to take it back.

"We dive," Sebastian said. "Today, this tide, before the Corvus works up the nerve to come in after us. But we dive smart. Shallow team on the superstructure first — nobody goes into the trench dark. And Priya—" he turned to her, and the grin he put on was the old one, the armor one, but his eyes weren't in it, "—keep us a way out. Whatever happens down there, I want to know that above us there's always a channel home."

"There's always a channel home," Priya said. "That's the one promise I've never broken."

She went below to plot the ascent routes and the escape lines and the tide windows, and it was only when she had the chart spread out under the lamp that she allowed herself the thought she'd been refusing since the basin opened beneath them:

The Corvus divers hadn't failed because of sharks or depth or current. She'd read enough salvage reports to know what a panicked ascent looked like, and that line had been cut from *below*. Something down there had happened in the dark of the trench, something bad enough to make hardened men abandon their gear, and the syndicate had responded not by retrieving the box themselves but by hiring a desperate, indebted crew with nothing left to lose.

Bait, Kip had said. And the hook was already set, and the line was already paying out, and the Meridian Star lay below them in the dark with her secret held against her chest like a passenger who would not disembark.

Above her, she heard Sebastian call down the companionway, voice too bright:

"Priya! Tide turns in twenty. Get up here — we're going to meet an old friend."

She looked at the chart, at the small grease-pencil cross she'd drawn over the trench, and thought: *no, Captain. We're going to meet six years of lies. The friend part is just how it introduces itself.*

She capped the marker, climbed into the light, and turned the boat toward the wreck.
