## 9. The Box That Sings

The water over the Meridian Star was the color of a bruise healing — deep purple shading into green where the light gave up trying. Sebastian Villalobos hung at fifteen meters, breathing slow through his regulator, and let the wreck come up to meet him.

She was bigger than he remembered from the survey scans. A coastal freighter, four hundred tons of honest steel that had once hauled rice and diesel and mail to islands that didn't have much of either. Now she lay on her port side in the trench like a woman who'd lain down mid-sentence and never finished the thought. Coral had already begun its patient work, orange and violet fingers prying at her railings, softening her edges, turning tragedy into architecture.

"Easy," Kip said in his earpiece, his voice crackling through the hardline tether. "You're doing the thing."

"What thing?"

"The thing where you go quiet and poetic and forget you're using air somebody has to pay for."

Sebastian smiled around his mouthpiece, which did nothing for anyone but made him feel better. "I'm being reverent."

"You're being expensive. Dive plan says forty minutes bottom time. We're at twelve."

They swam down along the starboard hull, fins kicking up little ghosts of silt, past a porthole where a school of sergeant-majors had taken up residence like tenants who'd won the building in a lawsuit. Sebastian kept his eyes moving the way Priya had drilled them: hull, column, hull, column. The Verge's drowned labyrinth was full of places for a diver to become a statistic, and they'd already used up their lifetime quota of statistics six years ago.

But his eyes kept drifting back to the wreck herself. To the superstructure amidships, where the bridge would be. Where the radio room would be.

Where Tomás would have been standing, if Tomás had ever stood on this ship at all.

He hadn't. That was the thing Sebastian kept having to remind himself. Tomás hadn't died here. He'd died forty miles east, alone, in a skiff, on a night so clear the stars had come down to watch. Whatever happened aboard the Meridian Star had happened without him — and yet every road out of that night led back to this rusting hull, and Sebastian had spent six years refusing to walk any of them until debt and Imogen Reyes dragged him here by the collar.

"There," Kip said. "Bridge access. Door's open."

The bridge door hung askew, framed in a ruff of white coral like an old man's beard. Beyond it, darkness with a green tint, the particular darkness of enclosed water where the light comes in sideways and gives up halfway. Sebastian clicked on his dive light and the beam cut a clean tunnel through it.

Inside, the bridge was a museum of the last ordinary minute. The helm wheel still stood upright, furred with algae. A coffee mug sat fused to the deck by six years of sediment, its handle reaching up like a small brass question mark. On the bulkhead, a duty roster in faded marker ink, names half-legible under a skin of calcium.

And there, bolted behind the helm console where regulations said it must be, the black box.

Except it wasn't black. It was a dull industrial grey, a cylinder the size of a man's forearm, and even before Sebastian got close enough to wipe away the silt, something about it made the hair on his arms stand up inside his wetsuit.

"Kip."

"I see it."

Because there, stamped into the casing in raised letters that the sea hadn't managed to erode, was a seal. A stylized D wrapped in a rope border. Sebastian knew that seal the way a man knows the face of the person holding his loan papers. Delgado Maritime Holdings. The syndicate's shipping arm. The syndicate's *everything* arm.

"That's not right," Kip said. His voice had gone flat, which for Kip meant furious; he only went quiet when the anger was too big for volume. "Black boxes don't get branded. They're not products. They're evidence."

"Maybe the ship's owner put their mark on it."

"Vessels owned by shell companies put *nothing* on them. That's the whole point of shell companies." Kip drifted closer, gloved fingers hovering over the seal without touching. "This is a claim. This is somebody saying *mine* before anybody even knew there was something to claim."

Sebastian looked at the box sitting in its bracket behind the wheel of a dead ship, wearing the sigil of the people who wanted to take his boat and his fingers, and felt the day tilt slightly beneath him, the way the deck of a vessel tilts when she takes water somewhere you can't see.

"Imogen said recover it," he said. "She didn't say whose it was."

"Imogen says a lot of things." Kip unclipped the pry bar from his harness. "We came for it. We're taking it. We can be angry on the surface where the air is free."

It took ten minutes to work the box free of its bracket. The mounting bolts had corroded into sculptures of themselves, and Kip cursed them with the fluent, inventive blasphemy of a man who'd grown up fixing engines with his grandfather, while Sebastian held the light and kept one eye on the doorway and the green dark beyond it. Twice he thought he saw movement out there — a shadow with intention — and twice it resolved into fish, or light, or nothing.

The box came loose all at once, and Kip caught it against his chest, and for a moment they just hung there in the drowned bridge of the Meridian Star, two men cradling a grey cylinder that weighed almost nothing in the water and everything everywhere else.

"Tomás," Kip said quietly. It wasn't clear whether he was praying or swearing. With Kip it was usually both.

* * *

The ascent was textbook. Slow, with the safety stop at five meters, the two of them hanging in the blue like ornaments on a Christmas tree nobody had gotten around to decorating. Sebastian watched his depth gauge count down and let himself feel it — really feel it, for the first time since they'd crossed into the Verge — the small hot coal of triumph in his chest.

They'd done it. Against the clock, against the Corvus fleet, against six years of sworn-off water and his own gut telling him this whole affair smelled like fish left in the sun. They had the box. Whatever was on it, whatever Imogen wanted with it, it was theirs now, in their hands, coming up toward their boat and their crew and whatever came next.

He should have known better. In Sebastian's long experience, triumph was like a fish you spotted from the dock: always bigger in the water than it turned out to be in your hands, and always, always surrounded by things with teeth.

The tender sat at anchor fifty meters off the wreck site, bobbing in a swell that had begun to organize itself into something with opinions. Bo's voice crackled in his ear as they surfaced alongside.

"Took your time. Also, heads up — I'm reading a contact south of you, fast mover, no transponder. Could be nothing. Could be Corvus."

"How long?" Sebastian hauled the box up onto the tender's deck, then reached back for Kip's arm.

"Contact was three miles out when I called it. So — however long three miles takes a boat that doesn't care what you think about it."

Kip came over the gunwale, ripped his regulator out, and pushed his mask up onto his forehead, grinning like a man who'd just robbed a bank and gotten away with the teller too. "We got it," he said. "Seb. *Seb.* We actually got it." He thumped the box with the flat of his hand. "Six years. Six years of that night sitting in my chest like an anchor, and the answer's been sitting in the mud the whole time wearing a *Delgado sticker*—"

"Start the engine," Sebastian said.

Bo was already on it, or would have been, except the tender's engine chose that moment to produce a sound like a cat being fed into a wood chipper and then die with a shudder that Sebastian felt through the deck plates.

Everyone looked at everyone.

"That," Bo said over the comms from the main boat, "was not me. I tuned that engine myself two days ago. That engine was purring."

"Later!" Kip was already scrambling aft, hauling the spare fuel line, because Kip processed fear by doing things with his hands. "Get the sails up, get us moving, anything—"

The Corvus chase boat came around the reef point at speed.

She was a beautiful piece of bad news, thirty feet of matte-grey hull with a low profile and twin outboards running quiet, the kind of boat syndicates buy specifically so that witnesses can't describe them later. She came on at maybe twenty knots, straight-lined, unhurried, the way a shark moves when it has already decided.

"Ramming course," Priya's voice said, calm as a weather report, which somehow made it worse. "Sebastian, they are absolutely going to ram you. Get clear of your anchor line."

Sebastian lunged for the anchor winch. Kip got there first, hands flying, and the chain rattled up and over and splashed free just as Sebastian threw the tiller hard over and the tender began her slow, heavy, sailless turn — a fat man starting to run in a dream.

The chase boat didn't adjust course. Didn't need to.

Impact came on the starboard quarter, a glancing blow that was clearly deliberate in its geometry — enough to spin the tender like a toy, not enough to sink her. Not yet. Sebastian went down on one knee, shoulder cracking against the gunwale, and heard Kip make a sound he'd never heard Kip make before, short and punched-out, the sound of a body meeting hardware.

The tender wallowed. The chase boat swung wide in a smooth arc and came around again, slower now, pacing them, and through the spray Sebastian could see figures at her rail. Masks down. One of them raised something that was either a camera or a weapon, and at that distance the difference was academic.

"Kip." Sebastian crawled toward the sound of breathing. "Kip, talk to me."

"'M fine." Kip was curled against the console, one arm clamped across his ribs, face the color of old candle wax. "Railing. Caught me. Cracked something, maybe. Ask me later when I'm dramatic about it."

"They're coming around again," Priya said. "Bo is bringing the *Provision* to you but she's four minutes out and the Corvus will be there in ninety seconds. Sebastian, I need a decision."

The second impact never came. Instead the chase boat slowed to a stop a hundred meters off, and a voice came across the water on a loudhailer, pleasant and bored, the voice of a man reading someone else's script.

"Tender *Provision*, this is a safety notice. You are operating in restricted waters without salvage authorization. Cease operations and await escort." A pause, and then, almost as an afterthought, with genuine warmth: "Nice dive today. Deep for amateurs."

Then the boat turned and ran, south, fast, gone behind the reef line before Bo's engines had finished their approach.

* * *

They got Kip aboard the *Provision* through the side gate, four hands and a lot of shouting, and laid him out on the deckhouse bench while Bo — who doubled as the crew's doctor in the way that engineers double as doctors, i.e., confidently and with tools — pressed along his ribs and watched his face.

"Bruised, probably cracked, not punctured," Bo announced. "You're going to live, and you're going to complain, and those are separate facts."

"I would never complain," Kip said, through his teeth, sweating.

Priya appeared at Sebastian's elbow with the chart table between them like a lectern. Her eyes went to the black box, sitting in its wet towel on the deck like a catch they weren't sure was legal.

"The chase boat knew we were diving," she said quietly. "They waited until we were in the water and our tender was anchored. That's not patrol behavior. That's surveillance behavior. Someone told them exactly where we'd be and when."

"Or they've been watching us since the Verge."

"Possibly. But Sebastian." She lowered her voice further, and Sebastian braced himself, because Priya lowering her voice was how artillery began. "The engine. It didn't fail on its own. I helped Bo tune it. And when I pulled the fuel line just now, the filter housing was finger-loose. Somebody opened it. Aboard this boat. Recently."

Sebastian looked at the box. At Kip, gray and stubborn on the bench, being brave at Bo. At the southern horizon where the chase boat had gone to report to whoever counted the money.

Nothing is won clean, he thought. Nothing ever had been. Every prize he'd ever pulled out of the sea had come up trailing hooks he hadn't set.

"Well," he said, and picked up the black box, and felt its weight resolve from ounces into tons. "Then I guess we'd better find out what's on this thing that's worth breaking our engine and our Kip. Because somebody wants it very badly — and I want to know if it's the same somebody who hired us."

From the south, faint under the wind, came the sound of engines. More than one.

Priya followed his gaze to the horizon, and her face did the arithmetic before her mouth did. "That'll be the rest of the Corvus fleet," she said. "Coming to see what survived the first attempt."
