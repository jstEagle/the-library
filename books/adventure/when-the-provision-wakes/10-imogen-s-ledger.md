## 10. Imogen's Ledger

The *Cormorant's Grace* was everything Sebastian's boat was not, and Imogen Reyes had built it that way on purpose.

She stood at the rail of her support ship and watched the *Provision* come alongside through the gray chop of the Fourth Tide, and she let herself feel, for exactly three seconds, the old satisfaction of having more. Bigger crane. Deeper hold. A galley that smelled of cardamom instead of diesel and despair. Then the satisfaction curdled, the way it always did now, into something closer to shame, and she went down to meet them at the gangway with her hands visible and her voice level.

Because hands mattered. She'd learned that in the syndicate. You showed your hands or you showed your intentions, and only amateurs confused the two.

"Villalobos." She nodded past him to the others clustered behind — Kip with his arm bound and his eyes like two lit fuses, Priya with her tablet hugged to her chest like a shield, Bo hanging back with the careful blankness of a man doing arithmetic he didn't want anyone to see. "You look terrible."

"You should see the other tender," Sebastian said.

"I did. It's confetti." She stepped aside to let them aboard. "Come below. There's coffee that isn't an insult, and there are things you need to hear before the Corvus finds its nerve again."

Kip didn't move. "Why would we tell you anything?"

"Because I'm about to tell you everything," Imogen said, "and you can decide afterward whether to throw me overboard. Fair warning: I float well. Years of practice staying above water while better people sank."

That landed harder than she'd intended. She watched it land — saw Kip's jaw tighten, saw Priya exchange a glance with Sebastian that carried six years of history in a single flicker. Good, some cold practical part of her thought. Let them be angry. Anger keeps people awake.

She led them down into her cabin, which she'd cleared of everything except a table, six chairs, and a bottle of rum she had no intention of opening. On the table, face down, lay a folder of documents so old the paper had gone the color of weak tea.

* * *

She waited until they were seated — all except Kip, who leaned against the doorframe as if standing between her and the exit were a job he'd assigned himself — before she turned the folder over.

"This is Delgado stationery," she said. "From eleven years ago. My first year as a fleet officer for their insurance arm."

Sebastian picked it up. His hands were steady; his eyes weren't. "I don't read syndicate mail."

"You'll want to read this one." She kept her voice gentle, which took more effort than shouting. "It's a hull valuation. The *Meridian Star*, assessed at four point one million. And beneath it—" she tapped the second page, "—a payout authorization, pre-signed, dated nine days after the assessment. Before anything happened to her. The payout was approved before the ship ever left port on her last voyage."

Priya's breath caught. Bo said nothing at all, but his stillness changed quality, the way ice changes just before it cracks.

"They scuttled her," Sebastian said flatly. "For the insurance."

"They scuttled her," Imogen confirmed, "and the black box you pulled off her seabed holds the captain's final hour. The order coming in. The crew being told to abandon and keep quiet. The pumps being opened by hand." She paused. "And other things. Things I haven't heard. Things I paid you to bring up because I couldn't go down myself without every diver in the Verge asking why Imogen Reyes suddenly cares about a ten-year-old wreck."

"Why couldn't you?" Kip asked from the door. His voice was quiet, which from Kip was worse than shouting. "Money's no object. You've got a fleet. Why hire the one crew on this coast with a reason to hate that wreck?"

Imogen met his eyes. This was the hinge. She'd rehearsed this conversation forty times in the dark of her cabin, and every version ended with her telling the truth, and every version also ended with them not believing her, and she had finally accepted that both things could be true at once.

"Because I signed the payout authorization," she said. "Page two. Bottom right corner. That's my signature, Kip. I was twenty-six years old and I needed the syndicate to trust me, and a junior officer who rubber-stamps a valuation is useful, and a junior officer who asks questions is cargo. So I stamped it. Nine days later the *Meridian Star* went into the Verge with her sea cocks open, and thirty-one people came home in boats, and one of them—" her voice did something she hated, a small fracture, quickly plastered, "—one of them wasn't Tomás Ferro."

The silence in the cabin had weight. She felt it settle on her shoulders like wet canvas.

"The box is proof," she went on, faster now, because stopping would be drowning. "Real, physical, datable proof. The syndicate has spent ten years making sure no such thing surfaces. The Corvus isn't racing you for salvage rights — salvage rights are worthless, the wreck's been stripped. They're racing you because Delgado needs that box destroyed, and they need it destroyed quietly, and they need the crew who found it to be either bought or buried. Preferably both. I know because until eight months ago, I was the person giving those orders."

"And then?" Sebastian asked. He hadn't touched the folder since reading the signature. He was watching her the way he used to watch weather — not the pretty clouds, the ones behind them.

"And then I found out what happened to Tomás." She laid the last document on the table: a radio log excerpt, syndicate-flagged, intercepted the night of the sinking. "He was a deckhand on the *Star*. He heard the order. He refused it. He keyed the open channel and started reading out the scuttling order, word for word, names and dates, before someone cut his transmission and, I believe, cut a great deal more than that. The official log says he fell overboard during evacuation. The intercept says otherwise."

Kip made a sound. Not a word. Something older than words. Imogen didn't look at him — looking felt like theft.

"So yes," she said. "I hired you because you're good. But I hired you *specifically* because you're the crew that swore off this water, and I knew you'd treat the box like it was made of bone. Everyone else Delgado could buy. I've watched them buy divers, harbormasters, coroners. I couldn't risk buying the people who loved him." She finally looked up. "I'm not redeeming myself. Redemption is a thing other people give you. I'm just trying to get the evidence above water before the last people who remember why it matters are dead or paid off. Call it whatever word hurts less."

* * *

For a long moment nobody spoke. The ship rolled beneath them, patient as a sleeping animal.

Then Sebastian pushed back his chair and stood, and Imogen braced herself, because Sebastian Villalobos angry was loud, but Sebastian Villalobos *quiet* was a barometer falling.

"Here's my problem," he said. "Every word you've said today checks out. The signature's real. The intercept's real. I believe the scuttling, I believe the cover-up, and God help me, I believe you about Tomás." He laid both palms flat on the table. "But here's what I can't get past. Eight months ago you stop being Delgado. Fine. And the very first thing you do is find the most indebted, most desperate crew on the coast and hand them a contract worth more than everything they owe? You didn't pick us despite our debt, Imogen. You picked us *because* of it. A crew that desperate doesn't ask questions. A crew that desperate delivers the box to whoever's holding the contract."

"That's not—"

"It's exactly what happened." His voice stayed low. Terrible. "We dove. We bled. Kip got rammed by a Corvus boat within an hour of lifting your precious box, and where was your fleet? Shadowing us at a distance, close enough to collect, far enough to stay clean. You want me to believe the woman who taught me how to run a salvage ledger suddenly forgot how contracts work?" He shook his head slowly. "You're not redeeming yourself. You're using us to carry it up, same as the syndicate used you to sign it away. The only difference is what happens at the handoff — and I notice you haven't told us what happens at the handoff."

Imogen opened her mouth. Closed it. Because the awful truth was that there *was* a plan for the handoff, and it involved a maritime court liaison meeting her at Port Calloway, and it involved the kind of legal machinery that required money and influence, and if she explained all that it would sound exactly like what it was: a woman with syndicate resources planning to use syndicate methods, and trusting no one, least of all the people she'd hired, to do it right.

"I haven't told you," she admitted, "because every version of the plan ends with the box leaving my sight, and I don't trust anyone else's hands. Including yours. Including mine." She laughed, short and bitter. "There it is. Ten years in the machine and I trust nothing that breathes. That's what they build in you, Villalobos. That's my ledger. Every line of it."

Kip pushed off the doorframe. "Tomás trusted you," he said. "Did you know that? After the *Star* went down, when everyone said it was bad luck, bad weather, bad seamanship — Tomás's last message to me, the night before he shipped out, was 'Reyes checked the manifests, so it's clean.' He vouched for you." His voice cracked down the middle. "So either you fooled him too, or you were already signing things you shouldn't have, and either way he died carrying your name in his mouth like it meant something."

Imogen had prepared for anger. She had not prepared for that. It went through her like a spar through a hull, and for a moment she couldn't speak at all, and the whole cabin watched her come apart and reassemble, piece by piece, in real time.

"He wasn't wrong," she managed finally. "In eleven years ago, I would have checked the manifests. In eleven years ago, I might have refused the stamp. I've spent eight months trying to find that girl again, and all I've found is her signature on the worst document of my life." She straightened. "So. Believe me or don't. Take the box and run, sell it back to Delgado, drop it in the trench — I genuinely can't stop you, and part of me, the honest part, says you'd have earned any of it. But the monsoon makes landfall in two tides, the Corvus is rearming, and somewhere behind us is a syndicate cutter that hasn't shown itself yet because it doesn't need to. Whatever you're going to choose, choose it soon."

* * *

They filed out one by one. Bo first, without a word, though at the door he paused and looked back at her with an expression she couldn't parse — something between pity and recognition, as if he'd seen a familiar animal in an unfamiliar cage. Priya next, with a single nod, the nod of a navigator marking a hazard on a chart: *noted, untrusted, accounted for.*

Kip last. He stopped beside her chair, and for a horrible moment she thought he was going to strike her, and she found she wouldn't have blamed him.

Instead he set something on the table. A small brass whistle, salt-green with age, on a lanyard worn soft.

"Tomás's," he said. "Off his skiff. I've carried it six years waiting for someone to tell me what it was for." His eyes were dry and that was the worst part. "Now I know. It was for calling out liars. He'd blow it across the deck whenever the skipper fudged a sounding." He looked at her a long moment. "You get one chance, Reyes. One. When the time comes, you stand where everyone can see you and you tell the truth with your own mouth, or I swear on his grave I will make sure the whole Verge knows exactly whose signature is on page two."

Then he was gone, and Imogen sat alone in her too-clean cabin with a dead man's whistle and a folder full of her own handwriting, and outside the porthole the sky had begun to organize itself into something with intent.

She poured the rum she'd sworn not to open, drank half of it standing, and reached for the radio.

There was one call left to make — the call she'd been dreading for eight months, to the one person who knew the rest of the ledger, the parts she hadn't shown the *Provision*'s crew because showing them would have broken the contract, and possibly the crew.

She keyed the channel. Static answered, then a voice she knew, calm and unhurried, with the particular patience of someone who has never once been surprised.

"Reyes. You're late."

"I know," Imogen said. "And I need to tell you the plan's changed."

On the other end of the line, something that was almost a laugh. "No," the voice said. "It really hasn't."
