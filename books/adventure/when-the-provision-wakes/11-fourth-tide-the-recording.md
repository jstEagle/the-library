## 11. Fourth Tide: The Recording

Priya had always believed that a boat at anchor made a particular kind of silence — not the absence of sound but the presence of held breath. The *Provision* sat on the black water of a cove she'd chosen herself, tucked behind a spine of coral that would hide them from the Corvus's sweep patterns for maybe four hours, maybe less. Four hours was enough. It had to be.

She stood in the galley doorway and watched her crew pretend they weren't waiting for her.

Sebastian sat at the table with the black box in front of him like it was a live grenade that had learned manners. Bo had rigged a playback rig out of the box's diagnostic port, three adapters, and half a soldering iron — "don't ask," Bo had said, and nobody asked — and now sat with their arms crossed, vibrating slightly, which was how Bo expressed patience. Kip leaned against the bulkhead with his arm still strapped from the Corvus ramming, looking like a man who'd been told he could open one present early and hadn't slept since.

"Nobody touches it until Priya says," Sebastian said, for the fourth time.

"I'm standing right here," Priya said. "I can hear you saying things about me."

"Just establishing protocol."

"Your protocol is that we've been staring at a metal box for twenty minutes like it owes us money."

"It might," Kip said quietly, and the joke landed wrong, the way jokes do when everyone knows they're true.

Priya came into the galley and closed the door. That was the ritual, such as it was: door shut, lamp dimmed, radio off even though the radio had been tampered with once already and Bo had rebuilt it from scratch twice. Outside, the fourth tide was building. You could feel it in the way the hull breathed against its anchor line — a longer pull, a slower release, the sea clearing its throat before the monsoon arrived to sing.

"Rules," Priya said, because someone had to say rules or this crew would improvise itself into a reef. "We listen all the way through. Nobody talks over it. Nobody decides anything until it's done. And if anyone cries—" she looked at Kip, who looked back with red-rimmed defiance— "that's allowed. Obviously."

"Gee, thanks," Kip muttered.

Bo reached over and pressed play.

* * *

The first thing they heard was static, long and flat, the ocean talking to itself. Then a click, and a voice so crisp it startled them all — the Meridian Star's bridge recorder had been a good machine, better than anything on the *Provision*, and six years underwater hadn't dulled it much.

"—nineteen-forty hours, Meridian Star, position logged. Captain Okafor on the bridge." A pause, papers shuffling. "Weather holding. Passengers settled."

"That's him," Kip said, then clamped his mouth shut. Tomás's voice. Not the recording yet — just Tomás doing his job, logging the evening, reading numbers off a gauge. He sounded exactly as he'd sounded. Younger, maybe. Unbothered. A man with no idea he had eleven minutes left.

Priya watched Sebastian's hands flatten on the table. She watched Bo stop vibrating entirely, which was somehow worse.

The minutes rolled by in the small administrative music of a ship at night. Depth soundings. A complaint about the coffee. Someone laughing in the background — a woman's laugh, quick and bright, gone again. Priya found herself cataloguing details uselessly, the way you do when you're bracing: the clock chimed eight; a hatch squealed; Tomás hummed two bars of something off-key and stopped when he noticed himself doing it.

Then the other voice entered, and the temperature of the room changed.

It was calm. That was the terrible thing about it, the thing Priya would remember for the rest of her life — how calm it was. An officer's voice, clipped and official, reading from what sounded like a prepared statement.

"All senior crew to acknowledge. This is a directed scuttling order, authorized under seal. Flooding sequence begins at twenty-one hundred. Lifeboats are pre-positioned. Insurance documentation is complete. Acknowledge."

Silence on the recording. Long enough that Priya counted her own heartbeats. Then Tomás's voice again, and it wasn't the same voice. Something had been taken out of it — the lightness, the humming, the man who complained about coffee — and what remained was very quiet and very steady.

"Say that again."

"You heard me, Villalobos."

The galley went absolutely still.

Sebastian didn't move. Priya didn't breathe. Of course. Of course the name would be there. Six years they'd carried Tomás's death like ballast, and the whole time his name had been sitting in a sealed box at the bottom of the Coral Verge, spoken aloud by a stranger, tied to the word *scuttling*.

"You're telling me," Tomás said, each word placed down carefully, like a man carrying explosives, "that you intend to sink this ship. With two hundred and six people aboard."

"The passengers will be evacuated to the escort vessel before the sequence. This has been arranged."

"Arranged." A short laugh with nothing in it. "And the crew below decks? The engine room? Marisol's watch doesn't get an escort vessel, does it. I've seen the evacuation manifest. There isn't one. There's a list of names and a column marked 'expendable.'"

"Villalobos. Think about your sister's medical bills. Think about what happens to men who—"

"Don't." The word cracked across the recording like a whip. "Don't you dare say her name. Don't you dare stand on my bridge and offer me money to drown my own shipmates."

Kip made a sound low in his throat. Priya reached over without looking and put her hand on his good arm and left it there.

On the recording, the calm voice tried again — softer now, the softness worse than the threat. "You're a good officer, Tomás. That's why you were chosen. Men who follow orders get taken care of. Men who make noise get lost at sea. It's an old story."

"Yeah," Tomás said. "I know how it ends. I read it."

A chair scraped. Footsteps. Then Tomás's voice came close to the recorder, close enough that it filled the whole galley of the *Provision*, close enough that it felt less like listening and more like being addressed directly, personally, across six years and forty fathoms:

"If anyone ever hears this — the order came from inside. Delgado marine syndicate, through the first officer, sealed orders, insurance job. I'm going to open the lifeboat davits myself and I'm going to get every soul on this ship off that I can, and then I'm going to stay at that board and log every name of every man who signed this. Let it sink with me if it has to. But it won't. Somebody finds these things. Somebody always—"

Static. A hard cut — someone had hit the recorder, or the power, or him. When the audio resumed it was muffled, distant, the microphone picking up only fragments: running feet, a klaxon, the deep groan of a hull taking water it had been ordered to take. And under it, faintly, Tomás's voice calling names. Calling them one by one, deck by deck, patient as a lighthouse, right up until the water reached the bridge and the recording became the sea.

Then nothing. Forty seconds of nothing. The tape ran out.

* * *

Nobody moved.

The lamp hissed softly. The hull breathed against its anchor. Somewhere above them, the fourth tide was turning, dragging the world another degree toward the monsoon.

Kip broke first. Not crying, exactly — Priya had seen Kip cry at Tomás's memorial and this was different, this was fury wearing grief's face, this was a man discovering that the story he'd been living inside for six years had been a lie with a body count. "They *told* him," he said. "They stood on his own bridge and offered him money and he — he stayed. He stayed and called the names—"

"He got them off," Bo said. Their voice was rough, stripped of all its usual machinery-humor. "Two hundred and six souls, the inquiry said. Two hundred and six survivors, zero passenger deaths. Everyone always said Okafor was a miracle worker. It was never Okafor."

"It was never an accident," Priya said, and heard her own voice come out flat and precise, the navigator's voice, the one she used for reefs. Because that was the thing, wasn't it. She'd spent six years treating Tomás's death like weather — a thing that happened, a thing you charted around, a thing you swore off seas about. Weather didn't have a first officer with sealed orders. Weather didn't have a column marked *expendable*. "It was murder. Slow, bureaucratic, well-documented murder, and we've been mourning it like a storm."

Sebastian finally spoke. "He said our name."

"What?"

"On the tape. The officer said *Villalobos*." Sebastian's hands were still flat on the table, pressing down as if the table might otherwise float away. "Tomás took my name onto that bridge. Whatever he did — whatever he refused — he did it under our flag. Under this crew. And we spent six years thinking he panicked, or got unlucky, or—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "I never read his final log. I told you that. I couldn't. And the whole time the real log was down there, waiting, singing."

"The box sings," Bo said. "That's what I kept saying. Everyone thought I meant the sonar ping. I meant this. It's been singing the whole time. We just needed to dive down and listen."

Priya turned it over in her mind, the navigator's habit, plotting courses through bad news. "Here's what it means. Here's what it actually means, so nobody drifts." She held up fingers. "One: the Meridian Star was scuttled for insurance by the Delgado syndicate. Two: the syndicate sent a rival fleet and a boarding cutter after us because they knew what was in this box before we did — which means Imogen knew, which means Imogen has known for six years. Three: the moment we surfaced with it, we stopped being debtors. Debtors get collections. We're witnesses now. Witnesses get weather."

"Cheerful," Kip said.

"I'm a navigator. Cheerfulness is above my pay grade, which is currently negative."

That got the smallest huff out of Bo, which Priya counted as a victory, because a crew that could still huff was a crew that could still steer.

"So what do we do with it?" Sebastian asked. And there it was — the question underneath the question, the one Priya had felt circling them since the cove, since the wreck, maybe since the day Imogen Reyes had walked onto their dock with a contract and a smile. Deliver it and be paid. Bury it and be safe. Or—

Kip answered before anyone else could, pushing off the bulkhead, strap and all. "We play it. Every frequency. Every relay in the Verge. We let the whole sea hear what he—" His voice caught, and he pushed through it. "Six years I thought he abandoned us. Took a berth on a big fancy liner and forgot the little boats. He didn't forget anything. He died doing the exact thing this crew does — pulling idiots out of bad water. Except he pulled out two hundred and six of them, and then he stayed to keep the record straight." He slammed his palm on the table, gentle, controlled, the way you'd knock on a friend's door. "We owe him the truth. It's the only thing he asked for. *Somebody finds these things.*"

"And the syndicate?" Priya asked, not to argue — to navigate. Someone had to say the rocks out loud. "Delgado owns the Verge's relays, half its harbors, and both judges who'd hear any case we filed. If we broadcast, we're not debtors anymore either. We're targets. Permanently. There's no paying that off, no sailing away clean. Every one of you needs to understand that's the tide we'd be riding."

"Been riding worse tides all week," Bo said.

"On a boat with half its fuel and a rammed tender," Priya said.

"Been riding those too."

Sebastian looked down at the box. In the lamplight it was just a dented canister, barnacle-scarred, syndicate-sealed — the most expensive object any of them had ever touched and worth precisely nothing except what it said. He put his hand on it the way you'd put a hand on a shoulder.

"He logged every name," Sebastian said. "Every man who signed the order. It's all on here, isn't it? Names, seals, dates. Enough to hang them."

"Enough to hang the whole ladder," Bo confirmed. "First officer up to whoever countersigned the insurance. It's a confession with a chain of custody."

"Then we don't sell it," Sebastian said. "And we don't bury it." He looked up, and Priya saw something settle in his face that she'd last seen the day they'd voted to break their oath — but different now, cleaned of desperation, burned down to the metal. "We finish his log. He started the record; we end it. Out loud, where nobody can buy it, drown it, or seal it in a box."

Priya nodded slowly. It was a terrible course. It was also, she realized, the only heading that pointed anywhere she wanted to arrive. "You understand the navigation problem," she said. "The only relay tower big enough to punch through monsoon interference sits past the storm track. To reach it before landfall, we ride the eye."

Silence. Bo whistled, low. "Through the eye."

"Through the eye," Priya confirmed. "Which means threading the one corridor of calm in the worst weather this sea produces, in a boat with patched fuel lines, while a syndicate fleet decides whether we're worth chasing into hell." She let that sit, then shrugged. "I've plotted worse. Once. For a bet. We lost the bet."

Kip grinned — wet-eyed, furious, alive. "When do we leave?"

Before anyone could answer, the lamp flickered. Not the lamp — the whole boat. The lights dipped, steadied, dipped again, and beneath their feet the *Provision* shivered like a dog hearing thunder that hadn't arrived yet. Priya was moving before she'd decided to move, out the galley door and up into the cockpit, and the others followed, and together they looked south.

The horizon had changed. That was the only way to say it. Where the stars had been an hour ago there was now a darkness with texture to it, a wall rising out of the sea, and lightning was working inside it like something trying to get out. The fifth tide wouldn't wait for the sixth. The monsoon was coming early, and it was coming straight at them.

Behind her, in the galley, the playback rig hissed softly to itself, forty seconds of recorded ocean where a brave man's voice used to be.

"Hold on to something," Priya said. "We leave now."
