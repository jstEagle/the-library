## 12. The Offer

The seaplane came out of the southeast at half past noon, fat and white and wrong, like a swan that had wandered into a knife fight.

Sebastian saw it first from the wheelhouse, a glint against the bruise-colored clouds stacking up over the Verge, and his stomach did the thing it always did when something with wings and money appeared over open water. Nothing good flew. Good things stayed on the ground where they belonged, near bars and hospitals and other places Sebastian had been banned from.

"Bo," he called down the companionway. "Company."

Bo's head appeared, grease on his chin, a wrench in one hand like he'd been hoping for an excuse. "The Corvus?"

"Worse. It's got landing gear."

They watched it come down on the flat water a quarter mile off the stern, throwing up a fan of spray, and then it taxied toward them with the unhurried confidence of a thing that had never once been told no. Painted on its flank, in cheerful yellow, was a stylized sunburst that Sebastian knew from a hundred late-night conversations and one very bad night with a baseball bat.

Delgado. The smile of the syndicate, come to call in person.

"That's Marisol's plane," Kip said quietly. He'd come up without being asked, the way he'd been doing since the dive, his arm still strapped from the ramming, his eyes doing that thing where they looked at everything twice. "I'd know that ugly sunburst anywhere."

"You know the pilot?"

"I know the *passenger*." Kip's jaw worked. "That's Vásquez. The one who smiled at us in the shack."

Sebastian felt the old fear, the dockside fear, the thirty-days-and-your-fingers fear, climb up his spine and settle in behind his teeth where he kept it. He rolled his shoulders. He put on the face.

"Right," he said. "Everybody remember the plan."

"We don't have a plan," Priya said, emerging from the nav station with a pencil still tucked behind her ear.

"Then everybody remember we don't have a plan, and act natural about it."

* * *

Vásquez came up the boarding ladder like a man arriving at a garden party, in a linen suit the color of sea foam, dry despite the spray, dry despite everything, because the world apparently declined to wet certain people on principle. He was carrying a small cooler. That was the detail that would keep Sebastian up that night, if he slept at all: the cooler.

"Captain Villalobos." Vásquez's smile was the same one from the shack — wide, warm, and about as sincere as a painted lighthouse. "You look well. Salt air agrees with you. Debt agrees with you even more, I find. Keeps a man *crisp*."

"Vásquez." Sebastian didn't offer a hand. Vásquez offered his anyway, to the empty air, and then withdrew it with a little laugh as though they'd shared a joke. "You're a long way from your shack."

"And you're a long way from yours. Funny how the sea does that. Brings people together." He set the cooler down on the deck, patted it once, and stepped back with his hands clasped behind him like a man admiring a sunset. "I brought refreshments. Real coffee. The good kind, from before. A small courtesy."

Behind him, at a respectful distance, the seaplane idled on the swell, its engines ticking. Sebastian counted the silhouettes inside it. Three. Maybe four. He'd counted three silhouettes in a lot of places in his life and been wrong about most of them.

"You didn't fly two hundred miles to bring coffee."

"No." Vásquez's smile didn't move but something behind it did, a shutter adjusting. "I flew two hundred miles to make you an offer. The coffee is just to make you feel appreciated while you hear it."

Imogen Reyes came across from her support ship in a rubber launch before Vásquez had finished speaking, which told Sebastian she'd been watching for the plane longer than any of them. She came up the ladder fast, rain jacket flapping, and planted herself on the deck with the box's location — Sebastian was sure — already calculated in her head. She'd been doing that since they'd raised it. Watching the box. Watching him watch the box.

"Vásquez." Her voice was flat as a chart table.

"Ms. Reyes." He inclined his head with genuine pleasure. "The prodigal. How is retirement?"

"Busy."

"I can see that." He turned back to Sebastian, and now the smile came off entirely, and what was underneath was almost a relief, because at least it was honest: a ledger. A man looking at a number and deciding what the number was worth. "Captain. Here is the offer, and I will make it only once, because I respect your time and mine, and because the weather respects neither."

He gestured at the sky, where the monsoon was building itself out of the horizon like a cathedral.

"The box. Intact. Unopened — and before you insult us both by lying, Captain, understand that we will know, the way we always know. You deliver it to our cutter at the shelter anchorage at Puerto Sombra. In exchange: every debt. Yours, your engineer's, your whole crew's. Erased. Not deferred. *Erased.* The paper burned, the names struck, the interest forgiven, the interest on the interest forgiven, which I grant you is the only genuinely miraculous part of this arrangement." He spread his hands. "You sail home free men. Rich, even. There's a salvage fee in it. A *legitimate* one. You could buy a new boat. A new life. You could finally fix that engine of yours, which I am told sounds like a dying whale."

"It sounds like a whale with opinions," Bo said from the wheelhouse door.

"Exactly so."

* * *

The silence that followed had texture. Sebastian could feel each of his crew in it, the way you feel the hull in heavy weather, every plank and seam.

"And if we don't?" Kip asked.

Vásquez turned to him with the gentleness of a doctor delivering bad news to a family he'd delivered bad news to many times before.

"Then the syndicate hunts you," he said simply. "Not the way we hunt debtors — that's business, that has rules, that ends with fingers or boats. This would be different. This would be the other thing. The thing we do to people who try to hurt the family itself." He let that sit. "You have heard of the other thing. Everyone in the Verge has heard of the other thing. Most of what they've heard is exaggerated." He paused. "Some of it is not."

"You'd hunt us over a *recording*," Kip said. "Over some dead company's dirty paperwork."

"I would hunt you over a *precedent*," Vásquez said, and for a moment — just a moment — the smile was gone and something tired and old looked out of his face, something that had been doing this for forty years and had stopped sleeping well somewhere in the third decade. "A box that talks, Captain. That's what it is. A box that can be opened by anyone, anywhere, and out comes the family's voice, ordering a ship full of people to the bottom for an insurance payout. Do you understand what that does? Not to us. To *everyone who works for us*. Every sailor, every dockhand, every poor devil making payments on a skiff, who hears that voice and thinks: *if they did it to the Meridian Star, when do they do it to me?* A box like that doesn't sink a syndicate. It sinks the *water* the syndicate floats on." He picked up the cooler again, held it out. "So. Coffee?"

"Get off my boat," Sebastian said.

Vásquez sighed, set the cooler down on the deck anyway — "Keep it, it's a gift, I'm not a monster" — and turned to go. At the ladder he stopped.

"Captain. One more thing, man to man, off the ledger." He looked back, and Sebastian saw it again, that tired thing, and hated how much he recognized it. "The woman Reyes. Whatever she told you. Whatever she promised you. Ask yourself why a woman with syndicate money, syndicate protection, and a syndicate past needs a *broke* salvage crew to fetch a box she could hire anyone to fetch." He smiled, small and genuine this time, which was somehow worse. "Ask her what she plans to do with it. Ask her what she did six years ago. The sea forgets nothing, Captain. Neither do we."

Then he was down the ladder, and the seaplane was lifting off in a wall of spray, and the sound of it faded into the growing growl of the weather, and the cooler sat on the deck between them all like a small white bomb full of very good coffee.

* * *

They gathered in the wheelhouse, because the wheelhouse was where they gathered, and because the rain had started — fat, preliminary drops, the monsoon clearing its throat.

Imogen spoke first, fast, before anyone else could arrange the room to their liking. "You heard him. Deliver it. It's the only play."

"Is it?" Sebastian leaned against the chart table and looked at her, really looked, doing the thing Vásquez had told him to do. "Funny thing, Imogen. The syndicate man and the rival captain both want the same box, in the same condition, going to the same place. When the wolf and the shepherd agree on where the flock should sleep, I start counting sheep."

"That's not — " She stopped. Started again, quieter. "Sebastian. The debts are real. Bo's surgery is real. You deliver that box and it's all *gone*. The whole weight, six years of it, gone in an afternoon. I have watched you people drown in that debt for two years. I'm telling you there's a ladder right there."

"And what's at the top of the ladder?"

"Survival."

"That's not what I asked."

She looked at him, and for a second — the same second Vásquez had gotten, the tired honest one — something moved behind her eyes and then was locked away again, quick as a diver going under. "It's what you need it to be," she said.

"See, that's the answer of a woman with a plan she doesn't want to discuss."

"Sebastian — "

"The box," Kip said.

They all turned. He was sitting on the engine cover, arm strapped, knuckles still scabbed from the Corvus boarding, and he had the voice he never used — the quiet one, the one from the night they'd played the recording, when Tomás's voice had come out of the speaker refusing the scuttling order with the whole ocean coming down around him.

"The box isn't a debt payment. It's not salvage. It's *Tomás*." His voice cracked and he pushed through it. "He's been down there six years, alone, telling the truth to nobody. And you want to hand him to Delgado so they can put him in a drawer? He *died* for that recording. He stayed at his post. He — " Kip's jaw worked. "He said no. When everyone with a rank said yes, he said no, and the sea took him for it, and the least we can do — the *least* — is say it back to the whole world."

"And the world says it back with what?" Priya asked softly. "Funerals? Kip, the syndicate doesn't do trials. We broadcast, and then we spend the rest of our lives looking over our shoulders in every port from here to the Passage."

"Then we spend it *honest*."

"Dead is dead either way," Bo said. "I'd rather be dead owing nothing."

"You'd rather be dead with *intact fingers*," Priya said. "There's a difference."

"Friends." Sebastian held up a hand. "Priya. Do the math. The real math."

Priya closed her eyes. He watched her do it — he could almost see the chart behind her lids, the wind vectors, the tide tables, the monsoon's leading edge crawling across her mental sea like ink in water.

"We can't outrun it," she said finally. "Either way. That's the truth nobody wants. The shelter anchorage at Puerto Sombra is forty hours hard sailing against the building swell; the monsoon's leading edge hits this stretch in under thirty, and after it hits, nobody sails anywhere except down. If we run for the syndicate cutter, we don't make it before the storm closes the strait. We'd have to hand the box to whatever Delgado asset can reach us — and Vásquez will make sure that's a *cutter*, with a *crew*, and we'll be delivering it into the hands of people who have zero further use for witnesses." She opened her eyes. "And if we run for the relay tower — the old storm-relay on Cielo Point, the one that can still push a broadcast across the whole Verge — that's *through* the leading edge. Through the eye, if the eye holds its track, which it might not. Nobody runs through a monsoon's leading edge. Nobody has. The Corvus won't. The cutters won't. It's the one heading nobody will be looking for and the one heading that kills you."

Silence.

"So," Bo said. "We can't deliver the box without dying, and we can't broadcast it without dying, and we can't keep it without dying, and the coffee's getting cold." He picked up the cooler and looked into it. "It *is* good coffee, though. He wasn't lying about that. It's the good kind from before."

"Bo."

"I'm processing through caffeine. It's how I process."

Sebastian looked around his wheelhouse — at Bo cradling the cooler like a baby, at Kip with his strapped arm and his burning eyes, at Priya with the pencil still behind her ear like a flag planted on a summit, at Imogen standing apart by the door, half in, half out, the way she'd stood through every conversation since the contract was signed. The fault line ran right through the middle of his boat, through the middle of his family, and he could feel it shifting underfoot like the deck in a swell.

Love on one side. Duty on the other. And the monsoon coming down on both.

"Here's what I know," he said, and his voice came out lower than he intended, rougher. "Six years ago I made a promise at a graveside with no body in it. I promised Tomás's mother we'd find out what happened. Then the debt came, and the fear came, and I let the promise go quiet, because quiet was easier, and I have hated myself in a small way every single day since." He looked at Kip. "You were right. He said no. That's in the box, and it's not ours to sell, and it's not theirs to bury."

Kip's face did something complicated and wet.

"And here's what else I know." He turned to Imogen. "Vásquez flew two hundred miles to tell me to ask you a question. So I'm asking it. What did you do six years ago, Imogen? And what are you planning to do with that box?"

Imogen Reyes stood in the doorway with the rain coming in behind her and the storm light on her face, and for a long moment she said nothing at all.

Then, out at sea, deep in the gray, the first thunder of the leading edge rolled across the water — long, low, and patient, like something enormous turning over in its sleep — and every soul in the wheelhouse felt the clock under the question tick one number smaller.
