## 13. Fifth Tide: Mutiny Weather

Bo could not sleep, which was nothing new. What was new was the reason.

For six years his insomnia had been a faithful companion — the hum of the engine room in his ears even ashore, the phantom rattle of a bearing that needed replacing, the low-grade anxiety of a man whose entire job was making sure nothing caught fire. He had made peace with it. He and his insomnia had an arrangement: it kept him up, and he fixed things at three in the morning, and by dawn the boat ran better than she had any right to.

Tonight he sat on an ammunition crate in the engine room and did not fix anything. Tonight there was a black box wrapped in a tarp two compartments aft of him, and there were numbers in his head that would not stop moving.

Fourteen thousand. That was what he owed the Delgado syndicate, though the number had a way of growing when you weren't looking, like mold or a rumor. It had started small — a loan for the surgery his mother needed back in Puerto Sombra, a friendly man with a friendly smile who said *no interest, hermano, we're practically family* — and it had become fourteen thousand, and then the friendly man's cousin had come aboard the *Provision* during a port call two years ago, looked around the engine room with professional appreciation, and said, *You know, a man with your hands could be useful to us. And useful men sometimes find their debts get... lighter.*

Bo had never done anything about it. That was the thing nobody knew, not Sebastian, not Priya, not Kip with his big grieving heart. For two years he'd carried it the way he carried everything — quietly, in the same pocket as his wrenches, where it didn't rattle against anything important.

Until now. Until there was a box aboard worth more than every debt on this boat combined, syndicate seals and all, and until Imogen Reyes's support ship had pulled alongside that afternoon long enough for a launch to deliver a crate of diesel and a note that Bo alone had signed for, because Bo alone always handled the fuel manifests.

The note had been brief. Delgado stationery, cream-colored, absurdly formal for a criminal enterprise:

*Mr. Okonkwo — Your account stands at 14,200. The box, delivered intact to the Corvus before dawn of the fifth tide, settles all balances and earns a bonus suitable to your talents. A man who fixes engines can also unfix loyalties. We trust your hands. — F.*

F. Ferro. The friendly man's cousin. Bo had burned the note in the bilge burner and then stood there watching the ashes and feeling like one of them.

*We trust your hands.*

His hands were trembling. He looked at them with something like betrayal. Twenty-two years turning bolts, and they'd never once shaken on him.

* * *

He went up top because the engine room felt too much like a confessional. The *Provision* rode at anchor in the lee of a coral shelf, riding light after losing half their fuel to the Corvus raiders, and the sky above was doing something Bo did not like at all. The stars were out, but wrong — smeared, as if someone had dragged a thumb across wet paint. The wind came in gusts now instead of breaths. Fifth tide. The monsoon wasn't coming anymore; it was *arriving*, the way a landslide arrives, the way a verdict arrives.

Kip had the watch, huddled under the dodger with a mug of something and Tomás's old jacket over his shoulders, which he wore more nights than not these days and pretended he didn't. Bo climbed halfway up and stopped, because Kip looked over and grinned at him, tired and real.

"Can't sleep either?"

"Engine room's haunted," Bo said. "Something keeps rattling."

"You fixed that rattle Tuesday."

"Maybe I broke it again. Maybe I'm a saboteur." He heard his own voice say it and felt the word go through him like a fishhook. Kip laughed, easy, trusting, and turned back to the horizon, and Bo stood there in the dark holding the laugh like stolen goods.

That was the hinge, right there. He understood it suddenly, standing on the companionway steps with salt spray on his face. Not the money. Not the fear. It was Kip laughing at a bad joke from a man who was planning, at some level he hadn't let himself look at directly, to sell them all out before sunrise.

Because that was the plan, wasn't it? That was what the note meant. Take the box off the boat tonight while the others slept, run the tender — no, the tender was gone, the Corvus had taken it, so take the skiff, the little one, the one that leaked — out through the shelf passage where Priya wouldn't hear the engine over the building wind, and hand it to whatever Corvus picket was waiting beyond the reef line. Fourteen thousand gone. His mother's surgery paid twice over. A bonus. *Suitable to your talents.*

And Sebastian in irons or worse when the syndicate came to collect the box that wasn't there. And Kip — Kip who had finally gotten his answers, who had heard Tomás's voice on that recording and cried in a way Bo had never seen him cry, quiet and terrible, like a hull taking water below the waterline — Kip would learn that the truth had been traded away by the crewmate he trusted most.

Bo sat down on the steps. He put his head in his hands. He thought about his mother, who had gotten her surgery, who was alive because of syndicate money, and who would be the first person to tell him that a debt was just a number but a crew was a soul.

"You look like a man doing arithmetic," Kip said, without turning around.

"I'm always doing arithmetic."

"Yeah, but usually it balances." Kip took a sip of his drink. "Wind's backing around. You feel it? Priya says the eye's going to pass close tomorrow. Real close. She got that look she gets."

"The lightning-navigating look?"

"The 'I have calculated our survival to within four percent' look."

Bo almost smiled. Almost. Then the wind gusted, hard, carrying a sound from the east — engines, far off, more than one, running slow and dark. Kip straightened. They both listened. The sound faded, or seemed to. Out here you couldn't tell whether the sea was empty or merely patient.

"That'll be the Corvus," Kip said quietly. All the laughter had gone out of him. "They lost their divers. They lost face. They won't lose twice."

"No," Bo agreed. His heart was hammering and he was suddenly, desperately certain of what he had to do, and equally certain he couldn't do it sitting on these stairs being honest with everyone except the one person who mattered most. "Kip. Wake Sebastian. Tell him — tell him I need to talk to him. Now. Before first light."

Kip looked at him then, really looked, and Bo watched something careful come into his face — the wariness of a man who'd been lied to before by people he loved. "What kind of talk?"

"The kind I should've had two years ago."

* * *

Sebastian came down into the galley with his hair flat on one side and his eyes already fully awake, because Sebastian Villalobos had never in his life been less than sixty percent awake, a condition Bo had always assumed was genetic and now suspected was guilt. Priya arrived thirty seconds later, drawn by some navigator's sixth sense, arms crossed, expression already reading Bo like a chart with known errors. Kip stayed by the door, arms folded, watching.

"Talk," Sebastian said.

So Bo talked. He told it all, standing, because sitting felt like surrender. The loan. Puerto Sombra. The friendly man with the friendly smile. The cousin in the engine room. The fourteen thousand that grew like mold. Two years of knowing and saying nothing. And then, last, the note — he hadn't kept it, he'd burned it, but he recited it nearly word for word, because he'd read it enough times that it lived behind his eyelids now.

The galley was very quiet when he finished. Somewhere above them, the rigging sang a low uneasy note.

"They offered me the box," Bo said. "Delivered to them before dawn. Debt cleared. Bonus on top." He made himself look at each of them. "I want you to understand I hadn't decided. I want you to understand that's not the same as deciding not to. I know the difference. I've been standing on that hinge all night."

"And now?" Priya asked. Her voice was flat, but Bo had sailed with Priya long enough to know flat was how she sounded when she was most afraid.

"Now I'm telling you they're going to board us at dawn."

Sebastian blinked. "That's your confession? Bo—"

"It's both. Listen." Bo leaned forward, hands flat on the table, engineer's hands, steady now, finally steady, doing the one thing they'd always been good at. "The note said *before dawn*. You don't schedule a pickup unless the pickup is yours. Ferro isn't waiting for me to bring the box out — he's coming to collect it, with or without my help. The Corvus has been running dark along the reef line since midnight; Kip and I heard them. They know exactly where we're anchored, because I signed for that fuel delivery and the delivery boat had time to report our position. They think they have a man inside this boat." He swallowed. "They don't anymore. But they don't know that yet, and that's the only advantage we have left."

Priya had uncrossed her arms. She was staring at him with an expression Bo couldn't parse, and then she said, "Two years. You sat across from me at dinner for two years."

"Yes."

"And you never—" She stopped herself. Recalibrated. Bo watched her do it, the way she recalculated a course around a storm. "Okay. Okay. Later. There'll be time to be furious later, if we're alive to schedule it." She turned to Sebastian. "He's right about the geometry. If they believe they have an inside man, they'll come in confident. Confident boarding parties make mistakes. It's the best tactical position we've held since the Verge."

"He sold us," Kip said from the door. Quiet. Not angry — worse than angry. Wounded. "Not to them. But he sat on it. While we voted. While we crossed the sworn-off water. While I—" His jaw worked. "While I told you about Tomás's last transmission, Bo. I gave you that, and you were carrying their note in your pocket."

"I know," Bo said. "There's nothing I can say that makes that smaller. I'm not asking you to forgive it tonight. I'm asking you to let me help you live through tomorrow."

Kip looked at him for a long moment. Then he pushed off the doorframe and came and sat down heavily at the table, which in Kip's language was a treaty, provisional and unsigned, but a treaty.

Sebastian rubbed his face with both hands. When he lowered them, he was wearing the expression Bo had seen maybe five times in eleven years — the one that meant the captain had stopped being a friend and started being a captain, and wished to God he could do both at once.

"If you'd brought that box out to them," Sebastian said, "I'd have hunted you. You know that."

"I know."

"Not because of the debt. Because of Kip. Because of what's on that recording." Sebastian's voice roughened. "Tomás died refusing those people. I wasn't going to let them win by buying the rest of us one at a time." He exhaled. "But you came to us instead. At the hinge. That counts, Bo. Tonight, it counts."

"It counts *mathematically*," Priya corrected. "Sentiment later. Dawn is in four hours and forty minutes. Can we please discuss the part where armed people are coming?"

* * *

They worked through the small hours, and it was, Bo had to admit, the best he'd felt in two years — hands busy, mind clear, every task pointing at one true thing. No more numbers growing in the dark. Just work.

Priya re-anchored them on a second scope off the bow, set to trip a silent alarm in the engine room rather than sound a bell — *let them think we're asleep*, she said, *confident men walk into rooms*. Sebastian doused every light that didn't need to burn and moved the black box from its hiding place aft to a new one Bo built on the spot: a false floor panel beneath the auxiliary pump housing, deep in the engine room's hottest, loudest, least hospitable corner, where any searcher would need to dismantle a running machine in the dark to find it. Bo welded the seams himself and then, for spite, wired the panel to the starter circuit, so that anyone who pried it open without grounding the relay first would get an education.

"What happens if *we* need it fast?" Kip asked.

"Then you ask me nicely," Bo said, "and I open it properly, like a civilized person."

Kip snorted despite himself. First crack in the ice. Bo would take it.

They armed themselves without ceremony — the boat's modest arsenal, mostly flares and a machete and Sebastian's ancient shotgun that he swore had been his father's and looked like it had been his grandfather's mistake. Kip took the foredeck with a boathook and a coil of chain. Priya stationed herself at the chart table with the alarm board and a flare pistol, mapping escape routes off the reef in case the fight went badly, which she announced aloud, "in the interest of honesty," with a pointed look at Bo that promised the fury she was scheduling.

By the time the eastern sky went the color of a bruise, the *Provision* was dark, quiet, and waiting. Bo stood in the engine room with his hand resting on the warm housing over the hidden box, listening to the wind climb through the rigging like something learning to sing.

Above him, faintly, through the deck plates, he heard Priya's voice, calm as arithmetic:

"Contact. Two boats, running dark, off the starboard quarter. They're coming in."

Bo closed his eyes. *Let them think they still own me,* he prayed, to no god in particular and to Tomás specifically. *Let them walk into the room.*

The alarm board clicked once, softly, like a knuckle cracking.
