## 14. The Corvus Boards

Kip felt the wind change before he heard it — a shift in the way the rain hit his face, from falling to flying sideways. He'd grown up on a fishing boat off the Verge coast, and his grandmother had a saying about weather like this: *when the sky starts throwing things instead of dropping them, it's not asking anymore.*

He was on deck watch at what Bo had started calling "oh-dark-thirty," which Kip thought was generous, because it wasn't even dark. The monsoon's leading edge had turned the horizon the color of a bruise going bad, and the sea beneath it had gone strange and glassy, like something holding its breath.

That was when he saw the lights.

Three of them, low and fast, cutting through the swells without running lights of their own — just the faint glow of instruments, the kind of glow you only spotted if you'd spent enough nights staring at nothing. Kip counted seconds between lightning flashes and used them like camera shutters. Corvus hulls. Two inflatables and something bigger behind them.

"Sebastian," he said into the intercom, keeping his voice level the way Tomás had taught him years ago. *Panic travels faster than radio waves, muchacho. Don't be the one carrying it.* "We've got company. Port quarter. Three contacts, coming hot."

The boat woke up around him. That was the thing about their crew — they were terrible at mornings and worse at paperwork, but you could drop a wrench down an engine hatch in the middle of the night and every one of them would be vertical before it landed. Sebastian came up the companionway already pulling on his jacket, hair flattened on one side like a rooster that had lost an argument. Priya appeared at the nav station without appearing to have been anywhere else. Bo came up last, moving carefully, favoring the ribs Kip had cracked for him during the tender collision — *your fault, technically,* Kip reminded himself, and filed the guilt away for later, because later was looking shorter by the minute.

"Corvus," Kip said. "They're not shadowing us anymore."

Sebastian looked at the bruised horizon, then at the three lights, then at Kip. "How long?"

"Four minutes. Maybe five if they're being polite."

"They're not being polite," Priya said quietly. She had her binoculars up. "I can see hands on rails. They're riding with boarding gear."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about a fight at sea: there is nowhere to run. On land, a fight has edges — alleys to duck into, doors to slam, ground that stays put while you decide whether you're brave or sensible. At sea, the whole world is the same wet moving thing, and the only question is which piece of it you're willing to bleed on.

Sebastian made the call in about nine seconds, which Kip would later consider a personal record. "They want the box. We don't let go of the box. Bo, kill the interior lights and get below — you're worth more with a wrench than a fist right now. Priya, take us into the squall line, hard to starboard. Make them board us in weather if they're going to board us at all."

"And us?" Kip asked.

Sebastian looked up at the rigging — the forestay, the spreaders, the ratlines Bo had re-tensioned two seasons back and complained about ever since. "We make the climb expensive."

There was a plan, sort of. The Corvus would come alongside, throw grapnels, and try to swarm across the rail. The counter was to cut the grapnels, keep them from massing on deck, and use the rigging as high ground — because four people who knew their own boat beat eight strangers on unfamiliar decking, provided the four could stay upright and the eight couldn't.

It was a good plan, in the way that all plans are good right up until contact.

The first inflatable hit them out of the rain like a thrown knife. Kip heard the thunk of the grapnel biting the rail, felt the whole boat shudder as the Corvus pilot matched their speed with a skill he grudgingly respected, and then the night filled with men.

"Expensive!" Sebastian shouted, and swung the boathook into the first face that came over the rail.

What followed was not, Kip would reflect afterward, anything anyone would put in a training manual. It was grappling hooks and gaff hooks, it was slipping on wet decking and grabbing whatever was nearest, it was Priya executing a turn so vicious that two boarders went over the side mid-climb with expressions of profound betrayal. It was Kip hauling himself up the ratlines with a boat knife in his teeth like a pirate in a picture book, except less glamorous and considerably more terrified.

He got to the spreader platform and looked down at the deck, and the sight below him was chaos lit by strobing lightning: Sebastian fighting two men at once near the wheel, using the boom as a swinging barrier; Priya locked in a shoving match with a big Corvus deckhand over possession of a fire extinguisher, which she won, and then used; and Bo — Bo, who was supposed to be below decks — emerging from the engine hatch with a spanner in each hand and an expression of serene apology.

"You're supposed to be below!" Kip yelled down.

"I got bored!" Bo yelled back, and clipped a man across the shoulders with a spanner so cleanly that even the man seemed impressed.

For maybe ninety seconds, it worked. The boarders couldn't mass. Every time three of them got a foothold, the boat itself seemed to conspire against them — a swell, a lurch, a well-placed extinguisher blast of chemical fog. Kip cut two grapnel lines from above and watched the inflatables slew away and come back, dogged as ticks.

Then the third boat did something smart.

Instead of coming alongside, it ran ahead of them, circled wide through the rain, and came back around on the opposite tack — and its grapnel took the tender.

Their tender. The little dinghy they towed, the one that had carried Sebastian and Kip down to the Meridian Star and back, the one with the dive gear still stowed under its bench seat. Kip saw the line snap taut, saw the tender swing out behind them like a kite tail, and understood one full second before it happened what the Corvus pilot intended.

"He's using it as an anchor!" Kip screamed. "Cut the painter! Cut the—"

The tender hit the end of its line and the stern of their boat slewed hard to port, and everything that Kip had spent ninety seconds making expensive suddenly became cheap. Boarders poured over the rail on both sides. A boot caught Kip's platform and shook the whole rigging. Somewhere below, Sebastian went down under two men, and Kip's heart stopped doing its job properly for a moment before Sebastian rolled, came up with the boathook reversed, and introduced its butt-end to someone's kneecap with a crack Kip felt in his own teeth.

"Painter!" Priya shouted. "Somebody cut the painter!"

Nobody could reach it. It was aft, past the melee, and the melee was everywhere now. Kip looked at the distance, looked at the tender dragging them sideways into the squall line where the Corvus's bigger ship was waiting with its lights doused, and did the arithmetic that Tomás had taught him: *the boat or the crew, muchacho. Never both. Choose fast and don't look back.*

"I've got it," he said, mostly to himself.

"What?" Priya's voice, sharp.

"The painter. I've got it." And Kip did the single most painful thing he had done since Tomás died, which was to slide down the ratlines, take two hits — one to the shoulder, one to the pride — shove through the tangle of limbs, and put his knife through the tender's towline.

The tender fell away astern. For half a heartbeat it sat in their wake, rocking, innocent, full of dive gear and six years of small memories — the time Bo had fallen asleep in it and drifted a quarter mile, the time they'd painted a smiley face on the transom and Sebastian had pretended to be furious for a week. Then the Corvus inflatable hooked it and dragged it away into the rain, and Kip stood at the rail with a knife in his hand and nothing left to cut, and discovered that grief and fury feel almost identical in the chest. Almost. Fury has more use.

"OFF MY BOAT!" he bellowed, and meant all of it — the boarders, the Corvus, the syndicate, the storm, the whole rotten arrangement of the world — and went back into the fight with something loose and burning in him.

* * *

They repelled the second wave the way you put out a kitchen fire: badly, completely, and with lasting damage.

By the time the last grapnel parted and the last Corvus hull sheered off into the rain, the sky had gone from bruised to apocalyptic, and their boat looked like it had hosted a very small, very personal war. Rail stanchions bent. The port lifeline gone entirely. A gaff hook embedded in the cabin door at shoulder height, quivering, which Bo removed and pocketed with a nod of professional appreciation. Two of the boarders hadn't made it back over the rail — one had, in fact, decided mid-fight that syndicate wages didn't cover this, and was now sitting meekly against the mast with his hands up, asking in careful English whether surrendering to pirates came with meals.

"We're not pirates," Sebastian said, breathing hard.

"Is okay," the man said. "I say I am captured by pirates. Better story for wife."

Kip laughed — a short, cracked sound that surprised him — and then stopped laughing, because that was when the radio crackled.

Imogen's voice came through thin and static-shredded, and Kip knew before she said a word. You spend enough years listening to people, you learn the sound of a sentence that has already abandoned you.

*"Villalobos. Do you copy."*

"Copy," Sebastian said. His voice was flat in a way Kip had learned to fear.

A pause. Wind shrieked through it. Then: *"Storm's turning northeast. My captain won't hold position — we'll lose the ship if we do. I'm sorry. Fall back to—"*

Static ate the coordinates. When her voice came back, it was smaller. *"Sebastian. I'm sorry."*

Priya reached over and switched the radio off. Nobody said anything for a moment. The surrendered boarder, sensing the mood, raised his hand slightly. "This is also bad news, yes?"

"Yeah," Kip told him. "Yeah, friend, this is also bad news."

* * *

They took stock at first light, or what passed for it — a gray smear of dawn behind the storm wall, light enough to count what was broken.

Half their fuel: gone, siphoned by the boarders into drums hauled back to the Corvus, a theft so methodical it was almost respectful. The tender: gone. The dive compressor: damaged, a boot print on its housing like a signature. Kip's shoulder: sprained, wrapped in a sling Bo improvised from a sailcloth bag and far too much medical opinion. Their prisoner: fed, at Kip's insistence, because his grandmother had also said you feed people before you decide what they are.

And Imogen Reyes: gone, her support ship a smudge of smoke on the southern horizon, running ahead of the monsoon with her apologies trailing behind her like oil on water.

"She knew," Kip said. He was sitting on the cockpit bench, working a rope through his fingers because his hands needed a job or they'd start breaking things. "She knew the Corvus would hit us. She knew her captain wouldn't stay. She bought herself a head start and sold us the bill."

"You don't know that," Bo said gently.

"I know that when my gran's roof leaked, she didn't move out and leave a note." Kip looked up. "Six years, Sebastian. Six years I wondered why Tomás's radio call never made sense, and now I'm wondering how many other calls never made sense either. How many times somebody said *sorry* into a radio and then turned the volume down."

Sebastian didn't answer right away. He stood at the rail looking south, where Imogen's ship had been, and Kip watched him carry it — the debt, the box, the dead man, the woman who might be their worst enemy or their most broken ally, and no way left to tell the difference. When he finally spoke, his voice was quiet enough that only the cockpit heard it.

"We're alone."

"We're always alone," Priya said. She was at the nav station, marking the storm's track with a grease pencil, and her voice had the calm of someone who had already done the terrifying math. "The difference is now we know it. Which means every decision from here is ours. Nobody else's."

She tapped the chart. The storm wall curved across it like a scythe, and beyond the scythe, a tiny marked point: the relay tower.

"And here's the decision," she said. "Because that wall arrives in"—she checked her watch—"less than a tide. And we are standing in its way."

The prisoner, chewing his ration bar, raised his hand again. "Also bad news?"

"Also bad news," Kip said, and got up to shorten the sails, because the wind was rising, and somewhere inside it, he was almost certain, the truth was waiting — and the only way to it was through.
