## 15. Imogen's Confession

The radio had been dying for two days.

Imogen Reyes knew every sound her equipment made the way other people knew their own heartbeat — the healthy hum of a clean signal, the dry click of a relay catching, the wet gargle of salt water finding its way somewhere it should never be. The set in her wheelhouse had developed all three ailments at once, and now it spoke to her in fragments, like an old man remembering a story he'd rather forget.

"—ogen. *Imogen.* Do you copy? This is—"

She sat with her hand hovering over the transmit key and did not press it.

Outside the wheelhouse windows, the sky over the Coral Verge had gone the color of a bruise going bad. Her barometer had been falling since noon, faster than any forecast she'd trusted in twenty years of this work, and her chief engineer — a laconic Samoan named Tavita who communicated primarily in sighs — had come up twice already to say nothing loudly until she acknowledged him.

"I see it," she'd told him the second time.

"You don't," he'd said, which was fair.

But the storm was tomorrow's problem, or possibly tonight's, depending on how cruel the monsoon decided to be about schedules. Right now the problem was the voice crackling out of her ruined radio, thin and warped and unmistakably Sebastian Villalobos, and the problem beneath that problem — the one she'd been carrying for six years, ballast she'd never once managed to jettison — was that he was calling her because his crew was hurt and alone and she had abandoned them, and she deserved exactly zero of the forgiveness his call implied.

"*—know you're there. Your transponder's still pinging. Bo says you're running dark on everything else but you forgot the AIS, which — honestly, Imogen, that's amateur hour even for you—*"

Despite herself, despite everything, Imogen laughed. It came out wrong, half a sob dressed in a laugh's clothing, but it came out.

She pressed the key.

"Sebastian." Her own transmission came out steady, which surprised her. "Your radio etiquette remains atrocious."

Static. Then: "*You're alive.*"

"Barely. My ship is intact, my crew is shaken, and my conscience—" She stopped. Looked at her hand on the key. Started again. "My conscience is the only thing aboard taking on water faster than the hull."

"*Where did you go?*"

The question had no anger in it. That was the worst part. After what her people had done — after the Corvus had boarded his boat, taken his tender, cost him half his fuel while her support ship turned tail and ran — he should have been screaming at her. Instead he sounded tired. Sounding tired was worse. Sounding tired meant he'd already done the arithmetic on her, run the numbers, arrived at the answer he'd probably suspected since the day she'd walked into his dockside shack with a contract and a smile.

"Can I tell you something?" she said. "Before the weather takes the radio entirely?"

A pause long enough that she checked her transmit light. Then: "*Go ahead.*"

* * *

Here is what Imogen Reyes had learned about confessions in her fifty-one years: they were like diving. You could prepare all you wanted, check your gauges, rehearse your descent, but the moment you actually went under, the pressure changed everything — the way words sounded, the way they bent, the way truths you'd carried at sea level became strange, compressed creatures down in the deep where you finally said them aloud.

She keyed the mic and dove.

"Six years ago I was a junior operations officer for Meridian Maritime Insurance. Do you remember what I told you I did back then?"

"*You said logistics,*" Sebastian said. "*Fleet coordination.*"

"Fleet coordination." She let the words sit in the static like something dead floating past the porthole. "That's one way to describe signing off on the deliberate sinking of a nine-hundred-ton cargo vessel with a crew of thirty-one."

The silence that followed had texture. She could feel him not breathing through four miles of storm-thickened air.

"The Meridian Star was over-insured. Her owners were drowning — the same Delgado money that's drowning you, as it happens; the syndicate has always loved owning both the fire and the fire brigade. The plan was elegant. A 'navigational error' in the Verge, a hull breach, a payout that would quietly settle a great many debts. All it required was paperwork." Her voice caught. She let it. "Paperwork requires a signature. I was twenty-nine years old and ambitious and I told myself the ship would sail empty. A skeleton crew, they said. Minimal risk."

"*Thirty-one people,*" Sebastian said quietly.

"Twenty-nine, once the two who refused to participate were put ashore at the last port." She closed her eyes. "I know that number the way you know your daughter's birthday, Sebastian. I have said it to myself every night for six years like a prayer I'm not sure anyone is receiving."

"*Tomás,*" he said. Not a question. A wound reopening.

"Your Tomás was one of the two who were supposed to be put ashore." She gripped the edge of the console. "He wasn't. There was a mix-up — or there was no mix-up, I've never been able to prove which, and believe me, I have tried — and he stayed aboard. And when the scuttling order came through the ship's internal channel, your Tomás — your quiet, stubborn, impossible Tomás, whom I never met, whom I know better than I know my own brother — he picked up the bridge mic and he said no."

"*We heard him,*" Sebastian said. His voice had gone rough. "*On the box. We heard him.*"

"Then you heard more of him than I ever allowed myself to listen to." She laughed again, that broken half-thing. "Do you want to know why I really hired you? Not the salvage contract. The real reason. The reason underneath the reason, the way the wreck is underneath the water."

"*Tell me.*"

"Because I've spent six years trying to get back to that black box and destroy it. Every legitimate route closed. Every back door led to Delgado. So I bought my way in — syndicate money, syndicate protection, the Corvus fleet, all of it — telling myself I'd recover the box first and melt it down before anyone else heard what was on it. Erase the last living proof that I—" Her throat closed around the word. She forced it out anyway. "That I killed thirty-one people. Twenty-nine. Thirty-one. I can't ever make the number small enough."

* * *

The radio chose that moment to nearly die altogether. The signal collapsed into a wash of crackle, and Imogen found herself leaning into the microphone like a woman leaning into wind, shouting her sins at a machine that might not carry them.

Then Sebastian's voice swam back up through the noise, faint and warped:

"*—still here. Go on.*"

And here was the thing she hadn't planned to say. Here was the thing that had been sitting in her chest since the Corvus pulled away from his battered boat, since she'd watched through her own binoculars as Kip — young Kip, furious Kip, who had every right to put a harpoon in her — stood on the deck of a crippled ship and didn't look away from her window.

"I lied to you," she said. "In the shack, when I offered you the contract. I told you the box contained evidence of insurance fraud and that the syndicate wanted it buried, and I let you believe I wanted it recovered for justice. For Tomás." She swallowed. "I wanted it destroyed. That was the mission. That was always the mission. Justice was just the wrapping paper."

"*I know,*" Sebastian said.

She blinked salt spray off her lashes. "You know."

"*Imogen. We played the recording three days ago. You think we didn't do the math? You think Priya hasn't traced the Corvus's salvage permits back through shell companies to your name? Bo cracked that encryption before we even reached the Verge.*" A pause, and then, gentler than she had any right to expect: "*We've known since the third tide. We took the job anyway.*"

"Why?" It came out as almost a whisper. "Why would you—"

"*Because Kip needed to hear Tomás say no. Because Bo needed the surgery money. Because we're broke and desperate and stupid, in roughly that order.*" She could hear him smiling, or the ghost of a smile, worn thin by grief and salt. "*And maybe because some part of us figured if we got close enough to the truth, it would stop being yours to bury and start being ours to carry.*"

Imogen Reyes, who had faced down syndicate enforcers and monsoon squalls and the flat gray stare of men whose job was making problems disappear, put her forehead down on the wheelhouse console and cried.

Not elegantly. Not the way she'd rehearsed it, in the rare honest hours, as something controlled and dignified. She cried the way the radio transmitted — in fragments, in static, in bursts that broke apart and reassembled into no shape worth naming. Six years of carrying a number. Six years of buying proximity to a wreck so she could unmake the proof of herself. Six years of being the most together person in every room, the rival with the fleet and the funding and the plan, and it had all been scaffolding around a hollowed-out hold.

When she could speak again, she keyed the mic.

"Sebastian. I need to ask you for something, and it is the only thing I will ever ask you for, and you are fully within your rights to refuse."

"*Say it.*"

"Let me come to the wreck." The words tumbled out fast, before her courage could reconsider. "Let me take a skiff down to the Meridian Star. Let me stand on her deck. I signed her death warrant from a desk in Singapore with a view of a swimming pool, and I have never once seen her face. I need—" Her voice fractured. "I need to apologize to her. To the water. To whatever's left. I know it's not rational. I know an apology doesn't raise the drowned. But I have to try, Sebastian. I have spent six years buying my way back to that ship to erase what I did, and somewhere in the last three days it turned into needing to face it instead. I can't explain the difference. I can only tell you it's the difference between drowning and learning to breathe underwater."

Static. Long static. Through the wheelhouse windows the horizon had gone from bruise to charcoal, and the first outer bands of the monsoon were beginning to comb across the water in long gray fingers, and somewhere behind her Tavita was climbing the stairs for the third time, and none of it mattered as much as the next ten seconds of a dying radio.

"*Imogen,*" Sebastian said at last. "*You understand what you're asking. The storm's coming in early. Priya says the leading edge hits by nightfall, maybe sooner. The eye is going to pass almost directly over the wreck site, and there is a window — a narrow, insane, Bo-certified window — where a small boat could reach the Star and get back. If you miss it, the monsoon closes like a door.*"

"I know."

"*If the syndicate's cutter catches you out there—*"

"I know."

"*If you go down there and the wreck claims you, nobody's writing a ballad about your redemption. They'll write a report. Filed and forgotten.*"

"That," Imogen said, and found she was smiling through the wreckage of her face, "is the single most comforting thing anyone has said to me in six years."

Another pause. When Sebastian spoke again, his voice had shifted — lower, careful, the voice of a man handing someone a knife handle-first.

"*There's something else. Something I have to ask you, and you have to answer straight, because Kip is standing next to me and he deserves the truth more than anyone alive.*"

"Ask."

"*The night Tomás died. The scuttling order went out, and he refused it, and twenty minutes later the ship went down. Was that the plan? Was he supposed to die for saying no? Or did something go wrong that even you didn't order?*"

Imogen stared at the microphone.

Because the truth was she didn't know. She had spent six years not knowing, turning it over in the dark hours, and every inquiry she'd funded had died in the same wall of sealed syndicate records. But there was one thing — one detail, buried in a maintenance log she'd bribed a man to let her photograph, a detail she'd never told another living soul because saying it aloud would mean admitting how much of that night she'd helped build—

"The pumps," she said. "The scuttling protocol called for flooding the aft holds only. Time enough for the crew to abandon ship. That was in the documentation I signed. But the damage reports afterward showed flooding fore and aft. Both ends. Someone changed the sequence, Sebastian. Someone flooded the bow first, so the ship went down by the head, fast, before the boats could launch." Her hands were shaking. "I have spent six years telling myself I didn't know. And I am telling you now, on a broken radio, in front of God and the monsoon: I don't know whether Tomás died because of my signature, or because someone above me decided a witness who'd said no on an open channel was a bigger liability than a hull."

The static breathed.

"*Kip heard that,*" Sebastian said softly. "*He's white as canvas. He says — hold on.*" Muffled sounds. Then Kip's voice, younger, rawer, cracking at the edges: "*Was it Delgado? Who gave the order to flood the bow? Was it Delgado?*"

"I don't know," Imogen said. "But I know where the answer is. The full scuttling protocol — the original orders, with the amendment and the authorizing signature — is in the master safe aboard the Meridian Star. Bridge deck, captain's day cabin. The black box was the backup. The paper is the primary. And it's still down there, Kip. In the dark. With his ship."

Silence on the line. Then Sebastian, low: "*Priya's plotting the eye track right now. She says we have one shot, and it's closing.*"

"Then take it," Imogen said. "And Sebastian — take me with you. Not for redemption. Redemption isn't a thing the sea hands out. Just take me to her. Let me see her face before the storm does whatever the storm decides to do with us all."

Through the wheelhouse glass, the first true rain of the monsoon came slanting across the water like a curtain being drawn, and far out at the edge of visibility, where the wreck waited in its trench, the sea had begun to stand up in long white-maned ridges that no boat her size was built to argue with.

The radio spat, guttered, held.

"*Come alongside before dark,*" Sebastian said. "*Bring everything you've got that floats. And Imogen—*"

"Yes?"

"*If this goes wrong, if the eye closes on us out there — I need you to know that Kip is doing this for Tomás. Not for you. Whatever you find down there, whatever the paper says — he's not forgiving you tonight.*"

"I wouldn't dream of asking him to," she said. "Forgiveness is a debt I stopped expecting to collect the day I signed."

She cut the transmission, turned from the console, and found Tavita filling the doorway, arms crossed, reading her face the way he read weather.

"Well?" he said.

"Ready the skiffs," Imogen said. "All of them. And Tavita — the big storm kit. The one we swore we'd never pack."

He looked at the wall of rain marching toward them across the Verge, then back at her, and sighed the longest sigh of his considerable career.

"For you," he said, "I'll pack two."
