## 16. Sixth Tide: The Wall of Water

Priya had a rule about weather, the same rule she had about men and about soup: if it looked too good to be true, it was lying.

The morning of the sixth tide looked *very* good. The sky over the Coral Verge was the pale, rinsed blue of a lie told well. The wind had dropped to almost nothing. The sea lay flat as poured pewter, and somewhere behind them the Meridian Star's trench was already filling with the dark, patient water that would eventually fold the wreck into itself like a letter no one would read.

"Too quiet," she said, for the fourth time in an hour.

"We heard you," Bo said, not looking up from the engine hatch. He had grease on his forehead, his forearms, and — through some mechanism Priya had given up investigating years ago — one earlobe. "You said it when you woke up. You said it at breakfast. You said it while brushing your teeth, which I know because you were holding the toothbrush like a divining rod."

"I was divining."

"You were foaming."

Kip sat on the rail with his arm still strapped from the Corvus ramming, eating rice out of the pot with a wooden spoon because all the bowls were dirty and nobody had the moral authority to complain. "Let her say it," he said. "She's earned it. She's been right about everything this week, which is honestly exhausting for the rest of us."

"I haven't been right about everything," Priya said.

"No," Sebastian agreed mildly, coming up from below with the chart case under his arm. "You've been right about everything except whether Bo's jury-rigged fuel line would hold, and it held, so technically you were wrong once, which by your standards is practically heresy." He set the charts down and looked west, where the horizon was doing something horizons should not do. "Say it again anyway."

The horizon was wrong. That was the thing about monsoons that no chart ever captured properly — they didn't arrive like weather, they arrived like a decision. The leading edge didn't build. It *presented itself*. And what presented itself now was a wall: a long, unbroken rampart of cloud running the whole width of the world, black-violet at its base, boiling white along its crest, so tall it bent the light around it. Beneath it, the sea had gone a color Priya had only seen twice in her life, both times from inside a survival suit.

She checked the barometer. It had fallen so fast the needle looked embarrassed.

"Tide's early," she said. "Monsoon's early. Everything's early. The whole ocean is running ahead of schedule, which means—" she did the arithmetic she'd been doing since midnight, the arithmetic that kept coming out ugly no matter how kindly she treated it, "—we have maybe ninety minutes before that wall arrives, and the syndicate cutter knows it."

Sebastian spread the chart on the deck box and weighted the corners with four empty shell casings, because the crew of the *Provision* had never once owned a paperweight that wasn't ammunition.

"Options," he said.

"One," Priya said, tapping the western notch in the reef line. "We run for shelter. Halcyon Cut. We can make it if we leave now and don't look back. We sit out the monsoon in a channel full of other boats hiding from the same storm, including—" she moved her finger east, to a small, patient triangle, "—that cutter, which has been sitting off Halcyon Cut since yesterday like a cat outside a mousehole. We go in there with the box, we come out after the storm, and Delgado's people take it off us before our engine's even warm. Politely, probably. They're very polite right up until the fingers."

"Option two?"

"There is no option two."

"There's always an option two," Kip said. "It's just usually worse."

Priya took a breath. She'd been dreading saying it out loud, because saying it out loud made it real, and because Bo was going to make a face, and Bo made *such* a face.

"Option two," she said, and tapped the chart again, far north, where a single inked symbol stood alone in open water. "The relay tower."

Nobody spoke. Even the sea seemed to lean in.

The tower was a relic of the old survey days — a hundred-meter spire bolted to a seamount in the middle of the Verge, built to bounce signals across two hundred miles of empty ocean, half-derelict, officially decommissioned, unofficially still alive because everything in the Verge refused to die properly. It was also directly in the path of the monsoon's eye.

"The eye," Priya said, "is going to pass almost exactly over it. Storms are honest that way — the center goes where the pressure tells it, and the pressure doesn't care about syndicate cutters or salvage contracts. If we ride the leading edge, tuck into the eye when it opens, we come out at the tower with maybe twenty minutes of calm. Enough time to climb, enough time to broadcast."

"And then the back side of the eye closes on us," Bo said, making the face.

"And then the back side closes on us."

"In a boat held together by prayer and my personal welding."

"Your welding has never failed us."

"My welding has failed us *twice*."

"Memorably," Kip conceded. "But character-buildingly."

Sebastian stared at the chart. Priya watched him do the same arithmetic she'd done — shelter and surrender, or the eye and a chance — and watched him arrive, as she had, at the place where the numbers stopped being numbers.

"It's not really a choice, is it," he said quietly.

"It's absolutely a choice," Priya said. "That's what makes it terrible. If it weren't a choice, it would just be weather."

* * *

They spent forty minutes preparing, and those forty minutes were among the strangest of Priya's life, because terror and clarity arrived together and refused to separate. Her hands were steady. Her mouth was dry. She found herself laughing twice at things that were not funny, and both times nobody judged her, because Kip laughed too, and Bo hummed while he lashed down the spare fuel cans like a man arranging flowers.

Bo stripped the engine's casing and rebuilt half of it in the time it took Priya to plot three courses into the eye — a main course, a fallback, and a third one she labeled, in pencil, *do not*. He talked to the engine the whole time, low and constant, the way he talked to it during every crisis, a stream of endearments and threats. "Come on, sweetheart. Don't do this to me today. I know, I know, I promised you a new impeller, I'm a man of my word, mostly, seasonally—"

Kip went forward and secured everything that could become a projectile, which, on the *Provision*, was most things. He worked one-handed, refusing help with the strapped arm, and when Priya came to check the anchor lashings he said, without turning around, "He'd have loved this."

Priya didn't have to ask who.

"He'd have stood right there"—Kip nodded at the bow—"with his hair doing that thing, yelling that we were all idiots and then steering better than any of us. Tomás hated storms and ran toward every one of them." His voice caught, and he covered it by yanking a lashing tight hard enough to make the cleat sing. "So. You know. Family tradition."

"We'll carry him through it," Priya said.

"That's the plan." Kip wiped his eyes with the back of his wrist, briskly, like swatting a fly. "Now go navigate. You're no use to me sentimental."

* * *

The wall arrived eleven minutes ahead of her revised estimate, which Priya chose to interpret as the ocean paying her back for the toothbrush comment.

There was no gradual worsening. There was a shadow, and a sound like a freight train crossing a steel bridge, and then the world simply ended and restarted as something else. The wind hit them sideways with a fist of rain so dense it was less like water than like gravel. The *Provision* heeled, recovered, heeled further, and Bo was suddenly at the wheel beside Sebastian, both of them leaning into it, and Priya was at the nav station with the dome light off because the lightning was giving her more illumination than any bulb could, the whole sky strobing white-blue-white.

"Course one-one-five!" she shouted, over a thunderclap that felt personal. "Hold one-one-five until I say otherwise!"

"Holding!" Sebastian yelled back, grinning — actually grinning, rain streaming off his jaw, because Sebastian met terror the way some men met old friends, with too much enthusiasm and no boundaries.

The sea stopped being a surface and became a terrain. Waves came at them from three directions at once, confused, furious, colliding and stacking, and the *Provision* climbed them like a mountaineer who had not been consulted about the mountain. Priya navigated by lightning flash: the reef line ghosting green-black to port, a wave cresting impossibly high to starboard, the compass card lit for half a second at a time like a photograph developing.

Something enormous groaned past them in the murk — a length of drifting wreckage, hull plating, moving faster than they were — and Kip, lashed near the bow, gave it a respectful nod as it went by, the way you'd acknowledge a hearse.

"Corvus is turning back!" Bo shouted, pointing astern through a gap in the rain. For one strobing instant Priya saw them: three shapes on the southern horizon, running for deep water, their wake a pale scar of retreat. Whatever the Corvus wanted from the Verge, they did not want it badly enough to follow the *Provision* into this.

"They think we're running for shelter," Sebastian called. "They think anyone sane is running for shelter!"

"Are we sane?" Kip called back.

"Statistically dubious!" Priya answered, and the whole boat laughed — a short, wild, terrified laugh, but a laugh, and it steadied her hands more than any amount of breathing exercises ever had.

Then the barometer, which had been falling like a stone in a well, went strange.

"Priya?" Bo said. "Tell me you're seeing this."

She was. The needle had stopped. Hovered. And begun — slowly, drunkenly, disbelievingly — to rise.

"Eye's opening," she said. "North-northwest. Tower bearing zero-three-five, nine miles. When I give the word, we turn, and we turn *hard*, because the eye is a room and the door is closing behind us."

The wall of the storm's forward edge was still around them, but it was changing — the rain thinning from gravel to needles, the wind swinging in great sickening arcs, the lightning growing lazy and wide-spaced. And then, between one wave and the next, the world went quiet.

Not calm. Quiet. The eye of a monsoon is not peace; it is a cathedral with a monster waiting in the vestry. The clouds stood in a ring around them, miles high, turning slowly, lit from within by buried lightning, and overhead there was a hole of actual sky — a small, obscene patch of evening blue, ringed by the tallest walls of water-cloud Priya had ever seen. The sea beneath it was chaos still, but a chaos without direction, great confused swells rolling from every quarter at once, the storm's own breath held.

"Nine miles," Priya whispered. "Twenty minutes of this, maybe less. Go."

Sebastian brought the *Provision* around and Bo fed the engine everything it had, and the little boat ran north through the eye's strange silver light, chasing a tower that appeared on the horizon like a needle standing in the sea — black against the blue hole in the sky, impossibly slender, impossibly far, impossibly *there*.

And then Priya looked back south, at the wall of the eye's rear edge, and saw that it was closer than her arithmetic said it should be.

The ocean, she thought, really did hold grudges.

"Bo," she said. "How much do you love that engine?"

"Not enough," Bo said grimly, "for what you're about to ask me."
