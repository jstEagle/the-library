## 17. Through the Eye

The wall of water did not look like water. It looked like a decision the ocean had been putting off for six years and had finally made.

I stood at the wheel of the *Provision* with my hands locked so tight around the spokes that I could feel my own pulse in my palms, and I watched a mountain of gray-green sea come walking toward us out of the dark like something that had heard about us personally.

"Sebastian." Priya's voice came through the rain hood at my ear, calm in the way that only truly frightened people can manage. "The eye is open. It won't stay open. We go now or we don't."

Behind me, Kip was lashing down the last of the deck cargo — what remained of it after the Corvus took our tender and half our fuel — and Bo was somewhere below, swearing at the engine in three languages, two of which I'm fairly sure he invented for the occasion.

And Imogen Reyes was forty minutes gone into that same storm in a skiff, cutting the line when I reached for her, smiling like a woman setting down something very heavy. I hadn't had time to grieve her yet. I suspected I wouldn't get time to grieve anyone properly until this was over, if it ever was.

"Course?" I shouted.

Priya had her chart spread across the nav table, weighted with a coffee mug, a wrench, and Tomás's old compass — which she'd taken to carrying since the recording, though nobody had said anything about it. The relay tower sat on a shoal spike sixty miles northeast: the last working transmitter in the Coral Verge, tall enough and stubborn enough to punch a signal through monsoon weather. The syndicate's cutter waited on every other heading. Delgado's people were patient that way. They'd built their whole business on being the only exit.

"Northeast by north," Priya said. "Through the eye. It's closing from the west, so we thread it while it's still a corridor and not a coffin." She looked up at me, and in the red glow of the binnacle light her face was wet — rain, spray, possibly neither. "Sebastian, I want to be honest with you, because someone on this boat should be honest at all times as a matter of policy."

"That would be new," I said.

"The eye passage is a coin flip. Maybe worse. If the walls collapse early—"

"They won't."

"You don't know that."

"I know Bo fixed the engine," I said. "Bo has never once fixed the engine without the universe feeling obligated to let us survive long enough to enjoy it."

From below, muffled through the deck plates, came a crash, a clatter, and a triumphant bellow of *"HA!"* that I chose to interpret as confirmation.

Priya almost smiled. Almost. "Turn us, then, Captain."

I turned us.

* * *

There is a thing that happens to a crew in weather like that, and I've felt it maybe twice in my life, and both times I've struggled to describe it without sounding like a drunk. The boat stops being a boat. You stop being people. There is just one animal with many hands, and every hand knows what the others are doing without being told.

It started with the first wall.

The *Provision* climbed it at an angle that I will not be describing to any maritime authority, ever, because no authority would believe we survived it. Forty feet of sea stood up under our keel and threw us at the sky, and for one long, weightless moment we were flying — engine screaming, rigging howling, Kip whooping from the deck in a voice that had no fear in it at all, only a kind of furious joy — and then the bow came down and punched through the trough and the world went white and green and upside down, and we were through.

First wall behind us. The eye ahead: a corridor of ragged calm perhaps half a mile wide, walled in on both sides by storm, roofed in boiling cloud, lit from within by lightning that came so often it was less like weather than like applause.

"Bo!" I hauled the wheel through a correction. "How's my heart?"

Bo's head appeared through the companionway, streaming rain, grease to his elbows, a spanner in one hand and an expression of profound offense on his face. "Your heart is held together with wire and prayer, same as mine. Third cylinder's flooding. I've got it drinking on a schedule."

"A schedule?"

"Every ninety seconds I open the bleed valve and let her cough. When I say *cough*, you ease her a quarter throttle and pray she picks back up."

"And if she doesn't pick back up?"

"Then we find out what kind of swimmers we are," Bo said cheerfully, and disappeared below.

That was the thing about Bo. Six years ago he'd have hidden in the engine room until the weather gave up out of boredom. Somewhere between Tomás's death and tonight he'd decided that hiding was a luxury, and now here he was, jury-rigging a dying diesel in the belly of a monsoon, humming.

Kip came up the ladder and braced beside me at the wheel, one arm still bound from where the Corvus chase boat's rail had opened him up. He should have been below. He was Kip, so he wasn't.

"Say the word and I'll spell you," he said.

"You can barely hold the rail."

"I can hold a wheel. Wheels are forgiving." He set his hand on the spoke next to mine anyway, not taking it, just resting there, the way you'd put your hand on a friend's shoulder. "Sixty miles?"

"Fifty-eight."

"At this speed, two hours. The eye closes in—" he glanced at Priya.

"Ninety minutes," Priya said, not looking up from her chart. "Maybe less. The western wall is eating the corridor."

"So we're going to arrive with the sky chewing on our wake," Kip said. "Good. I'd hate for this to be easy. Easy would feel dishonest."

I laughed — actually laughed, in the middle of it all, and heard Priya laugh too, a short sharp bark, and realized that this was what the eye did to people. It stripped everything down to the true parts. Fear was in there, sure, big as the storm itself. But underneath the fear was something older and tougher, and it turned out we'd been carrying it the whole time without knowing its name.

It didn't have a name. But if it had, it would have sounded like four people laughing in a corridor of dying weather, headed for a tower with a message in the hold that a dead man had paid for.

"Cough!" Bo bellowed from below.

I eased the throttle. The engine shuddered, hacked, spat — and caught. The *Provision* surged on.

* * *

The trouble came at mile thirty, and it came, as trouble always does aboard the *Provision*, wearing the face of a small problem with large ambitions.

"Lightning strike on the masthead," Priya reported, voice flat. "Antenna array's fried. Backup's fried. We are now navigating exclusively by—" she paused, and I could hear her deciding whether to say it, "—by lightning flash."

"By lightning flash," I repeated.

"It's a technique," she said. "Old coastal pilots used it. The flashes silhouette the wave trains. You can read the sea's shape off them, if you count the interval. It's imprecise. It's also the only instrument we have left."

"How imprecise?"

"Imogen Reyes once called it 'navigating by rumor,'" Priya said. "She wasn't wrong."

We did it anyway. Kip took the bow rail with a flashlight he knew better than to use, and became our eyes: counting the intervals between flashes, calling the shape of the swell — "*left wall building!*" — and Priya translated his calls into course corrections and fed them to me, and I fed them to the wheel, and the wheel fed them to the sea, and the sea, for its part, mostly ignored us but occasionally, grudgingly, permitted us to continue existing.

Somewhere in the middle of it — I couldn't tell you which flash, they'd all run together into one long stuttering heartbeat of light — I understood that we had stopped being a captain and a navigator and an engineer and a deckhand. There was no chain of command anymore. There was just circulation. Kip saw; Priya knew; I steered; Bo kept the blood moving. Signals traveled between us faster than speech, faster than thought, in glances and grip-tightenings and the particular creak of the wheel under a correcting hand. Once, when a rogue crest stood up dead ahead out of a flash-blind second, all four of us moved at once — Kip shouting, Priya calling the angle, my hands already turning, Bo easing the engine without being asked — and the *Provision* slid up the wave's shoulder like a hand slipping into a glove.

Afterward, in the dark between flashes, Kip said quietly, "Tomás would've loved this."

Nobody answered. Nobody needed to. The compass on the nav table — his compass — swung gently in its gimbal, pointing the way it had always pointed, which was forward.

* * *

Mile fifty. The corridor was narrowing. I could see it now with my own eyes: the eastern wall leaning in, the western wall leaning in, the strip of broken calm between them shrinking from a highway to a lane to a footpath. Lightning walked along the tops of both walls like sentries pacing a rampart.

"Twenty minutes of corridor left," Priya said. "Twelve minutes of running."

"Then we're early," I said.

"We're exactly on time," she said, "which is worse. On time means no margin. No margin means the next thing that goes wrong ends us."

"What's going to go wrong?"

As if summoned, the engine coughed.

Not the scheduled cough. A real one — a deep, wet, terminal-sounding hack, followed by a drop in the thrum under my feet that I felt in my teeth. Bo's voice came up the tube, stripped of all its humor: "Third cylinder's done. Not flooding — *done*. Con rod's knocking. I can give you three-quarters power for maybe ten minutes if I baby her, and then she seizes, and then we're a sailboat."

"We're not a sailboat," Kip said. "We're a wreck with opinions."

"Ten minutes," I said. "Tower's eight minutes out at three-quarters."

"Eight minutes if nothing else goes wrong," Priya said.

"Nothing else is going to go wrong," I said, and I said it loud, at the sky, the way you do when you know perfectly well that the sky isn't listening but you need your crew to hear you say it anyway. Because that's the last job of a captain in the bad hours. Not navigation. Not seamanship. Just voice. Just the sound of someone refusing, out loud, repeatedly, to agree with the ocean.

Bo babyed her. God, how he babied her. Through the deck plates I could feel him working — the rhythm of the bleed valve opening and closing, the engine's cough smoothed into something almost like a purr, a dying animal persuaded, stroke by stroke, to keep moving. Ten minutes. He gave us eleven. I found out later he'd been feeding the fuel line by hand, cupful by cupful, because the lift pump had quit and he hadn't mentioned it because mentioning it would have cost us the eleven minutes.

The tower rose out of the last flash like a bone.

It stood on its shoal spike against the boiling western wall — a rusted lattice a hundred feet tall, guyed with cables that sang in the wind, its beacon light blinking steady and idiotic and beautiful, indifferent to the apocalypse rearranging itself around it. And beyond it, visible in the strobing light, the sea behind the tower was already gone — swallowed whole, the eye collapsed, the walls married, a single unbroken chaos from horizon to horizon.

We came through the closing gap with maybe a hundred yards to spare. I will spend the rest of my life seeing those hundred yards: black water, white water, the tower's shadow sweeping over us like the wing of something enormous, and then — shelter. A lee. A pocket of relative calm in the tower's wind shadow, rocking hard but holding, while the storm closed its fist behind us and began, methodically, to erase the road we'd come by.

Bo killed the engine. In the sudden quiet — not quiet, never quiet, but a human scale of noise — I heard him laughing down in the engine room, breathless, disbelieving, the laugh of a man who has just argued with a diesel engine for two hours and won on points.

Kip slumped against the rail. Priya laid both palms flat on the chart table, over Tomás's compass, and bowed her head over it for a long moment, and I pretended not to see, because some things deserve privacy even in a crowd of three.

I killed the running lights. Then I looked up at the tower, at its stubborn blinking beacon, and I felt the box in the hold the way you feel a letter in your pocket that you haven't opened — heavy, warm, waiting.

"Range to the transmitter shack?" I asked.

"Base of the tower," Priya said. "Two hundred yards. There's an old service dock, if it's still standing."

"Is it?"

A flash lit the world. In the white instant, I saw the dock — warped, half-collapsed, but standing, its pilings thick as trees, close enough to touch.

"It is," Priya said.

I put my hand on Kip's shoulder. He looked up at me, rain running off his chin, his eyes clear and burning, and I didn't have to ask, because I already knew, because we all already knew, because somewhere in the last two hours the question had stopped being *should we broadcast it* and become simply *how fast*.

"Get the box," I said. "Bring Tomás."

Kip went below. Above us, the beacon blinked on, steady as a pulse, and the storm hammered its fists on the walls of the world, and the tower waited — sixty years of rust and steel and signal, the last honest mouth in the Coral Verge, about to speak.
