## 18. What Tomás Knew

The relay tower rose out of the sea like a rusted prayer.

Kip had seen it a hundred times on charts — the old Verge beacon, decommissioned before he was old enough to swear properly, its lattice legs standing in forty feet of water like the skeleton of something that had once been useful. Nobody used it anymore except smugglers and gulls. That was exactly why it would work.

"Antenna's still live," Bo said from the engine hatch, grease to her elbows, hair plastered flat by spray. "Somebody's been paying the power bill. Guess crime pays after all."

"Crime pays *terribly*," Priya said, not looking up from her charts. "That's why we're doing this for free."

Kip didn't laugh. He was holding the black box.

It sat in his hands heavier than its weight — eleven pounds of casing and syndicate seals and six years of silence. He'd carried Tomás's duffel bag lighter than this. He'd carried Tomás himself, once, out of the surf at Grave Marker Reef, and that had weighed nothing at all because dead men are mostly water and grief is mostly air, and neither of them weighs what you think it will until you try to set it down.

He hadn't been able to set this down since the fourth tide. He'd slept with it under his bunk like a dog that might wander off.

"Kip." Sebastian's voice, quiet, from the wheel. "You don't have to be the one who talks."

"Yeah," Kip said. "I do."

* * *

They'd argued about it, of course. They were a crew; arguing was their love language, their liturgy, their way of saying *I trust you enough to tell you you're wrong.*

Bo had wanted to cut the recording down. Just the scuttling order, just the names, clean and damning, two minutes of evidence and done. "Every extra second is a second the Corvus can find us," she'd said, tapping the bulkhead where the storm made the whole boat hum like a struck bell. "We're broadcasting from inside a monsoon. This isn't a press conference, it's a heist."

Priya had wanted timestamps and coordinates, receipts laid out in order, the accountant's version of the truth. "If we're going to burn Delgado," she'd said, "we burn him so thoroughly they can't sweep up the ash."

And Sebastian — Sebastian had stood at the wheel through all of it, saying nothing, which was how Sebastian voted when the vote mattered.

It was Imogen's broken transmission that settled it. Her voice crackling through the ruined radio the night before, ragged as torn sailcloth: *I signed the order. I was twenty-six years old and I signed my name under a man who told me it was arithmetic.* And then, softer, almost drowned by static: *Let me destroy it, Sebastian. It's the only mercy left in me.*

Sebastian had looked at the crew, one face at a time, and said: "She wants to bury it. So we're not going to bury it. We're going to say all of it. Every word. Including his."

Including Tomás.

Because here was the thing Kip had learned on the fourth tide, sitting in the dark galley while the box sang its dead men's voices into the lamplight: the world already knew the Meridian Star went down. The inquiry had called it weather. The insurers had paid out. The syndicate had collected. Six years of official story, smooth as ballast stone.

But the box held the part nobody knew. Nineteen minutes before the flooding started, a young officer named Tomás Ferreira — third engineer, terrible at cards, worse at keeping his opinions to himself — had stood on the Meridian Star's bridge and refused an order over an open channel.

*"Say it again,"* Tomás's voice had said, clear across six years and forty feet of saltwater, *"say it louder, sir, so the box can hear you too."*

And then the other voice — Captain Osei, or the man giving Osei his orders, the recording never quite said — and then the sound of a ship being murdered from the inside, valves opened, seams persuaded, and Tomás still talking, still refusing, right up until the channel died.

Kip had listened to it four times. The first time he'd cried. The second time he'd thrown a mug at the bulkhead. The third time he'd simply sat with his forehead against the cool metal of the box, the way you'd sit with your hand on a friend's shoulder at a funeral, and the fourth time —

The fourth time he'd understood what he had to do.

* * *

Now the boat wallowed in the tower's shadow, and the monsoon breathed on the back of Kip's neck like something enormous deciding whether to notice them.

The eye wouldn't hold forever. Priya had drawn it out on her chart in pencil — a wobbling circle of borrowed calm maybe nine miles wide, drifting east, closing behind them like a door. They had perhaps forty minutes of sky that wasn't trying to kill them. Maybe less. The Corvus had turned back at the storm's edge; somewhere south, a syndicate cutter was waiting for a box that was never coming; and overhead the clouds had peeled back into a strange, terrible cathedral of light, walls of rain a thousand feet tall rotating slowly around a patch of bruised and ordinary sky.

Inside that cathedral, Kip climbed the tower.

Bo had rigged the connection in twenty minutes flat, splicing their transmitter into the beacon's backbone with wire she'd salvaged from three different wrecks and, she claimed, one church. "Old girl's got range," she said, patting the antenna leg like it was a horse. "Decommissioned my foot. Somebody kept her fed. You'll reach every deck from here to Port Amaya."

"Good," Kip said.

"You sure about the open channel? Not encrypted?"

"Encrypted reaches one person. Open reaches everyone." He looked at her. "That's the point, Bo. Delgado's crews aren't the syndicate. They're sailors. Sailors deserve to hear it same as anyone."

Bo studied him for a long moment, rain running off her chin. Then she reached out and squeezed the back of his neck, brief and hard, the way you'd grip a brother. "Make it count, kid."

"I'm twenty-nine."

"Kid," she said again, and went down the ladder.

* * *

The transmitter room was a closet of rust and pigeon feathers, one working console, one chair with three legs and a sense of humor. Kip sat down. He set the box on the console beside the microphone. He keyed the channel.

Static first. Then the little green light. Then the whole listening sea.

"This is the vessel *Provision,*" he said. His voice came out steadier than he felt, which surprised him; he'd expected it to shake, but it turns out there's a kind of calm on the far side of terror, a flat bright place where everything is simple because everything is already broken. "Calling all stations, all frequencies. What follows is a recording recovered from the wreck of the *Meridian Star.* It has not been edited. It will not be edited. Some of you have been ordered to intercept us. I'm asking you to listen first. Decide after."

He paused. Somewhere below him, he could hear the *Provision*'s engine idling, patient as a heartbeat. He could hear the storm breathing against the tower legs. He could hear, or imagined he heard, the whole Coral Verge leaning in — a hundred decks, a thousand ears, syndicate men and honest trawlers and the Corvus fleet hove-to at the edge of the weather, all of them holding still.

He pressed play.

The bridge recording filled the little room. The creak of a ship at peace. A voice asking for confirmation of course and speed. And then — nineteen minutes in, though Kip knew every second of it now, had it mapped on the inside of his eyelids — the other voice. The order. Phrased in arithmetic, just like Imogen had said. *Adjust the ballast. Open the inspection ports. The company has calculated.*

And then Tomás.

*"Say it again. Say it louder, sir, so the box can hear you too."*

A silence on the recording that lasted four seconds and contained an entire career ending. Then the captain's voice, tired and terrible: *"Mr. Ferreira, you are relieved."*

*"No, sir,"* Tomás said. *"I'm staying. Someone should be on record."*

Kip let it play. All of it. The valves. The water finding its way in, patient as debt. Tomás moving through the ship, calling stations, getting men to the boats — the recording caught that too, caught him herding deckhands aft with that ridiculous gentle bossiness he'd had, *come on, come on, lifejackets are not pillows, move* — and Kip pressed his palm flat against the console and listened to his best friend spend his last nineteen minutes saving everyone he could and telling the truth while he did it.

The channel died at 0341. The recording ran another ninety seconds of ocean sounds, and then stopped.

Kip switched off the playback. The green light burned. The static waited.

He leaned toward the microphone.

"My name is Kip Alonzo," he said. "Six years ago I fished Tomás Ferreira out of the surf at Grave Marker Reef, and the inquiry told us it was weather, and we believed it because believing it hurt less. It wasn't weather. He died refusing an order, on the record, and the record kept it, and tonight the record talks."

He hadn't planned what came next. He'd thought he'd give the facts — dates, names, the insurance payout, the syndicate seals on the box, Imogen Reyes's signature on the scuttling order, all of it, receipts in order, Priya's ledger of the damned. And he did give those, in a voice like a man reading a verdict, each name placed carefully as a stone.

But then he stopped reading, and the eulogy came instead, the one he'd been writing in his chest for six years without knowing it.

"He was bad at cards," Kip said. "He lost to our engineer so consistently she started letting him win and he *still* lost. He talked to engines like they were shy animals, and honestly they responded better to him than to most people. He once spent an entire pay cycle teaching me to swim because I was too proud to admit I couldn't, and he never once told anybody, and when I finally got it he bought us both a beer and said 'told you the ocean likes you,' and I believed him, because when Tomás said a thing you believed it."

His voice cracked. He let it.

"The syndicate wants you to think loyalty means keeping your mouth shut. Tomás is here to tell you otherwise. Loyalty is opening the inspection ports of the truth and letting everybody see the water coming in. Loyalty is saying *no, sir* on an open channel. Loyalty is a third engineer with bad card sense standing on a dying ship making sure the box hears everything, because he knew — he *knew* — that someday somebody would come down for it."

He looked at the box. Eleven pounds. Six years.

"So this is for you, Tomás. Posthumous promotion, you stubborn son of a gun. Third engineer no more." He swallowed. "You're the loudest man in the Coral Verge tonight."

And he keyed the broadcast wide — every band, every channel, encrypted fleets and fishing nets and the syndicate's own cutter riding the storm's southern edge — and let the recording go again, all of it, unedited, into the rain.

* * *

Below, the crew gathered in the cockpit to listen, because even though they'd heard it twice already, some things you stand witness to together.

Sebastian stood at the wheel with his cap off, which Kip had seen maybe three times in eight years, all of them funerals. Priya had her eyes closed, lips moving, running numbers or prayers — with Priya it was always both. Bo leaned against the transom with her arms crossed and her jaw set like a woman daring the world to comment.

And when Tomás's voice came through the cockpit speaker — *say it louder, sir, so the box can hear you too* — Kip, watching from the tower window, saw Sebastian Villalobos, who did not cry, who masked fear with bravado and grief with jokes, put his hand flat against the wheel and hold on, and stay there, and not pretend otherwise.

Then the replies started.

At first just static, and Kip thought *well, we shouted into the void, that's a living.* Then a fishing boat out of Sable Cay, voice shaking: *Provision, Provision, we copy, we — God. We copy.* Then a Corvus tender, of all things, breaking formation protocol to transmit plain: *this is Corvus Three, we are standing down, repeat, standing down.* Then more, a dozen, two dozen — trawlermen, ferry crews, a lighthouse keeper, a man who identified himself only as "third watch, Delgado freighter *Anchises,*" and whose message was four words long and worth more than the whole salvage contract:

*"We heard him. Standing by."*

Standing by. The oldest phrase in the sailor's book. *We're with you. We're not moving. Whatever comes next, we heard it too.*

Kip sat back in the three-legged chair and laughed, wet-eyed and helpless, because somewhere out in the dark the syndicate's own sailors were turning their ships into witnesses, and there was no order in the world big enough to un-hear a dead man telling the truth.

The green light glowed. The storm's eye began, gently, terribly, to close.

* * *

He was still in the chair when the new transmission came through — weak, ragged, half-drowned in static, on a frequency nobody should have been using.

*"Provision. Provision, this is Reyes."*

Kip froze, hand hovering over the reply key.

Imogen's voice, thinner than he'd ever heard it, wind tearing at the edges of every word. Behind her, unmistakably, the sound of a small engine at full throttle, and under that the deep animal roar of the monsoon's returning wall.

*"I'm sorry about the fuel. I'm sorry about all of it. But the Star is mine to answer for, and the storm's given me a heading."* A pause, filled with thunder. *"Tell Sebastian — don't follow me. For once in your life, Villalobos, do as you're told."*

And then, fainter, almost gone, almost smiling:

*"He'd have liked you, Kip. Tomás. He always said the loud ones love the hardest."*

The frequency dissolved into rain.

Kip keyed the mic, already shouting for the others, already hearing boots on the ladder, the engine rising, Priya's voice cutting sharp through the cockpit — *"that skiff won't survive the outer bands, nothing survives the outer bands"* — and far to the north, past the closing eye, a single running light bobbing toward the wreck that had waited six years to collect what it was owed.
