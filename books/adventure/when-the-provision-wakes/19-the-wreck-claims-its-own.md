## 19. The Wreck Claims Its Own

The radio had been dying for an hour, and Imogen Reyes had spent that hour talking into it anyway, the way you talk to a grave.

"—so I told the harbormaster the manifest was wrong, and he believed me, because I was twenty-six and wore my father's coat and nobody ever believes a young woman is capable of anything except clerical errors." A burst of static. Somewhere beyond it, she could hear Sebastian breathing, which meant he was still holding the handset, which meant he hadn't hung up on her yet. Small mercies. "That's how it starts, you know. Not with a bang. With a signature. One line of ink and thirty-eight tons of ship becomes a rounding error."

"Imogen." His voice came through like something dragged over coral. "Stop talking."

"I'm almost finished."

"You're never almost finished. That's your whole problem."

She laughed — actually laughed, a real one, the first in six years — and then pressed her palm flat against the bulkhead of the *Corvus*'s aft cabin to steady herself, because the deck beneath her boots had begun to tilt in a way decks are not supposed to tilt. Through the porthole, the monsoon stood on the horizon like a second coastline, black and moving, and the sea between here and there was already coming apart in long white rags.

Six years. She had spent six years buying her way back to this exact patch of ocean. Syndicate money, syndicate favors, syndicate silence — every coin of it spent toward one destination, and now that she was here, the destination was dissolving under her feet at roughly four centimeters per hour, and the only thing left to decide was what shape her ending would take.

She had decided already, of course. She'd decided somewhere around the third tide, when the box came up out of the trench and she saw Kip's face and understood that no version of this story ended with her hands clean.

* * *

The skiff was tied at the *Corvus*'s stern rail, small and orange and absurd against all that gray water, like a toy someone's child had left behind. Imogen went down to it with a dry bag holding three things: the original scuttling order, signed by a junior officer named Reyes six years ago; a flare pistol; and Tomás's final log page, which she had stolen from a syndicate archive two years ago and carried ever since like a stone in her chest, waiting for a place heavy enough to put it down.

Bo found her there. Of course Bo did. The man had a sixth sense for other people's bad decisions; it was probably why his own were so rare.

"You're not taking that skiff out," Bo said.

"I'm taking that skiff out."

"The monsoon's leading edge is—" He stopped, recalculated, tried again with the patience of a man explaining weather to a wall. "Imogen. It's a wall. It's not wind anymore, it's architecture."

"Then it will be quick," she said, "which is more than Tomás got."

Bo looked at her for a long moment. Rain had started, fat and warm, the kind that comes before the real rain the way a knock comes before the door gives way. "Sebastian's coming," he said finally. "I'd hurry if you're going to be difficult about it."

"I'm always going to be difficult about it."

"Yeah," Bo said, and — this was the thing about him, the thing she'd never let herself notice back when they were rivals and she was busy despising how much she liked his crew — he reached out and squeezed her shoulder once, hard, the way you'd grip a rail. Then he climbed back up the ladder and left her to it.

* * *

Sebastian Villalobos arrived at a run, which for a man his age and his knees was less running than aggressive falling with style, and he caught her wrist just as she stepped down into the skiff.

"No," he said.

"Yes."

"Imogen—"

"Don't say my name like that." She pulled against his grip, not hard. Neither of them wanted to win this particular tug-of-war; they only needed the ritual of it. "You don't get to say my name like I'm somebody worth saving. You've known what I am since the box came up. Since before that, if we're honest, since the day I walked onto your dock with a contract and a smile and you looked at me like I was a debt collector wearing a nicer coat."

"That's not—"

"It is exactly." The skiff knocked against the hull, patient as a heartbeat. Behind them the monsoon made its low continuous sound, the sound of a very large animal deciding whether to eat. "I signed the order, Sebastian. I was the pen. Delgado supplied the motive, the insurance men supplied the arithmetic, but the ink — the ink was mine. And when Tomás refused to play along, when he sat in that radio room and said *no* into a live microphone with the water already coming in—" Her voice did something she didn't authorize. She let it. There was no one left to perform for. "He died being better than me. That's the whole story. That's the entire six years. And you can't forgive that, and neither can I, and the difference between us is that I've stopped pretending forgiveness is a thing that happens to people like me."

"So you're going to sail into a hurricane and drown next to a wreck." He said it flatly, testing the weight of it. "That's your plan. That's the redemption arc."

"It's not redemption. Redemption is for people who survive their mistakes." She smiled, and felt it land on her face strangely, like a hat borrowed from a younger woman. "This is just bookkeeping."

"Terrible bookkeeping."

"The worst," she agreed. "But the accounts have to balance, Sebastian. Somebody has to go down with the ledger."

The rain thickened. Out past the breakwater of the reef, the sea had gone the color of wet slate, and the Meridian Star's superstructure — what the divers' lights had shown them wedged in the trench, furred with six years of coral — was invisible now, somewhere under all that churning dark, keeping its dead.

And that was the thing, wasn't it. That was the whole unbearable thing. The wreck wasn't empty. It had never been empty. Thirty-one souls had gone down with the Star, and Tomás among them, and for six years Imogen had told herself the story where she was the investigator, the avenger, the woman working undercover inside the machine — and the truth was simpler and uglier: she was the machine. She had just been the part of it that felt guilty.

"There's another way," Sebastian said, quieter now. The fight was going out of his voice, or maybe going into it, settling down deep where the things he actually believed lived. "You come aboard. You testify. You stand up in some maritime court with your name attached and you tell them everything, and you live with it in daylight, in front of everyone. That's harder than drowning. Drowning is easy. I've seen drowning. It takes about four minutes and then it's done. Living with it takes decades."

"And who do I live it *with*?" she asked. "Your crew hates me. The syndicate will want me silenced. The courts will want me as evidence, not as a person. You'd carry me around like contraband, Sebastian, and eventually even you would look at me and see the signature instead of the woman." She shook her head. "No. I know how this ends for me either way. I'm just choosing the version where I pick the ocean instead of it picking me. There's dignity in that. Not much. But some."

"Imogen—"

"Also," she said, warming to it despite everything, because humor was the last tool in her kit and she'd be damned if she'd die without using it, "this way I finally beat you to a wreck. Six years of racing you across half the Verge and I never once won. Consider it my trophy."

"You never won because you cheated."

"I never won because you cheated *better*."

And he laughed — God help him, he laughed, a short broken sound torn sideways out of him by the wind, and for one moment they were twenty-five again, racing rust-bucket tenders through the shallows off Punta Verde, before any of it, before the debt and the death and the signature, two idiots who loved the same impossible sea.

Then she pulled the knife from her belt and cut the line.

* * *

The skiff swung free and the wind took it, and Sebastian shouted something after her that the storm ate whole — she liked to think it was profanity; she chose to believe it was profanity — and then the *Corvus*'s stern rail fell away behind her and there was nothing between Imogen Reyes and the Meridian Star but open collapsing water and her own two hands on the tiller.

It should have been terrifying. It was, a little. But mostly it was quiet, in the way that a room goes quiet after a long argument ends. Six years of noise — the syndicate's whispers, the insurance men's arithmetic, her own 3 a.m. rehearsals of confessions she never delivered — all of it cut off at once, and underneath it, simple: spray, wind, the knock of the hull, the pull of the trench.

She navigated by lightning. It seemed fitting. Every flash showed her the same picture: black water stacked into hills, the reef teeth showing white, and ahead, faintly, a smear of disturbance where the sea poured itself endlessly into the drowned ship's broken flank — the wreck breathing, or swallowing, or both.

Tomás had been dead six years and she had never once said his name out loud until tonight. Now she said it into the wind, over and over, not as a prayer — she'd lost the thread of prayers somewhere around the scuttling order — but as ballast. *Tomás. Tomás. Tomás.* Each repetition steadied the tiller. Each one was a debt she could actually pay.

The skiff shipped water early. She bailed with the dry bag's lid and kept the flare pistol dry against her body, and when the trench's pull took the bow and swung it, gentle as a hand on a shoulder, toward the dark seam where the Star lay, she didn't fight it.

There was a moment — she would have reported it, if there had been anyone left to report to — when the water rose over the gunwales and the cold closed around her ankles and she thought, with perfect clarity: *this is the first honest thing I've done since I was twenty-six.*

The last thing she saw above the surface was lightning, one great white sheet of it, and in its light the whole Coral Verge laid bare for a quarter-second — reefs like veins, the Corvus turning away small and far off, and somewhere out there a battered chase boat full of liars and heroes running for a relay tower with a dead man's voice in a black box.

Good, she thought. *Run.* Get it there. Make my signature worth less than the truth.

Then the Verge took the skiff, and the wreck below opened its coral-furred arms, and Imogen Reyes went down into the dark with her confession held tight against her chest — the order, the log page, the flare she never fired — arriving at last, six years late, at the only berth that would have her.

* * *

On the surface, the storm closed over the place where she had been like water closing over a stone, leaving no mark, telling no one.

But the sea keeps what it is given. And forty fathoms down, in the green silence of the trench, the Meridian Star settled a little deeper into its own grave — carrying, now, the last officer who had failed it, folded at last into the same dark that held the radio operator who had refused her.

Two signatures on the same disaster, finally filed together.

And far to the east, riding the storm's ragged edge toward a relay tower with the eye of the monsoon closing behind them, the crew of the *Provision* heard the last transmission Imogen ever sent — a single sentence, keyed in the clear on an open channel, calm as a harbor bell:

*"Tell them the truth came from the wreck. All of it. Even mine."*

Then static. Then the storm. Then Priya's voice, tight and shaking, calling Sebastian to the wheelhouse — because the relay tower was dead ahead, and the eye was closing, and whatever they were going to do with the truth, they were going to have to do it now.
