## 20. The Long Way Home

The monsoon, having spent its fury on us, now followed at a polite distance like a large dog that had bitten someone and felt bad about it.

Priya stood the dawn watch alone, which was how she preferred it. The sea behind them was still churned to a gray-green froth, littered with the monsoon's leavings — snapped palm fronds, a child's plastic boat, half a shipping pallet from some vessel that would never be identified. Ahead, the water lay flat as poured pewter, and the air had that scrubbed, ringing quality it only got after storms, as if the whole sky had been rinsed and hung out to dry.

*Provision* limped. There was no other word for it. Bo had done things to the engine during the eye passage that Priya suspected violated several maritime conventions and possibly a few laws of thermodynamics, and the old girl ran now with a stutter in her rhythm, a hitch every fourth revolution like a sailor favoring a bruised rib. Their tender was gone. Half their fuel was gone. The portside rail was bent into an abstract sculpture Bo insisted was "character."

And they were broke. Not ordinary broke — not *we'll eat rice for a week* broke. Broke the way only people who have just refused a fortune can be broke. Somewhere in the wheelhouse locker sat a black box worth enough to clear their debts twice over, and every nautical mile they put between themselves and Delgado's cutters made it less a bargaining chip and more a death warrant.

Priya did the math again anyway. She always did the math. It was her way of praying.

Fuel to home port: sufficient, barely, if the wind cooperated and Bo didn't push the engine past its stutter. Food: Kip had counted the cans last night and announced, with the gravity of a man reading casualty figures, "Eleven. And one of them is those beans nobody likes." Water: adequate. Odds of syndicate retribution: she'd stopped writing that number down, because every time she calculated it, the answer came back *high*, and her handwriting on the chart table had started to shake.

She heard footsteps on the deck plates and didn't turn. Only one person aboard walked that softly at dawn.

"Any sign?" Sebastian asked.

"Nothing since yesterday. The Corvus turned tail when the wall came through — we watched them run. But Delgado's cutter was positioned off Serrano Point, remember? Right on our line home. If they wanted us—"

"They'd have taken us by now." Sebastian leaned on the rail beside her. He looked ten years older than he had three weeks ago, and also somehow lighter, the way men do when they've set down something heavy even if they're not sure what they're carrying instead. "Either they don't know where we are, or—"

"Or the broadcast worked," Priya said.

They both looked aft, toward the relay tower long since sunk below the horizon, toward the invisible ripples of a voice that had gone out over every open frequency in the Coral Verge. Tomás's voice. *I won't sign it. I won't help you drown forty-one people for an insurance payout.* And then Kip's eulogy laid over it like a hand on a shoulder: telling him, finally, six years late, that he'd been heard.

"You think it worked," Sebastian said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I think," Priya said carefully, "that I've been listening to the traffic bands all night, and something strange is happening out there. Come hear."

* * *

The wheelhouse smelled of burnt coffee and electrical tape, which Priya considered the two great pillars of their profession. Bo was hunched over the radio console with his headphones half off one ear, wearing the expression of a man watching a magic trick he couldn't figure out. Kip lay sprawled on the bench under a blanket, his ribs strapped where the Corvus ramming had cracked them, one arm folded behind his head.

"Tell her," Bo said. "Tell her what channel nine sounds like right now."

Channel nine was the working frequency for half the small-boat traffic in the Verge — fishing skiffs, island ferries, the coastal freighters. Normally it was weather reports and complaints about fuel prices and the occasional argument about crab quotas.

This morning it sounded like a wake.

"—heard the recording twice through now, and my father crewed Meridian routes for thirty years, and I'm telling you—" A woman's voice, tired and steady. "—if Delgado Marine thinks any of us are going to keep quiet after hearing that boy refuse the order, they've got another thing coming—"

"—forty-one souls, and they called it *weather-related*—"

"—my brother was deckhand on the Star. Six years I believed it was the storm. Six years. Somebody find me whoever made this broadcast so I can buy them a drink—"

Bo pulled the headphones down around his neck. "It hasn't stopped since midnight. Every band. Fishermen, ferry crews, dock workers. Even some of the big freighter boys, and those guys never say anything about anything." He shook his head slowly. "You know what the syndicate runs on? Not guns. Guns are cheap. They run on silence. Everybody knows, nobody says. And we—" he gestured vaguely at the radio, at the box in the locker, at the whole improbable chain of events that had started with a debt collector smiling in their doorway, "—we broke the silence. On open channels. In front of their own crews."

"Their own crews," Priya repeated. She felt something loosen in her chest that she hadn't realized was clenched. "Delgado's sailors are hearing that their employer drowned forty-one people. And that one of their own kind — a kid with a radio — said no."

"Not just said no," Kip said from the bench. His voice was rough. He'd barely slept; none of them had, but his exhaustion had a different texture, a rawness underneath. "Died saying no. You can threaten a witness. You can't un-witness forty boats' worth of people who all heard the same thing on the same night." He pulled the blanket tighter. "My cousin works Delgado's Serrano depot. I called him on the shortwave before we cleared the tower — don't look at me like that, Bo, it was one call — and he said the yard's gone quiet. Not angry-quiet. *Scared*-quiet. Like everybody's suddenly very interested in their own work and very not interested in being the one who gives the order to go after us."

"Scared of what?" Sebastian asked. "Of Delgado?"

"No." Kip almost smiled. It hurt his face; Priya could see it. "Of being next on the recording."

Nobody said anything for a while. Outside, the sea ran smooth and silver, and the engine kept its limping rhythm, hitch-hitch-hitch-hitch, like a heartbeat learning to trust itself again.

"So we sail home," Priya said at last, "into the lion's mouth, and the lion is pretending to be furniture."

"That's one way to put it."

"It's the navigator's way. Everything is either an obstacle or a current, and you don't fight either one — you read it." She tapped the chart spread on the table, their homeward track penciled in with the little corrections and wobbles of a hand-drawn course. "Here's what I read: no pursuit since the Corvus broke off. No coded traffic on the syndicate bands — and believe me, I've been sweeping them. That's not how you act when you're planning a hunt. A hunt has chatter. This is—" she searched for the word, "—a held breath."

"A held breath," Sebastian echoed. "That's comforting."

"It's better than a knife. For now." She met his eyes. "But Sebastian — we should not mistake this for safety. Syndicates don't forgive. They wait. What we bought isn't freedom. It's time."

He nodded slowly, and then, because he was Sebastian and could not leave a heavy moment unbullied, he grinned. "Time to fix the rail, patch the engine, and figure out how eleven cans of food stretch across four adults for four days."

"Three days," Kip said. "If we eat the hated beans first, morale will improve through subtraction."

Bo snorted. "That's the most optimistic thing anyone's said aboard this boat in a month."

* * *

They fell into the old rhythms, because there was nothing else to do and because the rhythms were the only home some of them had left.

Bo lived in the engine room, emerging at odd hours covered in grease and speaking a private language of gaskets and compression ratios. By the second day he had coaxed the stutter down to a murmur, though he emerged to announce, with wounded dignity, that Provision would need "a proper rebuild, new mounts, maybe a full fuel system overhaul, and honestly a priest" once they were home — none of which they could afford, all of which he described in loving detail anyway, because Bo's dreams were the one currency they were all still rich in.

Kip healed the way he did everything: badly and stubbornly. He wasn't supposed to lift anything heavier than a coffee mug, so naturally Priya caught him on day two hauling the spare anchor line, white-faced and sweating, insisting through gritted teeth that "the ribs are fine, it's the *line* that's heavy." She made him sit down and drink water and told him, in the flat tone she saved for navigational errors and stubborn deckhands, that dead heroes were of limited use to the living. He sat. He drank the water. He gave her a look that said he'd do it again tomorrow, and she found, to her surprise, that she didn't mind. Some kinds of foolishness were load-bearing.

Sebastian cooked. This was the arrangement nobody discussed but everyone relied upon: when the world went sideways, Sebastian took the galley, because Sebastian's cooking was the one art form aboard that required no talent whatsoever and still comforted everyone. Rice, canned fish, hot sauce applied with the generosity of a man who had never once been wrong about anything. They ate together in the wheelhouse on the second night, all four of them, knees bumping, the hated beans sitting untouched in the corner like a diplomatic incident.

"To Tomás," Sebastian said, raising his tin mug.

They'd toasted him every night since the broadcast, but tonight Kip added something new. "To the log entry we never got to read," he said quietly. "Turns out we knew the ending all along. We just weren't ready to hear it."

Priya touched her mug to theirs and thought about the oath. Six years ago, four younger and more frightened versions of themselves had sworn off these waters, had sworn off each other almost, grief making them cruel in the ways grief does — silent at the wrong moments, loud at the worse ones. And here they were, sailing straight back through the middle of everything they'd fled, poorer than they'd ever been, and lighter than she could ever remember feeling.

Not because the grief was gone. Because it finally had somewhere to go.

* * *

On the third morning, the strangest thing happened.

A fishing skiff crossed their bow at midday — a battered little craft with rusted rails and a man in a rain slicker mending nets on its deck. Ordinary. Unremarkable. Except that as the skiff passed abeam, the man stood up, looked directly at Provision, and raised his fist to his chest — the old Verge salute, the one fishermen used for ships that had brought someone home safe. Then he touched his cap and went back to his nets.

None of them spoke for a full minute.

"Did he—" Kip started.

"He did," Bo said.

Another boat did it an hour later. Then a ferry pilot, leaning out his window to raise two fingers as he passed. Word traveled faster than diesel out here; the Verge was a small country made entirely of water, and apparently its people had decided what Provision was. Not debtors. Not fools who'd sailed into a monsoon for a black box. Something else. Something with a name that hadn't been spoken aloud yet but was being passed along the coast in galley kitchens and net lofts and bar rooms, growing slightly larger with each retelling, the way stories do.

"We're going to be insufferable," Priya observed. "By the time we reach harbor, Sebastian's going to believe he personally wrestled the monsoon."

"I did wrestle the monsoon," Sebastian said. "Ask anyone."

"You hid in the wheelhouse and shouted encouragement."

"Encouragement is a form of wrestling."

But she was smiling when she said it, and so was he, and the smile stayed on her face long after the banter moved on, because underneath the joke was the thing she'd stopped believing in somewhere around the third tide: that they might actually make it. That the math — her math, the cold honest arithmetic she trusted more than any prayer — might come out on the side of the living for once.

Home port showed up on the horizon at dusk on the fourth day, a smudge of lights against a bruised-purple sky, the breakwater lighthouse blinking its patient green. Priya brought them in on the flood tide, threading the channel markers by memory and habit, and the whole crew gathered on deck without being asked, standing in a row at the rail like a honor guard of the bedraggled.

The harbor was quiet. Too quiet, Priya thought — and then she saw why, and her breath caught.

At the fuel dock, where Delgado's enforcers usually lounged like well-fed crows, there was nothing. No black cutter. No cheerful man with a ledger. The syndicate office above the chandlery was dark, its shutters drawn, a paper sign taped to the door that she couldn't read at this distance but whose shape — official, rectangular, final — she recognized from a hundred harbors in a hundred trouble spots.

*Closed pending review.*

Bo whistled low. Kip said nothing at all, but his hand found the rail and gripped it.

They came alongside their own berth slow and gentle, the way you approach something you're afraid to believe, and Sebastian threw the lines himself, and the fenders kissed the pilings, and Provision settled against the dock with a sigh like a tired animal finally allowed to lie down.

A small crowd had gathered on the quay — dockworkers, a couple of the old-timers from the bait shack, the woman who ran the chandlery. Nobody cheered. That wasn't the Verge way. But as they stepped ashore, stiff-legged and salt-crusted and hollow-eyed, the crowd parted to let them through, and the chandlery woman pressed a bag into Sebastian's hands — bread, still warm — and the old-timers touched their caps, and somebody had already, impossibly, left a case of engine oil beside Provision's gangway with no note at all.

Priya stood on the dock a moment longer than the others, looking back at her boat, at the bent rail and the patched hull and the stuttering heart of her, riding easy against the tires.

Four days ago they had been fugitives. Tonight they were something the harbor hadn't decided to name yet, but was feeding anyway.

She caught up with the others at the top of the ramp, where Sebastian had stopped dead, staring at the syndicate office's dark windows. Beside him, Kip had gone very still, the way he did before saying something that mattered.

"Sebastian," Kip said quietly. "There's a letter nailed to your door."
