## 21. Settlement

The letter came on a Tuesday, which felt right. Tuesdays were the day the world had always chosen to hand Sebastian Villalobos his bills.

He stood at the dock office window with the envelope in his hands and turned it over twice before opening it, because an envelope with no return address and a wax seal was either a death sentence or a settlement, and after the last six weeks he'd learned to stop flinching at either.

Inside: one page, syndicate stationery, signed by a name he didn't recognize — some junior functionary in the Delgado legal apparatus, a man whose job it was to write sentences like the ones Sebastian now read three times through.

*In light of recent developments of public record, the Delgado interests formally withdraw all claims against the vessel Provision and her crew. All outstanding obligations are considered dissolved. No further contact will be made.*

No further contact will be made. Sebastian read that line a fourth time, just to feel it.

He walked out onto the dock where Bo was elbow-deep in the engine housing, Kip was sanding the rail down to bare wood, and Priya sat cross-legged on the hatch cover re-inking their charts by hand because they couldn't afford new ones — though *couldn't afford* had taken on a different flavor lately. It wasn't *or we lose our fingers* anymore. It was just *we're broke*, which was, Sebastian was discovering, an almost luxurious condition.

"Well?" Priya said, not looking up from the chart.

Sebastian held up the letter. "We're nobody's problem."

Bo's wrench stopped mid-turn. "Say that again slower."

"Dissolved. Withdrawn. The whole debt." He let the paper catch the wind so it fluttered, theatrical, because if you couldn't be broke and dramatic then what was the point of surviving a monsoon. "Apparently when your own deckhands hear your boss order a ship full of people drowned over open radio frequencies, bookkeeping gets complicated."

Kip set down the sanding block. He'd been quieter since the broadcast — not sad-quiet, which they'd all known before, but something steadier. A man who had said what he needed to say into every ear in the Coral Verge and found the ocean didn't end for having heard it. "Complicated," he repeated. "That's one word for it."

"Three of Delgado's own crews filed witness statements," Priya said. She'd been tracking it the way she tracked weather — obsessively, on borrowed radio time, with little pencil marks. "Two more refused orders to intercept us. There's talk of a maritime board inquiry. Talk of worse." She finally looked up, and there was the ghost of her old dry smile. "We didn't beat them, Sebastian. We made ourselves expensive."

"Expensive," Bo said, "is my favorite way to be."

Sebastian folded the letter and put it in his jacket, over the heart, which was sentimental and he knew it and didn't care. Six years ago Tomás had died carrying a truth no one would carry with him. Now the truth was public record, and the debt was ash, and the four of them were standing on a boat that smelled of fresh paint and salt and engine grease, and Sebastian found that his eyes were doing something embarrassing that he covered by pretending to inspect the horizon.

"You're crying," Bo said.

"I'm inspecting the horizon."

"At close range."

"It's a very important horizon."

* * *

They repaired the Provision the way they did everything now — honestly, which turned out to be harder.

The old way had been shortcuts. Wire patched with wire, a hull inspection waved through because the inspector owed a favor, fuel bought on credit from a man who smiled too much. The old way was how boats died and how crews got hollowed out, plank by plank, until there wasn't enough left of them to hold an oath.

The new way took three weeks and most of what remained of their money, and Sebastian wouldn't have traded a single hour of it.

Bo rebuilt the engine from the housing out, narrating every step to Kip, who had appointed himself apprentice on the grounds that "if I'm going to nearly die in your bilge again I should at least understand why." Priya redrew the charts of the Coral Verge from memory — the drowned labyrinth, the trench where the Meridian Star still lay, the relay tower standing black against the sky — and marked them with a new notation she invented on the spot: small circles around the places where truth had happened. The reef where Tomás's skiff washed up got a circle. The tower got two.

And Sebastian — Sebastian scraped paint. Twenty years of layers coming off down to honest wood, his hands blistered, Kip working beside him in silence that was comfortable in a way silence between them had never once been before.

It was Kip who broke it, on the fourth day, sandpaper rasping.

"I hated you," Kip said. Conversational. Like commenting on the weather. "After Tomás. Six years. You know that?"

"I counted on it," Sebastian said. "It was easier than hating myself."

"Yeah." Kip nodded slowly, working the sandpaper along the rail. "Well. Turns out you weren't the thing I hated. I hated the quiet. Everybody being polite about him. Everybody agreeing not to say his name near the Verge like that'd keep him safe somewhere." He blew sawdust off the wood. "You kept the quiet better than anyone. That's what I hated."

"And now?"

"Now I figure the quiet was just grief wearing a uniform." Kip glanced over, and his grin came crooked and real. "I buried the grudge last week. Little ceremony. Said some words. It was well attended — me, mostly."

Sebastian laughed until his ribs hurt, and then, because the coast was clear and the horizon unimportant, he let himself do the other thing for exactly as long as it took Kip to pretend not to notice.

* * *

On the first calm evening of the new season, they voted.

Not on money — there wasn't any, and for the first time in living memory that fact caused no argument. Not on routes or repairs or whether Bo was allowed to install the foghorn he'd salvaged from a wrecked trawler and described, without irony, as "a civic improvement." They voted on words.

Priya brought it up over dinner, the way she brought everything up: sideways, mid-bite, as if it had only just occurred to her. "We never un-swore the oath."

The table went still. The old oath — *never the Verge, never the Star, never speak of how we lost him* — had been spoken over cheap rum six years ago with shaking voices, and oaths spoken in fear don't expire. They just wait.

"We broke it already," Kip said. "Went back. Broadcasted. Whole thing."

"Broke it and it turned out fine," Bo said. "Maybe oaths are like engines. Sometimes you don't repair them, you rebuild."

"That's the same word," Priya said.

"It is not, and I'll die explaining the difference."

Sebastian set down his cup. "Then let's write a new one. Properly this time. Not scared words. Chosen ones."

They argued about it for two hours, which was the best part. Bo wanted the oath to mention the engine specifically; the crew ruled against this unanimously, including Bo, who voted against himself strategically to seem reasonable. Kip wanted a line about never leaving anyone's truth unsaid, phrased less pretentiously, and then spent twenty minutes failing to phrase it less pretentiously. Priya insisted on a clause about reading the final logs of lost crewmates *immediately*, and her voice cracked on *immediately* just enough that everyone pretended it hadn't, which was its own kind of tenderness.

What they finally scratched onto the underside of the hatch cover, in Priya's surveyor's hand, while the lamp swung and the harbor breathed against the hull, was this:

*We sail honest seas. We say the hard thing while the person can hear it. We read the log. We carry each other's griefs as ballast and jettison none of them. And when the storm comes — because it comes — we go through the eye together, or not at all.*

Four marks beneath it. No blood this time. Ink. Blood had been the old way, and the old way was retired with honors.

"There," Priya said, capping the pen. "Now it's load-bearing."

* * *

The syndicate stayed quiet, but quiet, they had all learned, was a kind of weather, and Priya watched it the way she watched all weather.

Reports drifted in with the supply boats. Delgado's fleet running short-handed, sailors walking off at port after port. A cutter captain court-martialed for refusing an interception order that half the sea had heard the reasoning behind. The maritime board inquiry announced, formal and slow and real. And everywhere, in the chandlers and the fuel docks and the bars where crews drank off their wages, the same low current: *the Provision's people. The ones who played the tape.* Said not with fear anymore, but with something warmer. Something that, if Sebastian was honest, sounded embarrassingly close to gratitude.

"They're going to want things from us," Priya warned one evening, tapping the latest report. "Interviews. Testimony. Courts love witnesses."

"Let them want," Sebastian said. "Wanting's free. So are we, apparently."

But it was Bo, of all people, who named the thing underneath. He said it at the dock one morning, wiping his hands on a rag, watching a syndicate-flagged freighter slide past at a respectful distance — respectful, these days, the way you're respectful of a reef you've charted.

"You know what actually killed them? Wasn't the tape." Bo nodded at the freighter. "It's that their own crews heard it and *agreed with it*. You can threaten a man's fingers. You can't threaten his agreement. That's gone now, all across that fleet, and money doesn't buy it back." He tossed the rag down. "We didn't sink them. We just told the truth where their people could hear it. Turns out that's the same weapon, used properly."

Sebastian looked at the freighter a long moment. Somewhere aboard it were sailors who had heard Tomás's voice refuse an order to drown a ship, and had carried that voice home in their ears, and had quietly, one by one, stopped agreeing. That was the settlement. Not the letter in his jacket. This. A sea slightly more honest than the one they'd inherited.

"Tomás would've hated how long it took," Kip said, coming up the dock with coffee.

"He'd have hated it loudly," Sebastian agreed. "For six years minimum."

"And then made a joke," Priya added.

"And then fixed something that wasn't broken," said Bo, "just to feel useful."

They stood together on the dock and laughed, all four of them, at a dead man's habits, which is the only true eulogy there is.

* * *

The Provision put out the next morning on the morning tide, no cargo contract, no salvage claim, no debt — just a season's worth of fair winds forecast and a crew with nothing left to hide from anybody, including themselves.

Sebastian took the wheel. Priya had the charts, circles and all. Bo had the engine purring in a register he described as "smug." Kip stood at the bow with the wind in his face, and when the harbor fell away behind them he turned and shouted back, over the water:

"Where to, Captain? Whole sea's honest now. Pick anywhere."

Sebastian thought about it — really thought, hand on the wheel, the new oath load-bearing under the hatch, the letter dissolving in his pocket like the last of a bad tide.

"Anywhere," he said, and rang the bell, and the Provision woke.
