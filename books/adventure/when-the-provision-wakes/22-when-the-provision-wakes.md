## 22. When the Provision Wakes

The letter came on a Tuesday, which felt right. Tomás had always said the sea saved its worst weather for Tuesdays, and Sebastian had never been able to prove him wrong.

It arrived the way official things arrive — too thick, too formal, smelling faintly of some government office's attempt to make paper feel important. The envelope bore the crest of the Regional Maritime Court, and beneath it, in small type that made Sebastian's chest tighten before he'd read a single word: *In re: MV Meridian Star — Findings and Posthumous Determination.*

He was sitting on the dock at dawn because he'd been sitting on the dock at dawn for a season now. It had started as insomnia after the monsoon, after Imogen, after all of it, and had softened into something closer to ritual. The harbor at first light was his favorite honest thing: fishing boats coughing awake, gulls conducting their eternal shakedown operations, the water going from ink to pewter to that brief, extravagant gold that made even the rusted hulks look like they'd been painted by someone with hope.

The *Provision* sat three slips down, freshly patched, her hull scraped clean of six years of grudges and one very bad season. Bo had re-caulked half the deck himself and complained about it the entire time, which everyone understood to be how Bo expressed contentment. Kip had repainted the name — *PROVISION* in letters slightly too large, slightly off-center, because Kip insisted the crookedness "gave her character" and no one had the energy left to argue.

Sebastian turned the envelope over twice before opening it. His hands were steady these days, mostly. There were mornings — usually when the wind came around from the northeast — when they weren't quite, but a man could live with that. A man could live with a lot, he'd learned, if he stopped pretending he had to live with it alone.

He broke the seal.

*Dear Mr. Villalobos,* it began, and then did something court letters almost never do: it got to the point.

*Following the broadcast transmission of 14 Vendémiaire last year, originating from Relay Tower Nine in the Coral Verge, this Court has concluded its inquiry into the loss of the MV Meridian Star. The evidence recovered — including voice recordings of the ship's final hours — has been deemed authentic beyond reasonable dispute.*

*The Court hereby posthumously clears Tomás Ibarra Ferro, deckhand and radio officer, of all findings of negligence previously entered into the record. Mr. Ferro's actions in the final minutes of the Meridian Star's voyage are now recognized as those of an officer who refused an unlawful order at the cost of his own life.*

Sebastian stopped reading.

Not because he couldn't go on. Because he needed to sit with that sentence for a moment, the way you sit with a wave before it breaks, letting it lift you instead of fighting it.

*Posthumously clears.* As if Tomás had ever needed clearing. As if a room full of people who'd never smelled salt could issue a verdict on a man who'd once talked a drunk smuggler out of sinking a dinghy full of orphans' supplies by offering to teach him dominoes until sunrise. Six years the record had said *negligence*, and six years the crew had carried that word like ballast they weren't allowed to jettison. Kip had carried it worst. Kip had carried it like a stone sewn into his coat.

And now a stranger with good penmanship had unsown it.

Sebastian read on.

*Furthermore, the Court has opened a formal investigation into Meridian Star's underwriters, Halloway & Grange Maritime, regarding the circumstances of the vessel's scuttling. Several former officers of the Delgado syndicate have come forward as witnesses. Cooperation has been described by investigators as, quote, "unprecedented."*

He laughed. Out loud, alone on the dock, startling a gull into indignant flight. *Unprecedented.* Of course it was unprecedented. You couldn't threaten a man's fingers anymore if every sailor within two hundred miles had heard, over open frequencies, exactly what your bosses had done to the last crew that stayed quiet. Delgado's own deckhands had become witnesses the moment the truth went out over the tower. The syndicate hadn't gone quiet because they'd forgiven the debt. They'd gone quiet because their muscle had grown ears.

The enforcer — the cheerful one, the one who'd smiled in their doorway with his ultimatum — had come by the dock once, months ago. No seaplane, no backup. Just him, holding two coffees, looking smaller out of context. He'd handed Sebastian one of the coffees and said, "You know what the funny thing is? Half our crews listened to that broadcast. Half of them asked why we were chasing you." He'd shrugged. "Can't run a syndicate on sailors who've started asking questions."

Then he'd walked away, and that had been the end of it. No reprisal. No hunt. Just a debt that dissolved like sugar in rain, and a coffee Sebastian had drunk because it seemed rude not to.

*The Court further notes,* the letter continued, *its gratitude to the crew of the vessel Provision, whose names have been entered into the inquiry record. A formal commendation ceremony will be held—*

Sebastian skipped the rest. Commendations were for people who liked rooms. He folded the letter along its creases, carefully, the way you fold something you intend to keep, and looked up at the water.

The gold light had burned off. The harbor was fully awake now, workaday and loud, and three slips down Kip was already aboard the *Provision*, arguing with the engine.

"—because if you flood her again I'm putting *you* in the bilge, see how you like it down there," Kip was saying, in the tone of a man who knew the engine couldn't hear him and didn't care. Bo stood on the dock beside the gangway, arms crossed, radiating the patience of a saint who had long since stopped expecting sainthood to be rewarded.

"She flooded because you primed her like she insulted your mother," Bo said.

"She *did* insult my mother."

"Your mother's been dead nine years, Kip."

"And she remains a sensitive topic."

Sebastian walked down the dock with the letter in his jacket pocket, and Priya emerged from the wheelhouse mid-argument, coffee mug in hand, squinting east with the particular frown she reserved for weather that was thinking about misbehaving.

"You're frowning at the horizon," Sebastian said. "That's either bad news or Tuesday."

"Both," Priya said. She handed him the mug — she always carried a spare; she'd learned that about him somewhere in the last season without either of them discussing it — and pointed. Far out, past the harbor mouth, past the shoals, where the sea went from green to that deep bruised blue that meant deep water and serious intentions, there was a smudge. Not cloud, exactly. Not yet. A gathering. A dark weight on the edge of the world, organizing itself.

"Barometer's been walking downhill since midnight," Priya said. "Coastal service says it's nothing. Coastal service also said the monsoon would make landfall on schedule last year, and we both remember how that went."

"We rode its eye to a relay tower," Sebastian said.

"We did. And I'd like it noted that I never want to do it again, and also that if we're going to do it again, I get first pick of the escape route."

Kip had gone quiet below. That was new. Usually Kip's engine arguments ran at least another twenty minutes. Sebastian leaned over the rail and found him standing in the engine room, wrench in hand, staring at nothing.

"You hear?" Sebastian asked, though he already knew. News traveled through harbors faster than through courts. Someone at the chandlery would have read the bulletin aloud before the sun cleared the breakwater.

"Yeah," Kip said. His voice was rough. He cleared his throat and tried again. "Cleared. All findings. *Refused an unlawful order.*" He laughed, wet and short. "Six years I thought — I don't know what I thought. That maybe he panicked. That maybe I'd have done better. And the whole time he was—" He set the wrench down very gently, the way you set down something you might otherwise throw. "He told me once that the job wasn't the boat. The job was what you refuse to do. I thought he was being poetic. He was being literal."

"He was being Tomás," Sebastian said.

"Yeah." Kip wiped his face with the back of a greasy hand, leaving a streak, and grinned through it. "Yeah, he was."

Bo appeared at the engine room hatch, having heard everything and pretending otherwise, which was Bo's love language. "If we're doing feelings," Bo said, "I'd like to register that the starboard fuel line is finally sealed, and that I expect this to be acknowledged at my funeral."

"Noted," Sebastian said. "Carved into your headstone. 'Sealed the fuel line.'"

"'Against considerable opposition,'" Bo added.

"*From the fuel line,*" Kip said, and the laugh that went around the engine room was the kind that had been six years and one terrible season in the making — loose, unguarded, the sound of men who had stopped waiting for the other shoe and started wondering what they'd do with all this free foot.

Sebastian climbed back topside. Priya was still at the rail, still watching the smudge on the horizon, and she didn't turn when he joined her.

"It'll be here in two days," she said. "Maybe three. It's going to be a big one. Not monsoon-big. But big enough to matter."

"Matter how?"

Priya finally looked at him, and there was something in her face he hadn't seen since before the Verge — not dread, exactly. Appetite. The navigator's oldest disease: the inability to look at wild water without wanting to know what was on the other side of it.

"The coastal stations are calling it a provision run," she said. "Storm's cutting off the outer islands. Three settlements, no resupply possible by air once it lands. They're asking for volunteers to run supplies through ahead of the worst of it." She paused. "Paid, technically. Barely. Enough for fuel and then some."

"Barely paid is our specialty."

"I know. I checked the rates specifically so I could be disappointed accurately."

Sebastian looked at the storm building itself out there in the deep water, patient and enormous, indifferent to courts and letters and the small bright business of human repair. A season ago, a storm like that would have meant one thing to him: debt. Weather as creditor. The sky keeping books.

Now it just meant weather. And three islands full of people who'd need flour and medicine and lamp oil while it passed, and a boat with a freshly sealed fuel line and a crew that had, against every law of nature and maritime insurance, become something that worked.

He took the letter from his pocket and read the last line again, the one he'd skipped past on the dock:

*A wilderness forgets nothing, Mr. Villalobos. Neither, we trust, will you.*

Court letters didn't usually editorialize. But whoever had written this one had signed it with a name Sebastian recognized — an investigator who'd spent three weeks aboard a support ship in the Verge last autumn, asking questions, taking notes, and buying rounds at the dockside bar while the crew taught her dominoes. She'd signed her note with a small drawn wave beneath her name. He'd thought it unprofessional at the time. He'd thought it perfect now.

"So," Priya said. "Do we vote?"

"Do we need to?"

"No," she admitted. "But I like voting now. We're good at it. Four to one used to mean someone loses. Now it just means we talk until it's five to nothing."

So they voted anyway, formally, absurdly, right there on the deck with the gulls auditing the proceedings. Motion to run provisions to the outer islands through the leading edge of a category-two system, proposed by the navigator, seconded by the engineer, endorsed enthusiastically by the deckhand currently hanging out of the engine room hatch to listen, and ratified by the captain, who didn't even pretend to deliberate.

Five to nothing.

Kip fired the engine. It caught on the second try — the first try produced a sound Bo described as "a whale with opinions" — and settled into that low, willing rumble that Sebastian had missed every single morning of the season they'd spent ashore. Priya took the wheelhouse and began laying in a course with the brisk joy of a woman who had finally been given a map worth reading. Bo cast off the lines with the economy of long practice, coiling rope like it owed him money and had agreed to settle.

Sebastian stood at the bow as the *Provision* swung toward the harbor mouth, toward the open water, toward the dark weight assembling itself on the rim of the world.

Somewhere behind them, in a court file, a dead man's name had been made clean. Somewhere ahead of them, three islands were waiting on flour and medicine. And somewhere in between was a stretch of ugly, magnificent sea that would try its best to kill them, honestly, for honest reasons, with no syndicate and no secrets and no sworn-off anything — just wind and water and a crew that had learned the hard way what it was for.

Tomás had said the job wasn't the boat. Sebastian finally understood the rest of it: the job was also the morning. The gold light on the rusted hulls. The crooked letters of the name. The argument about the fuel line. The vote that ended five to nothing.

The wilderness forgot nothing. Fine. Neither did they.

"Course laid in," Priya called from the wheelhouse. "Storm's going to be rude."

"Storms usually are," Sebastian said, and smiled, and the *Provision* woke, and ran for the weather.
