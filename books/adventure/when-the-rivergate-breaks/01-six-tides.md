## 1. Six Tides

The sixth tide of the week came in green and mean, dragging silt off the flats like a curtain being pulled across the sound. Zephyrine Vane felt it before they saw it—the way you feel a change in a room when someone enters behind you—and adjusted their grip on the mooring line accordingly. Below their fins, the oyster beds of Rivergate Sound lay in grey rows, and above them the sky was doing that thing it did here in late autumn: going the color of old pewter, promising weather it might or might not deliver.

They were on a cash job. No contract, no signature, no name attached to anything but the wad of bills waiting in a tackle box under the seat of Dree Halvard's skiff. Dree wanted her propeller shaft cleared of a snarl of ghost net, and she wanted it done without the harbormaster knowing, because the harbormaster took a cut of everything that passed through his office, including, apparently, other people's misfortunes.

Zephyrine had been clearing nets and inspecting hulls along this coast for eleven years. They knew how to make themselves unmemorable: pay cash, use a different surname at each dock, never let anyone see what was in the duffel bag that stayed packed by the door of whatever room they rented by the week. The bag was packed now, sitting in the room above the chandlery with its zipper half-open like a mouth about to speak.

The net came away from the shaft in one long, rotten sheet. Zephyrine bundled it, checked the shaft for scoring with a thumbnail, gave it a pass, and kicked for the surface.

That was when they saw the boat.

It sat at anchor a quarter mile east of the channel marker, riding the flood tide with the particular wrongness of an unmanned vessel—too still, too patient, the way a chair sits when the person who was in it has just left and isn't coming back. White hull, government grey trim, a survey pennant snapping flat and then lifting again in the gusts. *CSRV Tidewell*, said the transom, and beneath it, smaller: *Hydrographic Survey Division*.

Zephyrine hung in the water for a moment, treading, letting the cold work its way through the seals of their suit. They should swim back to Dree's skiff. They should finish the job, take the cash, and be gone before anyone asked them to be part of anything.

Instead they swam toward the boat.

* * *

The boarding ladder was down. That was the first wrong thing. Survey crews didn't leave ladders down; they were drilled out of that habit in their first week. Zephyrine came over the gunwale with water sheeting off them and stood dripping on the deck, listening.

Wind in the rigging. Water against the hull. And underneath both, faintly, absurdly, the hiss of a kettle.

Below decks, the cabin was tidy in the way that made the skin crawl—not abandoned, not ransacked, but *stowed*. Charts rolled and sleeved. Dive gear racked and rinsed, four sets, all dry. Boots paired neatly under the bunks. On the galley stove, a kettle sat warm, not hot, the burner turned to low as though someone meant to come back in ten minutes and hadn't.

On the chart table, a logbook lay open. The last entry was from yesterday, 14:20, in a small precise hand: *Running transect 7, anomaly noted at grid ref 44-C. Returning to site at first light.* No initials after it. No signature. As if whoever wrote it had been interrupted mid-thought—or as if they'd written everything they intended to write.

Zephyrine stood there a long time, water pooling around their boots, and felt something they recognized and hated: the pull. The same pull that had kept them diving wrecks off three coasts, the same pull that had made Odile Vane's daughter into a person who could read water the way other people read faces. Curiosity was a hook, and hooks were set deep, and Zephyrine had spent a decade learning to spit them out.

They went topside. They circled the boat twice. No tracks on deck except the crew's own, dried to ghosts. No tender missing from its davits—all four sets of dive gear accounted for, which meant nobody had gone into the water in gear. Nobody had left in the tender. The radio worked; Zephyrine knew because they lifted the handset, thumbed the channel the government used, and then set it back down without pressing transmit.

Calling it in would make them a person in a story. A witness. A name in someone's report. Zephyrine Vane did not appear in reports.

They dropped back into the sound and swam for shore.

* * *

Harbormaster Ede Prynne found them within the hour, because Ede Prynne found everyone within the hour—it was his gift and his curse, and he'd been exercising both on the Rivergate community since before Zephyrine was born. He was waiting at the top of the chandlery stairs when Zephyrine came up from the dock, a heavy man in a coat too good for the weather, breathing like the stairs had personally offended him.

"Vane." Not a question. "You were out at the Tidewell this morning."

"I clear nets," Zephyrine said. "I don't do sightseeing."

"You boarded her." He said it mildly, but his eyes were doing arithmetic. "Ladder down, kettle warm, four crew gone. Coast Guard's been notified, they'll have a cutter up from Corvane by tomorrow noon. In the meantime—" he produced an envelope from inside the coat, thick enough to have weight, "—finder's fee. Preliminary. There'd be more if your information leads anywhere."

Zephyrine looked at the envelope and did not touch it. "Information about what?"

"About where four trained surveyors went, Ms.—or Mr.—or whatever we're calling it—Vane, when their gear stayed dry and their kettle stayed warm." He smiled with the bottom half of his face only. "You're the best reader of tides on this sound. Everyone says so. Even people who don't say much else about you."

"The tide didn't take them," Zephyrine said. "There's no debris field. I looked."

Something flickered behind Prynne's eyes—disappointment, maybe, or recalculation. "Then find out what did. That's all the fee is buying."

"No."

He blinked. People didn't say no to envelopes; that wasn't how Rivergate worked, and Prynne knew exactly how Rivergate worked, because he skimmed it. "No?"

"I found a boat. You know about the boat now. That's the whole transaction." Zephyrine stepped past him on the narrow stair, close enough to smell wool and coffee and the brine that got into everything here. "Keep your fee. I don't sign things."

They heard him exhale behind them, slow and thoughtful, and they did not look back, because looking back was how hooks got set.

* * *

The second offer came at dusk, delivered by a woman in a fleet jacket standing at the end of the chandlery pier, where the light was bad and the wind carried voices out rather than in. She was from Meridian Salvage—the fleet had arrived two days ago, three vessels riding at anchor off the point like patient birds of prey, licensed, insured, and hungry. Their presence had been the talk of the harbor: a salvage operation this size didn't come to a backwater sound unless it expected to find something worth the fuel.

"Zephyrine Vane." The woman pronounced it carefully, having clearly rehearsed. "I'm Sera Quill, operations coordinator. We understand you were aboard the Tidewell this morning."

Word traveled fast in a town with nothing to chew on. "People talk."

"People do." Quill smiled. She had the easy competence of someone who'd never had to make herself unmemorable in her life. "Here's our situation. We hold a survey license for these waters. Whatever happened to that government crew, it happened inside our operating area, and when the investigation starts, every diver who touched that scene becomes evidence. Evidence gets deposed. Depositions get names attached." She let that sit. "Or—an alternative. You tell us what you saw, coordinates, conditions, anything anomalous, and you stay a private contractor who cleared a fishing net. We compensate discretion generously. More than the harbormaster's envelope. Considerably more."

The wind gusted. Off the point, the fleet's running lights came on one by one, three white stars strung across the darkening water.

Zephyrine thought about the logbook entry. *Anomaly noted at grid ref 44-C.* Grid 44-C was nothing—mud and eelgrass and the drowned foundations of the old ferry landing, according to every chart ever drawn of this sound. Except that Zephyrine had grown up on charts drawn by a hand that didn't trust official ones, and somewhere in a locked room in the house they'd sworn never to enter again, Odile Vane's notebooks had marked that same square of mud with annotations in red ink and a single word, underlined twice:

*Deliberately.*

Not "anomaly." Not "unidentified contact." *Deliberately*, as if the thing on the bottom of grid 44-C had been put there, or hidden there, or found there and then buried again on purpose by someone who knew exactly what she'd found.

Four surveyors had gone looking at first light. Four surveyors had not come back.

"I saw a boat," Zephyrine said. "Empty. That's all."

Quill studied them for a long moment, and Zephyrine watched her decide—correctly—that pushing would cost more than waiting. "The offer stands. Daylight tomorrow." She handed over a card, plain white, a phone number embossed in silver. Zephyrine took it because refusing would be memorable, and let the wind take it from their fingers a moment later, out over the black water.

* * *

They didn't go back to the room with the packed bag. They walked the long way, past the fish sheds, past the sea wall where the old men sat even in November, out to the point where the current ran hardest, and stood in the dark reading the water.

It was a habit older than sense. You could learn a sound's moods if you paid six tides' worth of attention, and Zephyrine had been paying attention since before they could swim. Tonight the flood was running strange—a back-eddy off the point that shouldn't have been there, a slack line hanging over grid 44-C like a held breath. Water going somewhere it didn't usually go. Water being *pulled*.

Over a hole, Zephyrine thought. Over a space. Something down there big enough to change the way a whole sound breathed.

They thought about the envelope. They thought about the card. They thought about four pairs of boots, paired neatly under four bunks, and a kettle turned to low.

Eleven years of never signing anything, of leaving before the leaving cost anything, of being a name the water knew and no one else did. Eleven years, and the sound had waited until now to open its mouth.

Zephyrine stood until the cold got through the coat, then turned and walked, not to the chandlery, but up the hill, toward the shuttered house with the salt-stained windows that they had sworn never to enter again.

Inside it, in a locked room, Odile Vane's notebooks were waiting.

The tide would turn at 03:40. Zephyrine intended to be underwater by first light—with or without their mother's maps, though they already knew, with a sinking certainty that felt almost like grief, that they would need them.
