## 2. What Odile Left in the Mud

The notebooks smelled of salt and camphor, the way everything Odile Vane had ever owned still did. Zephyrine sat cross-legged on the floor of the old boathouse—the one the town had never formally given her mother and never taken away—and turned pages soft as moth wing. Eleven years since the funeral. Eleven years since they'd stood at the edge of the graveside crowd in their one black shirt, heard the word *daughter* spoken over the coffin like a mispronunciation, and left before the first shovelful of earth.

The survey boat had been empty for six days now. The harbormaster's finder's fee sat unclaimed. The salvage fleet's offer sat unclaimed too, a thicker envelope with a thicker silence inside it. Zephyrine had taken neither, and had told no one why, and the why was sitting open on the floor in front of them: a water-stained field notebook with a sonar trace photocopied and taped to the inside cover, annotated in Odile's small, ruthless handwriting.

*Anomaly at 44°—grid seven. Not geology. Too regular. Too deliberate.*

Zephyrine had read that line forty times. It wasn't the line that kept them awake. It was the next one, written smaller, pressed hard enough to emboss the paper three sheets down:

*I will not give them a map. Let it sleep until the town is ready to hold it.*

Her mother had found something under Rivergate Sound and buried the finding. Zephyrine knew what that was like—knew it in their bones, in the bag that had stayed packed by the door for a decade. Hiding was a language they both spoke. It had never occurred to Zephyrine, until now, that they might have been speaking it for the same reason.

* * *

**14 March.** The tide gauge at Rivergate lies. Everyone here knows it and no one has corrected the record, because the lie is useful. The sound runs six tides where the charts admit five, and the sixth comes at slack water before dawn, quiet as a held breath. I have begun calling it the hidden tide in my notes and I can see the survey office's faces if they ever read this—*Vane's gone mystical again.* Let them think it. The sixth tide is real. I've measured it. And whatever is sitting in grid seven has been waiting under it for two hundred years.

The community elders fed me tonight. Mrs. Okonkwo said, *You keep finding things, Doctor. You sure you want to find this one?* I said I didn't know. She said, *Then you're smarter than the men with the contracts.*

I think I do want to find it. I think I'm afraid of what finding it will cost the people who trusted me with their water.

* * *

The dive was a falling tide, which meant the current ran out of the sound like a hand pulling a tablecloth. Zephyrine went down the mooring chain of the abandoned survey boat anyway, because the notebooks gave them a bearing and a depth and a deadline, and because they had spent their whole adult life being good at exactly this: going down alone into places that didn't want them.

The water at forty feet was the green of old bottle glass. Below that, brown. The sound's floor was a plain of silt and old pilings, and Zephyrine swam the bearing from the notebook with the compass strapped to their forearm, counting kick cycles, watching the depth gauge like a pulse.

The sonar trace had shown a regularity—a line, a curve, a suggestion of structure. What the trace could not show was the moment the silt gave way to timber. Zephyrine's gloved hand found it first: oak, black with two centuries of drowning, still holding its curve. A hull. A big one. Their fingers walked along a strake and found iron fastenings, then a name carved into a transom and softened by time until only three letters survived: *—R A T E.*

*Federate. Magistrate. Something colonial and certain of itself.*

Zephyrine hung in the green dark above a ship that had carried someone's law across an ocean, and understood two things at once, the way truth sometimes arrives in pairs. First: this was what the survey team had found. This was why their boat sat empty with the kettle warm. Second: her mother had been here first. The scrape marks on the hull were old, healed over with marine growth, years old. Odile had found this wreck, understood what it carried, and then—instead of a press conference, instead of a grant application, instead of the career-defining announcement every archaeologist alive would have made—had quietly let the silt close over it again.

*Let it sleep until the town is ready to hold it.*

Zephyrine's chest went tight around the regulator, and for a moment the anger came up hot through the cold water—*you found it, you knew, you could have called me, you could have—*—and then died just as fast, because the notebook pages rose in memory, all those entries about men with contracts, and the anger had nowhere honest to land. Odile hadn't hidden the wreck from her child. She'd hidden it from the wolves, and her child had spent eleven years proving she was right to.

* * *

**2 June.** I have confirmed it. The hold manifests, what's left of them, are legible under the concretion. This ship carried the original grant documents—the ones that established the *freehold* the Crown later "adjusted." The land the Rivergate families have farmed and fished for six generations was never legally theirs to lose, because it was never legally taken. The paperwork that would prove it is sitting forty feet down, wrapped in tarpaulin in the captain's strongroom, and it is worth more than every salvage contract on this coast.

Which is exactly why I am going to do the unforgivable thing. I am going to find it, and then I am going to lose it. I will file a false grid reference, salt the survey with noise, and let the sound keep its secret. A truth this heavy needs a community to carry it, and this community is not ready. They are still being offered pennies for their shoreline. If I surface these documents now, they'll be tied up in a licensed salvage claim before the ink dries, and the paper will belong to a company the way everything else here belongs to a company.

My daughter—my child—asked me last month why I never stay anywhere. I said the work moves. That was a lie. I stay away because the things I find are dangerous, and I have watched what happens to the people standing next to me when the dangerous things come up into the light.

I am leaving the map where only someone who reads tides the way I taught no one to read them could ever follow it. If Zephyrine ever comes home, really comes home, they'll find it. Not because I left it to them. Because I left it *for* them.

* * *

Zephyrine surfaced into grey evening light with the wreck's bearing burned into their memory and the taste of silt in their mouth, and the sound was wrong.

Not the water—the horizon. Four sets of running lights were coming up the outer channel in formation, riding low with equipment, and the lead vessel's floodlights were already sweeping the harbor mouth. The Meridian fleet. Not due for another three days, not due until the court's survey window opened—but they had a document Zephyrine hadn't seen yet, and Zephyrine knew the shape of it the way you know a storm front: a court injunction, an emergency salvage authorization, paper that would let them cordon the grid, drop their own divers, and legally own everything they brought up before the town could say a word.

Someone had talked. Or someone had found Odile's false grid reference and unpicked it. It didn't matter. The wolves had the scent.

Zephyrine swam for the boathouse, hauled out, and stood dripping on the planks with the notebooks clutched to their chest like a child's collection of stones, watching the fleet's lights grow. Eleven years of leaving had taught them exactly how long it took to disappear—twenty minutes, if they didn't look back. The bag was still packed. It had never been unpacked.

They looked at the bag. They looked at the water, where the wreck slept under the sixth tide with the town's stolen birthright in its belly, and where somewhere above it, six surveyors had vanished without a track in either direction.

The lead salvage vessel sounded its horn, once, a flat industrial note that rolled across the sound like a claim being staked.

Zephyrine picked up the notebooks, left the bag by the door, and went to find the harbormaster—because her mother had hidden this wreck to keep it safe, and the tide was going out, and Zephyrine was suddenly, terribly certain that the survey team hadn't left the sound at all.

They'd gone down.
