## 3. The Weight of Paper

The tide was falling when Zephyrine went down, and that was the whole problem. Falling water in Rivergate Sound didn't just drop—it drained, pulling hard through every gap and seam in the drowned hull below, turning the wreck into a throat with a current running through it. Six hours of slack, maybe less if the wind backed around to the northeast. Odile's tide tables, annotated in her small furious handwriting, gave them until the fourth tide after next before the interior passages silted shut for the season.

Zephyrine intended to be out long before then. They had not planned on being inside at all when the world decided otherwise.

The descent was a gray-green narrowing, the survey boat's last known position marked by a shot line they'd rigged two days ago off the old channel marker. Below forty feet the light went the color of weak tea. Below sixty, the *Meridian*—because that was what the transom said, what the sonar ghost said, what Odile's journal had named in a single underlined sentence buried between tide calculations: *Found her. Telling no one yet.*—rose up out of the mud like a cathedral that had fallen over mid-prayer.

Her hull planking had gone soft as wet cardboard in places and held like iron in others, three centuries of sediment deciding arbitrarily what to keep. Zephyrine swam the starboard breach where the frame had opened like a rib cage, fins clearing the lip by inches, and felt the exhale of the falling tide tug at their ankles, drawing them deeper into the ship whether they liked it or not.

*Like it or not,* they thought, and almost laughed into the regulator. That had been the shape of their whole life.

They'd found the first sign of the surveyors an hour ago: a dive slate zip-tied to a frame timber, Maren Kole's blocky capitals reading **AIR POCKET — 4 ALIVE — FOLLOW LINE — COME SLOW**. The knotted guide line ran aft along the port side, and Zephyrine followed it now through corridors of collapsed decking, past a ship's bell furred green, past the dark mouths of holds that breathed cold water at them with each swing of the tide. Their light caught movement ahead—silt hanging in the beam like snow that had forgotten which way was down—and then four faces, pale and salt-cracked, crowding the edge of a trapped bubble of air in the former great cabin, lit by one dying chemical stick.

Maren Kole surfaced into the pocket beside them, dragging off a mask crusted with dried brine. Her voice came out scraped raw.

"You're not fleet."

"No."

"Thank God." She laughed, or something near it. "We've been listening to them work overhead. They know we're alive. They sealed the breach yesterday morning."

Zephyrine went very still, the way they did before bad news finished arriving. "Sealed it how?"

"Cargo netting, chain, a steel plate off their own deck barge. Riveted plates over the skylight opening." Maren's hands were shaking; she pressed them flat against her thighs. "One of them looked down through the gap before they welded it. Made eye contact with me. Then he pulled his hood up and kept working."

The chemical stick guttered. In its failing light Zephyrine saw the others: two men and a woman, huddled on a shelf of exposed frame above the waterline of the air pocket, wrapped in the survey team's own mylar ground sheets. The woman was humming something tuneless and steady, the way you hum to keep a child calm, except she was keeping herself calm, and everyone knew it.

"They're betting the sound does it for them," Maren said. "No bodies, no witnesses, no injunction fight. The tide comes in, the pocket floods, and the *Meridian* becomes legally theirs by right of salvage and practically theirs by right of nobody left to argue." She looked at Zephyrine with eyes that had gone past fear into some flat country beyond it. "You came down here anyway. Knowing they'd sealed it."

"I came down the blowhole channel," Zephyrine said. "It hasn't been sealed because they don't know it exists."

"And getting out?"

"That's the part I haven't finished thinking about."

* * *

The documents were where Odile's journal said they'd be—not in the captain's cabin, not in any strongroom, but wedged inside a lead-lined document case bolted beneath the floorboards of the purser's berth, because whoever had hidden them in 1749 had understood exactly what kind of paper they were carrying and what men would do to possess it.

Zephyrine dug for forty minutes while the tide bottomed out and began, imperceptibly, to turn. The floorboards came away in pieces. The case came up crusted but intact, lead unbroken, and when Zephyrine cracked the seals underwater and shone their light inside, the parchment within lay dry and legible as the day it was signed: the original grant, the Crown's own hand, deeding the Rivergate shoreline and six miles of the Sound's eastern shore to the community charter—the same charter the Vane family's neighbors had watched get voided, resold, and fenced off, generation after generation, by people who'd never once gotten their boots wet.

Odile had found this. Had held it, probably, in these exact waters. And had put it back and told no one, because telling someone meant the fleet would come, and the fleet coming meant lawyers and injunctions and a court ruling that a piece of paper recovered by a licensed corporation belonged to a licensed corporation. Her mother hadn't rejected the town. She'd been standing guard over it alone, all those years, in the only way the law left open to her: silence.

Zephyrine's eyes stung behind the mask, and the salt water made it impossible to tell grief from the sea, which was probably the point.

They sealed the case in a waterproof bag, strapped it inside their buoyancy vest where it sat against their chest like a second heartbeat, and turned back toward the air pocket to do the thing they'd been avoiding doing, which was arithmetic.

Four civilians. One narrow diver. One blowhole channel that Odile's notes described as *passable on slack water, third quarter ebb, NOT on flood—will hold you like a fist.* Four people who hadn't dived in nine days, breathing from a shared emergency bottle they'd stretched by surfacing into the pocket. A channel that required timing to the minute.

And above them, somewhere in the gray, the fleet's tender circling, waiting for the sound to finish its work.

**INTERLUDE — MAREN KOLE**

*Day nine. The chem lights are nearly done. Ruiz has stopped talking about food, which is worse than when he talked about nothing else. I keep the log because Dr. Anand says structure is survival, and because if we die in here I want whoever finds this to know we were people, not a problem someone chose to make go away.*

*The diver came back. The one who isn't fleet. They showed us the papers—I couldn't read them underwater but I saw the seal, the wax, the age of them, and I understood why the harbor has been fighting the land office for a hundred years. We were never looking for a wreck. We were looking for proof, and it was down here the whole time, sleeping under our feet.*

*The diver is going to take us out through a hole in the rock. They drew it on the slate: a chimney, a bend, a squeeze, then open water behind the headland. They timed it against their mother's tables. Their mother. There's a story there I don't have room to ask about.*

*I asked them if they were scared. They thought about it honestly, which I respected, and then wrote: YES. BUT SCARED IS JUST THE BODY ASKING IF YOU MEAN IT.*

*I mean it. We all mean it. Tomorrow at slack water, we go.*

* * *

Slack water came at 4:17 in the morning, black and breathless, the sound holding itself still like a held note. Zephyrine went first, towing the document case, and the channel took them exactly as Odile had promised: a chimney of rock rising through the headland's root, a bend where the current on any other tide would have pinned and rolled them, a squeeze barely wide enough for shoulders and tank. Zephyrine came up in the blowhole's throat, gasping real air for the first time in hours, and went back down for the others.

Ruiz panicked at the squeeze. Of course he did. Zephyrine put his hands on the guide line and held them there, forehead to forehead in the dark, until his breathing steadied, and then pushed him gently through. Anand went smooth as water. The humming woman—Petra—went last, and paused at the mouth of the chimney to look back down into the wreck's darkness, and saluted it, absurdly, formally, and Zephyrine loved her instantly and completely.

They surfaced behind the headland in a chop gone silver with pre-dawn, four half-drowned government surveyors and one salvage diver clutching a bag of three-hundred-year-old paper to their chest like something newborn.

Behind them, through the blowhole's throat, they could hear the fleet's tender idling over the *Meridian*, patient as a spider. Ahead, across the gray water, the lights of Rivergate harbor were coming on one window at a time.

Maren treaded water beside Zephyrine, laughing and crying at once. "It's over," she said. "We made it. It's over."

Zephyrine looked at the harbor, at the boats already moving out from the docks—small boats, working boats, the whole town apparently awake and pointed in one direction—and at the fleet's two ships sitting fat and immovable over the wreck site, and shook their head slowly.

"It's not over," they said. "It's starting. And they've still got divers on that wreck who don't know we're out."

The first light hit the water, and Zephyrine started swimming for the harbor.
