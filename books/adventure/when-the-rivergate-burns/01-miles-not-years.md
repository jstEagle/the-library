## 1. Miles, Not Years

Orsen Montrose measured her life in miles. Other people counted years — birthdays, anniversaries, the slow accumulation of gray hairs and grudges. She counted ground covered. Eleven miles from the last waystation to the saddle below Graytooth Peak. Four more to the high shelf where the goats grazed in summer and died stupidly in winter. Sixteen after that down to the Rivergate itself, if you took the switchbacks, or eleven if you took the scree chute that had killed two men she'd known by name.

She trusted the miles. They never lied, never flattered, never promised something they couldn't deliver. A mile was a mile whether you were rich or poor, wanted or hunted, loved or entirely alone.

She was currently on mile nine of a client who was going to be a problem.

"Montrose." Aldous Wren said her name the way he said everything, like he was tasting it for flaws before deciding it was acceptable. He stood at the edge of the trail with his hands on his hips, surveying the drop-off as though the mountain had been built specifically to impress him. "How much farther to this pass of yours?"

"The pass is mine," Orsen said. "You're renting passage through it."

He smiled. He had the kind of smile that came from a lifetime of people laughing when they didn't want to. "You know, most guides are friendlier."

"Most guides are dead."

That wiped the smile off him, which was worth the effort. She turned back up the trail and let him stew. Behind her she heard his hired porter — a silent, broad-shouldered man named Ketch who carried Wren's second trunk like it was full of feathers instead of silver plate and good brandy — shift his load and follow.

The high passes above the Rivergate were the only country Orsen still walked freely. Everywhere else, sooner or later, someone recognized her name. Blacklisted guides didn't get work in the lowlands. Blacklisted guides didn't get work anywhere, technically, except from fools like Wren who heard the word *blacklisted* and thought it meant *cheap*. It didn't mean cheap. It meant she knew things about these mountains that no licensed guide would ever learn, because licensed guides stayed on the trails, and the trails were where the mountain kept its lies.

The truth was up here, above the treeline, where the wind scrubbed everything clean.

They made the saddle by late afternoon. Orsen called the halt at a spring that ran cold and iron-tasting out of a crack in the limestone, and while Wren complained about the accommodations — there were none; there was sky and stone and a view south all the way to the smoke haze over the Rivergate — she set the tent pegs and got a fire going with wood she'd cached here in the spring, because up here you planned six months ahead or you didn't come back.

"You've done this route before," Wren said. It wasn't a question. He'd been circling toward something all day, working up his nerve the way men did when they wanted information they hadn't paid for.

"Forty-one times."

"And yet you're not licensed. I checked, you know. The Guild has nothing on you. No record at all, which is stranger than a bad record." He warmed his hands at her fire without asking, another small theft. "A woman your age should have twenty years of entries. Instead it's like you appeared out of thin air eight years ago."

Orsen fed a stick into the flames and watched it catch. Eight years ago. That was when the miles started being the only thing she counted, because counting anything else hurt too much.

"I worked the lowland routes when I was young," she said. "Rivergate to the coast. Trade caravans, pilgrim parties, the usual."

"What happened?"

"You're paying me to guide you over Graytooth, Master Wren. You're not paying me to talk."

"I'm paying you *half* what the Guild charges, which means I'm entitled to twice the curiosity." He leaned back against his trunk, smug as a cat in cream. "Come on. Everyone's got a story. Yours must be better than most, or you wouldn't be up here selling it to men like me."

Men like him. She could have told him the story just to watch his face change. She could have told him about the Vessany caravan, the one she'd led through the border passes the year the war ended — except it wasn't a war anyone admitted to ending, so officially there had been no refugees, and officially no caravan, and officially no order that came down from the magistrate's office in Rivergate saying certain travelers were to be delayed at the high camps until the weather turned. Delayed. That was the word on the paper. Nine days of delay in a whiteout, and when the weather broke, thirty-one people who had paid her honestly to carry them to safety were frozen in their tents, and the magistrate's men were waiting at the bottom with wagons, and everyone involved got a cut except the guide who'd followed orders and then made the mistake of writing a letter about it.

The letter got her blacklisted. The wagons got whoever sent them rich. And the name Vessany became a word people stopped saying, the way you stop saying a word that tastes of blood.

But Wren didn't want that story. Men like Wren wanted a scandal with a romance in it, something they could retell at dinner. So she gave him nothing.

"Eat your supper," she said. "We cross at first light. The melt comes off the cornice around midmorning and I don't stand under falling ice for anyone's curiosity."

* * *

The cornice held. They crossed under it in gray dawn light, boots crunching on refrozen snow, and Orsen put the danger behind them in ninety minutes flat, which was good going with a soft client and a heavy porter. By noon they were on the descent, the southern slopes opening up below them in long brown waves of tussock grass, and Wren's mood improved the way it always did once the mortal peril was behind him and the bragging rights ahead.

"There it is," he said, stopping on the shoulder of the trail. Below them the valley fell away toward the river — the great gray-green sweep of the Rivergate, swollen with early flood, braiding and unbraiding across its floodplain like something alive trying on different shapes. Far downstream, barely visible through the haze, the city itself: walls, bridges, the citadel rock with its banner snapping red and gold.

"The flood's early this year," Orsen said.

"Is it? Good for shipping, I'd imagine."

"It's good for drowning." She watched the water. Something about it bothered her — the color, maybe, too much silt in it for this early, as if the river were carrying down more than meltwater. As if something upstream had been stirred loose. "The Rivergate takes what the mountains give it. This year the mountains are giving generously."

Wren laughed at that, delighted, and repeated it under his breath like he was adding it to his collection. *The mountains are giving generously.* Everything was material for men like him.

They made the valley floor by evening and camped in the lee of an old waystone, one of the survey markers from before the war, its carved letters worn to ghosts. Orsen cooked; Wren drank the brandy from his trunk and talked about himself, which required no encouragement. Shipping interests. Salvage concessions. He had friends in the magistrate's office, he said, important ones, and there were opportunities on the river this season for a man with capital and vision.

"Wrecks," he said, and Orsen's hands went still over the fire. "The river gives and the river takes, Montrose. Every year it takes barges, boats, whole cargoes. And every year the salvage rights get more valuable. Corvane's office controls them all now, you know. One office, one signature, and whatever the water gives back belongs to whoever paid for the privilege." He raised his cup to the darkening river. "I intend to be paying."

"The river doesn't give things back," Orsen said. "It lends them. And it keeps accounts."

"More material," Wren murmured happily.

She slept badly, which was unusual. Up here she usually slept like the dead — the honest dead, the ones who'd earned it. But the river noise carried up the valley, louder than it should have been, a deep continuous grinding, and somewhere past midnight she woke to hear Ketch moving around the camp, quiet as a cat, doing something at the packs.

She lay still and listened. He finished whatever he was doing and went back to his bedroll. In the morning she'd check. In the morning she'd check everything, because that was the price of walking through the world with a name nobody trusted: you learned to trust arithmetic instead. Weighs, counts, distances, sums. Numbers didn't have loyalties.

* * *

In the morning she checked.

It took her ten minutes, going through the gear with the methodical patience her father had beaten into her before the Guild ever saw her: every rope coiled and weighed in the hand, every ration sack hefted, every buckle and strap accounted for. Ketch watched her do it with a perfectly blank face, which told her plenty. Wren was still in his tent, snoring off the brandy.

The discrepancy was in the fee pouch. Her fee — half the Guild rate, agreed in Lowmark, witnessed by the ferry-master's clerk — had been paid in advance, half up front, half due at journey's end, and today was journey's end. The waystation at Ford Hollow sat four miles downstream, where Wren would take the barge north and she'd take her pay and walk away.

Except the pouch was light. She counted it twice, then a third time by feel alone, eyes closed, the way she counted pitons in the dark. Forty crowns short. No — forty crowns short and three coins swapped for slugs, lead blanks with a wash of gilt that would fool a man counting by firelight but not a woman counting by touch.

She opened her eyes. Ketch was watching her.

"Well?" Wren emerged from the tent, buckling his sword belt even though there was nothing within fifty miles to use it on. "Ready to be rid of us, Montrose? Onward to Ford Hollow. I'll confess I'm looking forward to a bed that isn't a rock."

"Your payment's short," Orsen said.

Wren paused. Then he sighed, theatrical, wounded. "Short? Let me see that." He took the pouch from her hand — took it, didn't ask, and she noted that too — and poured the coins into his palm, and looked at them, and looked at her, and performed a small frown. "This looks complete to me."

"It's forty short, and three of those are lead."

"Is it?" He poured them back, unhurried. "Well. Counting was never my strength. Perhaps you misremembered the figure. These arrangements can become confused." He handed the pouch back with a smile that was almost friendly, which was how she knew the theft was deliberate. "Consider it a lesson in negotiation. Half the Guild rate — really, Montrose, a guide who discounts herself that far can't expect merchants to honor the invoice. The market sets the price. You taught me that yourself, taking my coin in the first place."

The anger came up slow and cold, the way it always did, a tide rather than a fire. She'd been cheated before. She'd been cheated by professionals — magistrates, guildmasters, men with seals and offices — and she'd survived it by putting the miles between herself and the memory. But there was something about the smallness of this theft, the pettiness of it, that scraped raw.

Ketch hadn't moved. His blank face hadn't changed. But his eyes flicked — once, briefly — toward the waystation path, and Orsen read it plainly enough: *let it go, walk away, live.*

"Ford Hollow is four miles," she said. "You can find it yourselves. The trail follows the water. You literally cannot miss it."

Wren blinked. "You're abandoning a contract?"

"I'm completing one. The contract says delivery to the valley floor. Look at the ground, Master Wren. This is the valley floor." She shouldered her own pack — light, always light, everything she owned in forty pounds — and picked up her staff. "The rest of the way is flat and marked. Even you can't drown on it."

"You'll regret this. I have connections—"

"Everyone you know has connections. That's the whole problem with you people." She was already walking. Behind her, Wren's voice rose — threats, offers, the recitation of his own importance — and faded, and faded, and became the river again.

* * *

She took the high bank instead of the trail, because the high bank was hers and the trail belonged to clients. Four miles of ridge walking with the flooded river sliding along below her, huge and slow and brown, carrying whole trees in its current like straws. The floodplain had vanished. Where in autumn there would be fields and fence lines and the little hamlets that sold hay to bargemen, there was now just water, an inland sea six miles wide in places, dotted with drowned treetops.

The mountains are giving generously.

She stopped at a point above a wide bend and ate the bread and hard cheese that constituted lunch, and watched the water, and tried to let the miles settle her the way they usually did. It wasn't working. The theft sat in her like a swallowed stone. Not the money — forty crowns was forty crowns, and she'd been poorer — but the shape of it. The casualness. The way Wren had looked at her when he handed the pouch back, that smile, that lesson-in-negotiation line. He'd cheated her precisely because she was blacklisted, because a blacklisted woman had no guild to appeal to, no clerk to witness, no legal existence at all. He'd cheated her the way you'd shortchange a ghost.

Eight years ago she'd had a license, a partner, a route, a name people said without spitting. She'd had a sister in Rivergate who wrote letters. She'd had a life measured in more than miles.

Then the letter she wrote about the frozen caravan went to the wrong desk, and the right desk burned it, and the wrong desk sent men to her sister's door, and after that Orsen Montrose stopped existing on paper. She walked into the high country with a rope and a grudge and stayed there. The Guild struck her name. The magistrate's office filed her under *resolved*. And the mountains, which had never cared about any of it, kept accepting her miles one at a time, honest weight for honest weight.

Trust terrain, her father used to say. Terrain doesn't lie. People lie, charts lie, contracts lie. Terrain just is.

Her father had died in a crevasse, trusting terrain. So maybe his advice was imperfect. But it was the only inheritance she'd kept.

Below her, the river bent around a drowned orchard, and the current did something strange. It eddied — a slow rotation, wide as a wagon turn, spinning lazily in the middle of all that power like a thought interrupting a shout. Eddies meant obstruction below, something big enough to split the current. Flood debris, probably. A sunken barge. A house.

Orsen stood and watched it for a long time, chewing, her professional interest waking despite itself. The eddy held steady. Whatever was down there was anchored, and big, and sitting in the shallows close enough to shore that a woman with a hook and line and no respect for property law might reach it.

She had no food for tomorrow. That was the practical fact underneath everything else. Forty crowns short meant no resupply at Ford Hollow, no barge fare, no room at the waystation. The flood season had closed the passes behind her and the lowlands ahead were water, and she was alone on a ridge with a fishing line and a grudge.

The river takes, Wren had said. The river gives back.

"We'll see," Orsen told it, and began to climb down.
