## 2. What the Water Gives Back

The flood season turned the valley into a country that didn't exist on any chart Orsen had ever walked.

She'd seen it happen before — the Rivergate swelling fat and brown with snowmelt from the high passes, swallowing fence lines, orchards, the lower road to Lowmark — but never from this side of it, alone, with nothing to her name but a guide's rope, a skinning knife, and forty-one miles of anger she'd measured out between herself and the man who'd cheated her.

Forty-one miles. She counted them the way other people counted days. It was a habit she'd built young, back when her father still took contracts and still came home, back when distance was the only honest thing in her life. A mile was a mile whether you were rich or starving. A mile never lied about what it cost.

She'd spent three of those miles finding high ground above the drowned shallows, an old granite shelf where the pines thinned and the water spread out below like tarnished pewter, flat and patient as something waiting to be asked a question. She'd spent another six rigging a shelter from driftwood and canvas salvaged off a dead man's boat — she hadn't asked how he died; the river had already answered. The rest of the miles she intended to put behind her by week's end, once she'd eaten, once she'd figured out which direction the salvage fleets weren't looking.

Because that was the thing about being blacklisted in the guide trade: you learned quickly that the mountains had no opinion of you at all, but men did, and men talked. *Orsen Montrose left a client on the ice.* That was the story. Not the true one — not the part where Aldous Wren had cut his own rope line to save his cargo crates and let his porter dangle instead, not the part where Orsen had chosen the porter over the contract and carried him down eleven switchbacks with a broken wrist screaming in her ear. Just the clean version, the version that fit in a ledger: *unreliable. Do not hire.*

She crouched at the edge of the shelf now, morning fog burning off the floodwater, and rigged a fishing line from gut cord and a bent pin. Food first. Everything else after. Her mother's rule, or maybe just a rule she'd invented and named after her mother because it sounded better that way.

The flooded shallows were generous this time of year. The river drowned whole pastures, and the fish came up into the grass to spawn, fat and stupid and easy. Orsen baited the hook with a strip of dried venison she'd traded for in a nameless hamlet two days back, and cast out into the gray-green water where a fence post stood up to its shoulders like a drowning man who'd given up struggling.

The bobber sat. Sat. Sat some longer.

Orsen chewed a strip of jerky and watched the water with the particular patience of someone who had spent years waiting on weather. Above her, a hawk rode a thermal without moving its wings. Below her, the drowned valley held its breath. Somewhere far downstream — too far to see, close enough to feel, the way you feel a storm before it shows — the Rivergate citadel squatted over the falls, and somewhere beyond that, the sea.

The bobber twitched.

Then it didn't twitch so much as *commit*, plunging under with a violence that yanked the gut cord taut across her palm and nearly hauled her off her heels.

"Hello," Orsen said, to nobody, and set her boots in the granite cracks and hauled.

It wasn't a fish. She knew that within two seconds, because fish fought in pulses — run, rest, run — and whatever was on the other end of her line fought like a single long stubborn argument, heavy and steady and deep. She gave line. Took it back. Gave more. The cord sang against the rock edge, thinning, and she swore and wrapped it twice around her forearm, letting the burn anchor her.

Whatever it was, it was snagged. Snagged on something big.

Twenty minutes of hauling. Twenty minutes of the hawk circling overhead like it was keeping score. By the end her forearms shook and the cord had bitten a red groove into her skin, and the thing beneath the water had come up off the bottom enough that she could see it: a dark shape in the murk, angular, man-high, trailing weed like hair.

A chest.

Not a fisherman's chest, not a settler's strongbox lost off some capsized ferry. This was iron-banded oak, wide as a coffin, crusted white with mineral scale, and it was hung up on the ribs of something even bigger beneath it — a hull, she realized, her stomach tightening. The flooded shallows here went down twenty feet in the old channel, and down there, wedged in the silt, lay the bones of a ship. The chest had been inside it. The spring floods had shaken it loose, and her hook had caught the hasp.

Every sensible thought she owned said *leave it*. Salvage rights belonged to whoever held the fleet charter, and the fleet charters belonged to Magistrate Corvane, and Corvane's dredge crews dragged every bend of this river from thaw to freeze. Touching unclaimed salvage wasn't just theft. In the Rivergate territories it was a kind of blasphemy, and they burned blasphemers slowly, when they bothered with fire at all.

But the chest had caught her line, and the line was her last good gut cord, and Orsen Montrose had never in her life left gear in the field.

She worked the hasp free with the knife, levering the crusted latch until it cracked open with a sound like a knuckle popping, and the chest swung wide in the shallow water, spilling silt and darkness.

* * *

She got it onto the granite shelf by rigging a drag-sled from the dead man's boat planks, and by then the sun was high and the fog was gone and she could see what she'd pulled from the river properly for the first time.

The seal hit her before anything else did.

Stamped into the lid's iron band, repeated three times around a central lockplate, was a device she knew the way you know a face from a wanted poster: a bridge over three waves, crowned with a star. Vessany. The drowned nation. The one they didn't have a war for anymore because nobody would say the war's name out loud — not in taverns, not in markets, not in the confessionals of the river towns, where priests had taken to hearing that particular sin through a screen and forgetting it immediately.

Vessany had been a real place once. Orsen had met exactly two people who admitted to remembering it: an old ferryman on the upper reaches who claimed to have crossed its border as a boy, and a drunk in Lowmark who sang songs in a language the barkeep charged him extra to allow. Everyone else treated the word like a hole in the floor. You stepped around it. You didn't look down.

And here was its seal, staring up at her out of a chest full of river water, bold as brass.

Her hands were steady. She noticed that about herself the way you notice weather — distantly, clinically. Inside, something was doing the opposite of steady.

She bailed the chest out with her cup. Under an inch of muck, the contents lay arranged with a care that made her throat tighten, because careful packing meant careful hands meant living people had loaded this, expecting it to matter:

A roll of oilcloth, tied with waxed cord.

A brass cylinder the length of her forearm, capped at both ends, etched with tide-marks and numbers she didn't recognize — no, she corrected herself, numbers she recognized but had never seen assembled this way. Lunar tables. Tide tables. For a river, not a sea.

And beneath both, wrapped in rotting felt, a third object that made her go very still: a key. Brass, heavy, ornate past the point of function, its bow shaped like the same crowned star from the seal, its bit cut in a pattern too complex for any door she'd ever picked or locked. It was warm from the water somehow, or her hands were cold. She couldn't tell which.

She opened the oilcloth last. Inside was paper — actual paper, not vellum, not hide, paper, which told her the chest had been sealed against water by people who expected it to survive decades down there — and on the paper, in a cramped, beautiful hand, ink gone brown but legible:

*Aboard the Lament, third hold, vault aft of the ballast. Manifest of consignment, House of Vessany, in trust until the waters recede.*

*Item the first: treasury bonds of the Provisional Government, registered.*

*Item the second: plate and specie, inventoried, see annex.*

*Item the third: the rolls of the displaced. Let no hand be laid upon them but a hand of the nation itself.*

*Keys required: three. Two travel with this manifest. The third remains with the Keeper, who knows his duty.*

Below that, a list of names. Dozens of them. Then dozens more, the page folded twice, and folded again, names running down the columns like water running downhill — family names, holdings, ports of origin, all of it in that same cramped beautiful hand, all of it catalogued with the terrible precision of people who believed someone would eventually read this and act on it.

Orsen sat back on her heels.

The Lament. She'd heard the word before, she realized. Not as a song — as a *forbidden* song, the kind of thing you heard half of once and never asked about. A refugee ship. One of the ships that had gone down in the last days of the war nobody named, when the Vessany fleet had tried to carry their treasury and their records and their people out ahead of the armies, and the river mouth had closed on them like a trap. Officially, the ships had been lost to storm. Unofficially — in the drunk songs, in the ferryman's bitter mutters — there had been no storm that night.

There had been fires on the water, the drunk used to say. Fires from the shore.

Orsen looked at the key in her hand. Brass. Heavy. Cut for a vault, if she had to guess, and she'd spent enough years around mountain caches and smuggler stashes to guess well. Two keys traveled with this manifest, the paper said. She turned the chest over, heart going hard, and found what she was looking for: a second compartment in the base, hidden under a false panel she pried up with the knife.

Empty. Scoured clean. But the lock mechanism was identical to the first compartment's, and the shape of the dust told her something small and brass-shaped had rested there a long time and been removed recently — recently meaning within a year, maybe two. Someone had beaten the floods to this chest. Someone had taken the second key.

Which meant somewhere out there, right now, a person or persons unknown held one key to a Vessany vault, and Orsen Montrose was holding the other, and the manifest said the third remained with a Keeper who knew his duty — except the Keeper was thirty years drowned along with everything else, unless Keepers passed their duties down like eyeglasses and bad knees.

She should throw it back.

That was the sensible move, the survival move, the move the blacklisted woman who trusted nothing but terrain would make. Put the chest back in the water. Break the gut cord. Walk her remaining thirty-two miles and never think about crowned stars or drowned houses again. Corvane's dredges combed this river because Corvane knew — everyone knew, in the way everyone knew things they couldn't say — that the Vessany wreck had never been found. The magistrate had been dragging chains across these waters for fifteen years looking for exactly what she'd pulled out of the shallows before breakfast.

If the dredges found her with it, she wouldn't be blacklisted. She'd be a cautionary tale.

Orsen looked at the manifest again. At the names. Page after page of them, written by hands that had been shaking or steady, she'd never know, hands that had packed this chest in trust *until the waters recede* — and the waters, she thought, looking out over the drowned valley, the fence posts standing shoulder-deep, the dead man's boat planks under her knees — the waters had never receded. Not really. They'd just changed course and kept everything.

"Damn it," she said quietly, to the hawk, to the river, to whichever of her parents was listening.

She rolled the manifest tight, sealed it back in its oilcloth, and tucked it inside her shirt next to her skin, where the important things went. The key she strung on the gut cord — sacrificing her last fishing line to the cause, which felt fitting — and hung around her neck, where it knocked against her sternum with every heartbeat like a small insistent fist.

The chest itself she dragged back to the water's edge. Evidence was evidence, but she wasn't carrying an iron-banded coffin down thirty-two miles of hostile country. Before she slid it under, she took the knife and worked the seal free of the lid — prying the stamped iron plate loose whole — and tucked that into her pack too. Proof, if proof ever became useful. Or currency. With magistrates, those were often the same thing.

The chest sank without ceremony. The river closed over it the way it closed over everything, patient and total, and Orsen stood at the edge of the shelf watching the ripples flatten and thinking about fires on the water, and about a drunk in Lowmark who sang in a dead language, and about how the manifest had said *in trust*, and trust, in her experience, was the heaviest thing a person could carry and the easiest thing to steal.

She was halfway through breaking camp when she saw the sails.

Not sails — smokestacks, three of them, crawling upriver along the far channel where the water ran deepest, trailing barges behind them like leeches. Even at this distance she could read the colors at their mastheads: black and gold. Corvane's colors. Dredge fleets didn't work this stretch until midsummer. Dredge fleets especially didn't work in formation, in convoy, with the low fast silhouette of a patrol skiff cutting ahead of them like a dog ranging before the hunt.

They weren't dredging. She'd watched dredges work. Dredges crawled. These ships were moving with purpose, sweeping the bends in a grid, and the grid was tightening upriver toward her.

Toward the shallows. Toward the old channel. Toward, she understood with a cold settling certainty, the exact stretch of drowned valley where a chest had lain for thirty years undisturbed until a hungry woman with a bent pin had hooked it out of the dark.

The river gives, her father used to say, and the river takes back, and woe to the fool who can't tell which day is which.

Orsen kicked dirt over her fire, shouldered her pack, and started climbing — away from the water, away from the grid, up into the pines where the terrain was hers and hers alone. The key tapped against her breastbone with every stride. Behind her, faint on the wind, she heard the first chain hit the water with a sound like a struck bell.

They were looking for something. And she was carrying it.
