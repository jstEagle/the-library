## 3. The Fleet on the Horizon

Orsen measured the river in bends. That was how she'd always measured anything worth measuring — not in years or coin but in the distance between one place and the next, the honest arithmetic of ground covered. The Rivergate's flooded shallows gave her forty bends between her camp and the drowned orchards downstream, and she'd walked every one of them twice since pulling the chest from the water.

She should have sold it. She knew that. A brass key and a paper manifest were worth nothing to a woman who ate what she could hook from the floodwater and slept under an overturned skiff. But the seal kept catching the light when she turned it over — that stamped crest of the tower and the three waves, Vessany's mark, a nation that had been under the river for twenty years and whose name people still lowered their voices to say, the way you'd lower your voice passing a grave.

So instead of selling it, she'd spent four days learning everything the manifest could teach her, which was almost nothing, because half of it was written in a cipher of tide-tables and the other half in a hand so cramped it might have been written by a drowning man. She'd learned one word clearly: *Lament*. And beneath it, a string of numbers she eventually understood were depths and bearings, and beneath *that*, a line that had made her sit very still in the reeds for a long time:

*Third portion. Hold sealed against all but the keys.*

Keys. Plural.

She had one.

That thought had been sitting in her chest like a swallowed stone for two days when she saw the smoke.

Not camp smoke — she knew camp smoke, thin and domestic, the smell of someone boiling fish. This was funnel smoke, black and oily, rising off the water upstream where no funnel had any business being. She climbed the dead cottonwood at the top of the bank, the one lightning had split into a natural ladder, and looked north along the flooded channel toward the Rivergate itself.

Three ships.

They came downriver in echelon, heavy-bellied salvage hulks with dredging cranes folded along their spines like sleeping insects, and they flew colors she recognized from every wanted notice that had ever cost her a job: the black chevron on gray, the livery of Magistrate Corvane's commission. Between them ran a smaller vessel, low and fast, towing something behind it that caught the morning light in long silver flashes.

Chains. They were dragging chains across the bottom.

Orsen watched them work with the flat attention she gave to weather. The lead ship would anchor at a bend, swing its crane out, and pay chain down until it dragged the riverbed in a great arc, plowing up silt and sunken timber and whatever else the flood had buried. Then it would weigh anchor, move down a quarter mile, and do it again. Methodical. Patient. Not searching — *sweeping*. You swept a river when you weren't looking for a thing anymore. You swept it when you were looking for anyone who might have taken a thing.

She stayed in the tree until the ships passed out of sight behind the drowned mill at Bend Eleven, and then she climbed down faster than she'd climbed up, went back to her camp, and did the thing she'd been avoiding for four days.

She opened the chest again and read the manifest like a woman reading her own death warrant.

* * *

The trouble with knowing terrain as well as Orsen knew it was that you also knew exactly how exposed you were. Her camp under the overturned skiff sat in a cutbank that hid a fire from the water but not from the high ground. The flooded shallows offered cover to a wading man but none to a boat. Every route south toward Lowmark crossed open water somewhere, and open water meant masts on the horizon and lantern-light at night and chains dragging the bottom of every bend between here and the sea.

She broke camp in eleven minutes. It was a discipline older than her blacklisting: never own more than you can abandon, never sleep anywhere you can't leave blind. She rolled the chest into its oilcloth, wrapped the oilcloth in her spare shirt, and lashed the whole bundle inside her pack where it rode against her spine like a second set of ribs. The brass key she moved to a cord around her neck, under her clothes, where it lay against her sternum, small and warm and heavier than any metal had a right to be.

Then she went south, along the high ground, through the drowned orchard where the apple trees stood to their shoulders in green water, still fruiting, absurdly, their apples ripening over their own reflections.

She made six miles before she found the runner.

He came out of the reeds at Bend Fourteen — or rather his boat did, first, a long shallow river-runner with its bow stove in, drifting sideways with the current, and then him, clinging to the gunwale with both hands, half in the water, moving with the slow mechanical determination of a man who had decided he was not going to die today and was prepared to be extremely inconvenient about it.

Orsen waded out and took his collar before the current could argue further. He was young — younger than her, anyway, twenty-five maybe, with the ropey forearms of someone who poled for a living and a wound in his side that had soaked his shirt black from hip to armpit. She got him onto the bank, into the sun, and he looked up at her with the unfocused gratitude of the badly hurt.

"Water," he said.

She gave him water. She considered giving him nothing else, because a dying stranger on the bank was a complication and complications were miles, and she had learned a long time ago that the only way to keep your count clean was to refuse every debt before it was offered. But his eyes had cleared enough to focus on her face, and then past her shoulder, upstream, and whatever he saw there made him grab her wrist with a grip far stronger than his body had any right to contain.

"They're coming," he said. "You have to — you can't be on the water."

"I'm not on the water."

"You will be." He coughed, and the cough cost him; she watched the blood come up with it, dark at the corner of his mouth. "Everyone will be. They're sweeping every bend. Chain-dragging." His laugh was a wet, terrible sound. "You know what a chain does to a river-runner hull? Goes through it like a knife through bread. They don't even stop. They just log it."

"Log what?"

"The kill." He said it simply, the way you'd say *the rain* or *the price of salt*. "Every hull they break, they log it. Boat number, last port if they can read the paint. Corvane's commission pays by the hull this season. There's a bonus sheet." He tried to sit up and failed, and Orsen, against every instinct she owned, put her hand behind his back and helped him.

"Why?" she asked. "Why is a magistrate's fleet breaking fishing boats?"

"Not fishing boats." His eyes found hers and held there, and she saw the moment the pain in them sharpened into something else — urgency, and fear, and the particular desperation of a man who has information and knows he won't survive long enough to spend it twice. "Anyone who's touched them. The keys."

The word went through her like cold water through a boot.

"What keys?"

"Vessany." He said the name carefully, reverently, the way the old ferry pilots used to say it before the war, before the drowning, before saying it became dangerous. "The tide-keys. Three of them. My cousin ran cargo out of Lowmark — he heard it from a harbor clerk who heard it from a fleet purser. Corvane's been hunting them since spring. Anyone found with Vessany marks, Vessany papers, Vessany gold—" He stopped. Swallowed. "They don't arrest you. Arrest makes paperwork. They just sink you and log the hull."

Orsen was aware, suddenly and completely, of the weight against her sternum. Of the oilcloth bundle in her pack. Of the fact that she had spent four days sitting in the reeds studying a document that would get her sunk and logged, and that she had done it within thirty miles of a fleet doing exactly this.

"How many ships?" she asked, and her voice came out level, guide-calm, the voice she used above the icefall. She was proud of that, later. Not much else about that day.

"Seven that my cousin counted. More coming down from the Gate. They're going to sweep the whole valley to the sea." He gripped her arm again. "Listen. Listen. The clerk said the manifest names the wreck. If you ever — if anyone ever finds a manifest, they'll trace the bearing. They have the charts. Corvane bought the harbor charts off the admiralty ten years ago, whole Vessany survey, every sounding—"

"I know the name of the wreck," Orsen said quietly.

The runner stared at her. And she watched him understand, watch the arithmetic move behind his eyes — the stranger alone on the bank, the pack, the questions, the calm — and she watched him decide, with the last of his strength, to trust her anyway. People did that. It was the single most baffling habit of her species, and it had gotten her blacklisted once already, though that story was not his to hear.

"Lament," he whispered.

"You know it?"

"Everybody from the old runs knows the name. Refugee ship. Last one out of Vessany before the gates closed." His breath was going short now; she could hear the rattle in it. "It never reached the sea. Went down in the Silt Gates country with — with everything. With everyone who paid to get out." His hand slid off her arm. "My grandmother paid. We never learned which ship."

Orsen said nothing. There was nothing to say that wasn't either a lie or a promise, and she had given up both trades years ago.

The runner's gaze drifted upstream again, to where the funnel smoke had begun to show above the drowned treeline, three bends north and closing. "They'll reach Bend Twelve by dark," he said. "Maybe sooner. They put outriders on the banks — riders with glasses, walking the levees. You can't outrun them on foot and you can't hide on the water." He fumbled at his neck and pulled free a cord, a small carved wooden whistle shaped like a heron, and pressed it into her palm with fingers that were already cooling. "Maren Oduya. Ferrywoman. Runs the timber channels below the falls. Tell her Pell sent you. Tell her—" A cough took him, longer this time. When it released, he was quieter, and his eyes had gone somewhere above the trees. "Tell her the river remembers who paid the toll."

Then, because the world is efficient about these things, he died — not dramatically, not with any last speech, just a slowing, like a boat losing headway, and then stillness. Orsen sat beside him in the sun for a while, because leaving a body unattended in flood country meant the turtles, and whatever else she was, she wasn't that.

She took his boat pole. She left the whistle in his hand, because it was his, and because she intended to use his name instead.

* * *

She walked south through the afternoon with the funnel smoke growing behind her and the arithmetic running in her head the way it always ran, mile by mile, option by option, cold and clean.

Seven ships, maybe more. Riders on the levees. Chains on every bend. South to Lowmark: forty miles of open floodwater with no cover, or ninety miles of high ground through the hills where the riders' glasses would find her crossing every saddle. Stay: die at Bend Twelve by dark. The math had no good answer, which meant she'd been asking the wrong question.

The right question was: what did she have that they didn't?

She had the key. She had the manifest. She had, as of this morning, a name — Maren Oduya — and a dying man's certainty that the name opened doors. What she didn't have was a boat, a chart, or luck, and the manifest's six-tide window was ticking somewhere in those cramped cipher lines whether she understood it yet or not.

At dusk she climbed the last ridge above the timber channels below the falls, and looked back once.

The fleet had reached Bend Twelve. She could see the lead ship's lanterns swinging at anchor, and the smaller fast vessel working the bank with a boarding party, and — briefly, in the last light — a figure on the lead ship's deck wearing gray and black, standing very still, looking south. Looking, she understood with a certainty that settled into her bones like the key against her sternum, exactly the way a hunter looks when the trail has finally gone warm.

Somewhere downriver, in the drowned timber, a ferrywoman was poling a leaky barge through channels no chart admitted existed.

Orsen turned south, tightened the pack straps, and started down into the dark, measuring the miles.

There were fewer of them than she needed. For the first time in her life, she wished there were years instead.
