## 4. Six Tides

Orsen counted her assets the way she counted switchbacks on a climb: without sentiment, without hope, without lying to herself.

One chest, stolen from the flood. One brass key, heavy as a sin, stamped with a seal she'd only ever seen on gravestones. One sheet of paper that might get her killed in every town between here and the sea.

She sat in the hollow beneath a leaning cedar on the ridge above the shallows, the manifest spread across her knees, and made herself read it a third time. Reading it twice had been enough to scare her. The third time was for the details, and the details were where fear either got managed or got you.

The paper was Vessany work — she could see that now that she knew to look. The watermark was a river running through a mountain, faint as breath, visible only when she held it against the last of the daylight. The ink had gone brown with years underwater, but the hand that had written it had been steady, professional, hurried in the way of a clerk who knew the ship was sinking when he wrote it.

*Manifest of the Lament, out of Vessany-harbor, third day of the ebb-month.*

*Cargo: one (1) sealed vault-chest, tide-locked, requiring three (3) keys of brass for opening. Keys distributed per the Charter. This document to travel with the first key.*

*Destination: the Silt Gates, there to await the falling of six tides, at which window the channel behind the gates may be run and the vault recovered by the lawful bearers.*

*Signed under seal, in the name of the drowned and the scattered.*

The first key was in her pocket. The second and third were somewhere in the world, and a salvage fleet was already dragging the river for all of them.

Orsen folded the manifest along its old creases and sat with it a while, listening to the river work the shallows below. She had spent eleven years learning to read water — where it ran, where it lied, where it kept its secrets. She knew what the Silt Gates were. Every river-runner between the high passes and Lowmark knew, the way children know about the thing under the bed.

The Silt Gates were a canyon pinch forty miles downriver, where the old Vessany engineers had built a pair of stone towers and hung a chain between them, back when Vessany was a nation instead of a word. The chain had rusted through a century ago. The towers still stood. And between them, the river dropped its silt every year when the flood season broke — dropped it in a slow brown curtain that settled over everything behind the gates like a lid coming down on a box.

For most of the year, the Lament was buried. Whatever channel the old engineers had cut behind the gates was deep enough to hide a ship, but the silt didn't care about depth. It filled in. It always filled in.

Except once a year. Except when the river dropped after the flood crests passed, and the water running through the gates cut its own channel clean again, scouring the silt back out the way a tongue finds the gap where a tooth used to be. The runners called it the six-tide window. Six tides between the flood's last crest and the summer low, when the gates stood open and the channel behind them ran clear enough — clear enough, if you believed the stories — to reach what the water had kept.

Orsen had never believed the stories. She'd believed the water. And the water was telling her, right now, in the voice it had used all week — that low, gravelly, letting-go sound of a river in retreat — that the flood was breaking early this year.

She did the arithmetic and didn't like any of the answers.

The last crest had passed the high country three days ago. The river would drop fast now, fast the way snowmelt rivers drop when the heat comes, and the first clean tide through the gates would fall tomorrow. Tomorrow, or the day after at the outside. Then five more tides, each one lower, each one cutting the channel a little deeper and then a little shallower, until the sixth tide passed and the silt began its slow work of forgetting again.

Six tides. Maybe seven days, if the weather held. Maybe five, if it didn't.

And the fleet was already on the water.

She'd seen their masts that morning from the ridge — three hulls upriver, riding low with the weight of their chains, working the bends methodical as dredgers, which was exactly what they were. Dredging for her. For the chest. For whatever else the drowned nation had left lying in its own grave. Magistrate Corvane's colors at the mastheads, blue and iron-gray, the colors every orphan and debtor and blacklisted guide in the valley knew to walk away from.

They'd reach the gates before the second tide. Maybe before the first, if they pushed.

Orsen looked at the brass key in her palm and felt the cold, clean shape of the problem settle over her, and underneath the cold, the fear, and underneath the fear, something she refused to name because naming it would make it hope.

She needed three things. She said them aloud to the empty hollow, because saying them made them real and real things could be solved.

"A boat. A chart. And luck."

* * *

The boat first, because the boat was the only one she had any control over.

There was no boat. That was the honest size of it. She had walked into the flood season with a client's stolen fee in her boot and a pack on her back, and packs didn't float forty miles of floodwater. The fishing skiff she'd been using in the shallows was a rotten-hulled little tub that would swamp in the first real current, and the current behind the Silt Gates, in the window, would be very real indeed.

Which meant Lowmark. Lowmark meant people, and people meant the chest had to be hidden, and the chest had to be hidden well, because Orsen Montrose's name was already on enough lists and this would put her on the last one.

She spent the last hour of light on that. The hollow under the cedar was dry and invisible from the water, and the ground beneath the roots was soft enough to dig. She dug down two feet, wrapped the chest in her oilcloth, and buried it with the manifest inside — but not the key. The key stayed on her body, in a leather sheath against her ribs, because the manifest was paper and paper could be replaced by memory, but the key was the key, and keys didn't have twins.

She marked the spot the way she marked everything: with terrain, not trinkets. The cedar leaned at a specific angle over a specific split boulder, and the boulder had a specific scar of white quartz, and three of those things together made a location that no flood and no stranger would ever confuse. She could find it blind. She could find it in the dark. She could find it, she told herself firmly, even scared.

Then she shouldered her pack and started down toward the river road and Lowmark, walking fast, because the clock was already running and it didn't care that she hadn't slept.

* * *

The chart was the harder problem, and the harder problem had a name, and the name was Tam Vireo.

Orsen had never met the man. She knew of him the way everyone on the river knew of him: the cartographer who'd gone to prison for forging Vessany charts, which was a strange crime to commit and a stranger one to punish, until you understood that the charts weren't forgeries at all. They were real. Drawn from the memories of Vessany survivors, charts of a drowned country's waterways, and the magistrate's court had called them forgeries because a real chart of Vessany was a thing the magistrate could not permit to exist.

He'd done his years and come out, and now he drank in Lowmark and drew harbor charts for smugglers, and if anyone alive knew what the channel behind the Silt Gates looked like — the real channel, the engineered one, the one the silt forgot every summer — it was him.

She'd need him. She hated needing anyone. It was a flaw in her character she'd been meaning to correct for eleven years, and the river had just made the correction for her.

* * *

She came down to Lowmark after dark, when the town's lamps made a smudge of orange against the river fog, and stood at the edge of the mudflats doing the last of the arithmetic.

The boat: findable in Lowmark, for a price. The chart: findable in Lowmark, for a worse price. The luck: not for sale anywhere, which meant she'd have to steal it.

And behind her, upriver, the fleet's lanterns were moving through the dark like a slow procession of drowned men walking, and tomorrow the first tide would fall, and the window would open, and it would close again whether she was through the gates or not.

The river didn't wait. That was the first thing it had ever taught her, and the only lesson it had ever repeated.

Orsen Montrose pulled her collar up, stepped onto the mudflats, and went to find a ferrywoman who owed the river a living and a smuggler's town that owed her nothing at all.

The first thing she heard in Lowmark was a rumor, passed from a dockhand to a barkeep in the fog-thick air outside the ferry tavern: the fleet had posted a bounty that morning. Not for a chest. Not for a chart.

For a woman, traveling alone, last seen in the high passes.

Orsen kept walking. The clock was the antagonist now, and it had a head start.
