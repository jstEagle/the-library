## 5. The Ferrywoman's Price

The ferry was moored at a deadfall two miles downstream of the burned homestead, and Orsen smelled it before she saw it: pitch smoke, wet hemp, and something else underneath — river-mud and dried herbs, the smell of a place where someone lived alone on purpose.

She approved of that. She understood it.

The barge sat low in the brown water, forty feet of patched hull and mismatched planking, its single mast bare, its deck heaped with netting, crates, and coils of rope gone green with age. A lantern hung from the stern post, unlit in the afternoon light. Painted on the bow, half flaked away, was a name: *Kindly*.

Orsen stood at the edge of the willows for a long moment, reading the boat the way she'd read a mountain face — looking for the route up it, and for what would kill her if she chose wrong.

The hull leaked. She could see it in the way the barge rode: too deep at the stern, a bilge pump's handle sticking up through the deck boards like a periscope. The rigging was sound but old. The steering oar was new. That interested her. Everything else about the vessel said neglect; the oar said care. Someone had spent money where it mattered.

"Either come aboard or stop breathing at my boat," said a voice from under the stern awning. "You've been standing there ten minutes and the fish can feel you thinking."

Orsen stepped out of the willows with her hands loose at her sides.

The woman who emerged from under the awning was sixty, maybe older, built like a dock piling, with grey hair cut short as a hedge and eyes the flat grey-green of the river itself. She wore a man's oilskin coat over a dress that might once have been blue, and around her neck, on a cord, hung a cluster of small objects Orsen couldn't catalogue from this distance — bones, knots, a coin, a bird's skull. River-witch regalia, or just the junk a solitary woman collected. On the flooded river, the difference didn't matter much.

"You're Maren," Orsen said.

"I'm whoever you need me to be, and I cost extra for that." The woman looked her over without hurry. "Guide. High-country boots, river mud to the knee. You came down out of the passes fast, and you came down scared, though you're doing your best not to show it." She spat over the rail, an easy, practiced motion. "Scared people pay better. Come aboard."

* * *

The cabin was smaller than some closets Orsen had slept in, and it smelled of tar, tea, and dried eel. Maren poured two cups without asking and set one down across the table from her own, which meant sitting, which meant negotiation, which meant Orsen had already lost ground by accepting the seat.

She drank anyway. It was good tea, bitter and hot, and she'd had nothing since dawn.

"So," Maren said. "You want passage downriver. Through the timber."

"The flooded timber," Orsen said. "Past Lowmark. Past the Silt Gates."

Maren's eyebrows went up a fraction, then settled, deliberate as everything else she did. "That's not passage. That's a funeral with scenery. The whole valley's underwater six fathoms deep in the old pine stands, and every drowned tree's got a spear waiting under the surface. Fleet patrols dragging chains at every bend. And you want to go past the Silt Gates." She turned her cup slowly on the table. "Nobody goes past the Silt Gates during flood season except corpses going the wrong direction."

"I'll pay."

"Everyone says that first." Maren leaned back. "Here's how I work, guide. I don't take coin for suicide runs. Coin spends the same whether I'm alive or dead, and I plan on being alive a while yet. So before we talk price, you tell me what's so precious down there that you'd float a leaky barge through a drowned forest with Corvane's fleet combing the water."

Orsen weighed it. Tell her nothing and get nothing. Tell her everything and hand a stranger a knife with her own name on the handle.

Somewhere between those two points was a deal.

"There's a wreck," she said. "Down behind the gates. I have a manifest that says what's in her hold."

"A manifest." Maren tasted the word. "Stamped?"

Orsen reached into her coat and set the brass tide-key on the table between them, keeping her palm loosely over it. Not hiding it. Showing it the way you'd show a weapon — enough to make the point, not enough to lose it.

Maren went very still.

It lasted three seconds, maybe four, but Orsen had spent years reading clients' faces for the moment fear or greed took over, and she watched both cross the ferrywoman's face in sequence — greed first, quick and bright, then fear, slower and deeper, settling into something that looked almost like grief.

"Vessany," Maren said quietly.

"You know the seal."

"Everyone my age knows the seal." Maren didn't reach for the key. Orsen noted that. Greedy people reached. "I ferried refugees out of Vessany the summer they burned it. Eleven crossings. I got maybe sixty souls across, and I remember every one of their faces, and I remember the ones I couldn't wait for because the patrol boats were coming." She looked up, and her eyes were the river again — flat, cold, deep enough to drown in. "So don't sit at my table and dangle that key like bait, girl. Tell me plainly what you're after, or take your tea and go."

Plainly, then. Orsen laid it out: the chest, the manifest, the Lament, the six-tide window, the salvage fleet flying Corvane's colors, the dying runner's warning. She left out exactly one thing — that the chest was currently wrapped in oilcloth and buried under stones in a willow stand two miles back, and that she intended to keep its location her own business until she trusted someone with it, which might be never.

Maren listened without interrupting, which was rarer than honesty on this river.

When Orsen finished, the witch was quiet a long moment. Then she laughed — a short, harsh bark with no humor in it.

"The Lament," she said. "Behind the Silt Gates. During the drop." She shook her head. "Do you know why they call me a river-witch? Because it's easier than calling me what I am, which is the only pilot alive who's taken a boat through the Gates on a falling tide and lived to drink about it. Once. Twenty years ago, running medicine past a garrison that wanted it burned. I lost half my hull and all my hair's color." She tapped the table. "And you walk into my camp and ask me to do it again. For a stranger. For treasure."

"Not for treasure."

"No?"

"For what's in the hold besides treasure." Orsen held her gaze. "A manifest of names. Families. People Corvane robbed when he drowned their country. I don't know yet what I'm going to do with it. But I know it isn't his, and I know it isn't mine, and I know the only way it ends up anywhere good is if somebody carries it there."

Something shifted in Maren's face — small, quickly controlled, but real. Orsen recognized it because she felt the same shift in herself, and distrusted it equally. Two solitaries, each hearing something in the other's voice that sounded dangerously like a reason to trust.

"Cards," Maren said abruptly.

"What?"

"You want to know if you can trust me, and I want to know if you're worth drowning for. Cards settle it faster than talk." She produced a deck from a drawer, worn soft as cloth, and began to shuffle with a river-hand's economy. "Cutthroat. Loser answers questions, winner asks them. No lying — the river hears lying and holds grudges. First to five points picks the terms of passage."

"That's not a negotiation."

"That's exactly what a negotiation is, everywhere I've ever seen one." She set the deck down for the cut. "Well, guide? You came to me. Play or walk."

Orsen looked at the cards. Looked at the woman. Outside, the river moved past the hull, patient and enormous, carrying everything toward the sea and giving nothing back — except, sometimes, things people thought were gone forever.

She cut the deck.

* * *

She lost the first hand badly, and paid for it with her name and history. Maren listened to the story of the blacklisting with her chin on her fist, and when Orsen finished — the client, the false accusation, the name struck from the guides' rolls — the witch said only, "Corvane's magistracy did that?" and when Orsen nodded, wrote something invisible on the air between them, filing it away.

Orsen won the second hand and asked her question carefully: "Why does a woman who hates the fleet live alone on a boat in the middle of its territory?"

"Because boats move and houses burn," Maren said, and won the third hand before Orsen could follow up.

The third answer cost Orsen more than she expected to give. "What are you afraid of?" Maren asked, and Orsen stared at the cards a long time before answering, because the honest answer was *nothing*, and that was a lie, and the river heard lies.

"Losing count," she said finally. "I measure everything in miles. Miles walked, miles run, miles between me and whatever's chasing me. Miles are safe. They end. I'm afraid of the day I run out of things to measure and have to just... be somewhere, with someone, without a number for it."

Maren studied her for a moment with an expression Orsen couldn't read at all.

"That's the saddest thing anyone's ever told me at this table," she said, "and I once played cards with a man who ate his own boot on a bet." She dealt. "Your deal, guide. And for the record — I'm not going to ask what's buried in the willows two miles upstream. But I know it is, because you keep glancing north every fourth breath, and nobody glances at empty trees."

Orsen kept her face still. Inside, something cold turned over. *Handle-first,* she thought. *This is what trust looks like when it's offered handle-first — you notice the blade.*

The fourth hand went to Maren on a draw that should have been impossible, and Orsen called it — not cheating, exactly, but luck too clean — and Maren grinned for the first time, showing a chipped tooth, and said, "Now you're learning the river. The river cheats. I just pass it along."

Fifth hand. Winner took terms.

Orsen played it tight and mean and won by two points, and set her cards down with more satisfaction than she'd felt at anything in months.

"Terms," Maren said, leaning back. "Spend them."

"Passage to the Silt Gates and through them, on the falling tides, for me and whatever cargo I bring aboard. In exchange—" Orsen paused. This was the part she'd been building toward, the part that made her stomach tighten, because it meant giving instead of taking, and giving was a muscle she'd let atrophy. "In exchange, you get passage rights to the wreck. Whatever the Lament's hold is worth, you claim a share equal to mine. Before we ever see it. Written down, witnessed, binding."

Maren's eyebrows rose — genuinely, this time, no performance in it.

"That's a fool's offer," she said. "You don't even know what's down there. Could be silt and skeletons."

"It could be. But if it isn't, I'd rather split it with someone who knows these waters than keep all of it and die alone trying to carry it." Orsen shrugged, as if the logic were simple, as if it hadn't cost her anything to say. "Besides. You ferried sixty Vessany souls across a burning river. If there's a manifest of names in that hold, you've earned a look at it more than I have."

For a moment the ferrywoman said nothing at all. The river slapped against the hull. Somewhere in the flooded timber, a heron cried out, raw and lonely.

Then Maren stuck out her hand across the table — broad, scarred, rope-callused — and Orsen took it.

"Deal's made," Maren said. "Witnessed by the river, which holds grudges against backsliders, so mind yourself." She released the grip and stood, joints cracking like green kindling. "We sail on tomorrow's first ebb. Tonight you help me patch the starboard seam, because I'll be damned if I sink in the Gates with your fool treasure aboard." She paused at the cabin door, half-turned. "One more thing, guide. Word came downriver yesterday with a fish-buyer. There's a man in Lowmark they say can forge any chart ever drawn — Tam Vireo, they call him. Cartographer. Did time for making Vessany maps that were too good."

Orsen went still. "Too good?"

"Charts of places no living surveyor has been. Drawn like he'd memorized them." Maren's mouth twisted. "Like somebody who'd drowned taught him. If you're hunting a wreck behind the Silt Gates, you'll want a chart of waters that aren't on any map Corvane allows to exist." She jerked her chin toward the north, toward the willows and the buried chest. "Get your cargo. We leave at first ebb, and we make Lowmark by dark — because starting tomorrow, girl, the whole river starts hunting us, and I'd rather be moving when it does."

Orsen climbed out onto the deadfall and stood a moment in the failing light, looking north through the drowned trees.

Two miles away, under stones and oilcloth, a brass key waited with a nation's seal on it. Ahead lay a smuggler's town, a forged map, a garrisoned gate, and a canyon full of dead men's gold — and now, strangely, a leaky barge named *Kindly* and a woman who had heard her worst confession over a card game and asked for nothing but a fair share.

It wasn't trust. Not yet. Trust was a thing Orsen would believe in again when she saw it survive its first storm.

But it was a crack in the wall. And walls, she knew from mountains, always broke along their cracks.

She turned north and started walking, measuring the miles without meaning to — and for the first time she could remember, wondering what it would be like to stop.
