## 6. A Mapmaker's Ghosts

The cell in Lowmark had a window the width of a hand, and Tam Vireo had spent four years learning everything it could tell him.

It faced east. In the first hour after dawn, light came down the river gorge and struck the wall opposite, which meant the sun was clearing the ridge above Vessany Reach — which meant, if you were foolish enough to keep a map of that drowned country in your head, as he was, that you could fix your position to within half a mile. He did it every morning. It was the only surveying left to him, and he refused to let even a prison take it.

Below the window, the smugglers' town went about its business. He heard the chain-gate groan at low tide. He heard the fish-wives arguing over eel prices. He heard, twice a week, the jailer's boots on the stairs, and once — only once, three years ago — he had heard a woman's voice in the yard below saying words he still carried like a stone in his boot: *That's him. That's the man who drew us drowning.*

He had closed his eyes and agreed with her.

Now the boots on the stairs were wrong. Too quick, too light, and they stopped at his door instead of passing it.

"Vireo." The jailer's face appeared through the grate, gray with morning and bad temper. "You've got a visitor."

"I've had no visitors in four years."

"You've got one now." A pause, and something almost like pity crossed the man's face. "She's armed."

* * *

Orsen Montrose was not what he expected, though he couldn't have said what he'd expected instead. Tall, weathered, with the particular stillness of someone who spent her life reading ground — she stood in the visiting room the way he imagined a mountain goat stood on a ledge, weight settled, ready to move in any direction at once. She set a wrapped bundle on the table between them and did not sit.

"Tam Vireo," she said. Not a question.

"That depends who's asking, and what the answer's worth to them."

"A woman who hooks a chest out of the Rivergate floodwater."

The room went very quiet for Tam. Four years, and the word *chest* still landed in him like a dropped anchor. "That's a strange thing to say to a locked-up man."

"It's a true thing. I'm told you're the person to ask about true things." She unwrapped the bundle.

He knew before the cloth fell away. He knew by the shape of it under the wrapping, by the way she held it — carefully, but not reverently enough, not nearly — and when the brass caught the lamplight and the seal of Vessany looked up at him from the lid, Tam Vireo put both hands flat on the table because otherwise they would have shaken.

"Where," he said, and his voice cracked on the second word, and he hated himself for it, and kept going anyway. "Where did you pull this from?"

"Flooded shallows below the high passes. The river gave it back."

"The river doesn't give things back." He was leaning over the chest now, tracing the seal with one finger, not quite touching. "The river keeps. That's the whole lesson of Vessany. Everything it took in the war, it keeps — the ships, the treasury, the people. It only ever gives back proof." He looked up at her. "You're holding proof."

"I'm holding trouble. Salvage fleet's on the river flying Corvane's colors, dragging chains across every bend. A runner told me they're hunting keys. Plural." Her eyes didn't leave his face. "I'm told you know where the second one waits."

"Who told you that?"

"A dying man. And before him, a rumor in every river town between here and the Silt Gates: there's a mapmaker in Lowmark prison who forged Vessany charts so well the magistrate burned him for it. Charts nobody living should have been able to draw."

"They weren't forgeries." The words came out of Tam flat and hard, worn smooth by four years of saying them to walls. "That's the part nobody believes. I never forged anything in my life until they made me forge my own confession."

Orsen sat down across from him. Slowly, deliberately, the way you'd settle near a spooked animal. "Then tell me what you did."

* * *

What he had done began, as most ruin does, with an honest job.

"I was a survey cartographer. River charts, coastal soundings. Good work, clean work. Then the war ended the way wars end for the losers — badly and quietly — and Vessany stopped existing on paper. Officially it had never existed. The atlas houses pulled its plates. The harbormasters struck its lights. You couldn't buy a chart of the Vessany coast, because officially there was nothing there to chart." He turned the tide-key over in his mind as he spoke; he hadn't seen it yet, wouldn't let himself touch it, but he could feel it on the table between them like heat from a stove. "And then survivors started finding me."

"Survivors of what? Nobody names the war."

"Nobody names it because naming it means admitting what happened." His hands moved now, drawing on the scarred tabletop without ink, the old habit. "Corvane didn't just beat Vessany. He drowned it. The refugee fleet — eleven ships, maybe twelve, loaded with families and whatever gold they'd salvaged from their own banks — went down in the Reach in a single night, in fair weather. Every survivor I ever met said the same thing. No storm. Explosions below the waterline. Ships that had been seaworthy that morning, sunk by their own escort." He looked up. "Your salvage fleet isn't looking for treasure, Montrose. It's looking for loose ends."

"And the survivors found you because—"

"Because a mapmaker is the last person in the world who remembers a place after everyone else agrees to forget it." He laughed, and there was no humor in it. "They came to me in ones and twos. An old pilot who'd sounded the Reach for forty years and could recite its depths like psalms. A harbor girl who'd memorized the channel markers. A customs clerk with the shipping ledgers half copied into his head. They wanted me to draw it. All of it. The coast, the channels, the wrecks — especially the wrecks. Because the ships went down carrying more than people, and the people who lived wanted what was theirs back."

"So you drew it."

"So I drew it. Eleven sheets. The truest work of my life and the last honest thing I'll ever be accused of." He spread his hands. "Corvane's men took them off a courier at the Lowmark chain. I was arrested within the month. Forging charts of a nation that never existed, seditious cartography, aiding enemies of the realm — they needed a charge that would hold in court, and 'remembering' wasn't one. So they wrote forgery into my confession and held the pen while I signed."

"What happened to the survivors who dictated them?"

"Some paid me and vanished inland. Some—" He stopped. The woman's voice in the yard, three years ago. *That's the man who drew us drowning.* "Some are dead. The fleet has been dragging the Reach for years, Montrose. Every time they raise a wreck, they burn whatever's aboard that isn't gold. Papers. Possessions. Names. Corvane isn't salvaging a treasury. He's erasing a crime scene, one ship at a time."

Orsen was quiet for a long moment. When she spoke again, her voice had changed — lower, stripped of the transactional crispness she'd walked in with. "The manifest in this chest names a wreck called the Lament. Third of the treasury ships, by the count. Behind the Silt Gates, reachable in a six-tide window. First tide falls tomorrow night."

Tam felt the number land in his chest like a fist. Six tides. He had spent four years measuring time in dawns through a hand-width window, and now the whole shape of his life had compressed into six pulls of the moon.

"The Lament," he said. "I know the name. The old pilot — Brannic, his name was Brannic — he served on her before the war, ferry run up the Vessa. He drew her hull lines for me from memory. Double-keeled, built to take cargo up shallow water." He closed his eyes and the chart assembled itself behind his eyelids, sheet seven of eleven, the Reach in cross-section, every sounding Brannic had poured into him over one feverish winter. "She didn't sink in open water. She ran for the Silt Gates trying to shelter and went down in the narrows behind them, wedged in the canyon where the current drops its silt. That's why the fleet's never raised her. She's buried too deep for chains, and the gates are garrisoned."

"But she's reachable."

"When the river drops. Six tides a year, the snowmelt fails and the water falls off the canyon floor like a held breath let go. Brannic called it the river's grief — it mourns what it swallowed, once a year, and gives you a look at it." He opened his eyes. "Yes. She's reachable. If you have a boat that can survive the approach, papers that can survive the garrison, and a pilot mad enough to run flooded timber at night."

"I have two of those," Orsen said. "A ferrywoman named Maren holds the first. I'm working on the third."

"And the papers?" Tam leaned back. Something in his chest had shifted during the conversation, some long-seated load redistributing itself, and he distrusted the feeling profoundly. Hope was a current, and currents drowned more travelers than rocks did. "Because the garrison at the Silt Gates doesn't wave through boats with honest faces. They want fleet seals, fleet manifests, fleet authority. Which brings us to the question you actually rowed here to ask."

"Which is?"

"Whether the forger will forge again." He held her gaze. "Say it plainly. You need a man who can make false documents good enough to pass a Corvane garrison, and I'm the only one in this prison with the hands for it. So ask."

* * *

She asked. Of course she asked. But the asking wasn't what undid him.

What undid him was the key.

She slid it across the table before she left — brass, heavy, its bow cut in the shape of a Vessany river-lamp, the old lamp they used to hang at the harbor mouth to call the ferries home. Tam had drawn that lamp a hundred times. Survivors had wept over his drawings of it. And now here it was, real, warm from her pocket, sitting under his fingers for the first time since the world had agreed it never existed.

"She said you'd know what it opens," Orsen said. "The old woman in the village. She said the keys were cut in threes, and each one answers for a different door, and the doors don't matter unless the hands turning them are honest." She stood. "I don't know if your hands are honest, Vireo. I know you went to prison for remembering, and I know the difference between a forger and a man they called one. But I've got six tides, a leaky barge, a fleet on my wake, and no time to vet anybody. So I'm offering you the worst deal of your life: come with me, forge what needs forging, guide me to the Lament — and if we live, you get to stand in front of whatever's in that hold and decide what it's for."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I walk out, and you go back to counting dawns through a window, and the Lament stays in the dark until Corvane's divers crack her hold and burn the last of the names." She shouldered her pack. "I won't pretend that's a threat. It's just what happens."

He looked at the key. He thought about Brannic, who had died two winters after the war, coughing blood in a rented room, still dictating soundings with his last breath because *someone has to remember where they lie, boy, someone has to mark them proper.* He thought about the woman in the yard. He thought about eleven sheets of the truest work of his life, confiscated and probably burned, and how a man could spend four years telling himself he'd been punished for honesty and still, deep down, believe he deserved the cell.

"I drew them drowning," he said quietly. "Did you know that? That's what the survivors called it, afterward, the ones who saw the charts. The man who drew us drowning. Because I mapped the wrecks, and mapping a grave makes it real, and making it real made it mine somehow. I've had four years to argue with that." He picked up the key. It was heavier than it looked. Everything true was. "I'm done drawing graves, Montrose. Take me with you and I'll draw you a way *out* of one. Papers, seals, manifests — whatever the garrison wants to see, I'll make them see it. And when we reach the Lament, I want the manifests. Whatever passenger lists, whatever records are in that hold. The names come home before the gold does. That's my price."

Orsen studied him for a long moment, and he watched her do the arithmetic she must do with everyone — risk against use, trust against necessity — and he found he didn't resent it. It was honest arithmetic. He'd had enough of dishonest sums to last a life.

"Done," she said. "Can you be over the wall tonight?"

"I can be over the wall whenever the jailer's drunk, which is Thursdays." He paused. "Today's Thursday."

"Then tonight." She was already moving toward the door, that mountain-goat readiness in every line of her, and something in Tam ached at the sight of it — motion, purpose, a door opening after four years of walls. At the threshold she stopped. "One question. The charts. Sheet seven, the Reach. Is the Lament marked on it?"

"Every wreck was marked. Crosses, with the names beside them where I knew them." He smiled, and it hurt his face, the first smile in longer than he could measure. "Brannic made me promise. *Mark them proper, boy.*"

"Then you're worth the worst deal of your life," Orsen Montrose said, and was gone.

* * *

The rest of the day passed the way the last four years had passed, except that now every hour had a direction. Tam sat on his cot with the key hidden in the seam of his mattress and rehearsed, in his head, the documents he would need: a fleet salvage manifest under a plausible captain's name, transit writs bearing the Silt Gate garrison's countersign format, a cargo declaration boring enough that no bored officer would read past the second line. Forgery, he had learned in prison, was mostly empathy — you didn't draw what was true, you drew what a tired man expected to see, and you gave his expectations nowhere to snag.

Toward evening, sounds drifted up from the yard that didn't fit the town's rhythm. Shouting. Hoofbeats, more than one horse. The chain-gate being cranked open out of schedule.

Tam went to his window. Below, torchlight swung across the mud of the yard, and riders were coming through the gate at speed — dark cloaks, wet from the river road, moving with the particular urgency of men carrying news that outranked them. One of them shouted something up at the gatehouse, and the wind tore most of it away, but not all.

Tam caught three words. *Fleet.* *Warrant.* And a name.

Not his name. He'd have known his own name anywhere, had rehearsed hearing it in exactly this context for four years.

This name was younger, sharper, and it landed in the yard like a coin dropped in a chapel:

"—Corvane. The magistrate's son. Alone, unarmed, asking for the woman who came for Vireo—"

Tam stood at his hand-width of window with the tide-key warm against his palm through the mattress seam, and understood that the river had just given something else back — and that whether it was proof or poison, he'd find out by dawn.

He started packing. There wasn't much to pack. There never had been.
