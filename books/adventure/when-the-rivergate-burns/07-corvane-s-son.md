## 7. Corvane's Son

Dain Corvane had been riding for three days on a stolen horse, and the horse knew it.

It was a fleet remount, bay, deep-chested, branded with the magistrate's wheel-and-wave on its left haunch. Every time Dain reined it off the river road and into the timber, it fought him — not out of spite, he thought, but out of a kind of institutional loyalty. The horse belonged to his father's world. So did the saddle, the signal lantern in his saddlebag, the letters of commission folded against his ribs. He was carrying an entire life's inventory of belonging, and he was about to burn all of it in one conversation with strangers who had every reason to put a knife in him.

He'd rehearsed the conversation forty times between Lowmark and the timber line above Sallow Bend. In most versions of it, Orsen Montrose listened. In a few, she even believed him. In one memorable version she shook his hand, which he suspected said more about his desperation than his judgment.

The truth was simpler and worse: he didn't need her to believe him. He needed her to take the ledger.

* * *

He'd found them by accident, or told himself so. Three days ago he'd been doing what he always did — running his father's errands, this one a routine audit of salvage contracts at Lowmark — when he'd seen the ferrywoman's barge tied at the smugglers' dock and recognized the woman standing on it. Not from a portrait. From a wanted circular that hung in his father's study, pinned under a bronze paperweight shaped like a drowning man. ORSEN MONTROSE, the circular read. POSSESSION OF VESSANY MATERIALS. CONSIDERED ARMED AND EXTREMELY MOTIVATED.

*Motivated.* His father's word choices were always precise like that. Never dangerous. Dangerous implied fear, and Corvane feared nothing except being misunderstood by history.

Dain had stood in front of that circular longer than he'd ever admitted to himself, reading the small print beneath her name: *Apprehend alive if practical. The material matters more than the courier.*

That was the sentence that had done it. Not outrage — he'd been outraged for years, quietly, uselessly, the way a man is outraged by weather. It was the *practicality*. His father had drowned a nation and reduced its survivors to a phrase: *the material matters more than the courier.* Somewhere behind the Silt Gates lay ships full of people his father had sent to the bottom, and somewhere in his father's private strongroom lay a ledger proving it, and Dain had spent twenty-six years being the son of the man who kept both.

So he'd opened the strongroom. That part had been almost insultingly easy; his father trusted no one, which meant he'd never imagined needing to guard against everyone, least of all the heir. Dain had taken the ledger, taken the seal matrix for low-level correspondence, taken nothing else — because taking more would have been noticed sooner — and ridden west into the flood country after three strangers who owed him nothing and would likely give him exactly that.

Now he sat his stolen horse at the edge of a cut hayfield above Sallow Bend and watched the barge crew make camp along the willow bank, and every version of the speech he'd rehearsed evaporated at once.

There were four of them now, not three. A fifth figure moved among them — broad, quick, unfamiliar — and Dain felt his stomach drop through the saddle. Tam Vireo had hired someone in Lowmark. Of course he had. A crew on the run needed hands, and Lowmark was where desperate men went shopping.

The new man laughed at something the cartographer said, and the laugh carried across the water, easy and warm, and Dain thought: *that's the one who'll sell you.*

He didn't know how he knew. He just knew. He'd grown up at his father's dinner table, listening to captains and magistrates and salvage brokers laugh exactly like that — laughs that were currency, laughs that could be spent later.

* * *

He came down at dusk, leading the horse, because a rider at speed reads as an attack and a rider walking reads as a question. Even so, the camp erupted before he cleared the willows. The ferrywoman had a boathook leveled at his chest before he'd finished raising both hands, and Orsen Montrose came out of the dark on his left with a knife already open, moving the way water moves, no wasted motion at all.

"Name," she said.

"Dain Corvane."

The name landed the way he'd known it would. The boathook pressed harder. Montrose didn't flinch, didn't step back — she stepped *in*, close enough that he could see the tide-key's chain glinting at her collar, and that nearness was itself a message: *I'm not afraid of you, and I want you to know I know what you're afraid of.*

"Corvane," she repeated. "As in the magistrate."

"As in his son." He kept his hands high, palms out. "I know how that sounds."

"It sounds like a trap wearing a confession." Her eyes went past him to the horse, to the brand on its haunch, to the quality of his tack. "You rode a fleet remount down to a fugitive camp alone. Either you're the bravest idiot on this river or the bait is supposed to be obvious."

"The bait isn't me." He turned slowly, deliberately, and pulled the ledger from inside his coat — wrapped in oilcloth, bound in gray leather, his father's cipher stamped blind on the cover. "The bait is this. And I'd rather it be in your hands than in theirs."

Nobody moved. Downriver, a night bird called twice and quit, as if even the birds understood the pause.

Maren — the ferrywoman, though he only knew her reputation, *river-witch, don't board her boat without paying first* — hadn't lowered the boathook. "Orsen," she said quietly, "we kill him and sink the body, we're a day richer and a lifetime safer."

"We are," Orsen agreed, and didn't move, and Dain understood suddenly that the decision wasn't hers alone and never had been. She was watching him the way she'd probably watched the river her whole life — reading current, reading debris, reading what the surface refused to say.

"Open it," she said finally.

He unwrapped the oilcloth with numb fingers. The ledger fell open across his palms to a page he'd marked with a ribbon, because he'd known this moment would come and had prepared for it the way he prepared for everything: obsessively, alone, at night.

"Page forty-one," he said. "Read the column headings."

Orsen took the book from him — took it rather than let him hand it, he noted, another small subtraction of trust — and held it to the lantern light. He watched her face while she read. He'd expected anger. What he saw was worse: recognition. The flat, settling look of a woman confirming something she'd already suspected, the way you confirm a bone is broken by pressing on it.

"Shipments," she read aloud. "Dates. Tonnage. 'Recovered specie, Vessany mint marks, lot numbers.'" She looked up. "This is a salvage manifest."

"This is eleven years of salvage manifests." His voice came out steadier than he deserved. "My father didn't recover a drowned treasury. He *made* the drowning. The refugee flotillas out of Vessany — the ones the histories say were lost to storm and piracy? There's a storm in here." He tapped the page. "His signature. Approving the scuttling crews. Approving the sale of what they brought up. Lot by lot. Coin by coin. Names of the families each shipment was traced back to, because the buyers paid premiums for provenance." He heard his own voice crack on the last word and hated it. "They paid extra to know whose gold they were buying."

Silence. The lantern hissed. Somewhere in the willows the horse shifted and sighed.

Tam Vireo leaned over Orsen's shoulder, and Dain watched the disgraced cartographer go pale in stages, like a man descending into cold water. "These lot numbers," Tam whispered. "I've seen these. On charts I forged. On *real* charts — the survivors dictated cargo routes to me, and I thought they were remembering wrong, because the tonnage didn't match any wreck record I—" He stopped. Pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes. "Gods. They weren't wrong. They were robbed twice. Once in the water and once in the record."

"That's why I'm here." Dain spread his empty hands. "I can't fix what he did. Nobody can. But there are two more keys, and the Lament still holds a third of the treasury, and my father has a fleet dragging the river for it right now. You have the first key. I have the ledger that proves everything, and ten years of his private correspondence patterns, and—" he hesitated, then committed— "and his face. His name. His access. I am the one thing on this river he'll hesitate to shoot at, and that hesitation is worth more to you than my corpse."

Maren spat, eloquently, into the mud. "Worth arguing."

"You want to argue worth?" Dain turned on her, and the fear in him finally found a shape sharp enough to speak through. "Your barge leaks. Your papers are forged. You're four people against thirty sail. I'm offering you the enemy's own eyes and ears and you want to weigh it against the convenience of killing me?"

"I'm weighing it against the *certainty* of you turning us in the moment it profits you." Maren's voice dropped into something older and colder than anger. "I've ferried rich men's sons before. They all love justice right up until the water rises past their knees."

"Maren." Orsen said it softly, and the softness closed the argument like a door.

She walked a slow circle around Dain, the ledger tucked under her arm now, and he had the disorienting sense of being charted. Measured in miles, Tam had told him once in Lowmark, drunk, laughing — *she doesn't count years or friends, she counts distance, ask her sometime how far it is to forgiveness.* He'd thought it a joke then. Watching her circle him now, he understood it was a philosophy. She was calculating how far he was from trustworthy, and whether the distance could be covered before the fleet arrived.

"Here's what I believe," Orsen said at last. "I believe you hate your father. That's common enough; half the world hates its fathers. I believe you stole that ledger, which means you're either brave or stupid, and your hands aren't shaking enough for stupid." She stopped in front of him. "What I don't believe is that you've thought past tonight. You defected three days ago. Where do you sleep tomorrow? Whose side are you on when the shooting starts and it's your father's colors on the other boat?"

Dain opened his mouth. Closed it. Because the honest answer was *yours, obviously, yours,* and honest answers offered too fast sound like lies.

"That's what I thought," she said. But she didn't give the ledger back, and she didn't order him killed, and when she turned away she said, over her shoulder: "You sleep at the fire's edge, downstream of everyone. You touch nothing without asking. And the first time anything goes wrong on this river, boy, understand that everyone on this crew will look at you first, and I will let them."

"Yes," Dain said. It was the truest word he'd spoken in days.

* * *

They gave him salt fish and hard bread, which he ate sitting exactly where he'd been told, downstream of the fire, feeling the smoke blow into his face like a small ongoing punishment. The new man — Brannic, hired oars and muscle out of Lowmark — was friendly in a loose, generous way, clapping Dain's shoulder, asking questions about the citadel, about the fleet, about his father. Friendly questions. All of them friendly.

Dain answered none of them fully and filed all of them carefully.

Later, when the others slept, Tam Vireo sat down beside him without asking, which was its own small mercy. For a while the cartographer said nothing, just turned his own cup in his hands.

"My daughter was on the second flotilla," Tam said eventually.

Dain went very still.

"She'd be nineteen now. I forge maps because it's the only prayer I know how to say." Tam looked at him across the fire, and his eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely steady. "I'm telling you that so you understand what you're sleeping next to. If you betray this crew, it won't be Orsen or Maren you need to fear. It'll be a man with nothing left to lose, and those are the ones who don't hesitate."

"I won't betray them."

"Everyone says that." Tam rose, brushed off his coat, paused. "But you gave up the ledger first thing instead of bargaining with it. Rich men's sons bargain. That's the only reason I'm letting myself sleep."

He walked away toward the barge, and Dain sat alone at the fire's edge with the smoke in his eyes and the horse stamping softly in the dark, thinking about his father's study, the bronze paperweight shaped like a drowning man, the circular that said *the material matters more than the courier.*

Somewhere south, six tides were draining out of the river, and a canyon full of dead was waiting to be counted, and his father's fleet was coming with chains.

He was still awake, hours later, when Maren's voice cut low and hard from the bow of the barge:

"Orsen. Lantern on the far bank. Two of them. Riding the towpath fast."

Dain was on his feet before he'd decided to stand, heart slamming, every eye in the camp swinging toward him first — exactly as promised.
