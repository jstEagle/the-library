## 8. First Tide Falling

Maren Odile had run the flooded timber at night exactly twice in her life, and both times she'd sworn on her mother's grave she never would again. Her mother had no grave — the river took her — which meant Maren could swear anything and nobody could prove otherwise. It was one of the small comforts of a river-witch's life.

Now she stood at the tiller of her own barge, the *Sloehound*, with a blacklisted mountain guide at her elbow, a forger sweating below decks, and a magistrate's son coiling rope like he'd been born to it, and she swore a third oath: if they lived through this night, she was going to be insufferable about it forever.

"Timber line starts at the old ferry stake," Orsen said. She had a spyglass to her eye and the flat voice she used when the world was trying to kill them. "Deadfalls from the spring flood. Half of it's floating, half of it's just under."

"Just under is what kills you," Maren said. "Floating wood you can see. Submerged snags come up through your hull like a spear through a fish." She spat over the side, an old habit that did nothing and soothed everything. "You wanted a pilot who knew the water. This is the part where you find out what you paid for."

"I paid you passage rights to a wreck I haven't found yet," Orsen said. "So far I've paid for attitude and leaks."

"The leaks are free."

Behind them, upriver, the lanterns came out.

Maren saw them first — she always saw the lights first; the river taught her that before it taught her anything else. Three points of yellow swinging low over the black water, maybe two bends back, moving fast against the current. A patrol skiff didn't move that fast against the current unless it was under oars, eight men or more, and it didn't carry lanterns unless it wanted to be seen.

"They want us to know they're coming," Dain said. He'd gone very still by the mast.

"Or they're stupid," Maren said.

"Fleet skiffs aren't stupid. My father doesn't hire stupid."

"There it is," Maren said. "The word 'father,' dropped casual as a stone in a well. You do that every hour or so, boy. Like you're checking we remember whose blood you carry."

Dain looked at her. In the last grey light he had his mother's jaw and his father's eyes, and Maren hated that she knew what his father's eyes looked like, because Corvane had stood on the Lowmark docks five years ago handing out salvage writs like alms, and everyone had smiled, and Maren had smiled too, because that was what you did when the wolf counted the sheep.

"I'm not him," Dain said.

"No," Maren agreed. "He'd have reported us by now." She turned back to the water. "Tam! Get up here. Tell me what those papers of yours can do besides get us hanged."

* * *

Tam Vireo came up the ladder rubbing ink off his fingers onto a rag he'd been using for a week, and Maren wondered how a man could forge documents good enough to fool a garrison commander while keeping hands like a schoolboy's. He was thirty, maybe, with a scholar's stoop already set into his shoulders and the look of someone perpetually apologizing to furniture.

"The papers," Tam said, "are a Lowmark timber license. Salvage survey, category three, under charter to a firm called Halloway and Sons."

"There is no Halloway and Sons," Orsen said.

"There is now. I invented it Tuesday." Tam said it without pride, which somehow made it worse. "It's registered in the Lowmark rolls, countersigned by a harbor clerk whose signature I've been copying since I was nineteen, and stamped with a fleet transit seal. Category three lets us drag chains in marked channels. It does not let us run unlit at night through flood timber."

"So it's useless," Maren said.

"It's useful at dawn," Tam said. "At the Lowmark chain. The chain across the narrows — they stop every boat, check papers, wave you through or take you apart. If we're stopped at night running dark, we're smugglers, and smugglers hang. If we're stopped at dawn flying Halloway colors with a licensed survey crew, we're boring, and boring men live long lives."

"He's right," Dain said reluctantly. "Boring is armor."

"Boring is armor," Maren repeated, savoring it. "I've been called many things, but never boring. There's a first time for drowning, I suppose." She pointed her chin at the lanterns astern. "That's not dawn, though. That's now. So before we get to be boring, we have to lose that skiff, and there's exactly one way to do it."

Orsen lowered the spyglass. "The timber."

"The timber," Maren said. "Nobody with sense runs the Snagwater in the dark. Which means nobody will follow us in. We go in, we come out the other side of the drowned orchard, and we're ghosts until morning." She felt the old cold joy rise in her chest, the one that only came when the water got bad enough. "Everyone below. Tie down anything that floats. If you hear wood scream, don't come up. If you hear *me* scream—"

"What then?" Dain asked.

"Then you'll know I found something interesting."

* * *

The drowned orchard had been a real orchard once, before the war nobody named. Maren's grandmother had picked apples there, back when the valley belonged to Vessany and the river ran clear between stone banks instead of spreading fat and brown across drowned fields. Now three hundred apple trees stood dead to their shoulders in the floodwater, black branches clawing at the sky, and between them drifted the spring flood's harvest: whole pines, fence rails, a barn roof, a cradle — Maren tried not to look at the cradle — all of it riding the current in slow, patient convoys, waiting for a hull to open.

She steered by ear more than eye. That was the witchcraft people whispered about, and it wasn't witchcraft at all, just forty years of listening. Current made a sound around a submerged snag — a low worried hum, like a hymn sung badly. Floating logs knocked against each other, and the knock told you how fast the water moved between the trees. And silence — silence was the worst sound on a flooded river, because silence meant deep still water, and still water in flood season meant something enormous had sunk beneath it.

"Port," she said quietly. "Hard port."

The *Sloehound* swung. A pine trunk the length of a chapel slid past her starboard rail close enough to touch, barnacled and silent, going wherever dead things go.

"You see that?" Dain breathed from beside her. He hadn't gone below. Neither had Orsen. Of course they hadn't. Nobody ever went below; that was the curse of commanding a boat full of people who couldn't stand being commanded.

"I heard it," Maren said. "Eyes forward. Watch for the white foam-line — that's the old roadbed under the water, the current funnels along it. We ride the roadbed, we miss the orchard rows."

"How do you know there's a road?"

"Because my grandmother drove a cart on it." She said it flatly and felt him flinch, and didn't care. Let the magistrate's son sit with that a while. Every mile of this river was a graveyard wearing a pleasant face, and half the graves were dug by men with signet rings.

Astern, the lanterns reached the mouth of the timber. They hesitated there, three yellow eyes blinking at the black tangle of branches, and Maren allowed herself one crooked tooth of a smile.

"That's it," she murmured. "Be sensible. Be sensible and stay hungry."

But the lanterns didn't turn away. They split — two holding at the mouth, one pushing in, oars muffled now, running dark except for a single hooded lamp at the bow. Someone aboard that skiff had sense after all, and courage, and orders.

"Hold on," Maren said, and put the *Sloehound* into the current like a knife between ribs.

* * *

What followed, Maren would later describe — when she described it at all, usually to strangers in taverns who bought her drinks — as eleven minutes of the river trying to eat her boat while she argued it out of the idea.

The argument went roughly like this:

A snag loomed off the port bow, a drowned apple tree with a root-ball like a fist; she threw the tiller starboard and the barge answered slow, too slow, heavy with waterlogged cargo, and the root-ball scraped the hull with a sound like a giant dragging fingernails down a door. Below decks, Tam made a noise like a stepped-on cat.

A floating pine crossed their path beam-on, rolling; she threaded the gap between its trunk and a fence line that surfaced and sank like a sea serpent's spine, and the wake of her own maneuver slapped back against the rudder and stole her steerage for two full seconds, two seconds in which the *Sloehound* went where the river pleased, and the river pleased to aim her at a barn roof with a ridgepole sharp as a lance.

"Orsen!" Maren snapped. "Boat hook. Ridgepole. Push!"

Orsen was already moving — Maren would give her this, the woman moved like weather, no wasted thought between seeing and doing — and caught the roof on the push, shoving it aside with a grunt, and the ridgepole kissed the hull instead of opening it, and they were through.

The pursuing skiff was not so lucky. Somewhere behind them, in the dark, wood met wood at speed, followed by a crack like a musket, followed by shouting — real shouting, frightened shouting, the sound of eight oarsmen discovering that their officer's courage had outrun his pilotry. The hooded lamp swung wildly, dipped, steadied.

"They're hit," Dain said. "They're taking water."

"Good," said Maren.

"We should—" Dain started.

"Don't," Maren said, and her voice came out with the river in it, low and cold and older than she was. "Don't say we should help. I know you want to. It's decent of you. It's the worst thing about you." She kept her eyes on the foam-line ahead, silver-grey in the dying light. "If we put about in flood timber at night, we die, and then they die anyway, and the keys sink, and Corvane wins. Mercy is a luxury of calm water, boy. Learn it now or the river will teach you slower."

Silence behind her. Then Orsen's voice, quiet: "There'll be a fishing camp downstream. Kettle folk. They'll pull the skiff's crew out — the river spits back what it doesn't keep, and it rarely keeps fleet men; even the river has politics."

"And if it doesn't spit them back?" Dain asked.

"Then the river settles another account," Maren said, "that started long before any of us were born."

She didn't say whose account. She didn't have to. She felt Dain understand it anyway, the way you feel weather change.

* * *

They came out of the drowned orchard an hour after full dark, into the long straight reach above Lowmark, and the relief aboard was so thick you could have carved it. Tam came up on deck pale as paper and immediately lit a pipe with hands that shook, and dropped the match twice, and Maren handed him a third one lit from the galley stove without comment, because river-witches are cruel but not needlessly.

"They'll report the collision," Orsen said, standing at the stern rail, watching the black water. "By morning, every patrol between here and the Silt Gates will know a barge ran the Snagwater. They'll know our heading."

"They'll know a barge ran the Snagwater," Maren corrected. "They won't know whose. There are forty barges on this reach and half of them are running something illegal tonight. That's the beauty of Lowmark. Everyone's guilty, so no one's remarkable."

"Until the chain," Tam said around his pipe.

"Until the chain," Maren agreed.

The Lowmark chain was a fleet installation, strung across the narrows on great iron pontoons, raised at dusk and lowered at dawn, and every vessel that passed paid a toll in coin or in answers. At dawn, with forged papers, they might pass as boring. But dawn was four hours off, and the tide — the first of the six tides, the tide that opened the way to the Lament — waited for no chain, no patrol, no grief.

"We anchor?" Dain asked.

"We anchor," Maren said, "in the reeds off Sedge Point, and we wait for light, and we pray to whatever god forgives forgers that your Halloway and Sons holds up, Tam."

"It held up against a garrison clerk in Vessport," Tam said. "And a customs man in Brindle."

"A clerk and a customs man," Maren said. "Tomorrow it meets a chain commander with a hangman's budget. Different animal entirely."

Tam pulled on his pipe and said nothing, and the smoke went up into the dark like a small gray prayer.

* * *

They anchored in the reeds, and Maren took first watch because she always took first watch, because sleep on a river was a thing you earned and she'd never learned how. The others bedded down among the cargo — Orsen curled tight as a knotted rope even in sleep, Tam mumbling chart-coordinates into his collar, Dain lying awake, she could tell, staring at the stars like they owed him an explanation.

Around midnight, Orsen climbed up beside her without being asked and sat with her shoulder almost touching hers, which for Orsen Montrose was practically an embrace.

"You handled that well," Orsen said. "Back there. With the boy."

"I handled it honestly. There's a difference."

"He needs honest. He's had twenty years of polished lies served with dinner wine."

"Mm." Maren watched the reeds comb the current. "You know what I was thinking, running the timber? I was thinking about my grandmother picking apples on that hillside. Before your war. Before the flooding. She used to say the river remembers everything it's been given and everything it's been robbed of, and one day it hands it all back, and you'd best be standing on the bank ready to catch." She glanced sideways. "Then I hooked a chest out of the shallows, and here you come, asking me to steer you into the middle of the robbery. Standing on the bank, am I?"

"Something like that."

"Then let me tell you the price of tonight, guide, since you're fond of prices. Tonight we became a crew. Not a guide and a hired witch and a jailbird and a runaway — a crew. Under the lanterns, in the timber, when the ridgepole came at us — nobody asked, everybody moved. That's rare. That's worth more than gold, and I've seen what gold does, so believe me when I price it high." She fixed Orsen with a look. "Which means when it breaks — and crews break, always, usually over trust, usually over a Corvane — it'll hurt worse than a hull breach. Remember I told you."

"I remember everything," Orsen said. "It's my curse."

"Mine too," said Maren. "River-witch's privilege. Now get some sleep. Dawn comes early, and tomorrow we find out whether a dead man's signature can hold an iron chain."

* * *

Dawn came up the color of pewter, and with it the chain.

They raised Halloway colors — a plain gray pennant Tam had sewn himself, and Maren privately resolved never to tell him how competent the stitching was — and joined the queue of dawn traffic: eel boats, a grain barge, a timber raft, all of them drifting toward the narrows where the iron chain hung slack between its pontoons and a fleet cutter sat mid-channel with a swivel gun trained, casually, permanently, on everything that floated.

The chain commander was a young lieutenant with a clipboard soul, and he boarded them with two marines and read Tam's papers for a long time.

"Halloway and Sons," he said. "Salvage survey, category three."

"Chain survey and obstruction mapping," Tam said smoothly, "under commission to the district hydrographic office. Our schedule's attached. Counterstamped."

The lieutenant checked the counterstamp. Maren watched his thumb hover over the wax seal, and she watched Tam not sweat, and she thought: *this boy has lied to better men than you, lieutenant, and slept fine after.*

"Where's your survey gear?" the lieutenant asked.

"Chains and sounding lines, below," Tam said. "We ran the Snagwater last night to fix the new channel marks. Lost a dinghy to a snag. You may have heard."

Something flickered in the lieutenant's face. "A barge ran the Snagwater in the dark?"

"Idiots," Tam said, with feeling. "Whoever they were."

The lieutenant looked at the papers, at the gray pennant, at Tam's ink-stained fingers, at Maren at the tiller looking bored, at Orsen looking bored harder, at Dain — Dain, whose face was on recruitment posters in three provinces — and Maren felt the whole fragile morning balance on that one glance.

"Category three requires a bonded master," the lieutenant said finally. "Signature here."

Tam signed. The lieutenant stamped. The chain lowered, groaning, and the *Sloehound* slid through into the lower reach, and only when the cutter was a speck did anyone aboard breathe.

"Well," Maren said, and spat over the side with ceremony. "Halloway and Sons. May the firm prosper." She put the tiller over and brought the barge's head around toward the Silt Gates, where the first falling tide was already drawing the water down off the drowned land, exposing, inch by inch, the secrets the river had promised to give back.

She was trimming sail when Tam appeared at her elbow, gray-faced, holding his copy of the manifest.

"Maren," he said quietly. "The tide tables. I've been recalculating. The window isn't six days."

"It's six tides," Maren said. "That's what the old woman said."

"Tides fall faster than days." Tam's finger shook slightly on the paper. "If the first fell last night — the second falls tomorrow. And the last one—" he swallowed, "—the last one falls in four days. Four, Maren. Not six."

Maren looked downriver, toward the gates, toward the wreck called Lament, toward the fleet that was surely already moving.

"Then we'd best not waste daylight arguing with arithmetic," she said, and rang the bell to wake the crew.
