## 9. The Ledger of the Drowned

Tam Vireo had spent four years in a Lowmark cell learning to read the way other men learned to pray — obsessively, in the dark, with nothing but what he could carry in his head. He had memorized coastlines. He had drawn charts on the floor with a wetted finger and walked their contours until his dreams smelled of brine and vellum. And through all of it, one question had circled him like a gull over offal: *who gave the order?*

He knew now. The answer was sitting three feet away from him, wrapped in oilcloth, smelling of river damp and old leather, and it was heavier than any chart Tam had ever forged.

"Open it," Orsen said.

They were anchored in a backwater slough behind a curtain of willow, the barge tied to a drowned cottonwood that rose out of the flood like the ribcage of some enormous animal. Maren had rigged a lantern low, its light hooded by a tin plate so it fell in a single amber cone across the deck. Dain Corvane sat apart from the rest of them, near the stern rail, his father's ledger between his hands as if he didn't trust himself to set it down.

"It's yours to open," Dain said. "You're the one they'll hang for reading it."

"That's cheerful," Maren muttered from the tiller.

Tam reached out. His fingers weren't steady — they hadn't been steady since Lowmark, maybe since before — but he took the ledger anyway, because someone had to, and because he had spent four years being punished for a crime he hadn't committed while the man who committed the real ones wore a magistrate's chain and dined on river salmon. If there was guilt in this crew, Tam owned more of it than anyone. He might as well be the one to lift the lid.

The oilcloth came away. Beneath it: a book bound in black calfskin, brass corners gone green with age, no title, no seal on the cover. Only when Tam opened it did he understand why it needed none. The first page was a bill of lading.

*Vessany Treasury — Third Portion. Manifest of conveyance. Received under writ of Magistrate Aldous Corvane, Rivergate District.*

Below that, columns. Dates. Weights. Assays. Gold measured not in coins but in *lots*, as if a nation could be sold like cordwood.

Nobody spoke. The river moved past them in the dark, patient as a debt collector.

"Read it," Orsen said quietly. "All of it."

So Tam read.

---

The early entries were almost mundane. That was the worst part, Tam thought later. Not spectacle. Bookkeeping. Corvane had been meticulous the way only a thief who never expected to answer for it could be meticulous. Every lot numbered. Every buyer named — and the buyers were the shock that made Maren swear under her breath and Dain go very still. Guildmasters. Fleet captains. Two sitting members of the Rivergate council. A bishop whose name Tam recognized from the tithe ledgers of half the river towns.

Corvane hadn't hoarded Vessany. He'd *distributed* her, parcel by parcel, buying loyalty with another country's bones. Everyone who mattered on the lower river held a piece of the drowned nation, which meant everyone who mattered had a reason to make sure no one ever went looking for the rest of it.

Then Tam turned the page and stopped reading aloud.

"What?" Orsen leaned in. "What is it?"

Tam couldn't answer at first. The lantern light seemed suddenly very far away. He knew these waters. He had drawn them — or rather, he had redrawn them, again and again, from survivors' memories, trying to get the channels right, trying to honor the dead by getting the map true. And here, in his own hand's opposite number, in a dead clerk's careful script, were the same waters. But annotated differently.

*Conveyance of persons, Vessany refugee flotilla, eleven vessels,* the entry began. *Escorted from the shallows below Grey Bar under writ. Orders follow.*

The orders followed. Tam read them twice because his eyes refused to accept them once.

*At the second bend, scuttle escort. Allow no vessel to reach the Silt Gates. No survivors to reach Rivergate. Salvage of cargo to proceed at dawn tide. Wreckage to be reported as storm loss.*

Eleven ships. Tam had heard the survivors speak of nine. The old woman in the hidden village, Yeva, had counted eight in her telling, grief having already eaten two. Eleven. Eleven ships full of families who had fled a war with whatever gold they could carry, trusting an escort flying legitimate colors, and the escort had opened their hulls at the second bend in the dark, and at dawn the salvage crews had come for the cargo, and the report filed upriver said *storm loss, regrettable, no survivors recovered.*

"No survivors recovered," Tam said aloud, and his voice cracked on it. "He wrote *no survivors recovered* and then he wrote *salvage complete, 340 lots.*"

Maren had gone white. Even Orsen, who Tam suspected could watch a landslide bury a village without blinking, had her jaw set so hard the muscle jumped.

Dain stood up. For a moment Tam thought he meant to throw the ledger into the river, and part of Tam wanted him to — wanted the thing destroyed, wanted it never to have existed — but Dain just gripped the rail and stared at the water and said nothing at all.

"Keep reading," Orsen said. Her voice was flat and cold and very controlled, the voice of a woman standing on scree above a long drop. "There are names. I saw names further in."

There were names. Pages of them. After the treasury lots, after the ship-by-ship accounting of the refugee flotilla, the ledger changed character. It became something worse than a theft record. It became a census.

*House of Vaelin — gold plate, 12 lots; deeds to millworks; two sons, ages noted, unaccounted.*
*Family Serrano — coin, 40 lots; the mother's rings itemized separately; daughter, age six, unaccounted.*
*Vessany Cartographers' Guild — plates, instruments, the master atlas of the delta; guildmaster Oren Vireo, unaccounted.*

Tam stopped breathing.

He read the line again. *Guildmaster Oren Vireo, unaccounted.*

His father. His father's name, in Corvane's ledger, in the same column as stolen spoons and melted candlesticks, reduced to two words and a bureaucratic shrug. *Unaccounted.* As if a man could be mislaid. As if the guildmaster of Vessany, who had taught his son to draw coastlines before he could write his own name, who had stayed behind when the war came because *someone had to keep the charts, Tam, the charts are the country, borders die when the maps die* — as if that man were simply an entry that failed to reconcile.

"Tam." Orsen's hand was on his shoulder. He hadn't noticed her move. "Breathe."

"He listed him." Tam heard his own voice as if from underwater. "He itemized my father. Between a shipment of altar silver and a crate of surveying instruments. He kept *inventory* of the people he drowned."

"That's what this is," Maren said softly. She had come forward from the tiller without Tam noticing, and she was looking down at the ledger the way you look at a snake you've found in your bed. "It's not a theft record. It's a butcher's tally. He wrote down everything so he could prove to his buyers the goods were genuine."

"And the buyers signed," Dain said.

His voice was wrong. Tam looked up. Dain had picked the ledger back up and was turning pages with a speed that wasn't reading — it was hunting. He stopped at a section near the end where the entries thinned and the handwriting changed, became larger, more confident. Corvane's own hand, Tam realized. The clerk had done the early work. Near the end, the magistrate had stopped delegating.

*Received of Fleet-Captain Halloway, in exchange for salvage rights, eastern third: sworn silence re: flotilla disposition. Signed.*

And beneath it, signatures. Dozens. A constellation of the river's powerful men and women, each one signing away their conscience in exchange for a share of a murdered nation. Dain's finger came down on one signature near the top of the second page and rested there.

"Halloway," he said. "He commands the fleet's vanguard now. The ships chasing us. My father bought him twenty years ago with Vessany gold and he's been collecting interest ever since."

"Does Halloway know about this ledger?" Orsen asked.

"He knows he took gold. He doesn't know my father kept receipts." Dain laughed, and there was nothing good in the sound. "He kept *receipts*. Twenty years of proof that every captain, every councilman, every guild lord on this river is a partner in the drowning of a country. This isn't a ledger. It's a gallows with pages."

---

They sat with it a long time. The lantern burned low. Somewhere out in the flooded timber, a night bird called, and the water slapped the hull in its slow indifferent rhythm, and Tam sat with his father's name in his lap and felt something in his chest change shape.

He had spent four years hating himself for the forged charts. In Lowmark he had told himself the story over and over: that he had betrayed Vessany, that his fakes had helped smugglers loot the last unprotected sites, that his punishment was deserved even if the specific charge was false. Guilt had been the load-bearing wall of his whole self. Take it out and he didn't know what would stand.

But guilt was a small country, and this ledger was a large one.

"You said the Lament holds the last third," Orsen said at last. She was looking at Dain. "Confirm it."

"The ledger confirms it." Dain turned to the final pages. "Look. The first third went to the buyers — the lots we've been reading. The second third my father moved personally, overland, into the citadel vaults. There's a transfer record here, sealed wagons, armed escort, dates. But the third third—" he tapped an entry near the end, "—was never delivered. *Held aboard Lament pending secure conveyance.* The date next to it is three days before the Lament 'sank in a storm.'" His mouth twisted. "Another storm. My father's storms have excellent aim."

"So the gold is still aboard," Maren said. "Twenty years in the hold."

"The gold, and this." Dain touched the manifest pages. "Every family. Every lot. Every signature. If the Lament's hold is intact, then everything needed to hang half the river's power structure is sitting in the dark behind the Silt Gates, waiting on three keys."

"Two of which we have." Orsen patted her coat, where the brass tide-key rode against her ribs. Tam knew the second key was wrapped in waxed canvas in the barge's false bottom, pulled from the Lament's grave four tides ago while the fleet's masts filled the canyon mouth behind them. "One of which is on his hand."

"Which is why we're going to the Rivergate," Dain said.

"Which is why," Orsen agreed.

Tam barely heard them. He was looking at the guild entry again. *Vessany Cartographers' Guild — master atlas of the delta.* His father's atlas. The one Oren Vireo had been carrying when the flotilla sailed, the one every survivor had described to Tam in fragments, hoping he could reconstruct it: the true channels, the deep-water routes, the locations of the refuge caches. Corvane had catalogued it. Which meant it had been salvaged. Which meant it was either in a buyer's collection or—

Or in the Lament's hold, with the last third. Held pending secure conveyance. Some things a magistrate stole for money. Some things he stole because maps were dangerous, because *borders die when the maps die*, because a restored atlas in survivor hands was worth more than gold and Corvane had always known it.

"I'm coming to the Rivergate," Tam said.

Everyone looked at him. He realized he had spoken louder than intended, loud enough to startle the night bird into silence.

"You were going anyway," Orsen said. "We voted on it this morning."

"No. You don't understand." Tam pulled the ledger toward him and flattened it open on his knees, both hands pressing it flat as if it might fly away. "I came aboard for the wreck. For redemption, or whatever I told myself it was — absolution, a clean chart, a way to stop seeing my father's face when I close my eyes. Fine. But this changes what we're doing. This isn't a treasure run anymore. If we take that gold and scatter, we've just completed Corvane's transaction in reverse. Rich thieves, poorer thieves, and the signatures stay buried." He looked around the lantern circle, at each of them in turn. "The charter, the manifests, the signatures — those come out first. Those go to a court, to the survivors, to *anyone* who will read them aloud. The gold can rot in the hold if it has to. But the truth gets delivered."

Maren snorted. "Spoken like a man who's never been hungry."

"I've been hungry," Tam said. "Four years in Lowmark, remember. I know exactly what gold buys. That's how I know it isn't enough." He tapped the page with his father's name. "This man doesn't get to be *unaccounted*. None of them do. Three hundred forty lots, eleven ships, however many names in these pages — they get accounted. Every one. Publicly. With witnesses."

Silence. The lantern hissed. Out in the dark, the sixth-tide current dragged at the anchor line, impatient, like the river itself wanted them moving.

Orsen studied him for a long moment. Then she nodded, slow, the way she did things — deliberate, weighted, final. "Justice first. Gold second. Anyone disagree?"

Maren shrugged. "I want passage rights and my cut either way. Justice sounds fine as long as it floats."

"Dain?"

Dain was quiet. Tam watched him wrestle with it — watched him understand, visibly, that justice meant his father in chains or worse, and that he had known this since the day he defected with the ledger under his cloak, and that knowing and agreeing were different animals entirely.

"My father taught me that power is keeping accounts no one else can audit," Dain said finally. "He'd be proud of this ledger. So yes. Justice. Let's audit him."

"Then it's settled." Orsen closed the ledger and handed it back to Tam, handle-first, the way she seemed to hand over everything that mattered. "You keep it. You're the one who reads."

Tam took it. The calfskin was warm from all their hands, and heavier than it had been, though it weighed exactly the same. He understood the weight was his now. Four years of guilt, transmuted in one night into purpose — not clean, not painless, but pointed, like a keel. A boat doesn't need to be free of weight, his father used to say. It needs to know which way it's leaning.

"We have two tides left before the window closes," Orsen said, rising, moving to the tiller beside Maren. "The Rivergate is five days downstream at barge speed, three if we push and the weather holds. Corvane knows we have two keys. He doesn't know we have the ledger, and he doesn't know his son is aboard. Those are our only advantages, and they shrink every mile."

"Then we'd best not lose miles," Maren said, and put the tiller over.

As the barge swung out of the slough and into the main channel, Tam sat at the bow with the ledger open on his knees, copying names by lantern light into the margins of his own memory — the cartographer's habit, the archivist's reflex, the son's vow. *House of Vaelin. Family Serrano. Guildmaster Oren Vireo.* He would not lose a single one.

He was halfway down the page when Maren, at the tiller, went rigid.

"Lanterns," she said. "Aft. Coming fast, and they're not running dark."

Tam looked up. Behind them, far back up the channel but closing, a string of lights swung around the bend — too many, too orderly, too purposeful for fishermen. And above them, faint on the night wind, carried down the current like a promise, came the sound of a signal horn playing three short notes and one long.

Fleet code. Tam knew it from his forged papers, from Dain's lessons on the canyon run.

*Contact. Quarry sighted. All stations pursue.*

Orsen's hand found the tide-key at her throat. "Maren," she said calmly, "how much river do we have between us and the dark?"

"Not enough," Maren said, and opened the throttle.
