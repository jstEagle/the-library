## 10. Second Tide: The Silt Gates

Dain Corvane had spent twenty-nine years learning that his father's name opened doors. He had never once imagined it would open these.

The Silt Gates rose out of the river like the jawbones of something dead and enormous — two stone towers flanking a channel barely wide enough for two barges abreast, joined by an iron boom chain thick as a man's waist. Behind them, the river spread wide and brown and slow, running down from the canyon country where the Lament lay buried in her grave of silt. Fleet banners hung limp on both towers in the windless morning: black trident on gray, the magistrate's colors, the colors Dain had grown up beneath.

He stood at the barge's rail in a salvage officer's coat two sizes too big for him, Tam's forgery of his father's seal pressed flat against his chest inside it, and tried to remember how to breathe like a man with nothing to hide.

"You're pale," Maren said beside him.

"I'm always pale."

"You're *pale* pale." She spat over the rail, unhurried, a woman who had made peace with the river taking everything she loved and so had stopped loving anything the river could take. "When they hail us, let me do the talking until they ask for rank. Then you do yours."

"And if they ask for papers?"

"Then you show them the pretty paper Tam bled over, and you keep your face stupid, and you pray to whatever your father prays to that his seal is as good as his reputation." She looked him up and down. "It is. That's the problem with your family. Everything about you is good enough to be believed."

He wanted to answer that. He didn't. There was no answer that wasn't true.

Below decks, Orsen and the cargo were hidden — Orsen because a wanted woman's face was worth more than the gold she carried, and the gold because a garrison commander who found a Vessany tide-key in a salvage hold wouldn't wave them through, he'd wave them into irons. Dain had watched Orsen go down the hatch without argument, which frightened him more than the towers did. She argued with weather. If she'd gone quiet, it was because she thought the odds were bad.

Or because she trusted him. He honestly couldn't tell which possibility scared him worse.

The barge came on. The towers grew. He could see the garrison now — a dozen men on the eastern parapet, a swivel gun mounted behind sandbags, a sergeant leaning on the boom-winch with the boredom of a man who had checked a thousand barges and been impressed by none of them. A lantern hung from the chain even in daylight, burning green. Fleet signal oil. It meant the gates were at alert.

"They know something's coming," Tam murmured from the tiller, where he stood dressed as a pilot, hands steady on the sweep. His graze from the canyon had scabbed over into a dark crescent along his jaw. "Corvane's word went out after the citadel theft. Every gate between here and the sea has our description."

"Then we're already caught," Maren said.

"We were already caught the day I forged those charts," Tam said. "I've decided not to let it affect my work."

The hail came when they were forty yards out — a trumpet blast, then a voice through a speaking-horn, cracked and official:

"*Barge ahoy! Strike your sail and stand off! This is a closed water under fleet authority. Identify yourself and your cargo or be fired upon!*"

Maren cupped her hands. "Salvage charter out of Lowmark! Wreck assessment, upper reaches! We've got papers!"

"Papers don't matter! The gates are sealed by order of Magistrate Corvane himself! No vessel passes until the salvage fleet completes its survey!"

And there it was. The wall. Dain felt the crew's attention swing toward him like a compass needle finding north, and he understood suddenly what Orsen had known since Lowmark: this moment was always going to come down to him. Not the forgery. Not the river-craft. Him — the son, the name, the blood.

He stepped to the rail.

"Sergeant!" he called, pitching his voice into the register his father used for addressing subordinates — flat, unimpressed, entitled to obedience. "Is that how you address a commissioned officer of the salvage fleet?"

The speaking-horn went quiet. On the parapet, men shifted. The sergeant straightened.

"Name and rank," the horn said, less certain now.

"Corvane. Special commission, magistrate's office, attached to the survey fleet." He let the silence sit. Let them chew on it. "You have my father's chain drawn across a channel I am ordered to assess before the fifth tide. Do you intend to explain to the magistrate why his own survey is delayed? Or are you going to open my father's gates?"

He heard it happen — heard the exact moment the name landed, traveled, rearranged itself into fear. A murmur ran along the parapet like wind through wheat. *Corvane.* The son. Here. Unannounced. Which could only mean one thing in the arithmetic of a garrison: trouble above, and someone about to be blamed for it.

The sergeant appeared at the parapet rail, squinting down. "Beg pardon, sir. We've got no word of any special commission."

"You wouldn't. Commissions that get announced get sabotaged." Dain pulled Tam's forgery from his coat and held it up — not close enough to read, just close enough to gleam. The wax seal caught the morning light, red and heavy and perfect. "You'll take my word for it or you can send a rider to the citadel and wait three days for an answer while the tide window closes and I write down your name in my report. Your choice, Sergeant. I forget names slowly but I never lose them."

That last part wasn't even acting. It was inheritance.

The sergeant's face did the arithmetic visibly. Three days, a closed tide window, a report, a name. Against: opening a gate for a man wearing his master's seal, whose face — now that the sergeant was really looking — was the magistrate's face younger, softer, wrong only if you already suspected it.

"Winch crew!" the sergeant bellowed. "Stand to the boom! Officer coming through — move, you dogs, *move!*"

The chain began to rise. Links as thick as Dain's thigh came dripping out of the brown water, groaning through the winch gears, and the gap beneath them widened foot by foot. Maren exhaled through her teeth. Tam didn't move at all, which Dain had learned was the cartographer's version of screaming with joy.

"Easy," Maren breathed. "Easy. Tide's dropping. Current'll want to shove us left onto the stonework. I'll hold her center-line; you watch the clearance."

"I'm watching the clearance," Tam said.

"You're watching the sergeant."

"I'm watching the sergeant *and* the clearance. I contain multitudes."

The barge slid toward the gap. Ten feet of clearance under the raised chain, then eight, then six, the iron links swinging gently, pendulum-slow, dripping river water onto the deck in fat cold drops. Dain kept his eyes forward, his chin level, his posture a portrait of inherited authority, and inside he was twelve years old again standing outside his father's study waiting to be told whether he'd disappointed the name today.

The chain passed overhead close enough to touch. He didn't touch it. Some superstition he couldn't have explained.

They were through.

For eleven full seconds, Dain allowed himself to feel it — the gates falling away behind, the wide slow water ahead, the canyon country opening its brown arms. They had done it. They had walked the name like a key into a lock and turned it, and the lock had believed them, and they were through, and the Lament waited upstream with her second tide-key sleeping in her drowned hold—

"Hold," the sergeant called from the tower. "Officer! One moment!"

The winch stopped. The barge drifted. Dain turned, unhurried, heart slamming against the forged seal under his coat.

The sergeant was holding a message tube. Green ribbon. Fleet signal oil.

"Beg pardon, sir — this came upriver at dawn. Orders for all gates." The sergeant hesitated, and Dain watched a man decide, in real time, whether to read aloud to a Corvane or make a Corvane ask. He made him ask.

"Read it."

"*To all garrisons, Silt Gates to the sea:*" the sergeant recited, "*be advised: fugitives aboard a converted barge, posing as salvage personnel. Ringleader: Montrose, Orsen, mountain guide, blacklisted. Accomplices include Vireo, Tam, cartographer, convicted of forgery; ferrywoman Maren of the Lowmark reach; and — *" the sergeant paused, and Dain saw his eyes flick up from the paper, and everything in the world went very still and very loud, "*— Corvane, Dain. Son of the magistrate. Considered armed, deceived, or coerced. If encountered, detain and hold for the magistrate's personal judgment. Reward stated as:*" the sergeant swallowed, "*generous.*"

Silence on the water. The green lantern burned. Somewhere below decks, Dain knew, Orsen was listening to every word through the hull planking, hand on a knife, deciding whether today ended in a fight.

Dain laughed.

It surprised him more than anyone. But it came out right — a short, contemptuous bark, the laugh of a man hearing gossip too stupid to offend him. "There it is," he said. "My father's little drama. Yes, Sergeant, that's the situation. My son has fallen in with smugglers, and I am hunting him personally, quietly, before the scandal reaches the capital. Why do you think I'm traveling on a leaky barge instead of a fleet cutter?" He shook his head, weary, paternal, magnificent. "You've just read my orders to me. Now. Are you going to help me catch my son, or are you going to stand there making sure everyone on this river knows exactly what he looks like?"

The sergeant went the color of old cheese. "Sir — I — no offense intended —"

"None taken. You followed your orders. Keep following them. And Sergeant —" Dain leaned on the rail, dropped his voice, gave him the confiding tone his father used exactly twice a year and made men loyal for a decade. "If a barge answers that description anywhere in your stretch of water, you send word to me first. Not to the fleet. To me. My father wants the boy alive and unspoiled, and the fleet has a habit of shooting first and writing apologies after. Can I rely on you?"

"You can, sir. Absolutely, sir."

"Good man." He straightened. "Now get that winch turning again before the tide drops and strands me on your mudbank. And Sergeant — the reward. When it's paid, remember who told you to send word *first*."

"Yes sir. Thank you, sir."

The winch groaned back to life. The sergeant saluted. Dain returned it with a crispness that cost him something physical, some small internal tearing, like a stitch pulling loose, and then the barge swung with the current and the Silt Gates fell away behind them and he walked, very steadily, to the hatch, lifted it, and climbed down into the dark where Orsen sat among the crates with her knife out and her eyes shining.

"Well?" she said.

"He's sending word ahead."

"To whom?"

"To whoever commands the fleet's advance picket above the canyon. By fast boat. Within the hour." Dain sat down hard on a crate. His hands, he noticed distantly, were shaking now that no one needed them steady. "I bought us through the gates. I also told the entire garrison exactly who we are, where we're going, and what we look like. I turned myself into the most valuable fugitive on this river and then I handed them the map."

Orsen put the knife away.

"Dain." She said his name the way she said *belay* or *hold fast* — a command, plain and load-bearing. "Look at me." He did. "You talked us past a garrison, a swivel gun, and a sealed boom using nothing but your worst inheritance. That's the best sailing I've seen since Maren ran the timber. Whatever comes next, comes next. We've outrun word before."

"We've outrun riders," Maren called down through the hatch. "We have not yet outrun a fleet that knows our faces, our boat, and our destination. Just so we're all being honest about the ledger."

Tam's head appeared beside hers, upside down, grinning around his scab. "On the bright side, the reward for me is apparently smaller than the reward for Dain. I intend to bring this up at every opportunity."

"The reward for *him* isn't money," Orsen said quietly. "It says 'the magistrate's personal judgment.' That's not a bounty. That's a leash." She looked at Dain. "Your father doesn't want you dead. He wants you back."

"I know."

"And if he gets you back?"

Dain looked at his hands. They had stopped shaking. He wished they hadn't; the shaking felt more honest than anything he'd said on that tower.

"Then he'll parade me at the citadel as proof the corruption ends at his own hearth," he said. "And he'll forgive me publicly and destroy me privately, and everyone will say what a merciful man Magistrate Corvane is, and the Lament stays robbed, and the drowned stay drowned." He looked up. "So we don't get caught. That's the whole plan. We don't get caught."

Above them, the river carried them upstream into the narrowing brown throat of the canyon country, where the fourth tide would soon begin to fall, and somewhere behind them a fast boat with a green lantern was already cutting the current, carrying their names ahead of them like lit fuses.

* * *

They made the first bend by noon and the second by midafternoon, and at the mouth of the third bend Maren killed the sail and let the barge drift, because ahead, moored along the far bank where the canyon pinched tight, sat three fleet cutters at anchor, black-and-gray pennants snapping, a picket post with a signal mast and a clear view of ten miles of water.

No way past in daylight. No way past at all, maybe, once the word arrived and the picket started actively hunting instead of idly watching.

Dain stood at the rail beside Orsen and watched the cutters, and watched the signal mast, and did the arithmetic he was beginning to understand was simply what being on this crew meant: every door his name opened, another closed.

"There," Orsen said softly, pointing — not at the cutters but past them, high on the canyon wall, where a thread of white water spilled from a side-channel invisible until you knew to look for it. "Maren. Is that runnable?"

Maren studied it for a long time. The wind moved. The thread of white flickered.

"That's not a channel," she said finally. "That's a rumor of a channel." She grinned, and it was not a comforting grin. "Get below and lash the cargo. We're going to go lie to a fleet picket about a waterfall."
