## 11. Storm Over the Shallows

The sky turned the color of a bruise an hour before the wind arrived.

Orsen had been watching it build all afternoon — that green-black smear stacking up over the western ridgeline like something poured rather than formed. She'd spent enough years in high country to read weather the way other people read faces, and this face was saying nothing good.

"Storm," she said, and Maren was already at the tiller.

"I see it." The ferrywoman's eyes narrowed against the spray coming off the bow. "Question is whether we outrun it or hide from it."

"You can't outrun a squall on this river. Not in this tub."

"This tub has outlived three owners and two sinkings, girl. Show some respect."

The barge wallowed through the widening shallows south of the Silt Gates, the water gone the color of milk tea where the river dragged its bed of silt behind it. Tam stood amidships with his charts weighted down by river stones, trying to make sense of a stretch of water no chartmaker had ever bothered to survey properly. Dain worked the pump in the bilge because nobody trusted him with anything more delicate yet, and because he hadn't complained once about it, which Orsen found herself noticing despite her best efforts not to.

The wind hit them like a thrown door.

It came off the west in a single flat shove that laid the barge over hard enough to slide the cargo crates, and then the rain came — not drops but a solid gray curtain, the kind of rain that erased the world past arm's length. Maren shouted something Orsen couldn't hear. She leaned close.

"—channel! There's a channel—" Maren pointed off the port bow, into a screen of drowned timber where the water seemed to fold back on itself. "My grandmother ran grain through it during the embargo. If it's still open—"

"If it's not, we're kindling."

"If it's not, we were kindling anyway."

Orsen looked at the open water, where the squall was already raising waves she didn't want to think about, then at the timber. The timber. At least trees didn't move.

"Take us in."

Maren put the barge at the gap like a woman threading a needle with her eyes closed. The channel was barely wider than the hull, walled in by dead cedars standing chest-deep in the floodwater, their branches clawing at the rigging as they slid through. Rain hammered the canopy overhead so hard the sound came down like applause from a hostile crowd. Twice the keel kissed submerged roots and Orsen's stomach climbed into her throat, and twice Maren corrected with a touch of the tiller so small it might have been instinct rather than skill.

"How do you know this?" Orsen shouted.

"My grandmother knew it!" Maren shouted back, grinning through the wet. "I just remember being cold and scared and told to shut up!"

The channel bent twice, opened once into a flooded meadow where they nearly grounded, then bent again — and then, abruptly, the wind died. Not gradually. It simply stopped, cut off like a door closing, and Orsen realized they'd passed behind a ridge of high ground that the storm couldn't reach. The rain thinned to a whisper. The water here was still, black, mirror-calm, pooled in what looked like an old quarry or a collapsed oxbow ringed with willows.

And there were boats.

Three of them, drawn up under netting among the willows. Flat-bottomed, shallow-drafted, painted the dull gray of things meant not to be seen. And beyond them, half-hidden in the willow screen, a cluster of structures built on stilts above the flood line — low roofs, smoke holes, laundry lines strung between pilings. People. A dozen figures standing motionless at the edges of the village, watching them the way deer watch a treeline.

Nobody moved. Nobody spoke. The only sound was the storm raging on the far side of the ridge, muffled and furious, like a dog chained outside a house.

"Weapons down," Orsen said quietly, to her own crew as much as anyone. "All of you. Hands visible."

"They're going to think we're fleet," Dain murmured.

"Then we'd better be quick about convincing them we're not."

An old woman came down the near gangplank before Orsen could decide how to approach. She walked with a stick but without hurry, wrapped in a fisherman's oilskin three sizes too big for her, and she stopped ten feet from the barge's gunwale and looked up at them one face at a time — Maren, Tam, Dain, and last Orsen, whose hand was nowhere near a weapon and whose expression, she hoped, said *we're tired and lost and mean no harm*, because that much was true.

"You're not salvage fleet," the old woman said. Her voice was dry as reeds. "Fleet doesn't run the timber channels. Fleet doesn't know them."

"Maren Oduya's granddaughter knows them," Maren called down. "Ilena Oduya's granddaughter."

Something shifted in the old woman's face. "Ilena Oduya ferried my mother across the Vessa delta the week before the war ended. Carried forty of us in a boat built for twelve." She looked at Maren for a long moment. "You have her hands."

"She died two winters back."

"Then she died free, which is more than most of us got." The old woman turned her attention back to Orsen. "You're running from the fleet. That puts you either criminals or fools, and fools don't run the timber at night in squall season. So. Criminals, then. What did you steal?"

Orsen weighed the question, and how much truth it could bear, and decided — for reasons she didn't fully examine — to give it all of it.

"A chest out of the floodwaters. Stamped with the seal of Vessany."

The change that went through the village was physical. Orsen felt it the way you feel weather shift — a pressure change, a held breath passing through a dozen bodies at once. Somewhere behind the old woman, a man made a sound low in his throat. Two children appeared at a window and were pulled back by their collars.

"Show me," the old woman said.

They brought the chest down to the stilt-walk. Orsen opened it herself, slowly, palms out first, and lifted the brass tide-key into the gray light. It caught what little light remained and threw it back warm and gold, and around the walkway people began — quietly, without any order given — to gather.

The old woman reached out and touched it with one finger, the way you'd touch a sleeping child. Then she pressed the finger to her own lips.

"That's the key of the eastern vault," she said. "I haven't seen one since I was nine years old. My father wore its twin around his neck when they took him." She looked up, and her eyes were wet and absolutely steady. "Where did you find this?"

"Flooded shallows, north of Lowmark. Hooked it fishing."

"The river gives back what it's ready to give." The old woman nodded slowly, as if confirming something with herself. "I am Sefa Vessane. My family kept the vault records, before. Come inside. All of you. You'll eat, and you'll dry, and then we'll talk — because if you're carrying that key toward the Lament, there are things you need to know, and some of them will keep you alive."

* * *

The longhouse sat on stilts above the highest flood mark, built of salvaged timber and woven reed, warm inside with a peat fire and smelling of smoked fish and tar. Maybe thirty people lived here, Orsen gathered — Vessany survivors and their children and grandchildren, three generations living off the grid in a channel no chart showed, because no chartmaker had ever been paid to look.

Tam sat closest to the fire and couldn't stop looking at the walls. They were hung with drawings — charcoal and ochre on stretched hide and salvaged paper — and Orsen watched him recognize what they were.

"Charts," he said softly. "Hand-drawn charts. Of the delta. Of the old shipping lanes." He stood, crossed to the nearest one, and his fingers hovered an inch from the surface without touching. "These are accurate. These are *accurate* — the soundings, the seasonal shifts — who drew these?"

"Our grandmothers," Sefa said. "From memory. From the memory of their mothers. We redraw them every generation so the knowledge doesn't die with the oldest mouth holding it." She studied Tam with sudden interest. "You read charts?"

"I draw them." He hesitated. "I drew the ones Corvane used to hunt your people's wrecks. Forged Vessany originals so his salvage masters would believe his coordinates. That's why I was in irons in Lowmark." He said it flatly, without flinching, and Orsen gave him credit for that. "I've spent four years wishing I could unmake every line."

Sefa looked at him for a long time. Then she said, "Then perhaps you'll spend the next four making lines that undo them," and turned away, and Tam sat back down very quietly, and Orsen saw him press his palms flat against his knees like a man steadying himself.

Food came — smoked fish, dark bread, a broth of river greens that tasted better than it had any right to. Children crept closer to stare at Dain, who sat awkwardly with his fine clothes ruined and his father's ledger wrapped in oilcloth beside him, looking like exactly what he was: a rich man's son eating poor people's bread and not knowing where to put his elbows. One of the children, a girl of six or seven, finally approached and asked him if he was a soldier. He said no. She asked if he was a thief. He glanced at Orsen, and Orsen raised an eyebrow, and he said, "A little bit," and the girl laughed like that was the best thing she'd heard all year, and Dain smiled — really smiled, for the first time since Orsen had known him — and something in her chest she didn't want to name shifted an inch.

* * *

Later, when the fire had burned down and the children were asleep along the walls under shared blankets, Sefa took Orsen aside to a corner hung with drying nets, and there she asked to see the key again.

She held it in both hands this time, turning it, reading it like text. "Eastern vault. Third of three." She looked up. "You know what the keys open."

"The Lament's hold. We have the manifest. We have a second key already recovered from the wreck itself." Orsen paused. "We don't have the third."

"No," Sefa said. "You don't. And here is the thing you need to understand, mountain woman, the thing that has kept my people alive and quiet for twenty years while Corvane picked our nation's bones." She set the key down between them on a netting barrel. "The third key was never lost. Never sunk. Never hidden in any wreck or cave or grave."

Orsen felt the shape of it before the words arrived, the way you feel a rockfall start before you hear it.

"It sits on Aldric Corvane's right hand," Sefa said. "He wears it as a signet ring. Has worn it since the year of the drowning. He pried it from the hand of the last Keeper of the eastern vault — a boy of nineteen, who died on the deck of the *Lament* with his nation's treasury sealed beneath him." Her voice never rose. That was the terrible thing about it. It stayed level as a carpenter's line. "Corvane wears our dead around his finger, and signs his magistrate's decrees with it, and every survivor of Vessany who has ever knelt in his court has had to look at it and say nothing."

The fire popped. Out in the dark, the storm was dying, the wind unraveling thread by thread.

"He keeps it close," Orsen said. "Of course he does. It's proof. Anyone who sees it up close—"

"Sees nothing. To the fleet captains it's a magistrate's signet, a little gaudy, a little old-fashioned. Only someone who knew what to look for would see the tide-marks on the bezel." Sefa's eyes were sharp. "But you know what to look for now. And you have two keys. Which means you'll be thinking about the third, and I'm telling you plainly: if you go after it, you won't be stealing from a vault or diving a wreck. You'll be taking a ring off the hand of the most powerful man on this river, in the middle of his own citadel, surrounded by the fleet he owns."

"I've heard worse odds."

"You've said worse things in taverns, I imagine. That doesn't make them true." Sefa leaned forward, and the firelight carved deep lines around her mouth. "Listen to me. Twenty years ago there were thousands of us. Now there are villages like this one — eleven that I know of, scattered from the delta to the headwaters, living off the charts and off the channels, invisible. We survived by staying small and staying silent. But we never stopped keeping the names." She tapped the barrel top. "Every family the fleet robbed. Every ship Corvane sank. Every name on every manifest — my people memorized them, generation to generation, the way you'd memorize scripture. If you reach that vault, if you bring back what's ours, there are people alive today who can match every coin to every claimant. The gold isn't treasure, mountain woman. It's restitution. And restitution needs its ledger."

Orsen thought of the manifests in the chest. Thought of Tam's forged charts and Maren's inherited channels and Dain's stolen ledger, each of them a fragment of the same record, scattered like the survivors themselves.

"You're offering to help us."

"I'm offering you the truth, which is worth more." Sefa straightened, joints cracking, and looked at Orsen with an expression that was almost — almost — gentle. "You hooked a chest out of the river and thought you'd found treasure. But the river doesn't deal in treasure. It deals in debts. And it has chosen you to collect one."

Outside, the last of the squall was moving off east, dragging its thunder with it. Orsen stood at the longhouse door and watched the clouds tear open over the black water, and behind her the village slept — a drowned nation breathing quietly in the willows, keeping its names, waiting twenty years for someone fool enough to come asking.

Dain appeared at her shoulder, sleepless, the ledger under his arm.

"You heard," she said.

"Some." He was quiet a moment. "My father wears it. I've seen it my whole life. I kissed that ring when I was a boy, at the winter court. Everyone kisses the ring." His jaw worked. "I kissed Vessany's dead and thanked him for it."

Orsen looked at him — at the son of the man who'd drowned a nation, standing in a survivor village eating their bread — and found, to her irritation, that she believed him.

"Then tomorrow," she said, "you show me everything you know about that ring. Because we're going to need to take it off his hand."

Far downstream, faint on the night wind, a signal horn sounded twice — fleet code, distant but unmistakable — and every soul in the village who wasn't asleep pretended to be.
