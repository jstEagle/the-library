## 12. Third Tide: Betrayal at Anchor

Maren felt the betrayal before she saw it — the way you feel a storm two bends downriver, a pressure behind the ears, the water going quiet in the wrong way.

She was mending net on the barge's stern, third tide rising slow and brown beneath her, when Wick came up the plank road from the fishing camp with his collar up and his eyes anywhere but on her. He'd been Tam's hire back in Lowmark: a wiry salvage-hand with quick fingers and a laugh like gravel in a tin cup. Good with lines. Good with engines. Good, apparently, at lying.

"You're up early," she said.

"Couldn't sleep." He said it to the river, not to her.

Maren had ferried smugglers and debt-runners and one bishop in disguise, and in twenty years of it she'd learned that people who couldn't sleep told you why with their hands before their mouths caught up. Wick's hands were in his pockets. His boots were wet past the ankle, though there'd been no rain and he hadn't been near the water since dusk.

The camp lay half a mile downstream, tucked in the willows where the Vessany survivors smoked fish on racks. Old Sefa's people. The ones who'd fed them, hidden them, given Orsen the warning about Corvane's ring.

"Where'd you walk?" Maren asked, mild as milk.

"Nowhere."

"Mm." She went back to her net. "River tells me things, Wick. Always has. It's telling me something now."

He laughed — that gravel laugh, a beat too late. "You're a spooky woman, ferrywoman."

"I'm a honest one. That's spookier."

He went below. Maren sat with her net and watched the current carry a smear of ash downstream, thin as spilled tea, and knew.

* * *

She found Orsen at the bow, coiling rope in the grey light, doing it the way she did everything — precisely, silently, like the rope might report back to someone if she rushed.

"Smoke on the water," Maren said. "Going downstream. Fresh."

Orsen's hands didn't stop. "Camp smoke?"

"Cook-fire smoke doesn't smell like pitch and oil." Maren leaned on the rail. "And it doesn't come off a man's boots."

That stopped her. Orsen looked up, and Maren watched her do the arithmetic — the same arithmetic Maren had done, arriving at the same ugly sum.

"Wick," they said together.

* * *

They didn't shout about it. That was the thing about Orsen Montrose that Maren was still learning, three tides into knowing her: she didn't rage, didn't posture, didn't perform. She went still, and then she went to work.

By full light they had Wick on deck with the crew in a half-circle around him — Tam pale and furious, Dain leaning against the mast with his arms crossed and his face carefully empty, Maren herself by the tiller because somebody had to hold the boat steady while the people on it shook apart.

Tam had hired him. That was the wound under the wound. Tam kept saying it, quiet, like a man counting stitches: "I vetted him. I vetted him myself. He ran salvage out of Lowmark for six years, clean record, no fleet ties—"

"Clean records are for sale too," Dain said. "You'd know. You forged enough of them."

"Dain." Orsen's voice, flat as a keel.

Wick stood in the middle of it all with his chin up, which Maren had to give him credit for. Guilty men who grovel lived longer sometimes. Guilty men who stood straight had already decided how this ended.

"Say it," Orsen told him. "All of it. I'll know if you trim it."

Wick's jaw worked. "A patrol skiff came into Lowmark the night before we shipped. Fleet colors, running dark. A lieutenant. He had my sister's name." He swallowed. "My sister's in the Lowmark debtor cells. Three years for a salvage tax she couldn't pay. The lieutenant said — he said she walks free when I send word where the barge runs. One message. That's all it cost."

"And you sent it," Maren said.

"I sent it." His voice cracked on the second word. "Two nights ago. From the camp, after dark. There's a signal post on the bluff — a lamp code the fleet uses on this stretch. My father ran lamps on the Rivergate chain before the war. I know the patterns."

Nobody spoke. The river spoke instead, slapping the hull, patient, indifferent.

"The camp," Tam said hoarsely. "You gave them the camp?"

"They already knew about us. They wanted the camp because—" Wick's face folded. "Because the lieutenant said a burned camp reads better than a captured barge. Says it makes you run scared, make mistakes. Says the old woman's people would blame you for bringing the fleet down on them." He looked at Orsen. "I'm sorry. I'd say it fancier if I could, but that's the whole of it. I sold you, and I'd sell you again tomorrow, because they have my sister and none of you do."

* * *

It was Dain who broke the silence, and Maren noted — filed away — that it was the magistrate's son who understood what came next before anyone else did.

"He's right about the timing," Dain said. "If the patrol has our position, why haven't they hit us? Because they don't want the barge yet. They want us to lead them to the Lament's vault. We're the hound they're letting run." His eyes went to Wick. "But the camp — that was a message. And messages get answered."

"Answered how?" Tam asked.

Dain looked at Orsen. So did everyone else. That was the shape the crew had taken without anyone announcing it: every hard question bent toward her now, the way water bends toward the low place.

Orsen set down the rope coil. Slowly. Like laying down a weapon she could pick back up.

"Options," she said. "We kill him. Quick, over the side, weighted. That's what a fleet captain does. That's what Corvane would do."

"No," Maren said. Not because she loved the man. Because she'd buried enough bodies in that river to know each one cost more than it paid.

"I know," Orsen said. "I'm listing options, not taking them." Her eyes stayed on Wick. "Second option: we maroon him. Leave him on the bluff with a knife and his regrets."

"He'd be picked up by the patrol inside a day," Dain said. "Back in their hands, telling them everything he knows about us — which is everything."

"Third option." Orsen crouched, unhurried, until she was eye-level with Wick, and Maren saw the salvage-hand's bravado finally crack, because there is a particular terror in a calm woman at eye-level. "Third option is the contract."

"There's no contract," Wick whispered.

"There was. Day you signed on in Lowmark. Tam wrote it; I witnessed it. Standard terms — you break faith with the crew, the crew breaks faith with you. Every soul on this boat signed the same paper, including me." She glanced at Tam. "Read it to him."

Tam's hands shook getting the folded page out of his coat. He read it in a voice like dry leaves:

*"Any hand who sells the crew's course, cargo, or company to an enemy forfeits share, wage, and name. He shall be put ashore alive, provided with boat, bread, and water for seven days, and shall not be pursued, harmed, or aided by the crew thereafter. His name shall not be spoken aboard again."*

"That's it?" Wick blinked. "That's — exile? Just exile?"

"You thought we'd drown you," Maren said. "Disappointed?"

"I don't understand. Why would you—" He stopped. Looked around at them. "Corvane would hang me."

"Corvane isn't here," Orsen said. She rose. "That's rather the point of all this."

* * *

They put him ashore at midday, third tide near its peak, at a shingle bar forty miles downstream of anything worth betraying.

Maren steered the barge in herself. She didn't know why — except that she did know, and didn't like knowing it. Somebody should mark the moment properly. Somebody who wasn't angry. Tam couldn't; he took the traitor personal, and grief made him cruel. Dain wouldn't; Dain's mercy had a magistrate's arithmetic in it, cold sums about what Wick might still tell the fleet. And Orsen — Orsen had ordered the exile with a face like a locked door, and Maren had spent long enough on the river to know that locked doors floated on deep water.

So it was the witch-ferrywoman who ran the plank out onto the shingle, and handed Wick a canvas pack: bread, salt meat, a water skin, a patched skiff with one good oar and one fair.

"Seven days' food," she said. "Skiff's sound below the waterline. I checked her twice."

"Why?" Wick's voice was raw. "After what I did. Why check her twice?"

Maren looked at him a long moment. The river moved behind him, brown and endless, carrying its ash and its secrets.

"Because my mother drowned in this river," she said, "and the men who let her boat sink walked away rich, and nobody exiled them, nobody hung them, nobody even named them. They just went on being respectable until they died warm." She pushed the pack against his chest. "I've got no room in me for becoming them. Take your sister north when they spring her. Cross the border. Don't ever let me hear your gravel laugh again."

Wick's eyes were wet. "The lieutenant — he'll ask me where you're headed next. When they collect me."

"Tell him true," Maren said. "Tell him we're running the Lament on the fourth tide. Tell him whatever you like. It won't matter." She turned back toward the barge. "Water finds its level, boy. So do I."

* * *

The crew didn't speak for the first hour back on the water. That was the fracture, and Maren felt it in her bones the way she felt bad weather: not broken, exactly, but cracked along a seam that would never quite close.

Tam sat at the chart table and didn't look at anyone. He'd lost a hire and gained a lesson, and both were bleeding him. Twice Maren caught his lips moving — *I vetted him, I vetted him* — a prayer to a god of thoroughness who had never once answered.

Dain cleaned his father's signal lantern, piece by piece, laid out on a cloth like surgery. He worked in silence, but Maren saw him watching Orsen across the deck, and she recognized the look. It was the look of a man recalculating. He'd joined this crew betting on its ruthlessness, maybe — betting that people willing to steal from Corvane would be willing to match him method for method. And instead he'd watched them hand a traitor a boat and bread.

He didn't know yet whether he'd bet wrong. Neither, Maren suspected, did he want to find out too fast.

And Orsen stood at the bow the whole watch, saying nothing, doing nothing, being the fixed point the whole cracked crew swung around.

Near dusk Maren went forward and stood beside her. The fourth tide would begin turning in the small hours. Behind them, somewhere upriver, the Silt Gates garrison's word was already traveling; ahead, somewhere downriver, a fleet waited with a burned camp's smoke still hanging over it like a signature.

"You did the harder thing," Maren said.

"Did I?" Orsen didn't turn. "Half of me wanted the weighted sack. It would've been cleaner. Cheaper. Everyone sleeps better."

"But?"

"But then we're just another crew with rules that bend when it matters." Orsen's jaw tightened. "Corvane kills problems. Every man on that fleet follows him because killing problems is easy and profitable. If we win by being him, we haven't won anything. We've just changed whose flag flies."

Maren studied her profile against the dying light. "You know what it costs, though. Trust. Tam's doubting himself. Dain's doubting you. By morning half this crew will be wondering if mercy was wisdom or weakness."

"I know."

"And you did it anyway."

"I did it anyway." Now Orsen turned, and there was something in her face Maren hadn't seen before — not softness, nothing so simple. More like a load-bearing wall. "The survivors trusted us, Maren. Sefa's people fed us and we brought the fleet to their door. If I start spending lives to buy safety, I'm just Corvane with a smaller ledger." She looked downriver, toward the canyon, toward the Lament sleeping in its grave of silt. "Fourth tide turns at moonrise. We run for the wreck tonight, and we dive tomorrow, and if the fleet wants us, they can come explain themselves to the whole canyon at once."

"That's not a plan. That's a dare."

"It's both." Orsen almost smiled. It didn't reach far. "Get some sleep, ferrywoman. I need your hands on the tiller at moonrise, and I need the rest of you somewhere the cracks can't reach."

Maren lingered a moment longer. Downstream, the first stars were coming out over the black line of the gorge, and beneath them — faint, at the very edge of hearing, or maybe only imagination — the river carried a sound she knew too well: oar-strokes, many, keeping time.

The fleet wasn't waiting anymore.

It was coming.
