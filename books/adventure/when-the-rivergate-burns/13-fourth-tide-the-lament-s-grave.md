## 13. Fourth Tide: The Lament's Grave

The canyon announced itself before they saw it — a change in the river's voice, the water going from a broad mutter to a low, grinding roar, as if the whole current had dropped its shoulders and decided to push. Maren stood at the tiller with her head cocked, listening the way other people read.

"Stone on both sides now," she said. "Silt shelf on the north wall, maybe four fathoms down. The river's been chewing at it for twenty years." She glanced back at Orsen. "Your wreck's in there. I can feel it the way you feel a bad tooth."

The fourth tide had begun falling at dawn. By the manifest's arithmetic — Tam had done the sums three times, lips moving, charcoal stub clenched in his fist — the water over the Lament would drop below six feet by midday and stay there until the fifth tide turned tomorrow morning. One day. One window. Orsen stood at the bow and watched the canyon walls rise on either side of them, sheer planes of grey stone streaked with the high-water marks of two decades, and tried not to think about how narrow the window was, or how many things could go wrong inside it.

The Lament had been a refugee ship. That was the word Tam kept using, quietly, like a man handling broken glass. Not a treasure ship. Not a warship. A ship full of families who had paid everything they had to escape a country that was burning, and who had been sent to the bottom of a river by the man whose colors flew over the Rivergate citadel.

Orsen had pulled a drowned nation out of a flood channel with a fishhook. She had told herself it was salvage. Standing at the bow now, watching the canyon open ahead of them like a throat, she understood she had been lying to herself. This was a grave. They were about to rob a grave, and the only thing that made it different from any other robbery was what they meant to do with what they took.

"Anchor line's ready," Dain said beside her. He had the lead weight and line coiled over one shoulder, the diving hood and the brass-bound air barrel lashed to the rail. He'd been quiet all morning. Not sulking — Orsen had learned the difference. This was the quiet of a man doing arithmetic of a different kind: how much of his father lived in him, and what he was willing to do about it.

"You don't have to dive," she said.

"I know." He checked the knot on the air line without looking at her. "I want to see it. I need to know what he did. Not from a ledger. From the water."

Orsen didn't answer that. There wasn't an answer that helped.

Maren brought the barge in against the north wall, where the current eddied behind a spur of rock, and dropped both anchors with the economy of a woman who had moored in worse places during worse weather. The canyon narrowed ahead of them into a bend they couldn't see around. The water there ran green and deep, then shallowed over a sill of silt that gleamed like wet slate in the midday light.

"There." Tam was at the rail with the manifest open, his finger trembling slightly against the paper. "The Lament drove onto the sill during the sinking. The manifest says she listed hard to port, held, then the hull let go along the seams. The hold would have settled into the silt — sealed, if we're lucky. The superstructure broke up." He looked up, and his eyes were wet, which embarrassed him visibly. "There were four hundred and eleven people aboard. The manifest lists them. All of them. Names, ages, what they paid for passage."

Nobody said anything. The river said it for them, grinding against the stone.

* * *

They dove in shifts, Orsen and Dain first, Maren on the air bellows, Tam watching the canyon mouth with a spyglass because someone had to.

The cold hit Orsen like a wall. The river in flood season was snowmelt and shadow, and even with the hood and the greased leathers, the first plunge squeezed the breath out of her. She went down the anchor line hand over hand, Dain a body-length above her, and the light went from grey to green to a brown murk where her eyes were nearly useless and her hands did all the seeing.

The Lament's hull rose out of the silt like the spine of some drowned animal. Orsen's fingers found the planking and followed it — oak, still sound in places, rotten in others, furred with twenty years of river growth. She worked along the port side toward the breach Tam's charts promised, and when her hand passed over the edge of the torn seam, she stopped.

She could feel the ship. Not just the wreck — the ship. The way the deck planks had been scrubbed. The carved initials near what had been a hatch combing, cut by some passenger with a knife, two letters and a heart, the kind of thing a child does to say *I was here*. Her glove rested on it for a long moment while the current tugged at her like a hand on her sleeve.

Then the seam, and the dark beyond it, and she went in.

The hold was a cathedral of silt and shadow. Her lantern — the shuttered diver's lamp Maren had rigged — pushed back just enough of it to show her the shape of the space: cargo cradles collapsed, a shifted mass of ballast stone, and everywhere the soft grey drift of two decades settling over everything like snow that would never melt. And bones. Not many. The dead had mostly been washed out through the breach or taken by the river, but a few remained — small ones, in the aft corner, tucked together the way people huddle in cold.

Orsen hung in the water and let the grief come, because it was going to come anyway and it was better down here where no one could see her face. Four hundred and eleven. She thought of the child's carved initials above her, somewhere in the dark overhead. She thought of the old woman in the survivor village, and the way she had said *we were a country once* — not with bitterness, but with the plainness of someone stating the weather.

Then she made herself move. Grief was a luxury the tide didn't allow. The dropping water had a schedule, and the fleet had its own, and both of them were shorter than hers.

The strongroom was aft, behind a bulkhead door that had held when nothing else did. The lock was Vessany work — Tam had described it, drawn it from memory, drilled her on it for two nights — and her fingers, half-numb, worked the mechanism by feel while her lungs burned and the lamp guttered. The lock gave on the fourth try.

Inside: the second tide-key, wrapped in oilcloth and lashed to a ledger box, exactly where a captain who knew he was dying would put it so that someone might someday find it. Orsen took both. She also took the bundle beneath them — the survivor manifests, the true ones, the lists of every family Corvane had robbed — because Tam had said they'd be there and because names, Orsen was learning, were a kind of treasure too.

She was halfway back through the breach when her lamp swept the aft corner one last time, and she saw the small bones again, and beside them a tin whistle on a rotted cord.

She took that too. She didn't know why. She took it anyway.

* * *

Dain surfaced second, coughing, and Maren hauled him over the rail by the collar with a grip that would have cracked a weaker man's ribs. Orsen came up a minute later, and the first thing she saw was Tam's face — not looking at her, looking past her, at the canyon mouth.

The spyglass was still in his hand. His knuckles were white around it.

"How many?" Orsen said, water streaming off her.

"Three masts. Maybe four." His voice was flat, the flatness of a man holding himself together with both hands. "Sloops of the fleet pattern. They're a mile out, maybe less. They haven't rounded the bend yet."

The tide window had six hours left in it. The fleet had arrived with five to spare, which meant they'd known. Which meant the garrison commander at the Silt Gates had sent word ahead, exactly as Dain had feared, and Corvane's ships had been running the river for two days to close the trap.

Orsen looked at the brass tide-key in her fist, still cold from the deep water. Then at the canyon walls, rising sheer on both sides. Then at Maren, who was already at the tiller, reading the current with her whole body, the way she did, like a woman listening to an argument she intended to win.

"They'll block the mouth," Dain said. "Chain across it, if they're smart. Boarding parties if they're not."

"They're not smart," Maren said. "They're Corvane's. That means they're cruel, and cruelty's predictable." She spat over the side. "But they've got guns, and we've got a barge that leaks when you look at it wrong."

Orsen wrung out her hair and put the second key in the oilskin wallet with the first, next to the manifests, next to the whistle she still couldn't explain to herself. The fleet's masts were coming around the bend. She could see the lead sloop now, its rail lined with men, its bow gun run out.

"Tam," she said. "The charts. Is there another way out of this canyon?"

Tam was already unrolling them, his hands steadier now that they had a job to do, the way they always were. His finger traced the north wall, the silt shelf, the spur of rock.

"There's a channel," he said slowly. "Behind the spur. It's not on Corvane's charts because it's not on anyone's charts — it's a flood cut, maybe ten feet wide at high water, and we're not at high water." He looked up at her. "It's a rapids, Orsen. A bad one. Maren would have to run it blind, on sound alone, in a canyon that narrows to a boat's width."

Maren smiled. It was not a comforting smile.

"Then it's a good thing," she said, "that I've never once in my life needed a chart."

The lead sloop's bow gun spoke, and the first ball skipped off the water a hundred yards short, throwing up a column of spray that caught the light like a breaking wave. The hunt had found its prey. Orsen put her hand on the tiller beside Maren's and felt the barge answer, swinging toward the narrow dark of the flood cut, toward the sound of white water ahead.

"Hold on to the keys," she said. "Everything else, we can lose."
