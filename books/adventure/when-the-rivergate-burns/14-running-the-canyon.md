## 14. Running the Canyon

Dain Corvane had spent his whole life learning how his father thought. He had never once imagined the skill would save his life.

The first shot came off the canyon wall like a wasp finding his ear.

"Down!" Orsen's voice, flat and certain, and Dain was already moving — flattening against the deck planks beside Tam as the ball spanged off the barge rail and whined away into the gorge. Above them, the canyon walls rose sheer and rust-streaked, close enough on either side that he could have thrown a stone at either bank. Close enough that every rifleman up there could see them plain as a target painted on water.

"Range is closing," Maren called from the stern, hands moving on the wheel without hurry, as if she were steering through a churchyard instead of a shooting gallery. "They'll hold the high shelf at the narrows. That's where they'll try to kill us."

"Cheerful," said Tam, who was bleeding.

It wasn't bad. A ball had kissed him along the outside of the left arm when they'd first rounded the bend into the gorge and found the fleet's advance skiffs already waiting, lanterns doused, tucked into a shale cutout like spiders in a wall crack. The graze had opened a seam of red down his sleeve and turned his face the color of tallow, but he'd tied it off himself with a strip of sailcloth and refused to sit down. Now he crouched by the midships cargo hatch with the second tide-key wrapped in oilcloth against his chest, one hand pressed to his arm, and looked up at Dain with an expression Dain couldn't read at all.

"You said you knew their codes," Tam said.

"I know *some* of them."

"Then now would be the time."

* * *

Here was what Dain knew, and hated knowing: his father ran the salvage fleet like a ledger. Every patrol skiff answered to a signal hierarchy, and every signal had a meaning drilled into the crews until they obeyed them asleep. Three lanterns raised and dropped twice meant *hold position*. A blue flare meant *quarry sighted*. And there was one code, the oldest one, reserved for the magistrate's own vessels — a pattern so restricted that only fleet captains knew it existed.

Dain knew it because he'd been twelve years old, standing on his father's balcony over the Rivergate harbor, watching the captains drill below, and he had asked what the green-over-white-over-green meant. His father had put a hand on his shoulder — the only time he could remember his father touching him without calculation behind it — and said, *That one means the river itself has spoken. It means every ship answers.*

He hadn't understood then. He understood now. It meant: clear the channel. It meant: something more important than any of you is coming through, and if you're in its way, that is your error, not its.

"They won't question it," Dain said, digging in the signal locker for the flare kit. "Not from me. I've got my father's face and his seal-ring's twin on my coat button. If I fire the command pattern from this deck, the skiffs will fall back and hold the mouth of the gorge open for us."

Orsen crouched across from him, hair plastered dark with spray, and studied him the way she studied terrain — looking for the loose rock, the hidden drop. "And when they realize it's false?"

"By then we're past the narrows."

"And if we're not?"

"Then we die in a canyon," Dain said, "and it won't matter what anyone believed."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth. Not quite a smile. Acknowledgment, maybe. "Load it, then."

* * *

Maren took the narrows the way a knife takes a sheath.

Dain had heard the stories about her before he ever met her — everyone who worked the river had — the ferrywoman who'd run the Blackpool rapids backwards, the witch who'd talked a flood out of drowning a village, though nobody agreed on which village or which flood. He'd filed the stories under superstition, the way his father had taught him to file everything that couldn't be taxed or shot.

Then he stood on her deck and watched her take the barge into a chute of white water no wider than the vessel itself, walls rising on both sides, and understood that every story was true and also undersold.

She didn't fight the current. That was the thing. Every pilot Dain had ever watched fought the river — hauled against it, cursed it, treated it as an enemy to be beaten. Maren leaned into it. She read the water the way Tam read charts, three bends ahead, and where the current shoved, she let it shove, and where it curled, she slipped the hull into the curl like a hand into a glove. Twice the barge's gunwale came within a hand's width of the rock. Both times she'd already known it would, and both times she'd already begun correcting before the near-miss happened, so smoothly that the crew only felt the river's roar change pitch.

"Hold!" she shouted over the noise. "Whatever you're doing with that flare — hold until I clear the chute! They'll never see it over the spray!"

The narrows came fast. The fleet skiffs were there, just as she'd predicted — four of them drawn up on the high shelf above the throat of the gorge, crews kneeling behind stacked cargo, two swivel guns cranked around to bear on the channel. Dain saw a captain's hat among them, gold-braided, and felt his stomach turn over. He knew that silhouette. Captain Vess of the *Charity's Wage*, who had eaten dinner at their table a hundred times, who had taught Dain knots when he was nine.

He raised the flare pistol anyway.

Green. White. Green.

The charge went up through the canyon spray and burst above the narrows, and even over the water-noise Dain heard the shout go up from the shelf — confusion first, then the sharp bark of the captain's voice, then movement. The swivel guns swung *away*. Men scrambled off the firing line. Somewhere above, Vess was screaming a question no one could answer, because the answer was impossible: the command pattern didn't come from patrol skiffs. It came from the magistrate's own hand.

"Now, Maren!"

"Now is right." She put the wheel hard over, and the barge went through the narrows like a swallowed coin.

They scraped. Of course they scraped — the starboard rail shrieked along rock for one endless second, timber peeling away in a long pale ribbon, and Dain was thrown against the mast and caught a fistful of rigging and held on while the world became white noise and spray. Then the chute spat them out the far side into calmer water, and the canyon opened half a boat-length, and they were through.

Behind them, above, the fleet skiffs sat motionless on their shelf, holding position, clearing the channel — exactly as ordered — while their quarry ran out from under their guns.

Tam started laughing. It was a ragged, disbelieving sound, half pain, and he clutched his bandaged arm and laughed harder. "Your father's codes," he managed. "You beat him with his own alphabet."

Dain found he was grinning. He couldn't remember the last time he'd grinned. "Don't tell him. He'll disown me."

"He already did," Orsen said quietly, from the bow. But she was smiling too, and it changed her whole face — took ten years of flint off it — and Dain thought, absurdly, *so that's what she looks like.*

* * *

The canyon kept its own schedule.

Maren had warned them at dawn: the fourth tide was falling, and falling fast, and the Lament's gorge drained like a basin with a pulled plug. Every hour the water dropped another foot, and every foot exposed new rock — new teeth for the hull, new shallows where a loaded barge would ground and become a fixed target for whatever part of the fleet came next.

So they ran. There was nothing else to do but run, and after the narrows, running felt almost easy. Almost joyful. Dain worked the bow sounding-line, calling depths to Maren — *two fathoms, two and a quarter, rock on the left* — and Orsen stood at his shoulder reading the canyon walls, calling the bends before they appeared, and Tam, one-armed and stubborn, fed the little stern stove dry fuel so Maren could keep steam pressure up for the worst chutes. Nobody gave orders. Nobody needed to. The barge needed five things done at once, and five people did them, and somewhere in the third mile of the gorge Dain realized he had stopped thinking of them as *the cartographer*, *the ferrywoman*, *the guide*, and started thinking of them as simply *us*.

His father would have called it weakness. Crews were instruments. Instruments were replaceable.

His father had never run a canyon.

*Two fathoms!* Dain called, hauling the line. *Shoaling fast —*

"I see it!" Maren threw the wheel over and the barge slewed between two drowned pines, their dead branches combing the deck like fingers, and then the channel bent hard right into a stretch of water gone the color of milk-chocolate, boiling over hidden bars.

The shot came from behind them.

Dain spun. Far upstream, rounding the bend they'd cleared twenty minutes ago, came a long black pursuit skiff — not a patrol boat but a courier, lean and low, built for speed, with a bow chaser already smoking from its second discharge. The ball fell short, skipped once, and screamed past the stern close enough to wet them with spray.

"Courier boat," Orsen snapped. "They figured out the flare was false."

"How long?"

"Twenty minutes. Maybe less. They're faster than us in this water."

Maren's jaw set. "Then we give them water they can't run."

What she did next, Dain would think about for years. She steered *off* the safe channel. Deliberately. Into a stretch of broken water where the dropping tide had begun to expose a maze of gravel bars and drowned snags, and she threaded it — full confidence, no hesitation, reading the boils and slicks on the surface the way other people read print — trusting that anything chasing her would be going too fast to read it at all.

The courier came on anyway. Its pilot was good. Not good enough. Two hundred yards into the broken water the skiff's hull struck a snag with a crack like a musket volley, slewed sideways, and began shipping water, its crew scrambling, its bow chaser dipping under the brown flood.

Nobody cheered. Dain noticed that. Tam lifted his good hand — just briefly, a salute to the men in the water, who were enemies and also rivermen, and the river didn't care about the difference. Maren touched two fingers to her brow. Even Orsen inclined her head a degree.

Then the current took them around the next bend, and the wrecked skiff was gone, and the gorge narrowed again, and the fourth tide fell another foot beneath their keel.

* * *

They cleared the canyon mouth at dusk.

The walls fell away on both sides like hands opening, and the gorge spilled them out onto a broad gray reach of river under a sky going bruise-colored in the west. Behind them, the last light showed the canyon mouth closing — not with stone, but with water. The fourth tide, meeting the incoming sea-swell far downstream, backed up into the gorge, and the lower channels they'd threaded drowned one by one, white water smoothing over into deep, impassable flood. In an hour, maybe two, nothing larger than a canoe would enter or leave that canyon until the next window.

Nothing was following them out. Nothing could.

Maren brought the barge alongside a shingle bar sheltered by a stand of dead cottonwoods, and they tied off, and for a long moment nobody spoke. The engine ticked as it cooled. Water dripped off the gunwales. Somewhere upstream, faint as memory, a bird cried.

Tam sat down heavily on the cargo hatch and unwrapped his arm, and Orsen knelt to re-dress the graze without being asked, her hands quick and gentle in a way Dain hadn't seen her use on anything living. Maren stayed at the wheel a moment longer than she needed to, forehead nearly touching the worn wood, saying something silent to the river or to whatever she believed lived in it. Then she came aft and sat among them, and produced from somewhere inside her coat a dented flask, and passed it around without a word.

Dain drank last. The liquor was terrible. It was the best thing he'd ever tasted.

"We lost the rail," Maren said finally. "Starboard side, six feet of planking. And the stove pipe's cracked. And I don't love our draft with the tide still falling."

"We'll care about that tomorrow," Orsen said.

"Tomorrow we're in open river with a fleet behind us and a citadel ahead of us."

"Tomorrow," Orsen repeated, and there was steel under the softness of it, and Maren — to Dain's mild astonishment — subsided.

Tam laughed again, quieter this time, and lay back against the hatch with his bandaged arm cradled against his chest. "You know what I keep thinking?" he said to the darkening sky. "I spent six years in a Lowmark cell drawing charts of a country that doesn't exist anymore, telling myself somebody, someday, would sail them. And today your ferrywoman sailed one of my channels better than I drew it." He turned his head and looked at each of them in turn. "I'm just saying. Whatever happens at Rivergate. Today was worth the cell."

Nobody answered that, because there wasn't anything to say that wouldn't have broken it, and the silence held instead, warm as a shared blanket.

Dain sat with his back against the mast and let the exhaustion come, and with it something else, something he distrusted even as it settled into him like ballast. Belonging. Four days ago he'd been riding alone with a stolen ledger and a death wish, betting his life that strangers would be less cruel to him than his own blood. Now his arm ached where the mast had slammed it, and Tam's blood was on the deck planks, and Maren's flask was in his pocket, and Orsen was watching the canyon mouth with the tide-key wrapped safe against her ribs, and he understood — with the simple clarity of a man who has just survived something — that he would take a bullet for any of the three of them and consider it a fair trade.

His father had built an empire on the principle that people were levers. Dain had just learned, in one afternoon of gunfire and white water, that they were also anchors. You could be dragged down by them. You could also, apparently, be held steady by them in a current that would otherwise sweep you away.

He was still working out which kind he wanted to be when Orsen spoke.

"Dain." Her voice had changed. All the warmth was gone out of it, banked like a stove overnight. She was still facing the canyon mouth, but she wasn't looking at the water anymore. She was looking past it, south, toward the distant smudge of lights on the horizon where the river widened toward the sea.

"What?"

"That courier boat," she said. "Before it hit the snag. Did you see what its signalman was doing? At the top of the mast?"

He hadn't. He'd been calling soundings. "No."

"I did." She turned to face him, and the last light caught her eyes, and Dain felt the evening's warmth drain out of the air between them. "He wasn't signaling the skiff behind us. He was signaling *ahead*. Long-and-short, long-and-short, repeating. I've been on rivers my whole life, and I know a relay when I see one." She paused. "That's your father's private pattern, isn't it? The one you used at the narrows — inverted."

The flask suddenly weighed a pound of lead in Dain's pocket.

"It means," Orsen said, "that whatever's waiting for us downstream already knows we're coming. And it knows exactly whose son is aboard."
