## 15. Two Keys, One Ring

The sea cave had two mouths — one to the water, one to the sky — and Tam Vireo had counted every crack in its ceiling twice before he let himself sleep. Counting was what he did when the fear came. Numbers didn't lie. Numbers had never sent a man to prison.

He woke to the smell of Maren's cookfire, such as it was — driftwood and kelp smoke, barely a flame, tucked behind a curtain of hanging stone so no light reached the sea. The tide was out. Through the lower mouth he could see the mudflats glittering under a fat grey morning, and beyond them, far down the coast, the thin dark line of the Rivergate citadel on its bluff, like a knife left standing in a table.

Two keys lay on the flat rock between them. The brass one Orsen had pulled from the river, river-marked and patient. The second one, iron and salt-eaten, that Orsen had carried up out of the Lament's flooded hold in her teeth, nearly, because her hands were full of manifests and her lungs were full of nothing at all. It sat there now, ordinary as a door hinge, and Tam could not stop looking at it.

"Stop staring at it like it's going to confess," Maren said. She was mending a net, or pretending to. Her hands never stopped moving, which Tam had learned was how she prayed.

"I'm not staring. I'm thinking."

"You're staring. There's a difference. Thinking has a face."

Dain Corvane sat apart from the fire, as he always did, back against the wall where he could watch both cave mouths. He'd cleaned and re-cleaned his father's signal book three times since the canyon. Tam had watched him do it. It wasn't the book he was cleaning. It was himself, and it wasn't working.

Orsen came up from the lower mouth with the tide, wet to the waist, carrying a string of mussels and the particular set of her shoulders that meant she'd been out looking at the horizon and hadn't liked what she saw.

"Fleet's regrouping at the Gate," she said. "Four masts, maybe five. They're not searching the coast anymore. They're waiting for us to come to them."

"Because Corvane knows we have two keys," Tam said.

"Because Corvane knows we *need* the third," Orsen said. She dropped the mussels by the fire and sat, cross-legged, forearms on her knees, in the way she did when she was about to make the terrain into an argument. "He's not hunting us. He's herding us. Every road we can take ends at the Rivergate, and every road at the Rivergate ends with him holding the signet and us holding nothing."

"So we don't take a road," Tam said.

Everyone looked at him. He'd been waiting all morning to say it, turning it over the way he turned a chart over, checking the coastlines matched.

"The vault under the Lament — I've been through the manifests, the old surveys, everything I forged and everything I didn't. It's a triple-warded lock. Vessany's treasury engineers built it so no one man could ever open it. Three keys, three custodians, and the keys were never supposed to be in one room." He tapped the iron key. "Corvane broke that rule. He took the third key as a trophy. He wears it on his hand because he wants the world to know he owns what drowned."

"Get to the plan," Maren said.

"The plan is that a key is a *shape*. Nothing more. A shape cut into brass, and a shape cut into iron, and a lock that reads them." Tam pulled his satchel over and began laying things out on the rock with the care of a surgeon: wax, fine wire, a jeweler's loupe he'd kept hidden in his boot through two years of prison and had no intention of explaining. "I have impressions of both keys. I can take a third impression from the lock itself if I can get within arm's reach of the vault door — and I can, because I've already forged the salvage writs that say I can. Give me a foundry, or even a good hot fire and six hours, and I can cast a key that will turn that lock."

Silence. The tide sucked at the mudflats outside.

"You're saying you'll *fake* the third key," Dain said.

"I'm saying I'll *make* one. There's a difference, and the difference is that mine will work."

Maren laughed, short and hard, without any humor in it. "You're the man who went to Ironmark Prison for forging Vessany charts. You know what they do to forgers they catch a second time? They don't do anything. They give you to the families of the men your lies drowned. I've seen it. It takes a long afternoon."

"I know what they do," Tam said. "I've had two years to think about what they do. Every night, in a cell the size of a coffin, I thought about it." He looked at the iron key, then at the citadel's distant line. "I forged charts because a man paid me and I didn't ask who the charts were for. I told myself the shapes were just shapes. Then Corvane used my charts to find the refugee ships, and the shapes became graves." He closed his hand over the loupe. "I'm not asking you to like the plan. I'm asking you to let me finish paying for the first one."

Orsen was watching him with that flat, measuring look she gave to cliff faces and river bends — the look that asked not *is this safe* but *what will it do under load*. Tam had come to dread that look and to need it, in equal parts.

"How close is arm's reach?" she asked.

"Close enough that I'd need to be standing at the vault door. Which is behind the falls. Which means we'd need the barge, the channel, and a day the fleet isn't watching the falls." He shook his head slowly. "And that's the flaw. The falls are watched now. We bought ourselves a night with the canyon, not a week. By the time I cast a working key, they'll have a garrison on the bank."

"So the forgery buys us a key we can't reach," Dain said. "Elegant. Useless."

"Everything I make is elegant," Tam said. "That's the tragedy of me."

* * *

It was Dain who broke the silence after the mussels, and he broke it the way he did everything — too directly, with no chart of the shoals.

"Then we stop talking about keys," he said, "and start talking about hands."

Tam looked up. Maren's netting had gone still, which was the loudest thing in the cave.

"The signet doesn't leave Corvane's hand," Dain said. "I know that better than anyone alive. He eats with it on. He sleeps with it on. When my mother was buried he wore it at the graveside and I remember thinking, even at fourteen, that he was holding her hand through it — no. That's not true. I remember thinking he was holding the *ring*, and my mother was somewhere underneath it, forgotten." His voice stayed level. Tam had noticed that about him: the worse the thing he said, the flatter he made it, like a man walking on ice he knew would crack. "So we don't steal the key. We remove the hand that wears it."

"You're talking about killing your father," Maren said.

"I'm talking about justice," Dain said. "Say the uglier word if it makes you feel honest."

The fire popped. Outside, a gull cried once and thought better of it.

"He'll be at the citadel," Dain went on. "I know the citadel. I grew up in it. I know which stairwell the guards don't watch because my father ordered it unwatched — it's how *he* moves without being seen, and he's too vain to imagine anyone else knows. I know when his door is locked and when it's merely closed. I know that he takes wine after the second bell and that after the third he is a man who cannot hear a floorboard." He turned the signal book over in his hands. "I can put a knife in him before he knows the room isn't empty. And then the ring is ours, and the vault is ours, and every name in that ledger gets its day."

"And you?" Tam asked quietly.

Dain looked at him. "What about me?"

"After. When the son of Corvane has killed the magistrate Corvane. What do you imagine the fleet does? What do you imagine the *courts* do? What do you imagine *I* do, standing next to you?" Tam spread his hands. "You'd hand them a story where the man who robbed Vessany was killed by his own blood for the gold. That's not justice. That's a succession. The ring changes hands, and the story writes itself, and the story is a lie."

"The story would be *true*."

"The truest thing in the world is worthless if it's shaped like a lie," Tam said. "I know. I made a career of it."

Orsen hadn't spoken. Tam watched her watch Dain, and he saw something in her face he'd learned to read on the river — the look of a guide studying a route she might have to refuse. When she finally spoke, her voice was low and even.

"Who taught you the knife?"

"Nobody taught me. That's rather the point of me too."

"That's what frightens me." She poked the fire apart with a stick, deliberately, making the flames drop. "A man who's never killed deciding to kill — the plan always has a hole in it the size of the first time. And the first time is your father, in the dark, and he *will* speak, Dain. He'll say your name. He'll say your mother's name. And the knife stops, and then you're a son standing over his father with a bloody knife and a fleet outside the door." She looked up. "I've seen men freeze at smaller ledges."

Dain's jaw worked. "You have a better plan?"

"I have a *third* plan," Orsen said. "But I want to hear the rest of yours first. All of it. Because if we're going to do this, I want to know exactly which ledges we're standing on."

* * *

They argued through the afternoon. Tam kept notes, because notes were how he thought — little charts of the conversation, currents and counter-currents, where each man's plan grounded out on the rocks of reality.

Dain's plan grounded out on the guards, in the end, when Orsen made him walk it step by step through the citadel's third floor and he admitted, at the stairwell, that his father had doubled the watch since the theft of the manifests. "He knows I'm with you," Dain said, and the flatness in his voice cracked, just once, like ice. "He knows what I am now. He'll have planned for me. He's been planning for me my whole life. That's what fathers like mine do — they build the trap you're going to walk into before you're born."

Maren's contribution was Maren's contribution: she didn't propose a plan so much as she proposed a *condition*. "Whatever we do," she said, "we do it off the water. I'm not running this barge past the Gate again for a key, a knife, or a forgery. The Gate took the hull off my keel in the canyon and I patched it with pitch and prayer. You get the third key to me, and I'll put you behind the falls. But the key comes to the boat. I don't go to the key." She tied off a knot hard enough to snap the twine. "A woman has to keep one rule, or she's just weather."

And through it all, Tam worked. While they argued, he pressed wax around both keys and lifted the impressions, and began sketching the third shape in charcoal on a scrap of sailcloth — the negative space where the lock's third ward would sit, deduced from the geometry of the other two. It was the work he was made for, the only prayer he'd ever been any good at: making a thing true that had never existed.

It was near dusk, the light in the upper mouth going copper, when Orsen finally stood.

"Here's what I see," she said. "Tam's key needs him at the vault door, and the vault door is watched. Dain's knife needs him at his father's throat, and his father's throat is guarded against him. Both plans need us inside the citadel. So the question isn't forgery or murder." She looked around the fire, and Tam saw the guide in her fully now, the woman who had led rich men through passes that wanted them dead, the woman who measured everything and trusted terrain. "The question is: what's the shortest way to put Tam's hands on the vault door — or Dain's hands on his father's hand — with the fleet watching every road in?"

"Neither," Tam said. "The roads are all watched."

"Then we don't take a road." Orsen crouched, and drew in the ash with a stick: the Rivergate, the citadel bluff, the river, the falls. "Corvane is holding a victory feast."

Dain went very still. "The anniversary. The *Drowning Feast*. He throws it every year — the whole fleet, every captain, every magistrate's ally on the coast. He toasts the founding of the salvage charter." His voice had gone strange. "He toasts the *war*. He stands up in front of three hundred men and drinks to the day Vessany died."

"And he wears the ring," Tam said slowly, understanding arriving like cold water. "In front of everyone. He wears it because the feast is his trophy room. The ring *is* the toast."

"He lifts his cup with that hand," Orsen said. "Every year. Dain — does he?"

"He holds the cup in his left and the ring hand flat on the table. It's a habit. It's — " Dain stopped, and Tam watched him understand it too, and watched the understanding hurt. "It's so everyone can see it. He wants them to look at it while he toasts."

"Three hundred men in a hall," Orsen said, "every eye on a raised cup, every guard drunk or wishing he were, and the man we need standing at the high table with the key on his hand." She sat back on her heels. "Tam forges us salvage writs good enough to walk us through the citadel gate as fleet contractors — he's done it once already at the Silt Gates. Dain gets us past the doors he grew up behind. And I get close enough to Corvane to take the ring off his hand while three hundred of his captains watch him drink."

Maren snorted. "Take it *off his hand*. Just lift it. Off the hand of the man who sank a nation. While he's *sitting there*."

"Thieves have lifted rings off sleeping kings for a thousand years," Orsen said. "This one's awake. That's the only difference, and the only difficulty."

"And if he feels it?" Dain asked. "If he catches your wrist mid-toast, in front of his fleet?"

Orsen looked at him for a long moment, and Tam saw her decide how much truth to spend.

"Then I'm a dead woman in a room full of his men, and you're a dead man beside her, and Maren runs the barge out with two keys and a story." She said it the way she read rapids — no drama, just the water. "That's the cost. I'm telling you the cost before we pay it, because that's the only kind of leading I know how to do."

The cave went quiet. The tide had turned; Tam could hear it beginning, the faint hiss of water coming back across the flats, patient as everything the river did.

It was Maren who spoke first, to Tam, surprisingly. "Your forgery. Writs good enough to stand in front of the man himself? Corvane's own clerks will be at that gate. They'll know fleet paper better than fleet paper does."

"Then I'd better be better than fleet paper," Tam said. "It's what I'm for." He was already pulling out fresh vellum, his hand steadier than it had been in two years. "I'll need Dain's seal knowledge, the fleet's current countersign from the signal book, and six hours."

"You have until the tide turns," Orsen said. "We go with the next high water. We sail at night, we lie off the Gate until the feast is loud, and we walk in through the front door." She looked around the fire, at each of them in turn — the forger, the ferrywoman, the son. "Tam's key is the fallback. Dain's knife is the last resort. The theft is the plan. Everyone understand the order of what we're willing to become?"

Nobody answered, which was its own answer.

Tam bent over the vellum and began to rule his lines, fine and straight, and as he worked he found himself thinking about the ring — about the shape of it, the weight of it, a key worn as a trophy for twenty years by the man who had drowned a nation to hold it.

*Keys are just shapes*, he told himself. *And a shape can always be taken out of one hand and put into another.*

Outside, the tide was rising, and far down the darkening coast, the citadel was lighting its windows one by one — a knife in the table, beginning to gleam.
