## 16. Fifth Tide: Into the Citadel

The Rivergate citadel had been built to be seen from the water. Corvane's architects had raised it on a spur of black granite where the river narrowed, its towers lit with lantern-glass so that every barge captain running the night trade could look up and know exactly who owned the current. From the water it looked like a fist. From inside the salvage skiff, hugging the shadow of a coal barge, it looked like something worse.

It looked like a mouth.

"Third lantern from the left," Dain murmured beside her. He wore a fleet officer's coat two sizes too big in the shoulders — Tam had stolen it off a laundry line in Lowmark and let out the seams himself — and he kept his hands folded in his lap like a man at prayer. "That's the officers' gate. Feast traffic goes through the water stair tonight. Everyone's expected."

"Everyone," Orsen said.

"Everyone who matters." He said it flatly, without bitterness, which was somehow worse. "We matter enough. Salvage crews get invited when there's prize money to distribute. My father likes an audience for that."

Orsen checked the forged commission papers one more time — Tam's finest work, wax seal and all, naming *Salvage Master Oren Vask* and *Lieutenant Dane Corvane* of the *Kestrel's Wage*, independent contractors under fleet charter. The name was a risk. Using the magistrate's own surname on his son's false papers was either brilliant or suicidal, and Tam had grinned like a knife when he'd suggested it.

"No one checks a name they already believe," he'd said.

Maren held the skiff against the current with two fingers on the tiller, as if the river were a dog she'd trained. "Water stair puts you out below the feast hall. There's a colonnade, then the great doors. I'll be at the coal wharf past midnight. If you're not back by the last bell—"

"We'll be back by the last bell."

"If you're not," Maren said, unblinking, "I'm taking your share of the map and your boots. They're good boots."

Orsen almost smiled. Almost. She stepped onto the water stair and felt the stone take her weight, solid and indifferent, and behind her she heard Maren slip away into the dark without a sound, because that was what Maren did.

* * *

The feast hall of the Rivergate citadel smelled of roast pork, lamp oil, and money.

Orsen had been in rich rooms before — she'd guided mining magnates through passes no mule could climb, and they'd paid her in coin stamped with faces she didn't recognize. But this was different. This was wealth arranged as a weapon. Banners of the fleet hung from every pillar. Long tables groaned under silver. And above the high table, on a drape of deep blue silk, hung the thing that made her stomach turn over:

A chart of the river country. Vessany's rivers, Vessany's valleys — with Vessany's name scraped off the vellum entirely, replaced in fresh gold leaf with the words *CORVANE TERRITORIES*.

"They erased it," Dain said quietly at her shoulder. His voice had gone strange. "He had it erased. The whole nation. Like scraping mold off bread."

"Don't look at it."

"I grew up under that banner." He straightened his borrowed coat. "I never once asked what was underneath."

They moved into the crowd. That was the first lesson of infiltration Orsen had learned in another life, guiding clients through border towns where her face was worth a bounty: don't hide, belong. She carried herself like a woman who had been invited, nodded at the right people, took a cup of wine from a passing tray and held it without drinking. Dain walked half a step behind her, the way a lieutenant walks behind a salvage master, and the disguise worked better than any costume. People saw what they expected. A few officers glanced at Dain, frowned faintly at something familiar in his jaw, and then looked away, deciding they were mistaken. Believing you're mistaken is easier than believing the impossible.

Corvane sat at the high table.

Orsen had imagined him a hundred times since the dying river-runner gasped his name in a flooded willow stand. She'd built him tall, built him monstrous, built him with teeth. The man on the dais was none of those things. He was broad and gray-templed, with a heavy signet hand wrapped around a goblet and a laugh that came easily, warmly, the laugh of a man who had never once doubted he was the hero of his own story. He was telling some story about a storm. Half the hall was laughing with him.

On his right hand, catching the light of a hundred lamps: the signet ring. Old gold, worn smooth, set with a seal Orsen knew better than her own heartbeat now. The tide-seal of Vessany.

He wore a drowned nation on his finger like a trophy, and he was using it to gesture while he told jokes.

"Breathe," Dain murmured.

"I'm breathing."

"You're breathing like Maren threads rapids."

* * *

The toast came at the ninth bell.

Corvane rose, and the hall rose with him, three hundred officers and merchants and magistrates lifting their cups in a single motion, and Orsen understood suddenly why Tam had insisted they be in the hall for this moment and not lurking in some corridor. *You want to steal from a man,* Tam had said, *steal during a ritual. Rituals are locks everyone agrees to leave open.*

"To the fleet," Corvane called out, "and to the riches of these waters — opened at last to honest hands!"

Honest hands. The ring flashed as he raised his goblet. Around Orsen, three hundred arms went up, three hundred faces tilted toward the dais, three hundred pairs of eyes fixed on the man who owned the river.

Orsen moved.

She'd planned it a hundred times in the sea cave, walking it through with Tam until the cartographer could recite her footsteps in his sleep. Not from the front — the dais steps were guarded. From the side, along the serving corridor that ran behind the high table, where the kitchen hands ferried platters and no officer ever looked because officers never look at servants. She'd taken a serving girl's apron from a laundry heap by the water stair; it was folded in her coat. Two steps into the corridor, apron on, a platter lifted from a passing tray — cold capon, heavy, useful — and she was moving among the servants like water finding its level.

The corridor ran behind the dais. Through the service arch she could see Corvane's back, broad and gray, and his right hand resting on the table beside his goblet, fingers slack around the stem as he drank.

Three steps. She set down the platter. Two steps. The noise of the hall swelled — the toast ending, the roar of *to Corvane!* rising like weather. One step.

His hand lay on the tablecloth like something asleep.

She took the ring the way Maren took fish off a hook — quick, certain, no hesitation, because hesitation is what gets you bitten. The gold slid over his knuckle, warm from his skin, and Orsen felt the warmth of it and wanted to drop it in the river and scrub her hands for a week. Instead she closed her fist around it, turned, and walked back into the servant traffic with a platter of capon she hadn't picked up.

Behind her, Corvane set down his empty goblet and laughed at something.

* * *

She should have gone straight for the water stair. Every plan said so. Tam had drawn her a diagram, for mercy's sake — *ring acquired, exit east, do not improvise, Orsen, I am begging you* — and she was three steps from the colonnade and freedom when she heard Dain's voice carry across the feast hall, clear and terrible:

"Father."

The hall didn't go silent. Halls that big never go fully silent. But it dimmed, the way a fire dims when a door opens, and Orsen froze in the shadow of a pillar with three hundred people between her and the boy who was supposed to be walking beside her.

Dain stood at the foot of the dais in his borrowed lieutenant's coat, and he had not come to make a scene — she could see that, even from here, even with her heart slamming. He'd come to say something. Maybe he'd known all along he would. Maybe Tam had known too, and that was why the cartographer had begged her not to improvise: because some improvisations aren't yours to stop.

Corvane squinted down at him, wine-flushed, genial. "Do I know you, Lieutenant?"

"You used to." Dain's voice didn't shake. Orsen marveled at that even as she cursed it. "You taught me to read charts on that table. You told me the river was honest. You said everything a man needed to know was written in the water."

Something moved behind Corvane's eyes — a flicker, quickly drowned. "You have me confused with someone else."

"I have you confused with the man in the stories Mother told." Dain took one step up the dais. Guards shifted, but Corvane waved them down, curious now, entertained. "She died believing you were that man. I spent six years hoping she'd been right. Then I read your ledger."

The word *ledger* landed in the hall like a dropped glass. Nearby conversations faltered. Corvane's smile stayed perfectly in place, but Orsen, watching from twenty feet away, saw his hand drift toward the table edge — toward where the ring should have been steadying him — and find bare skin instead.

She saw the exact moment he noticed.

"And what did my son's imagination find in this ledger?" Corvane said lightly, loudly, playing to the room, buying seconds the same way she bought them.

"That you sank their ships." Dain's voice cracked at last, just once, and healed itself. "Refugees. Families. You sank them yourself and sold what floated. And the whole time you wore their seal on your hand like it was yours."

The hall was properly quiet now. Somewhere a guard captain was pushing through the crowd. Orsen began to move along the pillar line, angling for the colonnade, unable to leave, unable to stay, watching the boy walk toward the father who had never existed.

"Dain." Corvane said the name softly, and for one unguarded instant the mask slipped and Orsen saw what lived underneath — not a monster, which would have been simpler. Just a tired, clever, greedy man looking at the last piece of his life he'd failed to buy. "Walk out of here. Take a ship. Take money. Whatever you think you know, you're young, and youth mistakes truth for—"

"I know what I know." Dain reached into his coat and Orsen's whole body went tight — but he only drew out a single sheet of paper, Tam's copywork, and laid it on the dais step. "There are names in that vault, Father. Hundreds of them. Families you drowned. When they're read aloud — and they will be read aloud — I want you to remember that I told you first. To your face. While you still had a chance to be the man from the stories."

Then he turned and walked down the dais steps, past the frozen guards, past three hundred staring officers, and Orsen fell in beside him at the colonnade as if she'd been waiting there all evening, and together they walked out into the dark.

Neither of them spoke until the water stair. The river breathed below, black and patient. Behind them, up in the lit tower, a bell began to ring — not the feast bell. The alarm. And under it, thin and furious, a voice shouting orders.

"He looked for the ring," Dain said. His voice was steady. His hands weren't. "Right at the end. He looked at his own hand like it had betrayed him." He laughed, one dry note with nothing in it. "He never even wondered how. He'll assume thieves. Professionals. He'll never believe it was—" He stopped.

"Dain."

"He used to hold my hand crossing the ferry docks." He was looking at the river, not at her. "Same hand. I used to think it was the strongest thing in the world."

Orsen put the signet in his palm and closed his fingers over it. It seemed right that he should carry it across the water, even if only for a little while. "Maren's at the coal wharf. Last bell's in twenty minutes."

He nodded. They went down the stairs two at a time as the bells multiplied behind them, tower after tower taking up the cry, and somewhere above, torches bloomed along the citadel walls like the first sparks of a fire that hadn't started yet but was coming — coming downriver, coming for the falls, coming for all of them.

The skiff slid out of the coal-dark. Maren took one look at their faces and pushed off from the wharf before their boots were wet.

"Well?" she said.

Orsen opened her fist. The tide-seal caught the burning light of the citadel and threw it back, small and gold and impossibly heavy.

"He knows," Dain said. "And he knows we're on the river."

Maren grinned like a storm front. "Good. Saves us the trouble of surprising him."
