## 17. The Trap Springs

Maren felt the city close before she saw it close.

She was at the tiller of the *Salt Lark*, running the last bend above Rivergate with the current pushing hard at her spine, when the sound reached her — a deep, iron groan rolling up the water like the river itself clearing its throat. She had grown up on that water. She knew its voices: the mutter of silt, the knock of driftwood, the long complaint of ice going out in spring. This was none of them.

This was chain.

"Kill the lanterns," she said, and her voice came out flat and calm, which surprised her, because everything under her ribs had gone to water.

Ahead, the citadel's towers stood black against a sky still bruised orange from the feast-fires. And across the whole width of the Rivergate — the great fortified gate itself, the twin stone pylons and the iron portcullis hung between them like a guillotine blade waiting for a neck — torches were blooming one by one. Dozens. Then scores. The whole face of the gate lit up like a festival, except no festival had ever looked like that: every light a soldier, every soldier a crossbow, every crossbow pointed down at the only channel out of the city.

"They sealed it," Dain said behind her. His voice was strange. Stripped. "The gates. He sealed the gates."

Orsen stood at the rail with the signet ring in her fist, and Maren watched her look at it — really look — the way you look at a thing that has just shown its teeth. The brass caught the torchlight. It was real. It was genuine Corvane work, heavy and old and true. Which meant the theft had succeeded, which meant they had done the impossible thing, walked into the wolf's mouth and come out with the tooth.

And it had changed nothing.

"He let us take it," Tam said quietly. He was holding his charts against his chest like a shield. "Gods below. He *wanted* us to take it. We're not thieves tonight. We're bait."

"No," Orsen said. "We're proof." She turned, and in the torchlight her face was all planes and decision, the mountain-woman's face Maren had learned to read over six tides of running together. "He can't let us reach the falls with this. If we show the charter and the keys to anyone — the fleet captains, the guild courts, anyone — he's finished. So he seals the gate, burns the city exits, and waits for us to do the desperate thing." Her jaw tightened. "He wants us to run the gate."

"Nobody runs the gate," Maren said.

Everyone turned to her.

"I mean it." She kept her eyes on the water, because looking at them would make it worse, and looking at the gate would make it worse than that. "My grandmother ran cargo through Rivergate before the walls went up. My mother piloted timber rafts past the outer pylons. There are stories about the gate channel, and every single one ends the same way. The current through there isn't water anymore. It's a millrace squeezed between two stone throats, and the pylons have drowned timbers chained across them — old wreck-cribs from when they built the fortifications. They left them there on purpose. Anything that hits them comes apart like kindling. And if you somehow miss the cribs—" she lifted her chin at the portcullis, hanging in its raised slot, "—that grate drops in nine seconds. They've drilled it. Nine seconds from signal to seal. No ship survives. That's not a saying. That's arithmetic."

Silence on the deck, except the river talking underneath, patient as ever.

Then Orsen said, "Options."

It wasn't a question. It was how she started every impossible conversation, and Maren had learned to hate and love that word in equal measure.

"We go back upstream," Tam began, "we're dead. The fleet's canyon squadrons are already rowing for us — I heard the signal horns off the Silt Gates an hour ago. Downstream is the gate. Overland—" he gestured at the citadel heights, where beacons were now flaring one after another along the ridge road, "—they've lit the passes. Every road out of Rivergate will have riders on it by dawn."

"The sewers," Dain said.

"Sealed with the gates, I'd wager," Tam said. "Corvane's not a man who leaves one door open by accident. He leaves one door open *on purpose*."

That landed on all of them at once, and Maren saw it land — saw Orsen's eyes narrow, saw Dain go pale in the torch-glow, saw Tam clutch his charts tighter.

"He wants us in the water," Dain said slowly. "Because he thinks no pilot alive will try the gate. He wants us cornered until we beach the barge and come at him on land, where his garrison can take us quiet, away from witnesses. Or drown trying, and he fishes the keys out of the shallows tomorrow and calls it a flood loss."

"Then he's made one mistake," Orsen said.

"What's that?"

"He's never met my ferrywoman."

* * *

They put in at the ruined tannery docks downstream of the citadel, in the black shadow of the sea wall, and Maren walked the length of her barge alone while the others argued in whispers on the bank.

The *Salt Lark* was dying. She had known it since the canyon, since the chain-shot that opened her strakes below the waterline like a wound that wouldn't close. Tam had patched her twice with pitch and prayer. She took water now the way an old woman takes breath — constantly, painfully, and with the certain knowledge that each one might be the last. Running the gate in her would be suicide even if the gate weren't sealed.

But the *Salt Lark* was also the only hull in Rivergate that could carry what they carried: two tide-keys, the survivor manifests, the ledger, and the stolen signet. Four objects worth more than the city, and every one of them worthless unless they reached the falls.

Maren knelt at the bilge and put her palm flat on the wet planking, the way she had since she was six years old, and listened.

Her mother had taught her that. *The boat talks, little fish. Your job is to know when she's lying.*

The *Salt Lark* wasn't lying. She was scared. Maren could feel it coming up through the wood like a fever.

"You and me both," Maren whispered.

"Maren." Orsen's voice, low, from the dock above. "Talk to me."

Maren climbed up. The four of them stood in the dark among the rotting tanning vats, and she told them the truth, because they had earned the truth, and because lying to these three had become harder than any rapid she'd ever run.

"She'll float. Barely. Through the millrace, with the cribs and the grate and whatever else Corvane's engineers have waiting — she'll come apart, or she won't. I can't promise which. What I can promise is that I can hold her straighter through that throat than any pilot Corvane has ever bought. Whether that's enough—" she spread her hands, "—the river decides. Not me. Not him."

"So we don't run it," Dain said.

They all looked at him.

"We swim it," he said. "Four people, one crate, waterproof oilskin. We go through the gate channel in the water, under the arrows, under the grate timing—"

"In the millrace," Maren said. "In the dark. In water moving faster than a galloping horse, full of drowned timber and chain-link and whatever the flood season has pushed down from the high country this week. You'd hit the first crib in forty seconds and be cut to ribbons. If the cold doesn't stop your heart first. I've pulled swimmers out of half that current, Dain. I've never once pulled one out whole."

"Then what?" His voice cracked, and she heard the boy under the officer, the son under the name. "We sit here? We wait for his garrison to walk down the dock? You heard the beacons. We have hours. Maybe less."

"We vote," Orsen said.

* * *

They voted on the barge, by lantern-shielded flame, the way Maren's family had voted on hard crossings for three generations: everyone speaks, everyone counts, no one carries the decision alone.

Tam spoke first. He laid his forged Vessany charts on the hatch cover and put his hand flat on them. "I spent eleven years in irons for these waters," he said. "Every day of it I told myself someone would someday need what I knew, and it would be worth the chains. This is that day. I say we run it. I say Corvane has spent twenty years deciding what this river means, and I am done letting him decide anything." His voice shook on the last word, and Maren realized he wasn't afraid of the gate. He was afraid of going back to a cell, and he was choosing the gate anyway. That took more courage than fearlessness ever had.

Dain spoke last but hardest. "I'm the reason he knows your faces. My seal opened the Silt Gates; my name is on the patrol reports. Everything he does tonight, he does aiming at me." He stopped. Started again. "I spent my whole life thinking my father was hard because the world required hardness. Then I read his ledger. He sank refugee ships, Maren. Full of families. And then he built a feast hall on the money." He looked at the signet in Orsen's fist. "If we stop here, he wins forever, and I helped build the machine that lets him. Run it. I'll row if I have to."

Orsen spoke shortest. "I didn't pull a chest out of a flood to bury it in a garrison cell. We run."

Then they all looked at Maren, and she understood, finally, why her mother had made voting sacred. Because now the terror that had been sitting in her chest since the first iron groan rolled upriver had somewhere to go. It wasn't hers alone anymore. It was shared out four ways, like ballast, like oars.

"I'll tell you what my mother told me the night she ran the outer pylons in a flood," Maren said. "She said: the river doesn't care what you deserve. It cares whether you're honest. So be honest with it. Don't fight the current — argue with it. Give it your weight exactly when it asks, and not one breath sooner." She looked at the gate, blazing with torches, hung with death. "I can't get us through. Nobody gets anybody through. But I can keep us honest with the water, and I've been keeping that bargain my whole life, and the water has mostly kept its side."

She took the tiller.

"That's not a yes," Dain said.

"It's the only kind of yes I have."

* * *

They stripped the *Salt Lark* to racing trim in under an hour. Everything not essential went over the side into the tannery shallows — spare spars, the cooking box, Tam's spare inks, half their stores. What remained: the crate of keys and manifests, wrapped in triple-oiled canvas and lashed to the strongest timber amidships; the patched hull; five hearts, counting the boat.

Maren worked in silence, and the work did what work always did for her: burned the terror down to something usable. Fear was fuel if you fed it to your hands instead of your head. Her mother's saying. Another one. She was going to die rich in sayings if not in years.

Near midnight, Tam appeared beside her with two cups of something hot and terrible from the spirit stove.

"For the hands," he said.

She drank. It tasted like pine tar and courage. "Thank you."

"For what?"

"For not asking me if I'm sure."

He almost smiled. "I watched you thread the canyon, Maren. Asking you if you're sure is like asking the tide if it's wet."

They cast off at the turn of the tide, because the ebb would be running with them through the gate channel, adding the sea's own strength to the river's, and because Maren wanted every ounce of honest help the water would give.

The city slid past on both banks — burning windows, ringing bells, soldiers' torches streaming along the quay roads like sparks down a fuse. Somewhere up on the citadel heights, she imagined Corvane standing at his window, watching the river for a speck of movement, wearing the face of a man whose trap had already sprung. Let him watch. Let him learn what river-witches do with traps.

Then the pylons rose ahead, and the gate itself, and Maren forgot how to imagine anything at all.

* * *

Up close, the Rivergate was not architecture. It was a mouth.

Two stone pylons fifty yards apart, furred with weed to the waterline, and between them the channel — narrowed by the drowned wreck-cribs jutting from each pylon base like broken teeth, so the actual passage was barely twenty feet wide. Through that gap the entire river poured, ebb and current combined, and the sound of it was a continuous roar, and the spray off it hung in the torchlight like drifting snow.

Above it all, the portcullis. An iron lattice the size of a chapel roof, held in its slot by a winch the size of a wagon, and beneath it, on the gatehouse parapet, Maren could see the winch crew — eight men, a signal officer, a bell.

Nine seconds from signal to seal. Arithmetic.

"There," Tam breathed, pointing. On the parapet, a figure in magistrate's white, gold at the collar. Watching. Even at this distance, Maren knew the posture — she'd seen it in Dain's shoulders often enough, the family carriage of a man accustomed to being obeyed by cities.

"He came to watch us die," Dain said. His voice had gone very quiet. "He's giving us an audience."

"Good," Orsen said, and there was something in her voice Maren had never heard before — not rage, exactly. Certainty, honed to an edge. "Let him watch closely. He'll want to remember this night."

Maren took the tiller with both hands and made herself look only at the water.

The channel was worse than she'd feared and better than she'd dreamed. The current through the gap ran in three distinct veins — she could see them, could see the seams where they met and boiled — and the left-hand vein, the one that curled around the western crib, was the fast one, the one that would carry them through if they could hold it, and kill them if they missed it by a boat-length either way.

Twenty feet. At this speed, that gap would pass beneath them in the time it took to draw three breaths.

"Listen to me," she said, and the crew gathered close, and she gave them the only gift she had left. "When I call the vein, you move. No questions, no hesitation. When I say *down*, everyone flat on the deck — the grate clearance is a hand's width above our mast step, and I intend to shave it. When I say *brace*, grab wood and pray to whatever listens to boats. And no matter what you hear — the hull screaming, the water roaring, me shouting things that would shame a dockworker — nobody touches the tiller but me. Agreed?"

Three nods. One boat, groaning agreement of her own.

Maren spat over the side, the old blessing, the old bargain. *Keep us honest, old mother. I'll keep you honest back.*

Then she put the bow at the left-hand seam and let the river take them.

* * *

The first ten seconds were silence.

Not true silence — the roar was everywhere, filling the world — but the silence of the crew, four people holding their breath as the *Salt Lark* leapt forward like a creature released, and the pylons rushed at them, and the drowned cribs came up out of the water on either side, black and dripping and hung with chain, close enough to touch, close enough to—

"Vein holds!" Maren screamed, and hauled the tiller a hand's width to starboard as the current tried to slam them onto the eastern crib, and the barge answered, and the crib slid past close enough that Dain could have counted the barnacles.

Then the arrows came.

Flaming shafts arced down from the parapets in sheets, and the night filled with burning rain. One buried itself in the deck beside Tam's knee. Another splashed into the current alongside. A third struck the mast step and stuck there, quivering, painting the whole deck in jumping orange light.

"DOWN!"

They went flat. Maren stayed kneeling at the tiller, exposed, because the tiller was the one thing she would not leave, and she felt an arrow's heat pass close enough to singe her hair.

The portcullis loomed ahead, filling the sky, and she saw the winch crew moving, saw the signal officer's arm rise, saw the bell begin to swing—

Nine seconds.

The *Salt Lark* hit the gate channel's throat at full ebb, and the world became noise. The hull screamed. Water came aboard in tons, green and black and solid, and somewhere Tam was shouting something that might have been a prayer or coordinates, and the crate lashed amidships strained at its ropes like a living thing, and the iron grate overhead dropped—

Eight.

Seven.

Six.

Maren did the only thing left. She threw her whole body against the tiller, put the bow exactly on the fastest thread of the ebb, and *argued* — argued with the current the way her mother taught her, gave it her weight exactly when it asked, no breath sooner, no breath later—

Five.

The grate fell.

It missed the mast step by the width of a psalm. Maren heard it — the iron shriek, the boom of it seating into the stone, the bell tolling victory on the parapet — and she heard it *behind* them, because the ebb had taken the *Salt Lark*'s stern through the gap with three feet to spare, and the rivergate's iron teeth had closed on empty water.

On the far side, the current released them like a hand opening, and the barge wallowed in the sudden calm, shipping water, listing, alive.

For a moment nobody moved. Then Tam began to laugh — a wild, cracked sound — and Dain sat up from the deck with an arrow shaft in his fist, snapped it, and threw it into the river. Orsen rose, soaked, and looked back at the gate.

The torches blazed. The white figure on the parapet stood motionless, watching them go.

"He's not celebrating," Orsen said slowly. "Look at him. He's *counting*."

Maren followed her gaze, and cold walked up her spine, because the magistrate wasn't watching the barge at all. He was watching the water downstream — the wide, dark, moon-silvered reach below the gate — and his arm had risen, and far off in the night, downstream, past the last bend, horns were answering.

Not pursuit horns.

Ambush horns.

And then the *Salt Lark* lurched, groaned like a dying animal, and began to settle beneath them, and Maren understood with terrible clarity that the gate had won after all — just late, just slow, just patient — and that the sixth tide was rising, and the falls were still two hours downstream, and the fleet was waiting in the dark between here and there.

"Bail," she said calmly, taking up a bucket. "Bail like your lives depend on it. Because they do."

The crew seized buckets. The *Salt Lark* settled by inches, held together by pitch, prayer, and a ferrywoman's stubbornness, and Maren steered her wounded, leaking, magnificent boat into the dark downstream water — toward the horns, toward the falls, toward the sixth tide and whatever waited there — and refused, absolutely refused, to be afraid.

Mostly.
