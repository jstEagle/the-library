## 18. Sixth Tide: The Burning Gate

The rivergate stood ahead of them like a jaw of black iron, and it was already on fire.

Orsen crouched at the bow of the *Salt Lark*, watching the flames crawl along the timber galleries above the gate arch. Corvane's engineers had spent the night oiling the walkways, and now the whole structure burned with a low, greedy orange light that turned the floodwater below into a sheet of hammered copper. Arrows fell from the battlements in slow, hissing arcs, trailing fire, and where they struck the water they died with a sound like a thousand small breaths.

"Sixth tide's peaking," Maren called from the helm. Her voice was steady the way a rope is steady — taut, and only until something snaps. "We go now or we don't go."

Orsen looked back once. The citadel loomed behind them on its bluff, banners snapping, horns sounding the alarm they'd triggered an hour ago when they came over the wall with a magistrate's signet ring in Orsen's fist. Dain sat amidships, soaked to the bone, blood crusted at his hairline from the leap into the canal. He hadn't spoken since. Tam was bailing with both hands, the forged papers long since abandoned somewhere in the mud of the lower city.

Behind the gate was open water and the falls and the vault. Behind them was everything else.

"Now," Orsen said.

* * *

Maren took them in low.

That was the first thing Orsen understood about what happened next, though she would spend years afterward trying to describe it: Maren did not run the burning gate so much as slide beneath it, hugging the waterline so close that the barge's rail kissed the flood, using the smoke itself as cover. The archers above couldn't see through their own fire. The flames that should have been death became a curtain drawn between the *Salt Lark* and every eye on the battlements.

But the gate had teeth that didn't need eyes.

Chain-shot came out of the smoke without warning — two iron balls linked by a length of anchor chain, whirling flat across the water like a scythe. The first one passed overhead with a sound Orsen felt in her sternum rather than heard, a wet tearing of air. The second caught the barge's mast.

The mast didn't fall so much as vanish, sheared off a hand's width above the deck, taking the furled sail and half the rigging with it into the river. The *Salt Lark* lurched, spun a quarter turn, and Maren swore in three languages and corrected with the sweep oar alone, hauling them back onto the current line by main strength while splinters rained down around them like gray hail.

"Hold!" she roared. "Nobody moves! She'll carry her weight if you let her!"

Orsen held. It was the hardest thing she'd ever done. The tide was peaking and the gate was forty yards off and burning and the water through the arch ran fast as a millrace, white-capped even in flood, funneling between stone piers older than any nation still living. She could see the chains now — the great harbor chains, raised, dripping, strung across the arch at the waterline exactly as the plan had feared. No ship survives this passage. That was what everyone said. That was why no one had tried it in two hundred years.

"Maren," Tam shouted, "the chains—"

"I see them." Maren's knuckles were white on the sweep. "Orsen. The tide book. What's the peak?"

Orsen had the numbers in her head; she'd memorized them off Tam's charts three nights ago. "Two feet above flood stage. Ninety seconds of slack at the top, then it drops hard."

"Ninety seconds." Maren grinned, and it was a terrible thing to see, all teeth and river-madness. "Then I've got ninety seconds to put us under a chain built for ships that draw eight feet."

"We draw four," Dain said quietly. It was the first thing he'd said since the citadel.

"Aye," Maren said. "And I mean to use every inch of the difference."

* * *

They went through the arch at full flood, under the burning galleries, into the throat of the gate.

The heat hit first. Orsen threw her arm across her face as they passed beneath timbers that had been burning for hours, and embers came down on the deck like orange snow, catching in the bilge water, hissing out against the planks. Something above them groaned — a whole section of gallery letting go — and a burning beam the length of a rowboat crashed into the river ten feet off the starboard bow, close enough that the spray it threw was hot.

Then the chains.

They rose up out of the water dead ahead, two links thick, sagging in a shallow catenary across the entire arch, and beyond them the current through the gap ran white and screaming. There was no room. There was no time. There was only Maren, standing at the sweep with her gray hair whipping loose from its knot, reading the water the way Orsen read mountains — not looking at the danger but at the shape of the safe line hidden inside it.

"Down!" she screamed. "Everyone flat! Now!"

Orsen flattened. Tam flattened. Dain hesitated half a heartbeat — looking up at the chain, measuring it, the magistrate's son doing arithmetic with his own life — and then he dropped too, and it was the right choice, because the *Salt Lark* went under the chain with less than a foot to spare.

Orsen knew because she heard it. The chain scraped along the deck above her back, iron on oak, a long shriek like a nail dragged across the sky, close enough that she felt it through the planks, through her ribs, through her teeth. A single link caught the stub of the sheared mast and held there, groaning, dragging, threatening to pivot the whole barge broadside into the current —

— and then the tide peaked, and the water lifted them those last crucial inches, and the link slipped free, and they were through.

The river took them like a dog shaking a rat.

* * *

On the far side of the gate the flood spread wide and fast toward the falls, and the *Salt Lark* was dying.

She'd been dying for days — leaky, overloaded, patched twice with river-tar and prayer — and the chain had finished what the patrol skiff started. Water came up through her seams in silver ropes. Tam bailed frantically; Maren steered for the north shore, where the charts showed a gravel bar below the willow stands; and Orsen, kneeling in six inches of cold water that was rising faster than any of them liked, pulled the oilskin bundle out from under the thwart where it had ridden lashed down since the sea cave.

Three keys. The manifests. The charter pages Tam had copied. Everything. Everything they'd bled for, wrapped in waxed canvas, tied with cord she'd double-knotted herself because she trusted knots more than people.

The gate behind them was fully alight now. She could hear it — a deep roaring crackle, and beneath it the horns of the citadel, and beneath that, faint but unmistakable, the creak of windlasses as Corvane's crews began lowering boats. He wouldn't let them go. Not with the signet missing from his hand and his son's face burned into his memory and the truth of what he'd done sitting in an oilskin bundle on a sinking barge.

"She's going," Maren said. No drama in it. Just fact, the way she said everything. "Gravel bar's two hundred yards. Can everyone swim?"

Tam's face went gray. "I grew up in a chart room."

"Then stay close to me and don't fight the water," Maren said. "Fight the water and it wins. Let it carry you, kick when I say kick."

Dain stripped off his coat without being told, folded it once, and wedged it under the lashing beside the oilskin bundle — keeping the keys dry a few minutes longer, a small precise act of care that Orsen noted and filed away and said nothing about. The son of Corvane, protecting other people's property with his last dry cloth.

The barge grounded on the bar with a shudder, took one more wave over the rail, and began to come apart.

It wasn't dramatic. That was the strange part. After everything — the Silt Gates, the canyon, the burning arch — the *Salt Lark* simply settled into the gravel and opened along her seams like a coat giving up its buttons, and the river walked in and took her. Orsen was in the water before the deck tilted, bundle strapped across her chest, and the cold closed over her head and the world became noise and green murk and the pull of the falls somewhere downstream, patient as gravity.

Kick, Maren had said. Orsen kicked.

* * *

She came ashore coughing, a hundred yards downstream of the wreck, dragged out by the willow roots at the edge of the gravel bar. Maren was already there, hauling Tam in by the collar like a drowned kitten; Tam came up retaking river water and swearing at it, which Orsen decided was a good sign. Dain surfaced thirty seconds later, farther out than anyone liked, and swam in slow and deliberate, conserving himself, and walked out of the shallows streaming and silent.

Four of them. One bundle. No boat.

Behind them, across the widening flood, the Rivergate burned.

It burned beautifully, which Orsen hated herself for noticing. The fire had taken the galleries entirely now, and the great timber superstructure above the stone arch stood silhouetted against the dusk like the skeleton of some enormous beast caught mid-roar. Embers climbed on the thermal currents and scattered eastward over the water, hundreds of them, thousands, a slow black snowfall in reverse. The citadel horns kept sounding, but they sounded wrong — ragged, overlapping, the calls of men who had expected to be celebrating tonight and were instead watching their own gate come down.

"They won't cross tonight," Maren said, wringing out her hair. "Boats take an hour to launch, and the run below the gate is murder in the dark. We bought ourselves till dawn."

"Dawn isn't enough." Dain stood with his back to the fire, facing upstream toward the falls, which they could hear but not see — a low constant thunder under everything, like the river clearing its throat. "Corvane knows where we're going. He's always known. The vault behind the falls is in his grandfather's survey records. He'll send riders along the north bank trail tonight. They'll beat us there."

"Then we don't sleep," Orsen said.

They looked at her. All three of them, soaked and shaking and half-dead, and she realized she was doing it again — the guide's voice, the voice that made decisions for people who were paying her to make them. Except nobody here had paid her anything, and nobody here was following her because of a contract. They were following her because the alternative was Corvane.

"The north bank trail is twelve miles," she said. "I've walked worse in worse weather with worse company. We move now, we reach the falls before his riders, and we're through the vault door before the fleet finishes putting out their own gate." She tightened the cord on the oilskin bundle. "Everything we risked doesn't count for anything unless we finish it tonight."

Tam laughed — a thin, cracked, exhausted sound. "You know what I keep thinking? Two hundred years ago someone sealed that charter behind the falls and trusted that someday, someone would come for it. Three keys. Six tides. A burning gate. They made it almost impossible on purpose, so that only people who meant it would ever get through." He pushed wet hair out of his face. "I think we were meant it."

Maren snorted. "Chart-room poetry. Save it for after."

But she was already moving, checking the bundle straps, counting the keys by touch through the waxed canvas — one, two, three, the third still warm somehow, or maybe that was Orsen's imagination, the signet ring they'd stolen off a tyrant's hand at his own victory feast. Dain picked up a piece of driftwood and snapped it into torch-lengths without being asked. The crew, moving as one body. When had that happened?

Orsen looked back once more at the burning gate.

Most of it lost, she thought. The barge. The papers. The quiet anonymous life she'd been clinging to since the day they blacklisted her name. Maybe Dain's last chance at his father. Maybe, tomorrow, some of theirs. The river took its toll from everyone who used it; that was the oldest law there was.

But four of them stood on a gravel bar in the falling dark, alive, with the truth in a waterproof bundle and twelve miles of trail between them and the end of it.

"North bank," Orsen said. "Stay off the skyline. We move in ten minutes — eat while you can."

Above them, the gate burned, and the sixth tide began to fall, and somewhere upstream, behind the thunder of the falls, a vault two hundred years shut waited for its keys.

They went into the trees at a pace just short of a run, and did not look back again.
