## 19. The Vault Beneath the Falls

Tam Vireo had spent eleven years drawing lies for a living, and so he knew the truth when he finally held it: it was heavier than paper had any right to be.

The charter came out of the vault wrapped in oilcloth and waxed leather, a scroll as long as his arm, sealed at both ends with the Vessany crest — the river and the mountain crossed by a single unbroken line. He turned it over twice before he trusted himself to speak.

"That's not a copy," he said. His voice came out wrong, thin. "That's the *record*. The original. There's only one of these in the world."

"Then it's worth more than the gold," Orsen said.

She was standing hip-deep in the vault's flooded threshold, wringing out her sleeves, water still streaming off her from the swim beneath the falls. Behind her, Maren was hauling Dain up onto the stone lip by the collar of his coat like a drowned cat. The three keys — brass, iron, and Corvane's stolen signet — sat in a row on a flat rock where Tam had placed them with the reverence of a man laying out surgical tools.

The gold could wait. Tam didn't even look at it.

* * *

They had come to the falls at dawn, after two days hiding in the sea cave while the fleet's search parties combed the coastline for a barge that no longer existed. The *Lament* lay broken on the rocks below the drop, her hull split open like a fruit, exactly where the sixth tide had thrown her when Maren threaded the burning gate. Everything they'd risked — the gate, the fire, the chain-shot that had nearly taken Maren's head off — had bought them this: a wreck on the shore beneath a curtain of falling water, and behind it, if Tam's charts were right, a door.

His charts. Forged, every one of them, or so the magistrates of Lowmark had said when they clapped him in irons. *Forged from survivors' testimony*, he'd told them then, and they'd laughed. But the survivors had been right. The Lament's hold had carried the second key and the manifests, just as old Sefa's people remembered. And the manifests, read correctly, pointed here — to the vault the Vessany engineers had cut into the rock behind the falls, because a nation that saw itself drowning does not hide its treasure where thieves can sail to it. It hides it where only its own charts can find the way.

The way was a ledge. Of course it was a ledge. Eighteen inches wide, slick with spray, running along the cliff face behind the waterfall for forty yards to a seam in the rock that looked, from below, like nothing at all.

"I'll go first," Orsen had said, and Tam had watched her edge out into the white roar with the calm of a woman crossing a street, and he had hated her a little for it, and loved her a little too, in the way you love people who make your fears look small.

Now the door stood open. It had taken all three keys turning together — brass in the upper lock, iron below, signet dead center — and when the last tumbler fell, the sound had rolled through the rock like a bell tolling underwater.

* * *

Inside, the vault was dry.

That was the first miracle. Forty years beneath a waterfall, and the Vessany masons had cut the chamber so precisely, sealed it so perfectly, that the air inside was stale but sweet, like an attic full of old books. Tam stood just past the threshold breathing it in and felt something crack open in his chest.

He had spent eleven years imagining this room. In prison he had drawn it a hundred times on slate, on walls, on the backs of other men's letters home — always guessing, always inventing, because the survivors who dictated his charts had never seen the inside either. They had only known it existed. They had passed down its location the way you pass down a name, a prayer, a debt of honor: *the vault behind the falls, where everything we were is kept safe.*

Everything we were.

The gold was there, yes. Stacked in the back corner in sealed strongboxes, row upon row, the treasury of a nation packed away like luggage by people who believed — who *hoped* — they would come back for it. Tam registered it the way you register furniture in a stranger's house. Then he saw the shelves.

They lined three walls, cut directly into the living rock, and every shelf was full. Ledgers. Deeds. Ship manifests. Birth records. Marriage contracts. Land grants. School rosters. A nation's entire administrative memory, inked on vellum and rag paper, catalogued in a hand so disciplined it might have been typeset. There were thousands of volumes. Tens of thousands of pages. Every legal fact that proved Vessany had existed — not as a story, not as a rumor, but as a chartered, recognized, sovereign thing with borders and banks and citizens whose names were written down.

"Oh," Tam said. Just that. He put his hand flat against the nearest shelf and left it there.

"What is it?" Maren asked from the doorway. She had a lantern up, and the light moved across the shelves like water.

"It's not a treasury." His voice was steadier now, because the cartographer in him had taken over, and the cartographer in him was the only part of himself he'd ever fully trusted. "I mean — it is, but that's the smallest thing in here. This is the *archive*. The whole archive. Charters, censuses, court records—" He pulled a volume at random and opened it, and his breath caught. "This is a land registry. Parcels, owners, dates of title. You could reconstruct the entire country from these shelves."

Dain had gone very quiet. He was standing near the strongboxes with his arms folded, and Tam saw him looking at the archive the way a man looks at evidence of a crime scene he'd only ever heard described.

"The ledger," Dain said finally. "My father's ledger lists what he took. Ships burned, cargo sold, buyers' names. I thought that was the whole account." He shook his head slowly. "It's half an account. This is the other half. What was *taken from*. Every one of these names—"

"—is a family he robbed," Tam finished.

The words hung in the spray-damp air. Outside, the falls thundered on, indifferent, beautiful, the same sound it had made the day Vessany died.

Orsen crouched beside the strongbox she'd already cracked open and lifted out the scroll — the charter — and brought it to Tam without a word. She'd understood before he had. Of course she had. She was the one who'd pulled the first chest from the floodwater; she'd been carrying proof since the day this started, even when none of them knew what it was proof *of*.

Tam broke the near seal with his thumbnail. The wax crumbled, forty years old, and the scroll unrolled across the flat rock between the three keys like a road opening.

* * *

He read it aloud. He didn't decide to; the words simply demanded it, the way scripture demands reading in a voice.

"*Charter of Record, Sovereign Nation of Vessany, ratified by treaty under the signatures of the River Compact powers, bearing the great seal and countersigned by the accredited envoys of Corvane Province, Harrowmere, and the Free Ports.*"

He stopped. Looked up.

"Corvane Province signed this," he said. "Corvane *countersigned* it. Their envoy's name is right here — Aldous Renner-Corvane, plenipotentiary." He tapped the faded signature. "That would be the magistrate's grandfather. The Corvane family put their own hand to the document guaranteeing Vessany's sovereignty. And then twenty years later the magistrate sank her refugee fleet and sold her treasury piece by piece, and called it salvage rights, and no court ever heard otherwise — because the one document that could prove otherwise was locked behind a waterfall."

"Not locked," Maren said. "*Kept.* There's a difference. Her people kept it safe. That's not the same thing at all."

Tam looked at her. She was leaning against the doorframe, lantern in hand, watching him with those flat gray eyes that gave away nothing except, occasionally, everything.

"You knew," he said. "You suspected. That's why you wanted passage rights to the wreck. Not the gold."

"I wanted whatever was truest down there." She shrugged. "Gold spends. Truth *compels*. My mother taught me that, back when she still taught me anything." Something flickered across her face and was gone. "Read the rest."

He read the rest.

The charter ran to six pages, and page four was the one that mattered — the clause the Vessany archivists had sealed separately, wrapped in its own leather sleeve, marked with a red thread. Tam unwound the thread with fingers that were not quite steady.

"*In the event of the dissolution of the Sovereign Nation of Vessany by force, fraud, or act of war, all assets, territories, and records herein revert to the lawful custody of the Vessany people, and any party found to have profited from said dissolution shall stand liable before any court of the River Compact powers, per the terms of Article Nine, without limit of time.*"

"Without limit of time," Dain repeated softly.

"Any court of the River Compact powers," Tam said. "Do you understand what that means? It means the law isn't finished with your father. It was never finished with him. He's spent thirty years making sure no court would ever see this document — burning archives, buying clerks, drowning witnesses. But he couldn't burn *this*, because he never found it, because her people hid it better than he could hunt it." He looked around the vault, at the shelves, at the strongboxes, at the patient dark. "Everything he did — the fleet, the kill order, the keys, all of it — was him trying to destroy a piece of paper. Because a piece of paper is the only thing in the world that can hang him."

* * *

They worked through the morning.

There was no discussion about it; the work simply organized itself around them, the way real work does. Maren ferried loads along the ledge to the camp above the falls, moving with a sureness on the wet stone that made Tam's stomach clench every time he watched her. Dain pried the strongboxes and inventoried their contents in a neat column, using the back of his father's own ledger for scratch paper, which Tam decided was the single most satisfying act of vandalism he had ever witnessed. Orsen stood watch at the mouth of the falls, because Orsen always stood watch, because Orsen trusted terrain and weather and nothing else on this earth — though Tam had noticed, lately, that she trusted the three of them a little more than she admitted.

And Tam read.

He read until his eyes burned. He read land registries and shipping bonds and the minutes of the last session of the Vessany assembly, adjourned forever on a Tuesday in the spring of the drowning. He read a school roster with four hundred children's names, ages six to fourteen, most of whom had been aboard the refugee ships. He read a letter, unsent, found pressed between two ledgers, from a clerk named Ilva Sarn to her sister: *We are packing the records tonight. If the ships go down, let the papers live. A country is not its ground. A country is what it wrote down.*

Tam set that letter down very carefully, and walked to the edge of the vault, and stood in the cold spray for a long time, and no one said anything to him, which was its own kind of mercy.

When he came back, his face was composed and his hands were steady, and both were lies, and he had been telling professional lies for eleven years, so they held.

"We take all of it," he said. "Not the gold first. The records. Gold is heavy and everyone wants it; records are heavy and nobody wants them, which is why they survive. We wrap the charter separately — it travels on my body or not at all. The manifests and the school roster next. Then the registries. The gold comes last, if it fits."

"And if the fleet finds us before it fits?" Dain asked.

Tam met his eyes. "Then the gold stays and the paper goes. Every time. Without a vote."

For once, nobody argued with him. Even Orsen, from her post at the falls, gave a single nod, which from Orsen was a speech.

* * *

It was near midday when Maren, coming back along the ledge with the fourth load, went still at the vault mouth and raised one hand — the flat-palm signal, *hold, listen*.

The falls filled the world with noise, but underneath the noise Tam caught it: a low rhythmic pulse, felt more than heard, coming up through the rock. Oars. Many oars, timed together. Or engines — the fleet's new chain-draggers had engines.

Orsen appeared at his shoulder, and her face, when he glanced at it, had gone to the flat calm he'd learned to fear more than any expression.

"How many?" she asked Maren.

Maren tilted her head, listening the way she listened to the river, her whole body an instrument. "Two ships at least. Coming around the headland. They're not searching anymore." She lowered her hand. "They're coming straight for the falls. Somebody told them exactly where we are."

Nobody said the obvious name. Nobody had to. The traitor from Lowmark was days behind them, exiled on some empty beach, but betrayal, Tam knew, was like ink: once spilled, it soaked through everything downstream.

"They can't reach the vault," Dain said. "The ledge won't take a boarding party. We have the high ground—"

"They don't need the vault," Orsen said quietly. "They need the exit. One ledge, forty yards, single file. They can wait us out until the tide turns and starve us off it. Or they can put fire arrows into the brush above the falls and smoke us out." She looked at Tam. "How long to move the archive?"

"Days. With luck, two."

"We don't have two hours."

The pulse of the oars grew louder beneath the thunder of the water. Tam looked back at the shelves — at the school roster, at Ilva Sarn's unsent letter, at forty years of a murdered nation waiting in the dark to be carried into the light — and felt the old prison panic rise in his throat, the certainty that the world always arrived to take the true things away before they could matter.

Then Orsen slung her pack, checked the ledge with a guide's eye, and said the thing that turned his panic into purpose:

"Then we start now, and we get clever about it. Maren — how much of the *Lament*'s hull is still sound? Enough to float a raft?"

Maren smiled, and it was not a comforting smile. It was the smile she wore before rapids.

"Barely," she said. "Which is my favorite amount."

Above the falls, faint but unmistakable now, came the blast of a fleet whistle — long, long, short. The signal Tam had taught Dain to read. *Target sighted. Close and take.*

They had maybe an hour, and a nation to carry out through the rain of its own waterfall.

Tam picked up the charter, wrapped it in its oilcloth, and bound it to his chest with cord, tight, the way you bind the thing you cannot lose.

"Hold together," he murmured — to the paper, to the names inside it, to the whole drowned country riding against his heartbeat. "You've waited forty years. Hold together one hour more."

Behind them, downriver, the first mast slid around the headland.
