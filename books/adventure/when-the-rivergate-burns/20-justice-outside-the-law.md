## 20. Justice Outside the Law

The fleet came around the bend at dawn, and Orsen watched it come the way she'd once watched weather — counting masts instead of miles.

Fourteen ships. Corvane's colors snapping wet and heavy in the river fog, the magistrate's own barge riding fat at their center like a spider drawn to its web. Behind her, the falls thundered, white water veiling the vault mouth where Tam knelt over an open chest of documents that could hang a man twice over.

"They'll be here within the hour," Maren said. She stood hip-deep in the shallows, hauling the shattered remains of their barge onto the gravel bar as if wreckage were something you could salvage by stubbornness alone. "Maybe less. That current runs fast when it's angry."

"It's always angry," Orsen said.

"Then we've got half an hour."

Dain came down from the rocks at a run, his father's coat still buttoned wrong from the night before. "There's a trail up the north bank. We could make the high country before they land."

"And then what?" Tam didn't look up from the charter. His hands shook, but his voice was level. "We run until the gold runs out? Until he sends enough men to find us? No." He closed the chest with a click that sounded, to Orsen's ears, like a door shutting on every version of this story except one. "This ends here or it doesn't end."

Orsen looked at the falls. Then at the river, brown and swollen with the sixth tide, carrying the fleet toward them like a fist closing.

She had spent her whole life measuring distance because distance was honest. A mile was a mile whether you liked it or not. But standing there with fourteen warships bearing down and three keys in her pocket, she understood that some distances couldn't be walked alone — and that the farthest one, the one between a lie and the truth, was best crossed in company.

"Maren," she said. "How many of your river-folk live between here and Lowmark?"

Maren's eyes narrowed. "Ferry families? Salvage crews? The ones Corvane's chain-draggers have been robbing blind for six years?" A slow smile spread across her weathered face, and it was not a kind smile. "More than he'd want to count."

"Can you call them?"

"I can light a fire they've been watching for since before you were born." She was already moving. "But hear me, Montrose — once I light it, they come for blood. Yours had better be worth theirs."

"It is," Orsen said. "It's Vessany's."

* * *

The fire Maren built was no ordinary signal. She stacked driftwood in a pattern Orsen didn't recognize — three spars crossed over a ring of stones — and fed it with something from a pouch at her belt that burned green and smelled like a storm about to break. The smoke climbed straight up through the falling spray of the falls, a green pillar visible, Maren said, from every bend of the river for twenty miles.

Then they waited, and waiting was the hardest work Orsen had ever done.

Tam read the charter aloud twice more, quietly, committing it to memory the way he'd once committed coastlines. Dain paced. The old woman from the survivor village — Sefa, who had recognized the tide-key in the hidden channel — arrived within the hour with eleven of her people, walking out of the hills as if the green smoke had called them by name. She carried a ledger bound in eel-skin, soft as cloth from handling, and when Orsen asked what it held, Sefa said only: "Names. Every family. Every ship. Everything he took."

By midday, boats began appearing.

They came downriver first — coracles and patched skiffs, a ferry barge older than any of its passengers, fishing punts with their nets stowed. They came overland too, salvagers with mud to the knees and chains over their shoulders, men and women who made their living off the river's leavings and had watched Corvane's fleet claim those leavings by decree for years. By the time the sun stood two fists above the ridge, better than two hundred people lined the gravel bar and the rocks above the falls, armed with boathooks, skinning knives, and a silence heavier than any weapon.

And then the fleet rounded the last bend.

Corvane's flagship led, oars beating, marines ranked along the rails in polished breastplates. The other thirteen followed in formation, chain-rigging visible on their decks — the great drags they used to strip the riverbed, engines of theft dressed up as law. At the sight of the crowd on the banks, the oars backed water. Fourteen ships swung and steadied, drifting, while longboats dropped away and began pulling for shore.

At their center came a single figure in a boat of black lacquer, standing upright, unafraid. Magistrate Corvane wore grey and silver, and on his right hand, where a signet should have been, Orsen saw the raw pale band of flesh where one had been.

He stepped onto the gravel bar ten yards from her, flanked by eight marines, and looked at the crowd as a man looks at weather he intends to ignore.

"Montrose," he said. "You've cost me a signet, a citadel feast, and a very good year. I'm told you also cost me a gate." His voice carried easily; he'd had practice making crowds feel small. "Give me the keys and the documents, and I'll let these people walk away. Most of them."

"You don't understand what's happening here," Orsen said.

"Enlighten me."

She turned — not to him, but to the crowd. To the ferry families and the salvage crews, to Sefa and her survivors, to the green smoke still rising behind her. Her heart was going hard, but her voice, when she found it, was the voice she used on a mountain when the weather turned: calm, clear, pitched to carry.

"My name is Orsen Montrose," she said. "Six days ago I pulled a chest out of the floodwaters with Vessany's seal on it. Since then I've learned what that seal means, and so has Magistrate Corvane — because it's his nightmare. Everything I know, he taught me himself. He wrote it down." She lifted the ledger Dain had carried across half the river country. "His own hand. His own numbers."

She opened it.

"The refugee ships *Kindly* and *Sister's Mercy*, sunk at the Narrows with four hundred souls aboard — ordered by Corvane, paid from his discretionary fund, logged as 'channel hazards cleared.' The treasury convoy *Lament*, scuttled behind the Silt Gates by his own garrison, its cargo sold piecemeal over nine years to buyers in three cities. The salvage rights of every independent crew on this river, revoked by decree and resold to his partners." She turned pages. She did not hurry. "It's all here. Names, dates, sums. And it's not the proof."

She nodded to Tam.

Tam rose from the rocks with the charter in both hands, and Orsen saw that his shaking had stopped. He held the document up so the front ranks could see the seals — three of them, pressed in wax gone dark with decades underwater, the arms of a nation that officially had never existed.

"This is Vessany's charter of record," Tam said. "Signed by its council, witnessed by its registry, sealed under law the day before Corvane sank it. It names every citizen, every holding, every debt owed to every family on this river. Under the old compact — the compact every magistrate swears to uphold — this document is *binding*. Vessany was never legally dissolved. It was drowned. Which means—" his voice cracked, and he steadied it, "—which means every man who took its gold took it from a living nation. And every court in the lowlands that honors the compact must hear this charter read aloud."

"Forgery," Corvane said flatly. "The cartographer's forgery. I had him imprisoned for less."

"You had him imprisoned for *accurate*," Orsen said. "That was your mistake. You thought the truth needed forging."

She turned to the fleet. To the rail-lines of marines, to the captains on their quarterdecks, to the salvage crews whose contracts Corvane had signed and broken and signed again. She raised her voice to reach the water.

"Captains! You've dragged his chains up and down this river for years. Ask yourselves what he pays you with. Ask yourselves whose gold it is. Sefa—"

The old woman stepped forward and opened the eel-skin ledger. "I will read names," she said, in an accent worn smooth as river stone. "If any man on those ships has a grandmother from Vessany, he will hear her family's name. If any man has taken salvage from these waters, he will hear his own contract read back to him — and the clause where Corvane kept the share." She looked down at the page. "*Aldous Merren, captain of the* Kestrel. Contracted 60 percent of salvage rights, received 12. Year one. Year two—"

"That's enough," Corvane said.

"—year two, 9 percent. Year three—"

"*Enough.*" He turned to his marines. "Take the documents. Take her. Kill anyone who objects."

For a moment, nothing moved. Eight marines looked at two hundred people on the bank and fourteen ships' worth of witnesses on the water, and Orsen watched the arithmetic happen behind their eyes — the same arithmetic she'd done a hundred times on bad routes, weighing the ground ahead against the ground behind.

Then Captain Aldous Merren of the *Kestrel* stepped off his longboat onto the gravel bar, and the arithmetic finished.

"Magistrate," Merren said, and his voice was not loud, which somehow made it worse. "My grandfather ferried refugees out of Vessany. My contract says sixty percent. I have twelve years of receipts that say otherwise." He looked at his fellow captains along the line of ships. "How say the rest of you?"

What happened next, Orsen would remember all her life in fragments, the way she remembered avalanches — not as motion but as a change in what motion meant.

A shout from the *Kestrel*'s deck. Then another ship. Then three. Marines grounding their pikes — not all, but enough, and the ones who didn't found themselves standing alone. Corvane's lacquer boat pushed off empty. His own barge crew refused the lines. Somewhere in the crowd a ferryman began to sing, low and ragged, a song in a language Orsen didn't know, and half the bank picked it up, and the sound rolled across the water like the sixth tide itself.

Corvane stood very still on the gravel, eight marines become none, and for the first time since Orsen had seen him he looked his age.

"Dain," he said. His son stood at the edge of the crowd, his father's coat buttoned right at last. "Boy. Tell them it's forgery. Tell them what I taught you."

Dain walked forward slowly. He stopped an arm's length from his father, and Orsen saw him take out the private ledger — the one he'd carried since the ambush, the one that had cost him his name — and hold it out.

"I am telling them everything you taught me," Dain said. "That's the problem."

Merren's men took Corvane then, gently, almost respectfully, the way you handle something that might still be dangerous. He did not struggle. He looked past them all at the falls, at the vault behind the water, at the nation he had drowned coming up for air around him, and whatever he said next was lost under the roar.

* * *

It took until dusk to sort the river.

Merren proved an efficient man. By sundown, Corvane was chained in his own brig aboard his own barge, with a council of captains — Merren, two others, and, at Sefa's insistence, one gray-haired Vessany ferryman — empowered to carry him to the lowland courts under the old compact, charter in hand. "There are judges," Merren told Orsen, "who've been waiting nine years for paper like this. They just needed someone brave enough or stupid enough to bring it." He glanced at her. "You appear to be both."

The independent salvage crews got their contracts rewritten on the spot, Tam drafting clauses by lantern-light with Sefa dictating names from her ledger. The fleet's chain-ships were impounded. The green signal fire burned down to embers, and still people came, walking out of the dark along every trail, drawn by word traveling faster than any smoke.

Orsen stood apart from most of it, the way she always had, watching the fires on the gravel bar. Maren found her there with two tin cups of something hot and strong.

"You did it," Maren said.

"We did it."

"Don't argue with a witch, Montrose, it's bad luck." Maren handed her a cup. "Though I'll grant you one thing. All those years I lit that signal wondering if anyone would come." She looked out at the fires, at the singing, at Sefa surrounded by people writing down names. "Turns out everyone was just waiting for a reason."

Orsen drank. The cup was warm in her cold hands. Behind the falls, faint even through the roar, she could hear Tam and Dain still working in the vault by lamplight, cataloguing a nation's return.

"One thing left," she said.

Maren followed her gaze downstream, to where the barge lay at anchor with its prisoner, its escort of turned captains, its cargo of legal proof. Beyond it, the dark river ran on toward the lowlands, toward courts and clerks and a reckoning nine years overdue.

"Aye," Maren said. "Somebody has to carry it down. And somebody has to stay and see the gold gets home." She smiled into her cup. "Reckon we'll fight about who does which tomorrow."

Above them, on the ridge, the first stars came out over the falls — and Orsen, who had measured her whole life in miles, caught herself measuring something else entirely: the distance between the woman who'd walked into the flood season alone and the one standing here now, and finding it the longest journey she'd ever made without taking a single step.

Downriver, a lantern swung at the barge's stern, and Captain Merren's voice carried across the water, calling for volunteers to sail with the dawn.
