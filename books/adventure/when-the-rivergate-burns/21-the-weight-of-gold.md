## 21. The Weight of Gold

The falls still roared three days after the arrest, but the camp below them had changed its sound. Where once there had been the clank of salvage chains and the barked orders of Corvane's fleet, now there were cooking fires, children's voices, and the low murmur of people reading names.

Dain stood at the edge of it with his father's ledger under one arm — the private one, the plunder record he'd carried out of the citadel in a saddlebag that had cost him everything else. He hadn't opened it since the falls. He wasn't sure he could.

The old woman they called Sefa Vess had set up her table on a flat rock near the waterline, the survivor village gathered behind her in a crescent. Her ledger was older than Corvane's, older than the war no one named anymore — a book of names written in faded iron-gall ink, each family of drowned Vessany recorded in a hand so fine it looked like stitching. She'd kept it for forty years, she said, because someone had to remember who was owed what.

Now the gold was here to pay it back.

* * *

They'd hauled the treasury up from the vault behind the falls over two days of brutal work. Orsen had organized it the way she organized everything — by terrain, by load, by who could carry what without breaking. The chests came up the wet stone in relays, Maren's barge-crew hauling on lines while Tam counted and re-counted, and by the second evening the treasure sat stacked in the open air where everyone could see it: Vessany's stolen third, the last piece of a nation's treasury, glinting in firelight like something embarrassed to be found.

Corvane's captains had helped. That was the strangest part. After Orsen read the charter aloud and the fleet turned, after the magistrate's own officers put him in irons on his own quarterdeck, the ships had become just ships again — crews of men and women who'd spent years dragging chains for a thief and hadn't known it, or had known it and looked away. Some of them left. Most stayed to help carry.

Dain watched them from a distance. He didn't know yet which kind he was.

"You're brooding," Maren said, appearing at his elbow with two tin cups and handing him one without asking. The river-witch smelled of woodsmoke and tar as always, and her eyes went past him to the crowd at Sefa's table. "Brooding's a luxury. You did the hard part."

"I gave my father to a mob."

"I gave a traitor to exile," she said. "Everyone gives somebody to something. Drink your tea."

He drank. It was terrible. It helped.

Down at the table, Sefa Vess opened her ledger to the first page and began to read.

* * *

She read names the way a priest reads scripture — slowly, with weight, letting each one land. And beside every name she spoke a number, and beside every number a chest was opened, and coins were weighed into cloth sacks by Tam's careful hands and Orsen's steady ones.

Dain had expected chaos. He'd expected greed — he was Corvane's son; greed was the family weather. Instead he watched a fisherman from the survivor village receive a sack heavy enough to buy a fleet and stand holding it like it might bite him. He watched a woman with two children count out nothing at all, just touch the coins through the cloth, once, and then ask Sefa if the number was right, because surely it was too much.

"It's not enough," Sefa said, every time. "It's not enough. But it's yours."

The ledger didn't lie, and neither did the charter. Every name in Sefa's book matched an entry in Corvane's plunder record — Dain had checked them against each other the night before, alone, by lantern, until his eyes burned. Family by family, ship by ship, the two books told one story: a nation drowned on purpose, its treasury sold piecemeal, its survivors scattered and billed for their own rescue. His father's handwriting was neat throughout. That was the detail that would stay with him longest. Not rage in the ink. Just neatness. Bookkeeping.

"Vireo," Sefa called, and Tam flinched.

The cartographer stepped forward like a man approaching his own execution. Dain knew why. Tam had forged Vessany charts for smugglers — had traced the drowned coastline for profit while its people starved in exile. He'd confessed it on the barge, shame-faced, and Orsen had said only, "Then you'll know the waters better than anyone." But standing before Sefa's table, with the whole village watching, forgery looked different than it had on the river.

"You drew our coast," Sefa said.

"Yes, ma'am. I'm sorry—"

"Hold." She turned pages. Found one. Turned the ledger toward him. "This chart. The eastern shallows, before the drowning. My grandmother's fishing grounds are marked here — the eel banks off Cape Merren. No living person remembered those banks. We thought they were lost with everything else."

Tam leaned in. His face went strange. "I... copied them from somewhere. A man in Lowmark sold me a fragment, years ago. I assumed it was genuine antique. I built the rest around it."

"It is genuine," Sefa said. "My grandmother sold it to feed her children the winter after the drowning. She wept doing it. And now her banks are on your paper, and your paper is good, and every boat in this village will fish those waters again because a stranger's hand preserved them." She closed the ledger gently. "You will draw us new charts, Vireo. All of Vessany, restored. And you will be paid in the coin of the nation you thought you'd betrayed."

Tam sat down on a crate afterward and didn't speak for a long time. When Dain passed him, the cartographer said, quietly, "I came looking for redemption and got a commission instead. That's somehow worse. Now I have to do it well."

* * *

By the third day the sacks had gone out to nearly every name in the book, and the shape of the thing became visible: not a treasure anymore, but a future. Boats were already being talked about — real ones, not leaky barges. A school, someone said. A harbor at the mouth of the river, if the independent salvagers would sell their moorings, and after Orsen's reading, they would, and gladly.

Which left the question of Dain Corvane.

Sefa Vess saved him for last. He understood why when she turned to the final page of her ledger and read out a name he'd never heard spoken aloud: "Corvane. Illegitimate line. One entry."

The camp went quiet. Orsen, stacking the last empty chests, stopped moving. Maren's hand found her knife out of pure habit and then, deliberately, released it.

"That name," Sefa said, "was entered by my mother, who kept the book before me. A Vessany woman married a Rivergate tax official two generations back. Their daughter's daughter married up, and up again, and the blood thinned into respectability until nobody remembered it was ours." She looked at Dain across the table, her eyes old and unblinking. "Your great-grandmother was born on our coast. Which means the magistrate stole from his own kin. And which means, boy, that by the ledger, there is an inheritance with your name on it."

Dain's mouth was dry. He'd rehearsed nothing, because he hadn't known there was anything to rehearse. All the way down the river he'd told himself he wanted justice — justice for the drowned, justice for the burned fishing camps, justice even for the traitor he'd exiled rather than killed. Justice was clean. Inheritance was not. Inheritance meant taking a share of the gold his father had murdered for, laundering blood money through a technicality of genealogy.

"I can't," he started.

"You can," Sefa said, "and you will hear the number before you decide anything. That's how a ledger works. You don't get to refuse blind. Refusing blind is just pride wearing honesty's coat." She read the figure aloud. It was enormous. It was, he realized with a lurch, roughly the sum his father had paid to bribe the garrison commander at the Silt Gates — he'd seen the entry in the private ledger. The same money, offered twice, to opposite purposes.

The camp waited. Orsen's eyes found his — not pushing, never pushing, just present, the way she'd been present at every impossible decision since the flooded timber. *Terrain doesn't judge,* she'd told him once. *It just shows you where the footing is.*

Dain looked at the gold. Then at the ledger. Then at the boats being sketched in the dirt by men who'd spent forty years with nothing.

"I decline the inheritance," he said, and heard his own voice come out steadier than he felt. "All of it. Distribute it among the families shorted by the fleet's last salvage run — Corvane's records show which ones. There's a list."

"There is always a list," Sefa murmured, almost smiling.

"But—" He stopped. Started again. This was the part he'd actually come to say, the part that had been building in him since the burning gate, since the canyon, since the night Maren threaded rapids no pilot survived and he understood that the river was a country too, with laws and languages, and that he'd lived his whole life next door to it illiterate. "But I want something else. If it can be given."

"Name it."

"To learn the river." He said it plainly, because anything dressed up would have sounded like what it was — a rich man's son asking to be remade. "Not salvage. Not charts. The river itself. How to read the water the way Maren does. How to run timber in flood. Where the channels go when the maps are wrong." He glanced at Maren, who was watching him with an expression he couldn't parse and didn't dare hope about. "I'm told the best teacher on this water is a witch who threatens her passengers."

"Mm," Maren said. "I do charge."

"I can pay in labor. I'm told I'm strong and I follow orders badly, which builds character in apprentices."

A laugh went through the camp — the first real one since the arrest. Even Orsen's mouth twitched.

Maren studied him for a long moment, the way she studied water before committing the barge to it. "Apprentices drown," she said finally. "That's the first lesson. Still want it?"

"Yes."

"Then be at the barge at dawn. Second lesson: dawn means dawn." She drained her tea and walked off toward the waterline, and Dain understood that he had just been given more than any inheritance could hold.

Sefa Vess made a small mark in her ledger — declined, entered, closed — and looked up at him with something that was almost kindness. "Your father wore a ring with our seal on it," she said. "He used it to lock away what he stole. You've just used the same blood to open it. That's the whole difference between you, boy. Remember it on the cold mornings."

* * *

He found Tam at dusk, sitting on the rocks above the falls with fresh paper weighted by stones, sketching. Dain sat down beside him and looked at what the cartographer was drawing and felt his throat tighten.

It was the coast. Not the smugglers' coast, not the drowned outline the fleet's charts showed — but Vessany whole. Harbors labeled. The eel banks off Cape Merren. Roads inland that hadn't existed in forty years, drawn anyway, in confident strokes, as if the country were simply waiting to grow back into its own map.

"You're drawing places that aren't there yet," Dain said.

"I'm drawing places that are going to be there," Tam said. "There's a difference. Ask any survivor." He tapped the paper. "Sefa wants the full survey by spring. Orsen knows the high passes better than any map alive. Maren can sound every channel on this river. And you—" he glanced sideways, grinning now, "—you're going to be the man who carries the instruments and learns why the river bends where it bends. Congratulations. You've joined the most useless expedition in history: the reconstruction of a drowned nation, by four people and a leaky barge."

"The barge sank."

"The barge *shattered*, which is different, and Maren swears the new hull will be better. She says the river owes her one." Tam blew graphite dust off the page. Below them, the falls thundered, and beyond the falls the river ran silver toward the sea, toward the coast, toward everything that came next.

Dain sat with his father's ledger on his knees one last time. Then he stood, carried it to Sefa's table, and set it down beside her older, finer book — the plunder record surrendered to the record of the wronged, one archive at last. She covered both with oilcloth against the evening damp, and that was that.

At dawn, true dawn, gray light on black water, he was at the barge.

Maren was already there, of course. She handed him a sounding pole and pointed at the current.

"First lesson," she said. "The river doesn't care whose son you are. Neither do I. Now pay attention — I only explain everything once, and then you spend the rest of your life catching up."

Upstream, Orsen stood on the bank watching them go, and raised one hand — not waving, exactly. Measuring. As if, for the first time in her life, she was counting something other than miles.

And far downriver, at the mouth of the delta where the water met the sea, sails were gathering — independent salvagers, survivor boats, the first ships of a fleet that belonged to no magistrate at all, coming upriver to see the country that had come back from the dead.
