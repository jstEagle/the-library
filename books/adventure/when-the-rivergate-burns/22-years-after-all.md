## 22. Years, After All

The pass above the Rivergate hadn't changed. That was the first thing Orsen noticed, and the thing that unsettled her most.

She had expected the mountains to feel different now — smaller, maybe, or gentler, the way a room shrinks when you return to it as an adult. But the switchbacks were as steep and mean as ever, the scree fields still slid underfoot like they resented being walked on, and the wind at the col still came off the icefields with the same flat indifference it always had. The high country didn't care that she'd burned a gate, toppled a magistrate, and given away a nation's treasury. It cared about footing and weather and whether you'd brought enough water. That was why she'd loved it in the first place. Mountains were honest in a way people took years to learn how to be.

She stopped on the shoulder of the trail and let the others catch up, which they did slowly, because she'd made them carry their own packs and none of them had her legs.

Maren came up first, of course. The ferrywoman climbed the way she steered — with a kind of contempt for the terrain, as if the mountain were just another river running uphill and she refused to be impressed by it. Her gray-streaked hair was braided back tight, her hands wrapped in the same fingerless gloves she wore at the tiller, and she was already scanning the ridgeline with the flat professional squint of someone who had spent thirty years reading water and found that rock was only water that had made up its mind.

"Your pass is a liar," Maren said, arriving. "It said half a mile to the top. That was an hour ago."

"The pass measures in miles," Orsen said. "I measure in miles too."

"You used to." Maren unslung her pack, drank, handed the skin over without being asked. "These days you keep stopping to look at things. Flowers. Clouds. Dain's terrible posture. A guide who stops to look at flowers is a guide who's decided the destination matters less than the company."

Orsen drank and didn't answer, because the answer was yes, and Maren knew it, and saying it out loud would have been like naming a new star — true, but unnecessary, since everyone could already see it burning.

Tam Vireo came next, red-faced and muttering, his map case slung across his chest like a bandolier. He'd redrawn the route three times since dawn, not because the route needed it but because his hands got restless when he wasn't drawing, and Orsen had learned a year ago that a restless Tam was a Tam one step from spiraling into some old guilt or other. Better he wore out his pencils than his conscience.

"There's a discrepancy," he announced, planting himself beside them. "My chart shows a cairn at the summit. The survey I based it on is forty years old. Cairns fall down. Someone should have verified—"

"We're verifying it right now," Orsen said.

"With our *legs*, Orsen. Verification should be done by mules."

Dain Corvane crested the last rise behind him, breathing hard, saying nothing, which was how Dain said most things. He'd filled out over the year — the citadel softness gone from him, replaced by rope-callus and river-shoulder — but he still carried himself like a man walking through a house where he used to live. He set his pack down, looked at the summit ridge, then at Orsen.

"How far?"

"Half a mile," Orsen said.

"He's lying," Maren said.

"I'm measuring in miles," Orsen said. "It's a courtesy. You'll thank me at the top."

They went up.

* * *

The cairn was still there.

That surprised her more than anything else on the mountain. Forty years of storms and thaw-freeze and the slow shrug of the slope itself, and the stones still stood in their rough cone against the sky, maybe a hand's width shorter than the survey charts claimed, leaning east like an old man into the wind. Somebody had built it well. Somebody had wanted it to last.

Orsen stood before it longer than she meant to.

A year ago — almost exactly a year ago, though she'd stopped counting in anniversaries somewhere around midwinter — she had stood on this same shoulder with a wealthy client named Aldous Renk, a man who measured everything in coin and paid for mountains the way he paid for wine: cheaply, and with a complaint attached. She remembered standing here with the whole valley laid out below her, the Rivergate a silver thread in the distance, and feeling nothing but the arithmetic of the descent ahead. Miles to camp. Miles to the trailhead. Miles until the fee, and then miles until the next contract, because a blacklisted guide didn't get to choose her work, only endure it.

She had measured her whole life that way. Distances between places, because distances between people couldn't be trusted. A mile was honest. A mile ended where it said it would end. People — clients, employers, the guild masters who'd struck her name from the rolls over a lie she still didn't fully understand — people always kept moving the ending.

And then the flood season had come, and she'd walked into it alone, exactly as planned, and hooked a chest out of the shallows, and every mile she'd ever counted had turned out to be leading here.

"Orsen." Tam's voice, careful. "You want us to do something, or are we having a moment? Because I can sketch either way."

"A moment," she said. "Then something."

She knelt at the base of the cairn and opened the oilcloth bundle she'd carried herself, refusing all offers, all the way up. Inside, wrapped in wool gone soft with handling, lay the seal of Vessany.

Brass, palm-sized, worn nearly smooth. The drowned nation's crest — the river and the mountain joined — still legible if you knew what you were looking at, and everyone standing on this ridge knew exactly what they were looking at. They'd spent six tides and a burning gate earning the right to know it.

The old woman in the survivor village had pressed it into Orsen's hands at midsummer, at the distribution, after the last of the gold had gone out by name and family line. *"You carried our keys home,"* she'd said. *"Carry this a while longer. Seals belong on high ground. That's where nations keep their promises."*

Orsen had understood it immediately, in the way she understood terrain — not as sentiment but as engineering. A cairn marked a route. It told travelers: someone passed this way; the way holds; follow. A nation's seal planted at the top of the world told every Vessany survivor who ever climbed here, every child of the scattered families who grew up hearing that their homeland was a rumor and a shame, the same thing.

Someone passed this way. The way holds. Follow.

"It goes at the center," she said. "Under the capstone. Not displayed — *set*. Like a keystone."

Tam crouched beside her, already sketching the placement, then stopped himself and put the pencil away. Some things shouldn't be drafted first. He helped her lift the capstone instead, both of them grunting, Maren steadying the stack below with one hand while holding her flask in the other, and Dain — Dain simply watched, arms folded, wearing the expression he'd worn at every burial and every blessing that year: a man learning how to be present for things without trying to own them or flee them.

Orsen set the seal into the gap. The stone came down over it with a sound like a door closing gently.

"There," Maren said. "Now it's a grave and a signpost both. Very efficient. Your people would've liked that."

"They would've," Orsen said. She realized her voice had gone rough and didn't fight it. "They measured in years, you know. Vessany. The old woman told me. Farmers, fishers, cartographers — they counted seasons, generations, how long a family had worked a bend of river. Miles were just what was between the years."

"And you?" Maren asked, and it wasn't teasing anymore.

Orsen looked west, where the sun had begun its long slide toward the passes, dragging gold across the cloud deck like a barge wake.

"I'm learning," she said.

* * *

They stayed.

That was the whole miracle of it, and Orsen only recognized it as a miracle because of what she did *not* do, which was check the light and calculate the descent.

Every instinct she owned said go. Sun maybe two hours from the ridgeline, and the scree field below the col turned treacherous in shadow, and a blacklisted guide — no. Not blacklisted. Not anymore. Tam's restored charts had gone to the guild with the charter of record, and the guild masters, faced with legal proof that the blacklist itself had been bought and paid for by Corvane's coin, had reinstated her with an apology so groveling she'd made them put it in writing, mostly because Tam wanted it framed. She was a licensed guide again. And a licensed guide knew you did not linger on summits at dusk.

But nobody moved, so she didn't either.

They built a small fire in a hollow below the cairn — Maren's idea, technically against every rule Orsen had ever taught, and Orsen allowed it, which told her more about the year than any ledger could. Tam spread his current project on a flat rock: the new atlas, the one he'd been drawing since winter. Vessany charts, redrawn from survivor memory and his own forged-and-then-redeemed hand, the drowned coastline rendered in full detail, towns labeled, river-bends named, a nation returned to paper before it could be returned to anything else.

"Page nine," he said, tapping it. "The Rivergate delta. I've left the city blank."

"Why?" Dain asked.

"Because cities change fast, and I don't draw rumors." Tam glanced up. "Word from Lowmark says the interim council is voting on the harbor rebuild next month. When they decide, I'll draw what's real. Until then, blank space is honest."

Dain nodded slowly. He'd taken a seat on the rocks facing the sunset, and the low light caught the scar along his jaw — the graze from the canyon, the one he never talked about except to point out, dryly, that his father's marksmen had always been mediocre. He'd declined the inheritance a year ago. All of it. Every recovered coin that bore even a whiff of Corvane's original plunder had gone into the survivors' fund, and Dain had kept only his father's private ledger — not for the money it described, but because, he said, someone in the family ought to know exactly what had been done, and carry it honestly.

He'd asked Orsen for something else instead. Back at the falls, in the aftermath, with the fleet captains still hauling his father away in chains: *"Teach me the river. Not the salvage trade. The river itself."* And she had, and he was competent now, and sometimes — poling a barge through morning fog, or reading a rapid's tongue before committing — he wore an expression she recognized from her own mirror. The look of a person who has finally found a unit of measurement that doesn't lie.

"Question," Maren said, breaking the quiet. She was lying back on her pack, hands laced behind her head, watching the first stars. "A year ago, if someone had told you four outcasts would be sitting on a mountaintop dividing a flask and arguing about cartography, what would you have said?"

"I'd have said they were lost," Orsen said.

"Lost how?"

"Off-route. Off-chart." She turned the flask in her hands. "I'd have offered to guide them somewhere safe and charged full rate."

Tam laughed. Dain almost smiled, which for Dain was a festival. Maren reached over and took the flask back without apology.

"And now?" she asked.

Orsen considered the question the way she considered a route — honestly, from all approaches.

"Now I think maybe we weren't lost. Maybe we were just the first ones on the new chart." She nodded at Tam's atlas. "Blank space isn't empty. It's just territory nobody's drawn yet. We got drawn. That's all."

Nobody spoke for a while after that, which was its own kind of agreement.

The sun touched the far passes and began to pour itself out along the snowfields, and the whole western sky went the color of the Lament's brass fittings — deep, patient gold, the gold of things that had waited a long time underwater and knew the value of surfacing slowly. Orsen sat and watched it, and did not count the minutes remaining, and did not calculate the descent, and did not measure anything at all.

She just stayed.

When the light finally failed and the cold came down off the icefields, they doused the fire and shouldered packs and started down by starlight, Orsen leading, four sets of boots finding the switchbacks by memory and trust. Below them, faint in the dark, the Rivergate caught the moon — a silver thread again, but a free one now, running to a sea that had given back everything it owed.

Halfway to camp, Maren drew level with her and matched her stride.

"So," the ferrywoman said. "Same time next year?"

Orsen looked at her. At the woman who'd steered a burning gate because the crew voted to try. At Tam, behind them, already murmuring page-nine notes to himself in the dark. At Dain, silent and steady at the rear, watching their backs the way he'd learned to watch instead of flee.

A year ago she'd have answered in miles.

"Same time next year," Orsen said. "And the year after. I'll put it on the chart."

Maren grinned and clapped her shoulder once, hard, the way river people did instead of embracing, and the four of them went down together through the starlit passes — no longer counting the distance, only the company, and finding that the company, unlike the miles, never ran out.
