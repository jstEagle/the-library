## 1. The Wreck Nobody Names

The wreck we were robbing that morning had no name, which is how I liked them. Named wrecks have histories, histories have owners, and owners — especially owners who fly the navy pennant — tend to come looking for their property. An unnamed wreck was just a hole in the river with good manners, sitting quiet at forty feet and letting you take what it offered.

The *Magpie* hung at anchor over the site in the gray half-light before dawn, and I stood on her deck watching Juno suit up, because that was the part of the job I hated most. Not the diving. The watching.

"Buoyancy check," I said.

"Done it twice."

"Do it again."

Juno rolled her eyes — she was nineteen and had perfected the eye-roll into something close to a martial art — but she ran the check a third time while I held the rail. She was the best diver I'd ever put in the water, and I don't say that lightly, because I've put a lot of people in the water and some of them are still there. She'd come to me two years back off a grain barge with rope-burned hands and nothing to her name but a dive mask and a temper, and somewhere between then and now she'd become the thing I didn't have a word for. Family, maybe. But family's a word from Ash Hollow, and I didn't spend words from Ash Hollow.

"Bottom time's twenty minutes," I said. "Not twenty-five. Twenty."

"You always say that."

"And you always push it."

"That's why you love me." She spat over the side, checked her regulator one last time, and went down the ladder into the brown water like she was stepping off a porch.

I watched her bubbles climb away south with the current, and behind me the crew got to work without being told. That's the mark of a real crew, if you're asking: nobody needs telling. Dell cranked the winch and cursed it in three languages he'd invented himself. Old Tam sat on the cargo hatch mending net with fingers like knotted rope, and Priya ran the line counter, calling depth in a flat voice that never changed no matter what happened above or below it.

Forty feet. Forty-two. Then Juno's voice came up the comm line, tinny and cheerful.

"Big hold. Doors buckled inward. There's machinery down here nobody's ever catalogued."

"Don't touch anything you can't lift," I said.

"I never do."

She did, though. That was the trouble with Juno. Give her a sealed compartment and a crowbar and she'd open both.

* * *

The first sign of trouble wasn't a sound. It was Priya going quiet mid-count.

"Sable," she said. "Come look at this."

I crossed to the console. On the water's surface, maybe six hundred yards downstream, a wake was cutting against the current. Nothing unusual in the Delta — barges run all hours — except this wake was too straight, too steady, and moving too slow. Barges hurry or they lumber. This thing glided.

"Navy patrol?" Dell asked.

"Navy patrols don't glide," I said. "They wallow. They want you to see them coming."

I raised Juno on the comm. "Juno. Wrap it up. Ten minutes, not twenty."

Static. Then her voice, thinner than before. "Almost through the inner bulkhead. There's a strongbox—"

"Juno."

"—it's not even locked, Sable, it's just sitting here—"

The wake was four hundred yards out now, and I could see the shape under the morning haze. Low hull. Gun mount forward, canvas-covered but canvas doesn't hide a gun mount from people who grew up counting them. And it wasn't patrolling. It was holding station. Holding station over us.

"Juno, surface now. That's an order."

"I'm through! Hold on—" A scrape of metal on the line, her breathing hard. "There's paperwork in here. Sealed orders, navy stamps—"

"Leave it."

"It's why they're watching us, isn't it? Whatever this boat was carrying—"

The river answered before I could. Through my boots I felt it first — a deep, wrong shudder, like the Delta clearing its throat. Then the comm line filled with a groan of stressed steel and the hiss of silt collapsing, and Juno's voice cut to a shout: "Bulkhead's going! The whole frame's—"

The bubble plume on the surface stuttered. Stopped. Started again, ragged.

Every plan I had fell away except the oldest one. "Dell, get the spare bottle to the ladder. Priya, count her up. Tam—" But Tam was already moving, hauling the rescue line off its cleat with those knotted hands, and I was going over the rail with a mask in my teeth before anyone could tell me it was stupid.

The Delta at forty feet is the color of strong tea and about as informative. I followed her line down by feel, by the pull of current, by the sound — and there it was, a shriek of metal grinding metal, and then silence, the worst sound underwater, because silence means either everything's fine or nothing is.

I found her at the collapsed frame. The inner bulkhead had folded like wet cardboard and dropped a crossbeam across the passage out, pinning her tank harness against the doorjamb. Her lamp found me through the murk. She wasn't panicking. That scared me more than screaming would have.

I got my knife under the beam and levered, and the river pushed back, and I thought — not for the first time and not for the last — about how the water takes what it wants and gives back only what it feels like. I thought about a different collapse, years ago, a different body I hadn't reached in time.

Then Tam's rescue line snapped taut around the beam from above, Dell's muscle on the other end, and the frame shifted an inch. An inch was enough. Juno twisted free, kicked clear, and we went up together through the tea-colored dark, her hand fisted in my sleeve like when she was new and scared, though she'd deny it later.

We broke the surface thirty yards from the *Magpie*, and the navy gunboat was two hundred yards out and closing, its wake finally honest, wallowing like a drunk uncle.

* * *

They didn't board us. That was the strange part. The gunboat came alongside at a distance — close enough to read the numbers off her bow, far enough to pretend she wasn't there — and held position for an hour while we sat on deck dripping and pretending we weren't watched. Nobody hailed us. Nobody ran up flags. They just looked, the way a cat looks at a hole it knows something lives in.

"They've been on us three weeks," Priya said quietly, beside me at the rail. "Since the Corvo Channel job. I checked the harbor logs at Marsh Landing. Every move we make, there's a hull keeping station two miles back."

"Why?" Dell asked. "We're scavengers. We're beneath their notice. That's the whole point of being scavengers."

"Unless they're waiting for us to find something," Tam said, not looking up from his net. It was the most words Tam had spent in a month, and every one of them landed like a stone in still water.

I looked at the gunboat, and the gunboat looked at me, and I understood that the quiet life I'd built — the small jobs, the unnamed wrecks, the careful distances — wasn't quiet anymore. It was a held breath. And held breaths end.

* * *

She docked beside us at dusk, at the fuel pier at Graywater Bend, as if she'd been invited. The *Vance Line* colors on her stern were faded but unmistakable, and the woman at her rail needed no introduction, because Mara Vance had cost me a crew once already and a man doesn't forget the face of the person who did it.

"Sable Sterling," she called down. "Still stealing from holes in the river?"

"Still selling people out of them," I said.

Her crew threw lines across before I could order them thrown back, and Mara came down our gangway like she owned it, past Juno — who squared up until I put a hand on her shoulder — past Dell and Priya and old Tam, who looked at her the way men look at weather they remember.

"Ash Hollow," she said, stopping in front of me. No greeting. No apology. Just the name, dropped between us like a coin down a well.

"There's nothing at Ash Hollow but water."

"There's a ledger," she said. "In the flooded town hall, under forty feet of reservoir. Proof the dam collapse wasn't an accident. And your navy friends upstream—" she tipped her chin toward the gunboat, still hanging on the horizon like a bad memory, "—are going to burn the whole valley to keep it that way. I'm offering you a contract, Sable. One dive. Upstream of every promise you've made."

Juno's hand found my sleeve — the same grip as underwater, whether she knew it or not. Behind me, Tam set down his net for the second time that day.

And I heard myself say the last thing I ever expected to say again:

"When do we run the river?"
