## 2. Upstream of Every Promise

The river at night is a liar, and I've learned to read its tells.

Mara Vance knows the Rivergate Delta the way a thief knows a lock—by feel, in the dark, with her heart hammering. From the pilothouse of the *Corvid's Debt*, she watches the black water slide past, broken here and there by the pale scars of drowned treetops. Somewhere behind them, a navy patrol boat's searchlight sweeps the far channel, patient as a lighthouse that hates you.

"Kill the running lights," she says.

"They've been dead since the second bend," Sable answers from the wheel. His voice is flat as slate. "You'd know that if you were watching the river instead of the mirror."

Mara doesn't dignify that with a look. She's spent six years perfecting the art of not looking at Sable Sterling. It's a discipline, like free-diving. You learn to ignore the burning.

The *Debt* runs dark through the narrows, engines muffled by whatever black magic Juno has worked on the exhaust. The deckhand in question crouches near the stern rail, coiling line with the exaggerated calm of someone who'd rather be anywhere else. Mara has catalogued the crew in two days of uneasy cohabitation: Juno, all sharp elbows and sharper silences; Okafor at the engine, humming hymns off-key when he thinks no one can hear; the Voss twins who communicate in eyebrow dialect. And Sable, holding the wheel like it owes him money.

They pass the first navy checkpoint before dawn—a chain of buoys strung with sensor cable across the channel, marked by a single green lamp. Sable takes them over it at idle, drifting on the current, everyone breathing through their teeth while the hull scrapes something soft and forgiving. Weed, probably. Or the river's patience wearing thin.

Then they're through, and upstream, where the Delta's braided channels give way to the gorge country, and the water stops being a maze and becomes a throat.

* * *

It's Okafor who forces the conversation, because Okafor believes in confronting things, an affliction Mara finds quaint.

They're anchored in a flooded quarry, hidden under an overhang of rock, rain starting to tap the canopy like fingers testing a lock. The crew gathers in the cabin, lamplit, and Okafor sets his mug down with the deliberate clank of a man calling a meeting nobody wants.

"So," he says. "Ledger."

Mara had planned for this. She'd rehearsed versions of it on the run north—versions where she was honest, versions where she was strategic, versions where she was both, which is the only kind she trusts. She looks around the cabin: the twins, arms crossed in mirrored suspicion; Juno, perched on the chart table like a gargoyle with opinions; Sable leaning against the doorframe, deliberately outside the circle, which is very him. Always half a step out of every room he's in.

"The ledger from the Ash Hollow courthouse vault," she says. "It records the dam inspections in the three years before the collapse. All of them. Including the ones that were ordered removed from the official record."

"Ordered by who?" one twin asks.

"Navy engineers. On behalf of the Rivergate Authority." She lets that sit. "My father was the inspector who signed the last report. The one saying the dam was sound. He was hanged for negligence after the flood—or ruined, depending on which version of history you prefer—and I have spent six years listening to people spit his name. The ledger proves the reports were altered. That he flagged the fractures and was made to sign a clean copy."

"And you need us why?" This from Juno, quiet, chin on knees. "You've got money. You've got divers."

"I've got money and divers who won't go within ten miles of Ash Hollow," Mara says. "Because the navy burned every salvage license issued for that stretch of river, and because everyone who dives there either drowns or comes up changed." She looks at Sable. "I need someone the water owes."

The silence stretches. Rain thickens against the roof.

"You knew about the altered reports," Sable says finally. Not a question. "Before the collapse."

Here it is, then. The part she hadn't rehearsed well enough.

"My father told me," she says. "The night before he went back upriver to testify. He said if anything happened to him, the truth was in the vault, and no one would ever believe a word of it unless—" She stops.

"Unless what?"

"Unless someone pulled it out of the water themselves. Physical paper. Original ink. Because everything else—the copies, the testimony, the affidavits—it all burns so conveniently." Her voice stays level. Practice. "He drowned in the collapse, Captain. Along with a hundred and eleven other people. So yes. I knew. I was nineteen, and I did nothing, because I was busy hating him for signing the report in the first place. By the time I understood what he'd actually done, the navy owned the valley and my name wasn't worth the rope they'd hang me with either."

"You could have come to me," Sable says. "Six years ago. Before any of this."

That lands somewhere under her ribs, and she hates that it does. "Come to you? You disappeared, Sterling. You cut your name out of the record like a tumor. Nobody hires a ghost, and nobody trusts one."

"I trusted my team once," Sable says, and the temperature in the cabin drops. "Right up until the night somebody sold our dive plan to the Authority. Right up until four of them went into the water at Ash Hollow and only I came out."

The twins exchange a glance. Juno goes very still.

"That's not what happened," Mara says.

"No?"

"No." She stands now, done sitting, done rehearsing. "Your team didn't drown because of a leak. They drowned because your brother took them into the intake channel during a storm surge to reach the vault himself. He had his own buyer lined up, Sterling. Someone in the capital. He wasn't doing it for truth—he was doing it for leverage, same as anyone would. And the surge came early."

The words leave her and she immediately wishes some of them back—not the accusation, but the certainty she'd dressed it in. Because she's carried another version of that night for six years too, folded small, and it doesn't match this one, and she has never let the two versions touch.

Sable hasn't moved. That's the terrible thing—how still he gets, like deep water. "His name," he says quietly, "was Elian."

And Mara realizes, with a cold sliding feeling, that she has never once heard Sable say it aloud. Not in six years. Not once. The whole crew has gone careful and quiet around a name none of them know, and here it is, laid on the lamplit table between them like something dredged up.

"Sable," Juno says softly. A warning, or a hand extended. Hard to tell.

"He was twenty-four," Sable continues, to no one, to the rain. "He thought paper could beat power. I told him paper drowns like everything else." His eyes come up to hers, and they are not angry. That's worse. They're asking. "Was there a buyer, Mara? Tell me true, because I've built six years on the other story."

And here is the moment. She could give him the clean version, the useful version, the version that keeps her father's ghost and her own guilt in their separate graves. Instead:

"I don't know," she says. "I heard there was a buyer. I never confirmed it. My source was the same man who later sold your route to the Authority—and I paid him, Sable. I paid him for that name, and I've never been sure whether I bought a fact or a knife."

Juno slides off the chart table and crosses to stand beside Sable. Doesn't touch him. Just stands there, shoulder angled toward him, a small flag planted in disputed ground. "Doesn't matter whose knife it was," Juno says. "What matters is what's still in that vault. Everything else is weather."

Okafor clears his throat. "For the record, I'd like to state that this plan remains insane."

"Noted," Sable says.

"Seconding the insanity," says a twin.

"Carried unanimously," Sable says, and something almost like the old dry humor surfaces in it, brief as a fish. He looks at Mara across the cabin, across six years and one drowned town. "We dive at first light. You ride with Juno on the surface line. And Mara—if there's a buyer waiting at the end of this, you tell me before the navy does."

"There's no buyer," she says. "Not anymore."

It isn't quite a lie. It isn't quite the truth. It's the only currency she's ever traded in, and for the first time it feels like counterfeit.

* * *

She takes the middle watch, alone in the pilothouse while the quarry breathes fog and the rain settles into its long monsoon patience. The gorge downstream is a black seam. Somewhere up it, Ash Hollow waits under forty feet of green water, keeping its dead and its documents.

A board creaks. Juno, wrapping a blanket around their shoulders, settling against the doorframe without invitation.

"Can't sleep either?" Mara asks.

"Don't sleep much on runs." Juno studies the fog. Then, without looking at her: "He'll forgive you eventually. It's the forgetting he's good at. Don't make him do both."

"I'm not asking for forgiveness."

"Everybody asks for something." Juno pulls the blanket tighter. "Just know which thing you're asking for before the water decides for you."

They leave. Mara stands alone with the wheel and the fog and the ledger's coordinates burning in her memory like a coal she swallowed years ago.

Downstream, faint but unmistakable, an engine turns over in the dark. Then another.

Navy boats don't travel in ones.

She reaches for the bell.
