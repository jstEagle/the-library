## 3. What the Water Kept

The drowned town announced itself the way grief does — not all at once, but in pieces you keep tripping over.

First the church spire, leaning out of the water like a drunk leaning on a wall. Then the rooftops, a whole neighborhood of them just under the surface, shingles furred with silt, chimneys standing at attention as if the families below might still be home. Ash Hollow. Twelve years under, and the river had only half-finished the job of pretending it never happened.

"Sonar says the dam intake's at forty feet," Mara said, crouched over the scope in the *Magpie*'s wheelhouse. Her voice was flat, professional, the voice of a woman who had rehearsed not caring. "The ledger's supposed to be in the records vault. If it's still sealed, the silt's kept it dry."

"If it's still sealed," I said. "If the vault didn't crush. If the navy didn't already send divers."

"You always this encouraging, or is it a special occasion?"

"It's a special occasion. You're here."

Juno snorted from the deck, where she was rigging my lines with the particular violence she reserved for dives she didn't like. Which was all of them. "Captain," she said, "if you die down there, I'm not writing the eulogy. You'd want it wry, and I'm not in the mood."

"Noted," I said. "Keep the eulogy short and insulting."

I went down alone. That was the plan — Mara wanted two divers, and I overruled her, because some doors you don't want witnesses at. The water closed over my helmet and the world went green and quiet, and Ash Hollow rose around me like a memory I'd been swimming away from for twelve years.

I knew these streets. That was the thing nobody tells you about drowning a town — it doesn't stop being a town. The bakery on Corben Street was still there, its ovens cold, a shopping cart standing in the doorway like it was waiting for someone to come back and finish the errand. A school bus sat on its side in what used to be the playground, windows fogged with silt, and I made myself look away from it before I started counting seats.

The dam intake was at the far end of the gorge, a concrete throat big enough to swallow a truck, and the collapse had peeled it open like a can. The records vault had been built into the intake's shoulder — the town clerk's bright idea, keeping the town's paper trail somewhere flood-proof. The irony had a taste to it, coppery, through the regulator.

I found the vault door hanging by one hinge. Inside, the shelves had mostly gone to pulp, a century of Ash Hollow's paperwork dissolved into gray soup. But the clerk had kept the important things in a steel strongbox, and steel, unlike paper, holds a grudge.

I worked the latch with numb fingers. Inside: a ledger, oilcloth-wrapped, and beside it—

A dive tag.

Brass, hand-stamped. The kind salvage crews press into a find to claim it, the kind you wear around your neck until it wears a groove in your skin. I knew the stamp before I turned it over. I'd known it the way you know a face in a dream.

**E. STERLING.**

Ezra. My brother's tag. His final tag — the one he was wearing the night the dam went, the night the river took him and forty-one other people and my whole reason for ever going home. It wasn't lost. It wasn't swept away. It was *here*, wedged in a strongbox in a vault he had no business being near, unless—

Unless he'd put it there.

My hands were shaking inside the gloves. I pulled the ledger free and the oilcloth fell open and there it was: columns of dates, of payments, of signatures. Inspection reports signed and dated *after* the engineers had condemned the dam. Bribes logged like line items. And on the last page, in a hand I'd have known anywhere, cramped and furious, a note Ezra had pressed into the record like a splint on a broken bone:

*If I don't come back, this goes to the towns. Not the navy. The navy signed these pages. Give it to the people who lost the water.*

Ezra hadn't drowned running. He hadn't drowned at all, not the way the report said — swept off the dam road, poor kid, wrong place wrong time. He'd been diving. He'd gotten into this vault, he'd found the proof, and he'd left his tag so that whoever came looking would know the claim was already made. He died trying to hand the truth to the river towns, and the navy had spent twelve years telling me my brother was a footnote.

The vault groaned. Somewhere above, the gorge was talking to itself in the low voice it uses before it shouts.

* * *

I came up the ladder with the ledger strapped to my chest and Ezra's tag in my fist, and the first thing I saw was Mara's face, and the second thing I saw was why.

Two gunboats had come around the bend of the gorge while I was down there, gray hulls, deck guns covered but present the way a fist is present before it opens. They had the *Magpie* boxed against the cliff wall, and a voice on the loudhailer was saying words like *restricted waters* and *surrender all materials* and *final warning*, in the tone of a man who'd been told to stop saying final warning and start saying something else.

"They've been shadowing us since the Delta," Mara said. She wasn't looking at the gunboats. She was looking at the ledger. "Sable. What did you find?"

"Proof," I said. "The dam was condemned before it failed. They knew. They signed off anyway, and my brother found out, and they—" My voice did something I didn't authorize. I stopped it. "They killed him for it. Or close enough that the difference doesn't matter."

Mara went very still. "My father tried to report the same inspections," she said quietly. "They called him a drunk. They ruined him, and he drank until it was true." She looked up, and for one second the rivalry, the history, the whole ledger between the two of us was just — paper. "They'll burn it, Sable. They'll burn it and us."

"Captain!" Juno, at the rail, pointing upstream. "Rain!"

I looked up and the sky had gone the color of wet slate, and the first wall of the monsoon came over the gorge rim like a curtain being pulled across the world. The river rose around us in visible inches. In the gunboats, men were moving faster, less certain. The water was about to stop being anyone's territory.

The loudhailer crackled. *Final warning.*

"Options," I said, because a captain says options even when he's already decided.

"We surrender it," Mara said. "Live."

"We sink it," Juno said. "The river keeps its secrets. We keep our skins."

I looked at the ledger. I looked at the tag. *Not the navy. The people who lost the water.*

Twelve years I'd run from Ash Hollow, from the name, from the brother-shaped hole in every quiet night. I'd built a crew, a boat, a whole careful life upstream of my own history, and I'd told myself it was survival. It was just a longer way of drowning.

"No," I said. "We run it downriver. Every town landing between here and the Delta. Copies in every hand that ever lost something to this river. Let them try burning a thousand pages in a thousand houses."

"That's suicide," Mara said.

"It's a delivery," I said. "Ezra had it scheduled twelve years ago. I'm just the substitute driver."

I put the tag around my neck. It sat against my sternum like it had always been there, like a groove finally meeting the bone.

"Juno — full ahead. Mara — you know this gorge better than they do, so lie to me beautifully about where the water's deepest."

The first rain hit the deck like thrown gravel. The *Magpie* heeled away from the cliff wall, and a deck gun spoke behind us, and the gorge filled with thunder that wasn't weather.

The river was rising. The river had always been rising. And for the first time in twelve years, I was running *toward* something.
