## 4. The River Remembers Its Own

The lockhouse at Canebreak Narrows had been abandoned since before I was born, which made it the newest thing any of us had owned in years. It sat on a shelf of stone above the gorge like an old man who'd stopped caring whether the river noticed him, and when Sable ran the *Magpie* into the flooded boathouse beneath it with two feet of clearance and no lights, I decided right then that I would follow that man anywhere, mostly because I could not imagine surviving anywhere he wasn't.

The monsoon hit the gorge like a debt collector. Rain came down in ropes, then in sheets, then in something that wasn't rain anymore but just the sky giving up. Inside the lockhouse we hauled the crates up from the boat by lantern light while the water below us climbed the stone stairs one step at a time, patient as anything.

"Third step's gone," Rook reported from the doorway, dripping like something dredged up. "Fourth step's thinking about it."

"Then we're done going down," Sable said.

We were done going down. That was the whole shape of our situation. The *Magpie* was half-swamped in the boathouse, the gorge was a brown thundering artery, and stacked on the lockhouse floor under a tarp were forty-one copies of the Ash Hollow ledger, printed off the original on Mara's portable press during the worst night of my life, while gunboat searchlights swept the water and I fed paper into rollers with hands that wouldn't stop shaking.

Forty-one copies. One for every river town between here and the sea, give or take the ones that had already given up on us.

I'll be honest about what I expected that first night. I expected Sable to crack. He'd come up out of the intake with the ledger in one hand and his brother's dive tag in the other, and he hadn't said a word since—not on the run through the gorge, not under the searchlights, not once. He'd just sat in the corner of the lockhouse with the tag closed in his fist, and the rest of us had moved around him like water around a stone.

But that's the thing about Sable Sterling that people get wrong, and that Mara Vance got wrong for years: he doesn't crack. He sets. Like concrete. You can pour all your grief into him and he'll hold its shape until it cures into something you can build on.

* * *

By the second day the water had eaten the lower road, and we started moving copies anyway, because waiting was worse than drowning.

Rook knew a ferryman at Pell's Landing who owed him money and a grudge, so copy number one went across the flood current in a waterproof satchel strapped to a man who swore he'd deliver it to the town clerk even if he had to swim there holding it over his head. Copy three went to the widow Okafor at Two Rivers, who ran the only printing press left on the upper delta and had been waiting eleven years for someone to bring her a reason to use it. By the fourth day she'd printed her own copies—hundreds of them—and the navy's list of forty-one became a list nobody could burn fast enough.

That was the arithmetic of it, and I did most of the counting because somebody had to. Every copy we sent out was a copy the gunboats couldn't collect. The navy burned the print shop at Miller's Bend on day five; the widow Okafor's reprints reached Miller's Bend the same afternoon. You can't drown a story that's already swimming.

The gunboats came to Canebreak Narrows twice. Both times they passed us by, because from the water the lockhouse looked exactly like what it was—a dead building full of dead air—and because Sable had rigged the boathouse entrance to look collapsed. We sat inside with the lanterns hooded and listened to their engines churn past close enough to feel in our teeth, and nobody spoke until the sound faded, and then Rook said, "I hate this job," in the tone of a man who intended to keep doing it forever.

Mara stayed through all of it. She worked like she dove—fast, precise, no wasted motion—hauling crates, mending the tarp, running messages down the gorge to the ferryman's people. She and Sable orbited each other for six days without touching orbit, and I watched them do it, and I understood something I hadn't understood before: forgiveness isn't a door you walk through. It's a tide. It comes in or it doesn't, and no amount of pulling on it changes the schedule.

On the seventh morning the rain thinned to a drizzle and the river dropped a foot overnight, and Mara packed her bag on the lockhouse floor where everyone could see her doing it.

She didn't make a speech. I think that surprised me most. I'd built her up in my head as the villain of our story for so long that I'd forgotten she was just a person carrying a father-shaped hole around, same as Sable carried his brother. She rolled her charts, buckled her satchel, and stood at the door with the gray light behind her.

"The towns will need divers," she said, to the room in general. "Come spring. Survey work, salvage on the honest wrecks. There's a living in it now."

"There always was," Rook said. "We just weren't allowed near it."

Mara looked at Sable. Sable looked at the floor. And whatever passed between them took less than a second and cost more than I'll ever know, and neither of them spent a word on it.

"I'm not asking for—" she started.

"I know," Sable said.

"It wasn't—"

"I know that too." He finally raised his head. "Go clear your father's name, Mara. That's the only apology either of us has left to give."

She went. Down the stone steps, past the high-water mark, into the drizzle, without looking back, which is how you know someone means it. I watched her until the bend took her, and I found I wasn't glad or sorry, just lighter, like a knot I'd been carrying in my chest had finally been untied by someone else's hands.

* * *

It was that evening, with the river down another foot and the first dry sunset in a week laying gold along the gorge walls, that Sable said the name.

We were on the lockhouse porch—or the rotted shelf that had once been a porch—passing a bottle of terrible plum brandy that Rook produced from somewhere no one asked about. The water below us had gone quiet and brown and ordinary. Somewhere downstream, in forty-one towns, people were reading the ledger by lamplight, learning the names of the men who'd cut corners on the dam, learning why the sirens never sounded.

Sable turned the dive tag over in his fingers. Brass, river-worn, stamped with a number and a name. He'd carried it closed in his fist for seven days.

"Corin," he said.

Just like that. No windup, no ceremony. The name came out of him rough, like a stone pulled from a riverbed after years underwater, still holding the shape of everything that had pressed on it.

"Corin Sterling," he said again, steadier. "My brother. He died at Ash Hollow trying to tell the truth. And the truth got out anyway." He looked up at us—at me, at Rook, at old Tam who'd been asleep and woke up just in time. "His name was Corin. I figured you should hear it from me instead of the ledger."

The ledger had listed him. Page nine, witness testimony, recovered from the intake office: *C. Sterling, diver, age 24, last seen attempting to reach the dam office with documents.* We'd all read it. None of us had said it. It wasn't ours to say.

Now it was.

Rook raised the bottle. "To Corin."

"To Corin," we said, and drank the terrible brandy, and Sable almost smiled, which from Sable is a religious experience.

Later, when the others had gone in, I stayed out on the shelf beside him. The stars were coming out over the gorge, thick as spilled salt.

"So what now?" I asked. "We're famous. Or infamous. The navy wants our heads, half the delta wants to buy us dinner. Could scatter. Start clean somewhere they've never heard of Rivergate."

Sable turned the tag over once more, then pocketed it—pocketed it, not clenched it, which is different—which is everything.

"Mara said there's survey work in spring," he said. "Honest wrecks. Towns that pay in gratitude and not much else." He shrugged. "Or we stay right here, and when the water drops, we dive the *Magpie* out of that boathouse, and we go wherever the river tells us."

"That's not a plan, Sable. That's a mood."

"It's both." He glanced at me, and there was something in his face I'd never seen there before—not peace exactly, but the ground peace gets built on. "Eleven years I ran from one name. Turns out the crew I was running with was the family I was running toward. I'm done confusing the two."

Inside, Rook was arguing with Tam about whose turn it was to mend the tarp, in the comfortable endless way of people who intend to be arguing about tarps for years. The river moved below us in the dark, carrying the last of the flood down toward the sea, carrying our forty-one copies out into the world like seeds.

I went inside. We had a tarp to mend, and a boat to raise, and a spring coming.

The river remembers its own. Turns out so do we.
