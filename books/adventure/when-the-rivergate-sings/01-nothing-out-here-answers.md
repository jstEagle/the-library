## 1. Nothing Out Here Answers

The salt flats gave nothing back.

Mara Lockridge had learned that lesson eleven years ago, her first week out of the academy, when she'd stood at the edge of the White Reach and watched the horizon dissolve into a glare so complete it seemed to erase the difference between sky and ground. Nothing out here answers, her training officer had said, and he'd meant it as a warning about radios, about water, about the way sound died in the heat shimmer. She'd taken it as a promise instead. A place where a woman could walk until her name wore off her like old paint.

She was three days into the thief's trail now, and the thief was good.

Not good the way the academy meant it—good the way the desert meant it. He walked the flats at dawn and dusk, when the crust held his weight without the loud, betraying crunch of midday. He crossed the dry washes on the stone ribs instead of the sand, leaving prints only where prints couldn't survive the wind. Twice he'd backtracked through his own line to lay false sign, and both times the false trail was *too* false—too neat, too legible, a sentence written in a language the desert didn't speak. The real trail was the one that looked like nothing at all.

Mara crouched at the lip of a dry wash and read the ground the way other people read scripture: not for information, but for the shape of the mind that made it.

There. A crescent of disturbed crust at the wash's edge, no bigger than a thumbnail, where a boot had pressed and slid on the slope of a stone. He'd come down fast, heavier on the left foot. Carrying weight on one shoulder. And below it, half-buried in the salt skin, the faintest dimple where a pack strap's buckle had kissed the ground when he'd stopped to adjust it.

She checked the wind. It came off the flats from the northeast, carrying the smell of hot mineral and nothing else, and it had been from the northeast for two days, which meant the dimple was fresh. Hours old. Maybe less.

"You're tired," she murmured to the empty air. The thief had stopped to adjust his strap. Tired men adjusted straps. Tired men made dimples.

She rose and followed the wash south, moving the way she'd taught herself to move over a decade—weight through the balls of her feet, breath timed to her stride, a woman so quiet that the ravens stopped spooking from her and started simply watching her pass, the way they'd watch a rock roll downhill. The rangers had a name for what she'd become. They called it *ghosting*, and they said it with a kind of uneasy respect, the way you'd talk about someone who'd stopped sleeping. Mara had earned the name honestly. She'd spent ten years making herself untraceable—scrubbing her service photos, paying cash, letting her surname go unspoken in the stationhouse until the younger rangers genuinely didn't know it. The Lockridge money had bought her nothing she'd kept. The Lockridge name she'd buried so deep that sometimes, in the long dark of the winter stations, she could almost believe it had never been hers.

The wash narrowed, walled in by salt-crusted clay that glittered like broken crockery, and there—caught in the fork of a dead tamarisk leaning out of the bank—she saw it.

A scrap of color in a world that had none.

She went still. Ten years of habit held her in place for a full minute, reading the wash, the tamarisk, the angle of the light, before she let herself move. Then she crossed to it in a slow, deliberate approach, the way you'd approach anything in the desert that hadn't earned your trust.

It was a radio handset. Field model, military-surplus lineage, the casing scuffed white along one edge. A lanyard loop, snapped. And stamped into the casing, in the flat industrial lettering that no forger bothered with:

**RVG-EXP-7 — RIVERGATE SURVEY — VOSS MARINE CONTRACT**

Mara turned it over twice. The battery housing was intact. The antenna was fine. There was no reason on earth for a working handset to be dropped in the fork of a tamarisk forty miles from the nearest river, unless the man carrying it had dropped it running, or dropped it dead, or dropped it because he'd heard something through it that made him want it out of his hands.

She pressed the transmit key. The click was very loud in the silence.

The handset was tuned to a frequency she didn't recognize—147.325, hand-etched on the casing beside the call sign. She stood in the wash with the sun hammering the salt around her and looked at that frequency for a long time. Rivergate was a river town. She was a desert ranger. The two geographies did not overlap, had never overlapped, and the fact that a Rivergate survey handset was sitting in her wash meant that the clean border she'd drawn around her life had a hole in it the size of a man's trail.

She keyed the frequency anyway.

Static. The dry, oceanic hiss of an open channel, the sound the desert made when it impersonated the sea. She was about to lower the handset when the static *shaped* itself.

"—copy, say again, Expedition Seven, we are—" A voice, male, young, fraying at the edges. Wind tearing across his mic. "—at the quarry pool, the hull is *intact*, do you understand, the whole—" The transmission stuttered, dissolved, reformed. "—they're coming up the gorge, I can see their—"

The voice cut.

Not faded. Cut. The way a sentence ends when a hand covers a mouth.

Mara stood in the wash with the handset against her ear and listened to the static come back, smooth and empty, as if the voice had never been. The sun was a white weight on her shoulders. The salt glittered to every horizon. And the loneliness she had chosen—curated, maintained, defended for ten years like a border she alone patrolled—shifted under her feet and showed her its other face.

Exile. That was the other face. She'd known the word, of course. She'd just never let it apply.

She keyed the handset again. "Expedition Seven, this is Ranger Lockridge, White Reach district, I have your field handset, over." Her voice came out flat and professional and it took everything she had to make it do that. "Expedition Seven, do you copy?"

Static. Then, faintly, threaded through the hiss like a hair through water: a second voice. Not the young man. Older. A woman, calm as a ledger.

"—not a rescue matter. Maintain position. Seven, maintain—"

The channel died with a click that sounded, in the enormous silence, exactly like a door closing.

Mara lowered the handset. Her hands were steady—her hands were always steady, it was the one inheritance from her father's side she'd never managed to refuse—and that steadiness frightened her more than shaking would have. A man had been speaking and then had not been speaking, and the desert around her held forty miles of nothing in every direction, and somewhere upriver, past the edge of her maps, a woman with a calm voice was telling people to maintain position while a hull sat intact in a quarry pool.

*The hull is intact.* She turned the words over. She didn't know what hull. She didn't want to know what hull. She knew, with the flat certainty of ten years' reading of sign, that she was going to find out.

* * *

She made the station by last light, ghosting in off the flats with the handset in her pack and the thief's trail still unspooling south behind her, toward the river country she'd spent a decade pretending didn't exist. The White Reach station was a scatter of buildings the color of the ground, and the only soul in it after dark was Odile Vann, the dispatcher, who had been dispatching since before Mara was born and who greeted every transmission, every arrival, every catastrophe with the same expression of mild, geological patience.

Mara set the handset on Odile's desk without a word.

Odile looked at it over her half-moon glasses. She picked it up. She turned it so the stamp faced the lamplight, and Mara watched something move through the old woman's face—not surprise, exactly. Recognition, arriving a half-second before she wanted it to.

"RVG-Seven," Odile said. "Voss contract." She set it down. "Where."

"Salt wash, forty miles south. Fresh sign with it. One man, heavy pack, moving toward the river." Mara paused. "There was a transmission. Partial. A young man. He said a hull was intact at a quarry pool, and then someone cut him off."

Odile was quiet for a moment. The station's ancient fan ticked its slow circle. Outside, the first stars were coming up over the Reach, hard and white and indifferent.

"Describe the voice," Odile said.

"Young. Twenty-five, maybe. Scared, but trained—kept his call sign even while he was coming apart."

The fan ticked. Odile took off her glasses and pressed the bridge of her nose, and when she spoke again her voice had changed register entirely, the dispatcher's flat calm gone, and underneath it something old and tired and personal.

"That'll be Denny," she said. "Denny Achebe. My sister's boy. He took a survey contract with Voss Marine in the spring. Good money, he said. Six weeks, he said." She put her glasses back on, and her hands, Mara noticed, were not steady at all. "When did you hear him?"

"Two hours ago. He was cut off mid-sentence. Someone else came on the channel after—a woman, calm, telling Seven to maintain position. Not a rescue matter, she said."

"Not a rescue matter," Odile repeated. She said it the way you'd read an inscription on a grave. Then she was moving, and Mara understood that she was watching a woman shift from grief into the only armor she'd worn for forty years: procedure. Odile pulled the survey contract filings, the district liaison logs, the Voss Marine work orders, and laid them across her desk like a hand of bad cards. "Expedition Seven went upriver nineteen days ago. Barge survey, decommissioned route, above the second gorge. They're due to report every seventy-two hours." She tapped the log. "Last report was nine days ago."

"Nine days."

"Command flagged it. Command also noted that monsoon season makes the gorge unnavigable in—" she checked the chart pinned above her desk, the one with the river drawn in fading blue ink like a vein in the body of the country, "—six tidal cycles. So command's position is that any search waits until after the season. Personnel risk. Standard doctrine."

"They've been dark nine days and command's position is *wait*."

"Command's position," Odile said, "is that six people drowning on a schedule is cheaper than four rangers drowning off one." She said it without heat, which was somehow worse. "I've filed the request anyway. It'll be denied by morning. It's always denied by morning."

Mara stood in the lamplight and felt the last of her chosen solitude go quiet and cold inside her. She had spent ten years building a life with no one in it to lose, and she had believed—she had genuinely believed—that the emptiness was a kind of strength. Now a young man's voice was in her ear again, fraying, cut off, and the bureaucracy between him and help was a machine that ran on schedules, and the schedule said the river would be allowed to keep him.

"Odile," she said. "The thief I'm tracking. The one who dropped this." She laid the trail map on the desk, the neat false trails and the true invisible one. "He's heading for the river too."

Odile studied the map for a long moment. Then she looked up, and behind the glasses her eyes were wet and absolutely clear.

"Then I'd say you've still got a trail to follow, ranger," she said. "And six tides to follow it in."

Outside, the wind had come around from the northeast, off the river country, carrying nothing Mara could smell yet—but she stood on the station step a long time, listening to the static of the dark, and for the first time in ten years, nothing out here answered her, and she minded.
