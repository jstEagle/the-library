## 2. The Last Transmission

The ranger station at Salt Hollow smelled of burnt coffee and old paper, the way it had for forty years, and Odile Ferro had been there for thirty-nine of them. She sat behind a console that predated Mara's birth—a wall of dials and toggle switches wired to an antenna mast leaning against the wind like a tired old man—and she did not look up when Mara came in with sand still crusted in her boot treads.

"You look like something the desert chewed and spat back out," Odile said.

"It spat me out on purpose. It didn't want what I was carrying."

Mara set the handset on the desk between them. The expedition call sign—*Rivergate Survey Seven*, stamped into the casing in flaking white paint—caught the light of the single bulb overhead. Odile's hands stopped moving over her logbook.

"Where'd you get this?"

"Salt flats, eleven miles east of the Canebreak line. Dropped, not discarded. No scuff marks around it. Whoever carried it set it down gentle." Mara pulled the second chair close and sat, elbows on knees. "I raised their frequency last night. Got static. Then this."

She had recorded it on the station's ancient reel deck, because some habits died hard even for a woman who'd spent a decade making herself unrecordable. Now she threaded the tape with fingers that were steadier than she felt, and pressed play.

Static first—the dry hiss of a dead channel. Then a voice surfaced through it like a swimmer through dark water, young, male, breathless:

*"—anyone copy, this is Survey Seven, we are—"* A crackle swallowed half a word. *"—not a survey anymore. They know we found it. Repeat, they know we—"*

The transmission cut. Not faded. Cut, clean as a knife through rope, mid-syllable, so that the last sound in the room was the ghost of a consonant that never got to become a word.

Odile did not move for a long moment. In the silence Mara could hear the wind worrying the antenna guy-wires outside, a low plucked note, over and over, like someone testing whether the world was still strung tight enough to sing.

"Play it again," Odile said.

Mara played it again. And again. On the fourth pass, Odile reached out and stopped the reel with one finger, and Mara saw that the old woman's hand was trembling—not much, just enough to make the tape wobble against the head.

"That's Dev," Odile said quietly. "That's my nephew."

The words landed in Mara's chest like stones dropped down a well. She counted the seconds before they hit bottom.

"Devraj Ferro. He took the survey contract with Voss Salvage in spring." Odile's voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of a woman carrying something fragile across ice. "His mother—my sister—cried for a week when he signed on. Not because of the money. Because of the river." She looked up, and her eyes were dry but bright, the brightness of metal about to fail. "Everyone in my family knows better than to go upriver during survey season. Everyone knows."

"Tell me about him. What he sounds like normally. I want to know if that recording—"

"He's scared." Odile said it simply, without drama, which made it worse. "I've heard him scared twice in his life. Once when he was nine and fell through the ice on the millpond. Once at his father's funeral. That's the same voice. That's a boy who has realized the world is bigger and colder than anyone told him." She straightened in her chair, and thirty-nine years of sitting in this room seemed to gather into her spine all at once. "When did you record this?"

"Eleven-oh-four last night. I tried raising them again at dawn. Nothing. I ran the frequency sweep Odile—I mean, I ran every band they might have fallen back to. Nothing but fleet traffic."

"Fleet traffic." Odile's mouth thinned. "Voss traffic?"

"Some. Coordinated chatter, channel-hopping. Organized." Mara hesitated, then gave her the rest: "And there was a second transmission buried under theirs. Low power, short burst. Someone else was transmitting from near the same coordinates. It cut off before I could get a bearing."

Odile absorbed this the way old stone absorbs rain—slowly, deeply, showing nothing on the surface. Then she turned to her console with the economy of long practice, keyed the priority line to regional command, and asked for Major Castellanos by name.

They waited. The wind plucked its note outside. Somewhere in the back rooms, a clock ticked with the patience of an institution that had outlived everyone who cared about what time it was.

Castellanos answered on the third ring, voice tinny and clipped through the speaker. Odile explained. She was good at explaining—thirty-nine years of turning chaos into bullet points—but Mara watched her leave things out. She didn't mention the nephew. She kept it clinical: lost survey team, corrupted distress transmission, request for search authorization.

There was a pause on the line long enough for Mara to hear the major breathing.

"Survey Seven is a Voss-contracted civilian operation," Castellanos said finally. "Their employer is responsible for personnel recovery. That's protocol."

"Their employer is loading armed crews at the river mouth as we speak," Mara said, before she could stop herself. "I've seen their picket boats. If Voss is responsible for recovery, who's responsible for the fact that their own people are broadcasting maydays?"

Another pause. When Castellanos spoke again, his voice had acquired the particular smoothness of a man putting on gloves. "Ranger Lockridge. Your last filed report places you on detached assignment, solo tracking, no operational mandate in the river corridor. Is that correct?"

"It's correct."

"Then I'll thank you to let Dispatcher Ferro handle communications, and I'll remind you both of the season. Monsoon makes landfall inside the week. Command will not authorize any upriver deployment until the gorge is rated navigable again. That's not stubbornness, that's arithmetic. Every year we lose boats and rangers who think they can beat the weather, and every year I write letters to their families explaining that the river doesn't negotiate." A breath. "If Survey Seven is stranded, they'll be recovered after the storm. Voss has the equipment. This is not abandonment. This is sequencing."

"After the storm," Odile repeated softly.

"After the storm. Log the transmission, file your report, and stand by." A click. The line went dead.

For a moment neither of them moved. Then Odile reached out with two fingers and switched off the speaker, gently, the way you close the eyes of the dead.

The coffee pot had boiled itself down to tar while they talked. Odile poured two cups anyway, and they drank it because there was nothing else to do with their hands.

"They'll drown on schedule," Mara said. She hadn't meant to say it aloud. It came out level and cold, and hearing her own voice say it frightened her more than the transmission had.

"Don't." Odile set her cup down. "Don't say it like that."

"Like what? Like it's true?" Mara stood, crossed to the window. Outside, the salt flats ran silver to the horizon, flat and featureless and honest in a way no office ever was. Out there, if a man dropped a radio, you could read the story of it in the sand. In here, the story got filed, sequenced, and scheduled. "Six days, maybe seven, before landfall. After that the gorge runs brown and blind for weeks. If they're holed up somewhere with injuries, without supplies—" She stopped. Made herself turn around. "You heard him, Odile. *They know we found it.* Found what? A survey crew doesn't go silent because they found gravel deposits. Somebody upriver found something worth cutting a transmission over. And somebody else was close enough to hear them do it."

"I know what they found." Odile's voice was very quiet now. "Or I know the shape of it. My family's been on this river four generations, girl. We don't talk about what's under it. That's not superstition. That's policy." She rubbed her eyes with the heel of her hand, and for a moment she looked every one of her sixty-eight years, and then some. "Dev's mother is in Rivergate. His sisters. If I tell them command says *sequencing*—"

"Then don't tell them that."

"What would you have me tell them?"

Mara opened her mouth. Closed it. The truth was she didn't know—that was the whole rotten center of it. Ten years ago she had walked away from exactly this kind of room, this kind of arithmetic, this kind of letter-writing, and she had told herself the desert was cleaner because the desert didn't pretend. But here she was, standing in another institutional kitchen watching another institution decide how many people a schedule could afford to lose, and the desert had only ever been a longer hallway leading back to the same door.

What she said was: "Tell them the truth. Their boy called for help. Help is coming."

Odile looked at her for a long time. Something moved behind her eyes—calculation, or hope, or the beginning of grief getting organized into something useful.

"And where," Odile said, "is help coming from?"

Mara didn't answer. She didn't have to. The silence between them filled up with the answer, and Odile read it the way Mara read sign in sand: easily, completely, and with dawning dread.

"No," Odile said. "Absolutely not. You're one woman with a skiff and a grudge against geography. The gorge in monsoon season eats flotillas. It ate a barge in 1911 and nobody ever found so much as a plank, and that was in calm water."

"I've run worse."

"You haven't run anything. You're a *desert* ranger, Lockridge. Salt and stone. The river is a different animal entirely—it lies, it moves, it holds grudges." Odile pushed back from the desk and stood, and though she was a head shorter and twenty years slower, she managed to loom. "And there's a fleet up there with guns, and whatever Dev found, powerful people want it buried deep enough to drown a crew over. You'd be going alone into a fight that command won't touch, against men who've already decided the paperwork is done."

"Yes," Mara said.

"Why?"

It was a fair question, and Mara turned it over honestly, looking for the answer she'd have given a week ago. A week ago she'd have said: because the trail went cold and I don't leave trails cold. Because a dropped radio is a debt and I pay my debts. Because out there, alone, no one can make me complicit in anything.

But that wasn't it anymore, and they both knew it.

"Because ten years ago I erased myself from every record in this valley," Mara said slowly, "and I told myself it was freedom. Tonight I listened to a kid beg the air for help and get static, and then I listened to a major explain that help has a calendar." Her jaw tightened. "I spent a decade learning how to disappear, Odile. Turns out I'm very good at it. Turns out the whole world is built to help you do it. Nobody sees you, nobody owes you, nobody writes your name on anything." She looked at the handset lying on the desk, its call sign catching the lamplight. "Somebody has to see this. Somebody has to write it down while there's still somebody left to write it about."

Odile held her gaze for a long moment. Then she laughed—one short, cracked, disbelieving note—and turned away, pressing both hands flat on the console as if steadying herself against weather.

"God help me, you sound like my sister. Like every fool this family ever sent upriver." Her shoulders rose and fell once, twice. When she turned back, her face had settled into something older and harder than grief. Resolve, maybe. Or the thing resolve becomes when it stops asking permission.

"If you're going to do this," she said, "you'll do it with more than a grudge and a skiff. You'll do it with charts." She pulled open the bottom drawer of the console—the deep one, the locked one, the one Mara had never seen opened—and lifted out a bundle wrapped in oilcloth. She unwrapped it on the desk: river charts, hand-corrected, decades of pencil annotations in three different hands. Gorge profiles. Tide tables going back fifty years. And pinned to the top sheet, a note in faded ink: *High water marks — monsoon '61 — DO NOT RUN THE NARROWS ABOVE TIDE SIX.*

Odile spread the charts with both hands, smoothing them flat, and Mara leaned over them and felt the pull of the river for the first time in her life—a long blue-green vein running up through the paper toward the gorge, toward the silence, toward whatever it was that Dev Ferro had found and someone had killed him for finding. Maybe.

"Monsoon makes landfall in six tidal cycles," Odile said. Her finger traced the coastline on the chart, then the river mouth, then the first black scribble of the gorge. "After tide six, this becomes unnavigable. Not difficult—*unnavigable*. Whatever's up there gets sealed behind forty feet of floodwater until the season turns." She looked up. "So if anyone's going, they go now, and they count tides like a prayer."

Mara studied the chart. Six tides. She thought of the flats behind her, the decade behind her, the empty cabin with its empty ledgers waiting for her return. She thought of a voice cut off mid-word, a consonant that never became a name.

Outside, far off toward the coast, thunder muttered—the first rumor of the monsoon, testing its throat.

"Odile," Mara said quietly, "how fast can you fuel a skiff?"

The old dispatcher began, already, to answer.
