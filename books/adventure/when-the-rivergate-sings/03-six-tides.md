## 3. Six Tides

The charts in Odile's office were older than either of them, and like everything old in the Rivergate district, they had been corrected by hand rather than replaced. Pencil over ink, ink over pencil, decades of dispatchers leaning over the same paper to scratch out a shoal that had moved or a cut that had silted shut. Mara stood at the counter while Odile's fingers walked the river's spine, north from the station, through the delta, into the gorge country where the paper went thin with use and the corrections crowded so close they overlapped like scar tissue.

"There." Odile tapped a date written in red at the top margin, small and neat as a hymnal notation. "Monsoon makes landfall in six tidal cycles. Maybe five, if the pressure keeps falling the way it fell last night. After that, the gorge fills." She looked up. Her eyes were the pale, washed-out blue of river glass, and they did not soften. "Not dangerous. Not difficult. *Closed.* The current through the second gorge runs at better than twenty knots in flood, and there's no bank to stand on. The water takes the walls themselves. You could put a gunboat in there and it would come out the bottom as gravel."

"Six tides," Mara said.

"Six." Odile folded her hands on the chart, over the gorge, as if she could hold it shut. "The expedition went silent eleven days ago. Whatever's left of them has been sitting up there through eleven days of weather already, and the river's been rising the whole time. If they're alive, they're alive the way animals are alive in a flood — on high ground, hungry, waiting for it to end." She paused. "Or they're in the water, and it doesn't matter what we do."

Mara had spent ten years learning to keep her face still. It was the first skill the desert taught and the last one it gave back: out on the salt flats there was nothing to perform for, no audience, no reason for a face to do anything but be a face. So her face did nothing now. But Odile had been dispatching rangers for thirty years and had buried enough of them to read the ones who'd learned stillness, and something in her expression shifted — a softening of the mouth, a settling of the shoulders, the look of a woman about to say a thing she'd said before and hated saying.

"Command won't move before the season, Mara."

"I know."

"They'll tell you it's protocol. It *is* protocol. A search party in monsoon flood is a second search party to rescue. I've written the reports. I've watched them not read them." Odile's voice stayed level, dispatch-calm, and that was the worst of it — the calm of someone who had done this arithmetic so many times it no longer cost her anything visible. "They will let those people drown on a schedule, and the schedule will be *correct*, and no one will ever have to say they were wrong."

Mara looked at the chart. The river ran up the paper like a vein, and at the top, where the gorge country began, the contour lines pressed so tight they went nearly black. Somewhere in that blackness was a radio handset stamped with a call sign, and a voice that had cut off mid-word — *do not—* — and eleven days of silence since.

She thought about the flats. She thought about the ten years she had spent making herself into a thing that could not be tracked, could not be found, could not be asked for anything by anyone. She had been so proud of that. She had called it freedom.

"Where's the boat shed," she said.

Odile didn't answer right away. She opened the counter's low drawer instead, took out a key ring heavy enough to sag her hand, and set it on the chart between them.

"Skiff's the second bay. The green one. Hull's sound, engine's honest, and I serviced the fuel lines in the spring because I service them every spring whether anyone uses them or not." She pushed the keys across the pencil-scarred paper. "I'm sixty-three years old, I've got a bad hip, and I am not going up that river. But if a ranger were to take a boat without authorization — if she were to resign her post in writing first, so the district couldn't call it theft and her family couldn't call it anything at all — then I'd want that ranger to know the tide tables are in the chart case, the monsoon window is real, and I'd want her back before tide six, because after that I won't be able to listen for her either."

Mara looked at the keys. Then at Odile.

"You're retiring me," she said.

"I'm *filing* you." The old woman almost smiled. "There's a form in the top drawer. Resignation, effective immediately. Reason for separation — write *personal.* They love that. It means nothing and it closes the file, and a closed file is a file nobody reads." She turned back to her radio, her coffee ring, her forty years of pencil corrections. "I've spent my whole career sending people out and marking them returned or lost. I'd rather mark you *resigned.* It leaves a door open."

* * *

The form took four minutes to fill out. Name: Mara Lockridge. She wrote it and looked at it for a long moment — the name she had spent a decade scrubbing from every register, every permit, every ledger that would take her — and then she signed it with the same careful, anonymous hand she used for citations, the letters small and square, a signature designed to be forgotten.

*Reason for separation: personal.*

She left the form weighted under Odile's coffee cup and walked out into the white afternoon.

The station sat on the last hard ground before the delta began, a cluster of prefab buildings and a radio mast that leaned a degree off true, and beyond it the land went soft and green and then to water. Mara had come here from the flats that morning with a thief's cold trail in her pocket and a dead handset in her pack, and she had told herself she'd come to file a report. That was a lie she'd let herself keep for six hours. Now, crossing the yard with the keys in her fist, she let it go.

The green skiff sat in the second bay on its trailer, tarp cinched down, hull beaded with dust. She pulled the tarp and worked fast, the way she worked at everything — no wasted motion, no second thoughts given room to speak. Fuel check. Battery. The chart case latched under the console, tide tables inside, Odile's red-inked landfall date circled twice. A flare kit, half full. A first-aid box that had been restocked recently enough to still smell of plastic. She loaded her own gear last: the pack, the water, the rifle she hesitated over and then took anyway, because the river had a salvage fleet on it and salvage fleets, in her experience, did not stay polite for long.

The engine caught on the third pull and settled into a rough, honest idle.

She brought the skiff down the ramp and into the delta channel, and the desert fell away behind her the way a sentence ends — not all at once, but with a period she could feel.

She had not understood, until that moment, how much of her was made of the dry country. Ten years of salt and heat and silence had built her the way water builds stone: slowly, and entirely. She knew the flats the way she knew her own pulse. She could read a wind out of a dust curve, could tell you what had passed over a patch of ground three days ago and which direction it was limping and whether it had been afraid. Out there she was not nothing — she was the one thing the emptiness had made an expert of itself in, and that had been enough. That had been *company.*

The delta was different. The water moved under her hull with intentions. Reeds stood in ranks along the channels like witnesses. Birds went up ahead of her in shrieking clouds, announcing her to a country that was listening, and Mara felt — for the first time in a decade — *seen.* Not by anyone in particular. By the place itself. The green country watched, and it remembered, and it did not care how carefully she had erased herself.

Her hands were not quite steady on the tiller. She noticed, and made them steady, and understood that the steadiness was the fear, wearing its work clothes.

* * *

She ran the delta channels through the afternoon, following Odile's chart and the river's own stubborn logic, and by the time the light went long and copper she had cleared the reed country and entered the lower river proper — wide, brown, freighted with silt, moving with the patient muscular certainty of something that had been doing this for ten thousand years and would be doing it long after everyone currently alive had stopped.

She made camp on a gravel bar with the skiff beached and the rifle across her knees, and ate cold rations, and watched the first stars come out over the water.

The silence was the problem. She had chosen silence once — had chosen it with both hands, had built a life inside it the way a hermit crab builds inside a shell — and she had told herself the silence was the same everywhere. It wasn't. The desert's silence was a silence of absence: nothing there, nothing coming, nothing owed. The river's silence was a silence of *withheld* things. Under the flat brown water, under the night birds and the wind in the willows, the river was holding something — a barge, a manifest, a drowned year, eleven days of missing people — and it was holding all of it down where no chart went, and it would give it up only on its own terms or not at all.

She thought about the handset. She had charged it off the skiff's battery that morning out of pure stubbornness, and now, on the gravel bar, she took it out and turned it on and let it sit in her palm, its little screen glowing green in the dark.

The expedition's frequency was still in it. Of course it was. She raised it, and static filled the night around her, soft as rain.

"Any station, any station," she said. "This is Ranger Lockridge. If anyone is receiving—"

Nothing. The static went on being static. She had known it would. She keyed off anyway, and the handset's light died, and the dark came back in like water filling a footprint.

*Six tides.* She lay back on the gravel and did the arithmetic she'd been refusing to do all day. One tide to clear the delta. Two for the lower river if the current was kind. The first gorge after that, and Odile's black contour lines, and the second gorge beyond it where the paper went black entirely. Six tides to reach a place the river itself would be trying to keep her from, with a monsoon at her back the whole way, closing the door behind her as she went.

And somewhere ahead, a fleet. She had seen the Voss barges from the ridge above the river mouth on her way down from the flats — had counted masts, counted cranes, counted the armed silhouettes on the decks, and had told herself it was a salvage operation and salvage operations were legal. Eleven days of silence said otherwise. The expedition had been surveying for Voss. The expedition had gone silent. Voss's fleet was loading crews, and the tide tables were the same for everyone.

She was not racing the monsoon. She was racing the monsoon *and* the fleet, and she was alone, and the alone that had been her armor for ten years was suddenly just weight.

Mara closed her eyes and let the river's sound fill her — that low, ceaseless, muscular hush, like something enormous breathing in its sleep — and made herself say the true thing, once, in the privacy of her own skull, where no one had visited in a decade:

*I'm afraid.*

Then she said the second true thing, which was easier: *I'm going anyway.*

She slept in shifts, rifle in reach, and woke before dawn with the sky still black and the river a sound more than a sight, and broke camp in the dark with hands that knew the work.

* * *

The second day the river narrowed and the banks grew arms — willow and cottonwood leaning out over the current, their shadows striping the water like bars. She ran the skiff hard through the middle of the day and holed up through the worst heat, and ran again into evening, and the current, as Odile had promised, was already quickening. Debris moved past her: branches, a fence rail, a child's toy boat, painted red, riding the flood with terrible cheerfulness. The river was rising. She could read it now the way she read the flats — the stain line on the bank stones a hand's width higher than it should have been, the water's brown thickening, its voice dropping half a tone. The monsoon was not waiting politely at sea. It was already reaching up the valley with its fingers.

On the morning of the third day she came around a bend called, according to the chart, Harker's Point, and the river mouth country opened below her, and she cut the engine and let the skiff drift so she could look.

The Voss fleet sat at anchor across the channel like a town that had decided against becoming one. Three barges and a pair of fast gunboats, cranes folded, decks stacked with gear, and along the gunwales — she raised her binoculars, and the glass steadied her hand the way it always did, the world coming into focus because she made it — armed crews in company gray, moving with the unhurried discipline of people who had done this many times and expected to do it many more. A picket boat patrolled the channel mouth in slow, patient arcs. On the lead barge's superstructure, painted fresh over older paint, the Voss mark: a crane's hook through a circle.

They were not loading to leave. They were loading to *wait.* Fuel drums in ranks. Water cans. Ammunition crates stacked under tarp with the corners squared. A fleet provisioned to sit out a monsoon and come out the other side of it ready to work — or to make sure no one else worked first.

Mara lowered the glasses and sat in the drifting skiff with the current pulling gently at her hull, toward them, and understood the shape of the thing she had rowed herself into. Eleven days ago a thief had crossed her flats carrying something worth a fleet's patience. Four days ago that thief's trail had ended at a dead handset and a cut-off word. And here, at the door of the gorge country, sat the reason, patient and organized and gray, waiting for the storm to do its work the way Odile said Command waited for it to do its work — on schedule, correctly, and letting the river keep the difference.

She could go around. The delta had a thousand mouths; the picket boat could not watch them all, and the night was hers, had always been hers. She could slip past in the dark the way she had slipped past everything for ten years, unseen, unrecorded, unasked.

She waited for the fear to argue. It didn't. It had gone quiet somewhere in the second day, the way fear does when it finally understands it isn't being consulted.

Mara started the engine, turned the skiff's nose toward the reed line on the far bank, and began, coldly and carefully, to plan the crossing — the tide she'd wait for, the channel she'd thread, the hour when the picket boat's arcs would carry it farthest from the shallows.

Above the fleet, far up the valley, the sky over the gorge country had begun to stack with cloud, high and white and organized, like something assembling.

Three tides down. Three to go. And the river, rising all the while, ran north past the anchored fleet toward the drowned thing it was keeping — and Mara Lockridge, resigned, erased, and exactly as alone as she had made herself, went to go and ask it for what it had.
