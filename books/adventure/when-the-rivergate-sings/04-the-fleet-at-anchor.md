## 4. The Fleet at Anchor

The river announced itself before Mara saw it.

For three days she had walked out of the desert's grammar—dune giving way to hardpan, hardpan to saltbush, saltbush to the green rumor of reeds—and now the air itself changed, thickening with silt and rot and the mineral breath of moving water. She crested a low bluff of rust-colored clay and there it was below her: the Rivergate delta, wide as a lake, braided into channels that split and rejoined like something alive trying to decide where to go.

And anchored in the widest channel, riding at moorings in the gray morning light, was the fleet.

Mara went still behind a stand of dead tamarisk and counted. Four barges, hulls the color of old blood, cranes folded against their decks like sleeping insects. Two tugboats idling in the current, exhaust stacks coughing diesel haze. And tied alongside the largest barge, a gunboat—low, black-painted, its deckhouse slotted with ports that were not windows. She had seen craft like it on the border lakes, back when she'd still carried a ranger's badge instead of a ghost's silence. That was not salvage equipment. That was a statement of intent.

The Voss company flag hung limp from the lead barge's mast: a white circle on a field of deep blue, the ring broken by a vertical line. Mara knew the mark. Everyone in the territory knew it. Voss Marine Recovery had been pulling wrecks out of western water for sixty years—sunken barges, drowned locomotives, the occasional inconvenient body—and they did it with a patience that people mistook for respectability. Their contracts were immaculate. Their permits were always in order. Their crews were always, always discreet.

The expedition she was chasing—the one whose radio had died mid-word three days ago upriver—had been hired by Voss. She'd pulled that from the call sign stamped on the dropped handset: *VRX-7*, Voss River Expedition Seven. A survey contract. Mapping the channel above the gorge so the salvage fleet could bring heavy equipment through after monsoon season. Routine work. The kind of job that paid well and asked nothing of anyone.

Except Seven had gone quiet, and Voss had not reported them missing, and now here sat the fleet, loading armed crews in broad daylight as if waiting for a scheduled appointment.

Mara lowered herself onto her stomach and watched through the tamarisk branches.

On the lead barge's deck, men moved cargo under floodlights that burned even in daylight—crates too long and too heavy to be dive gear alone, though there was dive gear too, racks of tanks being lashed down under tarps. She counted rifles the way she counted everything, without hurry: six visible, probably twelve total, carried by men who wore no uniforms but moved like they'd all learned their drill from the same book. A woman in a charcoal coat stood at the rail of the gunboat, hands clasped behind her back, watching the loading with the expression of someone checking items off a list she had written herself.

Even at this distance, something about her stillness made Mara's spine prickle.

She stayed until the sun climbed high enough to burn the river mist away, memorizing what she could: the picket boat patrolling the downstream anchorage in slow figure-eights, the searchlight array on the gunboat's mast, the fact that the fleet's radios crackled constantly while the expedition's frequency stayed dead as bone. Then she withdrew, careful as breath, and spent the afternoon finding the shape of the night she would need.

* * *

The picket boat ran its circuit every forty minutes. Mara had timed it from the reeds for two hours, lying in cold shallows with only her face above water, counting heartbeats between passes. Forty minutes, give or take ninety seconds depending on how long the pilot lingered at the channel buoy where the current eddied. Ninety seconds was nothing. Ninety seconds was everything.

She waited for full dark and a sky without a moon. The monsoon was coming—you could taste it now, a metallic pressure at the back of the throat, thunder muttering somewhere beyond the hills like a dog dreaming—and the cloud cover had rolled in early, thick and low, swallowing the stars. Bad weather for travel. Good weather for becoming nothing.

Her skiff was a stolen thing, though stolen gently: an old aluminum workboat she'd found beached and half-sunk in a cut two miles downstream, patched with marine epoxy from her pack, its little outboard coaxed back to life with clean fuel and blasphemy. No registration. No name on the transom except a ghost of one, scraped away by whoever had abandoned it first. A boat with no name suited her fine. She had spent ten years making herself the same way—records closed, trails cold, a woman who existed only in the present tense. Ranger stations had stopped asking about her years ago. The desert kept her secrets because she had given it nothing else to keep.

She pushed off from the reeds with the engine tilted, letting the current carry her silent until the picket boat's running lights swept past on its circuit, heading away. Then she dropped the motor, started it on the second pull with the throttle barely cracked, and slid into the delta's dark veins.

The channels were a maze, but mazes had rules, and Mara read water the way she read sand: by what it chose to hold and what it chose to give up. Foam lines showed her where the current ran true. The smell of diesel told her which channel fed the fleet's anchorage. She kept to the eastern cuts, where the reeds grew tall enough to swallow a boat whole, and she killed the engine whenever the picket lights swung near, drifting with the pole until the danger passed. Once, a searchlight raked the reeds twenty yards off her beam, close enough that she could hear the boat's generator whining, could see the silhouette of the man at the light with a rifle slung across his chest like an afterthought. She lay flat in the bilge among the puddles and let the skiff drift, and the light passed over her, and she was reeds, she was shadow, she was the dark water itself.

Ten years of erasing herself, and this was what it was for. Not peace. This. Slipping past armed men in the dark because the world's paperwork had decided some lives weren't worth a search party.

The thought sat in her chest like a swallowed stone as she ghosted past the fleet's southern perimeter. Close now—close enough to hear voices carrying flat across the water, the clang of a crane hook, laughter from a crew mess that sounded ordinary and obscene all at once. On the lead barge, arc welders threw blue-white stroboscopic flashes through the cargo hatch. They were reinforcing something in there. Reinforcing, or armoring.

She was fifty yards clear, already turning upriver toward the gorge and the dead frequency, when the voice came over the fleet's radio traffic, loud enough to reach her across the water where a deck speaker had been left open:

"—Voss confirms. Nothing moves above the gorge until we say it does. If anything's still up there, the river will keep it fresh."

A pause. Static. Then, dry as dust: "It always has."

Mara lay low in the bilge and let the current take her north, and for the first time since the handset had gone silent in her hand, she let herself think the thought she'd been refusing: that whatever the expedition had found upriver, Voss had known about it before the expedition did. That the survey hadn't been a survey. It had been a question asked by people who didn't know what answer they were digging toward—and the people who owned the question had sent guns to make sure the answer never came home.

* * *

Ilsa Voss stood at the window of the *Meridian Star*'s deckhouse and watched the storm build over the western hills, and felt, as she always did before a difficult operation, perfectly calm.

Behind her, the deckhouse hummed. Charts covered the table, weighted at the corners with brass fittings salvaged from wrecks older than the company. Her operations chief, Brandt, stood over them with a grease pencil he was afraid to use, the way he was always afraid to use things around her until she told him otherwise.

"Say it," Ilsa said.

"Recon picked up a small craft in the eastern cuts around 2300. Aluminum skiff, single operator, no lights. It slipped the picket." Brandt's jaw worked. "We lost it in the reeds above the third channel marker."

"Description?"

"Too dark for detail. Operator was small. Moved like they'd done it before." He hesitated. "Could be a fisherman."

"Fishermen run lights, Brandt. Fishermen don't thread a picket line in the dark." She turned from the window. Rain had begun to stipple the glass, fat warm drops, the monsoon's scouts. "Someone is going upriver ahead of us. Someone who knows the river and knows how to be unseen. Which means either a survivor of Seven—which would be remarkable—or a tracker."

"Tracking who?"

"The person we're tracking." She let that sit. "Seven found the wreck. You understand that by now. The sonar returns were unambiguous—hull intact, hold sealed, sediment cover minimal. After a hundred and thirteen years." She crossed to the chart table and tapped one lacquered nail on a point above the gorge, where a pencil notation read, in her own handwriting, *Q-POOL*. "They found it, they opened a channel to their employers' frequency, and then they went silent. Which means someone aboard understood what they'd found and made a decision about it."

"You think they tried to report it."

"I think someone tried, and someone else cut the transmission, and then our friends topside had an accident of some kind." Her voice never changed register; that was the trick of it, the thing that made new crew uneasy. She spoke about drowning men the way other captains spoke about weather. "No bodies at the camp, per the flyover. Bodies are evidence. Evidence travels. So they took them—or the survivors scattered, which is worse, because scattered people talk eventually."

Brandt shifted his weight. "The client wants confirmation before we commit divers past the gorge. With the monsoon—"

"The client," Ilsa said, "has wanted confirmation for a hundred and thirteen years, along with everyone whose grandfather signed the same paper. They will get confirmation when the manifest is in my safe and the hold is collapsed under two hundred tons of quarry rock. Not before." She straightened the chart's corner, a small precise gesture. "Weather gives us five days of workable conditions, maybe six. After that the gorge closes and stays closed, and whatever happens up there happens without witnesses. That is not a problem. That is the design."

"And the skiff?"

"Double the picket, but don't chase it." She looked back at the window, at the rain beginning to silver the river, at the dark country upstream where the gorge waited with its secret held tight as a fist. "Anyone running that river alone, ahead of us, in front of a monsoon, isn't fleeing. They're hunting. Let them wear themselves out on the rapids. The river does most of our work for us—it always has." A faint pause, almost gentle. "But if they reach the pool before we do, Brandt, I want to know immediately. Not at the next scheduled check-in. Immediately."

"Yes, Captain."

He left. Ilsa remained at the window a while longer, listening to the rain thicken, thinking about manifests and grandfathers and the particular mercy of a drowned thing staying drowned. Her family had kept the valley quiet for three generations. It was not cruelty. It was maintenance—the way you maintained a levee, a lock, a lie that held back more water than any of them.

Upstream, thunder rolled along the gorge like something clearing its throat.

She went to brief the divers.
