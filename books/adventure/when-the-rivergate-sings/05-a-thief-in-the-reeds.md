## 5. A Thief in the Reeds

The reeds at Cutter Bend grew tall as a man and twice as stubborn, and Mara had been lying among them for three hours when the thief finally came.

She'd picked the spot the way she picked all her spots: by reading what the land wanted to hide. The bend was the last easy water before the gorge country began, a slack eddy where any sensible traveler would beach to rest, to eat, to breathe. The mud there held prints like a ledger holds debts—heron tracks, coot tracks, and, pressed deep near the willow roots, boot prints with a cracked heel. She'd found the same cracked heel forty miles downstream, two days ago, beside a cold fire ring built with a soldier's economy: three stones, no more, ash buried under silt.

Whoever it was moved fast, ate little, and knew someone might follow.

Mara lay flat on her stomach in the reeds with her rifle slung across her back and her knife loose in its sheath, watching the light go long and copper over the water. Her left arm had gone numb an hour ago. She didn't shift it. Ten years of erasing herself had taught her that discomfort was cheap and movement was expensive; she could lie still until her own heartbeat felt like someone else's, until the reeds forgot she'd ever arrived, until she was nothing but wind-shiver and waiting.

That was the whole trick of her life, really. Be nothing. Answer nothing. Want nothing loud enough to leave a mark.

Then the radio handset in her pack crackled, faint as a dying insect, and she remembered that wanting something was exactly how she'd ended up here.

* * *

It had started with the handset, four days ago, half-buried in salt crust at the edge of the flats—a salvage expedition call sign stamped into the casing, VOSS RIVER SURVEY 7, and inside it a corrupted final transmission she still hadn't fully played herself. Static, mostly. A voice fighting through it. *—if anyone reads this, we're moving upriver, they came at—* and then nothing, cut mid-word, the way a life gets cut.

She'd raised the frequency every night since. Static answered. Then, last night, static had answered back—which meant someone else was listening too, or someone else was transmitting, and either way the desert's silence had stopped feeling like the silence she'd chosen and started feeling like a door closing on people who were still alive behind it.

So she'd followed the cracked-heel boots out of the flats and into the river country, and now here she was, numb-armed in the reeds, watching the thief come down to the water.

He came carefully. That was the first thing she noticed. He kept to the shadow line of the willows, paused at the tree edge for a full minute scanning the bend, and crossed the open ground in short bursts rather than one walk. He carried a canvas duffel that rode wrong on his shoulder—heavy on one side, balanced like a man used to carrying weight he didn't want seen. He was lean, sun-darkened past his collar, maybe thirty-five, and he moved with a competence that made her revise her estimate of the danger upward, not down.

He knelt at the waterline and filled a tin cup. And while his head was down, drinking, Mara rose out of the reeds like the river giving up a dead thing, and put the muzzle of her rifle six inches from the base of his skull.

"Don't," she said.

He went absolutely still. Even his breathing stopped, which most people couldn't manage. Water dripped from the cup into the mud between his knees, each drop loud as a hammer.

"Slowly," she said. "Put the cup down. Hands flat on the ground. Fingers spread."

He did it. Textbook. Which annoyed her, because textbook meant trained, and trained meant hired, and hired meant Voss.

"The duffel," she said. "Open it with two fingers. Anything that isn't food or rope comes out slow."

His hands moved to the buckle. Stopped. "You're the ranger," he said.

Her finger tightened a fraction on the guard. "Turn your head. Slowly."

He turned it—and the world tilted sideways.

She knew the face. Not from a wanted sheet, not from a fleet roster, not from anywhere in ten years of files and salt flats and careful forgetting. She knew it from a kitchen in Rivergate twenty-two years ago, from a boy of thirteen sitting on the counter eating bread ends while their father read shipping manifests aloud, from a face that had looked at her across a supper table the night before everything broke, the night before she left and never came back and let the name Lockridge fall off her like a coat into deep water.

"Hello, Mara," Tam said.

* * *

For a long moment neither of them moved. The river ran. A coot exploded out of the reeds somewhere behind her and her heart slammed once, hard, before discipline caught it.

"You're dead," she said finally. It came out flat, which surprised her; she'd expected it to come out shaking.

"I get that a lot." He didn't smile. His eyes stayed on hers, careful, the way you watch a dog you've bitten before. "Father put it in the obituary when I was nineteen. Drowned in the spring flood. Very tidy. There was even a grave."

"There's a grave?"

"Empty. He paid for the stone himself." Something moved through his face then, quick and gone, like a fish turning under ice. "I checked, later. Dug down two feet just to be sure I wasn't haunting myself."

Mara's rifle didn't waver, but her balance did—some interior keel shifting, the flats tilting. Twenty years. Twenty years of being no one, of sanding the name off every document with her own hands, and the whole time there had been another ghost walking around wearing the same name, erased by the same hand.

"Why," she said. It wasn't really a question. It was the whole question, every version of it, compressed to one word.

"Because I saw something." He said it simply, no performance in it. "When I was nineteen. Before I ran. You know how Father kept the ledgers—the old ones, grandfather's, locked in the study cabinet."

"I know."

"Well. Nineteen-year-olds pick locks." He sat back slowly on his heels, keeping his hands visible, an old habit of men who'd spent time around guns pointed at them. "I wasn't looking for anything. I was looking for money to leave. And I found a page that didn't belong in a shipping ledger. A payment schedule. Dated 1911. Paid to a list of names for *services rendered, silence maintained*, going down generation after generation like a subscription. Our family has been paying families in this valley to keep quiet about something for a hundred years, Mara. Do you understand? It's not a scandal. It's an annuity."

The word landed in her chest like a stone dropped down a well. She thought of the great house above Rivergate, the one she hadn't seen since she was seventeen. She thought of her father's voice reading manifests at the supper table, steady as scripture, and how she'd always thought the steadiness was strength.

"What did they pay to keep quiet about?" she asked, though some part of her already knew, the way you know weather coming in your bad knee.

"That's the part the ledgers don't say. They only say *the Meridian*. Grandfather's barge. The one that sank in '11 with the loss of all hands—eleven dockworkers, according to the official record. But the payments start the same month, and they never stop, and nobody pays a hundred years of hush money for a normal shipwreck." He looked at the river. "So I spent twenty years finding out what happened to the Meridian. And three weeks ago I finally got close enough that somebody noticed."

"Voss."

"Voss." He said the name like spitting. "I stole from them. Not the expedition—I want to be clear about that, because I know what you think you're following. I broke into their survey office at the river mouth and took their dive charts and their sonar plates. Because their sonar found something in the flooded quarry pool above the second gorge, and their divers have been on it for a month, and what they brought up—" he tapped the duffel "—is in here. Or the parts of it I could carry."

"Open it."

He unbuckled the flap and drew things out one at a time, laying them on the mud between them like a card player showing a losing hand. Waterproof chart tubes. A slate covered in sonar tracings—the unmistakable long hull-shadow of a barge, intact, sitting upright in sixty feet of black water. And last, wrapped in oilcloth, a brass fitting crusted green with age, stamped with a maker's mark and a date: *MERIDIAN — 1908.*

"They know exactly what's down there," Tam said quietly. "And they're not raising it. They're sealing it. Blasting the quarry mouth shut with rock and concrete once the monsoon floods give them cover. By the time the water drops in autumn, there'll be nothing down there but rubble, and the ledgers will be the only proof left, and the ledgers are in a safe in a house full of people whose entire fortune depends on those pages staying quiet." He looked up at her. "Including yours. Including mine. We're not witnesses, Mara. We're beneficiaries. That's what I ran from. That's why I'm dead."

The reeds whispered. Somewhere upriver, thunder muttered—not rain yet, but the monsoon clearing its throat, days out, gathering.

Mara lowered the rifle. She told herself it was because her arm was numb, because a shaking aim was no aim at all, because there were a dozen tactical reasons. None of them were the real reason, and she knew it, and she hated him a little for making the real reason obvious.

"You let me track you," she said. "Forty miles. You buried your fires. Why bother, if you wanted to be found?"

"I didn't want to be found by *them*." He nodded upstream, toward the gorge. "There's a gunboat working the narrows. Voss fleet pennant. They've been sweeping the banks for two days. I heard their engines at dawn." He paused, and something almost like humor touched his mouth without reaching his eyes. "You, I figured I could handle."

"You figured wrong. I had a rifle at your neck."

"And you haven't fired it." He gathered his things back into the duffel, unhurried, as if the rifle had never mattered. "That's the thing about you, Mara. Father used to say you were the soft one. Sentimental. I used to believe him. Then I watched you walk out of that house at seventeen with one bag and never look back, and I understood—you're not soft. You're the only one of us who actually did what the rest of us only talk about. You cut the name off clean." He stood, shouldering the bag. "I'm asking you not to cut me off too. Not yet. Give me until the next bend."

"Give you what?"

"A hearing. That's all. One fire, one night, everything I know—including why the survey expedition went silent, because I know that too, and it's worse than you think." He held out his hand, palm up, empty. An offering or a dare. "Or shoot me and take the charts, and explain to yourself later why you did it. You're good at explaining things to yourself later. It runs in the family."

The anger came up in her then, hot and old, twenty years of it with nowhere to go—anger at him for existing, for being erased by the same hand that had erased her, for knowing their father's voice well enough to imitate its cruelty, for standing in the reeds with their grandfather's crimes wrapped in oilcloth and calling it family. She wanted to hit him. She wanted to turn around and walk back into the desert and be no one again, and she recognized, with cold clarity, that she couldn't. The handset in her pack had a dead man's voice on it. The quarry pool had a hull full of bones. There was no longer any direction to walk that led away.

"One fire," she said. "One night. And if you lie to me even once, I leave you for the gunboat."

"Fair terms." He almost smiled. "You always did negotiate like Father."

"Don't," she said sharply, and he had the grace to look sorry.

They walked upstream along the willow shadow as the light failed, two ghosts from the same grave, keeping ten feet apart because neither of them knew yet how close to stand. Behind them the river ran dark toward the sea, carrying everything it carried, saying nothing, the way it had said nothing for a hundred years.

By the time they reached the driftwood bar at the head of the bend, the first true thunder of the monsoon rolled down the valley like a door slamming, and Tam fed the fire low and began, quietly, to tell her what their grandfather had drowned.
