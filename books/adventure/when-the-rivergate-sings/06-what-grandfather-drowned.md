## 6. What Grandfather Drowned

The driftwood caught on the third match.

Tam Lockridge crouched over the small flame like a man guarding it from thieves, cupping his hands around the heat until the salt wind off the flats gave up trying to steal it. They had made camp in the lee of a collapsed boathouse half a mile upriver from where he'd ambushed her—or she'd ambushed him; the distinction still stung—and the tide was coming in below them, filling the channel with that slow, patient gurgle the river made when it was thinking about drowning something.

Mara sat across the fire with her knees drawn up and her rifle across them, and she watched him the way she'd watched him all day: like sign to be read. Like scripture. He'd forgotten how she did that—how she could make a person feel like a track in the mud, something to be interpreted and then either followed or shot.

"You said you could prove what's under the river," she said.

"I said I could."

"Then prove it."

He almost laughed. Twenty years, and the first conversation his sister offered him was an interrogation. But then, he'd known it would be. He'd been tracking *her* for three weeks before she ever picked up his trail—the great irony of it, the thief hunting the ranger who was hunting the thief—and everything he'd learned about Mara Lockridge in those weeks had come from other people's mouths, because she had erased herself so thoroughly that even her own file was mostly redactions. Ten years in the ranger service and no photograph newer than her induction portrait. No forwarding address. A commendation she'd refused to attend the ceremony for.

She hadn't left the world. She'd just stopped letting the world hold anything of hers.

Tam reached into his coat and drew out the oilcloth packet, and set it on the sand between them without opening it.

"That's not the proof," he said. "That's Grandfather's ledgers. The real ones, not the ones in the Rivergate historical society with the nice handwriting and the missing years. These are the ones he kept himself, in his own hand, the ones he paid four men to bury in a dry cellar in Cutter Bend." He turned the packet slowly with one finger, like turning a dial. "The proof is under forty feet of water at the bottom of the old quarry pool past the second gorge. And there are people upstream right now who are going to make sure it stays there."

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark, a night heron called once and went quiet.

"The expedition," Mara said. "The salvage survey. That's what your radio call sign was."

"Voss Marine Contracting hired them to do a pre-salvage assessment on the *Meridian*." He watched her face when he said the name. She didn't flinch, but something behind her eyes moved, the way water moves under ice. "You know the name."

"Everyone from Rivergate knows the name. The barge that sank in 1911. Eleven drowned. There's a plaque on the customs house."

"There's a plaque," Tam agreed. "There's also a hull, sitting upright on the quarry floor, intact, sealed, exactly where it went down. And inside that hull is the ship's manifest, the crew list, the cargo log, and—" he tapped the oilcloth packet— "the correspondence that explains why a barge loaded with dockworkers' strike pay and union ledgers happened to be taking on water in calm seas at two in the morning with no storm reported for sixty miles in any direction."

Mara was very still. The firelight made her face into something carved, all planes and shadow, and Tam thought: *she looks like Father.* He hated that thought and kept it anyway, because some thoughts you don't get to refuse.

"Say it plainly," she said.

"Our grandfather scuttled his own barge with striking dockworkers aboard." He said it flat, without decoration, because decoration would have been mercy and she hadn't asked for any. "November 1911. The Rivergate docks were locked out for five months. The workers were starving. A group of them seized the *Meridian* at anchor—it was our family's barge, carrying grain we'd bought cheap off their hunger—and they held it as leverage. They weren't pirates, Mara. They were men asking for wages they'd already earned. And Aldous Lockridge couldn't allow it, because if the strike held, the rail contract died, and if the rail contract died, the Lockridge fortune never happens, and if the fortune never happens, there's no Rivergate. So he opened the seacocks at night, with eleven men sleeping aboard, and let the river do the murder for him. Then he spent the rest of his life building a town on top of it and paying people to forget."

* * *

He'd expected her to argue. That was the strange part—he'd rehearsed the argument for weeks, built counterpoints like flood walls, and she gave him nothing to defend against. She just sat there, and the firelight moved on her face, and after a long time she said, in a voice with no inflection in it at all:

"How do you know?"

"Because I grew up on this river too." He fed a piece of driftwood to the flames. It was dense, salt-soaked, burned slow and hot and green-smelling. "Not the way you did. You got the house on the hill and the tutors and the summers in the capital. I got the other side of the family. My mother worked the Voss yards before Voss was a fleet—when it was one barge and a bad reputation. She loaded grain for your father's company. She knew things. Dockworkers always know things; it's the only inheritance the job comes with." He shrugged. "When I was fourteen she told me about the *Meridian*. Not the plaque version. Her grandfather's version—he was one of the lockout men, one of the ones who survived because he wasn't aboard that night. He carried it his whole life like a stone in his boot. The families that lost people in 1911, they never talked about it openly. You know why? Because half of them ended up working for Lockridge enterprises anyway, because there was nowhere else in the valley to work. Grief with a payroll attached. You don't bite the hand that drowned your father if the same hand feeds your children."

"And you believed her. Your mother."

"I checked." He smiled, and it came out crooked. "That's the difference between us, maybe. You ran from the name. I dug into it. I spent six years finding the ledgers. Four more finding someone who could read the water-stained pages without destroying them. And then eight months ago, I found out someone else had found the wreck."

"The survey expedition."

"Voss hired them. Officially: hazard assessment, structural mapping, whether the hull posed a navigation risk to the new shipping channel." He shook his head. "Unofficially—Ilsa Voss doesn't spend money on unofficial things by accident. Somebody tipped her that the hull was intact. Somebody who knew what was inside it. And Voss has been quietly buying silence up and down this valley for twenty years—she's the disposal service for every family that profited. Ours included. Do you understand what I'm telling you? The Lockridges aren't Voss's enemies. We're her *clients*."

Mara's jaw tightened. "Then why steal from them?"

"Because the survey team found the manifest." He leaned forward, and the heat of the fire pressed against his face. "Three weeks ago, Priya Chandrasekar—the team's archivist, a good woman, careful—sent a burst transmission on a private band. Not to Voss. To a number my mother's old union contacts still watch. The message was nine words: *Hull intact. Manifest present. They intend to destroy it.* I've been moving upriver ever since. I stole the dive manifests and the site charts out of Voss's field office at the mouth—not to sell, before you ask, though I gather that's what your file says about me—to find out where the wreck is before Voss's divers finish cutting into it. And then I made the mistake of using a radio."

"You led me straight to yourself."

"I led myself straight to *you*," Tam corrected. "I knew a ranger had picked up my trail. I hoped it might be someone who'd listen. I didn't dare hope it would be you." He looked at her across the fire. "But here we are."

* * *

The tide had turned while he talked. He could hear it below the bank, the channel emptying now, the river drawing its long breath back toward the sea. Mara hadn't touched the ledger packet. It sat between them on the sand like a third person at the fire, patient, waiting.

"You're lying," she said finally—but her voice cracked on the last word, just slightly, a hairline fracture in poured concrete, and Tam knew the lie wasn't aimed at him. It was aimed at herself.

"Am I."

"Our grandfather built the grain terminal. The levee system. The school. Half of Rivergate exists because of him." Her hands had closed into fists on her knees. "I grew up on those stories. Every Lockridge does. *Aldous Lockridge dragged a town out of the mud with his bare hands.* There's a statue of him in Founders' Square. Children put flowers on it."

"He paid for the flowers too."

"*Stop it.*"

The word came out of her like something breaking loose—a crack of sound that scattered a roosting bird somewhere down the bank. Tam stayed very still. He'd seen rangers handle spooked animals; you didn't move, didn't meet the eyes, you let the moment pass through. But Mara wasn't an animal. She was his sister, and she was staring into the fire with an expression he recognized from mirrors, from his own worst nights: the look of a person watching a load-bearing wall develop cracks.

"I used to be proud of it," she said, quieter now, and it cost her visibly, each word pried up like a nail. "The name. When I was young. Before I understood what the name cost everyone else. I thought—we were the family that *built* something. In a valley where everything washes away, we built something that lasted." She laughed, short and terrible. "It lasted because he killed eleven men to keep it. And then he wrote the plaque."

"Aldous was good at writing," Tam said. "It's all in the ledgers. He drafted the official report himself—the one about the storm, the shoaling, the tragic accident. He submitted it to the harbormaster's office with a donation attached. He sent condolence payments to the families. Small ones. Enough to keep them quiet, not enough to keep them comfortable. One payment per dead man, per year, for fifteen years, and then the line item in the books just... stops. As if grief had a term limit." He nudged the oilcloth packet an inch closer to her. "Page forty-one. His own hand. *Disbursements re: Meridian matter, concluded.* Concluded. Like a contract."

Mara picked up the packet. Her fingers were steady—ranger's hands, desert hands—but Tam saw the effort it took to keep them that way, saw it in the tendons standing along the backs of them. She unwound the oilcloth. Opened the first ledger. Turned pages in the firelight, fast at first, then slower, then stopping altogether somewhere near the middle, and Tam watched his sister read their grandfather's handwriting and felt the distance between them collapse into something else—not trust, not yet, but a shared gravity. Two people falling toward the same ground.

"November ninth," she read aloud. Her voice had gone flat again, but it was a different flatness now, the flatness of a woman reading coordinates before a shot. "*Barge secured. Matter resolved by morning. No witnesses required.*" She closed the book. "No witnesses required."

"He meant the crew he hired to open the seacocks. Three local men. He paid them each a year's wages and shipped them downcoast within the week. Their descendants are in Rivergate today. They don't know why their great-grandfathers suddenly left in 1911 and never spoke of it."

"And the eleven aboard."

"Drowned in their sleep. Or woke up in the dark with the water coming in, which is worse, and I try not to think about which." Tam stared into the fire. "The expedition's sonar maps show boot soles in the sediment, Mara. Boots, and belt buckles, and a tin cup fused to a rib bone. The hull's sealed. It's been a sealed room for a hundred and fourteen years. Everything they were is still in there, sitting at a table, waiting for somebody to open the door."

Silence, except the river and the fire. Mara set the ledger down with exaggerated care, the way you set down something that might go off.

"And Voss means to destroy it."

"Cut the hull open, strip the interior, melt the paper records, log it all as structural debris. By monsoon season there'll be nothing left but scrap and silt, and the plaque on the customs house will still say *lost in heavy weather*, and the statue in Founders' Square will still have flowers on it." He paused. "Unless."

"Don't say unless."

"Somebody has to reach that wreck before her divers do. Somebody who can run the gorge ahead of the fleet, who knows how to move unseen, who owes the river eleven names." He met her eyes across the fire. "I can't do it alone. I've been running solo for eight months and it nearly got me killed twice, and the second time was you."

"I should have shot you," Mara said. There was no heat in it. It was almost fond, the way a knife laid flat on a table is fond.

"But you didn't."

They looked at each other over the flames, and Tam felt the strangest thing loosen in his chest—twenty years of rehearsed speeches dissolving, because none of them had accounted for this: that she wouldn't argue, wouldn't deny, would simply pick up the ledger and read it and believe him, because she was a tracker, and trackers trust evidence over comfort. Their grandfather had bet everything on the valley preferring comfort. He'd bet right for a hundred and fourteen years.

Mara stood abruptly, walked to the edge of the bank, and stood looking upriver into the dark, where the channel bent north toward the flats and, beyond them, the black line of the first gorge. Tam let her stand there. He banked the fire low and waited.

When she came back, she was carrying her pack, and her face had settled into something he'd only seen in photographs of her—the induction portrait, the one commendation photo, the young ranger with everything ahead of her and nothing behind. Except now the face was older, and the thing behind it wasn't ambition. It was arithmetic.

"The survey camp," she said. "Where is it."

"Past the first gorge. Old ferry landing at Miller's Bend. Why?"

"Because if Voss's divers are already cutting into that hull, then the survey team that found it is either bought, gone, or dead—and every one of those possibilities tells us something we need to know before we run a gorge in front of an armed fleet." She slung the pack onto her shoulder. "We leave on the next tide."

"We?" Tam kept his voice level, but something in it must have shown, because she glanced at him—one flicker, there and gone.

"You said you can't do it alone. I'm saying you won't have to." She looked down at the ledger in his hand, then away, upriver, where the dark was deepest. "Don't make it mean more than it does. I'm not doing this for the name. I'm doing it because eleven men are sitting in the dark at the bottom of a quarry pool, and the whole valley has agreed for a century to pretend they're not there, and I am very, very tired of pretending."

She turned and started checking the skiff's lines, and Tam stood alone at the dying fire with the ledgers against his ribs and his heart going hard, and above the far bend of the river the first thin clouds of the monsoon season were beginning to gather like a debt coming due.
