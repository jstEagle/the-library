## 7. The Silent Camp

They smelled the camp before they saw it.

Not smoke—Mara had half hoped for smoke, the gray thread of a cookfire that would mean someone alive, someone waiting, someone who could tell them where the expedition had gone and why their radio had died mid-word. What she smelled instead was canvas gone sour with river damp, kerosene spilled and never wiped up, and underneath it all, faint but unmistakable, the sweet-rot stink of food left to spoil in the heat.

Tam stopped at the edge of the reed bank and put a hand on her shoulder. She shrugged it off before she knew she was doing it. He let his hand fall without comment, which was its own small apology, and crouched beside her in the cattails.

"That's not right," he said quietly.

"No," Mara said. "It's not."

A working camp had a rhythm you could read from a distance, the way she read sign on the salt flats: gear racked neat against weather, tarlines strung taut, boots paired under tent flaps. A camp abandoned in panic looked different—scattered, burned, marked by whatever fear had driven people out of it. This camp looked like neither. It looked *tidied*. And tidied was worse than either, because tidied meant hands, and hands meant intent, and intent meant someone had come here after the expedition left—or after it stopped leaving anything behind—and arranged what remained the way a housekeeper arranges a room after a death.

"Stay low," she said. "Follow my line. If I stop, you stop."

"You don't have to keep giving me orders like I'm one of your recruits."

"You're not one of my recruits." She pushed through the reeds. "My recruits do what I say."

Behind her, she heard him exhale something that might have been a laugh if it hadn't been so thin. Twenty years of silence between them, and already they'd found the old sibling grammar—the needling, the refusal, the way anger and alliance could live in the same breath. It unsettled her more than the camp did. The camp she understood. Family she did not.

* * *

The camp sat on a gravel shelf above the cutbank, six tents in two rows, a surveyor's table under a tarp frame, a generator with its fuel cap hanging open. The river ran loud below, brown-green and muscular this far north, carrying the first silt of the coming monsoon down from the high country. Everything was the color of wet iron.

Mara circled the perimeter first. Old habit: never walk into a site until you've walked around it. She counted the tents again from outside—six—and noted the picket line where a boat should have been tied off. Two mooring stakes driven into the bank, both empty, ropes cut clean. Not slipped. Cut.

She came back to Tam, who stood at the edge of the gravel looking at the nearest tent like it was a door he didn't want to open.

"They cut their own lines?" he asked.

"Somebody did." She drew her knife and slit the first tent flap along the seam rather than untying it. "Watch the tree line."

Inside, the tent told its story in the flat voice of things left behind. Sleeping bags rolled and strapped. A mess kit stacked. A paperback novel swollen with damp, spine cracked at the same page for days. A pair of boots by the flap, laces tucked in—the way rangers tucked laces when they expected to come back for them.

And across the canvas wall, four long slashes, opened like the ribs of the tent itself, the fabric curled back to show the sky.

Mara knelt and examined the cuts without touching them. Clean, single strokes, made by a blade drawn fast and confident—not hacking, not searching. Someone had opened every tent the same way. Not tearing through gear. Opening the tents so they could see inside them. Inventory, not violence.

Except that violence lived in inventory too, when what you were counting was people.

She backed out and moved to the second tent, then the third. Same story each time: gear sorted, valuables taken, personal effects left. In the fourth tent she found the radio base station, its handset cord severed, its casing pried open and the frequency module removed with a jeweler's precision. That was the wound that explained the static, the transmission that had cut off mid-word. Someone hadn't just silenced the expedition's radio. They'd taken its voice apart and carried the pieces away.

In the fifth tent, a surveyor's logbook, water-stained, entries stopping three days ago mid-sentence:

*Hull confirmed intact at grid 44-Kilo. Manifest casing visible through forward hatch. Requesting extraction schedule —*

Nothing after. The sentence hung there like a man halfway through a doorway.

The sixth tent was the leader's. It was empty in a way the others weren't—not looted, *emptied*, stripped down to the ground cloth, as though whoever used it had packed everything out themselves or been made to. On the ground cloth, pressed flat by a boot heel hard enough to bruise the fabric, a single boot print. Voss issue. Mara had seen that tread pattern on the salvage fleet's dock crews at the river mouth, stamped into the mud of their loading yard like a signature.

She stood in the doorway of the emptied tent for a long moment, letting the picture assemble itself the way it always assembled itself, piece by piece, until it became a single moving image she couldn't unsee.

Voss had gotten here first. Voss had come upriver after the expedition found the wreck, had taken the leader—willingly or otherwise—had stripped the camp of anything that pointed toward the find, had disabled the radio so no distress call would ever reach the valley, and had left the rest standing in the rain like a stage set after the play closed.

No bodies. No blood. No burn pile. Voss hadn't killed anyone here.

Which meant Voss had taken everyone somewhere.

"Mara." Tam's voice came from the surveyor's table, tight and strange. "You need to see this."

* * *

He was crouched at the table where the tarp frame met the gravel, at the spot where the legs of the frame anchored into the earth. Chalk. White surveyor's chalk, the kind that came in fat sticks and wrote on anything—rock, wood, wet steel. Someone had drawn an arrow on the underside of the table's cross-brace, low, angled away from the camp, pointing upriver toward the gorge where the walls narrowed and the water went white.

It was placed to be found only by someone kneeling. Only by someone who knew to look under things. Only, Mara thought, by another ranger, or someone trained like one.

Below the arrow, in the same chalk, small and hurried, three characters:

**2 LEFT**

Tam straightened slowly. "Two of them got out," he said. "Two of them were still here when—" He stopped, recalculated. "Or two of them came back."

"After Voss swept the camp." Mara was already moving, reading the ground around the table, dropping low enough that her cheek nearly brushed the gravel. "Look. Boot prints over prints over prints—Voss crews came through at least twice, maybe three times, heavy boots, moving in formation. But under all of it—" she touched a smudge of chalk dust ground into a heel mark near the table leg, "—someone crawled. On their belly, from the tree line to the table and back. Crawled *after* the last set of Voss prints, because the chalk transfer sits on top of the tread."

"So they hid in the trees while Voss tore the place apart."

"And waited until the fleet moved on. Then they came out, checked for survivors, found none, and left a message for whoever came next." She rose, knees cracking, and looked upriver. The gorge waited out there beyond the bend, a dark seam between green walls, already breathing storm-light. "They left it for a search party. For us. They couldn't know who'd come—they just gambled that *someone* official would eventually come looking, because that's what expeditions get. A search."

Tam was quiet a moment. "Nobody's coming," he said. "Command won't authorize anything until after monsoon. You told me."

"I know what I told you."

"Then who were they leaving it for?"

Mara didn't answer, because the answer was ugly: they'd left it for anyone. A message thrown into the dark. And the dark had sent a deserter and a thief, which was more than the message deserved, and less than the writers had hoped.

She turned back to the camp and made herself look at it properly now, not as a puzzle but as what it was: eleven people's worth of lives, folded and slashed and inventoried. She thought about the paperback cracked at the same page for days. About the boots with the laces tucked in. About the man who'd written *requesting extraction schedule* and never finished the sentence, because somewhere between that word and the next, the schedule had arrived without him asking for it, wearing Voss colors.

Grief came into her the way floodwater comes into a dry channel—not all at once but rising, filling the low places first, the places she'd kept emptiest for ten years because empty places can't be hurt. She had spent a decade making herself untraceable precisely so that no one could ever hand her a scene like this one and call it hers. No missing persons with her name attached. No failed searches bearing her signature. She had erased herself from every record so that grief would never again arrive addressed to her.

And now here it was anyway, forwarded twenty years later by a brother she'd buried alive in memory, delivered to a camp full of strangers whose only crime was finding the wrong hull in the wrong river.

"Two left," Tam said again, softly. "Priya Chandrasekar was on the manifest—I saw the roster when I lifted the ledgers out of Voss's office. Hydrologist. And the expedition leader, Ansel Roque. If two got out—" He looked at her. "One of them is probably Roque. Leaders don't run."

"Leaders also don't sell out," Mara said, and heard how flat her own voice had gone. "We'll see which one he is."

"You think he sold them out?"

"I think Voss took the leader's tent down to the bare groundcloth and left everything else standing. I think you don't strip a man's tent like that unless he's walking beside you when you leave." She picked up the surveyor's logbook, wrapped it in oilcloth from her pack, and stowed it. Evidence. Testimony. Whatever else it was, it was going downstream to the valley assembly if she had to carry it in her teeth. "I think the other survivor ran the other direction, alone, injured maybe, and left the arrow hoping someone better equipped would follow."

"Then we follow the arrow."

"We follow the arrow." She was already breaking camp discipline over the site in her head—what to take, what to leave, how long the light would hold. "But understand what we're following it *into*. That arrow points at the gorge. Six tidal cycles till landfall when we started; we've burned two getting here. Four left. After that, the gorge fills with monsoon surge and nothing that floats comes back out. Voss knows the clock same as we do. She's not chasing us anymore—she doesn't have to. She's just waiting for the river to do her work."

"Then why leave the camp standing? Why not burn it?"

Mara looked at the six slashed tents, the tidy rows, the stage-set stillness of it all.

"Because a burned camp says *murder*, and murder gets investigated," she said. "An empty camp says *misadventure*. Flash flood, bad anchorage, monsoon came early. The valley reads it as tragedy and goes back to sleep." She slung her pack. "Her grandfather drowned men in that river and called it weather. She's doing the same thing with better paperwork."

Tam stared at her. Something moved through his face—recognition, maybe, or the strange vertigo of hearing your own family's history spoken aloud by the one person who had every reason never to speak it. "You believe me now," he said. "About what's under the river. About what Grandfather did."

"I believed you around a driftwood fire two nights ago." She turned upriver, toward the arrow, toward the gorge. "What I'm deciding now is what to do about it."

* * *

They struck the camp the way rangers strike a site—everything squared, traces masked, the chalk arrow left exactly where its author had hidden it, because erasing someone's last message felt like a second drowning. Mara took the logbook, a coil of rope, three flares from the signal locker, and the expedition's spare chart tube, which proved, when she cracked the seal, to contain hydrographic surveys of the upper gorge annotated in a careful hand: depths, eddy lines, a side channel at the narrows marked *unsurveyed — local report only.*

Someone had talked to a local. Someone had listened. She filed that away.

At the tree line she stopped once and looked back. The camp sat in the failing light like a held breath, six torn tents exhaling their sour smell into the dusk, waiting for a monsoon that would finish the job Voss had started and wash the whole story into the sea.

"Two left," Tam murmured beside her. "Odds are bad that either one's still breathing."

"Odds are always bad." Mara stepped into the trees. "That's never stopped anybody worth counting."

Behind them the river ran on, patient and brown, carrying its cargo of silt and secrets down toward a valley that still believed its fortune had been earned. Ahead, the gorge walls rose black against the last light, and somewhere inside them—hurt, hiding, holding a chalk stub and a hope—two people had bet their lives that the world would send someone who reads sign.

Mara intended to make sure the bet paid out.

She lengthened her stride, and the forest swallowed them both.
