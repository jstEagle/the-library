## 8. Rapids and Rust

The gorge announced itself before they saw it—a low, grinding roar that Mara felt through the soles of her boots before she heard it with her ears, the way she'd once felt thunder coming across the salt flats an hour before the sky split. She stood in the bow of the wounded skiff, one hand on the gunwale, and watched the river ahead of them fold down into a slot of white water between walls of black basalt.

"That's the first gorge," Tam said from the stern, both hands white-knuckled on the tiller. "Wren's map has it as a class three. Maybe four this time of year."

"And after monsoon?"

"After monsoon it isn't a rapid. It's a meat grinder."

Mara looked back at him. Twenty years since she'd seen that face last, and it was still happening to her in pieces—the jaw first, then the way he squinted against spray, then a dozen small inheritances of their father's face arranged on a stranger who wasn't a stranger at all. He'd stolen from Voss. He was carrying ledgers that could drown a family name. And he ran a boat like a man who'd grown up on this river, which apparently he had.

"Talk me through it," she said.

"You see the tongue?" He pointed with his chin at the smooth green V of water feeding into the chaos below. "We enter on the left side of that. Stay off the right—there's a hole there big enough to swallow us. Then it's read-and-react for about four hundred meters. Three drops. After the third drop the river bends hard left around a spur, and there's an eddy behind it where we can pull out."

"Four hundred meters of read-and-react."

"Welcome to my childhood."

The skiff was already wrong for this and they both knew it. It was a desert boat, shallow-drafted, built for the braided channels of the salt country, not for a gorge river running high and angry with pre-monsoon melt. Its hull had a patched seam from the night they'd slipped past Voss's picket, and its freeboard was a joke against water like this. But there was no other way upriver, and there were five tides left, and Odile's charts didn't lie.

Mara crouched low, redistributed their weight over the two packs, and lashed everything down with the short length of rope Tam carried. The ledgers went into the waterproof satchel, and the satchel went inside her jacket, zipped tight against her chest. Whatever else the river took today, it would have to take that from her body first.

"Ready," she said.

Tam brought them onto the tongue.

The world became noise. Not sound—noise, total and physical, a pressure that lived in the sternum. The green water steepened under them and then simply dropped away, and the skiff went weightless for one long, screaming half-second before the bow punched into the trough and the river hit them like a wall swung by hand. Cold water sheeted over the transom. Mara's teeth slammed together. She grabbed the painter line coiled at her feet and held on, watching the next drop rise toward them out of the boil.

"LEFT!" Tam bellowed. "HARD LEFT!"

She threw her weight to port without question, because he knew this water and she didn't, and the skiff slewed sideways just enough to skirt the edge of a hole that would have folded them in half. They scraped something beneath the surface—a rock the size of a truck, she'd swear later—and the hull rang like a bell, and then they were through the second drop and into the third, and the third was worse than both of them together.

It happened fast, the way disasters do: no slow motion, no clarity, just sequence. A lateral wave took the bow and shoved it toward the right wall. Tam corrected too hard. The skiff came around broadside to the current, hung there for one impossible instant like a leaf deciding, and then the river picked its side.

The rock came out of the foam like a fist.

The impact went through the hull, through the floorboards, through Mara's knees, into her spine. She heard the crack—wood and fiberglass giving up together—and then they were both in the water, and the water did not care about either of them.

It tumbled her the way a dog shakes a rag. Up, down, up, no air anywhere, only white and cold and the distant drumbeat of her own heart trying to beat against a pressure that wouldn't allow it. Her shoulder struck stone. Her hip struck stone. Somewhere in the churn she registered, distantly, professionally, that she was being flushed downstream toward the bend and the eddy beyond it, and that if she could stop fighting—if she could get feet-down and point downstream—the river might, this once, give her back.

She got feet-down. She pointed downstream. The current spat her sideways into slack water behind the spur, and she surfaced coughing, one arm hooked over a submerged shelf of rock, dragging air in ragged strips.

"TAM!"

Nothing but roar.

"TAM!"

Then movement upstream—a dark shape tumbling through the tail of the rapids, arms windmilling, and she pushed off the shelf and swam for it, catching his collar as he came through, both of them nearly going under again from the combined weight. She hauled him toward the shallows at the eddy's edge, and they collapsed together on a gravel bar, side by side, chests heaving, while the gorge roared on above them like a thing satisfied.

Tam turned his head and spat river water. "Told you," he rasped. "Class three."

"I'm going to kill you," Mara said, "as soon as I can stand."

* * *

They inventoried their losses on the gravel bar, laid out like casualties.

The skiff: dead, holed amidships, half-sunk and pinned against the rocks downstream, its bow pointing at the sky at an angle of reproach. The food: gone, except two ration bars Tam had sealed in a tin that had somehow stayed shut. The spare rope: gone. The flare kit: gone. The tent: gone. What remained was the clothes they stood in, the tin, a knife each, Tam's battered radio, Wren's storm-proof route notes—which Tam, with the foresight of a man raised by rivers, had kept in a plastic sleeve inside his shirt—and the satchel of ledgers, warm against Mara's ribs where she'd never let go of it.

"One tide gone," Tam said quietly, doing the arithmetic neither of them wanted spoken aloud. "Maybe we've got five left. And now we're walking."

"Not walking." Mara studied the gorge wall across the eddy. "There was a lockhouse. On the old survey maps—this stretch of the river was canalized once, before the dams. Lockkeepers' houses every few miles. If any of them are still standing..."

"They're all still standing. Nobody tears down stone out here. They just let the rust hold it up." Tam was already looking upstream along the bank, reading the terrain the way she read the flats. "Half a mile up. We passed the lock gates underwater back there—that's what you scraped, probably. The house should be on the bluff above it."

They climbed. The bank was loose shale and slick clay, and they climbed it on hands and knees in places, and when they crested the bluff the lockhouse stood exactly as Tam had promised: a square block of blackened stone with a corrugated roof half-rusted to lace, windows empty, door hanging open on one hinge like a jaw gone slack. Behind it, the old lock chamber lay flooded and stagnant, its massive wooden gates rotted to skeletons. Moss had taken the walls. Ivy had taken the roof beams. But the bones were good, and the bones were shelter.

Mara went in first, knife drawn, because habit is the last thing the river takes from you.

The main room was one large space with a hearth at the far end, a table legged with bricks, and a iron cot frame against the wall. Dust lay thick everywhere except—

Except along a path from the door to the back corner, where the dust had been disturbed. Recently. Days, not weeks. And in the corner, half-hidden behind a stack of rotted lock ledgers, someone had made a nest: a groundsheet, a folded jacket, an empty water bottle, and a scatter of food wrappers arranged with a tidiness that spoke of discipline rather than desperation.

Mara raised a fist. Tam stopped.

"Someone's here," she breathed. "Or was."

"Expedition?"

"Could be Voss's people. Could be anyone."

She moved along the wall, silent on the dust-free path, and put her back to the corner, and said clearly to the room: "I know you're here. I'm not Voss. My name is Mara Lockridge, and the man by the door is Tam Lockridge, and we're looking for the Rivergate survey expedition."

Silence. Long enough that she began to doubt herself.

Then, from behind the stack of rotted ledgers, a voice—low, cracked with disuse, and sharp as a blade wrapped in cloth:

"Lockridge." A pause. "Nobody says that name in this valley unless they want something."

A figure unfolded from the shadows: a woman, mid-thirties maybe, brown skin gone gray with exhaustion, hair matted, one leg bound in a splint improvised from a tent pole and strips of shirt. She held a box cutter in a hand that trembled—from weakness, Mara judged, not fear. Her eyes moved between them with the flat, calculating speed of someone who had spent days deciding whom to trust and had decided on no one.

"Priya Chandrasekar," Tam said. "You're the hydrologist. You're the one who sent the chalk arrow."

Something flickered in the woman's face. "You found the camp."

"This morning. Tents slashed, gear stripped, no bodies. Arrow pointing upriver."

"That was me." Priya lowered the box cutter an inch, then another. "I cut the arrow before I crawled here. Three days ago. Four? The leg—" She gestured at the splint with bitter economy. "Fell in the scree during the raid. Compound fracture, badly set. I've been eating snails and praying nobody came up that bank."

"The rest of your team," Mara said. "Where are they?"

"Gone. Not dead—I don't think dead. Voss's crews took them downriver on the barges. Except Dev." Her voice hardened. "Dev Anand, our expedition lead. He wasn't taken. He walked down to Voss's boat himself, the morning after the raid, carrying our survey case like a gift."

Tam went very still. "He sold you out."

"He sold us out." Priya laughed, and there was nothing funny in it. "His brother works the Voss docks at Rivergate. Voss leaned on the family. Dev folded in one conversation. One. Twelve years I've worked with that man." She looked down at her ruined leg, then back up, and her eyes had gone bright and furious. "So yes. I hid. I watched. And I saw the other thing, the thing you actually need to know, so listen."

Mara crouched down to her level. "Tell me."

"Voss's divers reached the wreck." Priya's voice dropped, urgent. "Two nights ago. I watched their lights from the bluff—green dive lights, down in the quarry pool above the second gorge. They were down there for six hours. Six hours, and when they came up they had equipment cases, and they were careful with them, careful like you are with something that matters." She fixed Mara with a stare. "Your grandfather's barge is down there, and Voss has already been inside it."

The words landed in Mara's chest like stones dropped down a well. *Careful like you are with something that matters.* The manifest. The proof. Sealed in copper, sunk with the dead for a hundred years, and Ilsa Voss's hands had already been on it.

"Did they bring anything up?" Tam asked. "Did you see—"

"I saw cases. I don't know what was in them." Priya shook her head. "But here's what I do know, and I know it because I spent two days pretending to be unconscious on a barge deck listening to crew talk before they dumped me overboard near the scree." She grimaced. "Voss isn't salvaging. She's erasing. Everything her divers bring up goes into a furnace barge, not a museum. And whatever she can't burn, she sinks deeper." Her eyes narrowed. "Which means if there's a manifest down there, it's either already ash or it's about to be. Unless somebody gets to it first."

Rain began then—not monsoon rain, not yet, but a thin preliminary drizzle that ticked against the rusted roof like fingers testing a lock. Through the empty window, the gorge had gone gray, and the river below ran the color of gunmetal.

Mara sat down slowly on the floor beside Priya's nest, her mind moving through the wreckage of the plan the way she'd moved through the wreck of the skiff: no boat, most supplies gone, five tides left, and the enemy already six hours deep into the site. Every reasonable option said turn back. Every reasonable option said report, wait, let the bureaucracy drown people on schedule the way it always did.

She thought of Odile's face when command refused the search authorization. She thought of the static cutting off mid-word.

"We need a boat," she said. "And we need a route past the second gorge that doesn't kill us. And we need—" She stopped. Looked at Priya's leg. Looked at Tam. Something shifted in her chest, unfamiliar and uncomfortable, like a joint learning a new range of motion. "How bad is the leg? Can you travel?"

Priya barked another laugh. "Ranger, I've spent four days crawling on a compound fracture. I can travel. Whether I can *walk* is a different question, and honestly, I stopped caring about the distinction somewhere around day two."

"Then we carry you. Or build you something to carry yourself." Mara was already pulling the tin of ration bars from Tam's pack, splitting them three ways, pressing one into Priya's hand. "Eat. Then tell us everything else you saw—every light, every barge position, every hour. You've been watching them longer than we have. That makes you our best chart."

Priya stared at the ration bar, then at Mara, then at Tam, and something in her exhausted face loosened by a single degree—the first degree of trust, offered cautiously, like a hand extended over deep water.

"You're really going up there," she said. "Into the quarry pool. For a hundred-year-old piece of paper."

"For the names," Mara said. "The paper's just how the names get out."

Priya held her gaze a moment longer, then bit into the ration bar and nodded, and that nod settled something among the three of them that none of them said aloud. Not a pact. Not yet. Just three people in a rusted lockhouse with one knife apiece, a dead skiff, and a river rising.

Tam spread Wren's route notes on the brick-legged table, weighting the corners with stones, and the drizzle thickened against the roof, and outside in the gray the first real wind of the season came moaning up the gorge from the sea.

"Storm's early," Tam murmured, frowning at the notes. "Wren figured we had five tides. Looking at that sky..." He tapped the page. "I'd say we've got four."

* * *

They worked through the afternoon by the gray light of the broken windows, and the lockhouse slowly became something other than a ruin.

Tam pried boards from the rotted lock gates and lashed them into a travois for Priya's leg, padding it with moss and the groundsheet from her nest. Priya, propped against the cot frame, talked and talked—positions of Voss's picket boats, the rotation of the dive lights, the layout of the quarry pool as she'd mapped it on the survey, the depth readings, the submerged old quarry road that might serve as an approach. Mara committed all of it to memory, drawing the pool in her mind the way she drew waterholes in the desert: as territory, as terrain, as a place where someone could hide.

Twice, Tam climbed the bluff to watch the river, and twice he came back with news. The first time: a Voss patrol skiff had run the gorge below, sweeping its banks with a searchlight even in daylight. The second time, near dusk, he came down faster, and his face had changed.

"Mara." He crouched beside her, voice low. "Downstream. On the far bank. Someone's signaling."

Her whole body went cold. "Voss?"

"No. Look."

She climbed the bluff beside him and looked, and there, half a mile down the gorge on the opposite bank, a small fire had been lit in the lee of a rock—one flame, shielded, deliberately visible. And beside it, a figure stood holding up a lantern, swinging it in a slow, deliberate arc. Once. Twice. Three times.

Not a distress signal. A greeting.

Priya, hauled up between them on the travois, squinted down at the light, and then made a small sound in her throat.

"That's Wren's pattern," she whispered. "Three swings. The hermit of Cutter Bend uses it. The old river families used it. It means *safe harbor, come by night.*"

Mara watched the lantern swing, and swing, and swing, patient as a heartbeat, and felt the strange new thing in her chest shift again—that uncomfortable, unpracticed motion of trusting, of being met instead of merely surviving. Someone down there knew they were alive. Someone down there was waiting.

"Who told them we were coming?" Tam asked.

"No one," Priya said slowly. "That's the thing about Wren. Nobody tells Wren anything. Wren just knows. The graves at Cutter Bend—the 1911 dead—Wren tends them, and the old families say the river talks to whoever listens long enough." She looked at Mara. "If Wren's signaling safe harbor, then Wren already knows what you're carrying. And I'd bet the ledgers in your jacket that Wren also knows a way past the second gorge that isn't on any chart Voss owns."

Below them, the lantern swung its patient three-count, and the wind rose, and the first fat drops of true rain began to fall, hissing on the rusted roof, running in bright threads down the black stone of the gorge.

Mara shouldered the satchel higher against her ribs and made the decision the way she made all of them now—fast, cold, and forward.

"We move at full dark," she said. "All three of us. Downriver to the fire."

Tam glanced at the sky, at the rain, at the lantern. "And after Cutter Bend?"

Mara watched the light swing through the wet dark, three beats, over and over, like a promise the river was finally willing to keep.

"After Cutter Bend," she said, "we go find out what Voss pulled out of my family's grave—and whether she burned it, or whether there's still time to take it back."
