## 9. Ilsa Voss

The rain came in off the gorge like a rumor of worse things, and Ilsa Voss stood at the gunwale of the *Meridian's Daughter* and let it hit her face until her first officer stopped pretending not to worry.

"Captain. The prisoner's awake."

"I know," she said. "He's been awake for an hour. He was waiting to see if we'd notice."

She turned from the rail. Below decks, the salvage fleet rode at anchor in the drowned quarry pool—six hulls, forty-two crew, three dive rigs, and one man lashed to a chair in the hold of the flagship who had once been the best surveyor money could hire on this stretch of river. Edmun Hale. Forty-one years old. Two daughters in Rivergate, a wife with bad lungs, and a signature on a contract that had promised him discretion and paid him enough not to ask what the discretion was for.

Ilsa had known it would come to this from the moment she read his first sonar returns. Good men always thought they were being hired for their instruments. They were always hired for their silence.

She went down.

* * *

The hold smelled of bilgewater and lamp oil and the particular sourness of a man who had spent two days deciding how afraid to be. Edmun Hale sat in the chair they'd given him—unbound now; she'd ordered the ropes cut an hour ago, because fear worked better when a man could imagine leaving—and he watched her come down the ladder with the careful blankness of someone who had rehearsed a speech on the way upriver and was already hearing it die in his throat.

"Captain Voss." His voice cracked on the second word. He cleared it. "You should know your divers found nothing. The hold's silted over. Whatever your clients think is down there—"

"Edmun." She pulled a crate across from the bulkhead, set it facing him, and sat, knees apart, forearms on her thighs. The posture of a woman settling in for a long conversation, which is what frightened men most—the ones who wanted to hurt you were quick. "Your sonar mapped an intact hull. Nineteen meters, iron-banded, lying in the quarry scour pool under four fathoms of flood silt. You flagged it as *Meridian*, 1911, probable cargo loss. Then you filed a correction calling it a slag barge. Slag barges don't have passenger fittings. Your own drawings show bench seating along both rails."

Hale said nothing.

"You corrected it," Ilsa continued, "because someone reached you before the correction did. Not me. Not my office. So either you're cleverer than your file suggests—which it does not suggest—or you understood what you'd found and understood it was worth more buried than raised." She tilted her head. "Which means someone told you what it was worth buried. Who?"

"You already know," Hale said. "That's the whole point of people like you, isn't it? You already know everything, and the knowing is the payment."

It was, more or less. Ilsa let the silence sit. In her experience, silence was the only interrogation tool that never wore out. Men filled silences the way water filled holes, and what they poured into them was always the truth, or close enough.

"The manifest," Hale said finally. "There's a manifest. Sealed. Copper case, wax-stamped, in the strongbox under the pilot's deck plate. That's what they want destroyed. That's what you want destroyed."

"Do I?"

"Don't insult me." Some spine came back into him, and she noted it without warmth: here was the man who had run eleven surveys for the fleet, the man whose crews would have walked into the gorge after him. Fear made most men small. It made a few of them precise. "I've done the arithmetic. Six hulls, armed crews, dive gear rated past working depth, and a monsoon bearing down that any sane operator would be running from. Nobody spends this much to raise scrap. And nobody sends Ilsa Voss—Ilsa *goddamn* Voss—to lose a fight with weather. You're not salvaging. You're erasing."

"And if I am?"

"Then you're the grave-robber's maid service," Hale said, "and you'll forgive me if I don't weep when the river takes its fee."

Ilsa smiled. It was a real smile, thin and unhurried, and she watched it land on him wrong—he'd expected coldness, and coldness he could have fought. Warmth unsettled them every time.

"Let me tell you a story while we wait out the squall," she said. "Nineteen eleven. The Lockridge barge *Meridian* leaves Rivergate loaded with ore and thirty-one dockworkers riding the return leg for free passage home. Somewhere above Cutter Bend, in the narrows, she ships water and goes down. All hands lost. Terrible accident. The company pays out pensions to the widows—modest ones, paid irregularly, discontinued within a decade—and builds a lockhouse and a chapel, and the valley gets a story it can live inside." She spread her hands. "A good story. A *load-bearing* story, Edmun. Take it out and see what falls."

"You weren't born," Hale said.

"No. But my grandmother ran provisions for the Lockridge yards, and she kept a ledger of her own, and in it there's an entry for the night of the ninth of October, 1911. Thirty-one meals drawn from her stores for a work crew that boarded the *Meridian* at dusk and never drew rations again. Paid in full by A. Lockridge, personal account. My grandmother wrote one line beside it and then never spoke of it again as long as she lived." Ilsa leaned forward. "You know what the line said?"

Hale's jaw worked. "*No witnesses*," he said quietly.

"*No witnesses.*" Ilsa sat back. "My grandmother wasn't a monster. She fed her children on Lockridge coin same as everyone in that town did, same as everyone still does—the yards, the mills, the trust, the whole wet cathedral of it. And when the strike came and the old man decided the cheapest way to break it was to put the organizers on a boat and open the seams, half the town knew and half the town guessed, and the half that knew bought their silence with the pension list. That's not evil, Edmun. Evil is rare. That's just weight. Everyone downstream holds up a little of it, and nobody dares be the one to let go."

"So you do it for them," Hale said. "The families."

"I do it *as* one of them." She said it flatly, without shame, because shame was a currency she'd stopped carrying years ago. "Fourteen names on my client list. Fourteen families whose grandfathers signed something or saw something or were paid something, and every one of them has spent a hundred years praying the river keeps its mouth shut. And now your survey comes along with its beautiful sonar and its honest little corrections, and suddenly the prayer has an expiration date."

"The manifest proves nothing by itself," Hale said. "A cargo list—"

"A scuttling order countersigned by Aldous Lockridge, with the seam-cutting tools itemized as *ship's stores*, and the crew manifest listing thirty-one names where the official record lists nine." Ilsa watched him flinch. "Yes. We know exactly what's in the case. We've known since 1911. Knowing was never the problem. *Proof* is the problem. Proof can be read aloud. Proof can be printed. Proof can put fourteen family fortunes into receivership and turn Rivergate from a town into a lawsuit."

"And so the divers go down," Hale said. "And the case comes up. And it burns."

"And it burns," Ilsa agreed. "And the *Meridian* gets collapsed with charges, and the survey records get amended, and in six months there's a slag barge in the quarry pool and thirty-one dead men stay exactly as dead and exactly as forgotten as they've been for a hundred and thirteen years, and the valley keeps standing." She stood, crossed to the porthole, where the rain had thickened to a gray curtain. "You think I'm destroying history. I'm maintaining infrastructure. There are eight thousand people in Rivergate whose roofs, wages, and graves all rest on that story. You want to pull the keel out from under them for the sake of—what? Justice? Justice doesn't dredge. Justice doesn't rebuild. Justice is a word people shout right before the water comes in."

* * *

He was quiet for a long time. Outside, the squall worked itself up to a proper fury, and the hull groaned around them like something dreaming.

"My daughters," Hale said at last. "Is that why you took me instead of sinking the survey skiff? Because you need me to sign the amendment?"

"I took you because sinking the skiff creates a body, and bodies create inquiries, and inquiries create documents, and documents are the one thing I'm paid to prevent." She turned back to him. "But yes. The amendment needs the surveyor's seal. Yours. Which brings us to the actual conversation."

"Which is?"

"You have a choice, Edmun, and I want you to understand that it's a real one, because I despise men who offer false choices—it's lazy craft." She counted it off against her fingers. "One: you sign the amendment, take your fee doubled, and go home to your girls, and in ten years you tell yourself the slag barge story so many times you believe it. Most men pick this. There's no dishonor in it. Two: you refuse, and I keep you comfortable but confined until the monsoon passes, and then the amendment gets forged anyway—you should know I have a man aboard who's very good at your hand—and you spend the rest of your life knowing you changed nothing except how long you were away from your family."

"And three?"

"Three," Ilsa said, "is whatever you're about to propose, because you didn't survive eleven seasons on this river by having no third option. Go on. I'm listening. Genuinely."

He looked at her then with something that wasn't hatred—she'd seen hatred plenty, and this was sadder and more dangerous. It was the look of a man measuring her.

"There's a ranger," Hale said. "Desert side. She came through the flats asking after a stolen radio. Word is she's gone upriver alone, no flag, no filing. If anyone reaches that wreck before your divers finish—if anyone puts eyes on that hold and lives to talk—your whole tidy amendment dies anyway, doesn't it? Because proof that's been *seen* can't be un-seen, even if the paper burns."

Something moved behind Ilsa's ribs, slow and cold, like a door opening underwater. She had wondered, since the picket report came in—a single skiff, running dark past the fleet anchorage, too small and too skilled to be anything but trouble. She had assigned it a probability and filed it.

"She has a name?" she asked.

"They said Lockridge," Hale said. "Mara Lockridge."

The rain hammered the porthole glass. Ilsa stood very still, and let herself feel, for exactly three seconds, the shape of the thing coming for her: the old man's blood, come up the river at last, with a decade of vanishing behind her and nothing left to lose in front of her. The worst possible person. The only possible person.

"Well," she said. "Isn't that just like a Lockridge. Even the ones who walk away leave footprints."

"If she reaches the wreck—"

"She won't." Ilsa picked up the lamp. "The second gorge will handle her, or the narrows will, or the monsoon will. The river has been doing my family's quiet work since before either of us was born, Edmun, and it has never once asked for a fee." She paused at the ladder, and looked back at him sitting in the lamplight, and gave him the truth, because he'd earned that much and because truth, properly placed, was the sharpest tool she owned. "But if she does reach it—if she stands on that hull and opens that case—then no amendment, no fire, no forgery will save the fourteen families. And I will have to decide, very quickly, whether I'm the woman who maintains the peace or the woman who makes exceptions for it."

"And which are you?" Hale asked.

Ilsa climbed two rungs, then stopped.

"I've never had to find out," she said. "Pray I don't."

Above her, the storm bent the masts and the river rose in the dark, and somewhere up-gorge, in the teeth of it, a skiff with no lights was running toward the drowned dead with a thief and a survivor aboard—and Ilsa Voss, who had planned for weather, for wrecks, for witnesses and widows and the slow rot of a hundred years, began—for the first time in her career—to plan for a Lockridge.
